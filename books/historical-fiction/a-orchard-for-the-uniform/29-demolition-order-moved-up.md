## 29. Demolition Order Moved Up

The telegram came at dawn, as bad news prefers to.

Hargrove was at the window of the requisitioned harbormaster's office, watching the mist burn off the water, when the signalman's boy clattered up the stairs with the flimsy yellow slip and a salute that knocked against the doorframe. He took it without turning around. He had learned to read telegrams by weight before content — this one was short, and short ones from the District Office were never good.

*BY ORDER OF THE DISTRICT COMMISSIONER: DEMOLITION OF WORKSHOP STRUCTURE, FORMERLY HALLORAN & SONS, ADVANCED TO THE TWENTY-SECOND. CREWS TO ARRIVE NINTH INSTANT. ACKNOWLEDGE.*

He stood very still. The mist lifted off the harbor in slow rags, revealing the town below him — the crooked roofs, the orchard terraces gone brown and stubbled with autumn, the workshop itself sitting in its hollow like an old animal that had learned not to move. Ten days. The order he'd received in September had given three weeks past the first of November. Now the twenty-second. Someone had taken a pen to his timetable, and no one had consulted him.

He acknowledged it. What else did one do? He wrote *RECEIVED AND UNDERSTOOD* in his careful clerk's hand — the hand that had won him commendations for neatness in two campaigns and would likely be all anyone remembered of him — and handed the slip back to the boy, who saluted again and went down the stairs two at a time, glad to be rid of the room.

Hargrove remained at the window a long while after.

* * *

The trouble, he thought, pouring coffee he would not drink, was that he could not determine whom he had failed. That was the particular cruelty of the situation. A man could defend himself against accusation. He could not defend himself against suspicion, because suspicion had no face and therefore no mouth to answer.

There were two possibilities, and he turned them over like stones on a beach.

The first: someone had informed. The neighbor — Ferro, the man with the bitter mouth whose family's claim had been denied thirty years ago and who had come to the requisition office twice to say so, loudly, as though grievance were a debt the army owed interest on. Or the sergeant who had searched the light station and been bluffed so smoothly by the keeper's widow that he'd reported it himself, sheepishly, in a manner that might have read to a superior officer not as incompetence but as complicity. Or any of a dozen townspeople whose loyalty to each other exceeded their fear of the army by precisely the margin Hargrove had been counting on and should not have.

The second possibility was worse, and he kept arriving back at it the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth: his own superiors had grown suspicious of *him*. His requisitions had been too precise. His inventories too honest. He had signed the accounts with the same care he gave everything, never dreaming that accuracy itself could look like dissent. In the District Office, where men grew rich on the difference between what was seized and what was reported seized, a captain who filed exact figures was either a fool or a threat, and fools were promoted and threats were removed.

He thought of Brandt. The quartermaster's ledgers had shown discrepancies Hargrove had noted, initialed, and — here was the wound of it — forwarded up the chain, believing that procedure bent toward justice if only you fed it properly. He had sent those pages to the very men they implicated. He had, in effect, mailed the fire alarm to the arsonists.

The coffee went cold in its cup. Outside, the gulls argued over something dead on the tideline.

* * *

At nine he walked down into the town, because walking was the only form of thinking that still worked, and because there were things to be seen with his own eyes before the crews arrived and saw them wrongly.

The workshop sat in its hollow below the orchard terraces, long and low, roofed in slate gone silver with salt weather. Smoke rose thin from its chimney — the army had put a detail in to crate machinery, or claimed to, though the crates Hargrove had inspected last week had held mostly straw and optimism. He stopped at the edge of the lane above it and looked down, hands clasped behind his back in the posture of inspection, feeling like a fraud even with no one watching.

Six weeks. That was what the original schedule had allowed — six weeks between demolition notice and demolition, time enough for appeals, for claims to be processed, for the ordinary grinding mercy of bureaucracy to save what it could. Now ten days. And the press inside that building, the seals, the stamping irons — the only instruments on this coast that could make a document legally true — would be pulled out and shipped south, or worse, broken up for scrap with everything else.

Unless the work was already done. Unless whatever needed printing had already been printed.

He had noticed things. He was not a stupid man, whatever his superiors' arithmetic made of him. Fresh ink on crated documents — he had smelled it before he saw it, the sharp vegetative bite of new iron-gall against the older, dustier smell of paper that had waited years in a drawer. He had confronted the keeper at her lighthouse and she had given him nothing, which was itself an answer; an innocent woman would have protested, and Cyrus Reyes had simply looked at him with those grey eyes and let the silence do her lying for her, or rather had declined to lie at all, which was somehow worse and somehow more honorable, and he had left without arresting her because arresting her would have required him to believe something he did not believe, namely that she was guilty of anything except keeping a light burning in weather that would drown them all.

Ten days. If there was a forgery in progress — and he no longer permitted himself the softer word, *restoration*, though privately the word persisted like a splinter — then ten days was enough. It was more than enough. They had probably finished weeks ago and were now waiting, patiently, for him to be reassigned or discredited or both.

And yet he found, examining himself with the same rigor he applied to supply manifests, that he felt no relief at this. He felt the walls closing in, yes. But part of him — the part he did not enter into official correspondence — hoped they would finish.

That was the thought he could not file anywhere. He carried it up the hill like contraband.

* * *

Sergeant Doyle found him at noon in the harbormaster's office, standing over the wall map of the district with its pins and colored thread, looking at the workshop the way a man looks at a grave he dug himself.

"Sir. The demolition advance party's confirmed for the ninth. Engineers, two hundred fuses, a steam crane off the *Coriander*." Doyle hesitated. "They're asking about billets."

"Quarter them aboard the barge," Hargrove said. "I don't want engineers sleeping among civilians ten days before they pull down the civilians' roofs."

"Very good, sir." Doyle did not move. He was a heavy, patient man from somewhere wet and green, and he had served under Hargrove long enough to recognize the silence that meant the captain wanted to talk and would die before admitting it. "Begging pardon, sir, but there's talk in the town."

"There is always talk in the town, Sergeant."

"Aye, sir. This talk says the date moved because of us. Because somebody here stepped wrong." Doyle's voice dropped half a register. "And the men are asking whether it was them being talked about, or you."

Hargrove turned from the map. Doyle stood at attention and stared at a point six inches above his captain's shoulder, which was his way of being brave.

"And what did you tell them?"

"That the District Commissioner moves dates when it suits the District Commissioner, and no one on this coast has that kind of reach." A pause. "Though I'll admit, sir, I don't know that it's true."

"It isn't true," Hargrove said quietly. "Which means someone on this coast *does* have that kind of reach, or knows someone who does." He touched the pin marking the workshop. It was loose in its board, wobbling. "Thank you, Sergeant. Billets on the barge. Dismissed."

After Doyle left he stood a long time with his finger on the loose pin, pressing it into the map, feeling it rock in the hole it had made.

* * *

In the afternoon he did the thing he had been avoiding for two days: he opened the accounts.

Not the clean copies — the originals, the working papers, the requisition tallies with their columns of seized goods and assessed values and shipping manifests south. He spread them across the trestle table and went through them page by page with a rule and a pencil, the way he had done once before, weeks ago, when he'd first caught the scent of Brandt's discrepancies and made the catastrophic error of believing the chain of command would want to know.

The numbers had changed.

Not much. Not enough for a casual eye. But the assessed value of the workshop's machinery had been revised upward in a hand that was not Brandt's and not his own — a small, cramped, confident hand he did not recognize — and a supplementary line item had appeared authorizing "salvage disposition fees" that he had never approved. Someone above him was skimming through his own paperwork, using his signature's authority the way a thief uses a stolen coat. Every honest figure he had filed had become a garment someone else was wearing.

He sat back. The lamp on the table hissed softly. Rain had started against the harbormaster's windows, fine and horizontal off the sea, the first taste of what the old woman Vidal called the great storms, as though storms came in families, in dynasties, with names passed down like land.

So. Not suspicion then — certainty. His superiors knew exactly what he had found, because they had found his finding first, and the advanced demolition date was not merely efficiency. It was a message. *We see you, Captain. We are closing the window. Do not stand in it.*

A man with sense would request transfer. A man with ambition would bury the revised accounts and spend his remaining days being conspicuously obedient. Hargrove did neither. He squared the papers, tied them with the regulation cord, locked them in the dispatch box, and pocketed the key — and understood, with a strange calm, that he had just crossed a line from which clerks do not return. He was now holding evidence against his own chain of command in a locked box in a requisitioned fishing office at the end of the world, and the walls were closer than they had been at breakfast.

What frightened him was not that he had done it. What frightened him was how little it had cost him. Somewhere between the fresh ink at the lighthouse and this cold afternoon, the army had stopped being the thing he served and become merely the instrument nearest to hand.

* * *

Evening brought the rain in earnest, drumming on the slate roofs of the town below, and with the rain a second communication — not a telegram this time but a letter, hand-carried by a naval launch, sealed with wax instead of the usual gummed flap. Wax meant personal. Wax meant someone wanted deniability.

He broke it in the lamplight. The handwriting was elegant, unhurried, the hand of a man who had never once been rained on.

*Captain Hargrove — In view of the accelerated schedule, the District Office considers the workshop matter closed upon completion of demolition. All ancillary documentation pertaining to the structure may be disposed of locally at your discretion. Your diligence in the matter of quartermaster accounts has been noted. It is suggested that diligence of this kind, however admirable, is best exercised where conditions permit it. There is a staff posting in the capital that will suit you in the spring. — F.*

Hargrove read it three times. It was, he had to admit, beautifully done — a bribe, a threat, and a compliment braided into four sentences, none of which could be quoted in any hearing without sounding like gratitude. *Dispose of locally.* Burn the working papers, in other words. The revised values, the salvage fees, the whole paper trail of the theft — ash, and the ash his responsibility, and the spring posting his reward for lighting the match.

They thought he was for sale. They thought every man was for sale, at some price, and they had priced him modestly — a desk in the capital, dry floors, a career that would rise gently until retirement — because they had read his file and concluded he was a procedure man, and procedure men feared disorder more than dishonor.

He fed the letter to the stove and watched the wax bubble and go black.

Then he sat down and, in his careful clerk's hand, began drafting a request for a formal hearing — not an appeal, not a protest, nothing so legible — a request, couched in the most procedural language he possessed, to review certain irregularities in district requisition accounts prior to final disposition of assets. It cited regulations. It attached schedules. It was bulletproof, and it was also, both of them knew, a declaration of war delivered in the language of filing cabinets.

He would send it on tomorrow's launch. Tonight he would finish it, and check the lamp schedules, and sleep badly, and in the morning the walls would be closer still.

* * *

Near midnight the rain eased and he went out onto the harbormaster's gallery for air. The town lay dark below him, all but one point of light — the lighthouse, sweeping its patient beam across the water, steady as a pulse.

He watched it turn. Somewhere behind that glass was the widow Reyes, awake as always, keeping her light through the first spit of the great storms. Somewhere in that tower, if his suspicions were right — and he no longer troubled to call them anything else — lay documents that a dying town had bled to produce, finished or nearly finished, waiting out these last ten days the way seeds wait out winter.

*Dispose of locally.*

He realized he was smiling, and had no idea since when.

The beam swept the water, swept the shoals, swept the dark orchards climbing the hill, and came round again. Ten days. Let the engineers come with their fuses and their crane. Stone could be pulled down; he had watched it done in three countries and knew it took surprisingly little time. But paper, properly made, properly witnessed, properly entered into a register —

Paper, he thought, pulling his coat tighter against the wind, was the one thing in this war nobody had yet figured out how to demolish.

Below him, faint through the settling rain, a door banged somewhere in the town — and then, unmistakably, a lantern moving fast up the cliff path toward the light station, too late an hour for any lawful errand.
