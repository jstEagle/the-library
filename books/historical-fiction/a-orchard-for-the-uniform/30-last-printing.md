## 30. Last Printing

Ines had not slept in a bed since Tuesday, and it did not matter. Sleep was a country she could no longer afford to visit; she stood at its border and looked in and turned away. The forge was cold — the army had taken her coal weeks ago, along with everything else that burned — but her hands remembered heat anyway. They always would. Forty years at the anvil beside her husband had written themselves into her knuckles, and now those hands held a pen instead of a hammer, and they shook, and she let them shake, because shaking was only what honest hands did when they were tired.

The workshop smelled of oil, iron dust, and old paper — the smell of her whole life compressed into one room. Outside, the demolition crews slept in their tents on the common, thirty men who would arrive at dawn with ropes and crowbars to pull this building down around the press that had sealed every promise the town had ever made. Ten days early. The telegram had come like a slap: *demolition advanced, effective immediately upon crew arrival.*

So there would be no dawn's grace, no final morning of careful work. There was tonight, and there was nothing after tonight.

"Page forty-one," Rolo said from the corner, his voice cracked with fatigue. He was sixteen and looked fifty, ink to his elbows, hair standing up in black spikes where he'd pushed his hands through it a hundred times. "The Ferreira claims. Two orchard rows south of the mill race, half-share of the cooperage, dated — the date's smeared, Cyrus, I can't make it out."

Cyrus came to stand over him. Ines watched her — the keeper moved like a woman walking a plank over deep water, steady precisely because falling was unthinkable. She bent close to the water-stained page they'd recovered from Brandt's coat, pages that smelled of salt and of the man's fear.

"Eighteen seventy-one," Cyrus said. "There's a ghost of a seven under the blot. And look — Tomás marked his shorthand tick above it. His ticks always sat over the year."

Rolo squinted, then wrote the date into the fair copy in his careful schoolboy hand, each numeral placed like a stone in a wall.

Ines dipped her pen. The lamp above the worktable — one of six they'd lit, burning down the last of the oil Cyrus had traded away piece by piece — threw their four shadows huge against the whitewashed walls, so that the room seemed full of giants bending over giants' work. Marta Vidal sat at the press itself, eighty-one years old, feeding blank leaves through the roller and pulling the lever that brought down the seal, and every time the lever fell Ines felt it in her chest like a heartbeat. *Chunk.* Another page made true. *Chunk.* Another family nailed back into existence.

They had been at it nine hours. The stack of finished pages stood knee-high on the table, each one bearing the workshop's seal in faded violet ink — the same seal, the same press, the same iron hand that had stamped the original ledger sixty years before Marta was born. That was the whole trick of it, the beautiful, blasphemous trick: the army had seized the ledger, but the law lived in this machine, not in the paper. A document without the seal was a story. A document with it was a fact. The army had never thought to inventory a hand-press because the army had never understood that towns were made of more than land.

"Break," Cyrus said finally. "Ten minutes. Eat something or you'll start writing nonsense and we'll have to burn it by daylight."

Nobody argued. Ines unwrapped the cloth bundle she'd brought: hard bread, hard cheese, a jar of quince paste Marta had made in another life. They ate standing, like sailors, like soldiers — like people between watches. Through the cracked window Ines could see the tents on the common, dark shapes under a sky going the color of wet slate. Somewhere out there the sea was working itself up for something; you could hear it even from here, a long low grinding under the wind, like a millstone turning.

"They'll come at first light," Marta said. It wasn't a warning. It was arithmetic.

"Then we finish," Cyrus said. "Whatever else happens, we finish."

* * *

Ines worked on the Ferreira pages and thought about her husband Joaquim, dead eight years now, who had sharpened the seal-die himself once, when the edges wore soft. She had found his mark on the underside of the press frame tonight while moving crates — three small chisel strokes, the way he signed work he was proud of. She had put her thumb over them for a long moment and said nothing, because if she said anything she would cry, and crying used up time, and time was the only currency left in this room.

Her section was the smiths' and wheelwrights' claims, because she knew those families the way she knew iron — by grain and temper. The Almeidas. The Cordeiros. Old Senhor Bastos, who'd taught Joaquim, whose name appeared on page nineteen with a share of the charcoal rights that the army's inventory had simply erased, as though charcoal grew from government ground. She copied each entry twice, once for the register copy and once for the third record — Marta's insistence, the triplicate that would go three directions by three hands so that no single seizure could ever again undo them. One to the church, behind the saint's niche. One inland, to Marta's cousin's daughter, wrapped in oilcloth inside a bolt of linen. One to stay here, in this town, wherever here still existed after tomorrow.

The pen scratched. The press thumped. Rolo read entries aloud in a low monotone to keep himself awake, names unspooling like rope off a capstan: *Vidal, Reyes, Ferreira, Almeida, Cordeiro, Nunes, Bastos, Otero—*

"Otero," Ines said suddenly, and stopped writing.

Everyone looked up. She hadn't meant to say it aloud. But there it was on page twenty-six, in the recovered pages, in the clerk's cramped hand: *António Otero, east slope claim, denied — insufficient witness, 18—* The year blotted. The denial that had soured António's whole family against the ledger, against the town, against Marta most of all. The grievance that had sent him, two nights ago, to the army barracks with his hat in his hands.

Cyrus set down her pen. "We copy it as it is," she said quietly. "Denial and all. With the denial."

"With the—" Rolo started.

"It happened," Cyrus said. "That's what a true record is. It isn't a monument. It's a mirror." Her voice didn't rise, but something in it made the lamps seem to lean. "If we fix the old wrongs ourselves, we become just another hand rewriting history. The register gets the truth. What anyone does with the truth afterward is the next fight, and it will be fought by living people, not by us tonight."

Marta's jaw was set like a keel. For a moment Ines thought the old woman would fight it — she had fought Cyrus on exactly this ground a week ago, bitterly, in words Ines still heard in her sleep. But Marta only pulled the lever. *Chunk.* And said, to no one, "António can read the denial and weep, then. Better than reading nothing at all."

Ines wrote it down: the claim, the denial, the missing witness, all of it, her hand steadying as it went. Truth over comfort. She didn't agree with it entirely — her heart wanted to give the Otéros their slope, wanted it so badly her teeth ached — but she understood it the way she understood metal: you don't disguise a flaw in a weld. You grind it open, you see how deep it goes, and then you decide whether to fill it or cut it out. Anything else fails later, under load, when it matters most.

* * *

Past midnight the wind changed.

Ines felt it before she heard it — a pressure shift, her bad ear aching the way it did before weather, an old forge-worker's barometer in the bones of her head. Then the sound arrived: not the ordinary sea-noise but a deeper note underneath it, sustained, patient. The first great storm, coming up the coast the way the gulls had warned for two days, flying inland in ragged panicked ribbons.

"The supply ship's due in that," Rolo said, looking at the window. There was something complicated in the boy's face — he still couldn't quite hate the navy, even now, even after everything, and Ines had stopped trying to argue it out of him. Some loyalties wore out on their own or not at all.

"Then God help whoever's deck watch it is," Marta said, and pulled the lever. *Chunk.*

At half past one, the beam fell.

It happened fast, the way disasters do — faster than memory, slower than terror. Rolo had climbed the shelving at the back wall to reach the crate of blank stock, the last of the good rag paper, wedged high behind the roof trusses. Ines heard the crack of timber and looked up and saw the old tie-beam above him shed its rotten end where the damp had been eating it for years — saw the beam's sister rafter sag, groan, and let go a length of black oak as thick as her waist—

"ROLO!"

He twisted. It saved his life and cost him nearly everything else. The beam caught him across the shoulder instead of the skull, slammed him off the shelving, and drove him down onto the crate-strewn floor with a sound Ines would hear for the rest of her days — the wet crunch of a body taking weight it wasn't built for. Plaster snowed down. The lamps guttered. For one heartbeat the room was silent except for the storm finding its voice outside.

Then Rolo screamed.

Ines reached him first — she was nearest, and her legs were still the legs of a woman who had hauled pig iron for forty years. He was curled on his left side, right arm pinned under the beam's end where it lay across a crate, and his face was white as the plaster dust on it, and he was screaming words that weren't words.

"Hold him," she said, and her voice came out flat and calm, the voice she'd used over burns and crushed fingers and the day Joaquim's apprentice took a hot billet across the forearm. "Hold him still, Cyrus. Don't let him twist."

"We lift together," Marta said. She was already there, ancient hands hooked under the beam's far end. "On three. Ines pulls him clear, we don't drop it back."

"One. Two—"

They lifted. The beam weighed as much as sin. Marta's arms trembled and held anyway, and Ines got both hands under Rolo's shoulders and dragged, and he came free with a shriek that broke her heart straight down the middle, and they let the beam crash down where he'd been, splitting the crate beneath it, scattering sixty years of spare type across the floor like black seeds.

Ines knelt and ran her hands down his arm the way she'd learned over a lifetime of shop accidents. Shoulder — dislocated, she was almost sure, the joint sitting wrong under her palm, a knob where no knob should be. Upper arm — she pressed gently and he sobbed, and she felt the wrongness there too, a give in the bone that bones should never have. Not shattered. Cracked. Maybe.

"Broken," Rolo gasped. "It's broken, isn't it. I'm sorry — the paper — did the paper—"

"You idiot boy," Ines said, and her calm cracked clean through, and she was holding his dusty head against her apron and crying into his hair, great ugly sobs, the ones she hadn't cried at Joaquim's funeral because someone had to keep the forge banked. "You stupid, brave, ridiculous boy. The paper can burn. Do you understand me? Every sheet of it can burn to ash and it will not matter as much as your arm."

Cyrus crouched beside them, and Ines saw her face over the boy's head — white, stricken, the keeper's plank finally trembling under her feet. Then Cyrus closed her eyes for one breath, opened them, and was the keeper again.

"Ines. You've set bones before?"

"Bird bones. Farm bones. Joaquim's thumb, twice." She wiped her face with her wrist. "Not a boy's arm. But a break is a break."

"There'll be brandy in the stores crate. Marta — splints. Straight green wood if we must, anything. Rolo, listen to me." Cyrus took his good hand. "This is going to hurt worse than the beam. I won't pretend otherwise. But you'll write the last page yourself before morning, do you hear me? Your hand is going to sign this record. That's not comfort talking. That's the plan."

Rolo laughed — a wet, shocked laugh, the kind that comes out of young bodies when terror has nowhere else to go. "Yes, ma'am," he said.

Setting the bone was the worst hour of Ines's life, and she had buried a husband and a daughter. They did it on the worktable, swept clear, lamplight everywhere, the storm hammering the roof like a fist demanding entry. Cyrus fed him brandy until his eyes went glassy. Marta held his shoulder. Ines gripped the arm below the fracture, felt for the ends, and pulled and pushed in one motion the way Joaquim had shown her on a farm dog's leg when she was nineteen, and the bone slid home with a grating click she felt in her own teeth, and Rolo made a sound like a nail being drawn and then went gray and quiet and mercifully half-conscious.

Splinted. Bound. Sling from Marta's shawl. Alive.

Ines sat down on the floor afterward because her legs informed her they were done negotiating. From down there the room looked different — the scattered type, the fallen beam lying across the ruined crate, the towering stacks of sealed pages untouched, miraculously untouched, not one leaf damaged. Above her, Cyrus stood a moment with her hand pressed flat against the press frame, as if steadying herself against it, or thanking it.

"He was reaching for our future," Marta said quietly. "And the building tried to kill him for it."

"Then we outrun the building," Cyrus said.

* * *

Dawn was two hours off and the storm was fully arrived, rain flaying the windows sideways, and they went back to work because there was nothing else in the world to do. Ines's hands were steady again — stranger than tears, that steadiness, but she'd earned it honestly, the way you earn everything: by doing the thing with the shaking and letting the shaking wear itself out.

Rolo insisted on staying upright. They propped him in a chair at the table's end with his splinted arm cradled, and he took up his pen with his left hand and practiced loops on scrap until he could make letters that weren't insults to the alphabet, and then, when the final page of the final section came through the press, it was Rolo who leaned over it with his left hand and wrote the certification line in slow, ugly, magnificent characters: *Transcribed and verified complete, from the original record of the town, this night, by the undersigned.* Four signatures beneath it. Four names added to a book of names — the circle writing itself into the very document it had rescued.

Marta pulled the lever one last time. *Chunk.*

The seal came down on the last page, and the sound it made in that exhausted, storm-shaken room was, to Ines, the loudest sound she had ever heard. Louder than the beam. Louder than the sea. Sixty years of orchards and shares and small stubborn names, sealed in violet ink, stacked in three identical records, finished.

Nobody cheered. They were past cheering. Marta laid both palms flat on the press bed and bowed her head over it the way you bow over a grave or a cradle. Cyrus began wrapping the register copy in oilcloth with hands that finally, visibly, trembled. Ines gathered the third record into its linen bolt, and her fingers found the knot Joaquim had taught her, the one that holds in wet rope, and tied it.

Outside, the eastern sky stayed dark. Too dark. Ines had watched dawns from this doorway her whole married life, and she knew the color the sky should be turning by now, and it wasn't turning, and the storm showed no intention of spending itself.

And then, through the rain and the wind, from the direction of the common — a bell. Not the church bell. The army's camp bell, rung hard, rung wrong, over and over.

Boots on the lane. Shouting, thin with distance. And beneath it, rolling up from the harbor, a new sound: the boom of a signal gun, once, twice — wreck gun, danger gun, fired into the teeth of the storm.

Marta raised her head. "The supply ship," she said.

Ines was already moving for the door, the sealed records clutched against her chest like a child, and behind her she heard Cyrus say, very quietly, in the voice of a woman who already knew:

"It's on the shoals."
