## 32. The Reading

The lamp in the requisitioned town hall had been trimmed for a man doing inventory, not for a man reading his own indictment.

Captain Ellery Hargrove sat at the clerk's desk with the finished record open before him, and for the first hour he did nothing at all except look at it. The thing was enormous — two hundred and eleven pages of good rag paper, copied in four hands he could now distinguish as easily as faces: Marta Vidal's upright, unyielding script; Ines Corvo's quick slant; Rolo's careful boyish letters that grew steadier toward the end; and beneath them all, threading through the earliest pages like water through sand, a fifth hand he did not know but recognized anyway from its confidence — Tomás Reyes, dead two winters, writing names into oilskin by lamplight while the town above him slept.

Hargrove poured himself the last of the coffee the mess had sent over. It had gone cold hours ago. He drank it anyway, because procedure required him to be awake, and because the alternative to drinking cold coffee alone in a seized building was thinking about what the book on the desk actually was.

He opened it again at the first page and began.

*Orchard claims, north slope.* The heading was simple enough. Beneath it, entries in columns: family name, parcel, trees, shares in the press works below, and — this was what stopped him, what made him set down the cup — *witnesses*. Every entry witnessed. Not by magistrates or surveyors but by neighbors. *Witnessed by Anselm Duarte, who planted the eastern row.* Witnessed by grandmothers and blacksmiths and boys too young to hold a pen properly, their marks wobbling beside their fathers' signatures. The ledger recorded not ownership as the law understood it but ownership as memory understood it: who had planted, who had pruned, who had buried whom beneath which tree, and which child's name was spoken every autumn when the fruit came in.

He turned pages. The lamp hissed. Outside, the garrison sentries called the half-hour, their voices thin against the wind coming off the water.

At page forty he took out his own requisition lists — the fair copies, the ones he'd signed in triplicate with a straight back and a clear conscience — and laid them beside the ledger, and began the cross-checking that would take him until dawn.

It was not difficult work. That was the horror of it. The army's lists were accurate. He had made certain they were accurate; accuracy was the one virtue available to a man doing an ugly job correctly. Parcel 14, north slope, twelve apple trees and a stone wall: *requisitioned, standing structures and growing stock, valuation per schedule C.* The ledger said the same twelve trees, the same wall, and then added what Schedule C could not hold: *Planted by Joaquim Ferro in 1849, the year his daughter was born. The daughter is buried at the head of the row. Her name is Amélia. The family calls the tree by her name.*

There was no column for Amélia in the army's paperwork. There was no column for any of it. Hargrove read entry after entry where his own neat figures sat beside the ledger's crowded testimony like a soldier standing at attention over a grave he had dug without knowing whose it was.

* *

Sometime past midnight the wind rose, and he heard the lighthouse beam sweep the high windows of the hall, once around each minute, patient as a heartbeat. Cyrus Reyes had lit it. Of course she had. She was confined to her station under guard, awaiting whatever disposition the tribunal chose to make of her, and she had gone up and lit the lamp because the sea did not care about tribunals, and because — he was beginning to understand this now, three hundred pages deep into a stolen book — keeping a light burning was not a job to these people. It was a form of speech.

He thought about the morning at the lighthouse door. *You may arrest me,* she had said, or something near it, holding out the record wrapped in oilcloth the way one hands over a foundling. *But first you will have to decide whether you can carry it.* He had taken it because not taking it would have been cowardice dressed as duty, and he had told himself he would read it as an officer reads evidence: coldly, professionally, noting discrepancies.

The discrepancies were the whole of it.

By two o'clock he had filled six sheets of his own paper with comparisons, and the sheets had stopped being notes and become something else — a third document, the place where the two books met and contradicted each other. Here: a workshop share belonging to Ines Corvo's late husband, entered in the ledger in 1887 with three witnesses, valued by the army at forty crowns and signed away by a corporal who could not have been older than Rolo. Here: the Vidal orchard, central claim, oldest entry in the book, its first line written in faded iron-gall ink by a hand that must have been Marta's grandmother's — *so that it can never happen again*, and Hargrove did not yet know the story those words referred to, only that the ink had been pressed hard enough to bite clean through the page, the way a man writes when he has watched something taken once and means to prevent it forever.

Here, on page one hundred and ninety, the Ferro girl's tree. Here, page one hundred and ninety-one, the same tree listed on the army schedule as *timber stock, non-fruiting, valuation nil.*

Non-fruiting. He had signed that appraisal himself. He remembered the day — rain, mud, the surveyor hurrying because there were nine more parcels before dark. Someone had stood under that tree and told them it bore no fruit. Or no one had stood under it at all; perhaps they had simply written *nil* from the road, the way one writes *nil* about anything one does not intend to look at closely.

He got up and walked the length of the hall. His boots sounded wrong in the empty room, where the benches had been stacked against the wall and the notice still hung on the inner door: *BY ORDER OF THE MILITARY GOVERNOR, ALL PROPERTY WITHIN THE DESIGNATED ZONE IS HEREBY REQUISITIONED FOR THE DURATION.*

For the duration. The phrase had always struck him as honest, in its way. Wars ended. Durations ended. But a tree felled for timber did not stand up again at armistice, and a family scattered across three provinces did not reassemble, and a ledger locked in a transport barge's hold did not read itself out loud to the people whose names it held.

Except that someone had made sure it could.

* * *

He went back to the desk and kept reading, and somewhere in the small hours the nature of the work changed. He noticed it happening and could not stop it. For the first hour he had been checking the ledger against his lists. By three o'clock he was checking his lists against the ledger — reversing the order of authority, letting the older book judge the newer one — and finding his own signature wanting everywhere he looked.

Not fraudulent. He checked for fraud the way he checked everything, exhaustively, and the accounts were clean; Brandt's little arrangements had left fingerprints only in places Hargrove had already begun to suspect. No, the lists were honest within their terms. It was the terms themselves that stood revealed, under the ledger's long witness, as a language built to describe theft without ever using the word. *Requisitioned.* *Valuation per schedule.* *Compensation pending review.* Pending review by whom? By men in offices four hundred miles inland who would never smell cider rotting on the ground in October, never hear the presses fall silent, never learn that a tree could be named after a dead girl.

His father had been a customs man in a river port. Hargrove remembered, suddenly and completely, being nine years old and watching his father assess a cargo of wool while the merchant smiled and the tally-clerk scratched, and asking afterward why the numbers had to be written twice. *Because paper remembers longer than men do,* his father had said, *and shorter than the truth does. Your whole career, lad, is choosing which you serve.*

He had thought he'd chosen. He had believed, right up until this desk, this lamp, this hour, that procedure was a neutral country and that a man could live in it without taking sides. The book in front of him said otherwise. Procedure was a country with borders, and he had spent eleven months standing on the wrong side of one, waving other people across.

* * *

Near four o'clock the storm front arrived — not the great storm yet, the barometers still had falling to do, but its outrider, rain gusting hard against the tall windows so that the lighthouse beam came through the glass smeared and wavering, a wheel of light turning on the floorboards like something searching the room for him.

He read on.

The workshop families' section was the section that had nearly broken them all — he knew this now, from the interrogations, from the neighbor's report, from Brandt's gray-faced confession that had arrived on his desk like a dropped knife. Water-stained pages, restored at cost, deciphered in part by a boy of sixteen. Hargrove turned those leaves carefully. The press shares were intricate, generations deep, a widow's sixth-part passing to a nephew, a founder's portion split among five children who had quarreled and reconciled and quarreled again across sixty years of entries, the ledger recording even the quarrels, because whoever had begun this book had understood that a community's title to itself included its wounds.

In the margin of the workshop section, in the unknown hand — Tomás Reyes — a single note in pencil, faint: *Copied this page twice. Once for the packet. Once for my wife, if she ever wants to know why I loved this town more than she knew.*

Hargrove closed his eyes.

He saw her then clearly: the keeper's wife who had never belonged, delivering his lamp oil in the first week with a face like a locked door; the widow who had stood in this hall's doorway yesterday morning with two hundred soldiers' worth of dawn behind her and handed an officer a book instead of a weapon, daring him — there was no politer word for it — daring him to read.

He had read.

* * *

Dawn did not come gently. It came gray and wet, the rain easing to a fine salt mist, the garrison waking below with the ordinary sounds of kettles and boots, men who would breakfast and drill and stand their watches without knowing that their captain had spent the night learning the names of everyone their orders had erased.

Hargrove laid the two books side by side one final time — the army's lists, the town's ledger — and looked at them the way one looks at a scale after the weights have settled. Then he wrote, on a fresh sheet, in the small exact hand that had carried him through twenty years of service:

*Findings of preliminary examination, completed this date. The annexed record, hereinafter the Town Register, appears authentic in composition and continuous in custody. Its contents materially contradict the valuations and dispositions recorded under requisition schedules A through F. The contradiction is systematic. It is not the work of individuals. It is the design of the order itself.*

He read it back. It was the most dangerous paragraph he had ever written, because every word of it was true, and truth, entered into the record, became a thing that other men had to answer.

He did not sign it yet. His pen hovered. Signing it made it a document; leaving it unsigned kept it a private agony, and private agonies changed nothing.

Through the misted window the lighthouse beam turned and turned, steady as it had turned all night, and he understood that somewhere up there Cyrus Reyes was watching the light come up over the water, waiting — not hopefully, he suspected; she had passed beyond hope into something harder — waiting to learn whether the man she had chosen to read had been worth choosing.

Below, in the detention rooms, an old woman, a widow, and a boy sat waiting for transport.

Hargrove dipped his pen, signed his name, blotted the page, and reached for the telegram forms.

Then he paused, hand flat on the desk, and looked at the stack of blank flimsies a long moment — because there were two telegrams he could send this morning, and only one of them could ever be unsent.

Outside, the first thunder of the coming storm rolled up the coast.
