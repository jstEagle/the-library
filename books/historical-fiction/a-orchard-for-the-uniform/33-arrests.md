## 33. Arrests

They came at the hour when the lamp is most beautiful and least useful — that grey interval after dawn when the beam still turns but has lost its argument with the sun. Cyrus saw them from the gallery: six soldiers in a file along the cliff path, their boots dark against the pale grass, moving with the unhurried certainty of men who have been told exactly what to do and by whom.

She did not run. There was nowhere to run that was not already hers, already known, already mapped in Hargrove's requisition lists. She stood with her hand on the cold rail of the gallery and watched them come the way she had once watched storms come, measuring distance against time, and she found that her heart was strangely quiet. The shaking would come later. It always came later.

They did not come for her first. That was the cruelty of it, or perhaps the mercy — she could not decide which, standing there while Sergeant Doyle climbed the iron stair with his cap in his hands like a man arriving at a funeral he'd rather have missed.

"Mrs. Reyes." He would not quite meet her eyes. "Captain's compliments. You're confined to the light station. Effective now."

"And my friends?"

Doyle's jaw worked. "The Vidal woman, the blacksmith's widow, and the boy are being taken to the hall. Detained pending transport."

*Transport.* The word sat in the air between them like a stone dropped into still water, sending its rings outward — to the barge, to the coast, to whatever barracks or holding depot waited at the far end of the army's patience. Cyrus thought of Rolo's hands, cracked and ink-stained from three weeks of copying by lamplight, of Marta's spine straight as the lighthouse tower itself, of Ines who had not wept even at her husband's grave and would not weep now.

"Transport where?" she asked, and heard her own voice come out flat and cold, the voice she used on the radio wire when weather threatened shipping.

"I'm not told, ma'am."

"You're told everything. You just aren't told to say it."

Doyle flinched. He was young — younger than Rolo, nearly, though he carried himself older — and Cyrus saw in his face the particular misery of a decent man used as an instrument. She almost pitied him. Almost. Pity was a luxury the caged could not afford, and she understood suddenly, with terrible clarity, that this was what she was now: caged. Confined to a tower she had loved because it was hers alone, alone because no one had wanted her in the town below — and now that she finally belonged to them, belonging meant this. Watching through glass.

"The lamp," Doyle said, awkwardly, gesturing up toward the lantern room. "The Captain says — you're to keep the light. Regular schedule. You'll be supplied."

"Generous of him."

"Mrs. Reyes—" He stopped himself. Whatever he had meant to say, he swallowed it, put his cap back on, and went down the stairs with his boots ringing on the iron like small bells tolling.

From the gallery she watched them take her friends away.

Marta walked between two soldiers as though *they* were being escorted — chin high, hands folded, her shawl pinned at her throat with the same tarnished brass brooch she had worn every day of Cyrus's memory. At the bend in the cliff path she stopped, turned, and looked up at the tower. Cyrus raised her hand. Marta did not wave back. She simply looked — a long, level look that said *we knew this might come, we came anyway, do your work* — and then she turned and walked on, and the soldiers closed around her like water closing over a stone.

Ines went differently. Ines fought — not wildly, not uselessly, but with the blacksmith's widow's economy of motion, planting her feet so that it took two soldiers to move her, saying nothing, saying nothing, saying nothing, until one of them gripped her arm hard enough to leave marks and she made a single sound, low in her throat, that Cyrus would hear for years afterward. Rolo they took last, gently, almost kindly, and that was worst of all — Rolo looking back over his shoulder with his adolescent face stripped bare of all its armor, mouthing something Cyrus could not read across the distance, his copyist's fingers hooked into fists at his sides.

Then the path was empty, and the gulls were crying over the empty harbor, and Cyrus stood alone in the tower that had become her cell, and the shaking began.

* * *

She kept the light. That was the bitter architecture of Hargrove's sentence: the army needed the lamp, so the lamp keeper could not be taken, only walled in. She trimmed the wick at dusk. She wound the clockwork that turned the lens. She recorded wind speed and barometric pressure in the storm log with hands that remembered Tomás's shorthand even when her mind refused everything else, and she wrote *clear, moderate swell, no traffic* in a steady line while inside her chest something howled like weather.

The guilt of the free, she learned, is a specific poison. It does not burn like fear. It seeps. Every hour that Marta sat in a locked room below the town hall, Cyrus was climbing these stairs freely. Every hour that Rolo lay awake on some cot under guard, wondering if he'd broken his nerve forever, Cyrus slept in her own bed, in her own blankets, because the army required her well-rested enough to light a lamp. She ate bread from her own crock. She drank tea from her own chipped cup. Freedom tasted of ash and duty, and she could not set it down, because setting it down would mean the light going dark, and the light going dark — with the great storms gathering somewhere out past the shoals — would mean ships on the rocks, and whatever else she owed the world, she would not owe it drowned sailors.

So she worked. And working, she raged.

On the third night she caught herself talking to Tomás. Not praying — she had never managed prayer, not even in the worst winter — but talking, the way she used to talk across the kitchen table while he copied pages by lamplight and pretended not to be listening.

"You knew," she said to the dark window, where her reflection hung ghostly over the black water. "You copied that ledger knowing what it cost to make one. Knowing someone would pay someday. And you never told me. Two winters I lived in this tower with you gone, thinking I was keeping nothing but a light, and all the while—"

Her voice failed. She pressed her palm flat against the cold glass.

"All the while I was keeping *them*. All of them. Every name."

And that was the truth of it, she thought — the truth underneath the fury. Tomás had not betrayed her. He had trusted her with a inheritance she hadn't known she held, the way a father trusts a child with land the child cannot yet plow. She had spent three weeks learning to belong to this town, and the learning had cost Marta her liberty and Ines her dignity and Rolo his innocence, and now the record they had bled ink to finish sat somewhere in Hargrove's possession — read or unread, honored or burned, she did not know which.

That not-knowing was the cruelest confinement of all. They had walled her body into the station, but they had walled her mind into ignorance, and ignorance, for a woman who had spent a lifetime telling herself history happened elsewhere, was unbearable.

* * *

The soldier assigned to watch her was called Whitlock, and he was terrified of her, which she discovered on the second day and weaponized thereafter.

He was perhaps twenty, freckled, conscientious, and he stood at the foot of the tower each morning with written orders he consulted like scripture: the prisoner was permitted access to the lamp room, the oil store, the log room; she was not to communicate with persons outside the station; she was not to attempt departure; she was to be provided meals from army stores. He delivered her tray at noon and again at evening, set it down as though defusing ordnance, and retreated.

Cyrus learned that silence frightened him more than anger. When she spoke to him civilly — *thank you, Private; the wind will back around to the northwest by Thursday; tell your cook the tea is stewed* — he flushed and stammered. When she said nothing, watching him with the level gaze she had inherited from Marta Vidal without ever meaning to, he could barely meet her eyes.

"You can stop guarding me from me," she told him on the fourth day. "I'm not going anywhere. Where would I go? This is my home. That's rather the point of all this, isn't it?"

"Yes ma'am. Sorry ma'am." A pause. His conscience visibly wrestled him. "Mrs. Reyes — the old lady. Vidal. She's not eating."

The words went through her like a harpoon.

"Not eating?"

"She takes the food and puts it aside. Says she won't eat army bread while her town's ledger sits in a captain's desk." Whitlock's ears had gone red. "Sergeant thinks she's stubborn. Doctor's coming tomorrow."

Cyrus turned to the window so the boy wouldn't see her face. Marta Vidal, eighty-one years old, fasting in a requisitioned room, spending her body's last reserves as deliberately as she had spent everything else — as testimony, as argument, as proof that some things could not be requisitioned.

"She's not being stubborn, Private," Cyrus said quietly. "She's being consistent. Bring me my shawl tomorrow when you come. And tell her—" She stopped. *Tell her what? Hold on? We're winning? She wouldn't believe the second and would despise the first.*

"Tell her the light's burning regular," Cyrus said at last. "She'll know what it means."

Whitlock nodded solemnly, as though entrusted with state secrets, and went down the stairs, and Cyrus stood at the window a long time, watching the harbor where the transport barge lay at anchor like a patient animal, waiting for its cargo of names.

* * *

On the fifth day, rain came in from the southeast, soft and warm and wrong for the season — the leading edge of something larger, the barometer said, falling steadily since morning. Cyrus noted the pressure twice, then a third time, and watched the needle slide down the scale the way she had watched it slide before every great blow of her ten years here. Somewhere beyond the horizon, the second storm was assembling itself out of heat and water, indifferent to armies and ledgers and the small desperate justice of four people in a lighthouse.

It was raining when the boat came — a gig from the barge, pulling hard against the chop, carrying a single figure in a captain's greatcoat with the collar turned up. Cyrus watched from the gallery as Ellery Hargrove climbed the cliff path in the rain without an umbrella, without escort, without the folder of orders she had learned to dread. Just a man, wet and tired, coming to her door.

He knocked. No soldier had knocked before.

"Mrs. Reyes."

"Captain." She stood in the doorway and did not invite him in, and he seemed to understand that the threshold was a border he had forfeited.

"I've come about your friends."

"They're transported, then."

"No." Rain ran off the brim of his cap. "The hearing I requested — it's been granted. A board will sit in the town hall. Your people will testify. The record will be examined." He paused, and something moved behind his exhaustion — hope, maybe, or the fear of hoping. "Marta Vidal has stopped her fast. Ines Ford has threatened two corporals and a lieutenant, in ascending order of rank. The boy has asked, repeatedly, whether you're all right."

The lump in Cyrus's throat arrived without warning. She mastered it the way she mastered everything — late, and at cost.

"And me?"

"You're confined until the board rules. Those are my orders." He met her eyes then, straight on, and she saw that he had chosen to stand in the rain and deliver this in person rather than send a sergeant, and that the choice itself was a message. "But I am required to inform you that the light must be maintained, and that maintaining it requires certain... materials. Oil, wicks, glass. Which means someone must bring them to you."

"Does it."

"It does." From inside his coat he drew a small parcel wrapped in oilcloth — dry, despite the rain, tucked where a man keeps the thing he cares about most — and held it out. "Supplies."

She took it. It weighed nothing. Inside the wrapping she could feel paper, and within the paper, faintly, the ghost impression of type — a page, printed, sealed. One page. She didn't dare look at it in front of him.

"Inventory error," Hargrove said. "It happens, in wartime. Things go astray. Things come back." He turned up his collar. "The barometer's falling, Mrs. Reyes. Whatever else happens at that hearing — keep the light burning."

He went down the stairs into the rain, and she watched him until the mist took him, and then she shut the door and unwrapped the parcel with hands that shook worse than they had the day of the arrests.

One page. Sealed with the workshop's press, authenticated, dated. And written across the top in Marta's upright, unbreakable hand, meant for no board and no officer, meant only for her:

*We read the names aloud until the room stood. Keep the light, daughter. We will need it soon.*

Daughter.

Cyrus sat down on the stair with the page against her chest and the rain hammering the glass, and for the first time since the soldiers came, she wept — not for grief, but for the unbearable weight of being someone's, of belonging to people who were locked away for loving her, of being free in a cage with a lamp to tend and a storm coming and a word like *daughter* pressed into her hands like a coal that would not cool.

Outside, the barometer fell another two points. Out past the shoals, the sea was beginning to talk to itself in the low, continuous voice it used before great weather. Cyrus wiped her face, banked the stove, and climbed the stairs to trim the wick.

Whatever was coming — board or storm or both at once — the light would burn.
