## 37. Costs

The telegram came at noon, and by three o'clock the town knew.

Cyrus learned it the way she learned most things now — from the wind carrying voices up the cliff path, from Rolo running the switchback stairs two at a time with his cap in his fist and a look on his face she had come to dread, that particular mixture of news and apology that meant the world had moved again without asking anyone's permission.

"He's gone," Rolo said, breathless in the lamp room doorway. "Captain Hargrove. They put him on the morning transport. Sergeant Pellory has the command now. He posted it on the church door — orders from the district. Relieved of duty, pending inquiry." The boy swallowed. "They didn't even let him pack his books. Ines saw them carry out the crates."

Cyrus stood at the lens with her polishing cloth in her hand and did not move for a long moment. The great glass drum turned above her with its patient, indifferent whir, throwing its white blade across the water, and she thought: he read all night. She had known it without being told — had seen him at dawn on the thirty-first, gray-faced, the finished ledger under his arm like a man carrying something both precious and condemned — and she had understood that whatever came of it, he would not come out of that reading unchanged. A man could not pass four hundred nights of names through his hands and remain the officer who had arrived in June with his inventory lists and his courteous, exhausted efficiency.

She had thought, in the secret arithmetic she kept behind her ribs, that if the record entered the register, Hargrove would pay with his career but keep his self-respect. She had imagined him shipping out with the town's gratitude following him like a dog he would refuse to acknowledge. She had not imagined this: no hearing held, no farewell permitted, packed onto a transport between crates of requisitioned wool, his own signature on the register the last honest thing he would do in that uniform.

"They're saying," Rolo ventured, "that Brandt's testimony went up the chain wrong. That someone above him decided it was easier to remove the captain than to answer the questions."

"Someone above everyone always decides that," Cyrus said. "It's the one economy that never fails."

The boy looked at her, uncertain whether he was allowed to smile. She almost did herself. It was Marta's kind of bitter joke, and Marta was not there to make it, and the absence of it sat in the room like an empty chair.

* * *

She went down to the town the next morning, because the demolition was to resume, and because she had promised herself — in the same private ledger where she kept her arithmetic — that she would watch it. Not look away. That seemed to be the whole of what was asked of people now: to stand in the wind and witness what was done, so that somewhere, in some account that might never be opened, it would be written down that it had been seen.

The workshop stood at the end of the orchard road where the land flattened toward the harbor, stone-walled, slate-roofed, older than any living memory except Marta's, and Marta claimed to remember nothing about it except that her grandmother had set type in it during the cholera year, printing broadsheets of the dead so the families would know whom to mourn. Its press still stood inside under canvas — they had not dared move it yet; it weighed as much as a boat. Around it the soldiers had stacked the crated contents, and the crates were already going down to the barge, six men to a crate, swinging them on ropes with a rhythm that sounded, from a distance, like a slow heartbeat.

Pellory stood at the edge of the yard with his clipboard. He was younger than Hargrove and tried harder, and where Hargrove's courtesy had been exhaustion, Pellory's was fear. He nodded to Cyrus as she passed the rope barrier, and she nodded back, and neither of them said anything, and the nod contained everything either of them could safely say.

The demolition crew worked at the roof first. She watched the slates come off in courses, each man passing them down hand to hand, careful as harvesters, and thought how strange it was that destruction done properly looked so much like care. By afternoon the ridge beam was bare against the sky. By evening, when the light failed and the lamps were lit along the quay, the workshop stood open-topped to the weather like a ship with its deck planks torn away, and the stars came down into it and lay among the rafters.

Ines found her there. The blacksmith's widow had a way of arriving silently that Cyrus had never grown used to — a big woman made quiet by grief, moving through crowds like weather through wheat. She stood beside Cyrus at the fence and together they watched the soldiers bank their fires.

"Three copies," Ines said. It wasn't a question. It was a recitation, the way you repeated a thing to make it stay true.

"Three copies." Cyrus said it with her. "One goes to the provincial archive with the register clerk. One stays sealed in the church wall, behind the foundation stone of the north transept — Father Anselm mortared it in Tuesday night, and only he and I know which stone. And one—" She stopped.

"And one is wherever Marta keeps hers," Ines finished. "Which is nowhere you'll ever find unless she wants you to." She was quiet a moment. Below them a soldier dropped a slate and cursed, and the sound cracked flatly across the water. "Rolo says the register copy is already logged. Entered under Hargrove's signature before they took him. There's a clerk in the district office who owes him a favor, and the favor got called in. It can't be removed now without a written order, and nobody will write one, because writing things down is exactly what nobody wants anymore."

"Then it's done," Cyrus said. "That's what we wanted."

"Yes," Ines said, in the voice of a woman agreeing to a thing she did not believe. She pulled her shawl tighter. "My Tomás— my husband used to say you can't eat a document. I used to argue with him. Now I don't know which of us was right." She turned her head, and in the lamplight from the quay Cyrus saw that her face was wet and composed at once, the way it had been since the day the soldiers carried her husband's tools off the barge list and into army stores. "We won, Mrs. Reyes. Say it plainly and it sounds absurd. We won, and the press comes out tomorrow, and the roof comes down Thursday, and Marta sleeps in a room with no floor, and the boy asks me every night whether being right was worth what it cost, and I don't have an answer for him that isn't a lie in one direction or the other."

"No," Cyrus said. "You won't have one. I've stopped looking for mine."

* * *

Marta Vidal received the news of the demolition schedule standing up, which surprised no one. She had been released from detention the same week as the others — the charges dissolved in the general embarrassment that followed Brandt's testimony, the army preferring to unmake its mistakes quietly rather than try them publicly — and she had returned to her rooms above the chandlery with the same straight back she had carried into them, as though detention had been an errand she'd run for the cause.

Now she stood in her doorway with the eviction notice in her hand and read it twice, aloud, in the flat carrying voice she used for names.

"'Premises to be vacated by the fourteenth. Compensation assessed per Schedule C.'" She lowered the paper. "Schedule C. My grandmother set type in that building when the cholera came up the valley. My mother ground ink there. I learned my letters on its floor, from proofs they'd thrown out, upside down. And they've assessed me per Schedule C, which is the schedule for outbuildings." She folded the notice with great precision, corner to corner, until it was a small hard rectangle, and handed it to Cyrus. "Keep that. Put it in your next ledger. Let the future know what we were worth."

"Marta—"

"I'm not weeping, girl, don't look at me like that. Weeping is for the church wall copy and for Hargrove. This—" she tapped her own breastbone once, sharply, "—this is fuel. You burn it or you freeze. I intend to burn."

But that night, walking home past the darkened workshop, Cyrus heard the old woman's voice from an upper window, low and rhythmic, and realized she was speaking the names. Not reading them — she had no book in her hands, Cyrus could see that from the street — but reciting them, in order, from the beginning, the way you say a rosary. The Vidal claims. The Ferro claims. The orchard rows and workshop shares, generation after generation, spoken into the cold air over the stripped roof of the building that had been built to hold them. When Cyrus reached her own gate the voice was still going, steady as surf. It had not reached the letter F.

* * *

The neighbor came to the lighthouse on a Sunday, in his good coat.

Senhor Abel Cordeiro — Cyrus knew his name now, though for years he had been only the thin, stooped man who walked the cliff road with his head down — climbed the hundred and eight steps slowly, resting twice, and arrived at the gallery door breathing hard and holding his hat in both hands like a schoolboy summoned before a headmistress.

He was the one who had informed. Everyone knew it by now; the army had not troubled to keep it secret, and Cordeiro had not troubled to deny it. His family's claim had been denied in the original ledger — sixty years ago, before anyone living had been born — a dispute over a strip of orchard lost to a Vidal on a technicality of boundaries, and the resentment had gone down through the family like a root through clay, feeding quietly on everything above it. When he had seen the circle working by lamplight, seen his chance to strike at the ledger itself, he had walked to the barracks and given the names. He had gotten the demolition moved up ten days, and Hargrove relieved, and Marta detained, and he had gotten, in the end, precisely nothing, because the record survived anyway, in triplicate, and his family's name was still absent from it.

Cyrus let him stand in the doorway while the wind worried his coat tails. She did not ask him in. She was not sure afterward whether that was cruelty or simply the truth of the architecture — there was no room in a lighthouse for what he was asking for.

"Mrs. Reyes." He had to raise his voice against the wind, and the raising made it sound pleading. "I've come to say — I want you to know — I've asked the Father to announce it, but I wanted to say it to you myself, to the keeper of the light, because—" he faltered, started again. "What I did was wrong. I know what it nearly cost. I know what it cost the captain, God forgive me, I never meant— I only meant my father's field. Sixty years, Mrs. Reyes. My grandfather died calling that strip of orchard ours, and my father died the same, and I thought — when I saw you all with those pages — I thought, they'll write it down again, they'll write the lie again, and no Cordeiro will ever—" He stopped. The wind filled the pause. "I'm sorry. I'm asking your forgiveness. All of yours. Hers especially. Marta's."

Cyrus looked at him for a long time. He was smaller than she remembered, and older; informing had aged him faster than guilt usually managed. And she felt — this was the part she would turn over later, in the dark, at the top of the tower, and never fully resolve — she felt something that was not hatred and was not pity but was adjacent to both, the way a neighbor's fence is adjacent to your garden: close, fixed, and not yours to move.

"Senhor Cordeiro," she said, "I can't give you what you're asking."

His face did something complicated. "I didn't expect—"

"No. But hear me. It isn't mine to give. What you broke wasn't mine — it belonged to four hundred people, most of them dead, and the dead don't issue pardons. And it wasn't Marta's either, though she paid the most. It was the ledger's. You wronged a thing bigger than any of us, and forgiveness for a thing that size doesn't come out of one mouth at one door." She paused. The lens turned overhead, sweeping its blade across the sea, across him, across her. "But I'll tell you what I can do. Your grandfather's claim — the boundary dispute — it's in the original record. The denial is written there, and so is the reason, and so is the fact that the surveyor who drew the line was paid by the Vidals. It's all there. We copied it faithfully. Every word, including the injustice."

He stared at her. "You— included it?"

"We argued about it for two nights. Marta wanted it amended. Cleaned. She said a restored record should be a repaired record." Cyrus heard her own voice go quiet, the way it did when she spoke of things that mattered most. "I said no. Truth over comfort. If we corrected sixty years ago, we'd be doing to the Vidals' victory what the army did to our homes — deciding history by force instead of paper. So it's in there. The bribe, the lost strip, all of it. Someday, senhor — someday someone will read that page who isn't bound by anyone's grudge, and they'll see your family was robbed too. Robbed by us, by the town, by the very ledger we bled for. And maybe then your grandchildren get their strip of orchard back, legally, honestly, from people who owe it." She stepped back from the door. "But that mercy travels by a slower road than the one you're asking me for. I can't shorten it. Neither can you."

Cordeiro stood a moment longer, hat crushed in his hands. Whatever he had expected — absolution, fury, a scene — he had not expected a road. He opened his mouth, closed it, and finally bowed, a small stiff motion of a man setting down a weight he had carried so long he had forgotten its shape.

"Thank you," he said, and it came out ragged. "For telling me it's in there. That's — more than I—" He couldn't finish. He put on his hat and went down the stairs, slowly, resting twice, and Cyrus watched him from the gallery until he was a speck on the cliff road, and then until he was nothing at all.

She did not feel absolved. She suspected he didn't either. She suspected that was the honest outcome, and that honesty was the only currency left in the town that hadn't been requisitioned.

* * *

On Thursday the roof came down.

They did it with ropes and gravity, pulling the remaining trusses inward so the walls would stand — the army wanted the stone intact for a coastal battery, another irony Cyrus added to her collection — and the sound of it went through the town like a bell tolling wrong. She stood with Ines and Rolo at the orchard gate. Marta did not come. Later Cyrus learned why: the old woman had spent the morning at the church, sitting in the north transept with her hand flat against a foundation stone that looked, to anyone who didn't know, like every other foundation stone, saying nothing, asking nothing, keeping her vigil over the copy sleeping in the wall like a seed.

By Friday evening the workshop was a roofless shell with soldiers' tents in its yard, and the press had gone onto the barge, lashed under tarpaulin between cases of confiscated sewing machines. The barge was scheduled to sail with the next transport, ahead of the storms. The barometer in the lamp room had begun its long fall — Cyrus checked it hourly now, out of a keeper's habit that had lately become something closer to prayer — and the glass was lower than she liked, much lower, and falling faster.

Rolo found her at dusk on the gallery, wrapped in Tomás's old coat, watching the first clouds assemble themselves beyond the shoals in ranks that had nothing casual about them.

"The transport list went up," he said. "The barge sails Monday. And there's a navy cutter coming Sunday to take off the command staff — Pellory's staff, the new ones. Everyone's saying the storms are coming early this year. The fishermen are hauling everything above the tide line." He hesitated, then, carefully: "Mrs. Reyes — the list. The passenger list for the cutter."

"What about it?"

"It's got a name on it I didn't expect." He handed her the paper he'd copied in his round apprentice's hand, the ink still fresh enough to smudge. She read it by the last of the light, and the wind took the edge of the page and fluttered it, and she had to pin it against the rail with both hands to finish.

At the bottom of the list, under the officers being rotated out ahead of the weather, one line: *Capt. E. Hargrove (relieved), passage granted, disembarkation south.*

"He's coming back through," Rolo said. "Sunday. On the cutter. Just for the night — they anchor in the roads till the weather window opens."

Cyrus looked out at the gathering storm, at the barge lying heavy and low in the harbor with the town's press beneath its tarpaulin, at the sky assembling its argument in the west, and she felt the strange converging pressure of the week — departures and demolitions and a barometer falling like a verdict — and understood that Sunday night was going to matter, though she could not yet have said how, or to whom, or at what cost.

"Take the log down to Ines," she said. "Tell her to bring Marta. And Rolo—" he had already turned; she caught his sleeve. "Bring the good lantern. The brass one. If the weather holds even an hour, we may need to walk someone to the dock."

Below her, far out past the shoals, the first thunder spoke — not close, not yet, but patient, the way history was patient, the way it always arrived in the end exactly where you were standing.
