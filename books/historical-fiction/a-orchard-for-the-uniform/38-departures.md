## 38. Departures

The wind changed in the night, and Cyrus woke knowing it the way she knew her own heartbeat — not by hearing it, but by feeling it in the tower's stones. The barometer in the lamp room had fallen through the morning like a stone let down a well: 29.2 by noon, 28.9 by three. The glass was weeping condensation from the bottom up. Tomás had taught her that trick in their first year here, when she still asked him what every instrument meant and he still answered patiently, his hands cupped around hers on the brass.

*When the glass cries from below,* he'd said, *the sea is coming to collect.*

She stood at the gallery rail and watched the harbor do its arithmetic. The transport barge that had sat at anchor since autumn was taking on weight again — crates, mules, men moving in the slow, deliberate way of soldiers who have been told they are leaving before they've been told why. Down the hill, the town had come out onto its streets the way it always did when something arrived or departed, drawn to the waterline as if by tide. But this time there was no cheering and no jeering either. There was only watching, and the particular silence of people who have learned in one hard year exactly how much a government can take away and give back, and in what proportions.

The demolition crews had finished with the workshop eleven days ago. It was rubble now, a field of broken stone and splintered beams behind a rope fence, but the press itself had survived — Marta had seen to that, crating it under official orders with such flawless obedience that no one thought to question it — and it stood now in the rebuilt half of Ines's late husband's smithy, inked and ready, printing nothing yet but waiting like a heart between beats. The register bearing four hundred and eleven names sat in triplicate: one copy gone north with Hargrove's testimony, one locked in the town hall's new strongbox, and one kept here, wrapped in oilskin, in the place where Tomás had hidden the first pages. Cyrus liked the symmetry of that. History returning to its hiding place, made honest.

She counted the barges. Two for men, one for horses, one already riding low with equipment. And beyond them, far out where the gray water went to darker gray, the sky over the open sea had begun to organize itself into long, bruise-colored bands. The second storm. The papers Hargrove had shown her — he had shown her, which was its own strange courtesy — predicted landfall in thirty-six hours, perhaps less. The army intended to be gone in twenty-four.

They were cutting it close, and everyone on both sides of the rope fences knew it.

She went down the stairs two at a time, out into the wind, and began the day's work early: filling the oil reservoirs, checking the fog signal's compressed-air tanks, hauling the storm shutters down from the store loft. If the storm came while the last barges were still in the roads, there would be ships in her water when the sea came to collect them. She intended to have the light burning bright enough to shame the moon.

It did not occur to her until mid-afternoon, hauling the last can of oil up the spiral stair, that she was preparing the coast for the departure of the man who had taken everything and given most of it back — and that she could not decide which of those facts mattered more.

* * *

Hargrove spent his last full day in the town doing what he had done his first: inventorying.

He walked the perimeter of the camp with Sergeant Doyle at his elbow and a clipboard in his gloved hand, and he checked off crates the way he had once checked off orchards and workshops and families, though the pen felt different in his fingers now, heavier, as if the ink had learned something too. The men were subdued. Word had traveled — word always traveled — that Captain Ellery Hargrove had entered an unauthorized document into the official register, testified to its provenance, and named names above his own rank; that he was relieved of command pending inquiry; that the inquiry had somehow, inexplicably, confirmed everything and punished no one but him. They looked at him differently now. Not with contempt. With the careful, uncomfortable respect soldiers give a man who has done something brave and stupid in the same motion, because they cannot decide which it was, and neither, honestly, could he.

"The last of the workshop salvage is aboard," Doyle said. "The quartermaster corps signed for it."

"Brandt signed?"

"Brandt signed." Doyle hesitated. "He also asked whether you'd want to see him before embarkation, sir."

Hargrove considered the horizon, where the second storm was assembling itself out of cloud and distance. "Tell him yes. Tell him—" He stopped. What was there to tell Brandt? Thank you for your cowardice, which happened to be the correct shape? He had spent a career believing that procedure would bend toward justice if you pulled hard enough on the right levers, and the past month had taught him that sometimes justice came instead from a dying clerk's confession, a boy's deciphered shorthand, a woman handing you a book across a kitchen table and daring you to read it. "Tell him I'll find him on the dock."

"Yes, sir." Doyle paused again. "Begging pardon, sir, but the men wanted me to say — about the register. About the names. Some of them have family back home who'd have been on lists like these, if anyone had ever written them down. They're glad you did it. That's all."

Hargrove looked at his sergeant — twenty-two years old, freckled, competent, the kind of soldier the army consumed by the thousand without ever writing any of their names down either — and found, for the first time in months, that his throat was not entirely reliable.

"Thank you, Sergeant," he said. "That's all indeed."

At dusk he dismissed the escort at the foot of the headland path and climbed to the lighthouse alone. There was no regulation requiring the visit. There was, he suspected, very little left in his life that was required rather than chosen, and he found he preferred it that way.

* * *

Cyrus saw him coming up the path and had time to decide, three times, what she would say.

First she decided to be formal: the keeper receiving an officer, nothing more. Then she decided to be warm, because warmth cost nothing now that the uniforms were leaving and the rope fences were coming down. Then, watching him climb — hatless, coat unbuttoned against the rising wind, moving with the tired deliberation of a man who had stopped performing energy hours ago — she abandoned deciding altogether and simply put the kettle on.

He knocked at the door of the keeper's cottage, which amused her; he had knocked there a dozen times over the winter and never once entered, and tonight, on the last night, he waited on the step like a caller paying respects.

"Captain Hargrove," she said, opening wide. "Come in before the wind does."

"It's not captain anymore, as of Tuesday." He stepped inside, ducking slightly beneath the lintel as he always did, and stood dripping on her mat with the awkwardness of a large man in a small room. "Just Hargrove. Or Ellery, I suppose, if the army has finished pretending I don't exist."

"Cyrus, then," she said. "If we're trading ranks for names."

The kettle sang. She poured tea into the heavy brown cups she used for herself alone — the good china had gone into storage with everything else two winters ago, and she had never seen reason to dig it out for one — and set out the last of Marta's honey cake, which was dry at the edges and perfect in the middle. They sat at the table beneath the window where the first storm-lamp of evening was coming up gold over the water, and for a while they talked the way sailors and keepers talk: barometers, wind vectors, the shoal markers she'd need repositioned after the blow, the state of the fog signal's diaphragm. Safe water, all of it. The conversation of two people who know the sea is about to do something enormous and are politely declining to mention the other enormous thing standing between them.

It was Hargrove who finally set down his cup.

"I sail on the morning tide," he said. "If the tide holds. If it doesn't, we'll be here for the storm, which my executive officer regards as a fate worse than the inquiry." He smiled faintly. "I confess I'm not entirely opposed to it myself. There are worse things than being forced to watch your light run through a gale one more time."

"One more?" Cyrus raised an eyebrow. "You've watched it twice."

"I've watched it twice," he agreed, "and thought about it considerably more often than that, which I suspect is the more damning habit." He turned his cup slowly on its saucer. "I received my orders this morning. A coastal station in the north. Inventory work. Records, mostly. Someone upstairs has a sense of humor — they're sending the officer who couldn't stop reading other people's ledgers to count other people's stores for the next three years."

"They could have sent you somewhere with no records at all."

"They could have court-martialed me. The fact that they didn't is Brandt's doing, and I still haven't entirely forgiven him for it." His smile faded into something more complicated. "Three years of inventory, Cyrus. And I find I'm — not grateful, exactly. But willing. There's a version of me that would have burned that register six months ago because the orders said to burn it, and I'd like to find out what happened to him, and whether whatever replaced him survives a northern winter."

Cyrus looked at him across the table — at the gray coming into his temples that hadn't been there in autumn, at the hands that had inventoried her town and then, when it mattered, written her town back into existence — and understood that he was saying goodbye in the only dialect available to him, which was the language of duty examined at close range. She had buried a husband who talked that way. She knew how much could live underneath it and how little of it ever surfaced.

"Stand up," she said.

He blinked. "I beg your—"

"Stand up. There's something I've meant to give you since the night you read the register, and I kept finding reasons to wait, and I've run out of weather."

He stood. She went to the corner cupboard where she kept the things that were not tools and not food — Tomás's watch, her mother's thimble, the small archive of a marriage — and took down the oilskin packet, soft as cloth from years of handling. Inside, pressed between two slips of the storm-log paper Tomás had used for everything, lay a single blossom: five petals gone thin and papery, the color of old ivory, its stem flattened to a brown thread. An orchard blossom. From the trees that had fed three generations of names, from the spring some seven years ago when Tomás had walked down the hill with his coat pockets full of contraband springtime and tucked one flower into his copy packet, perhaps as a bookmark, perhaps as a prayer.

She held it out to Hargrove on her open palm.

"What Tomás hid behind the lead flashing, along with the pages," she said. "I found it the night I found everything else. I've been keeping it for — well. For someone who'd understand what it cost."

Hargrove did not reach for it at once. He looked at the small dead flower as though it might go off like a shell, and then, carefully, with both hands, the way one accepts a commission or a casualty list, he lifted it from her palm.

"An orchard blossom," he said quietly. "For the uniform."

"You know the story, then."

"Marta told it at the hearing. While the room stood up." He closed his hands around the flower with a gentleness she would not have believed of him a year ago. "Soldiers took the fruit and left nothing. So the town wrote everything down so it could never happen again. And then soldiers came, and took the ledger, and—" He shook his head. "And a soldier gave it back. I keep trying to make that balance in the accounts, Cyrus, and it doesn't. It never will. What my uniform did here can't be paid off by one man doing one decent thing at the end."

"No," Cyrus said. "But it can be carried. There's a difference. Debts you pay and forget. Carrying changes how you walk."

He laughed — a short, surprised sound, quickly gone. "You should write that down. You're better at it than I am."

"I intend to. I bought paper in the last supply shipment. Good paper." She looked at the window, at the dark coming down and the light she would soon go up to light. "Someone ought to start the next ledger, and it seems the town has decided that someone is me, since nobody objected loudly enough when Rolo announced it at the tavern as established fact."

"Rolo announced it?"

"With Marta's full endorsement and Ines's grudging approval. Apparently I've been appointed by acclamation. Democracy moves fast in a town of four hundred souls." She smiled, and found the smile was real. "There's a page reserved for the requisition year, Captain. All of it — the seizure, the copying, the storms, the hearing. The truth of it, including the parts that don't flatter anybody. Marta fought me on it for a week and then wrote the first entry herself. It begins: *In the autumn of the soldiers, we learned that our names were worth fighting for.*"

Hargrove stood very still. Outside, the wind had risen another notch, worrying the shutters, carrying the smell of the coming storm — salt and iodine and something colder underneath, the smell of the deep water coming inshore.

"When you write the part about the hearing," he said at last, "write the names of the townspeople. Write Marta speaking until the room stood. Write the boy deciphering the water-stained pages. Write Ines nursing a man she despised because mercy is an act of will." His voice roughened. "Don't write me. I was furniture. The record isn't about me."

"It isn't about you," Cyrus agreed. "But history keeps better books than the army does, Captain, and mine will note that when the record was handed to a soldier and he was dared to read it — he read it. Every page. Through the night. And then he bet his whole life on it." She met his eyes. "That goes in. You don't get to edit my ledger. Ask Marta."

Something moved through his face then — not quite a smile, not quite grief, some weather system of its own passing over — and he extended his hand, and she took it, and they stood shaking hands in her small kitchen like two people concluding an armistice that had quietly become something else, though neither of them named it, because naming it would have required a future neither of them possessed.

"Tenderness without promise," she thought, and was surprised to find she wasn't sad about it. Some things were complete at exactly the size they were.

"The tide is at 0640," he said, releasing her hand. "I'll come by before embarkation, if I may. To see the light lit."

"Come at dusk tomorrow instead. I light at dusk regardless of tides, and you can watch from the gallery where the view doesn't insult the lamp." She walked him to the door. "Bring the blossom. It wants salt air; it's been in a cupboard too long. Everything here comes back to the water eventually."

He paused on the step, the wind pulling at his coat, the last gray light catching the lines of his face.

"Cyrus Reyes," he said. "Keeper of the light. Keeper of the ledger." He touched his hat brim — the salute of a man who no longer outranked anyone, offered anyway. "Thank you for daring me."

"Thank you for reading," she said.

And then he was gone down the darkening path, one figure among the last figures of the departing army, and Cyrus stood in her doorway and watched until the headland took him, and the first drops of the second storm struck the stones around her feet like small, impatient fingertips.

* * *

Hargrove did not sleep well, which was customary now, but the quality of the sleeplessness was new. He lay in his bunk aboard the transport with the blossom's oilskin packet in his breast pocket, rising and falling with his breathing, and listened to the rigging begin its pre-storm complaints overhead, and thought about accounts.

All his life he had believed the world could be balanced — that if you entered every item honestly, debits and credits, takings and restorations, the columns would eventually agree, and agreement was peace. The town had broken that belief and then, in the same season, repaired it into something stranger and truer: the columns never fully agree, but the act of entering items honestly changes the enterer. He was evidence. He was going north to count stores for three years, and he would count them honestly, and somewhere in the counting he would become the man who deserved to have been handed a pressed orchard blossom by a woman who owed him nothing.

Near midnight he gave up on sleep, climbed to the deck, and looked ashore.

The lighthouse was burning. Of course it was burning — she would have lit it at dusk precisely, storm or no storm, army or no army — but seeing it was different from knowing it. The beam swept the low clouds in long, patient strokes, steady as a pulse, and beneath it the black town huddled with its few lanterns, and beyond it all the invisible orchards slept under the first spit of rain, holding their names in their roots.

*Four hundred and eleven names,* he thought. *And one pressed blossom. And one page reserved.*

The rain thickened. The barometer aboard read 28.6 and falling fast, faster than the forecasts promised, and the mate beside him muttered that they'd be lucky to clear the shoals by dawn. Hargrove touched the packet over his heart once, the way a man touches a talisman or a debt, and went below to write his last letter from this coast — to no one in particular, or perhaps to a keeper, or perhaps to the ledger itself — while offshore, unseen in the rain and the dark, the second great storm gathered its shoulders against the land.
