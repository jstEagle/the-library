## 39. The Second Storm

The barometer had been falling since noon, and by three o'clock the gulls had gone inland, riding the first gusts like scraps of paper flung from a child's hand.

Cyrus saw them go from the gallery of the lighthouse and felt the old familiar tightening in her chest — two winters of storms alone in the tower, Tomás's chair empty below her, the wind worrying at every seam of the stone as though it meant to pick the whole structure apart like a lock. But this time she did not climb down to sit alone with the log book. This time she went down the spiral stairs into a town that was waiting for her.

Marta Vidal stood at the foot of the lighthouse path with a lantern already lit, though the afternoon was barely gray. She was wrapped in a shawl that had once been red and was now the color of dried blood, and behind her came a straggle of others — Ines with her youngest on her hip, Rolo carrying a crate of candles against his chest like an infant, the Ferro brothers, the widow Serra, old Anselmo who had not left his house in a year except for this. They were walking toward the workshop, all of them, without having agreed aloud to do it, the way water finds the same channel year after year.

"The stone will hold," Marta said when Cyrus reached her. It was not quite a question and not quite a statement. Marta's voice had changed since the hearing; something in it had settled, the way sediment settles after a flood, leaving the water clearer and shallower than before. "It held in my grandmother's day. It held in the year of the great gale, when half the roofs in the lower town went into the harbor."

"And the army's barracks?" Cyrus asked. "The new timber?"

Marta smiled without warmth. "Timber is honest. It blows apart. Stone only leans."

They walked together through streets that were already beginning to sing — shutters banging, sign chains creaking, the loose tile on the cooper's roof clattering like distant applause. The requisition notices were still nailed to the church door, weathered to ghosts of themselves, and no one looked at them. The transport barge had taken the last of the soldiers away that morning, ahead of the weather, and Hargrove had gone with them, relieved of his command, standing at the rail of the cutter with his cap in his hands until the point of the headland took him. The town had watched him go the way one watches a tide turn: without celebration, without grief, with only the knowledge that something enormous had shifted beneath everything and could not be shifted back.

The workshop rose at the edge of the orchard land, squat and gray, its windows boarded since the demolition crews had stripped the roof lead but lost their nerve — or their orders — before the walls. Inside it smelled of oil and iron and forty years of ink. The press still stood in the corner under its tarpaulin, because there had never been anyone left to haul it away, and around it the townspeople were arranging themselves with the unselfconscious order of people who have done a thing many times before: blankets along the north wall, the children nearest the interior stones, the lanterns hung on hooks that had held harnesses and aprons in another life.

Cyrus set her own lantern on the workbench beside the press. And beside it, wrapped in oilcloth, she placed the ledger.

* * *

By full dark the wind had found its voice.

It came off the sea in long sustained howls that dropped suddenly to a pressure that made ears ache and then rose again, and between the gusts came the sound everyone dreaded and no one named: the groan of trees out in the orchard rows, roots shifting in soil gone soft as bread. Rain arrived not in drops but in sheets, hurled horizontally against the boarded windows so that the whole wall seemed to breathe.

Rolo sat cross-legged near the press with the youngest Ferro boy asleep against his shoulder, and he listened to the storm the way he used to listen to the sergeants' voices across the parade ground — cataloguing, measuring, telling himself stories about it. A year ago he would have been watching the ships. He would have been imagining himself aboard one, a uniform two sizes too large, the whole glittering machinery of the world carrying him away from orchard mud and ink stains. Now he sat in a demolished workshop with ink permanently black in the creases of his knuckles and felt, for the first time in his life, that he was exactly where he was supposed to be, and that the feeling terrified him more than any storm.

"Tell us," said the widow Serra, when the wind fell enough for speech. She was ninety if she was a day, wrapped in three blankets, her eyes bright as a bird's. "Tell us what we're sitting inside of."

Marta got up. It cost her something; everyone saw it cost her something, her hip and her years and the long autumn of detentions and hearings written now into the way she moved. She crossed to the workbench and unwrapped the oilcloth, and in the lantern light the new ledger lay open — the completed transcription, sealed page by page with the workshop's press, authenticated in triplicate, one copy already sent inland to the registry, one in the town hall strongbox, and this one here, which Marta had insisted would live among the people it belonged to or nowhere.

"Page one," Marta said, and began to read.

She read the way she had read at the hearing, each name given its full weight, each name set down like a stone in a wall. *Almeida, three shares, east orchard.* *Ferro, two shares, the smithy and the strip behind it.* *Serra, one share and the drying sheds.* Names of families that had scattered, names of families dead to the last soul, names of the living sitting right here in the lamplight hearing themselves pronounced back into existence.

Ines listened with her daughter asleep against her breast and her eyes closed. She had refused Brandt's presence at the hearing and she refused to speak of him now, though he had testified, though he had saved them all with his cowardice dressed up as courage, though he had been marched onto the same transport as the soldiers with his accounts in disarray and his career in pieces. Mercy, she had told Cyrus once, was an act of will and not of feeling, and she performed hers daily by not spitting when his name came up. Tonight she kept her eyes closed and let the names wash over her — her husband's name among them, the blacksmith Tomás Reyes had copied out by lantern light two winters before he died, and Ines felt the man's hand somehow present in the room, patient and exact, writing her family into permanence while she slept unknowing a street away.

Outside, something large gave way with a crack like a rifle shot, and the children stirred, and the adults went still, listening. A tree. Everyone thought: a tree, an orchard tree, one of the old ones. No one said it. Marta turned the page.

* * *

Past midnight the storm reached into the workshop itself.

It came through the gap where the roof lead had been stripped, a fistful of cold air that found the nearest lantern and put it out, and then a second, and for a moment they were in darkness absolute except for one flame guttering near the press, shadows lunging up the walls, the rain suddenly loud in the room itself, drumming on the tarped press, pooling on stone.

"Hold the light!" Marta shouted, and Rolo was already moving, cupping his hands around the last lantern, and Ines thrust the sleeping girl into Cyrus's arms and went up onto the crates with a hammer and the roll of canvas they'd brought for exactly this, nailing canvas over the gap while the wind tried to take the hammer out of her hand, shouting words nobody could hear over the roar, working by feel and fury until the drumming dulled to a muffle and the second lantern could be relit.

She climbed down soaked to the skin, laughing — actually laughing, wild-eyed, alive — and her mother-in-law wrapped her in a blanket and Ines said, breathless, "Let it try again. I've been through worse than weather."

"You haven't," said the widow Serra mildly. "But you will, and you'll win anyway."

And Cyrus, holding the child, understood that this was the thing she had watched from the lighthouse for six years and never once touched: not the storm, but the people inside the storm, the way they bent and did not break, the way terror passed through them and came out the other side as joke and blanket and song. History happened here. It had always happened here. She had simply been reading the wrong rooms.

They relit the lanterns. Someone produced a bottle that had been hidden from the army's inventory list with a patriotism everyone chose not to examine. Marta resumed reading where she had left off, as if the storm were merely an interruption in more important business, and perhaps it was.

* * *

Toward dawn the wind turned.

Everyone heard it — the shift in the howl, the note dropping lower, the pressure changing against the east wall — and the older faces went grim, because a turning storm is a cornered animal, and corners are where the damage lives. The rain became hail, brief and vicious, rattling off the boards like gravel thrown by a giant hand. Then came the quiet hour, the false lull, when the storm draws breath for its last argument, and in that hush Cyrus stood and went to the door.

She opened it on a world erased. The orchard rows were shapes, some of them, and absences where other shapes had been. The harbor was a white chaos with the light tower's beam sweeping through it steady and indifferent, doing its ancient work. And down the coast road, half-buried in wrack, she could see the roof of the ferry shed peeled back like the lid of a tin.

"We'll want everyone counting heads at first light," she said to the room. "Roofs, boats, walls, animals. And the orchard." Her voice caught on the word and she let it. "We'll want to walk the orchard together."

Behind her, Marta closed the ledger with both hands and looked around at the faces — the Serras and Ferros and Vidals, the widows and orphans and apprentices, the neighbor who had betrayed them and been forgiven nothing and had come anyway because there was nowhere else to be, and Rolo with the baby finally handed off, standing straighter than a boy should stand after the night he'd had.

"When the wind drops," Marta said, "we plant."

No one asked what, in a drowned orchard, in a stripped town, in spring still months away. Everyone knew. You planted what you had. You planted names, and trees, and the next ledger, and you planted them in the same ground the storm had just tried to take, because the ground was yours — written, sealed, pressed in ink and witnessed — and because the alternative had been tried once, by men in uniforms, and it had failed.

The wind gathered itself outside for the final hours of its rage. Inside, the lanterns burned low and were fed. And Cyrus Reyes, keeper of the light, sat down among her people to wait for morning, and found she was not waiting alone.
