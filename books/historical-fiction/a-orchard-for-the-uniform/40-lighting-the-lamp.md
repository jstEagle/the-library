## 40. Lighting the Lamp

The wick had been soaking in oil since noon, and still Cyrus trimmed it the way Tomás had taught her — two small cuts, angled toward the flame's future, never straight across. Her hands knew the work without her. That was the strange mercy of a decade at this station: the body learned first, and the heart followed slowly behind, like a cart horse that refuses to be hurried.

Spring had come up the coast the way it always did — not arriving so much as seeping, salt air turning soft, gorse going gold along the cliff path, the orchards below the town breaking into blossom all at once as though someone had tipped a basket of white cloth over the hillsides. From the gallery rail she could see the whole valley of them: the Almeida rows, the Vidal rows, the long terraces where the Ferro and Bastida and Quintela families had set their roots into soil their great-grandparents had cleared with hand and mule. Blossom season. The season the ledger had been made to protect.

She looked down at the workshop.

It stood again. Not as it had been — the fire of the demolition crews' first careless week had taken the eastern wall, and no amount of will could unburn timber — but standing, roofed in new slate that caught the evening light like fish scales, its surviving press housed inside on a stone plinth Marta had insisted be laid "properly, the way a foundation for a church would be laid." Ines had forged the new beams herself at the rebuilt forge, and Rolo had hauled every one of them up the hill on a handcart, sixteen years old and proud as a rooster about it. The town had needed somewhere to gather through the winter storms, and they had gathered there, in the shell of the building the army had meant to erase, reading the authenticated record by lantern light while the wind tried the shutters like a beggar at a door.

And now the press sat in its finished room, and twice a month the town came to print. Births. Marriages. Deaths. New claims, old claims amended, the neighbor Salgado's family finally granted their strip of hillside orchard — entered by unanimous vote, after a winter of silence between him and Marta that neither of them had called forgiveness but both of them had honored. The register bore the town's names now. All of them. In ink that Hargrove himself had carried into the official register before witnesses, in a town hall full of people who had stood when Marta began to read aloud and did not sit down again until she finished.

Cyrus had been there. She remembered most clearly not the reading but the sound the room made afterward — not cheering, exactly. Something quieter and heavier, like a wave that has traveled a very long way and finally lets itself down against stone.

*History happens here*, she thought, watching the light go amber over the orchards. *To us.*

She had spent nine years believing the opposite. She had come to this coast as a bride from a place so far inland it might as well have been another country, married to a lighthouse keeper whose own mother never quite learned to pronounce her name right, and she had folded her life small around the lamp room and the weather logs and the ache of Tomás's absence after the fever took him. She had watched the town from above the way one watches a play through glass — everything vivid, nothing belonging to her. Even after the requisition, even after the packets behind the lead flashing, some part of her had believed she was only borrowing her way into their story: the outsider who happened to hold the key.

But you cannot transcribe four hundred families' names night after night by lamplight without the ink getting into your blood. You cannot teach a widow shorthand from a dead man's marginalia, or bluff a sergeant with your keeper's authority while oilskin packets rustle under your floorboards, or run a light through the worst storm of the century to save a man who stole from everyone you'd come to love — you cannot do these things and remain a stranger. Somewhere in those months, between the copying and the forging and the arrests and the hearing, the glass had broken, and the play had turned out to be her own house.

She heard boots on the iron stair — the familiar double rhythm, quick then deliberate, a boy trying to walk like a keeper before he'd grown into his own stride.

"Mrs. Reyes." Rolo emerged onto the gallery with the logbook under his arm and the wind pulling at his hair. He was taller than last autumn; his voice had dropped into a register that occasionally surprised him mid-sentence. He wore the brass-buttoned jacket Ines had altered twice already. "Barometer's steady. Wind's northwest, light, going lighter. Visibility good."

"You checked the ventilators?"

"And the oil filters, and I cleaned the lens lower panes — there were gull marks." He said it with the gravity of a boy reporting a siege. He had been her apprentice since the spring equinox, formally signed into the station book in an entry Cyrus had written herself: *Rolo Quintela, apprentice keeper, bound to learn the light.* His mother had wept. Rolo had pretended not to notice, which was also the custom of the coast.

"Gull marks," Cyrus repeated solemnly. "The oldest enemy."

"The sergeant used to say soldiers were worse." A shadow crossed his face when he mentioned the army — not fear anymore, something more complicated, the residue of a boy who had once idolized the uniforms and then watched what the uniforms did, and had chosen, at fourteen, in a lamplit tower, which side of history he'd stand on. He still spoke of Hargrove sometimes, carefully, the way one speaks of a good teacher who died young. The captain's letters came twice a year now, postmarked from postings farther and farther north, each one shorter, each one asking after the lamp and the press and whether Marta still kept bees. Cyrus answered them. It seemed the least one could do for a man who had ended his career so that a town could keep its name.

"Log's current?" she asked.

"Through yesterday. Your entries for today are blank." He held it out. "I didn't want to write them without you."

There it was again — that thing the town did now, that thing she had stopped being astonished by sometime around midwinter. They waited for her. They brought her the blank spaces and trusted her to fill them. Once she had been the woman who delivered lamp oil to the occupying officer and hated how reasonable he seemed; now she was the keeper whose hand the apprentices wouldn't preempt.

"Then we'll fill them together," she said. "But first —"

She nodded toward the west, where the sun had come down to the water and was laying a road of copper across the swell. Dusk. The hour.

Rolo grinned and went down ahead of her, taking the stairs two at a time because he was seventeen and the stairs were steep and he had not yet learned, as she had, to let the tower set the pace. She followed more slowly, one hand on the cold iron rail, past the lamp room door with its new hinges (the old ones had been pried loose during the inspection, that terrible dawn when the soldiers surrounded the station and she had walked out with the finished record held against her chest like a shield she dared them to read), down into the service room where the smell of brass polish and machine oil lived permanently in the wood.

* * *

Lighting the lamp was not, strictly speaking, necessary anymore. The commission had installed a vaporized-oil mantle the previous autumn, automatic, reliable, the kind of apparatus Tomás had read about in the journals and longed for and distrusted in equal measure. But the old wick lamp remained, cleaned and mounted beside its modern successor, and Cyrus lit it every dusk anyway, and the commission's inspector — a young man who had arrived expecting the famous defiant keeper to be difficult and had instead been fed sardines and sent back to his steamer with cut flowers from the Vidal rows — had written in his report that the practice was "sentimental but harmless, and the station is immaculate."

Sentimental. Let them write it. She struck the match and touched it to the soaked wick and watched the flame take, blue then gold, and felt the small ancient satisfaction of it, older than any bureaucracy: *I make the dark retreat, here, in this place, tonight.*

Tomás had hidden pages of a stolen ledger behind the flashing of this very room because he believed paper could outlast power. He had been dead two winters when she found them, and he had been right, and she had finished his work, and the town stood unbought and unbossed beneath her window, and its children were learning to read their own names out of a book their grandmothers had defended with forgery and stubbornness and one very audacious afternoon of printing seals while guards carried boxes.

"Ines is coming by after supper," Rolo said, appearing in the doorway with the day's oil tallies. "She wants to show you the stamp die she's cutting — the new one, for births. She says the old seal's impression is getting worn at the edges."

"Worn at the edges," Cyrus murmured. Everything wore at the edges. That was what the ledger was *for* — not to stop wearing, but to keep the record true while the world rubbed itself smooth. "Tell her to bring it. And tell her—" She paused, smiling. "Tell her I'll want to test it on a real entry. There's news coming, isn't there?"

Rolo went pink to the ears, which confirmed everything. Ines's daughter and the Vasquez boy, and a wedding at midsummer, and a birth to follow, God willing, by the following spring — and every one of those events would pass through this tower's orbit, logged and stamped and witnessed, entered into the record by hands that included, now, a boy who had once idolized the wrong uniforms and a woman who had once believed she belonged nowhere at all.

"There's news," Rolo admitted, grinning. "Marta wants to be the one to tell you properly, though. She says she has seniority."

"Marta has seniority over the tide," Cyrus agreed. "Go log the wind."

He went, whistling, and she climbed back up to the gallery alone.

* * *

The first stars were out over the water. Below, lamps were kindling in the town windows, one by one, the way they always had — but she found she could not look at those lights anymore without seeing the ledger underneath them, the way a musician hears the score beneath the song. Every window was a name. Every name had been fought for. Old Marta Vidal, seventy-nine this spring and still terrifying, still keeping bees, still saying grace over the press every time it printed a page — Marta had told her once, in the tavern, in the days when defiance was hardening like tar, that the ledger existed because soldiers had once stripped the orchards and left nothing, and the founders had sworn *never again in ignorance*. Never again unwritten. Never again erased.

The uniform was gone now. The orchard remained. An orchard for the uniform — the debt reversed, the fruit staying home.

Cyrus leaned on the rail and looked east, toward the shoals where the supply ship had gone down, where she had saved Quartermaster Brandt's miserable life and nursed him in the town he'd helped strip until his cowardice chose the safer crime and became, by accident, testimony. Brandt farmed now, of all things, three valleys inland, on land nobody wanted, sending awkward parcels of dried apples every autumn that no one quite refused. Mercy as an act of will, not feeling — Ines still did not speak to him. But she accepted the apples, dried them further, and baked them into pies for the schoolchildren, and if that was not absolution it was perhaps something more useful.

The sea breathed below her, long and slow, the same sea that had taken and given and taken again. Tomorrow the *Almeida* boats would go out for the spring herring; next week the blossom would peak and the town would hold its festival, the first since the war, with dancing in the workshop yard and Marta judging the cider with the severity of a magistrate. History was not elsewhere. It was in the cider press and the christening blanket and the worn edges of a seal die, and it was happening now, to them, to her, and it would need keeping.

That was the thought that had been ripening in her all winter, quietly, the way fruit ripens in a closed room: the record must not merely be preserved. It must continue. Names end; ledgers shouldn't.

She went down to the service room, took a fresh quire of the good rag paper Ines's cousin traded up from the south, and uncapped the ink. On the first page, in her clearest hand, she wrote:

*A Ledger of the Light. Begun in the spring of the year the workshop was rebuilt. Containing herein all that the people of this coast do, and suffer, and make, so that no storm, no soldier, no forgetting may take it from them. Kept by Cyrus Reyes, keeper of the lamp — who was a stranger, and is not.*

Below it, the first entry: *Rolo Quintela, apprentice, aged seventeen, lit the ventilator check alone for the first time this day, and did it well.*

Her pen hovered. There would be hundreds of pages. Weddings and wrecks and blossom festivals and quarrels and reconciliations; the news Rolo blushed over; whatever Marta would announce with seniority; the whole unrolling ordinary miracle of a town that had refused to be deleted. She would write all of it. She would teach Rolo the shorthand, and Rolo would teach whoever came after him, and someday some keeper not yet born would trim a wick above these same waters and open this book and know exactly whose hands had held the light before hers.

From below came Rolo's whistle, and the creak of the stairs, and — faintly, up the cliff path — the sound of voices: Ines and Marta together, arguing cheerfully about something, carrying between them the warm smell of supper bread and, unmistakably, a jar of last autumn's cider for the occasion.

Cyrus dipped her pen. Outside, the great lamp turned its steady wheel of gold across the darkening water, and the blossom on the hillsides glowed white as paper, and the first line of the next hundred years waited under her hand.

She began to write.
