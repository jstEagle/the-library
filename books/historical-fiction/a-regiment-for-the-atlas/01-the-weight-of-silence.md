## 1. The Weight of Silence

There is a particular sound a lie makes when it has been rehearsed enough times: nothing at all. A fresh lie clatters like a dropped type case. An old one sits in the mouth the way a well-worn piece of furniture sits in a room — nobody notices it, and everybody would notice if it were gone.

By February of 1848 I had been keeping our lies dusted and polished for eleven years, and I could say *my mother died in '37* with the smooth ease of a woman remarking on weather.

That morning the cold had come down the river valley like an auditor, finding every gap. The frost had climbed the inside of the shop windows in ferns, and my breath hung over the type case in little clouds that made me think — as it always made me think — that even our exhalations were being counted by somebody.

I was sorting twelve-point bodoni into the job case, which is work for hands and no work at all for the mind, which is precisely why I do it before opening. The letters click against each other, small metal bones. *N*, *o*, *r*, *t*, *h* — I was setting a handbill for Halvorsen's dry goods, NORTH OF THE RIVER, BEST PRICES IN VELLMARK, and I confess I set the word *north* with something like tenderness. North meant across the ice to Denny. South meant the capital, the Ministry, the census men. Our whole city sorted itself that way in my head, like type into cases: everything either north or south, safe or counted.

The shop itself — Kavanagh & Sons, though there were never any sons on the sign and now, technically, only one — sat at the mouth of Ink Lane where it met Cordwainer Street, close enough to the river that in thaw we could smell it, all mud and fish and old rope. In February you smelled nothing but your own frozen nose. The front room held the jobbing press, the paper stock, the counter where customers leaned and haggled. The back room held —

Well. The back room held whatever the person asking believed it held.

That was the trick of it, and I had learned it from Papa without either of us ever saying it aloud: you don't hide a room. You let people furnish it themselves. To the tax assessor it was storage for paper stock, damp-prone, not worth inspecting. To the neighbors it was where Denny slept, because Denny was the nephew — not son, nephew, we had settled on that years ago and I had repeated the fiction so often at market and chapel and the fishmonger's stall that I sometimes caught myself believing it, which I suppose is what a good lie costs you, small pieces of your own memory paid out like coin until the purse runs thin.

And to anyone who asked after the woman on the deed — the woman whose name had been struck from every record in this city eleven years ago as neatly as a compositor lifting a line of type off the stone — well. She had died. 1837. Fever. Very sad. My father doesn't speak of it.

Three lies, then, kept in working order like presses: the closed room, the nephew, the dead mother. I wound them every morning the way Papa wound the flywheel, and if they ran rough I oiled them with repetition.

* * *

Papa came down at half past seven, coughing the way he always did in winter, a deep wet rattle he disguised as clearing his throat. He'd been doing it for two years. He did it louder when Dr. Pell attended, quieter when he thought I was listening, which told me everything about how the arithmetic of his health was going.

"You've got the Halvorsen bill set?" he asked, shrugging into the leather apron that had gone stiff as bark at the shoulders.

"Set and locked." I tapped the forme with a knuckle. "He wants it up by Thursday or he's taking his business to Moller."

"Moller can't set a straight line with a ruler." Papa peered at the case, then at me. "You've been up since when?"

"Five."

"Freya."

"Papa." I matched his tone exactly, which is a daughter's privilege and a father's punishment. "If I sleep past five I dream about the press running, and if I dream about the press running I wake up thinking someone's found it, and then I'm useless anyway. Five is cheaper."

He grunted, which in my father's language was agreement wrapped in disapproval, a very economical dialect. He went to the stove, fed it a knot of pine, and stood warming his hands while the shop came alive around us — the kettle beginning its low mutter, the frost ferns going glassy at their edges where the heat reached them, the smell of ink and oil and lampblack rising like the shop drawing its own breath.

It occurred to me, watching him, that love in our house had never once been spoken plainly. It was expressed in other currencies. It was Papa checking the back-room shutter twice before bed. It was Mama — I allow myself the word in the privacy of my own skull, where no census taker can reach — it was Mama teaching me to set type left-handed so I could keep working if the right was ever hurt. It was me lying about her death eleven years running, fluently, courteously, at every christening and funeral and government form, and calling that loyalty instead of grief.

In the Kavanagh family, we did not say *I love you*. We said *the shutter's latched*. We said *count the formes before you bank the fire*. We said *she died in '37, fever, very sad*. Same sentence. Same weight. You just had to know how to read the face of it.

* * *

Denny came in through the coal cellar at eight, smelling of river and woodsmoke and, faintly, of other people's fear, which has a smell — I'd know it anywhere, a kind of sour sweetness like apples going over. He stamped the snow off his boots too late and too loud, the way he does everything, twenty-two years old and built like a barge.

"Anything?" Papa asked, without looking up from the ledger. That was the whole conversation, the entire coded dispatch between father and son, and I have often thought a Ministry man could stand in our kitchen for a year and learn nothing, because our secrets traveled in words so small they were practically invisible.

"Quiet." Denny poured coffee that could have caulked a boat. "Ice is holding. Two more nights, maybe three, and then she's done for the season."

"Then we're done," Papa said.

"Then we're done," Denny agreed, and drank, and grinned at me over the rim with that grin of his that made lying to magistrates feel like a family game rather than a felony.

I should explain what Denny does at night, except that explaining it, even here, even in my own head, makes my chest go tight, so I will say it sideways, the way we say everything: my brother works nights on the river. He takes deliveries across the ice. Some of the deliveries are crates. Some are people. The people pay nothing, because the people are running from the same men who would pay us to report them, and there is a symmetry in that which Denny finds funny and I find merely necessary.

The nephew sleeps in the back room. The nephew is very industrious.

"Freya." Denny snapped his fingers. "You're a thousand miles south. Halvorsen's bill?"

"It's done. You're a thousand miles north. What's in the cellar?"

"Coal," he said solemnly, "and my second-best boots, which I am drying, because a man's boots are his livelihood."

His second-best boots were caked to the knee with grey river slush, and there was a long scratch down one side that no coal cellar had made. I said nothing. Saying nothing is also a skill in this family, and I am, if I may boast, the finest practitioner in Vellmark.

* * *

At nine I opened the shutters and the morning came in white and hard. Cordwainer Street was doing its usual business — the knacker's boy dragging his cart, Mrs. Ostrander beating her door mat with the fury of a woman at war with dust, the lamplighter making his rounds extinguishing what the sun had already finished. Ordinary. I watched it all with the ordinary hunger of the watchful, cataloguing faces, and I want to be honest about what that vigilance costs, because nobody tells you.

Weary. That's the word. Not frightened — frightened is for moments, and moments pass. Vigilance is a chronic condition, like bad teeth. You carry it to market. You carry it to chapel. You carry it into your sleep and wake with its arm around your neck. There is no morning in my adult life that has begun with anything other than the question: *is today the day?*

Most days the answer is no, and you make the coffee, and you set the type, and you tell the small lies, and you love your family in the currency of concealment, and it holds.

But some mornings the light comes through the frost ferns a certain way, gold and thin, and you think: *how much longer can a thing like this hold?* Ice holds too. Until the night it goes out all at once with a sound like the world cracking its knuckles.

* * *

Midmorning brought Mr. Quist from the assessor's office, round and apologetic, wanting to discuss the paper duty. This is the sort of visitor I prefer: predictable, greedy in a documented way, interested in figures rather than faces.

"Business seems brisk," Quist observed, squinting at the press, at the stacks of Halvorsen's paper, at me. "You pay duties like a manufactory, Miss Kavanagh, and yet I see no journeymen."

"My brother and I manage," I said, smiling the smile I keep for officials, which reaches the mouth and stops at the border like a foreign traveler without papers. "My father's hands aren't what they were."

"And the back premises? The assessor noted last year it's kept shut."

"Damp," I said. "Paper stock spoils. We use it for storage and regret it daily. If the Crown would care to subsidize a proper roof, we'd gladly open it to inspection." I said this with such cheerful reasonableness that Quist actually laughed, and wrote *storage, dilapidated* in his book, and left ten minutes poorer in suspicion and none the wiser.

Afterward I stood at the counter with my heart doing a slow heavy knock against my ribs, and I rehearsed it again, silently, the way a pianist runs a difficult passage: *damp, storage, nephew, died in '37.* All keys in working order. All lies dusted.

But my hands, when I looked down at them, had a fine shadow of black in the whorls of the fingerprints, ink worked so deep into the skin that soap surrendered and winter made permanent. A printer's hands. And I thought — standing there in the honest cold light of the front window, with the whole street able to see me — I thought: *they can read these hands like a page.*

* * *

The afternoon, the stranger came.

I want to record that I noticed him from the moment he turned onto Cordwainer Street, because I like to believe my vigilance is perfect, but the truth is I noticed him the way you notice weather — a figure among figures, dark coat, unhurried, carrying a leather satchel flat against his hip. It was only when he stopped opposite the shop and consulted a paper that my spine went to attention, because nobody consults a paper outside a print shop unless the paper says *print shop*.

He crossed. He opened the door. The bell over it gave its small cheap ring — I have often considered removing that bell; Papa refuses, says customers need announcing; I say customers are exactly who I don't need announced — and he stepped in, bringing a breath of the outside cold with him, stamping once on the mat like a polite wolf.

"Census," he said. "Elias Thorn, Ministry of Enumeration." He showed a card without my asking, which I noted; guilty men show papers before you ask, honest ones wait. Or perhaps it's the reverse. I was still deciding. "A routine verification of households in the print district. It won't take a moment of your time."

"Time is money, Mr. Thorn, and money is paper, and paper is my trade. Come to the counter."

He came to the counter. Up close he was younger than his coat suggested — mid-twenties, maybe, with a clerk's pallor and a pair of eyes that didn't quite match his manner. The manner was mildness itself. The eyes were doing sums. I know those eyes; I wear them myself some days.

"The household at this address," he read from his ledger, "Kavanagh. Head of household Bram Kavanagh, printer. One son. And—" his finger paused on the page, a pause so slight a less practiced liar would have missed it, "—one daughter. Freya."

"That's me."

"No wife listed currently."

"She died." Smooth as oiled rollers. "1837. Fever."

"Fever," he repeated, writing. Then, mildly, terribly: "There's a woman named on the original deed. From 1828. The transfer records list a joint signature."

The kettle began to whistle in the back, and I have never been so grateful to boiling water in my life. I let the silence stretch just long enough to be natural — a widow's grief needs a beat of silence, that's part of the composition — and then I said, "The deed is in my father's name and has been these eleven years. If the Ministry finds the old paperwork confusing, I'm sure that's a question for the land registry, not for a print shop." I smiled. "More tea?"

"I haven't—"

"We're having tea. It's February. Sit down before you freeze, Mr. Thorn; dead men make poor census takers."

And he sat. Which surprised me. They usually don't sit. They take their answers and go, satisfied with the shape of the lies because the shape matches the forms. But this one sat, and accepted tea, and asked questions about the press — how many impressions a day, what we printed, whether trade was good — pleasant questions, harmless-seeming questions, each one landing softly and each one, I realized halfway through the cup, aimed like a pick at a different lock. Journeymen? No journeymen. Duties like a manufactory? Business is good. Back room? Storage, damp, a tragedy of a roof. Nephew?

"He lodges here," I said. "Helps my father. Family keeps costs down."

"Family," Thorn said, and wrote something, and the nib scratched in the quiet of the shop, and I thought: *that is the sound. That is the small sound a big trouble makes.*

He stayed twenty minutes. He thanked me for the tea. At the door he paused with his hand on the frame and looked back — at the press, at the type racks, at the shuttered door of the back room, lingering there perhaps half a second longer than a man interested in roofs ought to linger — and then at me.

"Thank you for your time, Miss Kavanagh. The Ministry appreciates accurate counts."

"We aim to be accurately boring, Mr. Thorn."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth — not quite a smile, more the memory of one — and then he was gone, and the bell rang, and the cold rushed in behind him and settled over the counter like a judgment.

* * *

I waited until his footsteps faded up Cordwainer Street. I counted to a hundred, which felt insufficient, so I counted to three hundred. Then I went to the back-room door and put my palm flat against it, the way you'd check a horse for fever.

Papa was already there. Of course he was; he'd heard the bell stop ringing twice, the way old soldiers hear gunfire twice. His face had gone the color of the paper stock, and his breathing had that rattle in it again, louder now, unhidden.

"He asked about her," Papa said. Not a question.

"He asked about the deed. 1828. Joint signature."

Papa closed his eye — both eyes, but the phrase belongs to him alone, the way he closes himself, shuts down, becomes unreadable as a locked forme. "Eleven years," he said. "They struck her name from the rolls, the registers, the parish books. Struck it like a misprint. And now a clerk comes asking after the ghost."

"What do we do?"

He was quiet a long time. Through the wall I could hear the river, far under the ice, doing its slow secret work, the water moving beneath the stillness the way truth moves beneath a family.

"Now," Papa said finally, "we tell you things. Things you should have been told at sixteen, and weren't, because telling you made them real. Come downstairs, Freya. Bring the lamp."

And because I am my father's daughter — because concealment is my mother tongue and obedience to it is my inheritance — I picked up the lamp, and I followed him toward the back room, toward the shutters and the locks and whatever truth had been sleeping behind them all these years wearing the plain face of storage and damp.

The lamp flame bent as we descended, throwing our shadows tall and wavering against the stone, and I remember thinking, with a clarity that felt like cold water: *the census man counted wrong today. But he'll be back to count again.*
