## 2. One Question Too Many

The census man came on a Tuesday, which was fitting, because Tuesdays were the day nothing ever went right in our shop.

I had spent the morning at the stone with a case of long primer, sorting type for a job lot of funeral invitations — Mrs. Aldous's mother, dead at eighty-one, wanted *Beloved and Now at Rest* in the finest script we could manage — when the bell above the door gave its small, cheap jangle. I knew before I looked up. There is a particular quality to the silence that follows that bell when the person entering it has come to count you rather than to buy from you. It is the silence of arithmetic.

He stood just inside the threshold, hat in hand, snow melting off his shoulders in patient grey beads. A young man, younger than I'd expected from the letters the Ministry sent ahead — they always made their men sound like schoolmasters gone slightly to seed. This one had the look of a clerk who'd been fed on ledgers since boyhood: thin, neat, with ink already ghosting the side of his right hand and eyes the colour of dishwater that had learned to pay attention.

"Miss Kavanagh?" He said it as though testing the weight of it. "Elias Thorn. Census of households and manufactories, District Four." He produced a leather folio from under his coat with the tenderness other men reserve for love letters. "I wonder if I might trouble you for a quarter hour."

"Trouble," I said, "is free here. Everything else costs."

He smiled at that — a real smile, briefly, before he folded it away like a receipt — and I thought, *there is a man who has been laughed at politely his whole life and has decided to be flattered by it.* I have since revised that opinion considerably, but on that Tuesday morning it served me well enough, because a man who enjoys being laughed at will let you laugh him toward the door.

"Father isn't in," I said, before he could ask. "He's at the chandler's, settling an account."

It was not true. My father was forty feet away behind two doors and a false wall, breathing through lungs that sounded like paper being crumpled slowly, and if the census man had any sense of smell he might have caught the lye and lampblack on the air. But Bram Kavanagh did not exist to Ministry men on days ending in *y*, and certainly not on this one.

"A quarter hour," Thorn said again, opening the folio on the counter as though it had been granted to him by statute. Which, I suppose, it had. "Name of the head of household?"

"You'll find it written there already. You people are very thorough."

"The previous enumeration was eleven years ago, miss. Households change."

"Ours doesn't. We're terribly dull."

He wrote something. I watched him write it — *dull* would not have fit in the column, so it must have been my name, or my tone, or some private shorthand for the girl who answers questions with questions. The nib scratched with a sound like a mouse in a wall, and I found myself disliking the sound more than I can reasonably explain.

***

Here is what you must understand about counting, and why we feared it more than soldiers.

Soldiers come and go. A patrol marches down Copper Lane, boots ringing on the cobbles, and everyone shuts their shutters and counts their spoons afterward, and in a week the memory is as thin as winter broth. But a census is different. A census does not pass. It *stays*. Every name entered into that folio becomes a small brass tack hammered into your life, and the Ministry keeps its tacks forever, and one day — not today, perhaps, but some grey administrative tomorrow — a clerk in the capital will run his finger down a column and notice that a tack is missing. That a woman counted in 1828 is not counted in 1839. That a household pays rates like a manufactory but employs no journeymen. That the arithmetic does not close.

Our family lived inside that unclosed arithmetic like mice living in the walls of a granary. Comfortable, so long as no one brought a lantern.

"Occupation of the head of household?" said Thorn.

"Printer."

"And how many hands does he employ?"

"Hands?" I let myself look baffled, which was easy, because I had been rehearsing bafflement at the washstand since six o'clock. "You mean apprentices? Denny helps him. My brother. He's family, though, so I don't know if he counts."

"Family counts," Thorn said quietly, and wrote it, and I felt the words go into the ledger like a fishhook going into a thumb.

***

He was polite. I want to be fair about that, because fairness was not something I extended to him that day and it cost me later, in ways I could not then imagine. Elias Thorn was polite the way ice is smooth — no malice in it, only a surface you could not get a grip on. He asked his questions in the order the folio demanded, never skipping, never doubling back, and each answer he received he weighed on some internal scale I couldn't see, nodding at whatever I gave him whether it rang true or hollow.

Number of persons sleeping in the household last night. Three. (True. Unless you counted the fugitive who'd left over the back fence at four in the morning, whom Denny had walked down to the tannery culvert through snow up to his knees.)

Ages and conditions. Father, fifty-four, printer, infirm of chest. Brother, nineteen, labourer. Myself, twenty-three, compositor.

"Compositor," he repeated, glancing at my fingers. Any printer's daughter knows her own hands give her away — the ink in the knuckle-creases that no pumice ever fully lifts, the callus on the middle finger where the composing stick bites. "You set type yourself."

"I set everything myself. We're economical."

"Mm." His eye drifted along the shop with the unhurried appetite of a man reading a room the way other men read documents. And our room, I will admit, was a document worth reading, if you knew the language. Two presses where a shop our size ought properly to have one — the old Columbian out front for honest work, and the back room, shuttered, locked, and explained away as storage for wet paper. No journeymen at the cases, though the tax rolls listed us under *manufactory, printing, three hands or fewer* at a rate that assumed them. A stove burning hotter than thrift required. The smell of ink worked hard rather than ink kept tidy.

"No journeymen," he said now, conversationally, as though remarking on weather. "Unusual, in a shop with two presses. Compositors are cheap in Vellmark since the mill closures. Half the printers on this street keep two or three."

"My father prefers to work with family. He says journeymen argue about leading."

"He may be right. Arguing about leading has ruined many a good page." Another entry. The mouse-in-the-wall scratch. "And yet the rates you pay assume—"

"The rates assume nothing. The assessor came, looked about, charged us what he liked. You'd have to take that up with the assessor."

"I may do exactly that."

There it was. Not a threat — worse than a threat, because a threat you can defy and a promise you can dodge, but a *procedure*, mild as milk, following its course like water finding a low place. I put on my best dull face and poured him tea I hadn't offered, because a cup in a man's hands is a question he cannot write down.

***

He drank the tea standing, which I noted. Honest men sit. Men with warrants pending stand near doors.

It was going tolerably well — tolerably meaning he was halfway through his folio and I had told him nothing but truths bent into useful shapes — when he turned a page and stopped. I saw him stop. I had been watching his hands all morning the way a card player watches hands, and I saw the nib hover, and dip, and hover again.

"There's a name on the deed," he said. "For this property. Dated March 1828. Bram Kavanagh and—" he read it carefully, syllable by syllable, giving it the full ceremony of the record, "—Sigrun Kavanagh. His wife?"

The shop went very quiet. Even the stove seemed to hold its fire closer.

"Yes," I said.

"Is she enumerated in this household?"

"She died."

"I'm sorry. When?"

"In—"

And here is the thing I have thought about every day since, the hinge of that whole grey Tuesday: I did not know what year to say. Because Sigrun Kavanagh had died twice, in a manner of speaking. Once in the flesh, when the fever took her in the famine winter, and once on paper, when my father — with help from a man at the parish registry whose debts we printed away — struck her from every record that could be reached. No grave marker with a full name. No entry in the parish book. Nothing a Ministry clerk could follow back to a woman born across the water, in a country the Crown preferred not to name in its own documents, whose brothers were men the Crown preferred not to think about. My mother had been erased the way you erase a misprint: thoroughly, professionally, with the whole force of our trade behind it.

So I had two deaths to choose between, and no time to choose, and the census man's pen waited above the line like a small silver guillotine.

"Some years ago," I said. "I was a child. I don't recall the date precisely. You'd want the parish register."

"I did consult the parish register." He said it gently. Gently! That was the worst of it — he delivered the blow the way a doctor delivers bad news, with sympathy, having already decided the news was coming regardless. "There's no burial recorded for a Sigrun Kavanagh. In any year. In any parish in the district."

"Well," I said, "then she was buried somewhere else."

"Where?"

"I was a child, Mr. Thorn. Children are not consulted about burials."

"No," he agreed. "They rarely are." He wrote for longer than the answer deserved. I could feel each word landing on the page — *no record, subject evasive, recommends* — whatever the *recommends* would turn out to be. Verification. Re-inspection. A letter upward to someone whose job was to be interested in exactly this.

My father says fear is a compositor's emotion. You feel it in the hands first. My hands were steady on the teapot; I made sure of it; I had trained them on a thousand tight deadlines to be steady when the forme was loose and the press was waiting. But inside, something was setting itself, letter by letter, in a face I didn't much like: *this man will come back.*

***

At the door he paused, folio under his arm, snow beginning again outside with the fat, indifferent flakes of late February.

"Thank you for the tea, Miss Kavanagh."

"We charge for it, usually."

"I'd be happy to pay." He actually fumbled for coins, which was so absurdly earnest that I almost laughed, and laughing would have been fatal, so I waved him off.

"Consider it an investment. In accurate record-keeping."

Something moved behind the dishwater eyes — not quite amusement, not quite apology. "That's the whole of my work, miss. Accurate records. Most people find it dull. Some find it sinister. It's neither. It's just... names, in columns. Everyone wants to be remembered somewhere official. Very few want to be *counted*." He settled his hat. "I've never entirely understood the difference."

"Then you've never been counted wrong," I said.

He looked at me for a moment — a long moment, three heartbeats maybe, in which the snow fell past the doorway and the stove ticked and somewhere behind the false wall my father's breath caught and released — and then he inclined his head, precisely, like a man closing a ledger, and went out into the white street, and the bell jangled behind him with its cheap little voice, and I stood in the quiet hating how well I could still hear his boots on the cobbles after the door was shut.

***

Denny came up from the cellar at noon smelling of lamp oil and river, which meant he'd been at the boat, which meant I'd have to burn the oil rag and open the window in February, which meant the coal bill, which meant everything, always, costing. He took one look at me and said, "Who died?"

"The census man found Mother."

I hadn't meant to say it that way. What I meant was: the census man found the gap where Mother used to be, the hole in the record shaped exactly like her, and holes are harder to hide than women — a woman can wear a shawl and keep to the kitchen, but a hole in a register sits there in black and white asking to be filled. But what came out of my mouth was the other thing, the truer thing, and Denny went still with the oil rag in his fist like a man hearing ice crack under someone else's weight.

"All right," he said finally. "All right. Does Da know?"

"Not yet."

"Then you get to tell him." He grinned, because Denny's answer to dread has always been to hand it to someone else with a bow on it, and went back down the stairs whistling, the wretch, and I stood alone in the shop among the formes and the funeral invitations and the smell of ink, and I did what I always do with a thing too large to carry whole.

I broke it into type.

*He will come back.* That was one line, set solid. *He has found the gap.* Second line. *Father must move the pamphlet stock.* Third. *The deed — the deed can't be altered, deeds are witnessed — but the parish record, the parish record was already handled once, it could be—*

No. Stop. That was the trap, and I saw it even then, young as I was: the moment we began forging records to cover the gap, we confirmed that the gap mattered. An empty column can be carelessness. A corrected column is a confession with a wax seal.

What it needed — what it had needed for twenty years, and what my father's generation had never dared — was not a better lie. It needed the truth to become *useless*. A woman erased from the registers is a mystery; a mystery invites inquiry. But a woman whose story is known — plainly, widely, in ten thousand hands, in print that cannot be unprinted — a woman like that is not a gap in a ledger anymore. She's a fact. And facts, unlike families, cannot be deported.

I didn't know it yet, not fully, not the way I would know it by spring. But I stood there that afternoon with the funeral invitations drying and the snow coming down on Copper Lane, and I felt the shape of it the way you feel weather coming in an old scar.

Then I hung up my apron, went through the two doors and the false wall, and sat down beside my father to tell him that the counting had begun — and that this time, I intended to answer it.

Behind me, out in the grey street, I would have sworn I could still hear boots. Patient ones. Coming back.
