## 3. The Man Who Counts

There is a particular smell to the Ministry annex on Foundry Lane: cold tallow, wet wool, and the faint vinegar tang of the iron-gall ink we are required to use because it cannot be erased. I have come to think of that smell as the smell of truth, which tells you something about how long I have worked for the Ministry of Enumeration and Records — long enough to confuse permanence with honesty.

I filed my day's tallings at four o'clock, as I do every day. Forty-one households enumerated in the print district. Nine hundred and sixty-two souls counted, aged, classified by trade and condition. Eleven entries flagged for irregularity, which is the Ministry's word for anything that does not sit square in its column. A widow with three lodgers and one declared income. A chandler whose apprentice list exceeds his chimney count. And the Kavanagh establishment on Quill Lane, which sat in my ledger like a stone in a boot.

I entered it the same as the others. *Bram Kavanagh, master printer, forty-nine. Freya Kavanagh, daughter, twenty-three, occupation: compositor.* Then I closed the book, tied it with the regulation string, and handed it across the counter to Clerk Osgood, who weighs each ledger on a brass scale before accepting it, as though a thin day's work might be lighter than a full one.

And then I went home to my room above the chandlery, lit my stove, ate my bread and cheese, and thought about nothing but the girl who had answered every question I asked with a question of her own.

*How many persons sleep in this house?* — *How many do you intend to write down?*

That is not how subjects speak to takers. Subjects stammer. Subjects apologize. Subjects produce their papers with the fumbling eagerness of children presenting sums to a schoolmaster. This one had looked at me over her composing stick — she had been sorting type when I came in, I remember, her fingers moving through the little lead letters like a pianist's through keys, not even pausing — and had measured me the way a printer measures a page. For margins.

I had written down four. Father, daughter, son, and the nephew said to sleep in the back room. Four was what the household admitted to. Whether it was what the household contained was another matter entirely, and I found, sitting there in my rented room with the stove ticking, that the discrepancy bothered me the way a loose tooth bothers a tongue. Not painfully. Persistently.

Here is what you must understand about me, since no one else will bother: I am good at my work. Not diligent in the way the Ministry means it — diligence they can train into any clerk with a ruler and a schedule — but good at it in the way that cannot be taught. I notice things. I noticed that Kavanagh's shop pays manufactory rates on its water and coal, taxes assessed for an operation twice the size its visible floor could hold. I noticed that the shop employs no journeymen, which in Vellmark's print district is like a bakery employing no bakers. Every other shop on Quill Lane rings with hired hands from six in the morning; Kavanagh's doors were shut against the cold and the only sound inside was one woman and her type case. And I noticed the woman on the deed from 1828 — *Maren Kavanagh, née Voss* — whose name appears in the land registry and appears nowhere else. Not in the parish rolls. Not in the census of '38, where Bram Kavanagh enumerated his own household as himself, one child, and one male servant. Not anywhere. Twenty years, and the registry says a woman owned that building while every other record in the city says no such woman ever existed.

When I asked after her, Freya Kavanagh did not blush or flounder. She said, "The deed is older than the census. Perhaps your office should reconcile itself." And then she offered me tea, which was either courtesy or a way of watching me drink something she'd poured, and I honestly could not tell which, and I took the tea anyway, and I have been turning over the meaning of that for two days.

* * *

On Friday I was summoned up two flights to the office of Supervisor Grett, a man built like a filing cabinet and about as warm, whose function in life is to stand at the top of the Ministry's hierarchy of small men and remind them there is always a smaller drawer.

"Thorn," he said, without looking up from his desk. "Your print district numbers are clean."

"Thank you, sir."

"That wasn't praise." He set down his pen and finally regarded me with the expression of a man appraising livestock. "Clean numbers are unremarkable numbers. Unremarkable numbers are forgettable numbers. Do you know what the Ministry remembers, Thorn?"

"I would assume accuracy, sir."

Grett smiled, which on him resembled a crack in plaster. "The Ministry remembers irregularity. A household that counts wrong is worth ten that count right. There is a man in Harrowgate — you won't know him, Pellering — who found eleven families in one parish claiming exemptions they hadn't earned. Eleven. He was promoted within the year. Has a house now, near the capital. A garden." He let the word *garden* sit in the air between us like bait on a hook, then reeled it back in. "The point being, the Ministry rewards takers who find irregular households. It has no use at all for takers who find nothing."

"I found eleven irregularities this week, sir."

"You filed eleven irregularities." He tapped my ledger, which lay on his desk with the string already cut. "Filing is clerical. Finding is a vocation." He opened the ledger to my Kavanagh entry and ran one blunt finger down the columns. "A printer with no journeymen. Taxes for a manufactory. A back room nobody will describe." The finger stopped. "You wrote *nephew*. Who told you nephew?"

"The household."

"Mm. And did you see the nephew?"

"He was asleep. In the back room."

"Were you shown the back room?"

"It was described to me, sir. Through a door."

Grett closed the ledger and pushed it aside, and for a moment he looked almost gentle, which frightened me more than anything else in that office. "Kavanagh," he said. "Old Bram Kavanagh. There was a file on that shop once, years ago. Before your time. It went up to the capital and never came back, which usually means somebody important decided it wasn't a file anymore." He picked up his pen again, dismissing me. "Re-verify the household if you like. Takers have discretion in the field. Just make sure whatever you find is *worth finding*, Thorn. Ambition is permitted in this service. Wasted effort is not."

I stood there a moment longer than I should have. "Sir — the woman on the deed. Maren Kavanagh. Is there a record—"

"There's a registry," Grett said to his paperwork. "Registries are not records. Good afternoon."

I want to be honest with whoever reads this, even if honesty is only for me: walking down those two flights of stairs, what I felt was not duty. Duty is a flat thing, a thing I carry because it is assigned to me. What I felt was warmer than that and uglier, too. Grett had said *garden*, and some small starving part of me — the part that grew up in a foundling ward counting other people's arithmetic, the part that knows exactly what it is to be a number nobody valued — had heard *garden* and wanted it. Wanted the promotion, the house, the being-seen. I told myself on the stairs that I wanted the truth about the Kavanaghs because truth was my trade. I mostly believed it. That is the trick of ambition: it dresses itself in your best principles and goes out wearing them.

* * *

Saturday was my half-day, and I spent it, as I have spent most of my half-days these two years, walking. Vellmark is a city made for walking — a city stitched together by bridges, all of them toll-free since the riots of '41, which is the only generous law the Crown ever passed here and only because the soldiers couldn't collect tolls and shoot at the same time. I walked down along the river, where the ice has gone grey and rotten at the edges and the ferrymen stand around their moored boats smoking and arguing about whether the break-up will come early this year. Old men say the river is a calendar you can read if you're patient. The ice groans at night lately, a sound like a door opening very slowly in a very large house, and everyone pretends not to hear it.

Then I turned up into the print district, off-duty, out of uniform, in the plain coat I keep for exactly such walks, and I am aware of what that makes me, so we needn't pretend otherwise. I told myself I was simply passing through. I told myself the route to nowhere passes through everywhere. But my feet knew Quill Lane the way a dog knows the road to a butcher's shop, and I found myself standing across the street from Kavanagh's with my collar up and my breath steaming, looking at a shut shop on a working morning.

It was shut. That was the thing. Half past nine on a Saturday, and every other business on the lane had its doors open and its boys out sweeping steps and its smells rolling into the street — tallow and sawdust and frying onions — and Kavanagh's windows were shuttered to the sills and dark. Except.

Except that as I stood there, doing a poor job of being a man with nowhere to be, I heard it. Faintly. Underneath the street noise. A rhythm. *Clack-clack-clack — pause — clack-clack.* The sound of a press running. I know that sound; I grew up three streets from a newspaper works and fell asleep to it for the first twelve years of my life, the way other children fall asleep to surf. And a press runs for one reason: because someone is printing something, in quantity, behind closed shutters, on a day when the shop claims to be closed.

Now, a reasonable man would have walked on. A reasonable man would have noted the hour in his head and mentioned it Monday, properly, through channels, with his name attached to the observation. That is what the Ministry trains us to do: convert everything into paper, paper into procedure, procedure into safety. Observation without documentation is gossip. Documentation without authorization is insubordination. The whole apparatus exists to make sure that no single man ever knows anything alone.

But I stood there in the cold with the press-rhythm ticking under the street noise like a second heartbeat, and I thought about a girl measuring me for margins, and I thought about a woman who existed on a deed and nowhere else on earth, and I thought about Grett saying *a garden near the capital*, and I understood that I had already crossed some line, because I did not want to report what I heard. I wanted to *know* it. Reporting is giving the knowing away. Knowing is keeping. And keeping, I was beginning to suspect, was what that household did better than anyone in Vellmark.

The shutters moved. Just slightly — one slat tilting, a finger's width of darkness appearing at the sill — and behind it, I would have sworn, a shape withdrew. Watching me watch. Or perhaps not; perhaps it was only the wind, only the old wood settling, only my own guilty imagination dressing itself up as evidence. That is the occupational hazard of my trade: once you start counting, everything looks like it wants to be counted, including things that are merely ordinary. Most of the world's secrets, I tell my apprentices — I have no apprentices; I tell myself, in the evenings, in the voice I imagine an apprentice would need — most of the world's secrets are just ordinary things seen by a man who has decided to see.

I crossed the street. I don't fully recall deciding to; I recall the middle of the crossing, the slush sucking at my boots, and then my knuckles on the shop door, rapping the way a neighbor raps, casual, unhurried. Off-duty. Out of uniform. A man paying a social call, which no one in the history of the Ministry has ever done.

The press-sound stopped. Of course it stopped — the whole lane could have heard it stop, the silence arriving so completely that I felt I'd interrupted something intimate. Footsteps inside. A pause at the door long enough for whoever stood behind it to decide, deliberately, what face to wear.

The bolt drew back.

Freya Kavanagh looked out at me, and I watched her take in the plain coat, the bare head, the absence of ledger and satchel, and arrive — quickly, coolly, the arithmetic nearly visible behind her eyes — at the correct sum. She didn't ask why I'd come. She stepped back from the doorway and left it open behind her, which was either an invitation or a demonstration that she had nothing to hide, and both of us knew it might be either, and neither of us said a word about it.

"Mr. Thorn," she said. "You've caught us at our cleaning."

Behind her, somewhere past the dim mouth of the corridor, something metallic settled into stillness — a forme laid down, perhaps, or a press lever eased gently home. The air inside smelled of ink and coal smoke and, beneath both, something I couldn't yet name. Warmth, maybe. Or the memory of warmth. She waited, one hand on the door, composed as a line of set type, and I stood on the threshold understanding perfectly well that I was walking into a household that had already counted me — weighed me, priced me, set me in a column of its own — and that the only question left was which column.

"Re-verification," I said, and heard how thin it sounded, and stepped inside anyway.
