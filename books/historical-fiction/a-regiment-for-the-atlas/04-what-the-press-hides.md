## 4. What the Press Hides

There is a particular way a father looks at you when he has decided to ruin your ignorance — I learned it that February evening, though I did not yet know that was what the look meant. He was standing in the doorway of the type loft with his sleeves rolled and his forearms grey to the elbow, watching me set a wedding announcement for the Vellmark Courier's Tuesday edition, and he looked at me the way a man looks at a bridge before he decides whether to cross it.

"Freya," he said.

"Bram," I said back, because we had been on surname terms since I was eleven and it made him laugh, or used to.

He didn't laugh now. He came into the loft and stood at my shoulder, reading my galley upside down, which he could do faster than I could read it right side up, and for a while neither of us said anything. The stove ticked. Somewhere below, the shop cat — a creature of no discernible loyalty named Gutenberg, on the grounds that every print house should have one — knocked something off a shelf purely to hear it land.

"The census man," Bram said finally. "Thorn."

"What about him?"

"He'll come back."

I kept composing. A compositor learns to keep her hands moving while her mind does other work; it is the whole trade, really. "You don't know that."

"I know men like that. I've printed enough of them." He picked up a spare quoin key from the tray and turned it over, not because he needed it but because his hands wanted employment too. "They come once to look at you. They come twice to look through you."

"And when they do?"

"When they do," Bram said, "you'll need to know what you're protecting. You can't guard a door you don't know exists."

I set the last line of the announcement — *Mr. Aldous Petrie to Miss Constance Hale, joyfully* — and then I stopped, because my hands had begun to shake, and shaking hands drop type, and dropped type is a small sin that costs an hour.

"You mean the back room," I said.

I should explain what I knew by then, so you can weigh how much of this was surprise and how much was arithmetic. I knew our shop paid taxes as a manufactory and employed no journeymen. I knew the back room behind the paper store was kept locked, and that the lock was new, and that the key lived on a nail inside Bram's shirt collar where no census taker would ever think to look. I knew Denny slept there, or claimed to, and that Denny's comings and goings had the particular quality of weather — frequent, unpredictable, and never discussed indoors. I knew my mother's name had been scraped out of the parish register like a scar from a table, and that no one, in nineteen years, had ever said her name aloud in this house except once, when I was nine, and Bram had gone so quiet afterward that I learned never to ask again.

So no. It was not surprise. It was confirmation, which is worse, because confirmation means you have known all along and have been complicit in your own deception.

"Yes," Bram said. "The back room."

* * *

We went down together. That was the part I remember most clearly afterward — not what was behind the door, but the going down, the two of us crossing the shop floor past the big press with its iron arms folded like a sleeping ox, past the paper store with its reams stacked to the ceiling, and Bram stopping at the locked door and putting his hand flat against it for a moment, the way you'd steady yourself against a horse before mounting.

"Once I open this," he said, "it doesn't close again. Not for you. Whatever happens after, you know it forever, and knowing can't be unset." He glanced back at me. In the lamplight his face was all planes and shadows, older than fifty-eight, older than the century some days. "I'd spare you if I could. Your mother would have told me to spare you. But sparing you only works if nobody ever asks questions, and a man asked one today about a woman on a deed."

"Mama," I said. Just the word. Nineteen years of silence and then one syllable, and it didn't shatter anything, which felt like a betrayal of how carefully we'd guarded it.

Bram nodded, slow, like a man accepting terms. Then he took the key off its nail inside his collar and opened the door.

The smell hit me first. Ink, yes, we had ink everywhere in the shop, but this was a different ink, a cheaper, sharper ink, the ink of haste, and under it lamp oil and cold iron and something else — sweat, maybe, or fear, though fear has no smell until you know to name it. The lamp was already lit. Of course it was; Denny would be back before midnight and liked to find things ready.

The second press stood in the middle of the room, smaller than ours, a hand press of respectable age with a cracked platen someone had shimmed with folded tin. A forme sat locked in the chase beside it, and I read it upside down without meaning to, because that is the trade, because I am my father's daughter:

*TO THE WORKING MEN OF VELLMARK AND THE RIVER TOWNS: THEY COUNT YOU BECAUSE YOU ARE WORTH COUNTING. THEY TAX YOU BECAUSE YOU PRODUCE. WHEN THE HARVEST FAILS, WILL THEY FEED WHAT THEY HAVE NUMBERED?*

Below it, no signature. There never is.

"They go out Tuesdays and Fridays," Bram said. His voice had gone quiet and even, the voice he used for corrections of fact. "Two hundred a run, sometimes three. Marta Voss distributes them through the laundries — nobody looks twice at a woman carrying linens. Jozsi sets most of them now; his hands are quicker than mine and his eyes are younger. And Denny—"

"Denny runs them," I said. "Across the ice."

Bram looked at me.

"The south bank towns get them same night as we do," I said. "Someone's ferrying. And Denny comes home at strange hours smelling of river and lies about cards." I heard my own voice and didn't recognize it. It sounded like his. "Papa. Did you think I was stupid?"

"I thought you were safe," he said. "It's different, and I was wrong about both."

* * *

Here is the famine year, since Bram gave it to me that night whole and unsoftened, sitting on a paper bale with the forme still locked beside him because he wouldn't unlock it — he said I might as well see the thing as hear it.

In 1832 the Crown conducted a special enumeration of the river districts. Not a census exactly, Bram said; censuses are vanity. This was an audit. They counted mouths per household, bushels per cellar, and they compared the two columns, and wherever the second column ran short of the first, they adjusted. Not with soldiers. Soldiers are expensive. With ledgers. Grain requisitions timed to arrive after the granaries were already emptied by tax collection. Ferry licenses revoked along the north bank so relief couldn't cross. And in Vellmark itself, a list — an actual list, drawn up by a ministry clerk, of households whose numbers exceeded their means — and the surplus, Bram said, using the word the clerk had used, the surplus was allowed to resolve itself.

My mother's family was on the list. Her father kept a bakehouse. Four daughters and a widowed aunt, mouths without means. When the bread riots came that spring, it wasn't the riot that killed them — it was that when the soldiers finally came to restore order, they had the list, and the list told them which doors mattered and which didn't, and my mother's family had stopped mattering by then. She survived. She was sixteen and she survived because she had crossed the ice that winter to fetch flour from a cousin and was on the wrong side of the river when it happened, and she carried that arithmetic the rest of her life: alive by absence, saved by being elsewhere.

She met my father because she brought the list to him. She had copied it out by hand — she had beautiful copybook hand, Bram said, the kind they taught girls precisely so they could be trusted with other people's records — and she put it on the counter of the print shop and said, *Print this,* and Bram, who was twenty-six and careful, looked at this girl who was asking him to commit treason before he'd even learned her Christian name, and said, *How many copies?*

"She said ten thousand," Bram told me. "And I said we'd start with two hundred and see if anyone hanged us. And nobody hanged us. So we printed ten thousand."

That was the pamphlet business. Not ideology first. Arithmetic first. My parents had both been counted and found surplus, and everything after that was the same sentence written many ways.

"And Mama's name," I said. "Struck from the record."

"After the crackdown in '29. They came for everyone connected to the distribution lists. She'd married me by then, taken Kavanagh, but her maiden name was on the old deed and the old registers, and Renn's predecessor — a thorough man, worse than thorough — went backward through the paper. She struck herself out before he could strike her down. Changed her name in every book she could reach, burned what couldn't be changed, and became, officially, no one." Bram smiled, and it was a terrible smile, proud and broken at once. "Your mother understood something I only half-learned. Records are weapons. Whoever holds the ledger holds the truth. So we hold our own."

He tapped the forme. *THEY COUNT YOU BECAUSE YOU ARE WORTH COUNTING.*

"This is our ledger," he said. "Every pamphlet, every name we keep that they've erased, every person Denny carries across the ice — that's an entry. We're keeping books, Freya. The state keeps theirs. The question of the century is whose books survive."

* * *

Denny came home while Bram was still talking, which I suspect was arranged, because my brother is many things but accidental is rarely one of them. He came through the paper store's back window instead of the street door, snow in his hair, cheeks scalded red from the river wind, and stopped dead when he saw me sitting on a paper bale in the forbidden room.

"Oh," he said. "Oh, good. Finally. Do you know how exhausting it is lying to you? You have a face like a customs officer."

"You sleep here," I said, "and you smell of river."

"I sleep here and I smell of cards. Cards played near a river." He shrugged off his coat and hung it on the press frame like the press was furniture, like it hadn't just rewritten my entire understanding of our household. "Papa told you about the runs?"

"He's telling me now, apparently."

"Then let me do the useful part." Denny crouched in front of me, and for a moment the flippancy fell away and I saw the man he'd become without anyone authorizing the transformation. "Twice a week, sometimes three. Fugitives, mostly — printers from Harrowgate, strikers from the mills, last month a schoolteacher who taught the wrong alphabet. I take them across the ice to the tannery landing, and from there Marta's people move them south or east. The ice holds till mid-March, maybe later this year. After that, boats, which are slower and watched, and after the thaw, nothing." He looked at me steadily. "You're in it now. Not doing — knowing. Knowing's the job I can't do alone. Somebody in this family has to be able to lie to officials with a straight face, and Papa's face cracks like drying clay, and mine—" he gestured at himself, "—is famously untrustworthy. Yours, Freya. Yours is the best face in the building."

"I'm flattered."

"You should be. I've watched it work. You told Thorn today the back room was a storeroom and he believed you for almost four entire seconds, which is a record against a ministry man."

"That's not a compliment, that's a diagnosis."

"It's both," Bram said from the paper bale. "In this house it's usually both."

And there it was — the inheritance, handed over not with ceremony but with a joke, the way real inheritances always travel. Not gold. Weight. The knowledge that the locked room was unlocked now, permanently, inside me. I sat among the formes and the fugitive-smell and the cheap sharp ink and understood that I had crossed a border tonight and there was no stamp on the passport, because the passport had been my own face, and I had just surrendered it to the cause.

* * *

Later — Denny gone up to sleep, or to pretend to; Bram gone to bank the stove — I stayed in the back room alone with the forme and the lamp. I unlocked the chase, which felt like a liberty and probably was one, and lifted out a single piece of type from the last line. An em quad, actually, blank metal, worth less than a farthing. I held it because holding something was necessary.

Through the shuttered window I could see the river, a pale seam between the black banks, holding, for a few more weeks, all those crossings. Somewhere out there Denny's footprints filled in behind him with snow, erasing themselves nightly, the opposite of everything we did in this shop. We spent our lives making marks that lasted. He spent his making paths that vanished. Same trade, I suppose. Both of us deciding what the record would say.

The census man will come back. Bram said it like weather, like taxes, like death — the certainties. And when Elias Thorn walks through that door with his polite questions and his patient ledger, he will be asking after a woman on a deed from 1828, a woman who struck her own name from the world to save it, and I will stand behind the counter with ink under my nails and the best face in the building, and I will lie to him.

But lying, I understand now, is only printing in reverse. You set the type of what isn't true and hope the impression takes. And somewhere in the city tonight, two hundred sheets were drying that said *they count you because you are worth counting*, and somewhere on the ice my brother was walking the counter-argument, and somewhere in a ministry drawer a clerk held a page with my mother's name scraped away — and I thought, holding my worthless blank quad, that when the census man came back, I would finally have an answer ready for his question about the woman on the deed.

She was real. She is recorded. She is remembered.

Whether I tell him any of that depends entirely on what he does next — and as I turned down the lamp, I heard, faint through the shutters, boots on the frozen lane outside, stopping, briefly, at our door, before moving on.

Not tonight, then.

But soon.
