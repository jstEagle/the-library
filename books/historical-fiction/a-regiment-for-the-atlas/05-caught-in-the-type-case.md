## 5. Caught in the Type Case

There is a particular dishonesty required of a census taker, and it is not the kind you'd expect. The lie isn't in the questions. The questions are innocent enough — names, ages, trades, how many sleep under this roof, do you bake your own bread. No, the lie is in the posture. You must stand at a stranger's door holding a ledger like a small black Bible and pretend you are not, in fact, an instrument of the state's appetite for knowing everything about everyone. You must be a friend with a notebook. A confessor who writes down every sin.

I had been lying professionally for six years, and I thought I was rather good at it.

Then Freya Kavanagh answered her door on a Tuesday and asked me why I needed to know, and I discovered I had never once been good at anything.

* * *

I told myself I went back to the print district because of the paperwork. This is the first lie in my account, so let us be honest from the start: I went back because she had asked me *why*, and no one — not my supervisor, not the Ministry's manuals, not the gray-faced men who trained us to count souls like sacks of grain — had ever suggested that the question itself might require defending.

The file said Kavanagh & Son, Printers, 14 Coleraine Lane. It said Bram Kavanagh, proprietor, aged fifty-four. It said one son, Dennison, twenty-three. It said the shop paid rates assessed against two presses and four workmen, though I had stood in that shop and seen one press and no workmen at all, only the daughter sorting type with the quick economy of someone born to it. And it said — this was the entry that had kept me awake, that sat on my chest at night like a cat I hadn't asked for — *deed transferred 1828 to Bram Kavanagh and wife, M— Kavanagh*. The rest of the name had been blotted by water damage, or by something less accidental than water.

Fourteen Coleraine Lane sat low in its street like a man trying not to be noticed at a funeral. When I came around the corner that Thursday afternoon, off-duty, out of uniform, wearing the civilian coat I kept for occasions when I wanted to be a person instead of a function, I stopped in the shadow of the chandler's across the way — and heard it.

A press, running.

Not the front press. The sound was wrong for the front room; too muffled, too rhythmic, coming from below and behind, the way sound travels through old floors and older secrets. *Thump-thump-snick. Thump-thump-snick.* A heart beating under the floorboards of Vellmark.

Now, a reasonable man would have walked away. A reasonable man would have noted the discrepancy — unregistered press in operation during non-declared hours — and filed it Monday morning with his supervisor, Mr. Quill, whose name was the only joke the Ministry had ever permitted itself. A reasonable man would have understood that irregular households were the Ministry's favorite delicacy, that supervisors dropped hints about advancement the way rich men drop coins, and that a taker who found a hidden press might find himself promoted clear out of the counting business altogether.

I have never claimed to be a reasonable man. I am a curious one, which is worse, and cheaper, and gets you into far more trouble.

I crossed the street and knocked.

* * *

The press stopped before my second knock landed. That, more than anything, told me what I needed to know: they had been listening for footsteps. A household that stops its machinery when strangers approach is a household with something to stop.

Freya opened the door with the same unhurried expression as before, as though I were a delivery of paper stock and nothing more. She had ink on her fingers. I noticed it the way a customs man notices a bulge in a coat lining — instantly, helplessly, and with a certain professional pleasure that shames me even now. It was on the pads of her right hand, black as typographer's ink must be, and she did not wipe it, which meant either that she didn't care or that she wanted me to see it and draw the comfortable conclusion that a printer's daughter naturally has ink on her hands.

"Mr. Thorn," she said. "Twice in one week. The Ministry must think we breed."

"The Ministry thinks very little," I said. "That's rather its genius. Re-verification. Standard procedure for households flagged for assessment review."

"We weren't flagged."

"You are now."

She looked at me for a moment, and I want to be precise about what passed between us in that doorway, because I have turned it over so many times since that I know its texture better than my own hand. She wasn't afraid. That was the thing. Everyone else in Vellmark flinched when the ledger came out — a tightening at the eyes, a half-second where they calculated what their answers would cost them. Freya Kavanagh looked at me the way a card player looks at an opponent who has just made a bet he can't cover. Not fear. Arithmetic.

"You'll want to see the shop again," she said.

"I'm afraid procedure requires it."

"Procedure." She tasted the word like something gone off. Then she stepped back and let me in, and behind her I saw the forme — the locked frame of type sitting on the stone bench near the window — shift slightly, or I imagined it did, and beneath its edge, a corner of paper. Printed paper. Freshly printed paper, if I was any judge, and I was beginning to suspect I was.

* * *

Let me describe the shop as I saw it that second time, since I was now seeing it properly, the way you reread a letter after you learn the writer has died and find meanings everywhere.

The front room smelled of ink and oil and lamp-black, and beneath those, faintly, the iron smell of the river frozen hard outside. Type cases lined the wall in their shallow wooden drawers, each compartment a small coffin for a letter. The big press stood in the middle like a patient horse. Everything tidy. Too tidy. A working shop pays taxes for four men and shows me one press, and the press is clean, and the floor has no drifts of paper waste, and there is no journeyman's coat on any peg. Shops lie the same way people do: by being too neat.

And on the stone bench, that forme, and beneath it — I let my eyes pass over it once, casually, the way you're supposed to — the edge of a printed sheet, white and new against the dark wood.

"So," I said. "Re-verification. Household roster, if you please. Names and ages of all persons resident."

"You have them. You wrote them yourself last week."

"Papers change. People travel."

"People hide," she said, and then, smoothly, before I could decide whether I'd heard what I'd heard: "People go to market. Denny is at market. My father is resting. The household is as you found it."

"And the woman?"

I hadn't planned to ask it. It came out the way a splinter works loose — suddenly, and with blood. *The woman.* The deed said M— Kavanagh, 1828. Every record since said nothing. In six years of taking censuses I had learned that the gaps in documents are louder than the entries, that the Ministry's erasures have a signature all their own, clean and bureaucratic and total, and here was a family carrying one of those silences like a stone in the shoe.

Freya went very still. Not dramatically — a lesser observer would have missed it entirely. But her stillness had a quality I recognized from the ice on the river: the surface holds, and you understand that the whole depth of it is listening.

"What woman?" she said.

"The deed from 1828. Your mother, presumably."

"My father owns this shop. He bought it outright. You may verify with the assessor."

"I intend to verify everything," I said, and immediately wished I hadn't, because it sounded exactly like what it was: a threat wrapped in a clerk's collar.

"Do you?" she said. "Because it seems to me you verify whatever the Ministry tells you matters, and ignore whatever it tells you doesn't. You counted forty-one souls in the tenement on Marsh Lane last month, Mr. Thorn. Did you note that three of the beds held children under five? Did you note whether they had shoes?" She picked up a composing stick as she spoke, absently, the way another woman might pick up knitting. "You count us. Nobody counts *for* us. There's a difference between a census and a reckoning, and I wonder sometimes if your Ministry knows it."

I should report the exact sensation I felt then, which was this: as though someone had reached into my chest and rearranged the furniture. Because I had counted Marsh Lane. Forty-one souls, and yes, the children, and yes, I had written *5* and *4* and *2* in their proper columns while their mother watched me with the flat patience of the poor, and I had walked out into the snow feeling nothing at all, because feeling nothing is the first skill they teach you and the last one you admit to learning.

"I don't set policy," I said. "I take counts."

"Everyone says that." She set the stick down. "Everyone who does something says they don't really do it."

The press under the floor stayed silent, but I could feel it there, the way you feel a held breath. And the forme sat on its bench with its secret corner of paper showing, and I understood that I was standing in the middle of a machine far larger than any press — a machine of silence and arrangement, of tea and tidiness, running night and day under this roof — and that Freya Kavanagh was offering me a choice disguised as a conversation: press further and become an enemy, or step back and remain a fool.

I have never much cared for either role.

"Tea?" she said.

* * *

I want it recorded — somewhere, anywhere, since my ledger will never hold it — that she offered me tea instead of explanations, and that I sat down and accepted, and that we both understood perfectly what was happening. Hospitality is the oldest interrogation there is. She was measuring me over the cups: how I held mine, where my eyes went, whether I glanced at the cellar door (I did; I regretted it instantly; her eyes followed mine and came back with a small cold light in them). And I was measuring her right back, except that she was easier to read than most and harder to fathom than anyone, because everything she showed me was deliberate and everything she withheld was load-bearing.

Her father appeared briefly — Bram Kavanagh, big through the shoulders, moving with the careful economy of a man rationing his breath. Lung-rot, or something like it; I've heard that cough in enough mill towns to know it. He shook my hand with a grip that apologized for nothing, looked at his daughter for one unreadable moment, and said, "Mind the girl answers nothing she don't want to, Mr. Thorn. That's the law above your law."

"There is no such law," I said.

"No," he agreed pleasantly, and coughed, and went back through the door, and left me sitting in the enemy's kitchen drinking excellent tea.

That tea haunts me. It was properly made — scalded pot, short steep — and it occurred to me, sitting there with the wind rattling the shutters and the hidden press silent below my feet like a buried heart, that these people had offered me the best of what little they had, and that I had come to rob them, and that some traitorous chamber of my chest had already begun to notice how warm the room was.

"So," Freya said at last, turning her cup. "Will you write it down?"

"Write what down?"

"Whatever you decide I'm hiding."

"That's not how verification works."

"Isn't it? You look. You choose what it means. You write the meaning down, and then the meaning becomes true, because it's in a ledger, and ledgers are how the Crown remembers things." She looked up at me, and her eyes were tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep. "My father says a record is just a weapon that hasn't been fired yet. Is he wrong, Mr. Thorn?"

Outside, the light was failing. Somewhere down Coleraine Lane a cart creaked past, and the river ice boomed once, far off, the great slow voice of the winter settling its accounts. Under the floorboards: nothing. On the bench: that white corner of paper, showing like a tongue.

"No," I said, and heard myself say it. "He isn't wrong."

She nodded slowly, as if I'd passed something — a test, or a threshold, she'd never tell me which — and rose to refill my cup, and when she came back the forme had been moved, or covered, or I had simply stopped looking, because by then I was looking at her, and a man can't watch two things at once when one of them frightens him.

I finished my tea. I thanked her for it. At the door I paused, because there was one more thing, and honesty — which had been gaining ground in me all afternoon like an army at dawn — insisted.

"The sheet under your forme," I said quietly. "It was printed within the hour. I won't write that down today."

She didn't deny it. She didn't thank me either. She simply said, "Why not?"

And I stood there on the step with the cold coming off the river and the truth rising in me faster than I could govern it, and I heard myself answer:

"Because I haven't decided yet whose side of the ledger it belongs on."

Behind me, faint but unmistakable, the press began to run again — *thump-thump-snick* — printing God knew what for God knew whom, and Freya Kavanagh watched me walk away down Coleraine Lane without closing the door until I turned the corner, as though she wanted to be certain, one way or the other, of exactly which direction I took.
