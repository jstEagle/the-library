## 6. A Debt of Ink

The cellar smelled of tallow, damp stone, and the particular sourness of men who had been arguing since before midnight. I came down the stairs at half past one with a tray of tea I had no intention of letting anyone finish, and stopped on the last step to listen, because listening is the first trade my father ever taught me and I have never quite unlearned it.

"He's a clerk," Marta Voss was saying. Her voice has always reminded me of a file drawn slowly across iron — patient, precise, and guaranteed to remove something you'd rather keep. "A little man with a little ledger. You don't burn down a house because a sparrow landed on the sill."

"You don't," Denny said. He was still in his river clothes, wool gone stiff with frost, and he held his hands over the stove with the particular shame of a man who has been warm all his life until tonight and found out how much he minds it. "You wait until he flies off and then you break its neck quietly."

"That's very poetic for someone whose fingers haven't thawed."

"My fingers are fine." He wiggled them to demonstrate, which proved nothing except that he was my brother and would rather lose a finger than an argument.

Jozsi sat in the corner on an upturned crate, seventeen years old and trying very hard to look like a man who had opinions worth killing for. He had ink on his cheek — my ink, from my shop, which meant he'd fallen asleep at the stone again — and when our eyes met he straightened up as if the crate were a throne.

I set the tray down harder than necessary. Four heads turned. Well — three heads and Jozsi's, which turned halfway and then pretended it hadn't.

"Tea," I said. "And then someone is going to tell me why there are three grown revolutionaries in my cellar discussing murder on a Tuesday."

"It's Wednesday," said Denny.

"It was Tuesday when this conversation started and it hasn't improved since."

* * *

Here is what you must understand about my family, before you judge what happened in that cellar: we do not lie to each other. We lie magnificently, professionally, to everyone else — to the tax assessor, the parish registrar, the widow Ostrander who counts our chimney smoke in winter — but among ourselves we have never been able to afford it. A household built on secrets is like a press built on a warped bed; every sheet comes out crooked until you level the thing or abandon it entirely.

So when Bram came down the stairs behind me, slow as a sermon, and lowered himself into his chair by the stove, nobody pretended they hadn't been talking about the census man.

"His name is Thorn," Bram said. "Elias Thorn. Twenty-six years old. Orphaned in the famine year — workhouse north quarter, transferred twice. Took the census appointment two years ago and has filed four hundred and eleven household returns without a single irregularity flagged." He paused. "Until ours."

"How do you know all that?" Jozsi asked, with the naked admiration of youth confronted by competence.

"Because I've known the Ministry longer than it's known itself," Bram said. "And because Marta reads the personnel rolls the way other women read scripture."

Marta inclined her head, accepting the compliment as her due, which it was. She was sitting with her back very straight against the cellar wall, as she always did — never a chair, always the wall, so that no one could come up behind her. She had spent six years in the Kessel prison for printing something the Crown considered unprintable, and she had come out of it thinner, grayer, and approximately forty percent less willing to tolerate foolishness than when she went in. The remaining sixty percent had never been available anyway.

"So," I said. "He noticed the taxes. He asked about the woman on the deed."

"He asked about *her*?" Denny sat forward. The stove light moved across his face and made him look briefly like our father, which frightened me more than any soldier ever had.

"He asked politely. That's worse."

"It's not worse," Marta said. "It's simply different. Politeness is a technique. So is a boot in the door. They both get you inside."

"And what do we do about it?"

That was the question, wasn't it. It hung in the cellar air with the steam off the tea and the coal smoke and the smell of the river coming off Denny's clothes.

"We could talk to him," Jozsi offered.

Four people looked at him.

"I mean — not *talk*. But he's a person, isn't he? People can be turned. I read a pamphlet once—"

"You read a pamphlet," Marta said, "that I wrote, and it was about grain tariffs. Sit down."

He sat down. I poured the tea. It gave my hands something to do while the real argument assembled itself in the room, the way weather assembles over water.

* * *

"The sensible thing," Marta said at last, "is that he has an accident. Clerks fall into canals all the time. Vellmark is a city of canals."

"The sensible thing," Denny said, "is that I follow him home tonight and learn where he sleeps, and then we decide whether he falls into a canal."

"And if he's already reported us?" I said.

They looked at me.

"If he's already written it down," I said, setting my cup down with a click that sounded louder than it was, "then drowning him accomplishes nothing except telling the Ministry exactly where to look. A dead census taker's last route gets walked again. Door to door. With soldiers."

"Then we should have drowned him before he finished the route," Denny said.

"Denny—"

"No. Listen to me, Frey." My brother stood, and the frost in his wool creaked. "Three people crossed the ice last night. Three. A woman and two children from the Havelock mills, and I put them across on ice that groaned like a dying man, and I stood on the south bank afterward and thought: if one more person knows about that route than needs to, we are all dead. And now there is a man from the Ministry who has stood in our shop and counted our presses and asked after Mother, and everyone wants to *wait*."

"Waiting isn't doing nothing," Bram said mildly.

"Waiting is what Father did for thirty years and it worked because nobody looked. Somebody's looking now."

The stove ticked. Somewhere above us, through the floorboards, the shop clock struck three, and I thought about the forme locked in the chase upstairs, the type standing ready, and how type is nothing but metal until some hand decides what it will say.

"Marta," I said. "You've been quiet."

"I'm deciding whether to agree with your brother," she said. "It doesn't happen often. I want to savor the discomfort."

* * *

Let me be honest, since honesty is supposedly the currency of this family: for one long moment, there in the cellar, I wanted them to say yes.

I wanted it the way you want a splinter out. Elias Thorn had sat in my shop with his ledger and his careful questions and he had looked at me — not through me, not past me, the way officials look at women behind counters, but *at* me — and he had asked about my mother, and I had lied to him with my whole face, and some part of me that I keep locked in the back room with the second press had wanted very badly for him to simply stop existing. To become a gap in the census. An unexplained absence the Ministry would file under accident and forget.

That part of me is not a good person. I have made peace with her. Mostly.

But wanting a thing and arguing for it are different trades, and I was raised a printer's daughter, which means I was raised to know the difference between what the heart sets and what the hand composes.

"Here is my argument," I said. "Not mercy. Don't mistake it for mercy."

"Wouldn't dream of it," said Denny, who knew me too well.

"Strategy. Sit down, Denny, I mean it." He sat. "A census taker is a machine. You've all treated him like one — a device the Ministry points at households to extract numbers. But machines have maintenance schedules. Machines get repaired, replaced, recalled. If Thorn disappears, the Ministry doesn't grieve him, it *audits* him. They pull his route. They re-walk every door he knocked on. They find the shop that paid manufactory taxes with no journeymen, and they send someone less polite than he is, and that someone brings friends."

"They might do that anyway," Marta said.

"They might. But right now we're a question mark in his ledger, not an answer. A question mark can be left alone. An answer cannot be unsaid." I looked around the cellar. "We don't kill the doubt. We feed it. We give him a version of this house so boring he files it and moves on."

"A boring version," Denny repeated flatly. "Of us."

"Father is a widower printer with a bad chest. His daughter keeps his books. His son works the docks. There is no nephew, there is no back room, there is no woman on the deed because she died before I was born and grief makes clerks careless with paperwork. We are dull, Denny. We are the dullest household in the fourth district. I intend to be so tedious that man forgets my name by spring."

"And if he doesn't forget it?" Jozsi asked softly.

The cellar went quiet in the way cellars do, holding sound close the way they hold cold.

"Then we'll know," I said, "because he'll come back. And we'll deal with the man who comes back, not the one we imagine."

* * *

Bram spoke then, for the first time since he'd settled into his chair, and when my father speaks after silence, the silence pays attention.

"When your mother was alive," he said, "she used to say that ink is a debt. Every word you set down, you owe to whoever reads it. You can pay in truth or you can pay in lies, but the debt collects either way." He looked at me across the stove. "Your mother's name is a debt this family has been carrying twenty years. This clerk is asking us to pay it. Freya's right that killing the creditor solves nothing. But understand what she's proposing. She's proposing we keep owing."

"I'm proposing we keep breathing," I said.

"The two are related," Bram said, and coughed, and waved off Denny's hand, and the cough went on a moment longer than any of us liked, and then stopped.

Marta unfolded herself from the wall. When Marta stands, rooms reorganize themselves around her; even the stove seemed to draw back respectfully.

"Vote," she said. "Denny?"

"Spare him. Under protest. Under *loud* protest. I want it noted that I think we're wrong and that I reserve the right to say so at length later, possibly over your graves."

"Noted. Jozsi?"

Jozsi opened his mouth, closed it, and looked at me. "Spare him," he said. "Freya's logic holds. Also I don't think I could drown anyone and I'd like that kept between the five of us."

"Kept between the five of us," Marta confirmed, with something almost like warmth, if warmth were issued in regulated quantities. "Bram?"

My father nodded once. "Spare."

"Freya?"

"Spare," I said. "For strategy."

"For strategy," Marta agreed, and I heard, underneath the file-grate of her voice, that she didn't believe me for a moment and had decided to let it stand anyway. "Then here is how it goes. Nobody approaches him. Nobody follows him — Denny, I mean it, sit back down. Nobody feeds him anything but tea and boredom. Freya manages him in the shop. If he asks about the woman on the deed again, she died in 1831 of the lung fever, God rest her, and the paperwork was lost in the parish fire of '34, which is true, incidentally, which is why it's useful. Lies anchored to truth hold weight. Anything else is improvisation, and improvisation is how cells die."

She gathered her coat. At the stairs she paused, and looked back at me with those prison-gray eyes.

"One more thing, girl. Men like Thorn aren't machines and aren't sparrows. They're scales. Everything you put on one side, something weighs the other. Remember that whatever you feed him, you're feeding yourself to him too."

Then she was gone up the stairs, and Jozsi after her, and the cellar was just my family and the stove and the smell of the river on my brother's coat.

* * *

Denny stayed by the fire, working feeling back into his fingers, not looking at me.

"You argued for him," he said finally.

"I argued for *us*."

"You argued for him. I've watched you sort type since you were seven years old, Frey. You argue like you set a line — everything justified, nothing wasted. Tonight you justified hard." He looked up. "Why?"

I could have given him the strategic answer again. It was a good answer. It had carried a vote.

"He asked about Mother," I said instead. "Politely. Like she was a line item. And I realized nobody has said her name out loud in this house in six years except Father, late, to himself, thinking I don't hear." I wrapped my hands around my cold teacup. "If he's going to dig, let him dig toward something true for once. Maybe if I watch him closely enough, I'll learn how to say it myself someday."

Denny was quiet a long moment. Then he snorted — the Kavanagh snort, inherited entire from our father — and said, "You're going to get us all killed over a feeling you won't even name."

"Probably," I agreed. "But not this week."

He laughed, despite himself, despite everything, and took his frozen coat upstairs to dry, and I banked the stove and stood a moment in the warm dark of the cellar, among the smells of tallow and stone and the ghost of argument.

Above me, the shop clock ticked toward morning. Upstairs the type waited in its case, ten thousand small metal decisions, none yet committed.

And somewhere across the sleeping city, I thought, a man with a ledger was also awake — also weighing, also composing his next line — and I fell asleep that night, when sleep finally came, already rehearsing the small boring truths I would feed him, and telling myself firmly that strategy was the whole of it.

The lie held for three days. Which, for a lie of mine, was nearly a record.
