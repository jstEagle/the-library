## 8. Bread and Salt

There are two ways to disarm a man, my father taught me. The first is to break his arm. The second is to feed him.

The second takes longer, he said, but the results last.

I had spent three days composing the invitation in my head the way I'd once composed a difficult line of type — turning it over, testing it for kerning faults, checking that no letter stood where it could be read wrong. *Mr. Thorn, we find we've cooked too much stew.* No. Too coy. *Mr. Thorn, you look half-starved.* Worse — an insult dressed as concern. In the end I simply caught him at the door of the shop on Thursday evening, ledger under his arm and frost in his eyebrows, and said:

"We eat at seven. There will be more than enough. My father dislikes eating in silence."

It was not entirely a lie. Bram did dislike eating in silence. What I left out was that he disliked it because silence gave him room to think, and thinking, in our house, was a dangerous occupation.

Elias Thorn looked at me the way a man looks at a bridge he suspects will not hold his weight. "That's kind," he said, in the tone of someone naming a trap.

"It's soup," I said. "Kindness would be seconds."

And because he was a man who counted things — mouths, chimneys, irregularities — he counted himself through our doorway at seven o'clock precisely, hat in his hands like a schoolboy called before the magistrate.

* * *

I had planned the evening the way Denny plans a crossing: every variable noted, every escape route open.

The shop smelled of cabbage and bay leaf and the iron tang of ink underneath it all, because you cannot scrub ink out of a house built around a press; it lives in the grain of the floorboards, in the cracks of our hands, in the very bread if you leave it uncovered too long. I'd lit the good lamps. I'd hidden Jozsi's latest broadside proof inside the flour bin. I'd told Denny — who had come up off the ice gray-lipped and shivering two hours past dawn — to keep his mouth full or shut, preferably both.

"Mr. Thorn." Bram rose from his chair by the stove with the slow ceremony of a man whose lungs charge him interest on every movement. He extended his hand. "You're the census man."

"I'm afraid so."

"Then sit down and be afraid a little longer. Freya's stew forgives nothing."

Denny snorted into his bowl. Elias sat.

I will say this for him: he ate like a man who had learned young not to trust the next meal. Not greedily — he was too careful for greed — but thoroughly, attentively, as though memorizing the stew in case he should ever need to describe it in evidence. I watched him notice the fourth chair at the table, the one kept empty, and watched him decide not to ask about it. A wise instinct. It had been empty eleven years, and the asking of it had ended conversations in this house before they began.

"You have a well-kept establishment," he offered at last, which is what census men say when they mean *you pay taxes like a manufactory and employ no journeymen*.

"My father believes in order."

"Order," Bram said, ladling himself more stew with a hand that trembled only slightly, and not from age. "Yes. The Ministry loves order. Did you know, Mr. Thorn, that I once attended a lecture by a Ministry man on the science of population? He had charts. Beautiful things. Little bars of black ink marching across white paper like soldiers. He showed us exactly how many souls Vellmark could sustain, and how many it did sustain, and the difference between the two figures he called — I remember the phrase — 'surplus attrition.'"

The word *attrition* sat on the table between us like a stone.

"That was a long time ago," Elias said carefully.

"The famine year," Bram agreed pleasantly. "You'd have been—" he squinted, doing arithmetic with the cruelty of a man who has never forgotten a number, "—six? Seven? Old enough to remember whether there was bread."

I set down my spoon. Across the table Denny went very still, the stillness of a boy who has learned that sudden movements draw fire.

"Bram," I said, in the voice I use for setting type too fine.

"No, no, let him answer. It's a census, after all. We're all being counted tonight." My father smiled at Elias with terrible gentleness. "Was there bread?"

The lamp hissed. Somewhere upstairs a board settled in the cold. And I saw something happen in Elias Thorn's face that I had not seen in all his visits — the ledger behind his eyes closing, and something older and less defended opening behind it.

"There was a count," he said. His voice had gone flat, the way voices go when a man walks on ice he knows is thin. "In the parish where I lived. They came in the autumn and wrote down every name and every mouth. My father thought — everyone thought — that being written down meant something. That the record was a promise. You give them your truth, they give you their help." He turned his spoon over, laid it down. "In the spring they used those same books to decide which parishes got the relief shipments. Ours had been marked... I don't know the word they used. Overallocated. As though bread were a debt we'd incurred." 

He stopped. I found I was holding my own breath.

"There were four hundred and six people in my parish when the count was taken," he said. "I know because I've read the book. They let me read it, later, when I was training. It's very neat. Every hand accounted for. Four hundred and six names in copperplate, and then in the margin, in a different ink, added years afterward: *deaths subsequent to enumeration, 289.*"

Nobody moved. Even the stove seemed to burn quieter.

"They didn't starve us," he said, and now his voice cracked along some seam he'd been patching since he was seven years old. "That's the thing people get wrong. Nobody decided to kill us. They just... declined to save us. The arithmetic did it. You put four hundred and six in one column and the grain in another, and when the columns don't balance, somebody gets erased. It's very clean. There's no villain in it. That's what frightened me most, when I was small enough to be frightened properly. There was no one to hate. Just the book."

He looked up then, directly at Bram, and I understood that he knew exactly what this supper was — that he had walked into our kitchen knowing it was an interrogation dressed in wool, and had answered anyway, because some part of him had been waiting eleven years for someone to ask whether there was bread.

"My mother's name is in that book," he said quietly. "Row thirty-one. She died in March. My father in April. I was counted, and fed by neighbors, and counted again the next year as a ward of the parish, and I have been counting ever since. It's honest work. Somebody has to hold the pen." His jaw tightened. "Somebody has to hold the pen."

"Yes," Bram said softly. "The question is who holds the pen, and for whom."

And there it was — my father laying his cards face-up on the table with the recklessness of a dying man, and me wanting to overturn the whole game. But Elias didn't flinch. He looked at the empty chair, and then at me, and something passed between us that I couldn't set in type: not trust, nothing so clean, but the recognition of two people who had both grown up inside other people's arithmetic.

"Thank you for the supper," he said, standing. "Mrs.—"

"Miss Kavanagh," I said.

"Miss Kavanagh. Your stew forgives nothing. You were right."

He put on his hat at the door, and paused, and for a moment I thought he would say something else — something that would change the shape of everything. Instead he said: "The fourth chair. Was it your mother's?"

The question I had deflected from him twice in this shop. He asked it now not as a census man but as a man from row thirty-one.

"Yes," I said.

He nodded slowly. "Mine would have been row thirty-two, if she'd lived to be counted again." And he went out into the dark, boots crunching on the frozen lane, toward the annex where the ledgers slept in their shelves like well-fed animals.

* * *

Afterward, Denny washed the bowls and I banked the fire, and Bram sat breathing his slow, expensive breaths by the stove.

"Well?" I said. "Did we feed him into an ally, or into a better-informed enemy?"

Bram considered this with the seriousness he brought to everything now, time having taught him that opinions are cheap but breaths are dear.

"He told us the truth about himself unprompted," he said. "That's either the mark of an honest man or a very good liar, and the Ministry doesn't recruit liars that good — they'd be wasted on census work." He coughed, and waved off my hand. "No. He's what I thought. A man holding a pen he never chose, waiting for someone to tell him what else a pen is for."

"And if you're wrong?"

My father looked into the fire for a long moment.

"Then we'll know soon, and knowing soon is worth more than knowing safely. Besides—" the ghost of his old mischief crossed his ruined face, "—he asked about the chair, Freya. Census men don't ask about chairs."

I did not sleep well. I kept hearing *four hundred and six*, and *just the book*, and the sound of his spoon being laid down like a weapon surrendered.

And it was not until I was nearly asleep that I understood what should have frightened me most of all: not that Elias Thorn might betray us.

But that sitting at our table, breaking our bread, he had become a man who could be betrayed — by us, by Renn, by the Crown he served — and men who can be betrayed make choices. Choices are the one thing no column in any ledger can predict.

Three streets away, I would learn much later, he sat at his own desk with a blank page before him and the Kavanagh file open beside it, and for the first time in his careful career, the census man did not write anything down at all.
