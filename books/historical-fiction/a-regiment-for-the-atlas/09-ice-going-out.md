## 9. Ice Going Out

My sister thinks I don't know she's been watching the census man like a hawk watches a field mouse — not because it's a threat, but because she can't decide whether to eat it or keep it.

I knew Elias Thorn was trouble from the moment Freya described him. Not the dangerous kind of trouble, which I understand and can price accordingly. The worse kind. The kind that asks questions because he actually wants the answers. A man who lies for a living and hates it is more dangerous than a man who loves his work, because he'll eventually do something about the hating, and you can't predict what.

So when Marta Voss said at the cellar meeting that we needed to know what Thorn was made of, and everyone looked at me — because everyone always looks at me when the answer involves standing on a frozen river in the dark, which tells you something about my life choices — I said yes before I'd finished thinking about it. That's how Denny Kavanagh operates. Thinking is for afterward, when it's too late to matter.

I found him three nights later at the Ferryman's Rest, drinking one ale very slowly, the way men drink when they're pretending they came for the ale. I sat down across from him without asking.

"You're the brother," he said.

"You're the census man."

"That's not a denial of anything."

"Neither was mine." I signaled for two more ales. "Walk with me. I want to show you something."

He looked at me the way a man looks at a dog that's smiling too much. "Is this an offer or a warning?"

"Both. It's Vellmark. Everything's both."

* * *

The river at night in February is a country unto itself. You leave the lamplight of the print district behind and step down onto the ice, and the city stops existing. No walls, no wards, no Ministry clerks with their ledgers. Just the black sky, the white plain, and the sound — God, the *sound*. People who've never stood on breaking ice think silence is quiet. Silence is nothing. Ice going out sounds like the whole world clearing its throat, like a giant rolling over in bed, like something enormous deciding whether to keep holding you up.

Elias stepped onto the ice behind me and immediately did the wrong thing: he walked like a man walking on a street. Weight on his heels, confident, upright.

"Stop." I put out an arm. "Feet apart. Low. Slide where you can. And never — never — walk straight out onto flat ice. You follow the pressure ridges. The ice is thickest where it's been fighting itself."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because if you fall through, I have to decide whether to pull you out, and I'd rather not spend the whole night doing arithmetic."

He crouched then, and I'll give him this — he learned fast. We went along the ridge line toward the middle channel, lantern shuttered except when I lifted its slide, and I talked the whole way, partly because the route needed explaining and mostly because I wanted to hear how he answered questions that weren't on any form.

"Every winter since the famine year," I said, "this river has been a road. In '42 there were four hundred people crossed here between solstice and thaw. Millers who owed taxes they couldn't pay. Weavers blacklisted after the strike. A priest who said a funeral mass for a boy the garrison had shot, and found himself suddenly unemployed and urgently elsewhere." I lifted the lantern slide so he could see my face. "You count mouths for the Crown. I move them. I figure we ought to be introduced properly."

"And this isn't —" He paused, choosing the word with visible care. "This isn't a confession you'll regret?"

"It would be," I said, "if you were going to write it down. But see, here's what Marta figured out about you, and what Freya figured out, and what I'm confirming tonight: you had chances. Three weeks of chances. The shop pays manufactory taxes with no journeymen. There's a woman on a deed from 1828 who has no grave and no record. Any taker who wanted advancement could have filed all of it by now and been sipping sherry with Inspector somebody in the capital. Instead you falsified a date in your own ledger. Badly, by the way. Your sevens look nervous."

He stopped walking. Out there in the middle of the frozen river, with the wind coming down from the north like a debt collector, he just stood there and looked at me.

"You read my file."

"I *am* the file. I'm the irregular household, Thorn. I'm the entry you can't make add up."

The ice groaned beneath us then — a long, deep sound, like a whale singing through a floorboard, and I felt him flinch even though he didn't move. Good. Fear is the beginning of respect, and respect is the beginning of everything else.

"The ice is talking," I told him. "It does that when the current underneath gets hungry. Come on. There's someone I want you to meet."

* * *

Her name was Ilse Brenner, and she crossed that night with her daughter waiting on the far bank, and she was thirty-one years old and had been a schoolteacher in Halden before the strike, before the lists, before the word *teacher* became a thing you fled under. I tell you this now so you understand what happened next, because it's easy to make it into a story about ice, and it wasn't. It was a story about a woman who had walked nine days to reach a river and was not going to stop walking for anything as small as drowning.

We were at the narrows, where the channel runs fast under thin ice, and I was maybe ten paces ahead setting the route with my pole, tapping, listening, tapping again. Elias was behind Ilse, carrying her bundle like it was his privilege to do it, which — fine. Points for that.

The ice didn't crack so much as *decide*. One tap of my pole came back hollow, and I turned to say back up, back up slow, and the white sheet under Ilse simply opened, the way a book opens, and took her.

She didn't scream. That's the part people get wrong in the telling later. She went down with her mouth shut, because nine days of flight teaches you that noise is death, and the river closed over her like a lid.

But the current had her legs, and the ice had her arms, and she surfaced in the gap with a sound finally — one short bark of air, nothing wasted — and got her elbows up on the lip, and the lip broke, and she got them up again, and I was moving, sliding on my belly the way you're taught, distributing the weight, shouting at Elias without looking at him: "*Rope! Bundle strap! Anything!*"

What he did instead was something no one taught him, because there was no teaching for it. He lay down flat and slid past me — past me, past the safe distance, past every sensible line — and got his hands under her arms while the ice groaned and shifted under us both like something waking up annoyed.

"I have you," he said. Not to me. To her. "I have you, I have you, come *up*—"

She came up the way a drowned thing comes up, all weight and no help, soaking him to the shoulder in water cold enough to burn, and the ice complained the entire time, and I got my pole across the gap as a bridge, and between the three of us and the pole and whatever gods look after fools on frozen rivers, we dragged her out.

Then came the part that matters more than the rescue, and almost nobody understands this until they've done it: the ninety seconds after. Wet cloth kills faster than water. You have maybe two minutes before the cold starts making decisions for her.

"Run," I said to Elias, meaning the far bank, the fire we kept cached in a stone hollow — and he ran, and I mean *ran*, this clerk, this ink-stained counter of souls, sprinting across a frozen river in the dark with a stranger's bundle under one arm and the bundle already open and dry blanket half-unrolled before he reached us. He wrapped Ilse's core first, not the extremities — correct, nobody had told him, he just *did it right* — and got her walking, moving her, keeping her blood arguing with the cold.

By the time we had her at the fire on the north bank, blue-lipped and shaking and swearing at us in three dialects, which is how you know a fugitive will live, Elias was soaked from chest to knee and shivering hard enough to loosen his teeth, and he was still asking her questions. Quiet ones. Where it hurt. Could she feel her feet. What was her daughter's name.

"Marta," she said through chattering teeth. "After my mother. She's eight."

"She's watching the fire from the tree line," Elias said. "She hasn't stopped watching you since you came up. Go to her. Slowly. Don't sit down yet."

And Ilse Brenner, who had trusted no official person in eleven months, looked at this census taker of the Crown — this man whose profession was writing down names so the state could find them — and she took his hand and squeezed it, and went to her daughter.

* * *

We sat by the fire after, while the ferry network's north-side contacts moved mother and child inland. Elias had stripped off his wet coat and boots, and I'd given him the spare wool from the cache, and he sat there looking like a scarecrow that had lost an argument with a laundry line.

"Well," I said. "You passed."

"Passed what?"

"The test. Or the trap. We hadn't decided which. Still haven't, honestly. You hauling her out might have been the most heroic thing I've seen, or the most elaborate long con in the history of the Ministry. Marta will want to discuss it for six hours."

He stared into the fire. For a while he didn't say anything, and I let him not say it, because I know the shape of a man assembling words he's afraid of.

"When I was seven," he said finally, "the assessors came through our parish counting mouths against grain stores. My father hid my younger sister in the flour bin because she was small enough and the count determined the ration. She sneezed." His voice was very level, which is its own kind of frightening. "They struck our household to half-rations for fraud. My father died in March. My mother sent me to an uncle in Vellmark and stayed with the girls. Two of the four of us reached spring. I've spent my whole life inside their arithmetic, Kavanagh. Counting. Being counted. Tonight is the first time I ever used my hands for anything else."

The fire popped. Upstream, the ice gave a long moan, the sound of the season turning over in its sleep, and I thought: thaw is coming, and boats after that, and after boats, decisions.

"You know what happens now," I said.

"They take me in. Or they kill me. Or both, in some order."

"No," I said. "Worse. My sister invites you to supper."

He looked at me like I'd spoken in tongues.

"Bram will cook," I said, standing, shouldering my pole. "There will be bread and salt, and stories, and Freya will watch you across the table the way she watches a forme of type, checking for flaws, and you will eat every bite and answer every question, and by the end of it you'll belong to us whether you like it or not. That's how it works. That's how it worked on me, and I was born into it."

"And if I'm lying?" he asked quietly. "If all of this is a long con?"

"Then supper's poisoned." I grinned at him over my shoulder. "It's not. Bram's stew could bring peace to nations. Walk carefully on the way back. The ice is thinner than your certainties."

Behind us, the river groaned again — longer this time, deeper, a sound with a date in it. Going-out ice, my father called it. The season when the river stops being a road and starts being a clock.

We had less time than any of us knew.
