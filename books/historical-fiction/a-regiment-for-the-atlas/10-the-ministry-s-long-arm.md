## 10. The Ministry's Long Arm

There is a particular quality to bad news that arrives on official stationery: it is always punctual.

The summons came at half past seven in the morning, delivered by a boy who looked as though he'd been told not to wait for a reply. I read it standing in the doorway of my lodgings with my coat still unbuttoned and the February cold doing its work down my collar. *Mr. E. Thorn, Census Taker, Second District, will present himself at the Ministry annex, Quay Street, at nine o'clock precisely, for review of district files. By order of Inspector H. Renn, Office of Household Verification.*

I had never heard of an Inspector Renn. This was not reassuring. In my experience, the men whose names you don't know are the ones whose names everyone else knows — the kind who arrive in a city like weather, change everything, and leave the locals to explain themselves afterward.

I ate no breakfast. I did, however, polish my boots, which tells you something about me that I have spent four years trying not to learn: when I am frightened, I reach for the small dignities. A man can't control what an inspector thinks of his files, but he can control the state of his boot leather. It is a philosophy that has kept me employed and, so far, out of prison.

Quay Street was a forty-minute walk through streets still gray with dawn smoke. I used every one of those minutes rehearsing. The Kavanagh file sat in my satchel against my hip, thin as a hymnal, and I had been aware of its thinness the way a man is aware of a cracked tooth — not painful, exactly, but impossible to stop testing with the tongue. Four visits. A household of three where the deed implied more. A woman's name struck from the record in 1828, cause unrecorded. A back room listed as "storage" that I had never actually seen the inside of, because Freya Kavanagh had stood in its doorway with a tea tray and a smile that could have taught diplomacy to ambassadors.

In my reports I had written: *Irregularities noted; further verification pending.* Six words, repeated across four documents, each repetition a small stone laid across a hole I preferred not to fall into.

What I had not written down — what lived only behind my eyes — was Denny Kavanagh leading three hooded figures across the river ice at midnight, moving with the certainty of a man who has made that crossing a hundred times. I had watched from the embankment with my ledger closed in my pocket and done nothing. Worse than nothing. I had gone home warm with something I refused to name.

Nine o'clock precisely, then. I signed the annex register with a hand that did not shake, which I counted among the week's genuine achievements.

* * *

Inspector Halvard Renn turned out to be a small man, which surprised me until it didn't. He sat behind a desk too large for him the way a blade sits in a sheath too large for it — the size was a courtesy paid to the office, not the occupant. He had a clerk's face, gray and neat, and eyes that did all the arithmetic in the room before anyone else had found their pencils.

"Thorn." He said my name the way he might say *fourteen* or *irregular*. "Sit."

I sat. He let me sit. That was the first lesson of Halvard Renn: he understood that silence is a tool with a handle on both ends, and he held it by the correct one.

"You've been in Vellmark how long?"

"Four years, sir. Three as assistant tally-clerk, one as census taker."

"Mm." He opened a folder — my folder, I realized, my whole career reduced to a quarter-inch of paper — and turned pages without appearing to read them. "Your supervisor speaks well of you. Punctual. Accurate. 'A man who counts,' I believe was the phrase." He looked up. "Do you count well, Thorn?"

"I've never lost a tally, sir."

"No. I don't imagine you have." He closed my folder with a soft clap, like a door shutting on a room where something was kept. "And yet your file on the Kavanagh household, Printers' Row, would fit inside a communion card. Four visits. Six words apiece. 'Further verification pending.' Pending since October." He laced his fingers. "It's now February, Thorn. What, precisely, has been pending?"

"The household keeps irregular hours," I said. "The proprietor's health is poor. I prefer to verify thoroughly rather than quickly."

"Prefer." He tasted the word. "You know what I prefer? I prefer census takers who understand that a thin file is not discretion. It's a signature. Every blank line in a ledger is a place where somebody decided not to look, and the Ministry is very interested lately in why men decide not to look." He slid a second folder across the desk. It was fat. Fat folders in this building were never good news for whoever they concerned. "Open it."

I opened it. The top page was a shipping manifest. The second was an inventory of seized materials — type, galley trays, reams of paper — dated November, port of Vellmark, marked *destroyed*. The third stopped my breath in a way I felt in my teeth.

It was a broadside. A pamphlet, really, cheaply printed, the ink slightly offset where the pressman had been hurried. *TO THE WORKING MEN OF VELLMARK,* it began, and beneath it a column of accusations against the mill owners, the magistrates, the wage schedule, the winter of '47 when men froze within sight of full granaries. At the bottom, no printer's imprint. No name. Just the date of composition and the phrase *set and struck in this city.*

"Recovered in Harrowgate," Renn said, watching me the way a man watches a lock he is picking. "Two hundred miles south. Someone carried them. Which means someone printed them here, in quantity, on a press that does not appear on any tax roll in your district." He leaned back. "You visited the Kavanagh print shop four times, Thorn. Tell me about the presses."

"One press," I said. "A Columbian, older model. It sits in the front room."

"And behind it?"

"A storeroom. Type cabinets, I believe. Paper stock."

"You believe." He nodded slowly, as if I'd confirmed something. "Here's what I believe. I believe there is a second press in this city, and I believe it has been running pamphlets for eight months, and I believe the Kavanagh shop pays taxes like a manufactory while employing no journeymen, which is an arithmetic problem even a poor counter like myself can solve." He let that sit. "And I believe you have known most of this since autumn."

The stove ticked. Somewhere below us a cart unloaded barrels, each thump arriving through the floorboards like a slow pulse.

"If I may, sir," I said, and my voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect, "the Kavanaghs are a printer, a printer's son, and a daughter. The father is ill. The son does delivery work. There is nothing in my observations that constitutes—"

"Evidence." Renn smiled. It was the worst thing I had seen all winter. "No. Of course not. Evidence requires looking, and you've been very careful not to look. That's what interests me, Thorn. Not what you saw. What you chose not to see." He stood, and I discovered that a small man standing while you remain seated is somehow taller than a large one. He came around the desk and perched on its edge, close enough that I could smell the coal smoke of the night train still in his coat. "Let me tell you about the famine year, since we're both such students of arithmetic. The state counted mouths. Very accurately. My predecessor was proud of the accuracy. And then the granaries stayed shut, because the count said the surplus would keep, and eleven thousand people died, and the count remained accurate right up until there was nothing left to count." His voice didn't rise. That was the horror of it; it got quieter. "The Ministry learned something from that, Thorn. It learned that a ledger is a weapon, and that weapons must be kept out of careless hands. Your hands have been careless."

"My hands—"

"Or loyal to the wrong party. I haven't decided which story I prefer, and I'll be honest with you" — he said it as though honesty were a currency he dealt in at favorable rates — "both stories end with you in a room like this one. The only question is which side of the desk you're sitting on." He returned to his chair and folded his hands. "So. The bargain."

* * *

He laid it out with the efficiency of a man who had practiced on better material than me. I was to continue my verification of the Kavanagh household — openly, officially, with a warrant this time if needed. I was to find the second press, or establish where it had been, and identify the network that moved its product. Names. Routes. Dates. When I had enough, I would deliver the file personally to him, and the Ministry, in its generosity, would remember that Elias Thorn had been a diligent servant momentarily led astray by an irregular household's hospitality.

"And if I find nothing?" I asked. "If there is no second press?"

Renn looked at me for a long moment with those gray bookkeeper's eyes, and I understood that we had passed beyond the part of the conversation where either of us pretended.

"Then I will conclude that the census taker of the Second District conspired to conceal a seditious press from Crown verification," he said, "and I will write that conclusion into your file, and my conclusions, unlike yours, do not stay pending." He pushed a single sheet of paper toward me. Blank, except for a heading and a signature line. "You'll note I haven't asked you to sign anything yet. I find signatures obtained early go stale. You'll sign when you've earned the ink."

I took the sheet. It weighed nothing. Paper always weighs nothing; that's the trick of it, that's how they get you — the whole apparatus of the state arrives in your hand light as a love letter, and only later do you discover what the weight was distributed across.

"Sir," I said, "may I ask a question?"

"You may ask. Whether I answer depends on the question."

"The woman on the deed from 1828. Margit Kavanagh. Her name was struck from the rolls. Do your records say why?"

For the first time, Renn hesitated — a flicker, no more, like ice shifting under snow. "Struck names are struck for reasons the Ministry does not revisit," he said. "That's rather the point of striking them."

"Yes," I said. "That's what I thought." I folded the blank sheet into my coat, next to my heart, where treason apparently kept its lodgings these days, and left.

* * *

I walked home the long way, along the river, because I could not bear the straight road and my own thoughts sitting beside me on it. The ice still held — thick, white, rutted with the sled tracks of legitimate commerce — but I could hear it talking to itself under the surface, that deep groan and mutter it makes in late February, like an old man deciding whether to get up.

Deliver them, or be named their accomplice. Renn had presented it as a choice, but choices are like that: the noose doesn't feel like a noose while it's still being measured. And the terrible thing, the thing I turned over and over as the wind came off the water and found every gap in my coat, was how little of me wanted to choose the rope's other end. Some cold practical clerk in my skull had already begun drafting the report. *Second press suspected in rear premises. Son observed on river after dark.* Six words apiece. Neat. Accurate. A man who counts.

But another voice — and I knew whose it was; it had Bram Kavanagh's gravel and Freya's dry edge and Denny's laugh in it, a chorus I hadn't invited and couldn't dismiss — that voice kept asking the inspector's own question back at me. *Why do men decide not to look, Thorn?*

Because of what happened the last time the state counted mouths and let the surplus die. Because I was a surplus mouth myself once, and some family with no obligation to me fed me anyway, and the arithmetic that orphaned me is the same arithmetic now wearing an inspector's coat and offering me a pen.

By the time I reached my lodgings the light was failing. I lit the lamp, took out the blank sheet Renn had given me, and set it on the table beneath the flame. Then I took out my ledger — my own, the one with four visits and six-word reports — and read through the Kavanagh entries one final time.

They were accurate. Every one. That was the trouble. Accuracy, I was beginning to understand, is just another way of lying, if you choose carefully what to count.

Tomorrow I would visit Printers' Row again. Officially, this time. With Renn's shadow standing behind me like a second coat.

I put the blank sheet away unsigned — earned ink, indeed — and sat up past midnight listening to the river talk, trying to decide whether the sound it made was warning or invitation.

By morning, I still didn't know. But I had packed my satchel, and I had noticed, with the clarity that comes at the bottom of sleepless nights, that I had packed it the way a man packs when he means to come back lighter than he left.

One way or another, something was going to break soon. The ice knew it. So did I.
