## 12. The Pamphlet War

I have been a printer's devil for three years now, which means I have spent three years being the youngest person in every room and the last to be told anything. It means my hands are permanently black to the second knuckle no matter how I scrub them. It means Marta Voss can look at me across a cellar full of gunpowder-grade secrets and say, *Jozsi, hold your tongue,* and I will hold it, mostly, because a devil who talks gets turned out, and a devil who gets turned out goes back to the tanneries.

But that night — the night we flooded Vellmark — nobody told me to hold my tongue. That night they handed me a brayer and said, *Go.*

You have to understand what we printed. Not the words; the words were Bram's, and Bram's words are another story, one he tells better than I ever could. I mean the thing itself. A broadside, half a ream of them before midnight, and on it, in letters tall enough to read from across a street: the names. Fourteen names. The strikers at the Meridian Works who went out in November for the twelve-hour day and were fired on by soldiers under an inspector's order, and who were buried in a ledger as *casualties of civil disturbance* because the Ministry does not print names unless the names are criminals.

Renn had signed the order. We put his name under theirs. Same ink, same page, same size of type. That was Bram's idea, and when he said it out loud in the cellar, old Marta — Marta, who once sat on a man's chest until he confessed to selling out a cell in Harrowgate — actually smiled. It was not a comforting sight.

*Same size of type,* she repeated, like a prayer or a sentence. *Make it so, boy.*

So I made it so. I set Renn's name in the same fourteen-point as the dead men, and I will tell you something I am not proud of: setting those letters felt better than anything I had done in three years of holding my tongue. There is a moment when you lock a forme and you know — you *know* — that what is in the chase is true, and that truth has weight, physical weight, that you will lift and carry and press into paper, and after that it cannot be unsaid. Ink does not apologize. Type does not recant. I am sixteen years old and I already understand this better than most magistrates.

We ran the second press until four in the morning. Freya fed the sheets, Denny stacked and bundled, Bram supervised with his handkerchief pressed to his mouth — the lung-rot has him coughing in wet weather now, and February in Vellmark is nothing but wet weather wearing a disguise. Nobody spoke above a whisper except the press, which speaks plenty. A hand press has a voice like a slow heartbeat in a big empty chest. *Thump. Squeak of the platen. Thump.* You fall into its rhythm the way you fall into walking.

At some point Freya looked over the pile of finished broadsides — hundreds of them, fanned like the feathers of some enormous dead bird — and said, quietly, "They'll come for us for this."

"They were always coming for us," Denny said. He said it the way you'd say the sun comes up.

"Yes," Freya agreed. "But now they'll come *angry*."

And then — and this is the part I want written down somewhere, because I was there and I saw it — she picked up her stack anyway and started bundling. She didn't even pause. If fear lives in that girl anywhere, it lives somewhere I haven't found, and I've spent enough evenings watching her sort type to have looked fairly thoroughly.

* * *

Distribution was Denny's department, which meant distribution was mine too, because everything is eventually mine. We worked the scheme Denny had drawn on the back of a tax notice: broadsides pasted up at the fish market, the corn exchange, the Meridian Works gates themselves — yes, the gates, Denny insisted, *especially* the gates — and slipped under doors down Rope Walk and Tanner's Row, and left in chapel pews, and tucked inside copies of the legitimate newspapers at the reading rooms so that some respectable citizen would unfold his morning *Vellmark Courier* and have fourteen dead men fall out into his lap.

Denny did the pasting. I did the doors and pews, because I'm small and quick and look harmless, which is the entire sum of my strategic value to the revolution and I've made peace with it. I went out in the grey hour before dawn, when the city belongs to milk carts and drunks and the occasional constable thinking about his supper. The cold was the honest kind, at least. Cold you can trust.

Pasting the gates at Meridian was Denny's job alone, and he did it in view of the night watch, bold as brass, because Denny Kavanagh's relationship with caution is the relationship of a man waving to caution from a departing train. By the time the first shift arrived at six, the gates wore our broadside like a placard. I wasn't there to see it, but I heard about it by noon from a tanner's boy who'd heard it from his sister, who'd heard it from a mill girl: the workers stopped. All of them. Stopped at the gate and read, and nobody moved for a quarter of an hour, and then a woman began to sing — some old weaving song, the sad one about the loom and the grave — and the whole crowd took it up, and the foremen didn't dare stop them because the foremen wanted to read the broadside too.

Fourteen names. Same size of type.

By eight o'clock the city had them everywhere. By nine, people who had never spoken above a mutter about the price of bread were reading aloud in the streets. By ten, there was a crowd outside the Kavanagh print shop — not a mob, nothing like that, just people, standing in the snow in their work clothes, looking through the windows at the ordinary front-of-house displays of almanacs and wedding invitations and funeral cards, as if the paper itself might confess. As if the shop would admit what it had done.

Freya stood at the window on the inside, arms folded, looking back at them. I watched her from the stairs. She didn't wave and she didn't hide. She just let them look, and she looked back, and between her and the crowd was one thickness of glass and the entire difference between a crime and a history.

"Somebody in that crowd will inform," I said, coming down. "Somebody always informs."

"Probably," she said.

"Doesn't that worry you?"

"Jozsi." She turned and looked at me with those grey eyes that make grown men confess to things they haven't done yet. "Everything worries me. Worrying is my trade. My father prints; my brother runs the ice; I worry professionally. But those people out there aren't a danger to us." She nodded at the window, at the faces in the snow. "They're the point. Everything else is just logistics."

Then she went upstairs and made tea, because Freya Kavanagh meets the apocalypse with a teapot in her hand, and honestly, having seen the alternative, I think she may be right.

* * *

The response came by noon, as responses do.

First came the criers, official notices nailed to posts: the broadside was seditious libel, possession punishable, production punishable more, information leading to arrest rewarded handsomely. The reward figure made me laugh, actually — eleven crowns. Eleven crowns for betraying the only people who'd ever treated me as more than a broom with opinions. I have been offered more for a Sunday's work gutting fish. The Ministry genuinely does not know what loyalty costs, which is either reassuring or the saddest thing I know, and I haven't decided which.

Then came Renn's answer proper: soldiers. By mid-afternoon there were redcoats at the Stone Bridge and the Ferry Stairs and the Toll Bridge, checking papers, turning back carts, and — this was new, this was the part that made the whole street go quiet — turning back *people*. Foot traffic. A woman with a market basket made to open it in the snow while a sergeant watched with his hands behind his back. An old man asked where he was going and why and who expected him.

Bridges matter in Vellmark the way veins matter in a body. Cut the bridges and you cut the city in half, north bank from south, work from home, and everyone in Vellmark understood within the hour exactly what message was being sent: *we can close you like a book.*

Marta came to the shop at dusk, hood up, moving like a woman twenty years younger because danger does that to Marta Voss, I swear it takes years off her. She stood dripping in the kitchen while Freya poured tea nobody drank, and she gave us the tally. Two arrests on the south bank, both released by evening — sweeps, not strikes. A printer in Coppersmith Lane raided and cleared out, though he'd printed nothing but hymnals since autumn, poor devil, wrong trade at the wrong time. And the bridges held, all three, garrisoned for the night.

"He's not hunting the broadside anymore," Marta said. "He knows who printed it. He's known since the first hour. This—" she jerked her chin toward the window, toward the distant sound of boots on the quay, "—this is him squeezing. Cutting circulation. Making the city feel it. He wants whoever sheltered the press to come to him, or he wants the city to give us up rather than keep bleeding."

"Will they?" I asked. "Give us up?"

Marta looked at me for a long moment, and I got the full weight of those eyes, the ones that have outlived three cells and two governments. "Eleven crowns says some will," she said. "The singing at the gate says most won't. I've bet worse odds on less."

Bram said nothing through any of this. He sat in his chair by the stove with the blanket over his knees, and his breathing had that loose wet rattle in it that frightens me more than any soldier, and at last he said, "He'll escalate."

"We know, Bram," Marta said gently.

"No." Bram shook his head, and there was something in his face I hadn't seen before, something almost apologetic. "You don't. Renn doesn't squeeze. Renn is not a man who squeezes. I read his file years ago, back when reading such files was still possible. He burned a village in the border war and wrote a report calling it *a necessary economy of force*. When the strikers ran at Meridian he didn't order a warning volley. He ordered the square cleared and he cleared it. Squeezing is what careful men do. Renn isn't careful. He's thorough." He looked at each of us in turn, and finished, flatly: "There's a difference, and the difference is bodies."

Nobody argued. In that kitchen, nobody argued with Bram Kavanagh, not even Marta, though I suspect Marta privately considered arguing a form of exercise she denied herself on principle.

* * *

I couldn't sleep. This will surprise no one who knows me, since I've never in my life slept when I ought to, but that night it wasn't restlessness. It was the arithmetic.

Because here's what nobody tells you about printing the truth: the truth doesn't end when the ink dries. The ink is the beginning. Every broadside pasted on a wall is a stone thrown in a pond, and we'd thrown a very large stone indeed, and all night I lay in the loft above the shop listening to the ripples — boots on the quay, a whistle at the bridge, the ice on the river booming and groaning like something enormous turning over in its sleep — and doing sums. Fourteen names printed. Two arrests already. Three bridges held. One inspector, thorough. Five of us in this house, counting the devil, not counting the fugitives currently sleeping in the hidden room whose existence I was sworn to months ago on pain of Marta's displeasure.

Five against a garrison, plus whatever the city gave us, minus whatever the city took away.

And underneath the fear — I'll be honest, since honesty is apparently the house style now — underneath it there was the other thing. The exhilaration. I keep reaching for a way to describe it that doesn't make me sound like a fool and I've given up. It felt like the moment the forme locks. It felt like proof. For three years I'd been the boy who swept the floor while other people risked things, and today the whole city had read something that came out of my hands, my ink, my fourteen-point type, and strangers had sung in the snow because of it, and the government had chained its own bridges shut because it was afraid of a piece of paper I helped make.

You cannot give a person that feeling and expect them to go back to sweeping floors with their whole heart. That's what Renn doesn't understand, I think. You can burn a press. You can't un-teach the feeling.

Near dawn I gave up on sleep entirely and crept down to the shop, meaning to clean the press before the others woke — devils get up early, it's in the contract, the unwritten one written in ink stains — and found Freya already there. At the case, in the dark, setting type by touch alone. Her fingers moved through the letters like someone reading a letter from home.

"You'll wake the press," she said, without turning around. She'd known it was me. Everyone always knows it's me; I have never once successfully snuck anywhere.

"What are you setting?" I asked. "Another broadside? Because Marta said we should lie low, and lying low generally doesn't involve—"

"It's not a broadside." She pulled the composing stick into the lamplight, and I squinted at the line of type, still upside down and backward the way all true things are before they're printed.

I could read maybe half of it, and the half I could read was names again. But not the strikers' names.

Kavanagh names.

She saw me see it, and she didn't hide the stick, and she didn't explain, and she didn't smile. Outside, the ice on the river cracked like a rifle shot, long and rolling, the sound it makes when winter starts losing its grip — and somewhere south of the bridges, I would have sworn I heard answering boots.
