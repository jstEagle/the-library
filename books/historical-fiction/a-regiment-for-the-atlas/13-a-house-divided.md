## 13. A House Divided

There is a particular indignity in dying slowly. A quick death is at least a story — the fall from the scaffold, the musket ball on the bridge, something a man's children can tell with their chins up. But lung-rot gives you nothing so clean. It gives you mornings like this one: propped in the chair behind the front counter of my own print shop, wrapped in two blankets and a shawl that belonged to a woman whose name I have not spoken aloud in eleven years, listening to my lungs whistle like a badly adjusted press.

I had been composing a sentence in my head for an hour. Something for Freya, when the time came. *The type remembers what the men forget.* It sounded fine in my head. Everything sounds fine in my head these days; it is only the body that has gone to pieces.

The shop was quiet in the way I love best — the quiet of order. Cases squared, formes locked, the smell of ink and lamp oil and the faint iron tang of the river coming through the shutters. Forty years I have kept this room, and I will tell you what no census taker ever wrote down: a print shop is not a business. It is a memory that has learned to feed itself.

And now the Ministry wanted to eat it.

* * *

Marta Voss arrived at nine, letting herself in with the key she'd had since before some of you were weaned, and stood over me with her arms crossed the way she does when she has already decided I am being a fool and is waiting for me to confirm it.

"You've been thinking," she said.

"I'm always thinking."

"You've been thinking *loudly*. Denny says you asked him how many soldiers Renn keeps at the annex." She pulled the other chair close and sat, knees nearly touching mine. "So say it to me, Bram, whatever it is, instead of leaving it in your will like a coward."

That is the trouble with old comrades. They know your handwriting even when you haven't written anything down.

"I'm going to give myself up," I said. "Tomorrow, maybe the day after. Walk into the annex, name myself as the printer of the broadsides. One man, sick, half-dead already. What do they gain by hunting the rest? Renn wants a printer. I'll hand him one gift-wrapped."

"And the second press?"

"Melted. Freya saw to it."

"And the network?"

"Untouched, if they believe me."

Marta looked at me for a long moment, and then she did the thing I most feared: she laughed. Not cruelly. That was worse.

"Bram Kavanagh," she said, "you have spent thirty years teaching us that the state is not stupid. And now you propose a plan that requires Renn to be an idiot."

"Renn wants a trophy."

"Renn wants a *network*. You walk in there, he'll have you singing within a week — not because you're weak, but because they'll take Denny first and ask you to listen through the wall." She leaned forward. "This isn't sacrifice. This is vanity with a martyr's coat on. You want a good death because the living has gotten undignified. I understand it. I sympathize. And I won't allow it."

"There are things a man must be allowed—"

"No," she said, "there aren't. Not in a cell. Not in this. We don't get to choose our endings, Bram. We only get to choose what we put into the record while we're still above ground." She stood, straightening her coat. "Die at your case or die in their cell, but don't confuse the two. One of them is yours. The other belongs to them."

She let herself out. The bell over the door rang once, politely, as if nothing had happened.

* * *

Denny came at noon, smelling of river and horse, ice-melt frozen in the seams of his coat. He sat on the counter itself, which his mother forbade and which he has done since he was six, and I told him the plan the way I'd told Marta, minus the poetry.

He didn't laugh. That should have warned me.

"No," he said.

"You haven't heard the reasoning."

"I heard it through the wall last night, Da. You talk louder than you think your lungs let you." He swung his legs. "You give yourself up, Renn doesn't call off the hunt. Renn posts your description in every precinct in Vellmark and starts squeezing everyone who ever bought bread from us. Me. Freya. Jozsi. Marta. The Widow Anselm who buys our misprints for kindling. Everyone."

"The Ministry trades in certainty—"

"The Ministry trades in *fear*, and you'd be handing them a barrel of it." He was angry now, and I could hear his mother in his voice, which stopped me where I sat. "You want to know what happens? Freya stands in this shop alone, watching the door, waiting for the knock. You want to leave her *that*? After everything we've done to keep her from exactly that?"

"She'll have you—"

"She'll have a brother who's on the ice four nights out of seven, and a resistance cell that needs him there, and a father in a cell whose only use to anyone is as a hostage against her." He slid off the counter and crouched in front of me, and his anger went out all at once, the way it does, and left just the boy. "Da. Stay. Be stubborn. Be a burden. Be alive and in the way. That's worth more than a corpse with a clean story."

I looked at him — at the ice-scars on his knuckles, the ink under his nails that he'd never wash out entirely no matter how much river water he stood in — and I understood that I had raised children who could out-argue me, and that this was the only real success of my life.

"Your mother would have agreed with you," I said.

"She'd have said it meaner."

"Yes," I said. "She would have."

He squeezed my shoulder, careful, the way you handle a forme you're afraid of disturbing, and went back out into the cold to ferry other people's hopes across breaking ice.

* * *

Which left the afternoon, and the chair, and the whistling lungs, and me alone with the thought I hadn't yet spoken aloud to anyone:

They were both right, and it changed nothing.

Because here is what neither of them knows, what I have carried since the doctor in Tallow Lane told me in October, in that gentle voice doctors use when they're really reading you a bill: I have perhaps until spring. Perhaps less. The rot is past arguing with. So the question was never *shall I die* — the question was only *where shall I be standing when I do*, and *what will my hands be holding*.

A cell holds nothing. A case holds everything.

I got up — slowly, honestly, with the small shameful sounds of a man negotiating with his own body — and crossed to Freya's corner of the shop. My daughter works like her mother worked: silently, precisely, with her whole attention folded into her hands. She was at the stone, setting something, and when she heard me she didn't startle, only shifted her shoulders to make room beside her, which is its own kind of welcome.

"What are you composing?" I asked.

"Nothing," she said, too quickly, and angled her body to hide the stick.

Now, I have been lied to by professionals. Inspectors, tax assessors, census takers, one memorable bishop. None of them lie better than my daughter, and none of them lie worse, because none of them blush at the ears.

I waited. She lasted four seconds.

"It's nothing," she said again. "It's — a project."

"A project."

"For later. When there's time."

I reached past her and turned the composing stick toward the light, and there it was, set in eight-point Baskerville with her mother's old precision: *In the year of the famine, 1831, the state counted the households of Vellmark and found forty-one mouths it did not wish to feed.*

I read it twice. Then I read the galley behind it, and the one behind that. Names. Dates. The woman on the deed from 1828 — her name, spelled out whole, in metal, for the first time in eleven years. Denny's route across the ice. The strike at the mills. The strikers Renn's soldiers broke. Our own history, set line by line like a confession, except that every word of it was true.

"When," I said, and my voice failed on the word, and I had to begin again. "When were you going to tell me?"

"When it was done." She took the stick back gently. "Papa. Sit down."

I sat. On the floor, as it happened; my legs made the decision for me. She knelt beside me among the spilled sort, and I laughed until the laughing turned into coughing, and she held my forehead the way her mother used to.

"You're setting the true history," I managed. "Names and all."

"Somebody has to be in the record," she said. "Since nobody else will put us there."

* * *

We argued about it, of course. That evening, with Marta recalled from wherever Martas go, and Jozsi sent home early looking wounded, and Denny dripping on the rug. Marta called it suicide by pamphlet. Denny pointed out — fairly — that printing ten thousand copies of a document naming every member of the household was a strange way to hide from an inspector who already suspected us of everything. Freya listened to all of it with her hands folded and then said the thing that ended the argument, or at least adjourned it indefinitely:

"Renn already knows about the second press. He told Elias so. We are not printing secrets. We're printing the record *before he writes it wrong*."

And I sat in my chair with my blankets and my dead wife's shawl, and I watched my daughter defend the truth the way other people defend a doorway, and I thought: *there it is. There's the ending after all.*

Not a cell. Not a scaffold. A forme locked tight, ten thousand copies loose in a city ready to catch fire, and my name set in metal by the best compositor I ever trained — including, and I say this with all professional humility, myself.

I am not surrendering. I withdraw the proposal. Marta may gloat.

But I am going to ask my daughter for one thing, and I will ask it tonight, while the lamps are low and Denny is still on the river and the house is quiet enough to hear the ice groaning out there like an old man turning over in bed.

I want the last line.

Every printer knows there is a difference between the body of a book and its final line. The body informs. The last line survives. Let them have the dates and the deaths and the famine arithmetic. Let me set the closing line myself, with these ruined hands, one letter at a time, while I still can.

I have been drafting it all day in my head, between the coughing. It is short. It concerns the woman on the deed. It ends, if I have my way, with a mark no census ledger has ever permitted —

the question mark, turned like a key,

and tomorrow I will ask Freya for the case, and she will give it to me, and we will begin.
