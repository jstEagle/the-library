## 14. Renn's Visit

There is a particular quality to a legal search that an illegal one lacks, and that quality is paperwork. Inspector Halvard Renn arrived at the Kavanagh print shop on a grey Tuesday with a warrant in a leather folio, four soldiers in river-grey coats, and the expression of a man who has been told he will find something and intends to be personally insulted if he doesn't.

I had known for two days it was coming. Renn told me himself, in his office at the annex, while sharpening a pencil with a small knife he kept for no purpose I could ever determine except to watch people flinch.

"You'll accompany me," he said. "Your file on this household is thin, Thorn. Embarrassingly thin. I intend to see what your eyes missed."

"And if they missed nothing?"

"Then you're a worse census taker than I am a detective, and I'm a detective because I failed out of the cavalry." He smiled. Renn's smiles were like drafts under doors — technically present, functionally hostile. "Wear your badge. We want it all proper and lawful."

Proper and lawful. In my experience those words traveled together the way a fox travels with hens — officially as companions, practically as appetite.

So there I stood on the Kavanaghs' doorstep at nine o'clock in the morning, badge pinned to my coat like a wound, while Renn read the warrant aloud to Freya Kavanagh in a voice pitched for the neighbors' benefit. She listened with her hands folded, ink still ghosting the cuticle of her right forefinger — she never did get it all off; I'd noticed weeks ago and had stopped pretending not to notice.

"Search of premises," Renn concluded, "for unlicensed printing apparatus, seditious material, and persons evading enumeration."

"Enumeration," Freya repeated. "That's a lovely word for counting people."

"The Ministry thinks so."

"The Ministry thinks a great many things." She stepped aside. "Mind the type cases in the front room. Some of them are older than the Ministry."

I want to record, for whatever tribunal eventually reads this — and someone always reads everything, that is the whole disease of the age — that when we entered that shop I felt the specific dread of a man returning to the scene of his own salvation. Because that is what the Kavanagh shop was to me, though I'd have bitten through my tongue before saying so in Renn's hearing. It was the place where a woman had offered me tea instead of explanations, where an old dying printer had fed me stew and famine-year arithmetic, where a boy had shown me how to hear the ice before trusting it. And now I was walking through its door behind four soldiers and a man who smelled of pencil shavings and other men's ruin.

The search itself was thorough in the way of searches conducted by men who have already decided the outcome. They pulled books from shelves and riffled them upside down, which is no way to treat a book. They opened flour bins. One soldier stabbed a mattress with a bayonet and seemed disappointed by the straw. Renn moved through it all slowly, touching nothing at first, reading the room rather than the objects in it.

And the room, God help us, was innocent. Genuinely, provably innocent. The back room — the press room, the true heart of the house — stood open with its door swinging, containing nothing but candle molds in ranks upon a workbench, dozens of them, pewter-gray, cooling in their racks, and the sweet animal smell of rendered tallow. A chandler's corner. A cottage industry. Freya had even left a half-finished candle burning on the bench, which was either carelessness or theater, and knowing her, theater.

Renn picked up a mold. Turned it over. Set it down.

"Candles," he said.

"Winter's long," said Freya.

"Mm." He looked at the molds a moment longer. There are forty ways to say *I don't believe you* without words, and Renn knew most of them. But a warrant is a warrant, and a candle mold is a candle mold, and somewhere in the gulf between those two facts Halvard Renn's morning went wrong. He turned to the soldiers. "The cellar."

They found in the cellar: potatoes, a barrel of sauerkraut working away at its own opinions, and Marta Voss's knitting, abandoned mid-sock. Renn held up the sock. Three needles were still threaded through it.

"Somebody left in a hurry," he said.

"Somebody got cold feet," said Freya, "and went to warm them. It happens in February."

It was, I confess, the closest thing to a joke I'd heard her make, and I nearly ruined everything by smiling. I converted it into a cough. Renn glanced at me — one glance, unhurried, cataloguing — and I understood that my cough had been filed away in whatever cabinet he kept instead of a soul.

For two hours they searched. They found sedition nowhere. They found a family Bible with a birth register, and here Renn paused, running his finger down the names, and I watched Freya watch him do it and saw nothing move in her face at all, which is its own kind of eloquence. Bram Kavanagh sat by the stove through all of it, wrapped in a blanket, coughing in the wet ratcheting way that meant the lung-rot had gone deep, watching soldiers paw through his life's inventory with the mild contempt of a man watching weather.

"Well," Renn said at last, closing his folio. "You keep a remarkably clean house, Mistress Kavanagh."

"We sell cleanliness by the sheet," she said. "Ink washes out of nothing but paper."

He almost laughed. I saw it happen and die in him, the professional's horror of being charmed. Then his eye fell on the shelf by the front counter — and my stomach performed a maneuver I have no naval vocabulary for.

Because there, in plain view, in a row like good little soldiers, sat the census ledgers. District ledgers, household tallies, the reams of my own careful hand. Including the pages I had falsified. Including the entry where I had recorded the household as five souls instead of six, the date altered by one day, the small quiet crime I had committed to buy time — my first corruption of the Crown's record, committed with a steady hand and a liar's neat penmanship, and sitting there now in plain view like a confession bound in board covers.

"Those," Renn said, "are government property."

"They are," Freya agreed pleasantly.

"I'll be taking them."

* * *

Here is what I did not know until that moment: I had spent three months telling myself the falsified entries were nothing. A day here, a name softened there. Bookkeeping. Everyone rounds numbers; I rounded truths. I had built a whole architecture of *it means nothing* and lived in it comfortably, the way one lives in any house one refuses to inspect.

And then a soldier picked up my ledger and I discovered that architecture had a basement after all, and I was standing in it, and the water was rising fast.

"Those are current district records," I heard myself say. My voice came out level, which surprised me; I'd have bet on a wobble. "The verification cycle isn't complete. Removing them interrupts—"

"Interrupts what, Thorn?" Renn didn't look at me. He was flipping pages. "The sacred process of counting? Counting will survive. It's what we do instead of living."

He reached the Kavanagh folio. I watched his thumb find the page — my page — and stop. His thumbnail tapped once against the margin, where my amended date sat in ink slightly blacker than its neighbors because I had pressed too hard, because even liars have tells, because I had been a fool with a fountain of good intentions and a nib.

Tap. Tap.

"Interesting," Renn said.

"Is it?" said Freya.

"Houses change size, Mistress Kavanagh. People are born, die, run off to the gold fields." Tap. "Dates get corrected. It's human." He closed the ledger gently, almost tenderly, which from Renn was more frightening than slamming it. "What's not human is how rarely anyone corrects anything. Most takers would rather build a lie into the permanent record than admit a mistake. The record becomes a museum of everyone's small cowardices." He handed the ledger to a soldier. "All of them. Box them up."

"Sir," I said, "the district office expects—"

"The district office," Renn said, "will receive a receipt." And then, finally, he looked at me, and what was in his face was not suspicion. Suspicion I could have fought. What was in his face was courtesy, the terrible courtesy of a man doing me the honor of letting me see the trap close. "Unless you object to a receipt, Thorn?"

"No," I said. "Receipts are fine."

"Receipts are fine," he agreed, and smiled his draft-under-the-door smile, and ordered the soldiers to move.

They carried my ledgers out — three months of households, of names, of my handwriting climbing all over the truth like ivy — and loaded them into a cart, and Freya Kavanagh stood in her doorway with her arms crossed and watched them go, and I could not tell whether what I saw in her face was relief or farewell. Possibly both. With her they had always shared a border.

Renn paused at the step. "Thank you for your cooperation."

"You're welcome," said Freya. "Come again when you've a warrant for something that exists."

* * *

We walked back to the annex in silence. Four soldiers, one cart of ledgers, one inspector, one census taker whose career was ending in real time and whose pulse had only just gotten the memo.

At the annex door Renn dismissed the soldiers, took the cart handles himself, and wheeled the ledgers inside like a man wheeling in a harvest. I followed because following was all I had left.

"You searched that house twice before," he said, setting the cart down. "Unofficially. Off the record. Don't bother denying it; Vellmark has eleven thousand residents and every one of them sees everything, including the Ministry's own taker haunting the print district after dark." He opened the folio, produced the warrant, and laid it on the desk between us with the flat finality of a playing card. "Now I've done it properly, found nothing, and confiscated a box of paper. Do you know what that means?"

"That I'm free to go?"

"It means," he said, "that the only evidence in this entire affair is paper. And every page of it was written by you." He sat down. He took out the little knife and began sharpening a pencil, shavings curling onto the desk between us like pale wood chips off a coffin. "A falsified date here. An omitted soul there. A household of six recorded as five, the night after three strangers crossed the ice. You wrote the Kavanagh file yourself, Thorn. Which means when this goes to tribunal — and it will go to tribunal, because I have waited three months and I am owed — the question won't be whether the Kavanaghs broke the law."

He looked up. The knife stilled.

"It'll be whether you did. Whether you were a careless clerk — which is embarrassing — or a conspirator — which is hanging." He set the pencil down. "I've read your file the way you wish you could read mine. I know about the orphanage registers. Famine year. Both parents enumerated in spring, deceased by winter, the state having counted you precisely enough to know exactly how much bread you weren't eating." He said it without cruelty, which somehow made it worse; cruelty would at least have been honest weather. "I understand sentiment, Thorn. I've burned villages myself. Sentiment is cheap. What I can't abide is a man who lets it write in the official record."

"The record is accurate," I said. "As of today."

"As of today it's in a locked drawer." He locked the drawer. The sound of it was very small and very loud. "And tomorrow it goes to the capital with my report. Unless."

There it was. The word I'd been waiting for since the doorstep, arriving on schedule like the ice going out.

"Unless," I said.

"Unless you find something in that house worth the paper I just seized. A press. A pamphlet. A name. Something to make these—" he patted the drawer "—look like clerical errors instead of accomplice's work. You have until the boat leaves, Thorn. After the river breaks, nothing moves south but water and testimony." He returned to his pencil, dismissive, finished with me, a man closing accounts. "Go home. Sleep badly. That's an order."

I went home. I slept badly, per instructions.

But here is what I did not put in any report, and what I am putting down now only because paper is the one place I've ever told the truth: lying awake in my narrow bed above the chandler's on Rope Walk, I did not think about tribunals. I thought about a woman melting lead type into candle molds — because I finally understood what those molds were, understood it the way you understand a thing after it's already saved you, which is the worst order to understand things in. She had known. Days ago, maybe weeks. She had stood in her kitchen pouring her father's alphabet into tallow molds one letter at a time, turning language into light, and she had never said a word to me about it, because she couldn't, because I was the enemy wearing a badge, and she had saved us both anyway without asking.

And I thought about the drawer. That small, smug, locking drawer, holding every household in the district — the widow Hessel who took in seamstresses, the Ferris boys sleeping three to a bed to dodge the head-tax, every small irregular mercy this city commits nightly against its own arithmetic. All of it now evidence. All of it now *mine*.

Renn thought he had confiscated a weapon pointed at the Kavanaghs.

Renn had, in fact, concentrated three months of the Ministry's own paper into one wooden cart, one locked drawer, and one building guarded — I made a note of it on my walk home, professionally, helplessly — by exactly two night porters and a stove.

I have stolen nothing in my life. I have falsified a date, omitted a soul, lied to a supervisor, and loved people illegally, which is the commonest crime and the least punished. But theft — actual, physical, midnight theft of Crown property — that was a line I had drawn somewhere in my character and defended with great speeches to myself.

Tomorrow the ledgers go to the capital. Tomorrow the river starts breaking.

I lay there doing sums, which is all a census man really is: a machine for doing sums. Two porters. One stove. One drawer. One Elias Thorn, who had learned from a family of printers that the answer to a dangerous record is not to argue with it.

It is to burn it.
