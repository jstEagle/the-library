## 15. The Last Berth

The message came the way all the important ones came: folded into a loaf of bread, baked by a woman who had never once in eleven years sold me a loaf I could eat without wondering what else she'd hidden in it. Rye, this time. Dense enough to stop a bullet, which was fortunate, because the paper inside said the thing that had been coming for weeks and that none of us had wanted to read.

*One berth. Vellmark to Torvund, before the ice goes out. No second passage. Children count as half.*

I sat in the cellar of the Kavanagh print shop with the note flattened under my palm and thought, with the particular clarity that arrives only when the news is bad, that the baker deserved better than to have her bread used this way. Then I burned the note in the stove, because that was my job — Marta Voss, keeper of the network's small fires — and went upstairs to tell four people that there was one door out of a burning house and five of them standing in front of it.

I have been a smuggler for nineteen years. People imagine it is a profession of courage. It is not. It is a profession of arithmetic. You count boats, you count soldiers, you count hours between patrols, and every so often you arrive at a sum you wish you'd never learned. This was one of those sums. One berth. Five names. And arithmetic does not care whom you love.

* * *

We met at midnight, which was our habit, though nothing about that night resembled habit. The cellar smelled of ink and lamp oil and the sweet rot of Bram's lungs, which followed him everywhere now like a dog too loyal to leave. He sat in the corner on the good chair they kept for him, wrapped in two blankets, breathing in the shallow careful way of a man who has learned to treat air as rationed goods. Denny stood by the stairs with his arms crossed, still smelling faintly of river, because Denny always smelled faintly of river; I suspected he slept with his boots wet out of superstition. Jozsi sat on an upturned crate with his knees drawn up, seventeen years old and trying very hard to look like someone who understood what was being decided. Freya stood apart from all of them by the type cases, one hand resting on the frame as if the metal were a pulse she was checking.

And me. Standing with the empty bread plate, because I am the sort of woman who cannot bear to deliver bad news without something in her hands.

"One berth," I said. "Torvund run, the *Griet*, leaving before the ice breaks. The captain will take one adult or one adult and two children. That is the whole of it."

Silence, of course. There is always silence after numbers like that. People believe silence means nothing is happening, but silence is where everything happens — the bargaining, the grief, the cowardice, the courage, all of it conducted in the dark behind people's eyes before anyone says a word aloud.

It was Denny who broke it, because Denny has never once in his life let silence do its work.

"Then it's me," he said. "That's settled. I'm the network south of the river. If the cell's going to survive the spring, someone has to carry it, and that someone is me. I know the routes, I know the names, I know which harbormaster drinks and which one prays."

"You know the routes," I said. "You don't know Torvund. Nobody down there knows your face, which cuts both ways, and the southern cells are being rolled up one by one — we lost Ghent's people in January, all of them, because their courier talked after three days of questioning. Three days, Denny. Do you talk after three days?"

"I don't talk after any days."

"Everyone talks after some days." I said it flatly, without apology, because it was true and because kindness, in that room, would have been a cruelty. "The question isn't who wants to go. Everyone wants to go. The question is who *must* go, and who can be spared, and those are different lists."

Freya spoke from the type case without turning around. "Bram can't travel."

Everyone looked at her. She still hadn't turned around. Her hand hadn't moved from the frame.

"He can't walk to the boat," she said, "let alone survive a week in a hold. And he won't leave anyway, so we can stop pretending he's on the list and start admitting why he isn't."

"I'm standing right here," Bram said mildly, "and capable of deciding these things myself."

"Papa."

"I'm dying, Freya. Slowly, unglamorously, and on my own schedule, which happens to be slower than the ice's. Put me down as staying. Not as a sacrifice — as a man who prefers to finish his work at home." He coughed then, the deep wet cough, and when it passed he smiled at us all with the terrible serenity of a man who has already made his peace and now finds other people's unrest tedious. "Besides. Someone should be here when the city rises. It's going to rise. You can smell it."

Nobody argued. That was the strange part. We had all been dancing around Bram's death for months, and here he'd simply set it on the table like a dish, and none of us could pick it up. Freya finally turned around. Her face did the thing I'd seen it do before, the thing that made her so dangerous: nothing. Complete, disciplined nothing. A face like a closed ledger.

"So that leaves four," Jozsi said quietly. "For one berth."

"That leaves three who might fit through a hatch," I corrected, because Jozsi was six feet of elbows and growing. "And one of them is a child."

"I'm not—"

"The daughter," Denny said. "Ilse. The widow Anselm's girl. She's nine. She's on the list, Marta. Tell them she's on the list."

She was on the list. Of course she was on the list. Her mother had gone into the river in November — taken by the patrol boats, not by the ice, though officially it was the ice — and Ilse had been living since then in the loft above the cooper's shop, fed and hidden by the cell, waiting for exactly this boat, because when we took her mother into the network we took her daughter too, and I do not break my promises even when the arithmetic turns against me. Especially then. Break that promise once and no mother ever trusts you again, and a network runs on mothers' trust the way a press runs on ink.

"She's on the list," I said. "One adult and two children, or one adult alone. Ilse needs an adult with her. So whoever goes, if it's the pair berth, goes with Ilse and takes one more child besides."

Jozsi looked up sharply. He wasn't stupid. Seventeen, half-feral, ink permanently under his nails, but not stupid.

"There's only one child on the list," he said. "Me."

* * *

After that the meeting stopped being a meeting and became a knife fight conducted in whispers, because Renn had soldiers walking the district in pairs and we had learned to argue the way conspirators argue: mouths barely moving, voices pitched below the creak of the floorboards, fury compressed into a space the size of a confessional.

Denny wanted himself on the boat. His argument was strategy: he carried the network's future, the contacts, the codes, the whole living memory of nine years of crossings. Send him south and the resistance survived the crackdown with its spine intact. It was a good argument. It was also the argument of a man who had spent nine years running toward danger and had recently discovered, via a census taker and a burning district, that danger had started running toward his family. He wanted out and couldn't say so, so he dressed the wanting in duty, and duty is the most convincing disguise there is because the wearer believes it too.

I told him no. I told him the network needed him *here*, on the ice, for the last crossings, and that the southern cells were compromised past saving anyway, and both things were true, and neither was the real reason. The real reason was that sending Denny meant telling Freya her brother had been chosen over everyone else by a committee that included me, and I did not want to watch her face while I said it.

Freya wanted nobody. That was her opening position and, I suspected, her closing one. She argued that the berth was a trap — that the *Griet*'s captain had been seen drinking with a customs officer, that the whole offer had come down the chain too conveniently, that Renn was smart enough to bait a hook with hope. It was a fair suspicion. It was also, I thought, wrong; my contact in Torvund was solid, and hope doesn't need baiting when the truth is already desperate. But I let her make the argument, because making arguments was how Freya kept herself from screaming. I recognized the technique. I invented half of it.

And then there was Jozsi, who wanted to give the berth away, and gave the performance of his life doing it.

"Send the widow Anselm's girl with someone, send whoever, I'll stay, I'm useful here, I can keep the press running—" and Bram cut across him from the corner with a voice like a bell through fog:

"Boy. Sit down and be honest for one minute. You're seventeen. You've been printing seditious broadsides since you were fourteen and carrying messages since you were twelve. If they take this city — when they take it — they hang printers. They don't hang couriers twice; once is enough. Your name is in more Ministry files than mine is. You are not *useful* here. You are *condemned* here. The only honest thing you've said tonight is that you want to stay, and wanting to stay is not a reason. I've buried men whose last words were reasons."

Jozsi shut his mouth. But he didn't sit down, and he didn't agree, and I watched him fold his arms in the exact posture Denny uses, and thought: there it is. The inheritance nobody plans. Every person in this room had learned sacrifice from somebody, and now they were competing at it like it was a trade skill, like the one who gave up the most won something.

This is the part they don't put in the songs. The songs are all about the brave stand, the barricade, the glorious last charge. Nobody sings about the meeting in the cellar where good people discover that rescue is a quantity, and quantities run out, and someone must do the counting. I have done the counting for nineteen years. Let me tell you what it costs. It costs nothing the night you decide. It costs everything for the rest of your life, every time you remember the faces of the ones who drew the short straw, and you will remember, because memory is the one cargo no boat ever leaves behind.

* * *

It came apart near dawn, the way such meetings do — not resolved but exhausted, adjourned by the grey light seeping through the grate, everyone having said their piece and nobody having moved an inch.

"We reconvene tomorrow," I said. "I'll confirm the boat, the timing, the captain. Nobody decides anything alone. Nobody *acts* alone. Am I understood?"

Nods. Mumbles. Denny looking at the ceiling. Freya looking at the type case again, that hand resting on the frame, and I want it recorded — since apparently recording things is the family business — that I saw her face in that light. Calm. Settled. Too settled. A woman who has already made a decision and is merely waiting for the meeting to end so she can go execute it in private.

I have smuggled fugitives across a frozen river for nine years. I know what a person looks like when they have quietly stepped off the list and onto the ice in someone else's place. I arrested that expression in my memory and told myself I'd deal with it tomorrow.

Tomorrow, of course, is the great lie of conspirators. We always assume we'll get one.

I climbed the cellar steps past Bram, who caught my sleeve with fingers gone thin as candle wax.

"Marta," he whispered. "Whatever they choose — write it down somewhere. Names, dates, who argued for whom. Somebody should carry the truth of how hard they tried to save each other." He smiled. "Even if it's only you."

I said I would. And I meant it, which is more than I can say for most of what I've promised in this life.

Outside, the river groaned under the ice — the long complaint it makes in late February, the sound of a thing preparing to change its nature. Two more days, maybe three, and the crossing season dies until autumn. The *Griet* sails with the breaking water or not at all.

One berth. Five names, minus one who'd already freed himself by dying slowly, minus a child who couldn't go alone.

Which left, by my count, three adults and one boy, and one seat, and a city full of soldiers, and a census man whose loyalties had begun to bend in directions no ledger could hold.

And somewhere upstairs in that dark house, Freya Kavanagh was already awake, and already counting — and I would have given a year of my own life to know what number she'd arrived at.
