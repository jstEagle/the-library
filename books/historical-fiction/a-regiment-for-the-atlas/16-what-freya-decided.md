## 16. What Freya Decided

There is an arithmetic to cowardice that no one teaches you in school. It goes like this: take one life, subtract every other life from consideration, divide by fear, and you arrive at a number so small it fits inside a single berth on a smuggling boat.

I had been doing sums all week.

The message had come by way of the baker's boy, who was eleven and gloriously incurious about why Mrs. Halloran's bread order was written on the back of a hymnal page. *One berth before the ice breaks. Perhaps two children's weight more. Choose.*

*Choose.* As though the word were a door and not a knife. Marta Voss could make even mercy sound like a requisition form.

I sat at the sorting stone with my hands in the type case and did not sort anything. Above me, through the floorboards, I could hear Papa coughing in the rhythm I had come to dread — the long wet rattle, then the silence, then the rattle again, like a press that wouldn't feed clean. Denny was out, ostensibly delivering pamphlets to the chandler, actually sleeping three hours in a hayloft because he hadn't slept properly since the ice started talking. And Jozsi — Jozsi was downstairs in the cellar, ink to his elbows, pulling proofs of Papa's final edition with the solemnity of a priest handling relics. He was sixteen. He had been a printer's devil for two years and a fugitive-in-waiting for exactly as long as he'd known what we printed down there, which is to say he'd been brave for two years without ever being asked.

And the girl. Anka. Nine years old, her mother hauled out of the river ice by Elias Thorn's own hands, her father shot at the mills last autumn — she was asleep upstairs in my bed, curled around a rag doll Denny had won at the winter fair back when winning things was still allowed. She snored like a small bellows. She had asked me yesterday whether printers went to heaven, and when I said I supposed it depended on their kerning, she laughed so hard she fell off a crate. I would burn down the Ministry of Census with my own hands, letter by letter, for that laugh.

So the arithmetic was not difficult. That was the terrible part. Any fool could do it. The difficulty was only in the signing.

Marta kept the network's book of passage in a cipher of her own devising — columns of figures keyed to names, because Marta Voss trusted numbers the way other women trusted saints. I went to her at the chandlery, where she was ostensibly counting barrels of tallow and actually counting the days until the soldiers at the bridges got bored or got orders, whichever came first.

"Kavanagh," she said, when I told her what I wanted. Not a question. Marta never wasted punctuation.

"You heard me."

"I heard you say something foolish." She set down her chalk. Her hands were enormous, scarred from the mill years, and they rested on the barrel-head like a judge's gavel. "Your brother has been named. Renn's men have his description — tall, dark, limps left when tired. He crosses that river eight nights out of ten. He is the network's spine south of the water."

"He's also the network's future. You said it yourself — someone carries it south, rebuilds, keeps the lines open when Vellmark burns."

"And that someone should be you."

"It should be whoever can survive it," I said. "Denny knows every safe house between here and the coast. He knows faces, passwords, debts. I know type cases and how to lie to census men. One of those trades travels better than the other."

"Freya." She said my name the way Papa used to say *galley proof* — meaning: this is rough, and it will need correction. "You are asking me to strike the wrong name."

"I'm not asking anything. I'm telling you what's already decided." I heard my mother in my own voice and hated it, briefly, and then decided to be grateful instead, because my mother had gotten us through four years of being nobody by being very clear about who nobody was. "Jozsi goes. Anka goes. My name comes out."

"Your father—"

"My father is dying, Marta. We both know the boat is not for him. Don't dress it up as a choice among four."

She looked at me for a long moment. Outside, a patrol went past the window, boots ringing on the cobbles in that new martial cadence Renn had brought with him from the capital, and we both waited it out the way Vellmark waits out everything now — quietly, counting.

"You understand," she said at last, "that he'll fight you."

"He'll lose. He always loses arguments about sacrifice. It's his one consistent virtue."

"That's not a virtue, girl. That's a wound with good posture." She picked the chalk back up. "Give me until tonight. If I change your name in the book, it stays changed."

"It stays changed," I agreed.

Which is how I came to walk home through the falling dusk having done the single most selfish generous thing of my life, and feeling about it roughly the way one feels after vomiting — lighter, certainly, and ruined.

* * *

I should have known Denny would find out within the day. My brother has many qualities — stubbornness, courage, an unerring instinct for the last word — but subtlety has never been accused of being among them, and neither has patience. He learned of it from Jozsi, of course, who told him in the guileless way of the young, bursting with gratitude and entirely unaware that he was carrying a lit fuse into a powder store.

He came at me in the type loft while I was distributing forme, and he didn't knock, because Denny has never knocked on anything in his life including doors, conversations, and fate.

"You struck your name."

"I did."

"You struck *your* name and put in—" He couldn't finish it. He stood there with his fists half-curled, breathing like the river under the ice, and I kept sorting letters because if I stopped sorting letters I would have to look at him properly, and if I looked at him properly I might agree with him, and I could not afford to agree with him. "You put in Jozsi. A boy who's been with us two years. Against me. Your brother."

"The boy who's been with us two years is sixteen, weighs nine stone, and will fit in a fishing skiff without the boat sitting low enough to be boarded. You are none of those things, and additionally you're *named*, Denny. Renn has your description. You'd be arrested at the first checkpoint south of the bridges."

"So I go north first. Overland—"

"—through three provinces with patrols on every road, carrying a network in your head that gets our people killed if it falls out of your pocket. Yes. Splendid plan. Very survivable."

"Don't." His voice cracked on it. "Don't do that thing you do, where you make dying sound like bad bookkeeping."

I set down the composing stick. There was no pretending after that; my hands had betrayed me anyway, shaking too visibly to hold type. "What would you have me do? Take the berth myself and leave Anka here?"

"Anka isn't ours to save!"

"She's a child alone in a city that shoots children's fathers! Whose is she, then? The Ministry's?" I heard my voice rise and made myself come down. The loft walls were thin. Elias slept below — Elias, who had burned a ledger for us three nights ago and was now a dead man walking who didn't know it yet, or knew it and pretended otherwise with a steadiness that frightened me worse than any soldier. "She is ours, Denny. That's the whole point of everything we've ever done. Every pamphlet, every crossing, every lie we've told — it was all so that people could belong to each other instead of to the Crown. You don't get to draw the line at one small girl because she makes the boat sit lower."

"And you don't get to decide alone!" He was shouting now, and beyond caring. "You sat in that cellar for years letting Papa and me carry the danger, fine, that was yours to choose. But this — this is mine. Marta said it herself. The network goes south or it dies. *I* am the network. I know the houses, the faces, the debts, the—"

"—the routes. Yes. You've mentioned the routes."

"You think this is funny?"

"I think," I said, and here my wits finally failed me, or served me — I've honestly never determined which — "that you want to die, Denny. Or something near it. Ever since the mills, ever since Tomás—"

His fist hit the frame of the case. Type jumped in its compartments with a sound like teeth chattering. "Say his name once more and I'll—"

"You'll what?" I stepped toward him. We were of a height, my brother and I; we had fought across this same loft as children over whose turn it was to distribute type, and I remembered precisely where his guard was weak. "You'll leave? Go ahead. Take the berth. Tell Marta to scratch out a nine-year-old orphan and write in the man who's already half-caught, and let's see what the family looks like afterward. Let's see what *I* look like, rowing north knowing I traded a child for my brother's martyrdom."

"It isn't martyrdom to do your work!"

"No," I said quietly. "It's martyrdom to do it *instead of living*. And you've been confusing the two since autumn, and I have watched you do it, and I have said nothing, because saying it felt like kicking a man who was already down. Well. The boat leaves in two days. So now I'm saying it."

The silence after that was the worst silence I have ever stood inside, and I have stood inside the silence of a hidden press room with soldiers walking overhead.

Denny looked at me for a long time. His eyes were our mother's eyes — the ones no record held anymore, the ones only the two of us carried — and I understood, suddenly and completely, that I had hurt him in the one place he kept no armor, which was the place where he believed I thought him worth saving.

"You should have asked me," he said finally, and his voice was terrible in its quietness. "Whatever you decided. You should have asked."

"You'd have argued."

"Yes. And maybe you'd have lost." He turned to the ladder. Stopped. Didn't look back. "Papa's asking for you. He wants help with the last page." A pause. "Some of us are still doing the work, Freya. Even the ones you've decided are expendable."

He went down the ladder and out, and I stood among the type with my heart doing something arrhythmic and shameful, and I did not cry, because crying costs salt and salt costs money and I am, above all things, my father's daughter, a woman who keeps accounts.

But I did not change the book back either.

* * *

That night I went down to Papa. He was propped in the chair by the stove where the light was best, and the formes of the final edition lay around him like fallen masonry. He was setting the closing lines himself, refusing all help, his ruined lungs wheezing but his hands steady as they'd been forty years ago — hands that had taught me that a letter is a small machine for holding a breath.

"There you are," he said. "Denny says you've been playing ferryman with the passenger list."

"Denny talks too much."

"He does. Gets it from your mother." He set an em-quad, precise as a heartbeat. "Sit down, Frey."

I sat. The stove ticked. Somewhere outside, far off, the ice on the river groaned — that long subterranean complaint it had been making for days, like a giant turning over in a bed too small for him, and both of us paused to listen, the way you pause when an old dog whimpers in its sleep.

"Do you want to argue with me too?" I asked. "Everyone else has. It's nearly a family tradition."

"Would it change anything?"

"No."

"Then I'll save my breath for the type." He looked at me over his spectacles, and his eyes were failing but they saw everything, they had always seen everything, that was the whole trouble with him. "You did a hard thing today, and you did it in the ugliest possible way, and your brother will hate you for a fortnight and then he'll build you a house with his bare hands someday, because that's Kavanagh love — we express it as refusal and apologize in lumber." He smiled, and coughed, and went on before I could speak. "But hear me on one point, and then I'll sleep. You gave away your berth because you believe everyone else is worth more than you. That's not strategy, whatever you told Marta. That's just the famine year talking. The year the state counted us and found some of us surplus."

"Papa—"

"Count yourself, Freya." He took my hand, ink-stained and cold, and folded it closed around something small and metal — a piece of type, I realized. I opened it. A question mark, cast fresh, still sharp-edged. "Every night, count yourself. And when the answer frightens you, ask it louder." He nodded at the formes. "Now hand me the last galley. A man ought to finish his edition."

I handed it to him. And beneath us, faint as a pulse, patient as a promise, the river began — very slowly, very surely — to break.
