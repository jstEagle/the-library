## 18. Tribunal of One

They came for me at half past seven in the morning, which I considered a small courtesy. The Ministry of Census and Enumeration does not believe in dawn raids — dawn is for soldiers and poets — but neither do they believe in letting a man finish his breakfast. Sergeant Doyle took my tea out of my hands mid-sip, which told me everything about how the morning would go.

"Elias Thorn," he said, though we had shared a pot of coffee not three weeks prior at the annex, "you are detained under the Emergency Records Act for the destruction of official documents."

"Am I permitted to know which documents?"

He almost smiled. Almost. It was the look of a man who has been instructed to be humane and resents the instruction. "All of them."

Well. That simplified matters.

I will not pretend I was surprised. When you steal four ledgers out of a ministry annex, carry them through a city full of soldiers, feed them page by page into a print shop's stove while the river cracks outside like distant artillery, you have already written your own arrest warrant. You simply haven't set the type yet. Renn had. I could see it in his face when they marched me past him in the corridor of the Ministry annex — that particular satisfaction of an inspector whose arithmetic has finally come out even.

"Thorn." He did not stop walking. He never stopped walking; I think he believed momentum was a form of authority. "You understand what you've done."

"I've done several things, Inspector. You'll want to be specific in your charges. Sloppy paperwork reflects badly on the whole office."

That earned me a night in a cell to consider my wit. I considered it. I found it wanting. I also found, in the dark and the cold, something sturdier underneath it, which was this: I had burned those ledgers on purpose, every falsified entry and every true one, and given another hour and a free hand I'd have burned the whole annex down to its foundation stones. Whatever they intended to do to me, they would have to do it to a man who knew why.

* * *

The interrogation room was exactly what you'd expect: grey walls, a scarred table, a lamp positioned to make the sitter squint. There is a science to these rooms, and the Ministry has mastered it the way it masters everything — by spending just enough money to make it joyless rather than cruel.

Renn sat across from me with a fresh ledger open before him, a clerk named Pellwick at his elbow with a second one, and between us a pitcher of water nobody offered to pour.

"Let's begin simply," Renn said. "On the night of the seventeenth, where were you?"

"Here's my difficulty, Inspector," I said. "I've spent six years as a census taker. I ask the questions. It's remarkable how hard it is to answer them."

"Then you'll find the room familiar."

"I find it familiar," I admitted. "Though usually there's tea. And usually the person across from me hasn't already decided what the record will say."

Pellwick's pen paused. Renn's eyes narrowed by a degree — a man who measured everything in degrees. "This is not a census, Thorn. This is a tribunal."

"I know. That's why I came prepared."

And here is the thing I had decided in my cell, lying awake listening to the ice go out on the river with a sound like the world clearing its throat. They expected confession. A broken man, a list of accomplices, names traded for leniency — that was the script, and Renn had read it so many times he could recite it in his sleep. So I would give him a confession. I would give him the most thorough, most meticulous, most damning confession the Ministry of Census and Enumeration had ever entered into its records.

It just wouldn't contain the things he wanted.

"Proceed," said Renn.

"On the fourth of February," I began, "in the course of my duties as census taker for District Nine, I called at the premises of Bram Kavanagh, printer, Vellmark Print District, shop number eleven. I asked the standard questions. Name, age, trade, persons resident. Mr. Kavanagh answered them all. His daughter Freya answered most of them better."

"Thorn—"

"You asked me to proceed. I'm proceeding. Write it down or don't, Inspector, but I'll say it either way, and Pellwick there looks like a man who'd hate to miss a word."

Pellwick, to his credit, wrote the word.

* * *

I told them about the famine year.

I hadn't planned to start there. I had planned, insofar as I had a plan, to be difficult in an organized fashion. But when I opened my mouth, what came out was 1837, because everything comes out of 1837 eventually if you live long enough.

"In the winter of 1837," I said, "the Crown conducted an emergency enumeration of the grain districts. I have read the returns. Every mouth was counted, Inspector. Counted to the individual, to the household, to the child. The state knew precisely how many people were starving in each parish, because counting them was the first thing it did."

"And?" Renn said.

"And nothing was done with the count except to determine how little needed to be sent north to keep the granary cities quiet. The surplus died. The arithmetic survived. I know because I was part of the surplus. My mother put me on a relief cart in March and was struck off her own parish roll by April — dead, or gone, or counted into the column marked *deceased/removed*, which is the only column in the entire census that requires no follow-up visit."

The lamp hummed. Pellwick's pen scratched.

"You're telling me your history," Renn said slowly, "as though it were evidence."

"It is evidence," I said. "It's the best evidence I have. You want to know how I came to burn your ledgers? Then write down 1837. Write down the column marked deceased/removed. Write down that a boy of nine stood in a relief line and watched a clerk tally him — *one male child, unaccompanied* — and understood, even then, that the record was not made to protect him. The record was made to account for him the way a warehouse accounts for barrels."

"That is not—"

"It is entirely relevant. Your own procedural manual says testimony must establish motive. I am establishing motive. I am being extraordinarily cooperative."

Renn looked at me for a long moment, and I saw the thought cross his face — the thought that I was mad, or clever, or both, and that he couldn't tell which frightened him more. He nodded to Pellwick. *Write it.*

So it went into the record. 1837. The famine. The column. My mother, name unknown to me now, though she had one — everyone has one, that was the whole point, that was always the whole point — struck off the rolls like a misprint pulled from the forme.

* * *

"Now the Kavanaghs," Renn said, when I'd finished with the cart and the clerk and the nine-year-old who had learned to count before he learned to matter.

"The Kavanaghs," I agreed. "Bram Kavanagh, born 1801, apprenticed to his father in this same district, master printer since 1826. On the deed of purchase, dated 1828, two names appear: Bram Kavanagh, and a woman listed as his wife. Her name appears on no other document in the city. Not the marriage rolls. Not the parish books. Not the tax schedules. Not the censuses of 1831, 1841, or the emergency enumeration of 1848, which I personally conducted."

"Because she's dead."

"Because someone wanted her erased," I said. "And Inspector, I have come, over these months, to hold a professional opinion about erasure. I used to think a missing name meant a missing person — a gap, a nothing. Now I think a missing name means somebody powerful paid to have it removed, which makes it the loudest silence in any archive. You don't strike a woman from every record in Vellmark unless she mattered. You don't spend that much ink on nothing."

"Name her."

"I can't. That's rather the tragedy. But I can tell you she existed, that she had a son and a daughter, that her daughter keeps her mother's good shawl wrapped around the type cases in winter to keep the metal from contracting — a detail, Inspector, that appears nowhere in your files, because your files are concerned with sedition and mine are concerned with people."

Pellwick was writing very fast now. I noticed Renn had stopped telling him not to.

"Dennis Kavanagh," I continued. "Born 1829. Listed in the '41 census as a printer's devil. Currently employed — officially — as a carter. Unofficially, he knows the river ice better than the harbor pilot knows deep water. In February alone I observed him conduct three crossings with a total of—" I stopped. Considered. "No. Strike that. I observed him conduct crossings. The number is irrelevant, because I wrote none of them down, and a census taker's oath binds him to the record, not the memory."

"You're confessing to dereliction."

"I'm confessing to discernment. There's a difference, and I'd thank you to enter the distinction into the minutes, since no one else in this building seems to keep track of such things."

* * *

We went on for hours. I gave them the presses — both of them, the honest one in the front room and the other one behind the shuttered wall, though I described the second only as "a press," because its type was candle molds now and its formes were ash, and Renn could prove nothing he couldn't seize. I gave them the pamphlets, the broadside naming his massacred strikers, ten thousand copies loose in the city like a flock of black birds. I gave them Marta Voss, by description only. Jozsi, by age only. The cellar meetings, in the general terms of "gatherings," the supper I ate at their table, the stew, the bread, the way Bram Kavanagh spoke of famine arithmetic with the calm of a man reading scripture.

"Why," Renn said finally, and his voice had lost its inspector's smoothness, "are you doing this? This isn't confession. This is — hagiography. You're entering them into the record like saints."

"No," I said. "Like people. That's the entire crime, Inspector, yours and mine. Somewhere along the way this ministry decided that people were entries. Households. Tallies. Irregular or regular, surplus or essential, counted or struck. And I sat across kitchen tables for six years asking my questions and writing my numbers and telling myself the record was neutral — a record doesn't kill anyone, it only counts. But a count is a decision about who exists. I learned that in 1837 from the wrong side of the ledger."

I leaned forward, into the lamp, and let him see whatever was in my face, because for once I didn't care what it was.

"So yes. I burned your ledgers. And now I'm putting it all back — the famine, the dead woman on the deed, the presses, the crossings, the boy who became a man who became a fool who stole four books and fed them to a stove — I'm putting it all back in the one place you can't melt down. Because you'll sentence me, Inspector, and I'll go to the works, and that will be a line in a file. But this—" I tapped the table, tapped the pages Pellwick had filled, front and back, in his neat clerk's hand. "This is testimony. Entered into the record. Dated, signed, witnessed. You made me do it. You sat there and let me talk because you thought I was hanging myself. Read it back someday. All of it. Every word is true, and every word is in your hand now, and the Ministry's own rules say a properly entered record may not be altered or destroyed without tribunal review."

For the first time all day, Renn went completely still.

I confess I enjoyed it. Not triumph — I was too tired for triumph, and too aware of what it had cost. But there is a particular pleasure, known to every printer and every census taker and every clerk who has ever understood what he holds, in watching a man realize that ink is permanent and he has just authorized the wrong sentence.

"You can't be permitted—" Renn started.

"The record stands," I said. "Your own regulations, Chapter Three. Ask Pellwick. He'll know the citation; he copies them often enough."

Pellwick, wonderfully, horribly, knew the citation.

* * *

They took me back to the cell after that. Renn did not come down to gloat or threaten, which I took as the sincerest compliment available to him. Through the high barred window I could hear the river going out — the great plates of ice shouldering and splitting, the groan and boom of a winter surrendering — and somewhere eastward, faint under it, the sound of a crowd. Not a mob yet. A crowd gathering its arguments.

A guard brought me bread and water, actual bread, which I chose to interpret as professional respect.

I sat on the bunk and thought about Freya Kavanagh, who was at that moment — I was sure of it, as sure as I'd ever been of anything — standing at a case of type with ink on her fingers, setting the truth letter by letter the way I had set it tonight, word by spoken word. Two presses now, hers and mine. Hers printed faster. But mine had a distribution the Ministry itself maintained, in triplicate, under seal, immune by law from burning — a law written, I suspected, by men who never imagined it would be turned around and aimed at them.

They would come in the morning with questions. Accomplices, they'd demand. Names. Give us the cell, the network, the woman Voss, the boy Jozsi, and perhaps the tribunal shows mercy.

And I lay back on the bunk, and smiled at the ceiling like a man with a private joke, and rehearsed my answer until I could deliver it in my sleep:

*I'll name every soul in this file, Inspector. Every one. Starting with the woman on the deed.*
