## 19. The Final Edition

The riot reached us as sound first — a tide of it rolling down Cooper's Row, glass breaking somewhere near the chandlery, and under all of it that low animal hum a crowd makes when it has stopped being a crowd and become a weather system. I stood at the shuttered window of the back room with my ear pressed to the wood like a girl eavesdropping on her own funeral.

"They've found the annex empty," Jozsi said behind me. He was fourteen and had the nerve structure of wet paper, except when there was work to be done, at which point he became the steadiest creature in Vellmark. "They're saying the ledgers burned. They're saying the Ministry lost four years of counts for the whole district."

"Good," I said.

"Good? Freya, they're burning Renn in effigy on the bridge. There's a man selling roasted chestnuts *to the mob*. It's carnival."

"Then we'd better give them something to read while they celebrate."

I turned back to the press. My father's second press — the secret one, the one that had lived behind false shelving since before Jozsi could read — stood with its bed cleared, waiting. On the composing stone lay the forme we had been setting for three days: Bram's history, the true one, set in ten-point Caslon because my father had insisted that a lie deserves display type but truth should come to you quietly, like an honest clerk.

Ten thousand copies. I had done the arithmetic until the numbers stopped frightening me. At our best pace, with two of us working the press in shifts, that was thirty hours. Thirty hours during which the city outside would either burn down or rise up, and no way to know which until we opened the door.

Jozsi was already tying his apron. The boy had learned to smell a deadline the way dogs smell rain.

* * *

You should understand what we were printing, because I have spent my whole life learning that what a thing says matters less than who is allowed to say it.

Bram Kavanagh's history began in 1828, with a woman named Ilse Varga signing her name — her own name, in her own hand — to the deed of a print shop on Cooper's Row. Not *Freya's mother*, the way the parish rolls had it after they struck her through. Not *wife of Bram*. Her name first, her hand first, her ink. The state had spent nineteen years teaching Vellmark that she had never existed, and my father had spent those same nineteen years keeping her alive the only way he knew how: in type, in memory, in the discipline of small true sentences.

The pamphlet went on from there. The famine year, when the census takers counted every mouth in the river districts and the Ministry used those counts to decide which neighborhoods would eat and which would be left to "self-correct." The strikers at the tannery wharf whom Inspector Halvard Renn had ordered fired upon — eleven dead, their names listed one by one, because my father believed a massacre without names was only arithmetic. Denny's ice road, told plainly at last, route and season and all, so that no future government could pretend it hadn't happened. And mine. My name too, set by my father's hands: *composed and corrected by Freya Kavanagh, daughter of Ilse Varga.*

I had argued against that last line. He had overruled me with the particular deafness of dying men who have run out of time for caution.

"A name in print is a debt," he told me, standing over the stone with his hand braced on it because his lungs no longer trusted him upright unassisted. "You don't get to choose whether you owe it. Only whether you pay."

* * *

We worked through the night with the shutters closed and the stove fed low, because heat makes ink misbehave and also because the whole shop smelled of smoke lately anyway and I did not want to know if any of it came through our own chimney.

The rhythm of a handpress is a mercy. Pull, release, turn, pull. You cannot think about tribunals or soldiers or your brother on a boat at dawn while your arms are doing that work; the body refuses the luxury. Jozsi ran the tympan and I pulled the lever, and between pulls we talked the way people talk when silence would be worse — about nothing, about everything, about whether chestnut-sellers at riots were entrepreneurs or opportunists (Jozsi said both; I said the word he wanted was *Vellmarkers*).

By the third hour the pile of printed sheets stood taller than the boy. By the sixth it stood taller than me. We stacked them in bundles of fifty, tied them with the twine Denny used for his cargo crates, and slid them into the cellar hatch for distribution before dawn — Marta's network would take them from there, into bakeries and chapels and the pockets of coats left hanging in taverns, so that by morning the city would wake up wearing its own history.

Somewhere past midnight my father came down.

He had been sleeping — or lying down, which in the lung-rot is a negotiation rather than a rest — and he appeared at the foot of the stairs in his nightshirt and coat, moving with the careful slowness of a man carrying water across ice. I moved to help him and he waved me off with an expression I knew well, the one that said *I am still the printer here*.

"How many?" he asked.

"Four thousand. Maybe forty-five hundred."

He looked at the stack a long moment. Then he looked at the forme still locked in its chase — the last pages, the ending, the part we had not yet printed because he had refused to let us set the final line without him.

"Pull me a proof," he said, "of everything but the last line. I want to see her name."

I did. He held the damp sheet close to the lamp, tilting it so the ink caught the light, and read it the way he read everything: twice, silently, lips moving on the second pass. When he came to *Ilse Varga* his thumb settled under the words and stayed there, the way you'd touch a face.

"Good," he said finally, in the voice of a man accepting a delivery. "That will hold."

* * *

It was nearly four in the morning when he set the final line himself.

He had to sit to do it. He sat on the low stool before the type case with Jozsi holding the candle and me pretending not to hover, and his hands — which had shaken for months lifting a teacup — went into the case steady as church bells. That is the thing about craft. It lives deeper than the flesh. His fingers knew where every character slept, and they woke them one by one without a single fumble.

I read the line upside down as he set it, letter by letter, and by the time I understood what it said my eyes had fogged over entirely.

*Recorded, remembered, real.*

Elias's question, turned inside out. The question the census man had asked that frozen February morning — *and the woman on the deed?* — answered at last, publicly, in ten-point Caslon, in ten thousand copies that would be loose in the city before the sun rose. I do not know what my father intended by it. I know what I felt, which was that somewhere across the city, in some cell or annex or tribunal room, a man who had given up everything for us was going to hear those three words someday, and I wanted him to know they had been set in metal and pulled on a press and sent out into the world, permanent, inked, true.

"There," my father said. He locked the chase with two taps of the mallet, gentle ones, the way you'd settle a child. "Now pull it. All of it. And mind your impression on the last page — a man's last line should be crisp."

We pulled it. Every copy. He watched each sheet come off the press, counting under his breath, and when the count reached ten thousand exactly he nodded once, satisfied, a foreman closing out a job well done.

Then he looked around the shop — at the formes stacked and waiting, at the bundles bound for the city, at Jozsi asleep sitting up against the paper stock, at me — and he said, "Help me sit down among them, love. My legs are finished."

I thought he meant tired. God forgive me, I thought he meant tired.

* * *

He died the way he had lived: without wasting anyone's time.

I got him down onto the pallet of waste sheets between the formes, and he arranged himself with his head against a bundle of his own history — ten thousand copies of the truth, and him resting on top of them like a man leaning against a wall he'd built. Jozsi woke and didn't ask questions, just brought the blanket from the loft without being asked. Some things you learn young in a print shop: when to fetch, when to keep silent, when the press can wait.

"I'd hoped," my father said, "to see them in the streets reading it."

"You will," I said. "In the morning. They'll be everywhere."

"Mm." His eyes had gone to the ceiling beams, where the light from the lamp didn't quite reach. "Your mother set the first forme in this room. Did I ever tell you that? Before we were married. She pulled the first sheet off this press and it was crooked and she laughed at it." A pause, the length of two breaths. "She laughed at everything. That's how she hid being angry. Don't tell anyone I told you."

"I won't."

"Good girl." His hand found mine and gripped it, still strong, still a compositor's grip. "Freya. The berth. You gave it away."

It wasn't a question. Fathers in this family, it seemed, asked questions they already knew the answers to. I started to speak — to explain, to defend, to do all the things I had rehearsed for Denny — and he squeezed my hand hard enough to stop the words.

"Don't argue with a dying man. It's poor sport; he wins either way." His breath rattled and steadied. "You made the right choice. The children go. That's the whole arithmetic, isn't it? The one the Ministry never learned." Another pause, longer. "Tell Elias Thorn—" and here he stopped, and I leaned in, certain the next words mattered beyond all others, certain I was receiving the charge of a lifetime.

"He sets a good forme," my father said. "For a census man. Tell him he has the hands for it."

And then, because he was my father and had never once in his life left a job unfinished, he added: "Dismantle the press properly. Grease the screws before you crate them. Whoever finds it next should find it ready to run."

He closed his eyes after that. His breathing slowed the way a press slows when you ease off the power, evenly, without complaint, and somewhere in the hour before the rioters' bonfires lit the eastern sky orange above our shutters, it stopped altogether.

Jozsi cried. I did not, then. I sat on the floor beside my father with my back against the forme of his last sentence and I did the thing he taught me, the thing that had kept this family alive for twenty years: I waited, and I kept the record straight, and I did not let the shaking start until the work was accounted for.

* * *

We dismantled the press at dawn, because he had told us to and because grief, in a printer's family, expresses itself as obedience to procedure.

Jozsi took apart the frame while I greased the screws — properly, every thread, the way a man's last instruction deserves to be followed. The type we distributed into candle molds and crucibles as we'd planned weeks ago; Renn's men would find nothing but a storeroom full of wax. But the forme itself, the final edition, the last thing that press ever kissed onto paper — that I wrapped in oilcloth and carried up to the loft myself, and put into the crate with the coffin plates, where it would cross the river north with us or not at all.

Outside, the city was roaring. Through the gap in the shutters I could see the glow of fires along the quay, and hear chanting — not the mob's usual howl but words, actual words, rhythmic, repeated, and it took me a moment, listening, to place them.

They were chanting the strikers' names. All eleven. In order. The list my father had set.

Jozsi stood beside me at the shutter, his face streaked with grease and tears, and when he recognized it too he made a sound halfway between a laugh and a sob.

"They're reading it," he said. "They're already reading it."

"Yes," I said.

Below us in the back room, my father lay among the formes with his hands folded, waiting for the coffin that would carry him and the plates of his life's work across the breaking ice together. Above us, the city spoke his sentences back to him in ten thousand voices.

There was no time to mourn properly. The boat left at dusk, the soldiers were sweeping toward Cooper's Row, and Marta would be at the door within the hour expecting bundles, coffin, and Kavanaghs, in whatever order we could manage.

I washed the ink from my hands one last time at the shop sink — my father's sink, my mother's shop, my whole inheritance in a basin of gray water — and I went upstairs to wake Denny.

We had a funeral to pack, a river to cross, and a regiment to raise.

The last one, I suspected, would take years. But then, so had everything worth printing.
