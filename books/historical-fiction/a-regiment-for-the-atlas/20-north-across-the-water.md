## 20. North Across the Water

There is a particular kind of quiet that lives inside a coffin. I had never noticed it before that night, and once noticed, I have never been able to unhear it: the hush of a box built to keep the world out, which is, when you think about it, the same thing as a box built to keep something in.

Our coffin was keeping in forty pounds of type plates wrapped in oilcloth, and my father's body wrapped in the sheet he'd been born under — or near enough; Mother had embroidered it, so it counted — and every secret we had left.

"You're leaning on the wrong end," Denny said.

"I'm not leaning. I'm steering."

"It's a coffin, Frey. It steers itself."

He was right, of course. Bram Kavanagh had spent sixty years going exactly where he meant to go, and death had not improved his manners enough to make him drift. But my hands wanted a job, and the handles were there, and so I gripped the pine and pretended that grief was a thing you could pilot.

The streets above us were not pretending anything. The city had gone up like a struck match an hour after word spread that the census ledgers had vanished from the ministry annex — vanished, poof, into smoke and rumor — and Vellmark, which had spent twenty years swallowing its anger in polite mouthfuls, had finally choked. We could hear it through the shuttered windows as we worked: glass breaking in long bright sentences, the drum-boom of a crowd that had stopped being a crowd and become a weather. Somewhere north of us they were burning the tollhouse. Somewhere south, the bridge. The print district was burning too, which hurt worst, because those were our neighbors' presses going up — old men's lifeworks curling into the sky like prayers nobody had answered fast enough.

We moved by back ways. Marta led, because Marta always led, a lantern hooded to a coin of light, walking the ice-route alleys she knew better than the priests knew their psalters. Jozsi came behind me with the second coffin handle, seventeen years old and trying very hard to be solemn and mostly managing it, except when he tripped on a cobble and swore with a fluency that would have made Bram proud and fined him sixpence.

"Mind the corner," Marta whispered. "Cart coming."

We set the coffin down behind a wall of salt barrels and waited, four people and one box of secrets, pressed into the dark while a militia cart rolled past with lanterns swinging and soldiers who looked younger than Jozsi and just as frightened. I counted their boots. It was either that or count the things I would miss about this city, and the boots were finite.

When the cart turned toward the fires, Marta lifted the lantern and looked at me.

"Well," she said. "You're still here."

It was not a question. Marta Voss did not ask questions she already knew the answers to; asking them was a courtesy she reserved for people she intended to deceive, and she did not intend to deceive us. She had known since Thursday — since the moment I told her what I'd done with the berth — and she had not argued, which from Marta was a kind of knighthood.

"Still here," I agreed.

"You understand," she went on, in the tone of a woman reading terms of contract aloud, "that if you stay past dawn, you stay for the duration. There is no boat after this one. There may be no boats for a year."

"I can count, Marta."

"You can also lie beautifully on paper and set type faster than any devil I've trained, and I am telling you now, formally, with witnesses—" she nodded at Denny and Jozsi, who straightened like schoolboys "—that the network needs that more than it needs another pair of hands carrying messages. The north gets pamphlets. The south gets silence. Somebody has to keep the south talking."

"And that somebody is you."

"That somebody is me," she agreed placidly. "I'm old, ugly, and unmemorable. The soldiers will search a pretty girl twice."

"She means that as a compliment," Denny murmured. "I've known her thirty years and I still can't always tell."

Marta ignored him with the ease of long practice. She took my hand — hers dry and strong as a typecase lid — and held it a moment longer than she needed to, which from Marta was roughly equivalent to weeping.

"The woman on the deed," she said quietly. "That's how they'll know us now. That's your father's doing, and yours, and I want you to hear it said plainly before you get on that boat: whatever else burns tonight, that doesn't. Recorded, remembered, real. You wrote it. Now live up to it."

"I intend to be insufferable about it."

"Good." She let go. "Take the river lane. The ice is rotten past the tannery but the boat lies off the pilings at Miller's Reach. Denny knows. And Freya—" she paused, and for one unguarded instant she looked her age, which is to say she looked tired in the way only people who carry other people's lives look tired, "—tell the north we held. Whatever they hear, however bad it sounds, tell them we held."

* * *

Miller's Reach smelled of tar and thaw. The river had begun its great argument with itself two days early — the ice groaning offshore in long whale-sounds, cracks running out from the pilings like veins in marble — and the smugglers' boat sat low and black against the far blacker water, a river barge with its name painted over twice and a tarpaulin lashed over cargo that shifted sometimes, softly, the way living cargo shifts. The fugitive woman and her daughter were already aboard. So were eleven others. The berth arithmetic had been done without me, and done kindly, and I chose not to examine the kindness too closely because examination might reveal whose space Jozsi and the girl were occupying.

Mine. It was mine. I had given it away in a fit of what Denny called lunacy and I called bookkeeping, and now here I stood on the shore of everything I had ever known, holding my dead father's coffin, with no berth and no country and no plan more sophisticated than *north*.

I found, to my considerable irritation, that I was not afraid. I was furious. Fear is a fog; fury is a lamp. I could see every detail of the last hour of my old life by its light: the tar smell, the groan of the ice, Jozsi's breath standing white around his head, the coffin resting on the frozen mud like a piece of furniture delivered to the wrong address.

"We bury him where?" Jozsi asked. "I mean — eventually. When there's ground."

"There's ground everywhere," Denny said. "That's rather the point of it."

"He'd have liked a press room."

"He'd have liked a joke read over his grave and then everyone getting back to work," Denny said. "So that's the plan. Both parts."

We carried him up the plank and laid him amidships, beneath the tarpaulin, between a family from the tannery rows and a stack of barrels Marta had sworn contained flour and which I had personally packed with three reams of the final edition. Ten thousand copies, and we'd gotten perhaps nine hundred out before Renn's soldiers reached the bridges. The rest were ash now, or would be by morning, along with the shop itself if the fire kept its appetite. I had watched it start from the type loft window — had stood there with the last forme in my arms and watched orange light climb the face of the building across the lane — and I had felt, God forgive me, not horror but *editorial interest*, the cold professional thought: *well, that's the first edition gone, best make the second better.*

Grief does strange bookkeeping. Mine has never once balanced.

Denny took the tiller the way he took everything — casually, competently, as though rivers obeyed him out of personal regard. He looked older at the tiller. He looked, in fact, like our father, which was a cruelty the evening hadn't earned.

"Cast off in ten minutes," he called softly. "Anyone staying, swim fast."

Nobody laughed, quite. But the tension broke a little, which was the point, because Denny had learned long ago what Bram taught him: that a crew that laughs holds together, and a crew that holds together floats.

I stayed on deck. The others went below among the barrels and the breathing strangers, but I stood at the rail with my hands around the cold wood and watched the shore do its slow work of becoming memory.

The print district burned against the southern sky like a second sunset, one the day had no business producing. I could pick out buildings by their flames — the chandler's, the ministry annex with its broken windows lit gold from inside, the row of shops along Ink Lane where I had bought my first stick of type wax at eight years old and haggled the man down halfpence, feeling like a queen of commerce. All of it going. And somewhere in it, small and stubborn, a figure I couldn't see and didn't need to: Marta, walking unhurried through the smoke with her hooded lantern, being old and ugly and unmemorable, holding the network together with both hands.

And somewhere else — a cell, a tribunal room, a place with good light for paperwork — a man named Elias Thorn, sitting alone with everything he'd burned and everything he'd refused to burn, waiting to be asked the only question that mattered.

I had left him a message. It wasn't much. It was, in fact, technically evidence. Before we carried the coffin out, I had set one line in the shop's case and pulled a single proof, and tucked it into his coat pocket when he wasn't looking — the last time I'd see him not looking — and the line said what it needed to say, in type he'd recognize, in ink he'd know the provenance of. Let Renn find it. Let Renn read it upside down and backwards, the way censors do, and fail to understand that some sentences are addressed to one reader only.

*Recorded, remembered, real,* it said. *Your turn.*

Which was unfair of me. I knew it was unfair. I had handed a man a debt at the exact moment the bailiffs arrived, and then sailed north. But fairness, I have come to believe, is a luxury of people who are not on fire, and Elias Thorn had made his choices with his eyes open, and one of those choices had been to become a man who keeps accounts. I had merely entered the final line in his ledger and trusted him to settle it.

The plank came up. The lines came off. The river took us the way the river takes everything — without malice, without ceremony, with an indifference so complete it approaches tenderness.

The current swung the bow south for one sickening moment before Denny brought her round, and in that moment I saw the whole burning city framed in the gap between two warehouses, precise as a printed page: fire, smoke, soldiers, sparks rising, the annex glowing like a lantern with a liar inside it. My city. Twenty-three years of type and ink and small necessary lies, all of it burning down to a headline somebody in the capital would write tomorrow, and get wrong.

I looked back once. Only once. That was the discipline of it — Bram's discipline, the printer's discipline: you get one proof, you read it closely, you mark the errors, and then you tear up the sheet and set the page again, better.

I marked the errors. All of them. Every name the state had struck, every door we'd kept shut, every supper eaten in careful silence, every year my mother had been *the woman on the deed* instead of Ilse Kavanagh, typesetter, fugitive, wife, mother, ghost — I marked them all, and somewhere under my ribs the new forme began setting itself, the edition I would pull in a free port with borrowed type and unborrowed nerve.

Then I turned around.

"North," I said to Denny.

"Noted," he said, and eased the tiller over, and the boat leaned into the current like a pen leaning into a stroke.

Behind us the ice began, at last and all at once, to break — a sound like the world clearing its throat before it speaks.
