## 21. The Question Answered

They gave me three days in a cell to think about it, which was generous of them, since I had already spent the better part of a winter thinking about nothing else.

The cell was in the annex basement, below the room where the ledgers had lived. I could tell by the smell — dust and lamp oil and the particular staleness of paper that has been counted too many times. Somewhere above me, clerks were still scratching away at their tallings. The Ministry never slept, not because it was vigilant but because it could not bear to be alone with itself.

I passed the time the way I had learned to pass all my time: counting.

Four steps by seven. One window, barred, at street level, through which on the second morning I watched a woman's boots pause — good boots, patched twice, the left one resoled with leather that hadn't been matched — and move on. Marta Voss walked like a woman reading a ledger of her own, and I would have known those boots anywhere. She did not stop. She did not need to. Stopping was for people who had something left to say that couldn't be said in silence.

Three meals a day, each one an argument between bread and water about which of them had been wronged more.

One blanket, government issue, smelling of the last man's repentance.

And one question, arriving on schedule every few hours like a debt collector: *What will you give them?*

Here is what I had learned about questions, in eleven years of asking them professionally. A question is never neutral. It is a hand reaching into a house. It takes hold of whatever it finds and drags it into the light, and the light, more often than not, is a lamp over an interrogation table. I had spent my career believing that if I asked carefully enough — politely enough, precisely enough — the taking would amount to nothing worse than arithmetic. Names, ages, trades, number of hearths. The census was supposed to be a mirror held up to the city so the city could see itself.

It had taken me a winter to understand that the Ministry used mirrors the way Renn used soldiers: to show people exactly where they stood relative to the boot that was coming down.

* * *

On the third day they came for me at midmorning, which I took as a compliment. Mornings were for important cases; afternoons were for drunks and smugglers and men who had burned things in fits of passion. My burning had been deliberate, methodical, almost liturgical — I had fed the pages into Bram Kavanagh's stove page by page, the way he set type, letter by letter — and so I merited the morning docket.

The tribunal room was smaller than I expected. Three magistrates behind a bench too high for the room, so that their heads were cut off by the lamplight and only their collars showed: white, starched, identical. The Crown in miniature. On either side, clerks with fresh ledgers — new ones, unburned, their spines still cracking when opened. They wanted the record to begin again with me. There was a certain poetry in it. I had spent my life writing other people into books, and now I would be written in myself, against my will, in a hand not mine.

Inspector Halvard Renn sat apart from the bench, in a plain chair near the wall, as though he were merely an interested citizen. He wore his grey coat and his grey expression, and he looked at me the way a man looks at a machine that has failed him — not with hatred, with disappointment. That was somehow worse. Renn had offered me a bargain once, and I had answered him with a winter's silence, and now here we both were, spending the interest on it.

"State your name for the record," said the middle magistrate.

"Elias Thorn," I said. "Census taker, second district, Vellmark. Eleven years of service."

"You are charged with theft and destruction of Crown records, being the census ledgers for the districts of Vellmark north, confiscated under warrant on the fourteenth of March."

"That is accurate," I said.

A clerk's pen scratched. The sound filled the room the way ice sounds fill a river at night — small, constant, ominous.

"You are further charged," the magistrate continued, "with conspiracy to conceal persons from enumeration, falsification of returns, and aiding the flight of persons wanted for sedition."

"I am," I agreed, "though I'd want to see your wording before I signed anything. You've got 'aiding' where you mean 'accompanying,' and there is a difference. One is a crime. The other is a walk across a river."

The collar to the left shifted. Renn did not smile, but something around his mouth acknowledged that I was going to be this way, and adjusted accordingly.

"The tribunal is empowered to extend leniency," the middle magistrate said, in the tone of a man reading a menu aloud to someone who cannot pay, "in exchange for full cooperation. You will name your accomplices. You will identify the members of the Kavanagh household and their associates in the resistance network. You will describe the location of any remaining presses, type, or printed matter."

And there it was. The question. The one they had built the whole edifice for, the cell and the three days and the leniency held out like a lamp over dark water. Name them. Give us the hand that reaches into the house.

I thought about Freya then — not dramatically, not the way novels do it, with swelling music — but practically. I thought about her hands, always faintly inked at the knuckles no matter how she scrubbed, and how she had once told me that ink gets into the skin the way truth gets into a family: you stop noticing it's there until someone from outside points. I thought about Denny's laugh on the ice, wild and young, thrown back over his shoulder at the river itself as if daring it. About old Bram setting that final line while his lungs filled up with the rot of forty years at the press. About Jozsi, who was seventeen and had crossed half a kingdom folded into a coffin that smelled of fresh shavings.

They were not accomplices. Accomplices are people joined together in wrongdoing. What we had been was something else, and the language for it didn't exist yet, so the tribunals of the world kept reaching for the wrong word.

"No," I said.

The pen stopped.

"I beg your pardon?" said the magistrate.

"No," I said again, and found that the word had a texture I liked, grain and weight, like good paper. "No, I won't. But I'll tell you what I will do, since you've gone to the expense of the room and the clerks and the inspector's chair. I'll answer questions. I'm rather good at answering questions. Eleven years of practice. Ask me anything about the Kavanagh household and I will give you a fuller account than your census ever managed."

Renn leaned forward slightly. This was not in his script. "Thorn—"

"Begin anywhere," I said pleasantly. "Start with the deed."

* * *

Here is what I understood, standing in that room with my life arranged neatly on the scales before me: the Ministry had one weapon and one weapon only, and it was the same weapon it had always had. The record. The power to say what happened, who existed, who counted. Bram Kavanagh had spent thirty years teaching me otherwise without ever once speaking to me about it directly — that a press is just a census that answers back, that type is a way of saying *we were here* in letters heavy enough to survive the burning.

So I would give them a record. Mine.

"The deed to the print shop at Candlemaker's Row," I said, "was drawn in 1828, and lists among its parties a woman named Sigrun Kavanagh. Wife to Bram, mother to Denny and Freya. In the famine year of 1831, when the state counted the mouths of this city and then decided which of them would eat, Sigrun Kavanagh organized the bread lines at Saint Aldric's parish. When the count came back short — when the arithmetic said there were more souls than rations — she refused to let the surplus die. She bought flour on credit against a house she didn't own outright. She fed two hundred and eleven people that winter. The Ministry's own ledgers record the debt she incurred. Her name appears in the creditors' rolls of the Vellmark provisionary office to this day."

The pens had stopped again. All of them.

"She died in 1836," I went on, "of fever caught hauling water to the tenements during the quarantine. And in 1841, by ministerial order, her name was struck from the census rolls of this district. Not because she died — the dead are recorded, that is the entire point of the dead — but because she had made the state look bad. A woman who fed the hungry when the count said they should starve is an embarrassment to arithmetic. So she was erased. Unrecorded. As far as the Crown is concerned, Bram Kavanagh was never married and his children were never born of anyone."

I turned, slowly, and looked at Renn. "You asked me once why I falsified the entry. Here is the answer for your record: I didn't falsify it. I corrected it. The woman on the deed existed. The correction stands."

"Thorn," Renn said quietly, "you are digging your grave with your own testimony."

"With respect, Inspector, I am filling yours." I turned back to the bench. "Shall I continue? Because I have a great deal more. I have the famine year in detail. I have the names of the strikers shot at the mill gates in January, which your Inspector Renn ordered, and which appeared on a broadside the next morning in ten thousand copies, and which every soul in this city can recite. I have the history of a family that spent twenty years doing precisely what this tribunal claims to do — keeping the record straight — except they did it in ink instead of ash."

"The tribunal," said the left-hand collar, coldly, "does not require a lecture."

"No," I agreed. "It requires accomplices. And I have noticed, in eleven years of professional questioning, that when a man refuses to answer the question asked, he is usually answering a better one."

Silence. Somewhere in the building a door slammed; the sound came down through the floorboards like a knock from underneath.

The middle magistrate leaned forward into the lamplight at last, and I saw his face: tired, ordinary, a man who had probably started his career counting sacks of grain in some provincial customs house and had risen by never once asking whether the grain should be counted at all.

"You understand," he said, "that refusal carries a sentence. The works. Stone, timber, the northern roads. Men don't come back from the northern roads, Thorn. Not whole."

"I know the roads," I said. "I enumerated the camps along them, in '43. I counted the men. Four hundred and six, that year, in the quarry barracks alone. Average age twenty-nine. I remember wondering, even then, what they had done to deserve becoming figures." I paused. "Now I'll be one. It seems fair. At least I'll finally know the number from inside."

"You are throwing your life away for people who have fled the country."

"For people who have *survived* the country," I said. "There's a difference, and I've made a career of differences. Ask me another question."

He asked it. Of course he asked it. He was a magistrate; asking was all he had. And the question, when it came, was the one I had waited three days in the dark for, the one I had rehearsed against the ceiling of my cell until the words wore grooves in the plaster:

"Who else was involved? The woman on the deed, for instance — where is she now?"

And there it was. The question that had started everything, come home at last, wearing its winter coat. I had first asked it of a girl sorting type in February, polite and relentless, one question too many, and she had deflected me, and I had gone home unable to sleep, and every road I had walked since — the ice, the stove, the fire, this room — had begun at that question's door.

I smiled. I couldn't help it. The clerks would record the smile, and let them.

"And the woman on the deed?" I repeated. "She is recorded. She is remembered. She is real. Which is more than I can say for this tribunal, Inspector, or the Ministry, or the arithmetic that let two hundred people starve so the ledgers would balance. You struck her name because she embarrassed you. Well. She embarrasses you still. Her daughter prints it in ten-thousand-copy editions. Her son ferries it across rivers. And I—" I spread my hands, empty, ink-stained at the knuckles where hers had been inked, the last inheritance I would ever receive, "—I carry it north in my head, where none of your warrants reach. You can sentence the body. The record leaves tonight."

The gavel came down. I heard the sentence read — the works, the northern roads, term indeterminate — and it sounded, strangely, like a publication notice. *Hereby entered into the permanent record.* I had written enough of those to recognize the form.

As they took me past Renn's chair, he rose. For a moment I thought he meant to strike me, or spit, or say something that would make himself feel like the victor. Instead he said, very low, "You were the best taker I ever fielded."

"I know," I said. "That was the problem. I took everything down. Including you."

They marched me out through the annex, past the clerks with their fresh ledgers, past the shelf where new volumes already stood in place of the ones I had burned — blank spines, waiting, patient as snowfall. Let them wait. The Kavanaghs were printing.

At the door, the guard took my arm, and I looked back once at the tribunal room, at the little stage of it, the collars and the lamps and the pens poised over clean pages.

They would write down that I refused to speak.

Let them. I knew what I had said. And somewhere south, in a free port, a woman with ink in her knuckles was setting type that would outlast every ledger in this building — and when her next edition reached the northern roads, as it would, as it must, carried hand to hand like bread in a famine year —

I would be in it. Recorded. Remembered. Real.
