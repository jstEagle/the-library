## 1. The Ink That Outlives

The candle had burned down to a thumb's width, and Elodie Thackeray still had the east bank soundings to finish.

She worked the way she always worked after dark: badly. Slow, deliberate strokes, the kind an apprentice would make—competent enough to keep her place, clumsy enough to keep her place *low*. It was a performance she'd been giving for three years now, first to Aldous Vey and then, after the fever took him in the second month of the occupation, to an empty room. The habit outlived its audience. Most habits did.

Outside, Vellacourt lay under curfew the way a body lies under a sheet. The garrison's patrols went by at the half hour, boots on cobbles, lantern light sliding across the shutters like water over glass. Elodie had learned to count them without listening. Eleven paces between men. Two men. A pause at the corner where the street turned toward the bridge.

*The bridge.* She dipped her pen.

On the plate before her, the Pont des Martyrs ran in fine black line from bank to bank, its nineteen arches rendered with the loving precision only a man who hated something could achieve. Vey had surveyed it eleven times. She knew because she'd carried his chains and held his staves through every one of those surveys, standing in the rain on the towpath while he muttered about deflection and load and the way the third pier had settled two inches since the war maps were last drawn. He'd measured that bridge the way a physician measures a tumor.

She inked the fourth arch. Her hand did not shake. She made it shake—a small tremor at the curve's crown, nothing a master would trouble himself correcting—and felt the old shame of it settle over her like a coat. Twelve years old, she'd been, when she first came to the workshop off the Rue aux Laines, and Vey had looked at her practice plates and said: *You draw too well for a girl your age. We'll fix that.*

So they fixed it. And now the finest cartographer in Vellacourt was a plodding apprentice named Elodie who couldn't hold a curve true, and the dead man's atlas sat half-finished under her floorboard, and nobody alive knew either thing.

That was the part she hadn't expected. Not the fear—fear was simple, fear was just the patrol's lantern passing and passing again. The loneliness. A secret kept alone isn't a secret; it's a room you live in with no doors. Some nights she wanted so badly to tell someone—old Marta at the chandler's, anyone—that she'd composed whole conversations in her head, rehearsed confessions no one would ever hear. *I finished the survey of the Saint-Aubin ford myself. He was too sick to walk to the water. I measured it. It's my sounding on plate fourteen.*

No one left to say it to. Vey's fever had burned through him in nine days, and the garrison's surgeon had come too late or not at all, and then there had been the matter of the burial, which she had conducted herself at dawn with a borrowed spade and no witness but the river.

The candle guttered. She trimmed the wick with a fingernail and went back to the arches.

***

The requisition notice came pinned to the door two days later, printed on the garrison's cheap grey stock with the eagle-and-bridge stamp of the occupation authority. Elodie read it standing in the doorway, letting her mouth hang slightly open the way apprentices do when confronted with official language.

BY ORDER OF THE PROVISIONAL GOVERNOR. All stocks of paper, vellum, ink, and drafting instruments within the city are subject to inventory and requisition for military use. Holders shall declare their stores to the garrison quartermaster within three days. Concealment is theft. Theft is punishable.

Underneath, someone in the neighborhood had scrawled something in charcoal and someone else had scrubbed at it, leaving only a ghost of letters. Elodie didn't stop to decipher ghosts.

Inside, Tomas was sweeping—or performing the sweeping, which amounted to pushing dirt around the floorboards while looking mournful. He was sixteen, all elbows and anxiety, and he had been Vey's other apprentice for barely a year before the occupation began. He believed what everyone believed: that the atlas died with the master, that the great work on the bridges and crossings of Vellacourt would forever remain a beautiful fragment, that Elodie was a steady if uninspired pair of hands best suited to grinding pigment and running errands.

"They'll take the good vellum," he said when she showed him the notice. "All of it. You know they will."

"They'll take what we declare."

"And what we don't declare they'll find anyway." He leaned on the broom. "My mother says they weighed every sack of flour on our street. Every sack. They knew the weight flour should be."

Elodie folded the notice into quarters, then eighths, a nervous habit she permitted herself because apprentices were allowed nerves. "We declare the printing paper," she said. "The damaged stock. The sheets with foxing."

"And the rest?"

"The rest is already spoken for. Commissions in progress. Work owed to clients."

He looked at her with that open, worried face, and for one mad moment she nearly told him. The words climbed her throat like water climbing a well-shaft. *Tomas, the atlas isn't dead. I finish it at night. I've done six plates. I can do the rest. I need help—someone to grind ink, someone to watch the door—*

She swallowed it. Not because she distrusted him, exactly. Because a secret shared is a secret halved, and halved again, and eventually it's just gossip wearing a coat. Because Vey had taught her that. *A map is power,* he used to say, warming his hands over the brazier in winter. *And power that everyone holds is power no one holds.* She had thought he meant it as craft wisdom. Now, with him nine months in the ground, she suspected he'd simply been describing himself.

"We'll manage," she said.

***

They came on the third day, as promised. Two clerks and four soldiers, filling out inventories with the bored efficiency of men who had requisitioned a hundred workshops and would requisition a hundred more. The senior clerk was a narrow man with ink-stained cuffs and a voice like dry paper; he ran his finger along the shelf of vellum and asked questions without looking up.

"Drafting instruments?"

"Two compasses, a rule, dividers," Elodie said. "Master's set. One's broken."

"Printed maps? Surveys? Anything of strategic value?"

"Apprentice copies. Practice work." She gestured at the wall where she'd pinned a dozen of her deliberately mediocre exercises—the kind of thing that had fooled Vey's clients for years and now fooled the occupation. The clerk glanced at them the way a man glances at weather.

"Your master's own work?"

"Sold or scattered after the funeral. He had debts." This much was true, or true-shaped. Vey's debts had been real; the scattering had been Elodie's own doing, three frantic nights of moving plates into the false bottom of the map chest, behind the drawer of ruined copper, under the floorboard beneath the cold hearth.

The clerk wrote something. The soldiers stacked paper. One of them picked up a finished plate of hers from the drying rack—the north riverfront, inks still faintly tacky—and held it at arm's length with the expression of a man examining a fish.

"This any good?"

"It's mine," Elodie said, and let her voice go flat and apologetic. "It's not much."

He grunted and put it down. That was the trick of it, she thought, watching him turn away. You didn't hide the work. You hid yourself inside it, the way a smuggler hides contraband in a shipment of something worthless. No one searches the worthless.

They left with most of the printing stock, all the spare vellum, and a receipt written in triplicate that Tomas held afterward like a wound dressing. When the door closed behind the soldiers, he sat down heavily on his stool.

"That's the commissions ruined. Half those clients will want proof of delivery by spring."

"We'll write to them."

"With what paper?"

"I'll buy some." From whom, she didn't say. There was a stationer on the island side who sold under the counter, whose prices had tripled and whose conscience had halved, and whose shop Elodie could find blindfolded, though officially she'd never been.

Tomas rubbed his face. "Do you ever think about leaving? Before the walls close properly. My uncle has a farm past the hills. He'd take us both."

She thought about the floorboard. About the seven plates hidden under it, each one a month of candle-hours, each one a piece of a city drawn truly for the first time in forty years. About the eighth, unfinished, waiting.

"No," she said.

He nodded slowly, as if she'd given an answer to a different question, and maybe she had. He picked up the broom again. Outside, the patrol passed. Eleven paces, two men, the pause at the corner.

***

That night she worked until the candle was a puddle.

Plate fifteen was the last of the western approaches—the towpath, the ferry landing, the marsh edge where the river spread wide and shallow and lied about its depth. Vey's pencil underlay was complete; her job was the inking, the fine black truth laid over the grey speculation. She worked fast now, faster than she ever worked in daylight, because the performance was off and the real hand came out. Curves true. Lines confident. The river rendered the way the river actually behaved, which was to say: not the way the garrison's charts said it behaved.

Because that was the thing she hadn't told Corvin—no. That name belonged to tomorrow. That was the thing she hadn't told anyone: the atlas wasn't merely beautiful. Somewhere in these plates, buried in soundings and marginal figures she'd been transcribing without understanding, Aldous Vey had hidden something worth more than the paper it was drawn on. She'd begun to suspect it in the autumn, when she noticed the depth measurements at certain points disagreed with everything else she knew about the river—not by error, but by system. Errors that repeated. Errors with a pattern.

She hadn't solved it yet. But she was the only one reading closely enough to solve it, and somewhere in this city or beyond it, she suspected, other people might be starting to wonder what exactly the old man had spent his last year measuring so carefully.

The candle died. In the dark she sat still, hands flat on the plate, feeling the tooth of the paper under her fingertips the way you'd feel a sleeping animal's flank.

Then, because she couldn't help it—because she did this every night, the way you touch a bruise—her fingers found the margin of plate fifteen, where Vey's pencil note trailed off unfinished, the handwriting deteriorating in the last weeks of the fever:

*finish the riv*

She touched the broken word. Somewhere out there was the rest of it, in a dead man's intent or a living river's behavior, and she was going to be the one to find it.

Down the street, a lantern stopped at the workshop door and stayed there longer than the patrol ever stayed.

Elodie held her breath in the dark, one hand still resting on the unfinished river.
