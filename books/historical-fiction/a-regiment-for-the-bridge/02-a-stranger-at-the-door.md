## 2. A Stranger at the Door

The knock came at dusk, three raps spaced too evenly, like a man counting.

Elodie set down her pen before the knock finished. That was the first lesson Vey had ever taught her—*the hand betrays the heart, girl; still the heart and the hand follows*—and in four years of occupation she had learned to still her heart faster than any apprentice in Vellacourt. She capped the inkwell. She slid the plate she was working—the third bridge, the one with the soundings that did not match anything Vey had ever published—beneath the false bottom of the drying cabinet, which smelled of oak gall and old lies. She pulled Tomas's half-finished practice chart over the empty space on the desk, a child's rendering of the harbor, all wobbly coastlines and a sea serpent drawn where the customs house should be.

Then she opened the door.

The man standing in it was rain to the knees. He wore a drover's oilskin gone gray with wear, and beneath its hood his face was a map of its own: a scar running from the corner of his left eye down across the cheekbone, old and white, the kind that came from a blade drawn slowly rather than struck fast. He had river mud to mid-calf and eyes that did not move the way tired travelers' eyes moved. They went past her, into the workshop, cataloguing it—one glance, two—and came back to her face.

"Madam," he said. "I'm looking for the hand of Aldous Vey."

* * *

She had rehearsed this. Not this exact moment, but its shape. Every night for four years she had rehearsed some version of it: a knock, a question, a soldier's boot in the door. And every rehearsal ended the same way, with the same flat, plodding voice she had worn like a smock since the day they buried an empty coffin behind Saint Aubin's wall.

"The hand of—" She let confusion gather on her face like fog. "Master Aldous Vey is dead, sir. Drowned last spring, when the pontoon at Graveline went out. There was a service."

"I know there was a service." He said it without malice, which was worse. "I'm asking after his hand. His work. Maps, letters, plates. Anything in his script."

"His script drowned with him," Elodie said. "The garrison took what was worth taking. What was left is mine to sell or burn, and there's little enough of either." She gestured vaguely at the shop behind her, at the racks of town plans for pilgrims and the cheap coastal charts that hadn't been updated since before the war. Let him look. Let him see nothing but a struggling trade run by a struggling woman.

He didn't look. That was the trouble. He kept looking at her.

"You were his apprentice," he said.

"I was one of his apprentices. He had three, over the years. Two of them are dead and the third ran south with the militia." This was true. Truth was the best mortar for a lie; she'd learned that from watching Vey build his own.

"And you stayed."

"Somebody had to mind the stock."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth—not a smile, exactly. An acknowledgment. Rain dripped off the hem of his oilskin and pooled between them on the threshold stone, and she thought absurdly, *he's marking my door, the way the garrison marks the houses of collaborators,* and then thought, *no—the way you mark a place on a chart.*

"Then you won't mind if I leave something for the estate," he said.

"There's no estate."

"There's always an estate." From inside his coat he drew a letter. Not sealed—or sealed and then opened again, the wax cracked along its seam—and held it out. "When you find his hand, madam—and you will find it, because a hand like his doesn't drown—it should have this."

Every instinct she owned screamed at her not to touch it. Touching it made her part of whatever he was carrying. Not touching it meant letting a stranger stand on her step holding a letter addressed to a dead man, where any of Rennard's patrol could walk past and ask questions she couldn't afford to answer.

She took it.

It was heavier than paper should be. Folded twice, travel-worn, water-stained at one corner. She kept her face stupid and grateful, the face of a woman handed someone else's problem, and said, "I'll put it with the rest of his things."

"Do that." He turned to go, then stopped, one hand on the doorframe, and looked back at her with those cataloguing eyes. "The river's been high this year," he said. "Higher than your master's maps say it should be. Funny thing about rivers. They remember where the deep channels really run, no matter what the ink says."

Then he stepped off the threshold into the blue evening, and was gone up the street toward the water gate, unhurried, a drover with nowhere particular to be.

* * *

She barred the door and stood with her back against it until her breathing steadied, which took longer than it should have.

*Rivers remember.* It was nothing. It was a traveler's patter, a way of filling silence. Except Vey had said almost exactly those words once, crouched over the sounding tables at Graveline, his pen moving through the shallow water-lines of the chart: *Ink forgets, Elodie. The river never does. A good mapmaker lies with the ink and lets the river keep the truth.*

She carried the letter to the desk without deciding to. Her hands decided. That frightened her more than the stranger had—how quickly the apprentice reflexes came back, how her body still obeyed the shape of that dead man's teaching.

She turned it over. The address was on the back, in a small, upright, economical hand:

**To E. Thackeray, apprentice.**

Not *to the household of*. Not *to whoever keeps the shop*. Her name. Her actual name, spelled correctly, including the *ck* that everyone got wrong, that even the parish register got wrong, that she herself had spent four years misspelling on requisition forms so that no clerk would connect the mapmaker's quiet apprentice to anyone worth remembering.

She sat down slowly on Vey's stool.

Her invisibility had been a craft. She had built it plank by plank the way Vey built a bridge survey: the dowdy clothes, the hesitations, the deliberate errors in her spoken sums so that customers corrected her and left feeling clever and unthreatened. Four years of being nobody, constructed with the same care she now gave the atlas by candlelight. And a stranger with a scarred face had walked through all of it in the time it took rain to drip off his coat. Had known her name. Had known—had *assumed*, had counted on—the one thing she would have sworn under torture that no living soul knew.

That the hand of Aldous Vey was not done writing.

She broke the cracked seal.

The handwriting hit her first, before the words. She knew that hand better than she knew her own reflection—the slight forward lean of the letters, the way the crossbar of the *t* hooked like a mooring line, the numerals with their flattened tails. She had spent two years imitating it stroke by stroke, and she could not have forged the life in it. Because it was alive. Whoever had written this letter had written it recently, with a steady nib and a full inkhorn, somewhere dry.

*Elodie—if Corvin has found you, then I am sorry, because it means the search has narrowed to the only door I hoped would stay shut. You will want to burn this. Burn it anyway, but read it first.*

*You have finished more of the atlas than I told you to. Do not pretend otherwise; I count the vellum as I counted everything else. What you are completing is not what it appears to be, and neither, I am afraid, was I. The river remembers, girl. So do I. When you understand what the soundings are saying, come to the marshes—or don't, and let it burn, and live.*

*I never lied to you about anything except myself. —A.V.*

She read it three times. By the end of the third reading her hands were shaking, and it wasn't fear anymore, or not only fear. It was the specific, sickening vertigo of a floor giving way that you had believed was bedrock.

*Neither, I am afraid, was I.*

Four years. Four years of candle smoke and cold feet and lying to Captain Rennard's clerks with a straight, plodding face, all of it in service of a dead man's masterpiece—a work of devotion, she had called it, in the privacy of her own head. The last true thing in a broken city. She had guarded it like a relic. She had guarded it like a grave.

And the man in the grave had written to her from somewhere dry, in a living hand, and told her the relic was rotten at the center.

She heard, distantly, the patrol bell at the water gate marking the change of watch. Two hours until curfew. Two hours in which she could carry the letter to the garrison, hand it to Rennard himself, and buy safety with it—*look, Captain, a stranger came asking, look what he left*—and lose everything else forever.

Or she could go downstairs, uncap the ink, slide the third bridge out of the cabinet, and read the soundings the way the letter told her to. The way Vey had always intended someone to read them, eventually, when it was safe, when it suited him, when the price was right.

*Come to the marshes—or don't, and let it burn, and live.*

"He never lied to me except about himself," she said aloud to the empty workshop, and heard how young and bitter her voice sounded in it. "That's not an exception. That's the whole ledger."

Outside, the river ran dark past the pilings of the great bridge, higher than any map said it should be, keeping its memory to itself.

She picked up her pen.
