## 3. What the River Remembers

The letter was addressed to *Elodie Thackeray*, and that was the worst of it.

Not the hand—though the hand was Vey's, unmistakably, the same cramped italics he'd used to scold her penmanship for six years, the downstrokes bitten short as if ink were rationed even in his flourishes. Not the wax, cheap and unsealed properly. The name. No one in Vellacourt had called her Elodie Thackeray since the occupation began. In the workshop she was *the apprentice*. To Rennard's clerks she was *the boy*—they'd never looked closely enough to correct the error, and she'd never offered. Her own mother had been dead two years. There should have been no one alive who knew the name.

She read it four times by candlelight with the shutters barred and the atlas locked in its chest beneath the floorboards, and each reading made it worse.

*Girl — You were always better at my work than I ever told you. That was deliberate. A mapmaker who is invisible keeps his maps safe. I am sorry to have taught you invisibility so well. Burn nothing. Finish everything. If the scarred man comes back, hear him out before you decide he is a liar. Some truths arrive dressed badly. — A.V.*

*Finish everything.* As if she hadn't already understood the instruction written in a dead man's marginal note. As if the whole last month—the candles burned to stubs, the plates inked past midnight while the garrison drums counted the curfew hours—had been a misunderstanding requiring clarification.

She held the letter over the candle flame. Held it there until the paper browned at the corner and her hand shook.

Then she blew out the flame and put the letter away.

Because here was the thing she could not escape, the thing that sat in her chest like a swallowed stone: if Vey lived, then someone had lied to her about his death. And if someone had lied to her, they had done it competently, deliberately, with her grief as the tool—and the only person with both the skill and the reason was Vey himself. She had mourned him. She had *worked* through the mourning, plate after plate, because finishing his atlas was the only grief she knew how to perform. And if he had watched all of it from some marsh or garret, letting her believe—

She pressed her palms flat against the drafting table until the wood grain printed itself into her skin.

*Burn nothing. Finish everything.*

"All right," she said aloud, to the empty workshop, to the dark. "I'm listening."

* * *

Corvin came back on the third day, which told her something. A spy would have come back the next morning. A thief would have come back that night. Three days meant patience, and patience meant either honesty or very good craft, and she had decided she couldn't tell the difference from behind a barred door anyway.

She opened it before he knocked twice.

He looked worse than he had at the market—she'd seen him there first, three days past, a scarred man asking quiet questions near the fish stalls, too well-fed boots for a laborer, too careful hands for a soldier. Up close the scar ran from his left ear to the collar of his coat like a seam someone had sewn carelessly. He had a courier's lean and a gambler's stillness, and he stood in the doorway not entering until she stepped aside.

"You read it," he said. Not a question.

"I read it."

"And you didn't burn it."

"I considered it."

"That's fair." He looked around the workshop the way a man looks into a church—not the altars but the worn places, the kneeling rail polished by centuries of hands. His eyes found the drafting table, the racks of pens, the great press against the far wall, and stopped there with an expression she recognized: hunger, but disciplined. "You've kept it running."

"Kept what running?"

"The workshop. The trade. The fiction." He turned to her. "Vey said you would. He said you'd ink his plates better than he ever did and let everyone believe you were only copying them."

"Your master flatters himself."

"He's not—" Corvin stopped. Chose again. "He's not my master. I never met the man until eighteen months ago, and I've met him perhaps six times since, always at night, always in wet places. I carried his letters. That's all I am. A road between him and people who needed what he knew."

"What did he know?"

Corvin crossed to the window, checked the street through the shutter gap—a habit so practiced it looked involuntary—and then pulled from inside his coat a packet wrapped in oilcloth. He set it on the drafting table between them like a man laying down cards he wasn't sure won.

"Read them," he said. "They're yours more than mine now. But read them in order. He wrote them in order."

* * *

*(Corvin)*

He had delivered letters to smugglers, to priests, once to a woman who turned out to be the mistress of a garrison colonel, and he had learned to read rooms while pretending to read nothing. The girl was harder than most. Most couriers' clients wanted the package and wanted it fast; this one wanted to understand it first, and understanding took time he wasn't sure they had.

She read standing up, the way Vey had described her. *She reads like the page owes her money,* the old man had written once, and Corvin had thought it a joke until now. She read standing, one finger pinning each sheet flat, lips moving slightly on the difficult passages, and he watched her face do what faces did when a person discovered their entire picture of the world had a crack in it: nothing at first. Then the small rearrangements around the eyes.

The letters, in order:

The first, dated fourteen months back, was almost cheerful—Vey describing soundings of the river shallows, the silting of the old fords, complaints about his knees and the price of vermilion. Ordinary master-to-patron correspondence, except the patron was a name Corvin knew: a factor in the garrison's supply train.

The second was shorter. Colder. *They have asked for depths at the east bank in winter. I have given what any ferryman knows. They have asked again.*

The third was the one that mattered. Corvin had carried it himself, through the marsh causeway in February rain, and had not known what he carried until months later. In it Vey laid out, in the dry language of a surveyor, the corrected crossings—the true fords where the river could be waded at low water, the ones the city's own maps had gotten wrong for forty years because the river itself had moved after the flood year. Knowledge worth a regiment. Worth, specifically, the garrison's plan to move heavy guns across the river above the bridge and take the city's northern quarter without firing it.

And at the bottom, in the cramped italics: *I have sold the river. Forgive me or don't. I intend to live.*

The girl set that letter down very carefully, the way you set down something that might go off.

"He sold us," she said.

"He bought himself," Corvin said. "Same transaction. Different ledger."

"How many?"

"We think the garrison has three of the five true fords. The resistance holds one. Two are disputed—silted since his survey, maybe, or maybe not. Nobody knows, because nobody has the full atlas." He nodded at the floorboards, at the chest beneath them, and saw her weight shift toward it, cat-quick, before she caught herself. Good, he thought. She'd guard it. Guarding things was what she was for. "That's why it has to be finished. Not half-finished, not mostly finished. Every sounding, every correction, every ford. Finished and whole."

"So the resistance can burn it."

"So the resistance can *use* it, and then yes, burn it. A thing like that can't exist in two hands during a war. Vey understood that when he started encoding the truth into the plates instead of just selling it outright. He hedged. Cowards hedge. It's why he's still useful."

"He's alive." She said it flatly, testing the weight of it in the open air.

"He's alive. In the marshes north of the salt meadows. Old, wet, frightened, and brilliant. All four at once."

Something moved through her face then—grief and fury braided together, arriving simultaneously and refusing to separate—and Corvin recognized it because he'd seen it on other faces, other cities. The moment when loyalty realizes it's been spent without permission.

"Why finish it?" she asked quietly. "If he's alive, let him finish his own confession."

"His hand shakes now. And Rennard's clerks requisitioned your paper stock yesterday, which means Rennard is close, which means whatever gets finished gets finished soon or never." He paused. "Also—because you're better at it than he was. He told me that. It's the only honest sentence in all his letters, and he gave it to a stranger to deliver."

She looked at the letters for a long time. Then at him.

"If I do this," she said, "you don't touch the atlas. You don't see the encoding. When it's done, I hand it over sealed, and I never learn what happens to it after."

"That's not—"

"That's the price. Take it or walk out."

He studied her. Six meetings with Vey in wet places had taught him the old man's tells; six minutes with the apprentice was teaching him hers, and the tell was that she had none. She'd give him nothing to read. Which meant either she trusted no one—including, possibly, him—or she trusted the work more than any person, and he honestly couldn't decide which answer he preferred.

"Agreed," he said.

* * *

After he left she sat a long time in the gray afternoon light, the letters spread before her like an autopsy.

Here was the shape of it, and the shape was ugly: her master had sold the river's secrets to the men starving the city, then hidden the full truth of those secrets in the one place the garrison couldn't look—in the plates of an atlas only his invisible apprentice could complete. A ransom and a confession bound in the same calfskin. And she was the binding.

She thought of the marginal note. *Finish the river.* She'd touched it a hundred times thinking it was an instruction about cartography.

It was an instruction about her.

Elodie slid the chest up from the floorboards, unlocked it, and drew out the final unfinished plate—the great bridge, the one she'd been avoiding because it was the hardest and because finishing it felt like a door closing. She pinned it to the table, trimmed her pen, and mixed ink in the dying light.

Somewhere below, on the river, a garrison barge sounded its depth with a lead line, and the leadsman's chant carried faintly up through the floor: *by the mark, five.*

They were measuring her river with borrowed knowledge. Her pen hovered over the vellum.

Fine, she thought. Then they could borrow mine.
