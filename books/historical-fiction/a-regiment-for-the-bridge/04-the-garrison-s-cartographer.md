## 4. The Garrison's Cartographer

They came on a Tuesday, in the grey hour after the market bell, when the fog off the river was still thick enough to hide a man's face and the garrison knew it.

Elodie heard them before she saw them: boots, many boots, the particular rhythm of soldiers who expected the street to empty for them. She had learned the city's footsteps the way she'd learned its streets—by sound, by weight, by the small tells that separated a neighbor from a threat. These were threats. Six of them at least, and one man walking lighter than the rest, almost delicately, as though the cobbles were paper he didn't want to crease.

She had time to do exactly three things. She slid Vey's letters beneath the loose floorboard under the drafting table. She turned the current plate to an innocuous page—the old survey of the Tanner's Reach, useless, finished years ago. And she picked up her burnishing stone and bent over it with the vacant concentration of a girl doing simple work badly.

The door opened without a knock.

"Mapmaker's workshop." The voice belonged to a sergeant, bored and loud. "By requisition order."

The man who entered behind him was not bored at all.

He was perhaps forty, narrow through the shoulders, with the kind of face you forgot while looking at it—unremarkable features arranged around a pair of grey eyes that missed nothing and betrayed nothing. He wore no sword. That was the first strange thing. Every officer Elodie had seen since the occupation began wore his blade like furniture. This man carried instead a leather case, flat and long as a forearm, and he held it the way other men held a violin or a weapon.

"Captain Rennard," he said, to the room rather than to her. His eyes went over the workshop the way she'd seen buyers appraise horses—quick, then slow, then quick again. The presses. The racks of copper plates. The drawers of pens and burnishers. The great south window with its view of the river. "This is where Aldous Vey worked."

"Worked," Elodie said, keeping her eyes down, her voice pitched into the dull apprentice register she'd spent months perfecting. "Master Vey died in the autumn, sir. Fever. I'm only—"

"I know what you are." He crossed the room and stood before the drafting table, looking down at the Tanner's Reach plate with an expression she couldn't read. "You're the apprentice who stayed. There were two, I'm told."

"Tomas, sir. He runs deliveries." Her heart performed a small cold trick at lying about Tomas so casually, and worse, so competently. "I ink the plates. I don't understand them."

"No," Rennard agreed mildly. "Of course you don't."

He opened the leather case. Inside, nested in green felt, lay instruments she recognized and envied and feared in equal measure: a beam compass of German steel, a set of proportional dividers, a magnifier on a folding brass stand. Tools worth more than the whole workshop. Tools that meant someone in the occupying army understood maps not as decoration but as power.

"The garrison requires copies of Master Vey's bridge surveys," Rennard said. "All of them. You will work here, under my supervision. The originals remain in this room; the copies leave with me each evening. Paper has been requisitioned from the stationer on Rue Corbeau—you'll use nothing else."

"Sir, I don't know if I can—" She let her hands flutter, apprentice-stupid. "The master did everything himself. I only fill in hatching and lettering. If I copy wrong, you'd have bad maps, and—"

"And I would know precisely which lines were wrong, because I'll be checking every one against the original." He smiled. It was a small smile, economical, like everything else about him. "You may be surprised what an apprentice knows without knowing it. Hands remember, even when minds claim ignorance. Now. Show me the atlas."

* * *

It took him less than an hour to find the plates that mattered, and Elodie stood by the window pretending to grind ink while her entire future was lifted out of the flat files one sheet at a time.

Vey had kept the bridge surveys in sequence: the Old Bridge with its thirteen arches and its drowned Roman footings; the Ferry Stairs; the Weir Crossing; the fords at Saint-Aubin and the marsh channel below the tanneries. Rennard laid them out across two pushed-together tables and studied them with the magnifier, and Elodie watched his face for any flicker—and saw none, which frightened her more than delight would have.

"This is fine work," he said at last, and there was something in his voice she hadn't expected. Not greed. Respect. "Better than fine. Your master sounded these channels himself?"

"He rowed every line, sir. Took soundings morning and evening for two years. He said the river lies."

Rennard looked up at that, and for one moment the grey eyes fixed on her with an attention that made her skin prickle. "The river lies," he repeated. "Yes. It does. Which is why honest surveys are worth more than regiments." He tapped the plate showing the ford below the tanneries. "Your master marked depths to the inch. Current speeds by season. Do you know how rare that is? Half the surveyors in the empire guess and call it knowledge."

"He was careful," Elodie said. Careful, she thought, and wanted to laugh or weep. Careful enough to encode a betrayal into the very soundings. Careful enough to sell the truth twice—to the enemy first, and then, hidden inside the atlas, to whoever could finally read it.

"You'll begin with the Old Bridge," Rennard said. "Full copy. Arch positions, pier dimensions, waterline marks, all marginal notes transferred exactly." He gathered the plates back into their sequence with a gentleness that seemed almost tender. "The rest of the city thinks we want these maps to loot your bridges. What we actually want is to keep them standing. An army that burns the bridges it needs has already lost. But—" and here he paused, weighing how much to tell a girl who understood nothing, "—an army that holds the crossings holds the city. And an army that knows which fords are real, and which are rumor, holds the war. Your master understood that. I suspect better than anyone alive."

Elodie ground the ink a little harder than she needed to. "He's dead, sir."

"Yes," Rennard said, and there was the faintest hesitation, half a heartbeat, before he said it. "So I'm told."

* * *

For six days the workshop became a place Elodie barely recognized.

Soldiers came at dawn to fetch and return the requisitioned paper. A corporal stood at the door with orders admitting no one else. Rennard arrived each morning at eight and worked at the far table, making his own verification tracings, and left at dusk with the day's copies sealed in oilcloth. The room that had been hers alone—her candlelit kingdom of secret competence—was now occupied territory, and she moved through it performing the role of a lifetime: plodding, obedient, slightly slow.

And every night, alone, she committed treason in ink.

It was easier than she'd imagined it would be, and that frightened her most of all.

The errors had to be perfect—that was the lesson of the thing. An obvious mistake was a cry for help; a subtle one was a knife. So she thought like Vey. She thought like a man who had spent two years learning how a river lies, and she taught the copies to lie too.

At the ford below the tanneries she shifted the deep channel twelve feet eastward—enough that wagons following the copied map would find four feet of water where the original promised two. At Saint-Aubin she altered the seasonal note, marking the crossing safe in high water when Vey's true sounding showed a scour hole deep enough to drown a horse and rider. On the Old Bridge plate she did something subtler still: she adjusted a single marginal depth reading at the third arch, the one that mattered, the one whose true number she suspected meant everything—because Corvin's letters had said the confession lived in the soundings, and she would not hand that number to the garrison even garbled until she understood it herself.

Each night she checked her work against her memory of the originals. Each morning Rennard checked it against the originals themselves.

And each morning, Elodie discovered, she learned something new about fear.

Because fear was not the moment of falsifying. Fear was watching Rennard stand over her shoulder with the magnifier, comparing her copy line by line, saying nothing, saying nothing, saying nothing—and then straightening up and saying, "Acceptable," in that flat mild voice, and she could never tell whether she had fooled him or whether he was letting her think so, the way a fisherman lets a fish run before setting the hook.

On the fifth day he lingered over the Tanner's Reach copy—an old plate, harmless—and frowned.

"This shading is different from the master's hand."

"My work, sir. The master did the outlines, I filled the tones." She kept her breathing slow. "Is it wrong?"

"It's heavier than his." He tilted the plate to the light. "But consistent. You shade heavy when you're tired?"

"When I'm frightened, sir," Elodie said, and let her chin tremble just slightly, just enough.

Something moved behind the grey eyes—not quite sympathy. Recognition, maybe. "Finish your tea," he said, and moved on, and she sat afterward with her heart hammering and understood, for the first time, the intoxication underneath the terror. She had lied to the occupation's own cartographer, to his face, about her own trembling hands—and he had believed her, or pretended to, and either way she had walked away whole. Power, she thought. This is what it tastes like. Like swallowing a live coal and finding you can hold it.

She hated how much she liked it.

* * *

On the sixth evening, Rennard stayed later than usual.

The corporal had gone; the light had gone; only the candle between them remained, and the river beyond the window turning black. Rennard stood at the table where the finished copies lay stacked, and he did not seal them in oilcloth. Instead he turned one plate toward her—the Saint-Aubin ford, her best lie—and studied it in silence for a long time.

"Do you know why I became a cartographer?" he asked suddenly.

"No, sir."

"Because a map is the only honest thing a government owns." He set the plate down. "Every proclamation is a lie. Every speech. But a chart of a river bottom—either the channel is there or it isn't, and men drown either way. I have watched men drown because a surveyor guessed. I decided early I would be the man who didn't guess." He looked at her across the candle. "Which means I check everything. Twice. Three times. Soundings against soundings, season against season. Rivers don't repeat themselves perfectly, but they nearly do, and where they don't—"

He stopped. Elodie kept her face blank and her hands still and her pulse loud enough that she was sure he could hear it.

"—where they don't, there's usually a reason," he finished quietly. "Goodnight, apprentice. Lock the door behind me."

He took the copies. He left. She locked the door and stood in the dark with her back against it, shaking.

Had he seen it? Had he seen everything, and said nothing, letting the coal warm his palm too?

Or—and this thought arrived cold, unwelcome, inevitable—was there another possibility entirely? Because Rennard had hesitated, that first morning, before saying Vey was dead. *So I'm told.* As if he'd been told by someone. As if he'd wondered.

She thought of the letters under the floorboard. Of Corvin somewhere in the city, waiting. Of Vey, alive or dead or drowning in his own cleverness. Of the third-arch sounding she had garbled in every copy, guarding a number she didn't yet understand.

Tomorrow, she decided. Tomorrow she would take the original Saint-Aubin plate and compare it against the letters line by line until the encoding gave way. Whatever Vey had hidden in those soundings, she would read it herself before anyone else—before the resistance, before the garrison, before Captain Rennard and his honest maps.

She blew out the candle. In the dark, the river whispered against the pilings below the window, patient, lying.

And beneath the floorboard, wrapped in oilcloth, the letter she hadn't yet opened—Corvin's second, delivered by a child's hand while the corporal wasn't looking—waited with its single line of instruction:

*He checks everything twice. Ask yourself what he was checking the day your master supposedly died.*
