## 5. Letters from a Drowned Man

Corvin had carried a dead man's letters for three years before he learned the man wasn't dead.

He sat in the boathouse under the rotting pilings of the Fishmarket stairs, where the river smelled of tar and drowned things and no one came after dark. The letter lay open on his knee — the one he'd left for the girl, the one she hadn't yet read or perhaps had read ten times by now. He knew her type. He'd been her type once: the careful ones, the ones who hid their light under a bushel so heavy they forgot it was burning.

She would come to him angry. That was good. Anger meant she'd believed him.

He turned Vey's letter over in his hands. The handwriting was unmistakable even now, even after everything — the small precise ascenders, the way the *r* curled like a wave deciding whether to break. Corvin had spent two years of his life carrying that hand across half a country, through checkpoints and burned villages and one very bad winter in the passes above Sarnholt. He had trusted that hand. Everyone in the network had trusted that hand. Aldous Vey's maps were scripture to men who could not read anything else.

And then the garrison had started crossing rivers where no rivers should be crossable.

* * *

The first time Corvin understood what Vey had done, it was raining.

The resistance cell at Marle Ford had held the crossing for eleven months. Eleven months of mud and fever and holding a line nobody thought worth attacking — because the ford was a lie, everyone knew it was a lie, the water ran deep and quick over a shelf of soft clay and any column that tried it would lose its guns and half its men. Vey himself had surveyed the ford years before the war. His map hung in the command post like an icon.

Then on a grey morning in the third month of autumn, the garrison came across at dawn, four hundred strong, dry-shod, in perfect order, walking a line that no living man had ever walked there before.

Corvin watched them from the reeds with the survivors of the Marle company, and what he felt was not fear. It was recognition. The enemy column moved along a bearing — he could see it, even from the bank, even with his ears still ringing from the guns — a bearing drawn by someone who knew exactly where the clay shelf ended and the gravel began. Someone who had sounded that river inch by inch and written the numbers down in a small precise hand.

Someone who had sold it.

By the time they pulled him out of the water that night, Corvin had stopped thinking of Aldous Vey as a man. He thought of him as a document. A document that needed reading, and then filing, and then — if the reading went the way Corvin feared it would — destroying.

* * *

It took him eighteen months to trace the truth, and the truth was worse than treason. Treason was simple. What Vey had done was architecture.

He hadn't just sold the crossings. He had built them into his life's work, brick by brick, sounding by sounding. The atlas of Vellacourt — the masterpiece, the thing every cartographer in three kingdoms whispered about — contained the true river. Not the river as it appeared, but the river as it behaved. Every corrected depth. Every seasonal shift of the channels. Every gravel bar the floods had moved and every shelf of stone the old surveys had missed. Encoded, Corvin finally understood, in the marginalia and the soundings and the little decorative flourishes of inked reeds along the banks. A complete military key to every crossing within thirty miles of the city, dressed as scholarship.

Vey had sold the garrison the cipher months before the occupation. Then, when the war came to Vellacourt and the tide turned against the men who'd bought him, he had done what he always did: he had made himself a map out of trouble. He'd vanished. Left behind a dead master, an orphaned workshop, an apprentice who believed she was finishing a memorial — and a set of letters, written in advance, timed for delivery, that would let him buy his way back into favor with whichever side ended up holding the city.

Corvin had intercepted the last courier six weeks ago. The letters were addressed to Captain Rennard of the garrison engineers.

Rennard. The occupier's own mapmaker. The man now sitting in Vey's workshop copying Vey's plates with Vey's apprentice grinding his ink.

Which left the girl. Elodie Thackeray, who didn't know she was the most dangerous person in Vellacourt.

* * *

She came at dusk, as he'd known she would, taking the long way along the timber yard so anyone watching would think her running an errand. She stood in the doorway of the boathouse with her hood up and her jaw set, and Corvin saw immediately that she had brought the letter — he could see its corner folded inside her glove — and that she had not brought the atlas.

Good instincts. Better than Vey deserved.

"You're the courier," she said. Not a question.

"I was."

"Was."

"He stopped needing couriers when he stopped being alive." Corvin gestured to the upturned barrel across from him. She didn't sit. He hadn't expected her to. "You've read it."

"Three times." Her voice was flat, controlled, the voice of someone holding a door shut against a flood. "You say my master is alive. You say he's hiding in the marshes east of the city. You say—" and here the control slipped, just a hairline crack, "—you say he sold the crossings. Before the occupation. To Rennard's people."

"I say more than that. I can prove more than that."

"Prove it."

So Corvin did. He laid it out the way you lay out a map, piece by piece, north to south: Marle Ford, where four hundred men crossed a river that couldn't be crossed. The ferry at Ostrelle, taken without a shot because the garrison knew which channel silted in a dry summer. The pontoon bridges thrown across the Seraine at points that made no sense until you had Vey's soundings, at which point they made terrible sense. He named dates. He named officers. He watched her face while he did it, and he hated himself a little for what he was doing, and he did it anyway, because the alternative was a city that starved or burned or both.

"That's circumstantial," she said at last, but her voice had gone quiet in a different way. "Anyone could have surveyed those crossings."

"Not those crossings. Not those soundings." He leaned forward. "I've seen your work, mistress Thackeray. I've seen Vey's. Nobody else alive draws water the way he does — the way *you* do, now, in his hand. And here is the part you haven't let yourself understand yet." He paused, because this was the blade and he wanted her to see it coming. "You are finishing his atlas. Right now, tonight probably, by candlelight, you're inking plates that contain the same cipher. Every corrected ford. Every true depth. When you finish it, you will have completed the single most valuable military document in the province — and it will sit in a workshop that Captain Rennard has already requisitioned."

Her face did something complicated. Grief and fury fighting for the same ground.

"The atlas is a memorial," she said. "It's his life's work. It's all that's left of him."

"It's his ransom," Corvin said. "And his confession. Both. That's what makes it beautiful, in the way a knife is beautiful. If the garrison gets it, Vey lives comfortably on their gratitude for the rest of his cowardly life. If we get it finished — *properly* finished, with the cipher decoded and documented — we prove what he did. We prove it to the resistance, to the city, to whatever passes for a court when this is over. Those two purposes live in the same pages, girl. Only one of them can win."

"And you want me to decide which."

"I want you to finish it," Corvin said. "That's all. Finish the atlas you were always going to finish. Just finish it for us instead of for a ghost."

* * *

She left without answering, which he had expected, and took the letter with her, which he had hoped for. A person keeps the letter that hurts them. They reread it looking for the lie, and in looking for the lie they memorize the truth.

Corvin stayed in the boathouse another hour, listening to the river work at its pilings, and let himself feel the thing he kept locked below everything else: that he had liked Aldous Vey. That the old man had taught him to read contour lines on a frozen hillside with numb fingers, laughing at him, calling him *boy* the way you call someone *boy* when you mean *son*. That the betrayal wasn't a wound in a stranger; it was a wound in Corvin, and every day he spent hunting the old man's ghost he was picking at his own stitches.

Grief was a weapon. He'd learned that much in the war. You aimed yours at the enemy, and you hoped to God you didn't hit yourself.

* * *

Two nights later she came again, and this time she brought a plate.

Not the atlas itself — never that, not yet — but a proof impression on cheap paper, pulled from one of the finished copperplates. The middle reach of the Seraine, the reach below the great bridge. She spread it on the boathouse floor between them and weighted the corners with stones from the riverbank, and her hands were shaking, Corvin noticed. Not with fear. With the specific tremor of someone about to say something they can't take back.

"Show me the cipher," she said. "Show me how he encoded the crossings, and show me how you know."

He showed her. The reeds inked along the banks — count them in groups, the groups were fathoms. The little crosses marking eel traps — real eel traps, but their spacing marked the channel edges. The soundings themselves, honest numbers, but the honest numbers only told half the story; the other half lived in the decorative waves beneath them, each wave crest a marker for season and flow. It was elegant. It was the work of a genius. Standing over it in the lamplight, Corvin watched the girl's face as she saw her master's mind laid bare, and he watched the exact moment the admiration died.

"He was proud of it," she said quietly. "Look at this. Look at the reeds — he *redrew* them three times to get the counts right. He enjoyed this."

"People enjoy their sins when they think no one will ever name them."

"No." She shook her head slowly. "People enjoy their sins when they've decided they aren't sins. That's worse." She looked up at him, and her eyes were red-rimmed but absolutely dry. "There's something else. Something you don't know, courier. In the final plate — the bridge itself, the great bridge — there are soundings I haven't inked yet. Numbers in his margin that don't match any survey of the bridge foundations. I thought they were errors." Her jaw tightened. "They're not errors, are they?"

Corvin felt the cold move up his spine.

"What do the numbers say?"

"They say the bridge can come down." She said it evenly, terribly. "They say someone worked out, years ago, exactly where it would fail. Exactly which piers. Exactly how." She rolled up the proof with hands that had stopped shaking. "My master didn't just sell the garrison our crossings, Corvin. I think he sold them the bridge. And I think Rennard knows it, and I think that's why Rennard wants my atlas finished — because the last page isn't a map at all."

Outside, far off toward the citadel, a bell began to toll the curfew, and somewhere between the ninth stroke and the tenth, Corvin understood that whatever happened next, it would happen fast.
