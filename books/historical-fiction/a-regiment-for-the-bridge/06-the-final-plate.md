## 6. The Final Plate

Captain Rennard had taken to standing behind her while she worked.

He never touched anything. He never spoke at first. He simply arrived—boots on the stair, the creak of the third step that Vey had promised for years to fix—and then he was there, a presence at her shoulder like weather coming in off the river. He would watch her grind ink, watch her lay the burnisher across the copper, watch her hand move through the small liturgies of the craft, and after a while he would say something mild and terrible, like *You hold the burin differently than your master did*, and leave.

Elodie had learned to count his visits. Fourteen in nine days. She kept the tally scratched inside the lid of the ink box where Tomas used to hide his tobacco, and each morning the number looked less like arithmetic and more like a noose being tied one loop at a time.

Today he came twice.

The second time was near dusk, when the light through the workshop's east window went the color of weak tea and the garrison drums sounded the hour before curfew. He stood over the press, turning a finished proof of the Saint-Aubin reach with two fingers, as if it were something he'd caught.

"Your master's soundings," he said. "They're very fine."

"Thank you, Captain."

"Finer than any survey I've seen in twenty years of soldiering." He set the proof down with exaggerated care. "A man who measures a river that well is a man who knows exactly where a body could cross it. Or drown." His eyes came up. They were pale, unhurried eyes, the color of the sky before snow. "Where did Aldous Vey learn to measure like that?"

"The Academy at Torvane," Elodie said, and it was even true. The lie lived most comfortably inside truths; she'd learned that from him too.

Rennard nodded slowly, the way a man nods when he's filing something rather than accepting it. Then he left, and she listened to the third step complain under his weight all the way down, and she did not allow herself to breathe fully until the street door clacked shut.

* * *

The final plate was the great bridge itself—the Pont-Neuf de Vellacourt, five hundred years old, eleven arches, the spine of the city and now the artery the garrison meant to keep whole while everything else bled. It was the plate Vey had left unfinished, and Elodie had saved it for last the way you save a letter you're afraid to read.

She worked by candle because Rennard's requisition had stripped the workshop of its lamps—brass, oil, wicks, all carted off to the citadel—and because candlelight made shadows she could work in. The atlas's original plates were hidden beneath the false floor under the drying rack, and each night she brought up only what she could finish before dawn, copying the true lines onto the garrison's paper by day and inking the original by night, so that the lie and the truth grew side by side like twins who would never meet.

Tonight there was only the truth left to do.

The bridge plate demanded soundings. That was the convention, and conventions were where Vey hid things best. Every depth figure along the bridge's waterline—the numbers that told a pilot how much river lay under his keel—had been measured by Vey himself in the years before the war, lowered from a skiff with a weighted line, recorded in his small precise numerals. Elodie had inked a hundred of them already without thinking. Tonight, inking the figures along the fourth arch, she stopped.

*Seventeen feet,* said the sounding. But she had the field book. She had spent three nights with the field book during the long weeks after Corvin first came, matching every number against every number, and she knew—she had checked twice, three times—that at the fourth arch the line had found nineteen.

She laid down the burnisher. Pulled the field book from its hiding place. Held it beside the plate.

Fourth arch: field book says nineteen. Plate says seventeen.

Fifth arch: field book says twelve. Plate says fourteen.

Sixth arch: field book says six—a gravel shoal, unusable. Plate says nine.

Her mouth went dry. She turned back through the finished plates with the candle held low, and there it was, arch after arch, ford after ford, the discrepancies marching along the river like footprints. Every place the plate disagreed with the measurement, the disagreement was not random. It was directional. Read the errors alone—seventeen, fourteen, nine, eleven—and they traced a line downstream past the bridge's western abutment, along the shingle bar below the tanneries, to a crossing the maps officially marked as impassable.

Vey hadn't just hidden the true fords in the atlas. He had hidden his own guilt in the same ink.

Because this was the confession, she understood suddenly, coldly, completely. A man who sold crossing routes to an enemy needed a record of what he'd sold—not for the enemy, who paid in coin and got their copies fair—but for himself. For the day someone came asking. The soundings were his ledger. *Here is what I betrayed, written so small that only another mapmaker would ever read it.* He had trusted the truth to be invisible. He had trusted her not to look.

Or—he had trusted her to look. That was the thought that sat down in her chest and refused to leave. *Finish the river*, the margin note had said. Not *finish the atlas*. Finish the river. He had left the last plate unfinished deliberately, because a confession needs a reader, and the reader he chose was the apprentice who inked his lines.

Elodie put both hands flat on the table to stop them shaking.

"You knew," she whispered to the empty workshop. "You absolute coward. You knew I'd finish it."

Outside, the curfew drums sounded. Two hours past dusk already. The candles had burned to stubs.

She should have stopped. She knew that later—knew it in the moment, even, in some small prudent voice that sounded vaguely like Tomas saying *Elodie, go to bed*. But the confession was opening under her burin like a wound, and she wanted the whole of it, wanted to know the size of what her master had done, wanted the number of it: how many crossings, how much coin, how many men might die on the fords he'd measured with such loving care.

So she worked. And she lost the time entirely, the way she only ever lost it over Vey's lines, and the next thing she knew the candle was out and the room was grey-blue with the hour before dawn and there was a man standing in the doorway of the workshop holding a shuttered lantern.

* * *

Rennard closed the door behind him without hurrying. That was the worst part—how unhurried he always was, a man who had already won whatever race mattered.

"Curfew was at the ninth drum," he said.

"I fell asleep at my work, Captain."

"No." He crossed the room, set the lantern on the table, and opened its shutter a finger's width. Light fell across the plate, the field book lying open beside it, the two sets of numbers exposed to each other like conspirators caught in the same room. "You didn't."

Elodie stood very still. Her mind ran the exits—the false floor, the back stair to the alley, the river itself—but Rennard watched her think them all, and she saw in his face that he'd watched her think them every visit for nine days, and that he had chosen tonight because tonight there was nowhere the arithmetic didn't follow her.

"Do you know what I do with surveyors' errors?" he asked. "When my own men make them?"

"Punish them, Captain."

"I hunt for the pattern." He tapped the field book once, lightly, almost gently. "Random error is ignorance. Patterned error is intention. And intention, mademoiselle Thackeray—" he pronounced her surname carefully, fully, the way no one had since Corvin first said it across her threshold "—intention is worth more than the whole atlas."

"I don't understand what you mean."

"I think you do." He drew out the chair opposite hers and sat, which no officer of the garrison had ever done in her presence, and somehow that frightened her more than the sword at his hip. "Let me tell you what I see. I see a dead man's workshop producing the finest military cartography in the province, run by an apprentice who is either the luckiest dullard alive or the finest cartographer in the province herself. I see copies of these plates going to my engineers, and I see—" the pale eyes flicked to the field book "—a discrepancy between what the master measured and what the master published, which means either the measurements are wrong or the maps are lies, and Aldous Vey did not make wrong measurements."

The silence stretched. Somewhere outside, the river moved in the dark, indifferent, carrying everything toward the sea.

"I can offer you protection," Rennard said. "Real protection. Papers. A commission. There will be an inquiry into Vey's estate and his correspondence, and when it comes, a woman who cooperated fully—with documents, with testimony, with the originals—will have nothing to fear from it. You would be the garrison's cartographer. Paid. Housed. Safe." He leaned forward. "You have spent this entire war being invisible, mademoiselle. I am offering to make you untouchable. All it costs is the truth as I choose to write it."

And God help her, she wanted it. That was the obscenity of it—the thing she would carry afterward like a stone. She wanted it the way the starving want bread. Safety. To walk the streets without folding herself smaller. To sign her work. To sleep past the third step on the stair. Rennard had looked at her for nine days and seen not a plodding apprentice but exactly what she was, and there was a terrible relief in that, in being *seen*, even by a wolf.

"All I ask," Rennard said quietly, "are the original plates. The ones under your floor."

Her blood went still. He knew about the floor. He had probably always known about the floor.

"And the discrepancies," he added. "Explain them to me. Because if Aldous Vey encoded crossing routes into his soundings and sold them to the enemy before he died—or didn't die—then those routes are worth a battle, and I would very much rather buy them from a sensible young woman than dig them out of a burned building."

*A burned building.* Said lightly. Said the way men say things they have already decided to do.

Elodie looked down at the plate, at Vey's confession lying open under the lantern light, at the small treacherous numbers that had waited years in copper and ink for someone to read them. Her master's last message. The only honest thing he'd ever left her.

If she gave Rennard the originals, the truth died in that room. If she refused—

Well. Now they both knew what the fire was for.

"Captain," she said, and heard her own voice come out steady, which surprised her, "I fell asleep at my work. That's all I can tell you."

Something moved behind Rennard's pale eyes—disappointment, or respect, or the pleasure of a man confirming a suspicion. He rose, closed the lantern shutter, and walked to the door.

"I'll want your answer by tomorrow's ninth drum," he said. "Think about what burns fastest. Paper, or people."

The door shut. The third step creaked. And Elodie stood alone in the dark with the atlas, doing sums she already knew the answer to: it would take one hour to pack the chest, and Rennard's patience would not survive the daylight.
