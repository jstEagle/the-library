## 7. Ash on the Vellum

*Elodie*

The knock came at dawn, three blows of a rifle butt against the workshop door, and Elodie knew before she opened it that Rennard had found her errors.

She had been careful. That was the bitter joke of it—she had been *very* careful, moving each false sounding only a few feet, altering each ford by a hand's breadth, errors small enough to pass a casual eye and large enough to drown a company of soldiers. She had told herself that a man who copied maps all day would never truly read them, the way a scribe can copy scripture without once believing in God.

But Captain Rennard was not a scribe.

"Master Thackeray." He stood on the step with the gray light behind him, two soldiers at his shoulders, and he did not smile, which frightened her more than any smile would have. "You slept well, I hope."

"As well as the city allows, Captain."

"The Vellacourt fords." He said it flatly, like a man reading charges. "Your master's surveys place the crossing at Mill Reach four feet shallower than my own soundings from last autumn. The gravel bar below Saint-Aubin is marked two hundred yards downstream of where I've walked it myself, boots wet to the knee." He stepped inside without invitation, his boots leaving wet prints on the floorboards Aldous Vey had planed himself. "Errors happen. Apprentices are careless. But these errors all run in one direction, don't they? Away from every crossing an attacking force might use."

Elodie kept her face empty, the plodding-apprentice blankness she had worn for two years until it fit like skin. "I only copy what's on the plates, Captain. I wouldn't know a ford from a fencepost."

"No," Rennard agreed mildly. "But you'd know if someone had taught you to lie in ink."

He moved through the workshop like a slow tide, lifting sheets, tilting candles toward the drying racks, pausing at last before the great chest where the finished plates lay wrapped in oilcloth. Elodie watched his hand rest on the lid and felt her heartbeat move into her throat and stay there.

"These go back with me," he said. "All of them. And your correspondence. Your master's letters. Everything paper."

"He's dead," Elodie said. "His letters are grief and grocery lists."

"Then you won't mind surrendering them."

She minded. God, she minded—but there was nothing on her face, and that was the whole of her education: two years of learning that a face could be a locked room. She gathered what he asked while the soldiers waited by the door, and she did not look toward the loose floorboard beneath the drafting table, and she did not think about the original atlas sleeping under it, because thinking was the first mistake and looking was the second.

Rennard paused at the door, the requisitioned plates under his arm.

"You're a better mapmaker than you pretend, Thackeray. That's not a compliment. It will be, if you're wise. It won't be, if you're not."

The door closed. Elodie stood in the cold workshop and listened to her own breathing, and understood that the clock had begun running—not hours now, but minutes stacked on minutes, each one spent.

* * *

*Corvin*

Corvin heard the news before noon, from a fishwife who sold eels to the garrison kitchens and gossip to anyone with coin. The cartographer captain had seized Vey's plates. The workshop would be searched. There was talk of burning papers.

He moved through the back lanes with his collar up, counting the ways this became a disaster. If Rennard read the originals closely enough—and the man plainly did—he would find what Corvin had found: the corrected fords, the true crossings, the military secret sewn into the survey lines like thread through cloth. Worse, he might work backward from the falsified copies and understand that someone inside the workshop had been feeding the occupation poison. That someone was still there.

He found the signal chalked where they had agreed—a fishhook drawn on the third bridge piling, turned sideways, meaning *danger, come anyway*—and he came anyway, down the coal stairs into the tannery cellar where Elodie waited with a lantern turned low and a face like winter.

"They took the copies," she said. "Not the originals. The originals are under the floor."

"I know about the floor. I helped Vey hide things under worse floors than yours."

"Then help me get them out, because he'll be back. He gave me three days to 'reconsider my carelessness.' That's not mercy. That's a noose tied slowly."

Corvin crouched beside her over the lifted board, looking down at the oilcloth bundle that contained everything—Vey's genius, Vey's treason, Vey's confession written in soundings and riverbeds. "There's a chest," he said. "In the shipping office on Quay Lane. Double-bottomed, built for smuggling charts past customs in the old days. My people can move it as freight tomorrow morning, mixed in with the barge cargo going upriver."

"And between now and tomorrow morning?"

"Between now and tomorrow morning," Corvin said, "we put the atlas in the chest, and we put the chest somewhere Rennard's men won't kick it, and we pray the river does the rest."

"I stopped praying when my master started writing letters to both sides," Elodie said. But she was already wrapping the plates, her hands steady, her jaw set in a way that reminded him unpleasantly of Vey himself. "There's something else. Tomas."

"Tomas?"

"The other apprentice. Sixteen. Sweeps floors and grinds pigment and knows nothing except that I stay late and the master's letters come from nowhere." She looked up, and for the first time since he'd known her, Corvin saw fear in her face—not for herself. "If Rennard starts asking questions, he'll start with the boy, because the boy is easy to frighten. And Tomas is easy to frighten."

"Then we move tonight."

"We move tonight," she agreed. "And Corvin—if they take him, whatever happens, we don't come back for him. You hear me? We finish the delivery. Vey's crossings are worth more than one boy's silence, and if I go back for Tomas then everything is ash for nothing."

She said it like a woman rehearsing a line until she could say it without flinching. Corvin had seen soldiers do the same thing before an assault. He nodded, and hated the nodding.

* * *

*Elodie*

They moved at moonset, the atlas in its oilcloth slung inside Corvin's coat, Elodie walking three paces behind with a market basket over her arm like any insomniac housewife after eggs. The streets were curfew-quiet except for the patrol lamps swinging on the quay, and the river ran black and patient beside them, keeping its secrets the way it had kept Vey's for twenty years.

The shipping office smelled of tar and rat droppings. Corvin's contact—a barge-woman named Maren with forearms like mooring rope—pried open the double bottom with a boat knife and held it wide while Elodie slid the bundle in. Then the false panel went down, the legitimate cargo went on top—charts of the coast road, invoices, a sealed letter of lading—and Maren hammered the chest shut with four blows, soft-shod, like a coffin nailed quietly.

"Gone by first light," Maren said. "Upriver to the mill landing. After that it's your friends' problem, not mine."

After that it was done. Elodie stood in the dark office and felt the strangest sensation—the atlas, two years of candlelight and stolen ink and lying to everyone she met, no longer under her hands. It was weightless now, or she was. Either way, the feeling lasted eleven steps out of the office, which was how far they got before the whistles started.

They came from the direction of the workshop. Torches bloomed along the lane like rotten flowers. A squad of garrison troops moved with purpose, and ahead of them, dragged barefoot in the cold with his shirt half-buttoned, went Tomas.

Elodie stopped. Corvin's hand closed on her arm, hard, and she let it hold her there because otherwise she would have crossed the street.

"They took him," she said.

"They took him because he's nothing. Nothing gets released. Remember what you told me."

She remembered. She had made the speech herself, rehearsed it, armored herself in it. *We don't come back for him.* She watched the soldiers shove Tomas through the workshop door, watched torchlight spill across the windows she had inked maps beside for two years, and thought: *the price of survival is never paid by the survivor.*

Behind them, at the quay's end, smoke was rising from a burn barrel in the garrison yard. Even at this distance she knew what fed it—Vey's letters, the correspondence, the grocery lists and grief, the whole paper trail of a drowned man's double life going up in orange spirals. Rennard was erasing the evidence even as he hunted for it, burning everything that might tell him what he possessed, keeping only the maps themselves.

Which meant the atlas in the chest was now the only copy in the world of the truth about Vellacourt's crossings.

Which meant that every soldier, every patrol, every whistle in this city would soon be pointed at the girl who had made it so.

"Come on," Corvin said quietly. "Maren's barge leaves in an hour, and you should be on it."

"On it?"

"You heard me. Where the atlas goes, you go. Nobody else alive can read those soundings, Elodie. Not me. Not the resistance. If a plate cracks in transit or a question comes up mid-river, you're the only hand that can answer it." He was already pulling her toward the water stairs. "Besides—Rennard thinks you're staying put for three days. Best lie you ever told deserves to be used."

Elodie looked back once, at the torches, at the burn barrel, at the workshop window where a sixteen-year-old boy was being asked questions he couldn't answer about letters he'd never read.

*Tonight it's Tomas,* she thought. *And someday it's my turn in that chair. Unless the truth arrives faster than the fire.*

She went down the water stairs into the dark.
