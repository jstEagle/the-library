## 9. The Burning Bridge

The atlas weighed less than a man's head.

Elodie had carried it for six years in every way but this — in her memory, in her hands at the drafting table, in the ache of finishing what a dead man could not. Now it was bound in oilcloth and strapped beneath Corvin's coat, and it weighed almost nothing, and she could not stop feeling it against her ribs as if it were a second heartbeat.

They came down through the tannery district before dawn, when Vellacourt smelled of lime and rot and the river's cold breath off the water. Curfew had lifted an hour past; the garrison's patrols were changing watch, and Corvin knew the seams in their timing the way she knew the grain of vellum. He moved like a man who had spent his life walking through other people's houses.

"The defenders hold the east bank," he said quietly. "What's left of them. Commander Hessa — you'll give it to her, no one else. If anyone else claims to speak for the resistance, you walk away."

"I know."

"You don't. You've spent years being invisible. This morning that ends." He glanced at her sideways. "Whatever happens after you hand it over — you can't take it back."

She thought of Tomas in a garrison cell, of the workshop ash, of Rennard's voice saying *protection* the way a man says *trap* without noticing he's used the word. "I finished the last plate three days ago," she said. "I stopped being able to take anything back then."

The east gate was a wound in the city wall, half-collapsed from the autumn bombardment and never repaired because the garrison did not intend to defend this side. Beyond it, in the ruined cloth market under a roof of scorched beams, Vellacourt's defenders had made their last room. Elodie had imagined an army. She found instead perhaps two hundred people: militiamen with fowling pieces, women casting bullets in kettles, boys too young for any of it sharpening stakes with the terrible concentration of children doing adult work.

Commander Hessa turned out to be a woman of fifty with a carpenter's forearms and a sabre worn down to a sliver, sitting at a trestle table studying maps that were wrong.

Elodie saw it immediately — the maps were wrong the way hers had once been wrong, before she understood what Vey had done. The fords marked at Millrace were drawn a hundred paces downstream of the true crossing. The shallows below the bridge starlings were shown as impassable; they were passable in September water, knee-deep on a man, chest-deep on a horse. Whoever had surveyed this had either never waded the river or had wanted it believed unwadeable.

Hessa looked up. "You're the courier's mapmaker."

"His cartographer," Elodie said. "The atlas is real. And your maps are false."

The room went quiet around them. A boy with a whetstone stopped mid-stroke.

"Show me," Hessa said.

Elodie unstrapped the oilcloth with hands that did not shake, which surprised her. She opened to the soundings of the great bridge first — the final plate, the one she'd completed by candlelight while Rennard's shadow crossed the shutters — and laid it flat among the cups and bullet moulds. Then she walked Hessa through it: the true ford at Millrace, marked not with a symbol but with Vey's depth figures hidden in the margin numbers, readable only if you knew to read them. The gravel shoal upstream of the bridge that could carry infantry across in column if they entered at the willow bend. The channel under the third arch where the current ran backward at ebb tide, deep enough to drown a gun crew but shallow enough at dawn for men who knew the hour.

"How do I know these aren't the false ones?" Hessa said. But her finger was already resting on the willow bend, tracing the line of approach, and Elodie knew she had won before she answered.

"Because Aldous Vey sold your enemy the crossings that would let them take the bridge quickly," she said. "And then he wrote his guilt into the same plates, in numbers only a cartographer would catch, so that someday someone would know what he'd done. He couldn't confess out loud. So he confessed in ink." She met the commander's eyes. "I finished his confession. It cost me everything I had. You may check every sounding yourself — send swimmers tonight, at ebb, and measure."

"We don't have tonight," Hessa said. "We have until the garrison moves, which is soon. They've been massing on the west bank for two days." She looked down at the plate again, and something in her face shifted from doubt to a kind of grim hunger. "If these fords are true, we don't have to defend the bridge at all. We let them come onto it, and we cross behind them."

"That's what I hoped you'd say," Corvin said from the doorway.

* * *

Corvin had stood in a great many war councils, most of them shorter and bloodier than this one, and he gave this one an hour before it turned into what wars actually were: frightened men arguing about weather.

He said little. He watched Elodie.

She stood over the atlas like a priestess over relics, answering questions about gradients and bottom-mud and the load-bearing lies of gravel bars, and the militia officers who had looked through her all morning now hung on her smallest word. She had spent six years teaching herself to be overlooked, and now two hundred armed people leaned toward her voice, and she seemed — he searched for the word — *unburied*. Like something planted long ago finally breaking soil.

It would get her killed, or made, and both frightened him more than the battle coming up the road.

Hessa's plan settled into shape the way plans do: roughly, with gaps everyone agreed not to look at directly. Two companies would wade at Millrace before first light, using the true ford, and come up behind the garrison's staging ground on the west bank. The rest would hold the east approaches and wait. When the garrison committed its weight to the bridge — and Rennard would commit them to the bridge, because the bridge was the prize, the thing he'd requisitioned a workshop and burned letters and taken an apprentice to possess — the defenders would fire it from the east end, cutting the assault in half while the flanking force hit the survivors' rear.

"And the copies?" Hessa asked. "Your captain has been copying our dead master's plates for weeks. What does he believe about the river?"

"He believes what Vey sold him," Corvin said. "That's the beauty of it. Vey took garrison gold for crossings that were true — and then, in his own private atlas, corrected them. Rennard has spent a month copying maps whose errors are worth a regiment. When his men step into the Millrace ford expecting knee-deep water—" he shrugged— "the river keeps what it's owed."

"You're certain he'll use the bridge?"

Corvin thought of Rennard's careful eyes, his ink-stained cuffs, the way he'd touched Vey's plates like a collector touching a mistress's letters. "He's a cartographer's soldier," he said. "He trusts paper more than water. That's why we win."

He did not say the other thing, the thing he'd carried since the marshes: that Vey might still be alive out there in the reeds, and that a living traitor was a loose thread in any victory. There wasn't time. There was never time for truth except when someone bled for it.

* * *

The garrison came at gray noon, drums beating the step, and Elodie watched them from the bell tower of Saint Aubin's with Hessa's signal officer beside her and the whole doomed geometry of the day spread out along the river.

Rennard's column was four hundred men in good order, marching not toward the bridge but toward the willow bend upstream — toward Millrace. Her stomach dropped. *He knows,* she thought. *He found the errors. He found me out.*

Then the column halted at the bend, deployed into fours, and began to enter the water at the point her atlas showed as a gravel shoal — and Elodie understood with a lurch that Rennard was using Vey's *sold* crossings, the ones in the copies, the false ones. The garrison's own purchased intelligence. The ford that was knee-deep on paper and chest-deep, then neck-deep, then nothing at all in fact.

The first rank went in confident. The water took it to the waist. The second rank followed, and the river, running high with the week's rains, closed over the drummers' shoulders, and the order dissolved into the oldest sound there is — men discovering that the world is not the map. They turned back. They pressed on. They drowned each other holding each other down. From the tower it looked almost gentle, that brown water folding over steel, and Elodie pressed her hand flat against the cold stone and made herself watch, because she had drawn those numbers, corrected them, chosen them, and watching was the price.

"They'll reform," the signal officer said. "See — falling back to the bridge. He'll take the bridge instead."

And he did. Rennard pulled the wrecked ford attempt out of the water and threw it at the bridge, four abreast across the planks, pikes up, drums again, because a bridge at least kept its promises. The east end held the thin line of defenders who fell back firing, drawing them on, drawing them on, until half the column was on the span and the far half was jammed at the western ramp waiting for room to die.

Hessa's signal went up from the mill — a single red flare — and the bridge was fired.

They had packed the third arch for two days: pitch, powder, tarred rope, all of it ferried across at night through the channel the atlas said was navigable at ebb and the garrison's maps said was not. The explosion lifted the middle of the span like a thing alive. Men went up with it. The bridge folded inward on itself in sections, north half then south, timber and stone and soldiers going into the river together, and the smoke rolled downstream black and slow, and the sound arrived late and enormous and knocked birds out of the tower ledges.

Through it, through the gap where the bridge had been, Elodie saw them coming — Hessa's two companies out of the willow bend, wet to the collarbone, hitting the garrison's rear along the bank. The flank Rennard had posted at the ford was gone, drowned or scattered, and there was nothing between the counterattack and the garrison's stacked reserves but panic.

Rennard she never saw. Later there would be stories — that he'd rallied the rear guard, that he'd cut the colors from a dead ensign and carried them himself, that he'd stood on the western ramp reading a captured map while the world burned around him and refused, absolutely refused, to believe it. The story Elodie chose to keep was simpler: that somewhere in that smoke a man who trusted paper more than water died arguing with a river.

By dusk it was over. The garrison that could run had run west along the towpath, and the ones who couldn't lay in the shallows where the atlas said the water ran shallow, and the true fords of Vellacourt were crowded with victors.

Corvin found her at the tower door. She was still holding the oilcloth, though the atlas inside was only paper now, only knowledge, its work done. Soot had gotten into her hair and one sleeve was scorched where a burning ember had drifted on the wind, and she was looking at the broken bridge the way she had once looked at Vey's unfinished marginal note.

"Finish the river," Corvin said softly.

"It's finished."

"No." He nodded east, toward the marshes, where the reeds stood dark against the last light. "There's one page left. A living witness, a confession that needs a name attached, a resistance council that will want to know exactly how their intelligence was bought." He held out his hand. "You confronted him once. Now the whole city gets to."

Elodie looked at the water, at the smoke thinning over the ruins of the great bridge, and felt the old apprentice silence rising in her throat — the habit of hiding, of letting the dead man take credit, of being no one. She put it down.

"Tomorrow," she said, and took his hand.
