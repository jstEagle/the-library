## 10. A New Hand

The frost came early that year, silvering the broken parapets of the bridge before the masons had even finished shoring them. Elodie watched it from the workshop window—the same window she had inked plates beneath for three years, the same warped pane with its bubble like a trapped eye—and thought about how little glass had changed, and how much everything behind it had.

The city held. People said it now without flinching, the way you'd say the river runs east or winter follows autumn. The garrison was gone—what remained of it, scattered south across the hills, licking wounds Rennard had marched them into. They said he'd died on the bridge when it fired. They also said he'd cut his captain's sash off in the water and swum downstream disguised as a common soldier, and that he'd been recognized at a mill two leagues away and hanged from his own surveying tripod. Elodie didn't know which story was true. She suspected both were, depending on who needed telling.

What she knew was this: the bridge still stood, scorched along its northern third, and the crossing routes she had carried through the marshes in a map chest were drawn into every defender's plan before dawn. The ambush at the true fords had lasted an hour. The battle for the false crossing had lasted all night and cost the garrison four hundred men who had trusted a map.

She had made that map. Both maps. The lie and the truth, side by side on vellum, and only one of them had been hers.

* * *

Tomas came back on a Tuesday, thin as a lath and smelling of the garrison cells, and stood in the doorway of the workshop as if he'd forgotten what doors were for.

"They let me go," he said. "They just—opened it one morning. Nobody said why."

Elodie set down her pen. Her hands were steady. Three weeks ago they would not have been; she'd have knocked over an inkwell, fumbled her plodding-apprentice clumsiness into place like a mask slipping on. Now she simply looked at him.

"Because I told them where to look," she said. "And what they'd find there."

He stared at her. In the candlelight of all those hidden nights, Tomas had been the other apprentice—the faster one, the favored one, the one Vey praised aloud while Elodie's work went unsigned. He had never once suspected what she did after curfew. She could see him recalculating three years of shared silence, reweighing every quiet evening at the drafting table.

"You finished it," he said finally. "The atlas."

"I finished it."

"Vey's atlas." His voice caught on the name. Everyone's voice caught on the name now. The resistance had published the confession—the encoded soundings decoded by three different hands so no one could claim forgery—and Aldous Vey's name had gone from honored to cursed in the space of a printed broadsheet. There were people who wanted to dig up whatever grave the city had given him. There was no grave. There was a marsh, and letters Elodie had left weighted under a stone, and a man she had walked away from without turning around.

"It was never only his," she said. "That was the problem. Nobody ever asked whose else it was."

Tomas crossed the room slowly, the way you approach a dog that has learned something new about itself. He looked at the drafting table, at the new plate pinned there—the first map of the rebuilt city, its walls resurveyed, its burned quarter redrawn with honest lines.

"Will they reopen the workshop?" he asked. "Under a new master?"

"There isn't going to be a new master." Elodie turned the plate toward him. "There's going to be a cartographer. One cartographer. The council voted yesterday."

His eyes went wide. "You?"

"Me."

* * *

The ceremony, if you could call it that, took place in the guildhall on a morning so cold the river steamed. The council chamber smelled of woodsmoke and wet wool, and half the seats were empty—some men dead, some fled, some too ashamed of their own signatures on the capitulation papers to sit under the painted eyes of the founders.

Corvin stood at the back. He'd refused a chair. He always refused chairs; Elodie suspected he'd slept standing up so long during the war years that sitting felt like surrender. The scar down his cheek had faded to a pale seam. He'd brought her the letters that had started everything, and afterward he'd tried to leave twice, and twice the city had found reasons for him to stay. She suspected the second reason wore her face.

The council speaker—a gray woman named Maren Ostreva, who had lost two sons at the fords and read the decoded confession aloud in a voice like a blade being drawn—rose from her seat.

"The office of City Cartographer has been vacant since the disgrace of Aldous Vey," she said. "It will not be vacant again. The defense of this city was bought with a map, and the next war will be bought or prevented with one. We require a hand we can trust, because we have learned—at terrible cost—what an untrusted hand can do."

She looked directly at Elodie. "Elodie Thackeray. Will you take the office?"

Every eye in the chamber. Corvin's among them, steady, unsurprised, faintly amused—as if to say: *you could have told me they'd ask; I'd have worn better boots.*

Three years ago, Elodie would have looked at the floor. Two years ago she'd have looked at the floor while calculating how to survive the moment. A year ago she'd have looked at Vey's ghost for permission.

She looked at the table instead. On it lay the new atlas—not Vey's, though its bones were his, corrected and completed and annotated in a second hand throughout. The council had ordered it copied for the archives, the armory, and the guild school. On its final page, where the colophon belonged, there was a blank line beneath the words *Surveyed and drawn by.*

Maren slid the atlas toward her, along with a pen already loaded.

"Sign it," she said. "Your name. Not your master's. Not your office's. Yours."

Elodie picked up the pen.

Her hand did not shake. That surprised her most—not the weight of the moment but the lightness of it. All those years of hiding her competence behind a plodding manner, all those nights of inking another person's genius by candlelight, all that loneliness of guarding a secret no living soul shared—she had believed survival meant invisibility. She had believed being unseen was the price of being safe.

But she hadn't survived by hiding. She had survived by deciding. Deciding which errors to plant in Rennard's copies. Deciding to carry the truth through the marshes. Deciding, in a cold reed-bed with a coward's excuses hanging in the fog, to walk away from the man who taught her everything except how to be him.

Survival was never hiding. Survival was choosing what was worth being seen for.

She wrote her name.

*E. Thackeray.* Small, clear letters, the kind that survive copying. Beneath it, after a breath, she added a second line, unprompted:

*Completed from the unfinished plates of A. Vey, whose omissions are corrected herein.*

Let the record show both things. The debt and the betrayal. The teaching and the treason. A map that lied and a map that didn't, and the same hand on both.

She set down the pen. The chamber was silent, then Maren nodded once, and somewhere in the back Corvin exhaled like a man who'd been holding his breath for a decade.

* * *

That evening she walked out onto the repaired span of the great bridge. The scorch marks would never come out of the stone; the masons had stopped pretending otherwise and simply worked around them, so that anyone crossing would walk through the memory of the fire whether they wished to or not. Below, the river ran dark and patient, keeping its own records.

Corvin fell into step beside her. He didn't say anything for a long while. Neither did she. The best conversations between them had always been conducted in silences and margins.

"The guild school opens at midwinter," she said eventually. "I'll need apprentices. Ones I don't have to lie to."

"Tomas?"

"Already asked. He said yes." She glanced sideways. "I'm told the pay is poor and the hours are worse."

"I've had worse offers." He looked out at the water. "I got a letter this morning. From the south. People asking whether the ford surveys could be... adapted. For other rivers. Other cities."

Elodie stopped walking. "Whose letter?"

"That's the thing." Corvin drew it from inside his coat—creased, travel-worn, sealed with plain wax. "No signature. But the hand—" He hesitated, and for the first time since she'd known him, the scarred courier looked uncertain. "The hand is neat. Practiced. Trained."

He held it out to her.

Elodie took it. Broke the seal. Read the first line by the light of the bridge lanterns, and felt the frost of the whole winter gather at the base of her spine.

She knew that hand. She had spent three years learning it stroke by stroke, imitating it, finishing it, cursing it.

Aldous Vey was supposed to be dead in the marshes. She had left him alive—but she had left him nothing worth writing with.

Except, she realized, remembering the stone, the weighted letters, the ink she'd left in the pack out of some sentiment she'd hated herself for—

He had his pens. And someone in the south wanted maps.

She folded the letter closed.

"Corvin," she said, "how do you feel about poor pay and worse hours?"
