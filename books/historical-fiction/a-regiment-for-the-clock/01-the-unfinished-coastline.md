## 1. The Unfinished Coastline

The trouble with coastlines is that they refuse to hold still.

I don't mean the water. The water does what water does — comes in, goes out, sulks against the pilings of the fish market like a creditor who's been put off once too often. I mean the land itself. Every survey Halvard Reiner ever made of the Vellmark shore disagreed with the one before it by a matter of feet, sometimes yards, and he would stand over the plates with his loupe screwed into his eye socket and mutter that the Baltic was eating us alive, one honest fathom at a time. He treated erosion as a personal insult. A man who spent forty years drawing the world expects the world to have the decency to remain drawn.

He died in October with the atlas three-quarters finished and the eastern coastline still wet ink on his conscience, which is why I was awake at two in the morning in November tracing a shore I had never properly surveyed, from memory, because the notes that should have told me where to put every rock and shoal were gone.

Not lost. Gone. There is a difference, and I have learned to be precise about differences; it is the entire trade.

* * *

The shop smells of linseed oil and cold tea, and if you have never worked in a mapmaker's shop you will not understand that these two smells together are the smell of a life well spent. The tea because Halvard believed no plate could be finished by a man who hadn't drunk himself stupid on weak tea first — his words, delivered with the gravity other men reserve for scripture. The oil because everything in this trade is paper pretending to last forever, and paper needs help to lie about it.

I sat at the master's bench — my bench now, though I still caught myself waiting for him to come down the stairs and disapprove of my posture — with the eastern sheet pinned flat under glass weights, and I did what he taught me never, ever to do. I drew from memory.

"Memory," he used to say, "is a liar with good handwriting." He said it the way he said most things: loudly, to the room, whether or not the room contained anyone. "A man remembers the coast as he wishes it were. He straightens it. He rounds the ugly promontories. He moves the lighthouse to suit his mood. Ink forgives nothing, boy, so never ask memory for directions."

And here I was, asking.

The lamp guttered. I fed it more oil and kept going, because the alternative was stopping, and stopping meant sitting in this shop with all its silence and thinking about October.

Here is what I remember of the eastern coast, since apparently I am the survey now. From the mouth of the Alden river the shore runs low and gravelly for perhaps four miles, broken at Kessel Point by a shelf of grey rock that shows at half-tide and has drowned better men than me. Then the dunes begin — pale, restless, planted with the spiny grass the government pays farmers to maintain because sand drifts, and drifting sand makes liars of maps faster than anything except war. Past the dunes the land lifts into the pine country, and there the coast turns shy. Cliffs. Inlets too narrow to chart at our scale. Water that looks blue on a fair day and the colour of an old bruise on any other.

I drew all of it. My hand knew the line even when my head didn't, which is either a compliment to seven years of apprenticeship or an indictment of it.

The missing notes should have been in the flat drawer beneath the press. Halvard's field books — eleven of them, leather-bound, grease-spotted, each page a small furious argument between his handwriting and the weather. They held the soundings, the bearings, the tide tables he'd bullied out of fishermen by buying them brandy until they remembered things they'd sworn they'd forgotten. Without them, the eastern sheet is a portrait painted from a description given secondhand by a stranger who squints.

I checked the drawer again anyway. Fourth time that night. Empty, except for a pencil worn to the length of a fingernail and a dried herring skin, because Halvard ate at his bench and considered tidiness a form of vanity.

* * *

Let me tell you what I know about history, since I know a great deal less than I thought I did.

History happens elsewhere. That has always been the arrangement. History happens in the capital, where the ministers live, and on the frontier, where the soldiers go to be statistics, and in the papers, which arrive in Vellmark two days late and smelling faintly of someone else's breakfast. Vellmark gets the aftermath. We get the tariffs, the troopships, the price of grain doing something alarming. But the events themselves — the decisions, the betrayals, the men in rooms deciding things — those happen to other people, in other cities, and we read about them the way you read about a storm at sea while standing safely under a roof.

This is not cynicism. It is geography. Look at any map worth its ink and you will see it: the capital sits inland, fat and central, ringed by the towns that matter, and the coast is a hem. A hem is important — things fray at hems — but nobody writes history about a hem. Hems get mentioned.

Halvard understood this and found it funny. "We are the margin, boy," he'd say, tapping the edge of a plate with his pipe stem. "Everything interesting happens in the middle of the page. Our job is the edges. Nobody conquers the margins. Nobody dies for the margins." He said this the same year the government commissioned three new fortification surveys along exactly our margins, and he said it without any apparent sense of contradiction, which tells you something about masters, or about men, or possibly just about Halvard.

I believed him. That's the part I keep returning to. I built a whole philosophy on it, the way you build a house on ground you've never tested: history happens elsewhere, therefore I am safe, therefore the worst thing that can happen to me in this shop is a bad winter and the slow death of the trade at the hands of the lithography firms in the capital.

Then Halvard went out on a Tuesday in October to take a final bearing on Kessel Point, alone, in weather the harbourmaster described to me afterward as "the sort of weather God uses to make a point," and came back the following Thursday in a box, and the notes were missing, and somewhere between those two facts my philosophy quietly drowned without leaving so much as a floating hat.

* * *

The clock in the corner — Halvard's clock, brass-cased, perpetually four minutes fast because he believed punctuality was a virtue best practised dishonestly — ticked toward three, and I set down my pen and looked at what I'd made.

It was good. That's the humiliating thing. The line flowed the way his lines flow, confident, economical, every curve justified. Anyone looking at the finished atlas would find the eastern coast entirely credible. It looked surveyed. It looked true.

But I knew where I'd guessed. The shelf off Kessel Point — I'd placed it from a fishing trip when I was fourteen, and memory being what it is, I may have moved it fifty yards east to flatter my own recollection of nearly drowning there. The depth soundings past the dunes were pure invention dressed in plausible numbers. Somewhere in that stretch of blue I had written *7½ fathoms* in a hand indistinguishable from truth, and the only honest word attached to it was the small one in the corner of my mind that whispered *liar*.

An atlas, Halvard said, is a promise made to strangers. Sailors you will never meet will trust your ink with their hulls and their lives, and they will never know your name, and that asymmetry — their trust, your invisibility — is the whole moral weight of the trade. He said we are paid in anonymity and must spend it carefully.

I wondered, wiping my pen, what he would make of the eastern sheet. I decided he would say nothing at all. He would take off his spectacles, clean them very slowly on his sleeve — the gesture that preceded every disappointment of my apprenticeship — and hand the pen back to me without comment, and the silence would do the work of a sermon.

* * *

Around four I gave up pretending to work and did what grieving people do in workshops: I tidied. There is a particular comfort in putting objects back where a dead man kept them. His burnisher in the third slot. His dividers hanging from their nail, handles worn to the shape of his grip, useless to me in every practical sense and untouchable in every other. The stack of proof pulls from the northern sheets, squared and weighted, waiting for a correction he was never going to make.

The atlas itself lived in the long cabinet under the north window: thirty-one completed plates, each drawn at the scale of one inch to the mile, each corrected against the Admiralty charts and against Halvard's own stubborn field observations wherever the two disagreed. (They disagreed often. He regarded the Admiralty the way a devout man regards a rival church.) Thirty-one plates of a country rendered so faithfully you could navigate it blind, provided you trusted a dead man's arithmetic.

Thirty-two remained. The eastern sheet. The unfinished coastline.

I stood there with the cabinet open longer than tidying requires. The shop was cold enough that my breath showed, and the harbour fog had come up the street the way it does, filling the lane outside like something poured, muffling the one late cart, and I remember thinking — with the peculiar clarity that arrives only at hours when no sensible person is awake — that I had no idea why the notes were missing, and that I had been avoiding that thought for six weeks the way you avoid a loose tooth.

Because here is the thing about Halvard Reiner: he did not lose things. In forty years of practice, through two changes of government, one flood, and the fire that took the old chandlery next door, the man misplaced nothing larger than a pencil stub. His field books were his religion's relics. He carried them in an oilskin wrap, inside his coat, against his chest, and when I once asked — laughing — what he'd do if he fell in the sea, he looked at me with genuine surprise and said, "The books float better than I do."

So either the notes went into the sea with him, which the searchers would likely have mentioned, or someone removed them from his effects between the Tuesday and the Thursday, or —

Or he hid them himself, before he went out, the way a man hides something when he suspects he may not come back to guard it.

I did not like that third option. I want to be clear about that. I stood in the cold with the cabinet open and I actively disliked it, because the first two options left Halvard as what I needed him to be: an old man caught out by weather, a margin-dweller to the end, history happening elsewhere right up until it happened to his boat. The third option required him to have had a secret worth protecting from me, and I had spent seven years learning to read that man's silences, and I would have sworn on the atlas itself that he had none.

But then, I reflected, closing the cabinet, everyone believes they know the people they love. It is the one map nobody ever bothers to survey properly.

* * *

I climbed the stairs past his rooms, which I still have not entered. The landlady offered. Tomas offered, in his blunt way, saying it was morbid keeping a shrine, and that dead men don't need boots, and couldn't he sell the boots at least. I said the rooms would keep. They will keep indefinitely, at this rate. Dust settles differently up there, I imagine. Slower. More respectful.

In my own narrow room above the shop I lay down without undressing and listened to the foghorn out past the point, counting the intervals the way Halvard taught me when I was fifteen and afraid of the dark in a way I was too old for. Four seconds blast, eight seconds silence. A rhythm, he said, is just a promise that repeats itself. You can sleep inside a promise.

I slept maybe two hours, and dreamed of nothing useful — the usual fragments, water, his voice saying something about margins — and woke at dawn to the sound of Tomas clattering downstairs into the shop to steal tea, which is his form of affection.

By noon I had convinced myself the night had been ordinary. Grief, I told myself, makes mysteries out of empty drawers. Old men misplace things after all. The sea keeps what it takes. History happens elsewhere.

At half past twelve, while I was grinding ink and lying to myself with real craftsmanship, a courier in government grey stopped at the shop window, consulted a slip of paper, looked up at the sign — REINER & YARROW, MAPS AND CHARTS — and came inside, bringing the street's cold in with him and an envelope sealed with wax the colour of dried blood.

History, it turned out, had finished happening elsewhere.

It had come to collect at the counter, and it was asking for me by name.
