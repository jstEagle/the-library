## 2. A Letter Under Seal

The courier came at half past nine on a Tuesday, which I have since decided is the hour governments prefer for delivering ruin. Too late for a man to pretend the day might still be ordinary, too early for him to take to his bed and refuse it. He stood in the doorway of the shop with snow melting off his boots onto Halvard's good floorboards, and he held out a letter sealed with more wax than any letter I had ever seen — a fat red disc of it, stamped twice, as though one stamp had not been sufficient to say how seriously I ought to be afraid.

"Yarrow?" he said, though the sign above the door said YARROW & HALVARD, SURVEYORS AND MAPMAKERS, and had said it for nineteen years, and my name was on it in letters a foot high.

"That's me."

"For the household. All parties." He said *all parties* the way a man says *condolences* — with a certain professional satisfaction, as if the phrase itself were doing its work on me already.

I signed his book. My hand did it without my permission; that's the thing about hands, they keep working while the rest of you stands very still and listens for trouble. The courier touched his cap, looked once around the shop — at the flat cases, at the great press in the corner, at the unfinished atlas lying open under its cloth like a patient asleep — and left. His boot-prints filled with water behind him all the way down the step.

I carried the letter into the back room before I opened it. Some instinct told me not to open it among the maps, among Halvard's things, among the instruments that had never been anything but honest. As if the letter were a kind of weather and I could keep it off the furniture.

The wax took a knife. It fought coming off, which felt right. Inside there was a single sheet, and the paper was heavier than anything we stocked, the kind of paper that exists so that what's written on it can't be mistaken for anything casual. The ink was black and even, machine-set except for the signature, and the signature belonged to someone whose title ran three lines long, which is always a bad sign. Men who mean well sign short.

I will set down what it said, because I have learned since that setting things down is the only way I can make them hold still:

*To the household of Yarrow: You are named in connection with matters arising from the Vellmark inquiry. The master mapmaker Halvard being deceased, it is required that one Ivo Yarrow present himself at the Survey Office, Customs Quay, within three days of receipt, to answer such questions as may be put to him. Failure to appear will be construed. This letter is not an accusation. It is a summons.*

*It is a summons.*

They had thought about that sentence. Someone had sat at a desk somewhere and weighed *this letter is not an accusation* against every other opening available, and chosen it, and gone home to his supper satisfied. Because it was true, technically. It was not an accusation. It was worse than an accusation; an accusation names a crime, and this named nothing at all. *Matters arising.* *The Vellmark matter.* Words shaped like officialdom, smooth as river stones, and underneath them — nothing you could grip. A man accused of theft knows what he must deny. A man summoned to answer *such questions as may be put to him* must stand in front of whatever comes, like weather.

And then the phrase that made me read the letter four times, standing in the cold back room with my coat still on: *the household of Yarrow.* Not *the shop.* Not *the estate of Halvard.* The household. My mother's name was in this somewhere. Tomas's name. They had wrapped the whole family in the same envelope.

I sat down on Halvard's stool, the one with the worn seat shaped by twenty years of his particular weight, and I did what any sensible man does with dread. I looked for reasons it wasn't real.

There were plenty available. Letters get misdelivered. Names collide — there were Yarrows up the coast, fishermen, no account. Governments send out papers in batches, sweeping wide, meaning nothing personal by it. And surely — surely — if the family were truly implicated in something, something worth a double-stamped seal and a three-line signature, there would have been soldiers at the door, not a courier with wet boots. Summons first, iron later. That was the order of things. Everyone knew the order of things.

I believed all of it for perhaps four minutes. Then I noticed my own hands, which had gone about the morning's work while the rest of me argued — I'd trimmed a wick, I'd banked the stove — and I understood that some part of me, some older and less flattering part, had known from the moment the courier said *all parties* exactly what this was. Dread doesn't argue. Dread just starts making lists.

Here is what was on the list, in the order it arrived:

Halvard died in October. Fell ill in September, died in October, buried in the churchyard on the hill in November when the ground was already thinking about freezing. Nine weeks. In nine weeks a government that had shown no interest whatsoever in our shop for nineteen years had developed *matters arising.*

My master had spent his last month writing letters I was not allowed to see. I remember this now; I had filed it away as an old man's fussiness. He burned most of them. He burned them in the stove in the back room, the same stove I had just banked, and he watched me watch him do it, and he said — I can hear the exact dryness of it — "Paper is honest twice, Ivo. Once when it's written and once when it burns. Everything between is negotiable." I laughed. God help me, I laughed, because it sounded like one of his epigrams, the sort of thing he said about ink and margins, and I wrote it down afterward in my commonplace book under *On Cartography,* because everything Halvard said sounded like it was about cartography.

And the third item, the one I kept turning over like a coin I suspected of being clipped: the survey notes for the northern coastline were missing. Chapter One of the atlas, the Vellmark approaches, the water Halvard had sounded himself forty years ago — and the field notes gone. Not mislaid. Gone. I had torn the shop apart twice looking, and told myself the old man had simply grown careless at the end, which was a lie so comfortable I had lived inside it for two months without noticing the walls.

A man does not lose the notes to his own life's work. A man hides them, or a man has them taken, or a man destroys them because someone is coming for them. And a government does not write to a dead man's apprentice about *matters arising* unless the dead man left something behind that the government wants, or something behind that the government fears.

I put the letter face-down on the workbench, which changed nothing but felt like a strategy.

* * *

My mother was in the kitchen when I brought it home, kneading bread she had no appetite to eat afterward. I laid the letter on the table between us, unsealed now, and I watched her read it, and I want to be exact about what happened next, because for years afterward I tried to remember it differently.

She read it once, quickly, the way she read market prices. Then she read it again, slowly, her thumb moving along the margin. Then she set it down, wiped her hands on her apron with great thoroughness, as though the letter had made them dirty, and said:

"When do they want you?"

"Three days."

She nodded. She went back to the bread.

That was all. No *what have you done,* no *who are these people,* no *oh God, Ivo.* My mother, who interrogated Tomas for an hour over a broken window when he was twelve, who could extract a confession from a customs officer given five minutes and a cup of tea, read a summons naming our entire household in connection with a government inquiry and asked only about the deadline.

I stood there holding the letter and feeling the floor tilt by degrees, the way a ship's deck tilts — nothing dramatic, just enough that your body knows before your mind consents.

"Mama," I said. "Do you know what the Vellmark matter is?"

"The bread needs ten more minutes," she said. "Sit down. You look grey."

"I'm not hungry."

"You're always hungry when you're frightened. Sit."

I sat. She put tea in front of me without asking whether I wanted it, and she worked the dough, and the clock on the mantel did its dry ticking, and outside a cart went by selling nothing in February, the driver calling his emptiness into the cold. And I understood, sitting there, that my mother was not calm. Calm is loose shoulders and steady eyes. What she had was discipline. There is a difference, and the difference is that discipline costs. I could see the cost of it in her forearms, in the way she pressed the dough harder than bread requires.

"You knew Halvard a long time," she said at last, to the dough rather than to me. "Nineteen years."

"Since I was eleven."

"He was a careful man."

"He was the most careful man I ever met. He checked a bearing three times and then apologized to the compass."

"Yes," she said. "That sounds like him." And she turned the dough, and folded it, and turned it again, and whatever else lived behind her face stayed behind her face, locked up tight as a strongbox, and I realized I was watching a woman decide, deliberately, in front of her son, to know nothing.

"Mama. The letter says *household.*"

"I can read, Ivo."

"Then you're named too. If they come here—"

"They won't come here." She said it fast, faster than reasoning allows, and then caught herself, and slowed down, and became a woman discussing bread again. "Governments don't visit kitchens. They write letters. Eat something."

I didn't eat something. I sat with my cooling tea and studied my mother's back and understood that I had lived nineteen years in a house with a locked room in it and had called the lock *privacy.* Every family has one — a subject nobody raises, a year nobody mentions, a relative spoken of only in the past tense. Ours had always been small and dull-seeming: my father, dead before I could hold a memory of him, a dock clerk, drowned or diseased depending on which aunt you asked, unimportant either way. Fine. But now the summons lay on the table with its double seal, and the lock on that old room suddenly looked newer than the door.

"Halvard paid you," I said. "Didn't he. All these years. Not just wages for me. There was money for the house."

Her hands stopped.

For one full second the kitchen was silent enough that I heard the ice shifting in the harbor, a long groan like something enormous rolling over in its sleep. Then her hands started again.

"He was generous," she said.

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one you're getting tonight." She turned around. Her face was composed now, fully, the cost of it hidden somewhere below the collarbone. "Listen to me. In three days you'll go where they tell you and answer what they ask, and you'll answer politely, and you'll come home. Whatever they ask you about Halvard, you tell them what you know, which is maps. You are a boy who draws coastlines. That is all you have ever been. Say it plainly and often."

"And if they ask about things I don't know?"

"Then you'll have the advantage of me," she said, and almost smiled, and the almost was the worst part, because it meant she knew exactly how much the question mattered and had priced her refusal accordingly.

She wrapped the dough in cloth and set it near the stove to rise, and then she did the thing I keep returning to whenever I try to make sense of that night. She picked up the letter. She carried it to the stove. She opened the firebox door — and stopped. Stood there with the paper held over the coals, my mother, who burned nothing, who kept receipts from shops that had closed a decade ago, who had my father's ration books from a war nobody remembered the dates of.

Then she closed the firebox and put the letter in the drawer with the knives.

"Burning it changes nothing," she said, not turning around. "They keep copies. That's the first thing to understand about governments, Ivo. They never need your paper. They only need you to think they might."

She said nothing else that night. She said nothing at breakfast either, nor at supper, and by the second morning I had stopped watching her mouth for openings and started watching her hands instead, which were more honest. On the second evening I found her in the parlor with her sewing, and beside her chair, where she usually kept her mending basket, there was instead a small canvas bag, packed, squared corners, drawstring tight. Travel-ready. She saw me see it.

"Winter roads," she said. "One keeps a bag ready."

We both let that lie where it fell, because neither of us could afford to pick it up.

Tomas I told nothing, which was cowardice dressed up as protection, and which lasted exactly as long as it takes a dockworker to hear gossip, which is to say Tomas knew before I'd finished reading the letter the second time. He came round that first night after Mother had gone to bed, smelling of tar and the Anchor Tap, and stood in the doorway of my room with his cap in his hands, and said, "You got a letter."

"How—"

"Ferryman's boy saw the courier come up our street and took it upon himself to be informative. Half the quay knows by now, Ivo. *All parties,* they're saying. They're saying the Yarrows are mixed in the Vellmark business." He sat down on my bed without being invited, which was his way, and put his head in his hands. "What's the Vellmark business?"

"I don't know."

"You don't know." He looked up. My brother is four years older than me and has a face built for grievance, all brow and jaw, but that night there was nothing hard in it, only fear wearing Tomas's features like a borrowed coat. "Ivo. There's men down the docks who've been asking after Halvard's papers since before he died. Asking polite, paying for drinks, not saying why. Ferenc's men, they call them, though nobody's met a Ferenc. And now the government writes us a letter with two seals on it. Two seals, Ivo. I count seals for a living, sort of. Nobody uses two unless they're frightened of being ignored."

"Go home, Tomas."

"Our mother is in that house."

"She's asleep."

"She's awake," Tomas said. "She's been awake for years, I think, and none of us knew it." He stood, put his cap on wrong, took it off, put it on right. At the door he paused, and what he said next I wrote down that same night, because it sounded like a prophecy and prophecies should be recorded against their authors. "Whatever it is Halvard left you — and he left you something, don't tell me different, I've seen your face — whatever it is, burn it or bury it or give it to the sea. Because men like that courier don't come twice, and men like Ferenc don't knock at all."

Then he was gone, and I was alone with the letter in the drawer with the knives, and the atlas under its cloth across town, and three days become two.

I did not sleep. I sat at my desk with a lamp turned low and I drew, which is what I do instead of praying — coastlines, mostly, from memory, the pen moving and the mind going quiet behind it. And somewhere past two in the morning, drawing the northern shore I knew better than my own handwriting, I caught myself leaving a gap. A blank stretch where the marsh country began, south of the salt meadows, where Halvard's missing notes should have told me what the land did. My pen had stopped on its own. Nineteen years of training, and my hand refused, all by itself, to invent that ground.

I looked at the gap in the paper for a long time.

Then I blew out the lamp, because there are questions you can only ask in the dark, and the first of them was this: what had my master drawn, in forty years of honest work, that was worth two seals and a household?

The second question I asked the dark was smaller and colder: when I walked into the Survey Office on Thursday morning, would I be walking in as a man answering questions — or as bait, laid out neat on the hook, waiting to see what came to feed?

The dark, being dark, declined to say.
