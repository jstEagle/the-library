## 4. The Woman at the Survey Office

The Survey Office stood on the corner of Customs Square like a man who had once been handsome and now made a living from it. Grey granite, tall windows, a coat of arms above the door whose heraldic animal — a bear, or possibly an ambitious dog — had been worn featureless by a century of gulls. I had passed it perhaps four hundred times in my life and thought about it perhaps never. It was the sort of building that existed to be ignored, which is precisely what buildings are for when the state wants to take something from you quietly.

Thursday came grey and dry, the kind of weather my master used to call "good drawing light" because it committed to nothing. Halvard would have liked it. I did not. I shaved twice, which is one more time than the occasion required and one less than my nerves demanded, put on my good coat — the black one with the fraying cuffs that read as dignity at a distance of six feet — and walked down through the fish market with the half-map sewn into the lining of my sleeve like a man carrying a stolen saint's fingerbone.

I should say something here about how I felt, since everyone always asks afterward how one felt. The honest answer is: like a man rehearsing. All night I had been running conversations in my head, casting myself in the role of the innocent apprentice, the grieving craftsman, the loyal subject who found the letter as bewildering as anyone. I had answers prepared for questions no one had asked yet. I had a story about where I'd been on certain evenings. What I did not have was any notion of what they actually wanted, and so my rehearsal was a play performed before an empty house, every part mine, all of it probably rubbish.

The clerk at the desk inside was a young man with a neck like a plucked goose and the manner of someone who has learned that small powers are best enjoyed slowly. He took my name, checked it against a list with unnecessary ceremony, stamped something, initialed something else, and told me to sit.

I sat. The bench was oak, polished by decades of anxious trousers. Across from me hung a survey of the northern coast done in 1861, before my time, its inlets rendered with a loving precision that made me homesick for a place I'd only ever known secondhand. Someone had drawn those bays by standing in cold water with a chain and a theodolite, and whoever he was, he hadn't signed it. That seemed worth remembering. Most of the work that holds the world together goes unsigned.

I waited forty minutes. I know it was forty minutes because I counted the strikes of the tower clock across the square and then lost count and started over out of spite. Around me clerks came and went with their ledgers, and a soldier with mud on his boots argued quietly at another desk about freight manifests, and the great machinery of the state ground along, indifferent, taking its toll of mornings.

At eleven o'clock precisely, a door at the far end of the room opened and a lieutenant said my name aloud — "Yarrow" — the way you'd announce a horse.

* * *

The room beyond was smaller than I expected, and better lit: two windows facing the square, a plain table, three chairs, a map cabinet against the wall, and a stove that had gone cold sometime around the previous government. There were papers on the table arranged in neat squares. Neat squares are worse than disorder. Disorder means a busy man; squares mean a patient one.

She stood at the window with her back half-turned, in the uniform of an intelligence captain — dark blue, plain, no decoration beyond what rank required — and for the first three seconds she was nobody. A stranger. An officer. Then she turned her head toward the light to look at me, and eleven years fell off the world.

I want to be precise about this, because precision was my master's religion and I have inherited it whether I wanted to or not. Recognition does not arrive all at once. It arrives in pieces, the way a coastline resolves from fog. First the way she held her chin — slightly up, slightly left, as if perpetually listening for something behind her. Then her hands, folded loosely at her back, the right thumb moving against the fingers in a small counting motion she used to do when she was thinking and pretending not to. And last the eyes themselves, grey-green, unimpressed, amused at some private joke the world had not yet gotten around to telling her.

Lira. Lira Voss, of the lakeside summers, who could name every bird on that water and refused to learn their proper names because "the proper names are just somebody else's nicknames." Lira, who beat me swimming across the narrows three years running and let me win the fourth summer out of what she insisted was charity and I knew was mercy. Lira, nineteen years old and furious at her father, sitting on the end of the old pier with her boots off, saying: *if I ever need to get out of somewhere, truly out, you'll draw me the way. You're the only person I've ever met who knows where things actually are.*

And me, laughing, meaning every syllable: *anywhere, Lira. You give me the word and I'll draw you anywhere.*

That girl was standing at the window of the Survey Office wearing a captain's uniform, and the letter under seal on the table in front of me had my family's name on it in official ink, and she did not know me.

Or rather — and this is the thing I understood perhaps four seconds too late, standing there like a fool with his cap in his hands — she knew me perfectly well, and she was not going to admit it, and neither, God help me, was I going to be the one to crack first.

"Mr. Yarrow." Her voice was level, official, pitched to carry exactly as far as the walls required and no farther. "Thank you for coming. Sit, please."

I sat. She remained standing a moment, looking at me with the mild professional interest of a woman examining a specimen, and then she crossed to the table and took the chair opposite mine. Up close there were new things: a thin scar through her left eyebrow I didn't remember; tiredness under her eyes that the uniform couldn't quite requisition away; a wedding-band absence where a ring might have been, though why I noticed that I decline to examine even now.

"You received our letter," she said.

"On Tuesday."

"And you've come within the allotted time. Good. Some men run." She said it without judgment, as one notes the weather. "You didn't. That suggests either courage or stupidity, and in my experience the two present identically until questioned."

"I've been told I'm difficult to tell apart from myself."

Something moved at the very edge of her mouth — a twitch, quickly suppressed, that might once have been a laugh. She opened the file in front of her. I caught a glimpse of typescript, a photograph clipped at an angle, a list. My own name appeared on the top sheet. It is a strange experience, seeing your name in a stranger's handwriting inside a government building; it makes you feel briefly like a place on a map, surveyed, bounded, belonging to the crown.

"You are the apprentice of Master Halvard Renner," she said, "cartographer, of Wren Lane."

"I was. He died in October."

"I'm aware." A pause, calibrated. "My condolences. Your master was highly regarded. His coastal work in particular." She turned a page without looking at it. "Tell me about his final months."

This was the question. I had rehearsed a hundred questions and somehow not this one, or rather I had rehearsed it badly, with answers full of flourishes. I gave her the plain version instead, and found, oddly, that the plain version was true enough to hold weight: his health failing through the autumn; his work on the atlas continuing regardless; his trips — I hesitated here, one heartbeat, and felt her notice the hesitation the way a fish notices a hook —

"His trips?" she said.

"He traveled. For surveys. He was thorough; it was his vice." *Eleven times*, said the watchman's ledger I had not yet read, because I had not yet thought to read it; that discovery lay ahead of me, and I can only report what I knew that morning, which was nearly nothing. "Toward the interior. He never said much about them. Masters rarely explain themselves to apprentices. They save it for the grave, where explanation becomes impossible."

"That must be convenient for masters," Captain Voss said, "and trying for apprentices." She closed the file. "Mr. Yarrow. Do you know why your family's name appears in connection with the matter I'm investigating?"

"No, Captain. I hoped you'd tell me."

"We don't generally tell people things. It encourages them." She leaned back, and the light from the window caught her face fully for the first time, and for one unguarded instant the officer's mask slipped — not into recognition, but into something older and wearier underneath it, the face of a woman doing arithmetic she disliked. "But I'll tell you this much, because I believe you don't know, and because men who don't know are more useful than men who guess. Something valuable is going to move through this city before spring. Certain people intend to move it. Your master's name has surfaced in connection with certain documents. Documents that concern routes."

"Routes," I repeated. My voice sounded very far away to me.

"Maps, Mr. Yarrow. Roads. Ways through and ways around. This empire runs on paper, and paper runs on ink, and ink runs on men like your master." She stood, and I stood, because one does. "You'll be asked back. Consider today a formality. Consider it also a kindness — there are people asking these questions who do not begin with kindness, and I preferred you meet me first."

At the door I paused, hand on the frame, and made the mistake of turning around. She was watching me with an expression I spent the walk home failing to classify. Not suspicion. Not warmth. Something between inventory and farewell.

"Captain," I said, "have we met? You seem—" I stopped myself, which was the wisest thing I did all day.

"No, Mr. Yarrow," she said, evenly. "We haven't met. But I grew up near water, and you have a mapmaker's hands. We're both easy to recognize and hard to place." A beat, precise as a surveyor's mark. "Good day."

* * *

I walked home the long way, along the quay, because my legs wanted distance and my head wanted noise and the gulls supply noise cheaply. The half-map sat in my sleeve like a coal. *Certain documents. Routes.* She had said it lightly, the way you drop a line into water to see what rises.

And all the way home, one sentence kept circling me like the gulls: *we haven't met.* Delivered flatly, officially, while her thumb counted against her fingers — the same small motion, I would have sworn it, the same counting she did on the pier at fourteen when she was deciding whether to tell me a secret.

She had known me. She had known me, and she had chosen the uniform, and she had sent me home alive and unhurt and thoroughly frightened, and none of those facts fit together into anything I could draw.

At the shop door I stopped. Through the window I could see the atlas open on my worktable, the unfinished coastline waiting, patient as ever. History, I had always believed, happened elsewhere, to other people, and got written down afterward by survivors.

Standing in the doorway with a captain's voice still in my ears — *you'll be asked back* — I understood, with the particular clarity of a man reading his own name on a boundary stone, that history had finished happening elsewhere.

It had arrived in Vellmark, and it knew my address, and it had sent a woman I once promised the whole world to collect me.
