## 5. Terms of Interrogation

The Army teaches you three ways to conduct an interrogation, and I had been taught all of them by men who could not have talked a fish out of water. There is the hammer, which produces noise and nothing else. There is the long dark room, which produces whatever the subject believes you want to hear. And there is the third way, the one they never put in the manuals: you make the room comfortable, you make yourself reasonable, and you let the man convince himself that telling you the truth was his own idea.

I had booked the small interview room at the Survey Office for this reason. It had a window — barred, but a window — and afternoon light came through it at an angle that made even the government-issue furniture look almost domestic. There was a table, two chairs, a pitcher of water, two glasses. No chains bolted to anything. In Vellmark in winter, this passed for hospitality.

He arrived eleven minutes early, which told me something. Guilty men are late; innocent men are early; frightened men are exactly on time. Eleven minutes early meant he had stood outside in the cold working up his nerve, or he had come early on purpose to see what he could see of the building before anyone saw him. Either answer interested me.

"Apprentice Yarrow," said the clerk at my elbow, and then left us, because I had instructed him to leave us, because everything in this room was going to be arranged down to the minute.

"Captain Voss," he said.

And there it was again — that flicker. I had seen it yesterday when he reported as summoned, the half-second where his eyes did something no stranger's eyes do. Strangers measure you: height, uniform, the braid, the sidearm. He had done all that in one blink and then, in the second blink, done something else entirely. Something warmer and far more dangerous. Recognition, if I had to name it. But recognition of whom?

I knew the Yarrows the way intelligence officers know everyone — by file. Mother, Marta, widowed, keeps a lodging house above the map shop. Brother, Tomas, dockworker, debts with two different lenders, neither of them banks. Father dead of the lung fever, unremarkable. The apprentice himself: twenty-three, no record, no politics, no debts worth the ink. A blank page. In my trade, blank pages are either genuinely boring or very carefully kept, and I have learned never to bet on boring.

But there was another Yarrow in my head, one no clerk had filed. A boy of nineteen at a lakeside survey camp, sunburnt across the nose, who drew maps in the dirt with a stick and explained, with tremendous seriousness, why every map is a lie agreed upon. That boy had called me Lira. That boy had made me a promise so reckless and so kind that I had carried it seven years like a coin sewn into a coat lining — forgotten until it jingled.

That boy was not sitting across from me now. Across from me sat a suspect in a good grey coat with ink stains on his cuff, and I was Captain Elira Voss of the Third Section, and if he recognized me, then one of us had already broken a rule, and it was not going to be me.

"Sit, please." I poured the water myself. Small theatrics. "You've read the letter."

"I've read the letter." He held the glass without drinking. "Though I confess I don't understand it. The Vellmark matter. Nobody will say what the matter *is*."

"No. They won't." I opened the folder in front of me — thin, deliberately thin. "Let me be plainer than the letter was. Three weeks ago, a courier carrying sealed dispatches between this office and the frontier garrison disappeared between here and Kolvary. Two weeks ago, a customs officer at the river station was found to have accepted payments he could not account for. Ten days ago, your master Halvard Reval died."

"He fell ill," Ivo said. "It was his heart."

"It was his heart," I agreed, and watched him. "His heart stopped. The question my superiors ask is whether anything helped it along, and the question I ask is quieter: what was Halvard Reval doing in the last year of his life that required eleven trips toward the southern marshes?"

I had not intended to say *eleven*. It slipped out ahead of the plan, the way true things sometimes do, and I watched it land on him like a dropped glass. His face did not change — apprentices learn stillness the way soldiers do — but his hands changed. The thumb moved against the glass, once, a small circular motion, a man polishing away a fingerprint.

"You keep records of my master's travels?" he asked.

"We keep records of everyone's travels. That is most of what an empire is." I smiled, to take the edge off the truth. "Roads, bridges, ferries, toll ledgers, night watchmen's books. You'd be astonished what a night watchman will write down for a copper and forget he wrote at all."

Something crossed his face then — not fear. Interest. The particular interest of a craftsman meeting another craftsman's work. As if I had complimented a joint he'd cut and he was checking the fit. It lasted less than a second and I would have missed it if I hadn't spent six years training myself not to miss seconds.

"The marshes are poor ground for surveying," he said carefully. "Maybe he was doing it for the atlas. The coastal atlas. He died finishing it."

"The atlas," I said. "Yes. Tell me about the atlas."

So he did, and here is what I learned while he talked: he loved it. Not the way a man loves his work — the way a man loves a grave. Every plate he described, he described with his whole chest, the coastline coming north from the sandbars, the soundings in fathoms, the lighthouse bearings corrected to the year. Halvard Reval had built this boy into the atlas and the atlas into the boy, and when Reval died, some load-bearing part of Ivo Yarrow had gone into the ground with him. You cannot interrogate that. You can only break it, and broken, it tells you nothing, the way a smashed clock tells you nothing about the hour.

Which is why I did what I did next, and I want it recorded somewhere, even if only in my own head, that I knew it was a choice and not a technique.

I closed the folder.

"Master Yarrow," I said. "Look at me. Not the folder. Me."

He looked. Grey eyes, tired, guarded, and underneath the guard — I will swear to this under any court they like — underneath the guard, hope. A terrible, specific hope, the kind a drowning man aims at a passing light.

"The letter you received was bait," I said.

The room went very quiet. Somewhere below us a cart went over the cobbles, and the window buzzed faintly in its frame.

"Bait," he repeated.

"My superiors believe Halvard Reval was involved in something larger than cartography. They do not know what. So they cast a wide letter — named his household, demanded attendance, used language alarming enough to shake loose whatever the family knows. Someone who is merely innocent writes back asking for clarification. Someone who is guilty runs. Someone in between—" I spread my hands. "Someone in between comes in person, eleven minutes early, and sits very still while his thumb does arithmetic on a water glass."

His thumb stopped.

"Am I under arrest?" he asked.

"No."

"Then what is this?"

"This," I said, "is the only honest conversation you are going to be offered, and I would think hard about wasting it. Listen to me. There are people in this city — not in this office, *in this city*, civilians with money — who wanted what Halvard Reval had. Whatever it was. If it was papers, they'll come for papers. If it was knowledge, they'll come for knowledge. And if papers and knowledge fail, they will come for the people who might know where both are kept. Your mother keeps a lodging house above a map shop with a single stair and a lock older than the century. Do you follow me?"

I watched him follow me. It was not pleasant to watch. It was like watching frost cross a window — the understanding spreading outward from some central point, whitening everything it touched.

"You're saying cooperating protects them," he said slowly.

"I'm saying cooperation is the only shape this takes where your family is a name in a ledger instead of a lever in someone's hand. Yes."

"And if I cooperate," he said, "what happens to the atlas? To his work?"

"That depends on what's in it."

"Nothing is in it but coastlines."

"Then you can cooperate with a clear conscience," I said, "and I can close this file, and we can both go home to our suppers. But we both know I didn't bring you here to talk about coastlines, and you didn't come eleven minutes early because you're punctual."

For a moment — one moment — I thought he would tell me. I have replayed that moment since, the way you replay a card you should have played differently, and I still don't know what tipped him. Perhaps nothing tipped him. Perhaps he had decided in the cold outside, standing those eleven minutes under the eaves, exactly how much truth he could afford, and I was watching a man spend according to a budget drawn up without me.

He set down the glass.

"When he died," he said, "I went through his desk. Properly. An apprentice inherits the mess." His voice was level, almost bored, and it was the best acting I had heard in a year, and I include my own. "Ledgers. Correspondence with the Hydrographic Office, mostly complaints about paper stock. Receipts for engraving plates. Nothing about the marshes. Nothing about couriers or customs men or anything that would interest the Third Section. If my master was involved in your matter, Captain, he kept it somewhere I couldn't find, and I'm the one who knew how he hid things. He taught me himself."

Every word of it was smooth. Every word of it was shaped. And every word of it was a lie — I knew it the way you know a forged banknote, not by any single flaw but by the total effect, the too-good surface. Because I had said *eleven trips* and he had not asked me *how do you know*. A man who truly found nothing in that desk would have seized on the number, demanded its source, argued with it. This man had absorbed it, filed it, and moved on, because it confirmed something he already knew.

He was lying to protect something. Or someone. Or some *thing* — and I thought of the atlas, of the way his voice had gone reverent over the plates, and a cold little idea walked into my head and sat down and refused to leave.

*What if the thing everyone wants is the atlas itself?*

I let none of this reach my face. Six years of practice. We looked at each other across the table, the liar and the woman whose job was catching liars, and I felt something I had not felt in an interrogation before, which was respect, and after respect, unwelcome as a draft under a door, something warmer, which I firmly declined to name.

"Thank you," I said. "You'll sign a statement to that effect."

"If I must."

"You must. Signatures are how empires remember things." I slid the paper across. He read it — actually read it, every line, which few do — and signed, and his handwriting was beautiful, of course it was, a draughtsman's hand, each letter placed like a boundary stone.

At the door he paused. He did not turn fully around; he gave me his profile, jaw set, eyes forward, a man looking at a horizon.

"Captain," he said. "The letter named my family. My mother. My brother. When this — matter — is resolved, however it's resolved, I'd take it as a courtesy if their names came out of your files."

"They were never really in my files," I said. "They were in someone else's. I'm trying to get them out of those."

He considered that. Whatever he decided about it, he kept, and then he was gone, boots on the stairs, the outer door, the street, the cold swallowing him.

* * *

I sat alone in the interview room for a long time after, which is a habit of mine and a bad one. The light had gone from amber to grey. I poured myself the second glass of water and drank it warm and flat and thought about lying.

Here is the trouble with my profession, laid out plainly: we assume everyone lies, we train ourselves to catch it, and then one day a man lies to our face, well, for reasons we can see are decent — protection, love, grief — and we discover that the instrument we sharpened has no opinion about *why*. Truth and falsehood are just marks on paper to us. Motive is somebody else's department. The chaplain's, maybe.

He had lied. I was nearly certain. And I had let him sign the lie into the record, and I had done it deliberately, because the alternative was the hammer or the dark room, and because — here is the sentence I will deny writing — because some part of me wanted to see what he would do with the rope I'd left him.

Because there was a second conversation running under the first, the way a river runs under ice. *Eleven minutes early.* The flicker of recognition. The way he had said *Captain Voss* like a man testing whether a floorboard holds. I had worn a different name once, summers at the lake — no title, no braid, a girl with blistered hands helping stretch canvas and getting paid in swimming lessons and bad poetry. He had known me as that girl. I would stake my commission on it. And he had walked into this room, seen the uniform, understood in one blink exactly what I was now, and said nothing. Had given me the gift of not knowing me. Had protected *me*, unprompted, the way he was now protecting whatever was in that desk.

Two loyalties, shaking hands over a table. His and mine. Neither quite trusting the other, both agreeing, for the length of an afternoon, to pretend the handshake was official business.

Colonel Brandt would call this a failure. Brandt measures outcomes: statements signed, files closed, names delivered. By that arithmetic I had gotten a signature on a document full of nothing. But Brandt has never understood that in this work the statement is rarely the point. The point is the man. I now knew four things about Ivo Yarrow I had not known at noon: that he lies beautifully under pressure; that he loves a dead man's atlas enough to risk prison for it; that he recognized me and chose silence, which is a kindness, and kindness in a suspect is rarer than innocence; and that whatever Halvard Reval hid, this boy would go into the fire for it rather than hand it to a uniform he didn't trust.

Including mine. *Especially* mine, probably. I had earned exactly one blink of trust today and spent it warning him about his mother's door lock, and I'd do it again.

I gathered the folder, blew out the lamp I hadn't needed, and went down into the corridor where the clerks were lighting the wall brackets. On the noticeboard by the duty desk, someone had pinned the weekly circular, and my eye caught on a line near the bottom, an administrative nothing, the kind of line you read a hundred times and never hear:

*Requisition approved: corduroy timber, southern district, spring delivery, requisitioning officer Col. D. Brandt.*

Corduroy timber. Logs laid crosswise to make a road passable for heavy loads. Through ground where no road was marked on any chart I had ever pulled from the archive — and I had pulled them all, last month, hunting the ghost of General Aldric's vanished fortune, and found the southern marshes blank. Uncharted. *Deliberately* uncharted, if half of what I suspected about old Reval was true.

Brandt was building a road into the one place in the province no map admitted existed.

I stood in the corridor with my thin folder under my arm and felt the case turn under me like a tide. The boy upstairs was protecting a secret. My colonel was building a road to it. And somewhere between the two, a dead mapmaker's eleven trips lay scattered across the winter like cards waiting to be picked up in order.

I went to find out who had signed the requisition's delivery manifest — and discovered the receiving end of it was a firm whose name I had seen once before, in a file I was not cleared to open, attached to a courteous foreign gentleman who bought maps.
