## 7. The General's Coffin

There is a particular quality to the corridor outside Colonel Brandt's office that I have never encountered anywhere else in the fortress, and I have decided it is the smell of other people's ambitions. Not his — his are too well-bred for scent. I mean the ambitions of the officers who wait here, caps in hand, rehearsing their reports the way actors rehearse in wings. They come out of that room either glowing or grey, and the difference between the two outcomes has almost nothing to do with how well they performed and everything to do with what use he found for them.

I have been coming out grey for eleven months. It is a discipline.

The adjutant waved me through without looking up from his ledger, which I took as a good sign. When Brandt wants an audience to be uncomfortable, he makes them wait. When the waiting room is empty and the door opens on the first knock, it means he has something he wants from you, and wants you to know the transaction has already begun.

"Captain Voss." He was standing at the window, hands clasped behind him, looking out at the harbour as though the ships were reporting to him personally. Perhaps, in his mind, they were. "Sit."

I sat. The chair across from his desk is positioned, I am fairly certain, so that the light falls full on the occupant's face while his own stays half in shadow. He thinks this unsettles people. It does unsettle people. I simply made a note of it, the way one notes the location of a tripwire, and now it no longer works on me, which I suspect annoys him more than anything else I do.

"You interviewed the mapmaker's boy today."

"I did. Ivo Yarrow. Apprentice to Halvard, the surveyor who died in November."

"And?"

"He's frightened, cooperative, and almost certainly knows nothing worth knowing. He answered everything directly. He showed me the shop's ledgers without being asked. If Halvard was moving anything through Vellmark, the boy wasn't part of it."

Brandt turned from the window. In the winter light his face has the colour and texture of old paper — not aged, exactly. Filed. "You believe that?"

"I believe he believes it. There's a difference, and I've flagged it in my report." I had, too. Page three, in the margin, where only a careful reader would find it. Whether Brandt was a careful reader was one of the questions I had not yet resolved about him. "The letter we sent flushed nothing. No visitors to the shop, no burned papers, no midnight errands. Either Yarrow senior's family is clean, or they're better than we are."

"Or the letter went to the wrong address."

"It went to the right address. That's what troubles me. The name Yarrow appears twice in the customs correspondence we intercepted — once in a freight manifest from two years ago, once in a note we've never been able to attribute. Someone in that house was touched by this business. The boy just may not be the someone."

He came around the desk then, slowly, and perched on its front edge rather than sitting behind it — a manoeuvre he deploys when he wants a conversation to feel like a conspiracy of two rather than a superior addressing a subordinate. I have watched him do it to three other officers. It works on all of them.

"Tell me what you make of Aldric," he said.

It was not a change of subject, though it wore one's clothing. I chose my words the way one chooses footing on river ice. "General Aldric died in exile fourteen months ago. Officially of a congestion of the chest. Unofficially—" I paused, because unofficially is a word that requires permission.

"Unofficially," he prompted, with the patience of a man feeding line to a fish he has already decided to keep, "we have no body. We have a death certificate signed by a physician whose practice, upon investigation, consisted largely of signing things. And we have a funeral conducted with remarkable haste for a man of his rank — closed coffin, no public viewing, interment before his own staff could arrive to pay respects."

"You think the coffin was empty."

"I think," Brandt said, "that General Aldric spent thirty years building an army that loved him, and eight months losing a war, and I do not believe a man like that lies down in a foreign bed and dies politely. I think he died of nothing but ambition, and ambition is very hard to kill." He let that sit. Then: "And I think that before he lost his command, he moved something out of the country. Something that needed moving quietly, in pieces, by men he trusted absolutely. Do you know what a war chest weighs, Captain?"

"Gold weighs roughly its own weight in problems, sir."

A flicker at the corner of his mouth. Whether it was amusement or the beginning of irritation, I could not say; with Brandt those two expressions share a border, and like much of the border country in this empire, the exact line is disputed.

"By spring," he continued, "that chest comes home. Or goes somewhere. Either way, it moves through Vellmark — there is no other port with both the traffic and the indifference to question it. My sources tell me arrangements are already being made. Boats chartered. Men hired. And somewhere in this city is the man who can tell us the route."

"The route, sir?"

"Aldric won't move gold down the king's road. He'll need ways that aren't charted — or ways that were charted once, by one man, and then erased." He looked at me then with an expression I have thought about considerably since. It was the look of a man checking whether a tool will hold an edge. "Halvard surveyed the eastern marches for twenty years. Every marsh crossing, every timber track, every ford that drowns in spring and every causeway that holds. If anyone drew the map Aldric needs, it was him."

"Halvard is dead, sir."

"Yes. Conveniently." He said it flatly, and let the flatness do the work. "Heart failure, alone, in a city where he knew everyone. A man with his habits does not die alone unless someone wanted him to. And his papers — his *complete* papers — have never surfaced. An apprentice inherited a shop and some plates. Where are the field notes, Captain? Twenty years of surveys don't evaporate."

"They may simply be filed, sir. Surveyors are hoarders. His office—"

"I have seen his office. Through your report. You described it as 'orderly.'" He straightened off the desk and walked back toward the window, and I understood the audience was ending, or rather that it was entering the portion that mattered. "Here is what I want from you. Continue with the boy. Gently. He trusts you — or he will, given time and repetition. Find me Halvard's field notes. Find me the route. And when Aldric's people begin to move — and they will, by the thaw — I want to know before they do."

"And if Aldric himself surfaces, sir?"

Brandt was quiet for a moment. Out in the harbour, a steamer was being warped against the ice floes, small and black against the white, and I remember thinking it looked like a beetle trying to cross a tablecloth.

"If Aldric surfaces," he said at last, "he becomes a matter for the Ministry, the courts, the newspapers — a spectacle. Trials. Commutations. Men like Aldric have friends in high rooms even after they fall; he might walk out of a courtroom in two years and into a country estate. Dead men are simpler, Captain. But dead men can't testify, and living men with fortunes can buy governments. Either way—" he turned, and the light caught him squarely for once, and I saw in his face something I had been refusing to see for eleven months, something patient and green and growing, "—either way, the man matters far less than the money. The money changes hands, and whoever receives it owes a debt that cannot be entered in any ledger. Do you understand me?"

I said I believed I did.

"Good." He returned to his desk and opened a folder — my folder, I realized; my own reports, annotated in his hand, margins dense with his small precise script. He had read me closely. That should have flattered me. What it actually did was remind me of the night watchman's maxim: the thing that watches you most carefully is the thing preparing to swallow you. "You've done good work this year. Quiet work. It hasn't gone unnoticed at the Ministry."

"Thank you, sir."

"There's a majority in it. For an officer who delivers the route." He said it the way one sets a plate in front of a guest — casually, as though it cost nothing, as though the food were not baited. "Vellmark is a posting officers endure. A majority and a transfer west would suit a woman of your record, I imagine."

I imagined several replies. I selected the one with the fewest edges. "It would suit any officer, sir. I'll continue as ordered."

"See that you do." And then, as I reached the door, without looking up: "One more thing. The apprentice — if he finds anything in the master's effects, I want to hear of it from you within the day. Before it reaches anyone else. Anyone at all. There are parties in this city, Captain, who would pay a great deal for Halvard's papers, and I'd prefer our discoveries stayed ours."

*Our*, I noted. Not *the Ministry's*. Not *the Crown's*. Ours — meaning his, with me attached like a satchel.

I thanked him again and left, and my boots sounded very loud on the stones of that ambitious corridor.

* * *

I did not go directly back to the survey office. I walked instead along the quay, because I think best with water in front of me and cold on my face, and because I needed to stand somewhere and admit to myself what I had just heard.

Colonel Brandt believes General Aldric is alive. Very possibly he is right; I have reviewed the same death certificate and it has the authenticity of a laundry ticket. But listen to what he actually said, stripped of uniform. The man matters less than the money. The trial would be a spectacle. The money buys debts that no ledger records. He was not describing a counter-intelligence operation. He was describing an acquisition.

And the majority — the transfer west, the career that officers like me are supposed to want — offered against the delivery of a smuggled route like a purse set on a fence post. Take it and go. It was generous. It was also, I understood, the sound of a man pricing me. Everything has a price with Brandt; loyalty is simply the item he prefers to buy cheapest, because he believes it is always in surplus.

I stopped at the rail where the ice had piled against the pilings, plates of it shoved up and standing on edge like the pages of a book someone had dropped, and I made myself ask the question properly, because a question deferred is a question that answers itself later, badly.

If I found Halvard's route tomorrow — found it whole, found it true — would I put it on Brandt's desk?

I stood there long enough for my hands to go numb inside my gloves, and I noticed that the answer did not arrive. It simply declined to attend. And a soldier learns to be frightened of many things, but nothing frightens quite like an answer that will not report for duty.

Because here is what eleven months in Vellmark had taught me that I had not yet said aloud, even to myself: duty is a clean word until you watch it being used. I had watched it used all winter. Used to open letters. Used to justify a dead man's files being read by men who would trade them. Duty, in Brandt's mouth, smelled increasingly of something else entirely — and I am a woman raised on a farm who knows the smell of a barn when she walks into one, whatever flag is flying over it.

There was also, I confess, the other thing. The smaller thing. The thing I had held very still about all afternoon while the mapmaker's boy sat across from me answering questions with his collar too tight.

He had grown into his face. That was my first thought, unprofessional and irretrievable, delivered to me somewhere behind the sternum before I had finished saying his name. Ivo Yarrow, who at fifteen had ink on his fingers permanently and opinions about contour lines that he defended like theology. He had looked at me across that table with the particular attention of someone recognizing, and choosing silence, and I had done the same, and between us we had constructed a fiction so complete that for forty minutes I was able to pretend I had never spent a summer being nobody at a lakeside camp, laughing at a boy who swore — laughing, meaning it — that if ever I needed to vanish, he would draw me the way out.

I had told myself it was tact. It was tact. It was also cowardice, and I have never confused the two since.

Because the letter we sent to his shop — *my* letter, drafted in this building, sealed with wax I may as well have softened myself — had gone to a frightened family, and I had watched him flinch at the sight of official ink and thought: I did this. And then thought, immediately after, with the ruthlessness I reserve for myself: yes, and you would do it again tomorrow, because the alternative was watching some other officer do it worse.

That is the arithmetic of this work. You keep paying in coin you tell yourself is counterfeit.

I turned from the rail. Across the water, the fortress walls held the last of the light, and above them the garrison flag snapped in a wind that had teeth in it. Somewhere behind me, in a warm office smelling of paper and other men's ambitions, Colonel Brandt was writing my name into plans I had not yet seen — and I understood, walking back through the gates with my report unsigned in my satchel, that I had just been issued two kinds of orders. The ones written down.

And the ones that were coming.
