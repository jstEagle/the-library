## 8. Summers We Were Nobody

There is a rule in mapmaking that I have broken exactly once, and I broke it the summer I was nineteen: never let the territory matter more than the map. A surveyor who falls in love with a place stops measuring it honestly. He rounds the bay where he swam. He exaggerates the hill he climbed to see a girl. The line on paper begins to flatter the land instead of describe it, and then it is not a map at all — it is a memory wearing a map's clothes.

I am telling you this now because you will need it later, when I tell you about the plate I drew twice. But first I have to tell you about the lake.

* * *

Halvard sent me north in June of 1891 with a theodolite, two crates of instruments, and a letter of introduction to the Survey Corps field camp at Lake Veren. "Six weeks," he said, not looking up from his desk. "Triangulate the eastern shore. Do not fall in the lake. Do not fall in love." He said the second part without any particular emphasis, the way he said everything, which was why I didn't hear it as a warning until years afterward, when it had already come true.

The camp sat on a shelf of granite above the water, eleven tents and a cook shed and a flagpole flying a survey pennant so faded it looked apologetic. There were nine soldiers attached to us for protection and hauling, and one of them was a lieutenant's daughter with no rank at all, which was the first strange thing about her.

She introduced herself as Voss. Just Voss. She was seventeen and she carried a rifle slung across her back like an afterthought and she corrected my bearing calculations before I'd finished writing them down, standing at my elbow with her arms crossed, smelling of pine resin and gun oil.

"Your third decimal," she said. "You've carried an error forward."

"It's within tolerance."

"Tolerance is what people say when they're tired." She took the pencil out of my hand — out of my hand, without asking, the audacity of it — and fixed the figure. Then she gave the pencil back and looked at me as if daring me to object.

I should explain that I had spent my entire life up to that point being either invisible or useful, usually both at once. Apprentices are furniture that can do arithmetic. Nobody corrected me; nobody bothered. And here was this girl with sunburnt cheekbones and a soldier's posture treating my work as though it mattered enough to be wrong.

"You're not army," I said. "You're not survey corps. What are you?"

"Bored," she said. "My father commands the detachment. He brought me because my mother thought a summer outdoors would improve my disposition."

"Has it?"

"No," she said, and almost smiled, which from her turned out to be worth more than other people's laughter. "But the lake has improved yours. You stood up straighter when I walked over."

That was Lira Voss. She saw things. I want to be clear about this before anything else, because everything that happened later — the letter under seal, the cell corridor, all of it — grew out of this one fact like a root out of stone: she noticed what other people missed, and she could not stop doing it even when noticing would cost her. It was a talent. It was also, I understand now, the whole of her tragedy, packed into a single habit at seventeen years old.

* * *

We fell into a routine that first week, the way water finds a channel. Mornings I shot my angles along the eastern shore while she prowled the treeline, ostensibly keeping watch for smugglers — the lake touched the frontier districts, and there was talk — but mostly keeping watch for interesting things, which was a different profession altogether. Afternoons I plotted in the big tent. Evenings she appeared at my table uninvited and read over my shoulder.

"Who taught you to draft?" she asked one night, watching me ink a shoreline.

"My master. Halvard Renner, in Vellmark. He does the coastal atlases."

"The ones they sell to shipping firms."

"The ones everyone steals from," I said, with the modest pride of an apprentice quoting his master's reputation. "Half the charts on the Baltic are traced from his plates by men who've never seen salt water."

"And you'll do that someday? Trace coastlines until your eyes go?"

"I'll make them. Not trace. Make."

"Mm." She picked up my dividers, weighed them, set them down again. "And if someone told you to draw the shore wrong? To leave something off?"

I laughed. "Why would anyone—"

"Because maps are orders waiting to happen," she said. "My father says a chart is a weapon with the violence printed small. Whoever decides what goes on the paper decides what the ships do. What the armies do." She said it lightly, testing it against me like a coin bitten for gold. "So. Would you draw it wrong?"

"Halvard says a mapmaker who lies once is finished. Not punished — finished. Because after that, nothing he draws means anything, including the true parts."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only kind of answer I have. I've never been asked."

She looked at me for a long moment in the lamplight, and whatever she found there, she kept it. Later — much later — I would understand that she had been collecting answers to that question her whole life, because of who her family was, because of what she would become. That night she just nodded once, satisfied or pretending to be, and said, "Show me how you fair a curve. Mine look like a drunk drew them."

So I taught her drafting in the evenings, and she taught me the land in the mornings — which trees were birch and which were their impostors, how to read a bog by its smell, how the old logging roads ran under the moss like veins under skin. She knew the country the way I knew paper, and between us we covered everything, and neither of us mentioned that the six weeks were going to end, because mentioning it would have made it real, and we were both, in our separate ways, experts at not making things real.

* * *

Here is the thing I want you to hold onto, the thing the whole chapter turns on: that summer we were nobody.

Not nobody in the sad way. Nobody in the free way. I was not the Yarrow apprentice with a dead father's debts and a mother counting coins behind the shop curtain. She was not the commandant's daughter being polished into some garrison marriage. Out there we had no names that meant anything. The soldiers called me "the pencil." They called her — well, they called her things I won't repeat, and she made them regret it, and after that they called her sir, half in mockery and half in genuine terror, and she answered to it.

Nobody. Two nobodies on a lake at the top of the world, with the whole century still unspent in front of us.

We raced rowboats across the narrows and she cheated every time, starting early and blaming the current. We ate char cooked on a flat stone and she claimed she'd caught hers honestly, which the absence of fishing tackle on her person suggested was a lie. We climbed the ridge above the camp one evening in July to watch the light go long and coppery across the water, and she lay back in the grass with her hands behind her head and said, apropos of nothing, "Do you ever think about running away?"

"From what?"

"From anything. From the whole arrangement. The life somebody else drew for you before you could read the drawing."

I thought about the shop in Vellmark, the smell of size and lampblack, the ledger of debts my father had left, my mother's face going carefully blank whenever money came up. I thought about Halvard's voice saying *do not fall in love* in exactly the tone he used for *mind the humidity.*

"Sometimes," I said.

"There's a way out of anywhere," she said. "That's the secret nobody tells you. Every place on earth touches another place. There's always a door. Most people just never look at the walls."

"You've looked at the walls."

"I'm always looking at the walls." She rolled her head toward me. Grass in her hair, a mosquito bite on her jaw she kept scratching and losing the argument with. "Someday I might need a wall looked at very carefully. Professionally."

"Lira—"

"If it happens." She said it fast, the way you step onto thin ice quickly so it doesn't have time to decide about you. "If it ever happens. Would you draw it? The way out? For me?"

And here is where I must confess to the exact quality of that moment, because the truth of it matters more than the romance of it. I laughed. I did. I laughed, because she was seventeen and armed and asking me to imagine a world where *she* needed rescuing, this girl who had already corrected three of my surveys and terrified a corporal into saluting her, and the idea was so obviously impossible that laughing was the only honest response.

But I meant it. That's the terrible arithmetic of it — I laughed *and* I meant it, both at once, the way you can laugh at a funeral and still grieve. I said:

"Lira Voss. If you ever need to disappear — truly need to, if the walls close in — I will draw you the way out myself. Every road, every ford, every door. In full. Signed."

"In full," she repeated. "Signed."

"A Yarrow doesn't promise otherwise."

"What's a Yarrow?" she said, grinning, and threw a grass stem at my face, and I caught her wrist, and for a moment neither of us moved, and the lake below us went on reflecting a sky that had no idea what was coming.

Then she pulled her hand free, casually, cruelly, the way she did everything, and said, "Witnessed," pointing at a crow on a snag above us. "He saw the whole thing. Crows live thirty years. He'll hold you to it."

I believed none of it and all of it. That is, I believed it the way you believe summer — completely, without thinking it would ever have to be cashed.

* * *

The last week of August, her father received transfer orders and the detachment struck camp in a single gray morning, tents folding like the whole season being put away in a drawer. She came to the tent where I was packing the plates at dawn — she was already in traveling clothes, already half the officer she would become, and it frightened me a little, how quickly the nobody could be taken back.

"Well, pencil," she said.

"Voss."

She looked at my open crate, at the neat foam of wood shavings around the instruments, at everything except my face, and I understood she was doing deliberately what I did accidentally — refusing to make a thing real.

"Keep your decimals honest," she said.

"Keep your walls in view."

And then she did the most Lira thing possible, which was to turn a goodbye into an inspection. She stepped close, straightened my collar with two brisk tugs like a sergeant fixing a recruit, looked me in the eye from four inches away — brown eyes, flecked, I remembered the flecks for years — and said quietly, so no one else could hear:

"Crows live thirty years. Don't forget which bird is holding the debt."

Then she walked down to the boats, and I watched the launch get small, and I went back to my plates, and I drew the eastern shore of Lake Veren with a rounding on the northern bay that no honest survey supports, because that was where we had swum, and I have never corrected it. It is in an archive somewhere now, flattering the land. Let it stand.

* * *

For seven years, that was all. Seven years of letters that thinned from monthly to seasonal to a card at midwinter to nothing — the natural decay of a peacetime acquaintance, I told myself, the ordinary entropy of distance. Seven years of Halvard aging at the drafting table, of the atlas growing plate by plate, of history happening elsewhere to other people, which was the arrangement I believed I had with the world.

Seven years, and then the letters stopped entirely, and I learned why from a newspaper: Lieutenant Elira Voss, decorated for service on the frontier, transferred to military intelligence, mentioned — the name alone, bare, in a list — in connection with certain internal security matters.

Internal security matters. I read that phrase the way you touch a stove you've been told is hot. The girl who looked at walls had been given a uniform and ordered through them, and the papers spoke of her in the language of locks.

I thought of the crow. Thirty years, minus seven. Twenty-three years left on the debt, I calculated, sitting in the shop with the news-sheet trembling slightly in my hands, and then I caught myself calculating it at all, and put the paper down, and told myself I was a fool. Promises made at nineteen on a ridge, witnessed by a bird, sealed with grass stems — those don't survive contact with the world. Everyone knows that. Everyone sensible knows that.

Halvard found me still staring at the folded paper an hour later. He looked at it, and then at me, and said nothing, which from Halvard was a paragraph. He went back to his coastlines. Three years after that he was dead, and the letter under seal arrived, and the name *Voss* was spoken aloud in my own shop by a woman in a captain's uniform who did not know me, who had never met me, whose eyes passed over my face like light passing over glass —

Except.

Except that she paused. Half a heartbeat, no more, on the second day of questioning, when I handed her a plate and our fingers did not quite touch, she paused, and something moved behind those flecked brown eyes like a fish under ice, and then it was gone, and she was Captain Voss again, reading my master's coastline for errors.

Carried forward. Third decimal. Tolerance is what people say when they're tired.

I stood there in the survey office holding a map I had drawn, being questioned under seal, while the crow on the summer snag shifted its weight and looked at me with one dry eye, and I understood — with the calm of a man watching his own floodwater rise past the doorstep — that the walls had closed in on her at last, and she had come looking for the person who promised, laughing, meaning it, to draw her the way out.

And that somewhere in Halvard's effects — in the desk I had searched, in the papers I had catalogued, in the atlas lying open downstairs — was a route I had not yet learned to read, waiting to find out whether a promise made to a girl by a lake could survive being called in by a captain of intelligence in a country that had decided she was its enemy.
