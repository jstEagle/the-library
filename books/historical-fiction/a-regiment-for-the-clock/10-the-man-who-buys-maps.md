## 10. The Man Who Buys Maps

The bell above the shop door had a cracked note in it — Halvard never fixed it, saying a mapmaker's shop should announce visitors the way weather announces itself: imperfectly, and with a suggestion of trouble. For six years I'd heard that bell and thought nothing of it. Customers came for survey instruments, for schoolroom globes, for the cheap printed maps of Vellmark harbor that tourists bought and then lost. The bell rang, I looked up, life stayed ordinary.

On the fourth day after my summons to the Survey Office, it rang at quarter past four on a gray afternoon, and the man who came through the door was not the sort of person who lost tourist maps. He was the sort of person other people were lost by.

He was perhaps fifty, dressed in the kind of dark wool that doesn't advertise its cost, with a face like a well-used ledger — every entry recorded, nothing wasted. He closed the door behind him with a care that suggested he'd noticed the cracked bell and found it charming, or useful.

"Master Yarrow?" His accent was southern, soft around the vowels, the kind that had money folded into it somewhere. "I hope I'm not intruding on the working day."

"You're intruding slightly," I said. "But the working day is mostly me arguing with a coastline, so you're welcome company."

He smiled as though I'd said something cleverer than I had. "Then may I impose a little longer." He crossed the shop without hurrying, and here was the first thing wrong about him: he didn't look at the merchandise the way customers do, with the vague appetite of people deciding whether they want something. He looked at it the way I did. The brass alidades. The plane table with its worn legs. The long drawers of plates under the counter, each one labeled in Halvard's hand. He catalogued. I watched him do it and felt something cold settle behind my sternum, because I recognized the habit from the mirror.

"Your master's work has a reputation," he said, stopping before the framed proof of the northern coastline that hung by the window. "Even beyond the province."

"Halvard Reiner drew maps for thirty years," I said. "Reputation seems fair payment."

"Thirty-one," the stranger said mildly. "He began in the spring of '66, with the coastal survey out of Dravik. There's a plate of his from that year — the estuary, unfinished, the marshes left blank. Collectors speak of it."

I hadn't moved, but I had stopped breathing for a moment, which amounts to the same thing. The estuary plate. The blank marshes. I had spent three nights now with half a map cut from some larger sheet, trying to make its edges confess where they'd come from, and here was a stranger reciting Halvard's career like a psalm.

"You knew him," I said.

"I corresponded with him. On matters of cartography." He turned from the frame and offered his hand. "Ferenc. I'm a collector, Master Yarrow. Of maps, chiefly. And papers relating to them."

His hand was dry and warm and entirely unremarkable, which I later understood was the point. Everything about Ferenc was designed to be forgotten except what he wanted remembered.

"A collector," I said. "We get the occasional one. They usually want the harbor proofs. Those aren't for sale either, but they like to ask."

"I don't want the harbor proofs." He said it without emphasis, the way you'd correct a small factual error. "I want the remaining papers of Halvard Reiner. Surveys, notes, drafts, correspondence. The whole estate of his craft. I'm prepared to pay—" and he named a figure.

I'll write it down here because writing it makes it real: he named a sum equal to eleven years of the shop's income. Eleven years. Enough to clear the mortgage, enough to keep Mother in comfort until she died of something other than worry, enough to send Tomas wherever men go to escape their debts. Enough, in short, to buy back everything the sealed letter had put at risk, with change left over.

"That's an absurd number," I said.

"It's an accurate number," Ferenc replied. "Absurdity is only accuracy applied to things people prefer not to price. Your master understood that better than most. Why do you think he kept his best work unsigned?"

* * *

I made tea, because refusing would have been rude and because I wanted my hands doing something while my head worked. He sat at the workbench like a man who had sat at a hundred workbenches, and he drank the tea properly, and he complimented the mending job on the shop's second astrolabe, which no visitor had ever noticed existed.

"Mr. Ferenc," I said, "you quoted Halvard's first commission to the season. You know about the estuary plate. And you've offered me more money than this shop earns in a decade for papers whose market value, even to genuine collectors, is a fraction of that." I set down my cup. "So let's skip the part where we pretend this is about collecting."

For the first time, something moved behind the ledger of his face. Not surprise — I don't think Ferenc was ever surprised — but a kind of professional appreciation, the way a chess player nods when the other side sees the trap two moves early.

"Your master spoke well of you," he said. "In his letters. 'The boy draws coastlines from memory,' he wrote, 'and hates himself for how well it goes.'"

"He wrote that?"

"He wrote many things." Ferenc set down his cup and folded his hands, and I had the sense of a door opening onto a room I couldn't see into. "Here is what I can tell you honestly, Master Yarrow, because you've earned honesty by not flinching at arithmetic. There are parties — several, and they do not like each other — who believe your late master possessed documents of strategic rather than aesthetic value. Some of those parties are willing to pay. Others are less patient. My offer is, if you will forgive the phrase, the civilized option."

"The letter from the government," I said. "Was that yours?"

"Good heavens, no." And he meant it; I could hear it. "That letter comes from people with uniforms. I am not a man with uniforms. Uniforms complicate transactions." A pause, delicate as a knife laid on silk. "Though I observe they have already complicated yours."

"My family's name is in government files because of whatever Halvard carried. Now a stranger offers me eleven years' income for his desk contents. What exactly happens, Mr. Ferenc, to sellers who decline?"

"What happens," Ferenc said, rising, "is nothing. That is the tragedy of your position and I want you to understand I take no pleasure in it. Nothing happens today. Nothing happens next week. But the parties remain interested, and interest of this kind does not decay — it compounds. A declined offer is not a refusal, Master Yarrow. It is merely a higher opening bid."

He took a card from his breast pocket — heavy stock, no name, only an address in the old town and, embossed below it, a small device: a compass rose with one point broken off. He laid it on the workbench between us, square, deliberate, the way Halvard used to lay down a finished proof.

"When you reconsider," he said, "send word. No obligation attaches to inquiry. And Master Yarrow—" he paused at the door, hand on the latch, and gave me one last look over his shoulder, courteous to the bone, "—do mind the shop's security. Papers of value attract damp, mice, and worse. It would be a shame if anything were lost before its owner understood what it was worth."

The bell rang its cracked note behind him.

* * *

I stood in the empty shop for a long time. Outside, the lamplighter was making his rounds along the quay, and gulls argued over something in the gutters, and the ordinary world went on being ordinary, which suddenly felt like a performance I could no longer sit in the audience for.

Eleven years of income. He'd chosen the number carefully — large enough to be temptation, precise enough to say *we know exactly what your life costs*. And the threat had been perfect in its way: no threat at all, just a forecast. Damp, mice, and worse. *It would be a shame if anything were lost.*

I went to the hidden compartment and took out the half-map and read it again by lamplight — the clean cut edge, the marsh symbols, Halvard's cramped annotation in the margin: *the route is not for sale.* Not *cannot be sold.* Not *must not.* *Is not.* As though sale were a thing that simply hadn't happened yet, and might.

Had Halvard sat where I sat, across from some earlier version of Ferenc, hearing some earlier version of that same polite arithmetic? Had he named a number too high, or refused outright, and gone home thinking the matter closed? The master had died in the autumn, on wet stairs, of a heart that stopped — an honest death, everyone said. Everyone said it quickly, which I was beginning to understand was how lies travel fastest.

I put the half-map away and turned the card over in my fingers. The compass rose with its broken point. A rose that couldn't show north. A rose for people who didn't want to be found.

Then I heard footsteps in the street outside — unhurried ones, pausing opposite the shop — and through the window glass I watched a figure stand in the lamplight long enough to be sure I saw him, tip his hat to the shop front with ironic precision, and walk away toward the harbor.

They weren't going to wait for me to reconsider. The market, as Ferenc would put it, had opened.

And I understood, standing alone among Halvard's instruments with a stranger's card in my hand, that the bidding had never really been about the papers at all.
