## 12. Half a Promise

The bell over the shop door had a particular voice for Captain Voss.

I'd learned this in the week since my summons to the Survey Office — that every visitor rang the bell differently, the way every hand holds a pen differently, and that after enough years behind a counter you can read a person's whole biography in the first three seconds of brass on iron. The courier from the Ministry rang twice, sharp and official, like a man who wanted witnesses. Ferenc — when he'd come back a second time, all patience and cologne — didn't ring at all; he simply let himself in as though the shop were a room in his own house, which was its own kind of signature. My mother rang once and waited, always, even though she owned half of what stood behind the counter.

Captain Elira Voss rang once, softly, and then stood in the doorway with her collar up and her cap pulled low, looking for all the world like a woman who hoped nobody was home.

It was Thursday evening, near closing, the light going amber through the front glass and catching the dust that no amount of sweeping ever fully defeated. I was at the big table with Halvard's plates spread out under weights, pretending very hard to be doing routine work — checking margins, squaring borders, the kind of labor a man does with his hands while his mind is somewhere it shouldn't be. Which, in my case lately, was everywhere: the marshes, the cut edge of a half-map, the word *unpublished* sitting in my chest like a swallowed coin.

"Closed," I said, without looking up. "Come back at nine."

"I won't."

I knew the voice before I knew anything else. Not the Survey Office voice — that one had been lacquered, official, pitched to carry across a table with a stenographer taking notes. This was lower. Rougher at the edges. A voice I had heard across a campfire eleven summers ago, laughing at my terrible attempt to roast a fish.

I looked up. She pushed back her hood, and there she was — not Captain Voss of military intelligence but someone older and wearier than the girl at the lake, with shadows under her eyes that hadn't been earned by any honest work.

"Lira," I said. Just that. Her name, the old name, dropped into the middle of my shop like a stone into still water.

"Don't." She closed the door behind her and turned the little latch, which did nothing against anyone determined, but made us both feel as though a line had been drawn. "Not here. Not where the walls have ears. Walls in this city have better hearing than watchmen have eyes."

"That's a strange thing for an intelligence officer to say about her own capital."

"It's a true thing," she said. "I've stopped saying strange things. They get a man promoted."

She crossed the shop slowly, the way she'd moved through the survey camp — reading everything, touching nothing. Her eyes went over the plates under their weights, the drawers with their brass labels, the great flat cabinet where Halvard's finished work slept in its paper shrouds. I watched her look and understood, with a small cold feeling, that she wasn't admiring. She was inventorying.

"You came alone?" I asked.

"I came alone."

"And off duty?"

"Off duty is a fiction we tell ourselves between orders." She stopped at the table, opposite me, and put both hands flat on the wood — a gesture so deliberately unthreatening that it could only have been rehearsed. "Ivo. I'm going to say some things now that could have me shot if they're repeated. I need you to understand that before I say them, because afterward you'll want to pretend you misheard, and I can't afford for either of us to be stupid."

"That's a promising opening."

"It's the only opening I have." Something flickered across her face then — not quite a smile, but the ghost of one, the muscle memory of a girl who used to laugh easily. "You haven't changed. You make jokes when you're frightened. You did it the day the canoe nearly swamped us and you did it just now."

"The canoe was your fault. You steered like a woman fleeing a crime scene."

"I was twelve and furious with my father. It amounted to the same thing." She let the ghost of the smile die properly this time, and what was left underneath was tired and serious and, I thought, a little afraid — though whether of me or for me, I couldn't yet say. "Sit down, Ivo."

"I'm standing."

"Sit down anyway. What I have to tell you goes down easier seated."

I sat. It seemed the sort of evening where one did as one was told by people holding secrets, since everyone else in Vellmark that season seemed to be holding them too.

* * *

She didn't sit. She stayed on her feet, moving along the table's edge like a cat deciding whether a room was safe, and she talked quietly, without flourish, the way people talk when they've decided that plainness is the last honesty available to them.

"General Aldric is dead," she said. "That's the official account. Died of fever in custody at Kesselhof, buried in the prison yard, witnessed by two chaplains and a doctor. There's a grave. There's paperwork. There is, I'm told, a very dignified report in three copies."

"But."

"But the grave has never been opened. The doctor who signed the certificate retired within the month and moved to a town so small it doesn't appear on any map I've seen — and I've seen most of them. And the war chest Aldric was supposed to have salted away before his fall — forty thousand crowns, some say sixty, in coin and paper and whatever a man like that keeps in diamonds — that chest has never surfaced. Not one coin of it. Not in any bank, not in any port, not in any exile's pocket from here to Vienna."

I turned a weight a quarter-turn on the table, needing something to do with my hands. "Men hide money well. That's rather the point of money."

"Not this well. Not for four years. Money wants to move, Ivo. It wants to become houses and uniforms and influence. Money that stays perfectly still for four years isn't money anymore. It's a plan."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere out on the street a cart went by, and I heard the driver curse the ice-rutted cobbles, and the sound passed over the shop and was gone, and the silence it left behind felt very large.

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because of the letter."

"The letter named my family."

"The letter was bait," she said, flatly. "I wrote the list of names that went into it. Yours was on it because your master's was, and your master's was because Halvard Reiner surveyed more of that border country than any living man, and because in the last year of his life he made eleven trips toward the marshes and filed survey reports on none of them."

My blood did something complicated. Eleven trips. I hadn't told her about the watchman's ledger — hadn't told anyone — and here it was coming back at me out of her mouth like a thrown boomerang. I kept my face still. Mapmakers learn to keep their faces still; a cartographer who flinches at bad news doesn't last long in territory disputes.

"You've been busy," I said.

"So have you." She said it lightly, but her eyes didn't. "Eleven trips, Ivo. Toward the one corridor in the entire frontier that no army has ever charted, because no army has ever managed it. The marshes eat roads. They ate a pontoon company in the last war — I've read the after-action report, men drowned in mud up to their chins with their boots full of water and their rifles pointing at the sky. If Halvard Reiner found a way through, he found the only way through. And he died for it, and I don't believe he died of the pneumonia on his death certificate any more than I believe Aldric died of fever."

The shop felt colder. I thought of the hidden compartment, the clean-cut half-map, the note in Halvard's cramped hand: *the route is not for sale.* I thought of how carefully I had said nothing about it at my interrogation, and how she had watched me lie, and how I had believed — arrogantly, comfortably — that the lie had landed.

"You think he was murdered."

"I think powerful men do not tolerate a door only they don't hold the key to." She finally sat, across from me, and leaned forward, forearms on the table, close enough that I could see the fine lines at the corners of her eyes that the lake summers hadn't put there. "Now listen to me, because this is the part that matters. The chest is real. Aldric may be alive. And there are men in this city — men with money, men with rank, men whose names would make you laugh if I told you, because you'd refuse to believe them — who would burn this whole coastline to get their hands on that route. Not for the country. For themselves."

"Ferenc."

Something moved behind her eyes — surprise, quickly mastered, and then something almost like approval. "You've met him."

"He buys maps. Courteously. He offered me sums that would buy the building this shop stands in, and when I refused he thanked me for my time and left a card, and somehow the leaving of the card felt worse than a knife on the table would have."

"That's Ferenc. He's never once needed to raise his voice in thirty years of acquiring things people don't wish to sell." She glanced toward the window, at the darkening street, then back. "He isn't working alone. And neither, officially, am I. Which brings me to the reason I'm here, off duty, risking my neck in a mapmaker's shop after closing."

"Which is?"

"Honesty," she said, and laughed — short, bitter, real. "God help me. Honesty. I've spent six weeks inside this investigation wearing the uniform and saying the correct things, and every day the correct things smell worse. My colonel wants the chest. The Ministry wants the general, dead again and properly. Ferenc wants everything. And somewhere in the middle of all of it there's a route through the marshes that currently exists in exactly one place —"

She stopped. Looked at me. Waited.

"— in Halvard's plates," I finished, because she was going to wait all night, and because lying to her a second time in one conversation suddenly seemed less like prudence and more like insult.

"In Halvard's plates," she agreed softly. "Or in the head of the man who inherited them. I don't know which, and I've stopped pretending I don't know that you know."

* * *

We sat there a moment, the two of us, with the truth finally on the table between us like an object we could both see, and I found that my hands had gone cold around the edge of the table and my heart was doing something undignified. Not fear, exactly. Or not only fear.

Because here was the thing I had been carrying since the Survey Office, since before that, since the letter arrived in its official envelope with its official seal: the memory of a summer when I had promised this woman, laughing, sunburned, seventeen years old and certain of nothing except that I meant it, that if she ever needed to disappear, I would draw her the way out. I had forgotten it for years — the way you forget a debt that costs nothing to service. And then she had walked into my shop wearing a captain's coat, and the debt had woken up and started charging interest.

"Do you remember," I said, before I could stop myself, "the lake camp. The summer of the flood year."

Her stillness was total. Even her breathing seemed to pause.

"You were furious with your father. You'd run away to the survey camp because he wanted you to apply for the academy and you wanted — I don't know what you wanted. To be nobody for a month. You told me that. You said, 'Here I'm just the girl who can read a sextant,' and you said it like other girls say a prayer." I kept my eyes on the plate in front of me, on the neat engraved curve of a coastline Halvard had drawn before I was born. "And one night, at the fire, you asked me — joking, I thought, mostly joking — what I'd do if you ever needed to get out of the country. And I said I'd draw you the way out. Any border, any season. I said a mapmaker's promise was the only kind worth having, because it couldn't be burned or confiscated. It lived in the head."

"Yes," she said. Very quietly.

"I meant it."

"I know you meant it. That's why I'm here." She exhaled, long and slow, and some of the captain went out of her posture, and for a moment the girl at the lake sat across from me, twenty-two years old and carrying things no girl should carry. "Ivo, I'm not asking you to give me the route. Do you understand? I'm not here to requisition it. If I wanted it officially, I'd have brought soldiers and a warrant, and you'd have given it up or gone to prison, and either way the state would own it by Friday. I'm here because of what happens *after* Friday."

"Go on."

"If Brandt gets it, it becomes leverage — he'll trade it, or sell it, or use it to stage an interception that makes him a hero and fills his own pockets, and I genuinely no longer know which of those frightens me most. If Ferenc gets it, it becomes merchandise — sold to the highest bidder, probably two highest bidders, because that's how he works, selling the same door key to every house on the street. Either way, the route stops being a route. It becomes a weapon pointed at whoever's poorest when it fires." Her hands flattened on the table again. "But if it's found first by someone with nothing to gain — someone who just wants the thing drawn honestly and kept honestly — then maybe, maybe, it stays what Halvard meant it to stay. A way through for people who need one. Not a prize."

"And you think that someone is me."

"I think that someone is the man who told me a mapmaker's promise was the only kind worth having." She held my eyes. "I think it's the man who lied to me beautifully at his interrogation to protect a dead master's secret, and who has been quietly going through Halvard's papers at night ever since, and who hasn't sold anything to Ferenc despite being offered enough money to never work again. Yes. I think it's you. God knows it shouldn't be. You're a civilian, you're already suspected, you have a mother and a brother and a shop full of irreplaceable paper. Every sensible part of me says walk away, Ivo, lock the cabinet, let the wolves divide it."

"But?"

"But I watched you trace a coastline today from memory because the survey notes were missing." She said it simply, and it went through me like a splinter — how did she know that, what else did she know, how long had I been watched — "and I thought: there aren't five men in this country who'd bother. Everyone else would draw it wrong and bill for it. You'd sooner starve than publish a lie." A pause. "Which is ironic, considering what I'm about to ask you."

"Which is?"

"To find the route first. Before Brandt. Before Ferenc. Before spring, because everything moves in spring — the thaw opens the marshes, and whatever's been waiting four years will start moving with it, and after that it's knives all the way to the border." She leaned back. "Find it. Hold it. Don't give it to me, don't give it to anyone — not even me, do you hear? Especially not me; I have a colonel who reads my reports. Just find it and keep it alive until we know who deserves it. That's all. That's the whole request. Find the truth and sit on it, which should be the easiest thing in the world for a Yarrow."

I laughed. I couldn't help it — a short, cracked sound that surprised us both. "You have no idea," I said, "how untrue that is becoming."

"No," she said. "I have some idea. That's why I came in person instead of sending a note. Notes get found. People get searched. But a promise made across a table, with no witness but a lamp—" she touched the lamp's base, lightly, as if swearing on it, "—that leaves no evidence. That's the safest document in the empire."

* * *

She rose to go. At the door she stopped, hood up, hand on the latch, and stood a moment with her back to me, and I saw her shoulders rise and fall once, twice — a woman assembling herself back into a captain piece by piece, like a man repacking a kit.

"Ivo," she said, without turning. "If it comes to it — if it all comes apart, if the wolves win, if there's ever a night when I need what you promised at that fire —"

"There won't be," I said. "Because you're going to control this. That's the whole point of finding it first."

"Of course," she said. "That's the whole point."

But she said it the way people say things they want to be true, and I stood in my shop listening to the latch click and the bell speak its soft single note, and I understood that she had just asked me for half a promise — the easy half, the finding — and had deliberately, kindly, left the other half unspoken.

The other half being the drawing. The way out. The border, and the season, and the road.

I stood there a long time after the street had swallowed her. Then I went to the cabinet, took out Halvard's final plate, and laid it under the lamp — and there, in the margin, in a corner so small I'd missed it a dozen times, I found a mark I had no name for. A tiny symbol, inked and half-erased, like a house with a light in its window.

I knew the shape of that shoreline. I had swum in that water.

Halvard had marked the lake camp.
