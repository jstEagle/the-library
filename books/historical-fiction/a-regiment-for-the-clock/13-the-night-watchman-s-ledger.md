## 13. The Night Watchman's Ledger

There are two kinds of knowledge in a port city. The kind you buy with coin, and the kind you buy with silence.

Halvard taught me the second kind first, though I didn't know I was being taught. *Never ask a man what he saw,* he'd say, trimming his pen. *Ask him what nobody's asked him lately. The first question is an accusation. The second is a gift.* He'd learned it somewhere — he never said where — and for years I assumed it was just the peculiar wisdom of a man who spent his life making pictures of places he could not afford to visit twice.

Now I understood it differently. Now it sounded like tradecraft.

The night watchmen of Vellmark keep ledgers. It is one of those facts that everyone knows and no one thinks about, the way everyone knows the tide tables but no one consults them unless their livelihood depends on it. Every watchman on every beat records the hour of his rounds, the lamps lit, the doors checked, and — this was the part that mattered — anything unusual. A cart moving at odd hours. A stranger asking after a warehouse. A gentleman walking a route no gentleman walks at three in the morning.

The ledgers were technically the property of the harbor authority. Technically. In practice they belonged to whichever watchman had signed them, and watchmen were paid less than lamplighters and drank more than sailors, which made them the most purchasable men in the city if you approached them correctly.

*Correctly* meaning: never as a buyer. Always as a friend of a friend.

I spent two evenings establishing my credentials before I spent a single coin. Tuesday I bought a round for old Mikkelsen at the Anchor & Chain, who had once done survey work with Halvard and who owed me nothing but liked talking about the good years. Wednesday I let slip to Mikkelsen that I was trying to trace some of the master's last movements — for the atlas, I said, for the record of the survey seasons — and watched him nod with the particular solemnity of a man who approves of memorial work. Thursday, Mikkelsen walked me down to the harbor wall and introduced me to a watchman named Piet Aune, who kept the eastern beats: the salt road, the timber yards, the causeway out toward the marsh country.

Piet Aune was sixty if he was fifty-five, with hands like braided rope and eyes that had seen everything on his rounds and judged none of it. We sat on a bollard in the grey afternoon and talked about weather for twenty minutes, because that was the price of admission, and then about Halvard for another twenty, because Piet had liked him.

"Quiet man," Piet said. "Polite. Always asked how my feet were."

"How were your feet?"

"Terrible. Same as always." He almost smiled. "He'd bring me a poultice sometimes. Winter before last."

Winter before last. The year Halvard died.

"Piet," I said, and here I made the mistake of reaching for my purse, and watched something close behind his face like a shutter — so I stopped, and put the purse away, and said instead: "My master died with questions around him. Not debts. Questions. I'm trying to answer them so the family can sleep."

It was true, or true enough. He looked at me a long moment, reading me the way he read streets at night, and whatever he found there apparently cleared the ledger, because he said:

"I don't have them anymore. Last year's books went back to the office in January."

"But you remember what's in them."

Another long look. "I remember everything in them. That's the trouble with this work. Nothing to drink away, because the drinking comes free with the job."

We sat while a collier worked itself against the tide below us, ropes groaning, gulls conducting their endless audit.

"He walked your beat," I said. "In his last year."

"Eleven times." No hesitation at all. "I marked each one. Unusual hours, unusual direction — eastward, past the last lamp, toward the marshes. Nobody goes east past the last lamp. Even smugglers come back west to be paid."

Eleven times. My chest did something complicated, part lift, part drop. Eleven trips. In his final year. The quiet man who asked after Piet's feet, who brought poultices, who never in fifteen years of working for him had traveled further inland than the cathedral — walking alone into marsh country, again and again, in the cold months.

"Did he go alone?"

"Alone going out. Coming back—" Piet frowned, reconstructing. "Twice I think there was another set of prints beside his on the frost. Once, a cart. Timber cart, unmarked, heading back toward town very late. And the last time—" He paused, and I felt the pause like a change in pressure.

"The last time?"

"The last time he wasn't walking right. Came back along the salt road slow, holding his side, and stopped at my lamp and stood there a minute without saying anything. I asked if he wanted help getting home. He said—" Piet's voice changed slightly, taking on the careful cadence of quotation. "He said, 'Not home, Piet. Not anymore.' And then he thanked me for the poultices and walked on."

The wind came off the water and I was suddenly very cold, in the way that has nothing to do with weather.

"When was that?"

"Three weeks before he took ill." Piet looked out at the harbor. "I've thought about it since, with all the talk. About whether I should have said something to somebody."

"To whom? What would you have said?"

"That's the thing." He rubbed his rope-hands together. "I still don't know what I'd say. Man walked east eleven times. Men are allowed to walk east." He glanced at me sideways. "But you're not asking me what to say. You're asking me what happened."

"Yes."

"Then I'll tell you the rest, and then we'll talk about the weather again, loud enough for anyone listening, and afterward neither of us remembers this conversation. Agreed?"

"Agreed."

So he told me. The eleven dates, which he recited from memory like scripture, and I wrote them on the inside of my cuff in pencil small enough to be mistaken for lint. The first trip in late autumn, the intervals shortening through winter — a fortnight, ten days, six days, four — the way a man's visits to a sickbed shorten as the end approaches, or the way a courier's runs compress when the cargo grows urgent. The frost prints. The timber cart. And the last walk, the one where Halvard came back holding his side.

"Injured," I said. "Or hurt himself."

"Or was hurt." Piet stood, joints cracking like kindling. "There's a difference, and I've seen both, and his walk that night was the second kind." He straightened his coat. "Right. Weather."

"Rotten weather," I agreed.

"Coming up from the southeast. Bad for the fishing, worse for my feet." He stuck out his hand and shook mine warmly, a performance for any distant eyes, and murmured as we clasped: "The office keeps the old books. Warehouse cellar off Cordwainer Lane. If a man were foolish enough to go looking, the night clerk drinks at the Anchor & Chain Tuesdays and Saturdays, and leaves the cellar key hanging on its nail behind the door, because he trusts everybody, which is why he's a night clerk."

"And why would a man be foolish enough to go looking?"

"Because," Piet said, releasing my hand and turning back toward the water with terrible mildness, "a man who asks after feet is a man who understands that everything wears out eventually. Books included. Paper doesn't last forever, young Yarrow. Some things ought to be copied before they rot."

* * *

I did not go that night. Or the next.

This requires explanation, because you would think — I thought — that a man who has found half a cut map, a note reading *the route is not for sale*, and a hidden symbol marking a lakeside camp would run to the harbor office cellar with his hair on fire. But fear works strangely on the careful mind. For two days I did the ordinary work of the shop with exaggerated diligence — inking coastlines, answering correspondence, eating my mother's bread and listening to Tomas complain about dock wages — and all the while a voice in my head conducted a tribunal.

The prosecution argued: Halvard walked into the marshes eleven times and came back hurt the last time. Three weeks later he was dead of an illness that the doctor, when pressed, described with the word *consistent* — consistent with fever, consistent with exposure, consistent, if one insisted, with other things. If someone killed my master over that route, the same someone might take an interest in the apprentice who inherited his papers. Going to the cellar was not detection; it was volunteering.

The defense argued: they had already searched the shop. Ferenc's polite threats, Elira's official letter, the ransacked drawers I kept imagining — the attention had already fallen on me, and the only safety lay in knowing what Halvard knew. A man cannot hide from a story he hasn't read.

The defense also noted, in closing, that I had promised a woman — two women ago, in another life, in summer — that I would draw her the way out. And that promises do not keep well in ignorance.

Saturday night, the tribunal adjourned in favor of the defense.

Cordwainer Lane at midnight is a corridor of sleeping warehouses, smelling of tar and tallow, lit by lamps spaced so far apart they seemed apologetic. The night clerk's door had the key on its nail exactly as advertised, which offended something in me — a whole city's secrets hanging on nails, guarded by trust — though I suppose I was the beneficiary of that philosophy, so I swallowed my objection.

Inside, the cellar smelled of lamp oil, mouse, and the great patient boredom of municipal paper. Ledgers in rows by year, by beat. I found the eastern beats for Halvard's final year — Piet's own volumes, returned to the office as regulations required — and carried two of them to the single lamp, and began to read.

Reading a watchman's ledger is like listening to a man talk in his sleep. *Lamps all lit. Door of the cooperage secure. Dog loose again near the salt sheds, spoke to owner.* Pages and pages of the city breathing. And then, punctuating the monotony like stones dropped into still water:

*11th Oct. 2 a.m. Passed Mr. H—— on the causeway road, walking east. Wished him goodnight. He asked after my feet.*

And eleven times more, in Piet's cramped honest hand. I copied each entry verbatim into my pocketbook, dates, phrasing, even the little marginal marks Piet used — a small cross for unusual events, and beside the fourth trip, a cross and a sketch: a timber cart, drawn from memory with the loving precision of a bored man. Beside the ninth trip, another mark I stared at for a long time: not a cross but a small circle, and inside it, in tiny letters, *2nd man?*

The tenth entry recorded the return of the cart. The eleventh — the last — read simply:

*3 a.m. Mr. H—— returning, unwell. Walked him as far as the salt sheds. He would not say what ailed him. Asked me to forget the year.*

*Asked me to forget the year.*

I sat back on the crate and let the lamp hiss. Forget the year. As if Halvard had known, even then, that the record itself was dangerous — that a ledger full of his comings and goings was a map of his guilt or his martyrdom, depending on who read it. Had he gone back for these books? Was that among the reasons he visited Piet, the poultices, the questions after feet — keeping the one witness friendly, keeping the one record kind?

Or had he simply been a tired sick man asking a friend for mercy?

Both, probably. Both at once. That was the discovery that cost the most: not the eleven trips, not the cart, not even the second man. The cost was realizing that my master — steady, meticulous, incorruptible Halvard, who had taught me that a coastline drawn carelessly was a lie told to strangers — had lived his final year as a conspirator. Had walked east in the dark carrying something worth hurting him for. Had come back holding his side and said *not home, not anymore*, and then had gone home anyway, because there was nowhere else, and had died three weeks later among his plates and pens while I inked coastlines in the next room, believing history happened elsewhere.

History had happened forty feet from my desk. I had slept through it with the lamp lit.

I copied everything twice — once into the pocketbook, once onto a slip I folded small and sewed, with a competence that frightened me, into the lining of my coat. Then I put the ledgers back in their exact positions, angled to the finger-width, because I am my master's apprentice and misalignment is a confession. I hung the key on its nail. I let myself out into the lane.

The sky was going grey at the edges, that hour when Vellmark belongs to no one. And standing under the last lamp before the harbor square, stamping the cold out of his boots, was a man I knew by sight and reputation both: one of Colonel Brandt's intelligence clerks, the ones who haunted the survey office corridors with their inkstained cuffs and their too-casual questions.

He nodded to me. Civilian to civilian. Stranger to stranger.

I nodded back and walked west toward home at a pace I calibrated carefully between *innocent errand* and *flight*, feeling the sewn slip against my ribs like a second heartbeat, understanding two things with perfect clarity.

First: I had not been followed. I had simply been *positioned*. Someone had decided the shop, the street, the apprentice were worth a lamp and a pair of boots at three in the morning, and had been right.

Second: eleven trips east meant a destination, and a destination meant a place, and Halvard — my Halvard, who never drew a road without measuring it twice — would never have trusted his secret to memory or to a cut map alone.

Somewhere in his papers, in his habits, in the arithmetic of his final year, there was a third record. And now I knew exactly where to look for it: not where he hid things, but where he *went*.

The lake camp.

I turned the corner onto our street as the first bells rang five, and found the shop door standing open, and my mother in the doorway with her shawl clutched at her throat, saying my name in the voice she saved for fires and drownings.

Behind her, in the grey light of the workroom, someone was sitting at my master's desk.
