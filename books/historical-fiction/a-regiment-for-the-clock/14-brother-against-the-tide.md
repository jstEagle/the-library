## 14. Brother Against the Tide

My brother Tomas has always been a man who sweats before he's guilty. I noticed it first when we were boys, after he broke Mother's blue bowl — the one with the painted herons — and spent three days carrying it around inside his coat like a wounded bird rather than confess. He'd stand in doorways with that particular shine on his forehead, that look of a man rehearsing sentences he couldn't finish, and Mother would say nothing, and eventually the truth would fall out of him like a coin from a torn pocket.

He was doing the doorway thing now.

It was Thursday evening, and I was at the big table with Halvard's plates spread out in careful rows, working by lamplight on the corrections to the estuary soundings, when I heard him come in. I knew it was him because he didn't knock. Nobody else in Vellmark came into our shop without knocking except family and creditors, and creditors knock louder.

"You're dripping on the floor," I said, without looking up.

"It's raining."

"It's been raining since October. You've lived here your whole life. You'd think you'd have developed an arrangement with it."

He laughed — a short, wrong-sounding laugh, the kind you make when someone tells a joke at a funeral — and hung his coat on the peg, and then stood there not taking off his boots, which for Tomas was like standing in church with his hat on. He looked at the plates. He looked at me. He looked at the lamp as if it might offer him a way out of the room.

"Supper?" he said.

"There's bread. There's the fish stew from Tuesday."

"That's not supper, that's archaeology."

"Eat it anyway. It's had two days to think about what it did."

He got himself a bowl and sat across from me, and for a while the only sounds were the rain against the shutters and my pen scratching and Tomas performing the fiction of eating. He wasn't hungry. Tomas is never not hungry; the man eats like the sea eats a coastline, patiently and without remorse. A full bowl going cold in front of him was a message written in a language I could read.

I kept drawing. That's the trick of it, if you're ever interrogating anyone — keep your hands busy so they forget theirs are shaking. Halvard taught me that too, though he taught it about maps: *the surveyor who stops moving looks lost.*

"So," Tomas said finally.

"So," I said.

"I've been meaning to talk to you."

"You've been meaning to talk to me for four days. You've come round twice and stood in the yard smoking until Mother made you come in. Whatever it is, it's either terrible or expensive, and we can't afford either."

He set down his spoon. He looked at the stew as if it had betrayed him personally, and then he looked up, and what was in his face made me put my pen down.

"Ivo," he said. "There were men."

* * *

Here is what I learned over the next hour, and I will set it down plainly because setting it down plainly is all I have ever known how to do, and because if I dress it up I will lose my temper, and if I lose my temper I will say things to my brother that cannot be unsaid, and he is the only person alive whose opinion of me I actually fear.

Three weeks ago — before Ferenc ever darkened our door with his card and his courteous voice — two men came to the docks. Not gentlemen. Dockside men, the kind whose coats hang wrong and whose hands remember work they no longer do honestly. They found Tomas at the rope-walk where he takes piecework when the harbor wages run thin, and they bought him a drink at the Anchorline, and they talked to him the way you talk to a horse you're thinking of buying.

They knew his name. They knew our mother's name. They knew the shop, and they knew that Master Halvard had died — they used the word *died* in a tone that suggested they considered it a generous description — and they knew that Ivo Yarrow was finishing the old man's atlas.

"They asked me," Tomas said, staring into the middle distance, "what hours you kept. Who came to the shop. Whether you'd had letters. Whether anyone foreign had been round."

"And you told them?"

"No." He said it fast, and then slower. "No. I told them I was just a dockhand, that I barely saw you, that you kept scholar's hours and I kept sailor's ones and we passed each other like ships. And they smiled at that, the way men smile when they already know better, and then the tall one put money on the table. Real money, Ivo. More than I make in a month. He said it was a retainer. He said all I had to do was *keep my eyes open.*"

"Keep your eyes open," I repeated.

"That's all. That's what he said. Like it was nothing. Like it was weather."

"And you took it."

The silence went on long enough that I could hear the lamp burning. Tomas's jaw worked. When he finally spoke, his voice had dropped to almost nothing, which for my brother is the loudest he ever gets.

"I owed eleven crowns to the tally-man at the Anchorline. Eleven. And Krysta's got the baby coming in March, and the winter work's been half-days, and I thought — God help me, Ivo, I thought, it's only watching. Everybody watches everybody in this city. The customs men watch the smugglers and the smugglers watch the customs men and the landladies watch us all. What difference does one more pair of eyes make?"

"What difference," I said, "does one more pair of eyes make."

I hadn't meant to say it out loud. It came out flat and cold, and I watched it land on him like a slap, and part of me — the small, mean part that lives in everyone and admits it to no one — was glad it landed. But most of me felt something worse than anger. Anger would have been simple. What I felt was recognition. Because I had been sitting in this shop for weeks telling myself the same arithmetic: *it's only a letter, it's only a question, it's only keeping my head down until the tide goes out.* We were both Yarrows, doing sums with other people's lives, hoping the numbers came out kind.

"How much did you take?" I asked.

"Five crowns. First payment." He reached into his shirt and pulled out a little oilskin packet and put it on the table between us, next to the estuary plate, and I confess I looked at that money the way a starving man looks at bread, and I hated myself for it, and I hated Ferenc — for I was quite sure whose money it was by then — for knowing exactly how much five crowns weighs against a winter.

"Have they been back?"

"Twice. Last time was Sunday. They wanted to know if anyone had been asking about marsh roads." His eyes came up, sharp and frightened. "Marsh roads, Ivo. Why would anyone ask me about marsh roads? I'm a dockman. What do I know about marshes?"

"Nothing," I said, too quickly.

He heard it. Of course he heard it. We shared a bedroom for sixteen years; I know every register of that man's hearing, and he knows mine. He leaned forward across the table, and the lamplight caught the sweat on his forehead again, the boy with the broken bowl, twenty years older and no better at hiding it.

"You know something about the marshes."

"I know what's in the atlas."

"That's not what I asked."

"Tomas—"

"Don't." He held up a hand, and for a moment he wasn't the shamed younger brother at all; he was the man who'd hauled me out of the harbor current when I was nine and gone blue, the man who'd stood between me and the Kessler boys more times than either of us counts. "Don't do the mapmaker's voice. Don't tell me it's complicated and official and above my head. These men mentioned Mother. Did I tell you that? The short one, Sunday — he said, very friendly, very pleasant, he said, *your mother's getting on, isn't she, be a shame if the city office started asking questions about the property.* Property. We don't own property, Ivo. We rent everything including our luck. He meant her. He meant *her*, and he smiled while he said it, and I took his hand and shook it and said I'd keep my eyes open."

His voice cracked on the last word. He put his face in his hands, and my brother — who once carried a splintered oar six miles through a gale because a stranger's boat was adrift — sat at our mother's table and shook like a man in a fever.

"I'm not a traitor," he said into his palms. "I want you to know that. Whatever happens. I need you to know that I am not — I would never sell you. I took their money and I've told them nothing, nothing true, and I don't know why that makes it worse instead of better but it does, it makes it feel ten times filthier, like I'm a lock they've bought the key to and just haven't turned yet."

* * *

What do you say to that?

I'll tell you what I said, because I'm honest even when honesty humiliates me. I said nothing for a long time. I picked up my pen and cleaned the nib, which is what I do instead of praying. And then I asked him the only question that mattered.

"Do they know where you live? Where *we* live?"

"They found me at the rope-walk. They've never come here."

"They bought you drinks and paid you five crowns and mentioned Mother by implication, and they've never followed you home."

He went still. I watched him arrive where I'd already been: that men who spend money on watchers also spend money on finding watchers' doors. Somewhere in Vellmark there was probably a page in a ledger with our address on it, in a neat hand, underlined.

"So what do I do?" he whispered. "Do I go back and tell them I'm out? Do I give them back the money? Ivo, men like that don't take refunds."

"No," I agreed. "Men like that don't take refunds."

"Then what?"

And here is where I did something I am still not sure was wisdom or cowardice, and I expect I'll be deciding for the rest of my life. I looked at my brother — frightened, indebted, owned to the tune of five crowns and a threat delivered with a smile — and I did not tell him about the half-map in Halvard's desk. I did not tell him about Captain Voss, or the letter under seal, or the route through the marshes that everyone from a colonel to a courteous stranger seemed prepared to kill for. I did not tell him that the atlas on our table might be worth more than the building it sat in, or less than the lives of everyone in it.

Instead I said: "You go back to them. You smile. You keep your eyes open, exactly as promised. And you tell me everything they ask you, the day they ask it. Every question is worth more than any answer you could give them. Do you understand? Their questions are the map, Tomas. You're going to draw it for me."

"That's double-dealing."

"That's survival. There's a difference of about one degree, and the whole world lives in it."

He stared at me. "When did you learn to talk like that?"

"Recently," I said. "It's been an educational few months."

He almost laughed — a real one this time, cracked down the middle but real — and then his face folded again, and he said, quietly, "Mother can't know. She'd — she'd stop eating, Ivo. She'd carry it like she carries everything."

"Mother," I said, "knows considerably more than either of us has ever given her credit for. But no. She doesn't hear it from us. She's carried enough."

We shook on it, which felt absurd and ancient and necessary, my brother's dockworker's hand swallowing mine, calluses against ink stains. Then he ate the Tuesday stew, all of it, cold, because apparently absolution improves the flavor, and he took the oilskin packet of Ferenc's money off the table and weighed it in his palm and asked what he should do with it.

"Buy Krysta something for the baby," I said. "Consider it a loan from a man who'll want it back with interest someday, in a currency we haven't invented yet."

He left close to midnight, out into the rain, and I stood at the window and watched him go up the street with his collar up, and I felt the shape of the trap around us both — not closed, not yet, but drawn, its lines surveyed and fixed, waiting only for the ink.

Because here is what I did not say to him, what I could not say: those men had asked about marsh roads three days before I understood there was anything to ask about. Which meant somewhere out in the wet dark, people already knew what Halvard had hidden — knew it well enough to be hunting its edges, feeling along the seams of our family for the way in.

They had bought my brother for five crowns.

I wondered, cleaning my pen a final time, what they imagined the rest of us would cost — and whether they had already sent someone to find out.
