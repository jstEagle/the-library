## 20. Midwinter Truce

The coach from the Salt Gate went north along the frozen river road, and I spent four hours pretending to be a commercial traveler with nothing on his mind but felt boots and the price of tallow. It is remarkable how much of espionage consists simply of looking bored. I had been training for it my whole life; boredom was my native tongue.

I got off at the Kesselbrücke crossroads and walked the last three miles, because a coach leaves tracks in snow and a man does not. The lake lay white and featureless ahead of me, the far shore indistinguishable from the sky, and somewhere between them — if Halvard's plate could be believed, and I had spent two sleepless nights believing it — stood a survey hut that officially did not exist, drawn on no map but mine, marked with a symbol no cartographer would ever ink: a triangle with a dot, tucked into the margin like a printer's error.

Their summer place. *Our* summer place, once, in the grammar nobody used aloud.

The hut sat where memory said it would, half-swallowed by pines, its roof sagging under a foot of snow like an old man under a blanket. Smoke threaded from the chimney.

Someone was already there.

I stopped among the trees and considered, briefly, the wisdom of walking back to Vellmark and taking up felt boots after all. Then I thought about what waited in Vellmark: a letter under seal, a colonel with soft hands and hard arithmetic, a courteous stranger named Ferenc who collected maps the way other men collected debts. There was no version of my life anymore in which I got to be merely bored.

I knocked on the door of a building that didn't exist, in the middle of nowhere, and heard a bolt drawn back with the crisp efficiency of someone who had done it a hundred times.

Captain Elira Voss opened the door with a pistol held low against her thigh, saw it was me, and did not lower it.

"You're late," she said.

"The coach was slow."

"I said come at dusk."

"Dusk," I said, "is when everyone watches the roads. I came when honest men are asleep." I nodded at the pistol. "Are you going to invite me in, or shoot me on the threshold? Only I've walked three miles in snow and either way I'd prefer to do it sitting down."

* * *

*He came when honest men were asleep.* Of course he did. Of course Ivo Yarrow — who at nineteen couldn't lie about a miscounted survey without his ears going red — arrived at my safehouse by night, unannounced, having presumably evaded two garrisons' worth of patrols through sheer stubbornness and local knowledge.

I should have been angry. A handler is not supposed to be grateful to her asset for making her life difficult; it sets precedents.

Instead, watching him stamp snow off his boots inside her door, I felt something loosen in my chest that had been clenched since the day the letter went out under my name. He had come. Not summoned, not coerced, not marched in under escort — *come*. There is a difference, in this work, between a man you bring to a place and a man who finds his own way there. The first kind you can use. The second kind you have to trust, or kill, and I had known Ivo Yarrow long enough to know that killing him would be a waste of the only honest cartographer left in the province.

"Sit," I said, and put the pistol away, and put water on to boil, because in the field one learns that everything negotiable begins with tea.

The hut was as I remembered it and nothing like I remembered it. Smaller. Colder. The bunk frames still stood where the survey corps had left them fifteen years ago, and the stove still drew badly, and the window still framed the same slice of lake that had seemed, that summer, like the whole world. He looked at the window longer than he looked at me.

"You kept the notebook," I said. It was on the table between us, his doing — he'd set it down like a man surrendering evidence.

"You left it."

"I left a lot of things."

"Yes," he said. "You became one of them."

And there it was, laid on the table alongside the notebook, and I found I had no orders prepared for it. I am good at interrogation because I prepare. I had not prepared for Ivo Yarrow telling me, mildly, over a bad stove, that I had abandoned him, and being right.

* * *

She didn't deny it. That was the thing I hadn't expected. I had rehearsed the whole way north — the accusations I'd make, the ones she'd deflect, the careful fencing of two people pretending the past was a locked drawer. Instead she poured tea into tin cups, sat across from me with her hands wrapped around hers, and said:

"You're right. I let you become a thing I'd outgrown. Commissioned officers don't keep apprentices. And then I told myself you'd forgotten me anyway, which was cowardice dressed up as mercy." She drank. "So. Now we're both cowards in the same room. Shall we discuss the money?"

"Is that what we're calling it?"

"What would you call it?"

"A war chest belonging to a dead general who isn't dead, hidden along a route only one map in the empire shows, hunted by a colonel who wants to steal it and a merchant who wants to buy it." I turned the cup. "I'd call it the reason neither of us will see spring unless we're very careful or very lucky."

"Careful," Elira said. "Luck is for people without maps."

* * *

We talked until the stove burned low and the cold crept back in around us, and what we built in that hut had no name I knew. Not an alliance — alliances have signatures. Not a conspiracy — conspiracies have more than two people and less honesty. It was closer to a survey: two parties independently measuring the same ground and agreeing, reluctantly, that their instruments matched.

His account of Brandt's offer confirmed what I'd suspected from my side of the colonel's desk: the interception was designed to fail upward, the theft laundered into patriotism. My account of Ferenc made him go quiet in the particular way of a man re-pricing his own danger.

"So," he said finally. "Brandt wants the chest seized clean. Ferenc wants it bought cheap. Aldric — if he lives — wants passage. And we want—"

"To find it first," I said.

"And then?"

That was the question I'd been circling all evening, the one I'd come north to ask and had hoped he'd answer so I wouldn't have to. Because there were answers a loyal officer gives, and answers a traitor gives, and Ivo Yarrow was watching me across the stove light waiting to learn which woman he had walked three miles through snow for.

"If we find it first," I said slowly, "we hold the only currency in the empire worth more than money, which is leverage over men who have money. We can trade it for what we need. For my mother's safety," he said immediately, and I noted it — family first, always first with him. "For your disappearance," I continued, and watched his face do something complicated. "For ours."

"Ours."

"Don't make me say it twice, Yarrow, I'm a captain, repetition is beneath my rank."

But he wasn't laughing. He set down his cup with the care of a man handling something explosive and said, "You're asking me to help you defect. To take state secrets across the border. You understand what they'll call you."

"Treason has such a short word for such a long process," I said. "Yes. If it comes to that. If Brandt closes the trap the way I think he will, there will be no staying — not for me, not honestly. I've read his correspondence. I know how this ends for people standing where I stand." I looked at him. "Six years ago somebody promised to draw me the way out if I ever needed it. I'm telling you I need it. I'm telling you now."

* * *

There is a moment when a debt comes due and you discover whether you signed it in ink or in weather. I had thought, all these years, that the promise was weather — a summer thing, dissolved by autumn like the lake mist. She sat across from me in our summer place in midwinter, uniformed, armed, an enemy of everything I'd been raised to respect, and asked me to honor it, and I found the signature had never faded at all.

"Then here's my price," I said. "Not for drawing you out. That I'd have done for free, God help me, and we both know it. The price is this: nobody else drowns in it. Not Tomas, not my mother, not the general's officers if they're what you say they are. If we take this route out, we take people with us who deserve out. No bargains that spend other lives to save ours."

"That's not a price," she said. "That's a conscience. Do you know how rare those are in this business? Men offer me loyalty, information, their firstborn. Nobody's ever offered me terms before." Something moved behind her eyes — the old Lira surfacing through the captain like a fish through ice. "Accepted. On condition: you tell me everything Halvard left you, as you find it. No private hoards, no clever half-truths. I've been lied to by professionals all winter. I haven't the patience left for amateurs."

"Agreed," I said. "On one more condition of mine. When it ends — however it ends — you don't look back at me across a courtroom. Either we're both beyond the frontier stone, or we're both in the same cell. None of this business where the officer saves herself and testifies about the poor deceived mapmaker."

"That," she said, "is the most romantic thing anyone has ever proposed to me, and I want you to understand how damning that is about my life."

* * *

We shook on it, absurdly, formally, two hands meeting over a table in a condemned hut while the wind walked the lake ice outside with a sound like dragging furniture. His hand was cold and inkstained and I held it perhaps a half-second past the terms of the contract, and he noticed, and I let him notice.

Afterward we worked. That was the other thing I'd forgotten about him — or maybe never known, since the boy I'd known had talent but not yet method. I spread Halvard's recovered half-map on the table; he produced his reconstruction from inside his coat, rolled tight, guarded like scripture; and we fitted them together by candlelight, and the marsh corridor assembled itself between us stroke by stroke, cordage and causeway and the deep black thread of the drainable channel, and I watched a man's dead hand reach out of the paper and show us the border's naked spine.

"He died for this," Ivo said quietly. Not a question. He'd stopped asking questions about Halvard somewhere around chapter three of his grief.

"He refused to sell it twice," I said. "That I know. Once to us, once to them. Men who refuse twice usually get a third visit they don't refuse successfully."

"Then we refuse better," he said, "or we die slower," and I realized I was looking at a man who had decided, sometime on the coach north, exactly what he was willing to become. It steadied me more than the pistol had.

* * *

Dawn was graying the window when we finished — the route annotated, the timings sketched, the safehouse symbol circled twice. Too late to walk back unseen; he'd stay till dark. There was one bunk frame with straw, one without, and I took the one without because I outranked him and because chivalry in a freezing hut is just a slower way of sharing.

I lay listening to the wind and to his breathing even out toward sleep, and I did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding. Somewhere south of us a colonel was selling us both, a merchant was buying us both, and a general who ought to be bones was waiting in the wings of history for a mapmaker to draw him an exit. Every rational projection ended in a cell or a grave or a border crossing at night with dogs behind us.

And underneath the fear, warm as a coal under ash, sat this: *he came when honest men were asleep.* Six years, and one thumbprint in a notebook, and he walked three miles through snow because I asked.

Outside, far off across the ice, something cracked — the lake shifting its weight, the sound carrying for miles like a shot.

I did not sleep again.

Neither, I think, did he.

* * *

Near noon he shook me awake, already dressed, face gray in the thin light.

"Wagon tracks," he said. "On the north shore road. Fresh — since dawn. Two sleds, heavy loads, heading for the old logging spur."

Toward the marshes. Toward the corridor. In midwinter, weeks before any legitimate hauler moves freight over thawing ground.

Elira was at the window, and I watched six years fall away from her posture in a single breath — the girl gone entirely, the officer arriving whole.

"Well," she said, pulling on her gloves. "It seems the truce is over before the tea's cold. Someone's started moving the chest."
