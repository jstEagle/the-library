## 21. Tomas Makes His Choice

I have always been good at lifting things.

Crates, barrels, a man twice my size if he's drunk enough and I'm angry enough. What I have never been good at is putting things down — debts, mostly, and now this: the knowledge that my little brother draws maps for a living and I unload herring for one, and somewhere between those two facts our family name got written into a government letter with a seal on it like wax poured over a wound.

My name is Tomas Yarrow. I am twenty-six years old, I owe eleven crowns to a man named Pekk who collects on Fridays and smiles when he does it, which is worse than if he didn't, and until three weeks ago the most dangerous thing in my life was the ice on Pier Four.

Then the courteous one came to the yard.

* * *

He didn't look like trouble. That's the thing about trouble in Vellmark — it never does. It looks like a clerk, or a fish factor, or a gentleman slightly overdressed for the weather, standing at the edge of the loading yard with his hands folded and his hat held just so, waiting for you to notice him the way you'd notice a bill left on a table.

"You're the brother," he said. Not a question. "Tomas."

"And you're lost," I said. "The atlas shop's up the hill. Turn left at the church, follow your nose past the chandlery."

"I know where the shop is." He smiled. He had the kind of smile that had been practiced in front of a glass until it stopped being a smile and became an instrument. "Ferenc sends his regards."

I put down the crate I was holding. Slowly, because my hands wanted to shake and I've learned that if you move slow, nobody sees it. "Don't know him."

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "You owe money to people who know him, though. Pekk. The Vasa brothers. The widow Lindqvist, who has been remarkably patient." He recited it like a grocery list. Eleven crowns to Pekk. Six to the Vasas. Nine and some change to the widow, God rest her patience, because nine crowns from a dockworker takes years and she'd been waiting on me since before the ice formed last winter.

"Debts are private," I said.

"In Vellmark they are," he said. "Elsewhere they're leverage. It depends entirely on who's asking." He let that sit in the cold air between us, then nodded toward the harbor, toward the gray water and the gray sky and the gray city climbing its gray hill. "Your mother lives above the shop still. Fourth step creaks. She hangs her washing on Tuesdays whether it rains or not, because your father did it that way and she says changing would be disrespectful to a dead man."

The cold went through my coat then. Not the wind. Something else.

"You've been watching us," I said.

"We've been *aware* of you," he corrected, gently, as if I'd used the wrong word in church. "There's a difference. Awareness is free. It's the next part that costs." He took off one glove, finger by finger, unhurried, a man demonstrating that time belonged to him and not to me. "Mr. Ferenc would like to settle your accounts. All of them. Eleven, six, nine — call it twenty-seven crowns and we'll say the interest is a courtesy. Twenty-seven crowns, Tomas, and your Friday visitor stops smiling, and your mother's Tuesdays stay boring forever."

"What does he want?"

"To be kept informed. Your brother works late. Your brother receives visitors. Your brother has been summoned by military intelligence, which even a dockworker must admit is an interesting thing to happen to a mapmaker's apprentice." He smiled the instrument-smile again. "You wouldn't have to do anything. Just talk. A word here, a word there. Who came, what they asked, whether he seemed frightened. You can talk, can't you? Dockmen talk all day. It's practically the job."

"And if I don't?"

He put the glove back on, finger by finger, same unhurried ceremony. "Then Mr. Ferenc will conclude the family isn't reasonable, and he'll stop being aware of you, and start being something else. He's very fond of your mother's washing days, I'm told. So punctual. So predictable. It would be a shame if predictability ever became a problem for her."

I want to tell you I hit him. I want to tell you I picked up a crate and broke it over his elegant skull and dumped him in the harbor and went home and burned the whole business down. Here is what actually happened: I stood in the yard smelling of herring and fear, and I heard myself ask, "How much of the debt today?"

And he reached inside his coat and counted out eleven crowns into my palm, one at a time, letting each coin land so I'd feel the weight of every single surrender.

"Pekk first," he said. "Fridays matter."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about being bought: it doesn't happen all at once. It happens in installments. Same as debt.

That night I paid Pekk. He didn't smile. He looked almost disappointed, and I understood suddenly that my debt hadn't been money to him — it had been ownership, and I'd sold it cheap, and he knew it, and now someone else owned me instead. The new owner was worse, because Pekk only wanted crowns and Ferenc wanted my brother.

I told myself the story you tell yourself. *I'm protecting them. I'll give him nothing real. I'll watch Ivo's back from the inside. Better me than some stranger.* You can build a whole cathedral out of sentences like that if you work at it. I built one. I lived in it. It had very thin walls and I heard everything through them.

Three times I reported to the courteous one — whose name turned out to be Melle, which fits, a name like a wet rag — standing in doorways and alley mouths, saying: he worked late. A woman came once, civilian clothes, didn't leave a name, he walked her out and watched the street after she went. Soldiers called twice more. He seemed tired. No, not frightened. Tired, like a man carrying something heavy upstairs in the dark.

Each time, Melle paid me. Two crowns, sometimes three. Small payments. Installments. And each payment bought less of my soul than the last one cost, because that's how souls depreciate — fast at first, then you stop noticing the exchange rate at all.

The eleventh crown of my old life was long gone. But there was one debt left. One coin in my pocket that wasn't Ferenc's yet.

* * *

The date.

That's what Melle wanted by midwinter. Not gossip, not descriptions. A date. When does the atlas finish? When does whatever Halvard started finally *move*? Because everyone in this business — the colonel in his uniform, the gentleman buyer in his gloves, whoever else was circling our shop like gulls over a gutting table — everyone believed there was a moment coming. A shipment, a route, a chest. Everyone wanted the hour of it.

And I didn't know. That was the one clean thing left in my hands: I genuinely didn't know. Ivo told me nothing. My little brother, who used to tell me everything — who at nine years old explained tides to me for two hours on Pier Four until I understood them better than the harbormaster — had shut the map-room door on me without ever saying he was doing it. Maybe he knew. Brothers know things they don't know they know. Maybe he'd smelled Melle on me, or maybe he was just carrying his heavy thing upstairs in the dark and couldn't put it down for anyone.

So I did what a drowning man does. I grabbed the nearest floating thing and called it a plan.

The thaw. Everyone knows the thaw. The marsh roads flood in autumn and freeze solid in winter and drown again in spring — Ivo told me that once too, back when he still talked to me, back when maps were just pictures of places and not weapons. Nothing heavy moves across that country except in the narrow window when the ground firms up and the ice goes out of the culverts. If something was going to move — a convoy, a chest, a general's fortune, whatever ghost it was everyone kept whispering about — it would move at the thaw. Every sensible smuggler in the Baltic works the thaw. Even I knew that, and I know cargo weights and knot names and which pier inspectors take bribes, and nothing else.

So I gave Melle the thaw.

I stood in the doorway of the Sailors' Rest with beer I didn't drink, and I said, "Spring. When the ice goes. Whatever it is, it goes then — that's when the marsh road carries weight. Ask anyone."

Melle wrote it down. He wrote it down slowly, enjoying it, a man entering a figure in a ledger that would earn him a promotion off my brother's spine. "The thaw," he repeated. "And when exactly?"

"When it thaws," I said. "I'm a dockworker, not a prophet."

But here is the sentence I built the cathedral around, the keystone, the beam that held up the whole lying roof:

*I'll warn him first.*

That was the plan. That was the whole beautiful plan, and God forgive me, I loved that plan like a child. I would give Ferenc a date — a vague, mushy, thaw-shaped date worth nothing precise — and then I would go home, climb the hill, knock on my brother's door, and say: *Ivo. They know. They're coming at the thaw. Get Mother out tonight, burn whatever needs burning, take the atlas apart and scatter it like ashes.* I would pay Ferenc with fog and pay Ivo with fire, and everyone would live, and someday when we were old I'd confess the whole thing over aquavit and we'd laugh until we cried.

That was the plan at eleven o'clock that night.

By midnight I'd refined it. By two in the morning I had it perfect. I lay in my room above the cooperage listening to the harbor ice groan against the pilings — that long low sound like the sea clearing its throat — and I rehearsed what I'd say to Ivo. I rehearsed it four ways. Calm. Urgent. Written down, in case speaking was too dangerous. Drawn, even — God help me, I considered drawing it, because my brother reads drawings better than words and always has.

One day. I'd wait one day, no more. Let Melle carry his fog back to Ferenc, let the machinery start turning on a worthless answer, and then warn Ivo with a full day's lead. A day is a lot, in a city like Vellmark. In a day you can cross a border. In a day you can disappear.

I fell asleep believing it. That's the part I can't get out of my head, even now. I fell asleep *believing it*, warm under the blanket, having done the worst thing of my life and filed it neatly under protection.

* * *

The morning I meant to warn him, it snowed.

Not much. A dusting, the kind that makes the hill streets slick and makes the harbor look briefly innocent. I climbed up from the cooperage early, before the shift, taking the steps two at a time with the whole speech hot in my mouth. *Ivo, listen. There's a man named Melle. He works for a buyer called Ferenc. They've been paying me and I took it and I'm sorry, I'm sorry, I did it for Mother, and you have one day—*

I got as far as the lane behind the church.

There was a carriage there. Black, plain, government-plain, the kind of carriage that doesn't advertise anything because it doesn't have to. A soldier stood at the corner in a greatcoat, stamping his feet, bored the way soldiers are bored when the interesting part already happened or hasn't happened yet. And up at the shop, in the blue-gray light, men were moving in and out of the door with the unhurried purpose of ants at a killed thing.

I stood in the falling snow with my speech in my mouth like a stone, and I did the arithmetic every coward does, fast and filthy: *if I walk up there, I'm in it. If I'm seen walking away from it, I'm in it differently.*

A neighbor — Mrs. Kask, who feeds the church cats and knows everything — passed me and murmured, without stopping, the way people deliver news they're afraid to hold: "They've taken the mother. Some question about papers. The boy's released, they say. Watched."

Taken the mother.

My mother, who hangs washing on Tuesdays for a dead man. In a black carriage, or a cell, or wherever the state keeps its leverage while it waits for a mapmaker to bend.

And I understood — standing there, snow gathering on my shoulders like the world quietly covering something — that my one-day lead was already spent. That while I'd been asleep in my warm cathedral, Melle's fog had traveled faster than my warning ever would. That the date I'd given away, soft and vague as it was, had gone into a machine with gears I'd never seen, and the machine had moved overnight, and my brother was being watched, and my mother was gone, and none of it — *none of it* — had waited for the thaw.

I turned around. I walked back down the hill. I won't dress it up: I walked back down the hill like a thief leaving a house where something had died.

But halfway down, at the turning by the chandlery, I stopped. Because there was one thing left. One coin Ferenc hadn't touched, one fact in my possession that no ledger recorded: that I had lied about the date. That whatever the machine expected at the thaw was built on a dockworker's guess dressed up as intelligence. That somewhere in the gears there was a flaw shaped exactly like me.

They had taken the wrong Yarrow first. Which meant they were afraid of the right one.

And I made myself a promise, standing in the snow with my hands shaking at last because there was no one left to hide them from: whatever it cost, however deep the hole went, I was going to stand in front of my brother and tell him all of it — the coins, the lies, the cathedral, the thaw — and then I was going to help him burn Ferenc's machine down from the inside, even if I had to crawl into the furnace myself to do it.

I just needed to find him first.

Because Ivo had slipped his watchers — Mrs. Kask said so, with something like admiration — and a Yarrow who slips watchers is a Yarrow heading for the marshes, and the marshes in March are the closest thing this country has to a mouth that swallows.
