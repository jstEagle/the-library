## 22. The Colonel's Ledger

There are three reliable ways to learn what a man truly wants. The first is to ask him, which is useless. The second is to watch him, which is slow and produces a great deal of information about his appetite for pickled herring and very little else. The third is to read his correspondence when he isn't home.

I had been a faithful practitioner of the second method for eleven years, and I want it noted that I had never once needed the third until Colonel Brandt made it necessary. That is the sort of thing a person tells herself at one in the morning while picking the lock of her commanding officer's desk drawer: *this is an aberration, this is not who I am.* The lock disagreed. It opened on the second try, smooth as a well-fed animal, as though the desk had been waiting for someone with my particular talents and had simply grown tired of waiting.

The office smelled of brandy and lamp oil and the particular staleness of a room where a man makes decisions he doesn't intend to explain. Brandt kept his quarters in the eastern wing of the garrison, above the records annex — a detail that had always struck me as either devotion or vanity, since no colonel genuinely needs to sleep forty paces from his filing cabinets unless he believes the files might conspire against him. Perhaps they had. Perhaps that was why I was there.

I let myself in through the window because doors have witnesses and windows, at half past one in February, do not. The frost on the sill cracked under my glove like thin ice on a puddle, and I stood very still for a full minute afterward, listening to the building breathe. Garrison buildings breathe. They settle and tick and groan the way old men do in their sleep, and if you know the rhythm you can tell when something has interrupted it. Nothing had. Somewhere below, a sentry coughed and resumed his patrol, boots crunching the frozen gravel in the unhurried way of a man counting the hours until relief.

Brandt's desk was oak, heavy, and organized with the false tidiness of a man who wants visitors to see order without ever looking too closely. Papers squared to the edge. Inkwell centered. A photograph of his late wife in a silver frame, angled so that anyone seated across from him would find her gaze resting on them like a benediction. I had sat across from that desk four times in six months and had been watched over by that dead woman every time. She had kind eyes. I had begun to feel she disapproved of me, which was unreasonable, since I was fairly certain she'd have disapproved of her husband more.

The top drawer held nothing but stationery and a pistol cleaned so recently the oil still shone. The second held requisition forms, all correct, all dull. It was the third drawer — the shallow one, the one a man keeps for things he means to burn eventually but hasn't yet, because burning requires deciding, and deciding is the one activity Brandt performed only under compulsion — where I found the ledger.

Not a ledger, precisely. A notebook, quarter-bound, unmarked, the kind sold in any stationer's in Vellmark for a few coppers. Which was itself the first wrong note. A colonel's private papers should live in a locked box with a wax seal and a sense of occasion. This was disguised as nothing. Men hide important things in important containers; clever men hide them in boring ones.

I knelt on the cold floorboards with the notebook open across my knee and began to read, and I will be honest about what I felt, because honesty is the one luxury left to a woman doing burglary in her own garrison: I felt vindicated first, and sick second, and the sickness lasted considerably longer.

The entries began in September. Dates, sums, initials. Not code, exactly — Brandt was not a subtle man, and subtlety would have required imagination he reserved entirely for self-preservation — but discretion. *F. — received. 400.* *F. — balance outstanding, per discussion.* *F. — confirms spring. Route per map. Payment on delivery.*

*F.*

Ferenc.

I turned pages with a care that surprised me. My hands were steady — they always are; it's a curse of the profession that your hands behave better than your stomach — but I handled the notebook as though it were something living, something that might startle and cry out. Eleven months. He had been selling the interception since before I'd been assigned to the Vellmark matter. Before Halvard Yarrow died. Before the letter went out baiting a dead man's apprentice. Possibly — I did the arithmetic twice, kneeling there while the stove ticked down to embers — before Aldric's death had even been announced.

Which meant the operation I had been running, faithfully, competently, at considerable cost to my sleep and my conscience, was never an intelligence operation at all. It was a sales catalogue. Brandt had identified the merchandise — a deposed general's war chest moving toward the frontier — and had spent the autumn arranging buyers. Ferenc was one. There were others. Initials I didn't recognize: *M.*, who paid in advance and received "the northern option." *K.B.*, whose entries were marked with a small cross that might have meant anything or everything. A colonel does not run two buyers for the same goods unless he intends to deliver to neither, or unless he enjoys the sensation of standing between loaded guns, which some men do, God help them, the way other men enjoy brandy.

And threaded through it all, in entries growing more frequent since midwinter, the phrase that stopped my breath: *per V.'s report.*

*Per Voss's report.*

My own surveys. My reconnaissance of the marsh corridor — the corduroy logging, the staging points, the crossing at the old ford — written up in good faith, filed through channels, and passed along to Ferenc and whoever *M.* and *K.B.* were, priced and parcelled like cuts from a butchered animal. I had walked that frozen marsh in January with ice in my boot seams and frost nipping the skin off my cheeks, believing I was serving the Crown, and every step I measured had been entered into a book and sold.

I sat back on my heels. The dead woman in the photograph looked at me with her kind eyes, and I looked back at her, and for a moment we shared what I can only describe as marital sympathy.

Here is the thing they don't teach you at the academy, or rather here is the thing they teach you and you refuse to believe until it happens: treason is almost never dramatic. It doesn't arrive with masked men and midnight oaths. It arrives in a tidy hand on good paper, in a quarterly sum, in the word *outstanding* applied to a payment rather than a debt of honor. Brandt hadn't betrayed the service in a fit of passion or ideology. He had done it the way a man lets a house rot — one deferred repair at a time, each one reasonable, none of them feeling like ruin until the roof came down. The first entry was small. Two hundred crowns, October, for "preliminary assurance." He had probably told himself it was an informant's fee. Then four hundred had seemed like the natural next figure, and then a percentage, and then the whole architecture of the thing stood finished around him and he could no longer remember which lie he had told himself first, or whether it mattered.

I copied the notebook. All of it, by pencil, into a memorandum pad I'd brought for the purpose, my shorthand tight enough to fit three pages of his into one of mine. It took the better part of an hour. Twice the sentry passed beneath the window and I closed the notebook and became, for ninety seconds at a stretch, a woman admiring a photograph of a stranger's wife. The rest of the time I worked, and the work had a strange cleanliness to it. Betrayal, I discovered, is contagious in both directions. Every stroke of the pencil was a small act of war against my own uniform, and I found I did not mind. That frightened me more than the notebook did. A woman who discovers she can steal from her commanding officer without her hands shaking has learned something about herself that cannot be unlearned.

Near the back of the book, wedged between the last entries and a blank page, I found the item that turned sickness into something colder and more useful. A letter, unsent, folded once. Brandt's hand, but composed — the phrasing was too careful, the courtesies too elaborate. Drafted for him, or dictated.

*Colonel — Your proposal regarding the final disposition of the cargo is noted. You will understand our position: we have committed substantial resources on the strength of your assurances, and assurances are not collateral. If the goods move on the date indicated, and if interference is limited to what we have discussed, the arrangement concludes to mutual satisfaction. Should the date shift, or should your own government prove inconveniently diligent, our relationship must necessarily become public. We would regret this. Regret, however, is inexpensive.*

No signature. None needed. Ferenc's voice lived in every clause — that courteous menace, that grammar of velvet over wire. I had met the man once, in a café, for eleven minutes, and I had disliked him with unusual efficiency. Now I understood the dislike as instinct doing its proper work.

*Should your own government prove inconveniently diligent.*

He meant me. Or he meant whatever remained of diligence in the Vellmark garrison once Brandt had finished hollowing it out. Either way, the letter said plainly what the ledger implied: Ferenc owned Brandt now, not the reverse. The colonel who imagined himself a merchant brokering a windfall was in fact collateral with legs. And when the transaction concluded — when the chest moved in the spring thaw and the buyers collected — the man holding the notebook would be the man with everything to lose. Evidence, motive, and a signature on every page.

I put the letter back exactly as I'd found it, fold aligned to the crease. I returned the notebook to the third drawer, centered it by fingertip measurement, and closed the drawer with the same soft click it had made opening. The pistol stayed oiled. The wife kept her vigil. On my way past the desk I paused, absurdly, and straightened the photograph by a degree it hadn't needed straightening, and I have decided never to examine why.

The window took me back out into the cold, and the cold was a mercy. My face had gone hot inside the office, fever-hot, the heat of a body fighting something. Outside, Vellmark lay blue and silent under the last of the moon, rooftops furred with frost, the harbor a sheet of black iron beyond the mole. Somewhere across that iron, beyond the frontier, a general who was supposed to be dead was presumably alive, and a chest of gold was presumably waiting, and every party to the transaction — the colonel, the broker, the buyers, the smugglers, the wolves of three nations — had now seen my handwriting in one form or another.

I walked back to my quarters the long way, along the seawall, because I wanted the wind. It came off the harbor hard enough to make argument pointless, and I stood at the rail and let it strip me down to essentials.

Essentials, as best I could inventory them:

One. Brandt was selling the operation, and had been since autumn, and I was an ingredient in the merchandise.

Two. Ferenc held paper on Brandt — the unsent letter proved the threat existed in draft, which meant it existed in fact somewhere, in duplicate, in a strongbox I now needed to find. A blackmailer always keeps duplicates. It is the one sentimentality of the profession.

Three. The date. *If the goods move on the date indicated.* The thaw. Everyone had converged on the thaw — the smugglers, the buyers, the colonel. Which meant the entire conspiracy, rotten at every level, would compress itself into a single week of breaking ice, and whatever happened in that week would happen fast, and badly, and to people who hadn't agreed to any of it. People like a mapmaker's apprentice who had answered a summons out of fear for his mother, and who had lied to me across a table with eyes I'd known since we were nobody in particular.

Four — and this was the one I saved for last, walking home, because I already knew it and saying it early would only spoil the walk — four: I could not report any of this. Upward was impossible; the corruption ran to the top of my channel. Sideways was worse; I couldn't name a colleague in Vellmark I'd bet a dog on. And downward — to the boy with the ink-stained fingers and the dead master and the half-map hidden in a desk — downward meant making a conspirator of the one honest man in the affair, which was its own species of theft.

Or perhaps not the one honest man. He had lied to me about the desk. I had known it while he did it — had watched him choose the lie with the same care a draftsman chooses a line — and I had let him keep it, because the lie told me something truer than the truth would have: that he had found something, and that he understood, without being told, that finding it was dangerous. Halvard Yarrow had taught him well. Whoever killed the old man had taught him better.

So. No report. No channel. Just me, a stolen transcription drying in my coat lining, and a decision that had apparently made itself somewhere between the third drawer and the seawall: if the Crown would not protect the route from the wolves wearing its own colors, then I would have to do it privately, illegally, and with the assistance of a mapmaker who owed me a promise he'd made laughing, years ago, when neither of us knew what promises cost.

I reached my quarters as the sky went the color of dishwater. Inside, I fed the stove, sat at my desk, and unlocked the drawer where I kept the things that defined me: my commission, my mother's ring, and a surveyor's pencil worn to half its length. I laid the pencil beside the commission and looked at the two of them together for a long moment, the oath and the instrument, the life I'd built and the tool I'd use to take it apart.

Then I pulled a fresh sheet of paper toward me and wrote, in my neatest official hand, a request for leave — family matters, five days, beginning within the fortnight. If I was going to commit treason properly, I intended to do it with paperwork in order. Brandt would sign it without reading it. He signed everything without reading it now; his attention had moved elsewhere, into a notebook, into a future that no longer included the service that made him.

Let him sign. Let him initial the warrant of his own destruction and square it neatly to the edge of his desk.

Because tomorrow — or the day after, once leave was granted and my absence had a legitimate shape — I was going to walk into a map shop on Candlemakers' Row and say the words I'd sworn I'd never say: *I need the route, Ivo. All of it. And I need to know I'm not handing it to another wolf.*

And God help me, watching him realize what I was asking — watching the promise wake up in his face — I was going to have to decide, right there among the ink and the plates, whether I was recruiting him or ruining him.

The kettle began to whistle. I made tea I didn't taste, and outside the window, the ice on the harbor groaned once, long and low, like something enormous turning over in its sleep.
