## 26. Thaw

The river broke on a Tuesday.

I know this because the watchman assigned to me — a broad, incurious man named Dettlof who had been assigned to follow me since my release from Brandt's custody three weeks earlier — kept a habit of remarking on the weather as though it were intelligence worth reporting. *Cold today.* *Rain coming.* *River'll go soon.*

He said it that Tuesday morning outside the bakery where I bought bread we did not need, and by Thursday afternoon the Vellmark was a brown animal thrashing in its cage, hauling plates of ice downriver like dishes it meant to break.

I stood on the warehouse quay with Dettlof two steps behind me and watched it, and I confess I felt something close to joy, which felt like a betrayal of everyone who had died for what came next.

You have to understand what thaw means to a man who makes maps. Winter is honest. A frozen river is a road you can draw with confidence, a white line, dependable. But spring is when the country lies. Marshes that will hold a cart in February will hold nothing but a drowning horse in April. The ice road out of Vellmark that forty smugglers have used all season becomes, in six weeks, a corridor of black water and rotten reed-beds. Every route I drew in Halvard's atlas carried an asterisk in my mind that no reader would ever see: *true only until the melt.*

The whole conspiracy — Aldric's chest, Ferenc's buyers, Brandt's greed, whatever Tomas's confession had set loose — now moved or died in the narrow window between ice-out and mud-in. The country itself had become the clock everyone raced.

"They say it went early this year," Dettlof offered behind me. He had a voice like gravel poured slowly.

"Early," I agreed. "Everything does."

* * *

My release had come with conditions, because Brandt's releases always do. I was to remain in Vellmark. I was to present myself at the survey office twice weekly. I was to make no journey beyond the city walls without written permission. And I was, in the colonel's phrasing delivered through a lieutenant who clearly found the phrasing absurd, "to continue my late master's work undisturbed."

Undisturbed. With Dettlof trailing me at eleven paces, with his relief taking over at dusk, with a second man — thinner, quieter, better at his trade — watching the shop's back door from the chimney-sweep's attic across the alley. They thought they were subtle. They were not subtle. But then, subtlety is a luxury; what they needed was coverage, and coverage they had, and for three weeks I had performed the role of the grieving apprentice so faithfully that I half-believed it myself. I worked on the atlas. I ate supper with Mother, who had come home from her week in custody thin-lipped and refusing to discuss it, which is her way of discussing everything. I let them see me wind the shop clock, trim the lamp, lock up at nine.

What I did not let them see was the ledger page I had copied before my arrest, folded into the binding of a hymnal Mother sang from without ever singing. Eleven trips toward the marshes in Halvard's final year. Eleven dates. And beside each date, in the master's cramped hand, a single letter of the alphabet — not initials, I'd realized finally, but tide-marks. Stages of water. Halvard had recorded not where he went but how wet the ground was when he got there, because he knew, even dying, that the route's only true secret was its calendar.

The route through the marshes was not a line. It was a line plus a date. Drawn wrong, it killed. Drawn right, in the wrong month, it killed anyway.

Which is why the thaw terrified me more than any soldier.

* * *

On Friday I presented myself at the survey office, as required, and was received — "received" being generous — by a clerk who stamped my card without looking up. Captain Voss was not there. I had known she would not be there; her absence had been arranged between us in the language of absence, which after twenty years of knowing someone is a language you speak fluently. She had been ordered south to survey the military road, officially. Unofficially she was doing what she could to slow the machinery she served, and every day she managed it was a day the convoy waited somewhere north of the marshes, engines of men and mules idling against the calendar.

I missed her the way you miss a wall you've been leaning against when someone finally moves it.

"Captain's compliments," the clerk said, bored, sliding a slip of paper across with my stamped card. "She asks whether your work progresses."

It was a scrap of survey paper, the kind used for field notes, ruled faintly in blue. On it, in a hand that pretended to be administrative: *Progress noted. Recommend continued attention to southern approaches. Weather deteriorating.*

Southern approaches. The marshes lay south. Weather deteriorating — the window closing faster than anyone had calculated, because this winter had been long and the melt, once started, was running hot. Somewhere out there, men who believed they had until May were about to discover they had days.

And beneath that, almost invisible, a small drawn symbol: two short strokes over a dot.

The safehouse mark. Their summer place. *We are still where you think we are. Come when you can.*

I put the slip in my pocket and thanked the clerk, who grunted, and walked home past Dettlof, who had taken up his station by the chandler's and was eating a sausage with the concentration of a man performing his duty.

* * *

That evening I told Mother I might be late at the shop.

She looked at me across the supper table with the particular expression she had perfected during Father's lifetime and my entire childhood since — the look of a woman who knows exactly what is happening and has decided that knowing aloud would be worse than ignorance. Since her arrest, something in her had shifted. She had gone into Brandt's custody a frightened woman and come out a flint one. Whatever they had done to frighten her had, perversely, finished the job of making her unafraid.

"Tomas will walk with you partway," she said.

"He doesn't need to."

"No," she agreed. "He needs to." And that was the end of the negotiation, because Mother's sentences are load-bearing structures and cannot be argued with, only lived inside.

Tomas caught up with me at the corner of the ropewalk. His arm had healed crooked from the beating Ferenc's men had given him — or rather, from the beating he had accepted in place of worse done to us — and he swung it a little as he walked, the way a man does when he's testing whether pain is still news.

"Dettlof's behind us," he said.

"I know."

"And the quiet one?"

"Took over at the back door at six. He'll be there till midnight."

"You're going tonight, then." Not a question. My brother had learned, these months, to read me the way I read maps — badly at first, then all at once, then forever.

"I'm going tonight."

He was silent for half a street. Then: "The thing I did. Telling them the thaw date. I've been turning it over." His voice was low and rough. "I keep thinking if I'd held out a week longer—"

"If you'd held out a week longer they'd have broken Mother instead. You chose. It was a bad menu and you chose the least poison on it. I've stopped keeping accounts, Tomas. There isn't time."

"There's always time for accounts. That's what Da used to say."

"Da also said never lend money to a man with a boat, and you lent money to a man with a boat."

That startled a laugh out of him, brief and cracked, and I was glad of it, because I didn't know when either of us would laugh again. At the mouth of Tanner's Lane he stopped.

"Mother says to tell you—" He stopped again. Started differently. "She says come home. After. Whenever 'after' is. She says a family is a route too, and routes are meant to be returned to."

"That doesn't sound like Mother."

"She cried it, mostly," Tomas said. "I translated."

* * *

Now. The craft of it, because the craft is the only part I can tell cleanly.

Losing a tail in Vellmark is trivial; every city offers alleys and crowds. Losing a tail in a city where your tail expects you to try is harder, and losing one when your tail reports to Colonel Brandt, who owns half the watchmen and all the patience, is a professional problem. So I had spent three weeks solving it the way Halvard would have: not with speed, but with geography.

A city is a map that draws itself daily, and most people navigate it by habit — same streets, same crossings, same sightlines. Habits can be mapped, and once mapped, they can be lied to. For three weeks I had been teaching Dettlof my habits. Bakery at eight, office at ten, chandler's corner at noon, shop by four, church on Sunday. He had a notebook; I had watched him write in it. He thought he was learning my pattern.

He was learning the pattern I intended him to report.

The flaw in surveillance is that the watcher must stand somewhere, and every somewhere in Vellmark has a blind side, because I have personally surveyed every somewhere in Vellmark. Behind the chandler's runs a drainage cut, arched over, dry except in flood, wide enough for a thin man carrying nothing. From the drainage cut you pass under the tannery wall into the salt-yard, from the salt-yard through the cooperage — whose owner owed Halvard for thirty years of favorable boundary lines and paid his debt without ever once asking why — and out through the timber gate onto the river road below the quay, where the noise of the breaking ice covers everything and every face is turned toward the spectacle.

Dettlof, at his post by the chandler's, saw me go into the shop at four o'clock. The lamplight in the front room stayed lit — I had rigged it to a string and a chair-back, a trick Halvard invented for nights he slipped out to meet whoever it was he met. The chimney-sweep's attic watcher saw the lamp too, through the gap in the shutters I had measured to be visible from exactly his position. Two watchers, two confirmations, one apprentice quietly drawing maps by lamplight until morning.

By the time anyone checked, I would be miles south, and the checking would take hours, because Dettlof would first assume I was working late, then assume I had stepped out briefly, then begin his search at the places a man goes — taverns, the office, the girl he fancies on Fish Street, none of which existed — and only last arrive at the truth, that his subject had walked out through a drain.

I want it noted that I felt no triumph crawling through that culvert. I felt the cold of it in my knees and the smell of it in my collar, and I felt chiefly an enormous tenderness toward Halvard, dead three years, who had crawled this same pipe ahead of me for reasons I was only now beginning to count. Every trick I owned had been inherited. I was, in the end, simply his route walking itself.

* * *

The river road south was loud with thaw. Chunks of ice the size of coffins ground past in the current, and the water had climbed its banks and drowned the low pasture, so that the road became a causeway with brown sea on both sides. I walked it in the blue hour with my satchel — the true plate wrapped in oilcloth against my ribs, bread and a knife, and the reconstructed route memorized so thoroughly that even if the satchel were lost I could redraw it from sleep.

Twice I passed soldiers. Once a patrol, moving fast toward the bridge, faces set — Brandt's men were concentrating somewhere, though I couldn't guess where. Once a lone corporal leading a limping mule, who nodded at me as a fellow traveler and asked nothing, because a man walking away from Vellmark in thaw season looks like a fool, and fools are beneath suspicion. That is another lesson of my trade: disguise yourself as the least interesting thing on the map.

At the toll oak — a tree older than the city's charter, where the road forked — I left the signal we had agreed on: a strip of white rag tied at shoulder height, visible from the track that came up from the east. If Elira made it out of the southern survey ahead of schedule, she would come by that track. If the strip was gone in the morning, I would wait. If it still hung, I would assume she was delayed or discovered, and proceed alone.

Proceed alone to where? To the corduroy crossing, where Halvard's eleven trips ended. Where, if my arithmetic and the weather both held, the convoy would have to move within days — because the chest could not wait for full summer and could not move before the marsh firmed, and the only firm stretch left in the calendar was now. Everyone's plans had narrowed to a single thread of days, and I was walking along it toward the knot.

Somewhere behind me, Vellmark's lamps were burning in a dark shop, and a watchman was deciding whether to knock. Somewhere ahead, men with Brandt's insignia or Ferenc's gold or Aldric's desperation were loading something heavy onto something wooden. And somewhere east, a woman in an officer's coat was reading roads the way I drew them, looking for a strip of white rag.

I tied the rag, tested the knot twice — Halvard's rule: a signal that fails is worse than no signal, because it teaches your allies not to trust signals — and took the south fork as the last light went off the water.

The marsh began around midnight, and it sounded like the river had followed me. Under the stars the whole flat plain was making noise — settling ice, releasing water, the groan of peat remembering it was soft. In daylight it would be a green-gray nothing, featureless to any eye. To mine, in the dark, it was legible as print: the alder line marking the old channel, the hummocks where the ground ran firm in strings, the smell change where the peat went hollow. I moved from landmark to landmark like a man reading aloud, slowly, careful of every word.

Once, crossing a string of firm ground between two black pools, I heard horses. Many, far off to the west, moving on some track I couldn't see, muffled by fog coming off the meltwater. I stood still among the alders until the sound passed, heart going like a bird's. Convoy, patrol, or Ferenc's hired wolves — impossible to say. Impossible, and irrelevant: whatever they were, they were moving west of my line, and my line was the only one that mattered, because it was the only one that would still exist tomorrow.

Near dawn I reached the ridge above the corduroy crossing — the old log road Halvard's trips had all bent toward — and looked down on it gray in the pre-light, and my breath stopped.

Fresh logs. Pale-cut, sap still bleeding amber at the joints, laid across the soft span within the last week. Corduroy, new-built, wide enough for wagons — for heavy wagons, for something carried carefully by many men.

Someone had finished preparing the route. Someone had spent the winter building the road the melt was supposed to close.

And there, at the far end of the crossing where the trees closed in, a lantern swung once and went dark, and I counted — I couldn't help counting, Halvard trained me to count — at least a dozen shapes moving in the gray, organized, patient, armed.

Not waiting for the thaw to end.

Moving with it. Tonight, maybe. Perhaps already.

I sank down behind the ridge-stones with my heart hammering and did the only useful thing left to me: I took out my notebook, and by the light of a match cupped in both hands, I began to draw.
