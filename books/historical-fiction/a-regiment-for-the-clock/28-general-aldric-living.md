## 28. General Aldric, Living

The lake was exactly as I remembered it, which felt like an accusation.

Eleven years since I'd stood on that shore with a theodolite and a girl who thought contour lines were a form of poetry, and the water hadn't moved so much as a hand's width. The same grey morning light lay on it like a sheet over furniture in a house nobody lived in anymore. The survey camp had been struck years ago — I could see the ghost of it in my memory laid over the actual ground: where the tents had been, where Lira used to sit sharpening her pencils with a knife she held like it owed her money, where I had once said the most reckless sentence of my life and meant every word of it.

Now there was a boathouse. Someone had built a boathouse.

"That's new," Elira said quietly beside me.

"Everything about this is new," I said. "The lake is the only honest thing left."

We had come the last three miles off any road at all, along a line only I knew — Halvard's line, from the reconstructed route, through birch scrub and a stretch of frozen bog that would have swallowed anyone who trusted the official maps. That was the thing nobody in this whole filthy business seemed to understand yet: the marsh didn't kill you because it was treacherous. It killed you because you were reading someone else's map. Every drowning in that country was, in some sense, a clerical error.

Elira knocked on the boathouse door in a pattern — two, pause, one, pause, three — that she clearly hadn't invented herself. I filed that away with all the other things she wasn't telling me. My collection was getting large enough to require its own drawer.

The door opened on darkness, then a lamp, then a face.

I had expected a guard. What I got was a man of perhaps fifty, grey-bearded, wearing a fisherman's coat over what I recognized — because I had drawn its buttons often enough for regimental plates — as the uniform tunic of the Fourth Frontier Corps, stripped of everything that identified it. He looked at me the way a man looks at a horse he's considering buying.

"The mapmaker," he said. His accent was borderland, softened by years of somewhere else. "You're younger than they said."

"They say a lot of things," I said. "Most of them are on paper."

Something moved behind his eyes that might have been amusement. "Inside," he said. "Before someone else reads you."

* * *

The boathouse interior had been converted with the thoroughness of a man who expected to be comfortable nowhere for long. A stove. Cot. Crates stacked into furniture. Charts — real ones, army issue, pinned to the walls and annotated in a small furious hand. And at the far end, where the boat slip ran down into black water under the floorboards, a table had been set with a lamp and two chairs, arranged for conversation rather than interrogation, which after the week I'd had struck me as almost luxurious.

And by the stove, wrapped in a blanket, hands around a tin cup, sat an old man.

I knew him. Of course I knew him. Everyone in the Empire knew the face; it had hung in every garrison office and half the taverns for twenty years before the fall — the jaw, the moustache going white at the edges, the eyes that photographs always made look like a man listening to a cannon he hadn't fired yet. General Aldric. Dead two years. Buried with full honors in the capital, or so the newspapers said, in a coffin that had apparently contained either sand or another general.

He looked up at me and did not stand, which I understood later was not rudeness but honesty: standing cost him something now. His legs were bad. History had gotten into them somehow.

"So," Aldric said. His voice was gravel poured slowly. "Halvard's boy."

"I'm not—" I started, and stopped. There was no point denying it. I had been carrying the old man's atlas around inside me for two years like a second skeleton. "His apprentice."

"He spoke of you." The general took a sip from his cup and grimaced at it, as though the tea were also part of some larger defeat. "Letters came to me through third parties. He wrote that you drew coastlines 'with a conscience.' Do you know what that means, boy? It means most men draw coastlines with an ambition."

"Halvard didn't have ambitions."

"No." Something crossed the old face, quick and cold. "That was his entire problem. Come. Sit. You've walked far and you're looking at my charts instead of my face, which is flattering but rude."

I sat. Elira remained standing near the door, arms folded, watching the general with an expression I couldn't read and didn't try to. There was a formality between those two that predated me and would outlast this room.

"You faked your death," I said. It came out more accusatory than I intended. "There was a state funeral. There was a *coffin*."

"There is always a coffin," Aldric said. "It's the cheapest prop in the theater. Half the men this Empire has buried in the last decade are drinking coffee in Zurich or running ferries on the Danube. The other half wish they were." He set the cup down. "I lost a war I should never have been asked to fight. The court needed a corpse to blame it on, and I obliged them with one. The price of my disappearance was my fortune — thirty years of pay, prizes, and quieter earnings — which I could not carry across a frontier and could not leave behind for the wolves to fight over. So it went into the ground too. Figuratively speaking."

"Literally," I said. "The marsh road. Corduroy logging laid last autumn. Heavy transport, spring thaw."

The general looked at me for a long moment, then laughed — a short, rusty sound, like a gate opening after a winter. "Halvard's conscience," he said to the room, "and a clerk's eye. You read a logging road and saw a war chest. Yes. It comes out with the melt, when the ground is soft enough for wagons and wet enough to hide their tracks. Eleven days from now, if the weather holds, and eleven days from now every wolf between here and the frontier will know it too."

* * *

What followed was the strangest hour of my life, and I include the hour I spent hiding in a cellar while soldiers tore apart my mother's kitchen.

Aldric talked. Not about money — he mentioned the fortune the way a man mentions weather, as a fact of the landscape — but about men. Names. I counted forty-one of them before I stopped counting. Officers who had stood with him at the end and been cashiered for it, stripped of rank and pension, scattered through the border provinces with nothing but their names and the government's attention. Men who had followed him into a war the court had ordered and then disowned.

"Half of them are being watched," he said. "Three have already been arrested on charges invented for the purpose. Two died in custody, and the reports call it illness." His voice stayed level, but his thumb pressed hard against the tin cup until I heard the metal creak. "They served me because I asked. Now they're paying for it, and I am sitting by a lake drinking bad tea. I have spent two years being dead, mapmaker, and let me tell you what I have learned from it. Being dead is easy. It's the living who are expensive."

"And the chest buys them out," I said. "Passage. New names. Across the frontier."

"The chest *is* them," Aldric said flatly. "Every coin of it was already spoken for before I buried myself. Passage papers for forty-one families. That is the whole arithmetic. Which is why I find the current market so distasteful."

"The current market," Elira said from the doorway, dry as August.

"Your colonel wants it to fund a career," Aldric said, not looking at her. "The broker wants it because he has wanted everything I've ever touched for twenty years. And the government wants it because a fortune in a dead man's name is a fortune no ledger can audit." He turned back to me. "You see the difficulty. If any one of them takes it, my officers hang or rot, and the money changes masters and changes nothing. I need a fourth option."

"And the fourth option is a mapmaker's apprentice."

"The fourth option," Aldric said, "is the only man alive who knows both halves of the road out of these marshes, whose master died rather than sell it, and who — according to my information — has so far refused to sell it himself despite offers that would make your grandchildren weep." He leaned forward, and the lamplight caught his eyes, and I understood suddenly why forty thousand men had once marched where this man pointed. "So I will ask you plainly, Ivo Yarrow, and you may refuse, and nothing will happen to you for refusing except that history will go on happening without your permission. Draw us out. All forty-one. Draw the road, walk the road, put them across that frontier. Take whatever fee you like. Name it yourself."

* * *

I want to record honestly what happened in my head at that moment, because it was not noble.

My first thought was arithmetic. Forty-one officers, their families — call it a hundred and sixty souls, wagons, horses, provisions, moving by night through bog country during the narrow window of the thaw. It was insane. It was the largest cartographic commission ever proposed to me, and I had never actually led anything larger than a mule.

My second thought was Ferenc, and the card in my coat pocket, and the polite threat folded inside the politeness. Whatever I chose, that man would learn of it, and he did not forgive choices.

My third thought was my mother, burning nothing, saying nothing, and Tomas with his debts and his shame, and the letter under seal sitting in the shop drawer like a loaded gun.

And underneath all of it, quiet as groundwater, my fourth thought: *Halvard drew the truth and they killed him for where it led.* And here was the place it led, finally arrived at, asking me personally.

"You should know," I said, "that I'm already compromised. The government has my family. A colonel has my cooperation. A broker has my brother. If I draw this road, I'm drawing it from inside a cage."

"Yes," Aldric said simply. "I know all of it. I've known since the letter was sent — mine, incidentally. The bait that flushed you out of your shop was mine, passed through channels I trust. I apologize for the fright. I needed to see whether Halvard's boy would come when history called, or bolt." He smiled without warmth. "You came. Slowly, suspiciously, lying to several people on the way — which shows sense — but you came."

"You—" I looked at Elira. She was examining the wall charts with tremendous attention. "The summons. The *letter*. That was—"

"A test with excellent production values," Elira said mildly. "I told you the letter was bait, Ivo. I neglected to mention whose hook."

"You neglected," I said, "with precision."

"I'm an intelligence officer. Precision is the whole profession." But she wouldn't quite meet my eye, and something in my chest did a complicated maneuver — anger and relief and the absurd urge to laugh, all trying to use the same door at once.

"Captain Voss vouched for you," Aldric said. "At some professional risk, I gather. Her judgment of character has been reliable since she was seventeen years old, so I extended it to cover you." He rose, slowly, using the table, and I saw the effort it cost him and pretended not to. From a crate beneath the cot he lifted a canvas roll, untied it, and spread it on the table between us.

Maps. Old ones. Halvard's hand — unmistakably, achingly his, the confident strokes, the tiny exact letters. Survey notes from the marsh corridor, dated across four years. And at the center, a sketch of the lakeside camp itself, with the safehouse symbol I'd found marked on the reassembled route, annotated in two hands: Halvard's, and the general's.

"He surveyed for me for six years," Aldric said quietly. "The corridor, the crossings, the ground truth. He was the finest field man I ever employed and I paid him in silence and he accepted, because silence was what he valued." The old man's voice roughened slightly. "When they killed him, they took the notes from his office. These are his drafts. The copies. I have kept them for two years waiting for hands I trusted to finish what he started." He pushed the roll across the table toward me. "Finish it, Yarrow. Draw them out."

* * *

Outside, the light had gone silver. Somewhere across the lake a bird was making an argument with the morning. I stood at the boathouse door with Halvard's drafts in my arms — his handwriting against my chest like a heartbeat borrowed from a dead man — and I thought about the sentence I'd said here eleven years ago, laughing, meaning it: *if you ever need to disappear, I'll draw you the way out.*

One promise. One. Made by a boy who believed history happened elsewhere, to other people, preferably in books with the boring parts abridged.

And now it had collected. Not from Elira alone. From forty-one officers and their hundred and sixty ghosts-in-waiting. From a general who should have been a gravestone. From the whole crooked century, arriving at a boathouse door with its hand out.

"All right," I said, turning back into the lamplight. "I'll draw it. But I set the terms."

Aldric's eyebrows rose a fraction — the expression of a man discovering that the merchandise talks back.

"One," I said. "Nobody moves until I say the road is ready. Not Brandt's timetable, not the thaw, not yours. The marsh decides the schedule, and I speak for the marsh. Two — your people cross clean. No ambushes, no intercepts, none of your officers keeping souvenirs for leverage. They go out as refugees, not cargo. Three—" and here I looked at Elira, who had finally stopped studying the wall charts, "—when it's done, Captain Voss goes out with them. That's not negotiable. That clause was signed years ago."

Silence. The stove ticked. The general and the captain regarded each other across the length of the boathouse with an expression I couldn't parse — something old passing between them, some history of summers and commissions and debts I was only beginning to count.

"You realize," Elira said at last, to me, very evenly, "that crossing that frontier makes me a traitor under articles I swore an oath to."

"You swore an oath to me first," I said. "Summer of '86. Witnesses present." I nodded at the lake. "Him, mostly. Water and birches. Very reputable."

And she laughed — short, unwilling, real — and the sound did something to the room that all the lamplight couldn't.

"Done," Aldric said. "All terms. Now sit down, mapmaker, and show me what my road looks like — because I have news that will shorten your evenings." His face had gone grim again, the commandant reassembling itself out of the tired old man. "My people on the dock at Vellmark report that your friend Ferenc has been buying boats. Not one or two. Eleven. And yesterday he sent a rider north on the forest track."

Elira straightened. "Toward the lake?"

"Toward the lake," Aldric said, "arriving tomorrow."
