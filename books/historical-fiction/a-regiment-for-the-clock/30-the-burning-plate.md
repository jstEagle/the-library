## 30. The Burning Plate

There is a particular kind of silence that a workshop makes when you know it is about to stop being yours.

I had heard it once before — the night after Halvard died, when the lamps were out and the presses cold and the whole shop seemed to hold its breath around his empty chair. That silence had been grief. This one was arithmetic. Somewhere across Vellmark, Ferenc's men were counting doors and lamplighters and the habits of a mapmaker's apprentice who had grown predictable in his mourning, and their sum was coming due tonight.

I knew it the way you know weather before it arrives: not by evidence but by the smell of it. Tomas had come by at dusk with his collar up and his hands shaking, saying only, "They asked me again. When you'd be alone." He hadn't needed to say more. My brother has never been able to lie to me with his face; he lies with his feet instead, and his feet had been pointing at the door all evening.

So I worked while the light lasted, and then I worked by lamp, and what I worked on was the choice.

* * *

Let me explain what a man holds in his hands when he holds two versions of the same map.

The true plate was Halvard's, finished by me, corrected by me against the survey notes I had bought back from a dying man's memory. It showed the marsh corridor as it actually was: the corduroy crossings laid down over winter for heavy transport, the firmer ground along the old logging spurs, the one dry line from the lakeside camp to the frontier stone that no army had ever charted because no army had ever thought the marshes worth the trouble. It was, I do not exaggerate, the most valuable piece of paper between the Baltic and the border. Men had died for less complete versions of it. Halvard had been one of them.

The false plate was mine entirely, and I confess a certain pride in it that I am not sure sits well on a Christian soul.

A forgery, done properly, is not a drawing of a lie. Anyone can draw a lie; a lie drawn crudely is a confession. No — a forgery must be a truth with one organ removed or misplaced, so that everything else keeps beating convincingly. The route I drew for Ferenc followed the real corridor for nine-tenths of its length. It used the real spurs, the real crossings, the real landmarks Halvard had noted in his own hand. A buyer would check it against whatever scraps he'd gathered from other sources, and every check would pass, because nearly all of it *was* the source.

But the final third of the false route bent east along the low flats — plausible, even elegant, the kind of alignment a draftsman might choose to spare his convoy two river bends. And the spring melt, which was already muttering under the ice when I drew it, would turn those flats into a shallow brown sea by the time anyone marched a wagon train onto them. Corduroy floats, incidentally. Did you know that? I did not know it until March, when I tested a section near the millpond and watched a log road lift off its bed like something surfacing to breathe. Halvard would have known. Halvard knew everything about water except how to stay out of its politics.

I engraved both plates. That is the part I want on the record, whoever ends up reading this, if anyone does: I did not sketch the forgery in charcoal and hope. I cut it into copper, the way Halvard taught me, with the same burin, the same pressure, the same patience. Two plates, identical to the touch of a fingertip, differing in perhaps four millimetres of line in a marsh no honest map had ever bothered to name.

And now Ferenc's men were coming, and I had to decide which one to lose.

* * *

You would think the decision would be simple. Keep the truth, give away the lie — any child could sort those two piles. But here is what nobody tells you about your first deliberate treachery: it is not the act that costs. The act is easy. It is the moment before, when the thing in your hand stops being ink and copper and becomes, unmistakably, a person's life — several persons' lives — and you understand that whichever pile you keep, you are keeping blood in it.

Because the false plate was not going into a drawer. It was going to Ferenc, and Ferenc was going to put men on it, and those men were going to drive wagons onto ground that would open beneath them like a mouth. I told myself they were Ferenc's hired wolves, that Brandt's soldiers would be among them, that every soul on that drowning road would be there to rob or be robbed. I told myself the general's officers — Aldric's tired, hunted remnants — would travel the true line, my line, the one I kept. I told myself all of this, and it was all true, and none of it made the copper weigh any less.

There is a difference, I discovered that night, between lying to survive and lying to kill. I had done the first my whole life, cheerfully — lied to customs men about shipments, lied to my mother about where the money for Tomas's debts went, lied to Elira in her interrogation chair about what waited in Halvard's desk. Those lies had been walls. This was the first time I was building a floor over water and inviting men to walk on it.

I sat with both plates on the bench before me and let myself feel the whole weight of it, because I suspected — correctly, as it turned out — that I would want to remember having felt it. A man who chooses a lie without weighing it will choose anything afterward. The weighing is the last piece of the honest man you get to keep.

Then I wrapped the false plate in oilcloth, the way one wraps something precious, because Ferenc's men would search the wrapping before they searched the contents, and thieves respect care.

* * *

They came at the second hour past midnight, which flattered me — I had guessed the first hour, and it pleased some petty corner of my mind to have overestimated them.

There were three, and they were good. No banging at the shutters, no official voices demanding entry in the name of the Crown. Instead the watchman's rattle passed my corner twice at the wrong interval — our street's watchman always hesitated at the cooper's awning to scratch a dog behind the ears, and tonight the rattle went by without hesitation, which meant either the dog had died or the man walking in his coat had never met it. Then the lamp above the sign of the atlas went dark. Ours was the only lamp on the street still lit; someone had climbed and pinched it quietly while I sat inside pretending to work.

I had prepared the stage for an hour. The true plate slept where no searcher looks first because no searcher believes anyone would be fool enough: pressed flat beneath the lead lining of the etching trough, under an inch of spent acid we kept for stripping old grounds. Let them find it if they tore the shop to its bones; I judged they wouldn't, because I intended to make finding things unnecessary.

The knock, when it came, was polite. Of course it was. Everything about Ferenc was polite; his menace arrived gift-wrapped, with a card.

I opened the door to a broad, pleasant-faced man with the hands of someone who had done labour and the posture of someone who now paid others to do it. Behind him, half in shadow, two shapes that were trying very hard not to look like soldiers.

"Master Yarrow." He inclined his head. "Forgive the hour. Mr. Ferenc understands you've been… difficult to reach during business hours."

"Business hours are when I'm working," I said. "Which is now."

"So I see." His eyes went past me to the bench, to the oilcloth bundle sitting on it with theatrical innocence. "Mr. Ferenc sent me with a final offer. He worries the market for your master's papers may close shortly. Markets do that."

"And when they close," I said, "the goods are worthless. Is that the threat, or is there a better one you're saving?"

The pleasant face didn't move. "Mr. Ferenc admires craftsmanship. He asks only that you consider whose hands the craft ends up in. There is a colonel who wants your route. There are buyers across the border who want your route. Mr. Ferenc merely wants to be the one holding it when the bidding stops. You've met his courtesy. I'd hate for you to meet the alternative."

I looked at him for a long moment, and I did something I had rehearsed at the bench until my hands stopped shaking: I sighed like a tired man doing arithmetic, and I stepped aside from the bench.

"I'll tell you what I told your principal," I said. "Halvard's papers go to one buyer, whole, or to no one. I don't auction my dead master."

"One buyer whole," he repeated pleasantly.

"The plate. The notes. The lot." I gestured at the bundle. "That's everything worth carrying. The rest is proofs and spoiled copper."

He crossed to the bench and unwrapped the oilcloth with the reverence of a man handling relics, and I watched him examine the false plate — watched him run a thumb along the eastern bend, exactly where I wanted thumbs to run — and I understood that I was watching a man fall in love with a lie. It is a strange feeling. Not triumph. Nothing so clean. More like watching a guest drink from a cup you know is poisoned, except the poison takes three weeks to work and the guest is a wolf and the wolf is going to drown in a marsh wearing my coastline.

"It's genuine?" he asked, without looking up. As if genuineness could be confirmed by thumb.

"It's Halvard's engraving and my finishing," I said, and every word of it was true, which is the dirtiest trick a liar has.

"The price stands. More than stands." He named a figure that would have bought our building twice over, and I let my breath catch audibly, because a man who doesn't haggle is a man who doesn't believe his own goods. We haggled. I conceded ten percent. He counted out a portion and promised the rest on delivery, which suited me perfectly — I wanted this transaction to have the texture of greed, not desperation.

At the door he paused. "Mr. Ferenc says you're too young to have built all this alone. He says your master must have had partners. He'd like their names eventually."

"He can have my master's name," I said. "It's carved on the stone in the churchyard. The rest of him kept his accounts privately."

The pleasant man smiled as if we had shared a joke, tipped his hat to the dark street, and took my lie out into the night.

* * *

I gave them a quarter hour. Long enough to clear the corner; short enough that if they doubled back to watch the shop, they'd see a light burning and a silhouette working, an apprentice grieving over his plates, industrious and broken and harmless.

Then I lifted the true plate from the acid trough, wiped the lead dust from its face, and held it to the lamp.

The marshes lay there in Halvard's lines and mine, honest to the last contour: the safe corridor, the camp marked with the symbol only two living people could read, the way out that I had promised a laughing girl beside a lake seven summers ago and had finally, in copper, begun to keep. It weighed almost nothing. Copper is lighter than guilt; I recommend it to anyone choosing materials.

I thought about burning it. That was the romantic option, and I will admit the romance tempted me — feed it to the stove, let the truth go up in a column of sparks, stand pure and ruined among my ashes. But purity is a luxury purchased with other people's deaths. The general's officers needed this route drawn out. Elira needed it. Somewhere ahead of me, in chapters I could not yet read, I sensed the true plate would need to exist, would need to be printed, carried, consulted, maybe even surrendered to someone I hadn't yet decided to trust. You cannot guide anyone along a line you've burned.

So instead of fire, I did what mapmakers do. I hid the truth inside the ordinary.

I pulled a rejected proof from the scrap bin — a misprinted test of the estuary sheet, smudged, worthless, one of dozens — and I printed the true plate onto its back in pale ink, lightly, the ghost of a ghost, then set it to dry between two boards of genuine rubbish. Anyone searching the shop would find a spoiled proof. Only a man who already knew there was something on it would think to hold it against the light at the correct angle, and only two people alive knew the angle, and one of them was across town pretending she didn't love me and the other was me.

The copper plate itself went into the wall cavity behind the drying rack, behind a loose brick Halvard had mortared crooked years ago, back when I'd assumed it was simply poor workmanship and not a man building himself hiding places against a future he saw coming. Master, I thought, pressing the brick home, you taught me the coastlines and the contours and the burin's angle. But this — the crooked brick, the poisoned route, the truth printed faint on the back of failure — this is what you were really teaching me all along, and I'm sorry it took your grave to finish my education.

* * *

Tomas came back near dawn, grey-faced, letting himself in with the key he'd had since boyhood.

"They took it," he said. "The big one's men were drinking at the Anchor by three. Celebrating. They said the deal was done." He stood wringing his cap. "Ivo — what did you give them? Please tell me you gave them nothing real."

I was printing a clean copy of the estuary sheet, ordinary and blameless, because my hands needed occupation or they'd start remembering things. I looked at my brother — my brother who had sold my habits to Ferenc's men out of love and terror, who had confessed it to me with his eyes down, whom I had forgiven in the specific and unforgivable way family forgives — and I weighed what to tell him.

Here is another thing I learned that night: a chosen lie does not stay singular. It breeds. First I lied to Ferenc with copper. Then I lied to his messenger with my voice. And now my brother stood in front of me asking to be protected from the truth, and I found the lie rising in me smooth as water finding its level.

"I gave them the route through the eastern flats," I said.

Tomas frowned. "Is that bad?"

"It's the route I chose to give them," I said, which was true, and let him make of it what he could. "Tomas. After the thaw — whatever happens out on the marshes, whatever people say happened — I need you not to ask me questions. Can you do that? For Mother, if not for me. She sleeps easier believing her sons are dull."

"She doesn't believe that," Tomas said. "She just hopes loudly enough that we pretend to." He hesitated at the door. "Ivo. The men Ferenc has — some of them wear uniforms under their coats. I've seen the buttons."

"I know," I said, and did not tell him whose buttons, because Brandt's name in my brother's mouth was a death sentence looking for a witness, and my brother had paid enough already.

When he was gone I sat down at Halvard's chair — not my bench, his chair, the one I still didn't use for work — and let the night catch up with me.

* * *

People imagine that a man's first great sin arrives with thunder, with a blackened heart, with some visible mark. Mine arrived with a wrapped parcel and a haggled discount and a polite hat-tip, and the most terrible thing about it was how competent I had been. I had not trembled when it mattered. I had conceded ten percent like a tradesman. Somewhere in the last year, between the letter under seal and the dying colleague's testimony and the woman who would not say my name in daylight, I had become the kind of man who could sell a drowning to strangers and count the change.

And yet — I keep circling this, and I suppose the circling is the point — I could not summon regret. That frightened me worse than the competence did. Because the men who would ride my forged route were coming to steal a dead general's fortune under the protection of a colonel who meant to rob his own country, and if the marsh swallowed a few of them, the world would lose nothing it would miss. The lie was cruel and the lie was just, and I no longer believed those two qualities cancelled each other out. Halvard had refused to draw a single dishonest line and it had killed him. I had drawn mine, and somewhere across the city a spider was coiling it into his web, satisfied, certain, already spending the money.

Ferenc believed he had bought the border. Brandt believed his trap was baited. Even the general, I suspect, believed his passage was arranged by friends.

Only I knew that the map in Ferenc's hands ended in water, and that the true line slept behind a crooked brick waiting for the thaw — and that within the fortnight, every one of those certainties was going to collide out on the marshes, and the collision would need a mapmaker standing in the middle of it.

Outside, far off toward the river, the ice boomed once — a long, cracking report, like a signal gun fired by nobody.

The thaw was coming early.
