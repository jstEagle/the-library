## 33. What Tomas Paid

The road out of the marshes was not a road so much as a rumor of one — a seam of frozen mud and corduroy logs that the thaw had begun to peel apart like old wallpaper. I walked it at the head of eleven exhausted men, counting them every hundred paces the way Halvard taught me to count anything you couldn't afford to lose. Eleven officers, one general gone ahead with his own guide toward the lakeside camp, and behind us, somewhere in the drowning ground, a colonel's ambitions sinking under the same brown water as his convoy.

And limping beside me, refusing my shoulder twice an hour on principle, my brother.

Tomas had taken a bullet through the meat of his left arm — through, not into, which the old dockworkers say is the luckiest kind, though I noticed he didn't say it himself. He'd taken it at the crossing, of all places, standing in the open with Ferenc's men coming down both banks, waving Aldric's stragglers toward the treeline like a man directing cargo at the harbor. He'd done it badly, too. He'd shouted. He'd waved both arms. Everything they teach you never to do if you want to live, he did, because Tomas had never been trained for anything except lifting heavy objects and loving people too obviously to hide it.

"You're walking crooked," he said.

"I'm walking the way the map says."

"The map says crooked?"

"The map says everything worth knowing is crooked. Straight lines are what men draw who've never been anywhere."

He grunted at that, which from Tomas was a round of applause. We went on. The ice under the corduroy made sounds like distant furniture being dragged across a floor, and every officer behind us flinched at each groan, and I pretended not to notice that I flinched loudest of all.

* * *

We made the treeline by dark and I called the halt where Halvard's notes said a charcoal burner's hut once stood, and where — because Halvard's notes were always half prophecy — a charcoal burner's hut still stood, leaning but willing. I got a fire down to embers small enough to hide under a kettle, and the officers arranged themselves around it with the particular silence of men who have stopped believing in fires.

Tomas sat apart, which was unlike him, holding his arm against his chest the way you'd hold a parcel someone else might want back. I sat down next to him and offered him the flask, and he took it, and we watched the ember-light do its poor best.

"Say it," he said finally.

"Say what?"

"Whatever you've been not saying since the crossing. You've got a face like a ledger with a bad column. Say it so we can both sleep."

I considered lying to him. It occurred to me, with some professional interest, how easy it would be. I had spent the winter learning to draw two versions of everything — two routes, two faces, two truths, one for the light and one for the drawer. Lying to Tomas would have been as simple as breathing, and about as private.

"I gave them a date," he said, before I could decide. "The thaw. Ferenc's men came to me — Mother's name in their mouths, our street, our door. And I gave them the date because I thought—" He stopped, worked his jaw. "I thought I could warn you after. There'd be time after. There's always time after, Ivo, that's what I believed. Like a tide. You get knocked down, the water goes back out, and then there's all this beach to walk on and fix things."

"And instead," I said, "the water took Mother first."

His face did something I hope never to see again on any face I love. "They told me she'd be questioned. Questioned. A word like that — you think paper and tea. You don't think soldiers at dawn."

"No," I said. "You don't. That's the whole art of it. Nobody draws the dawn arrest on a map. Cartographers are sentimental cowards, all of us. We mark churches and wells and leave out the places where the world turns wrong."

"I sold you," Tomas said flatly. "That's the plain shape of it. Don't dress it in tides for me."

"You didn't sell me," I said. "You bought Mother. With the only coin you had. There's a difference, and the difference matters, though I grant you it doesn't feel like much tonight."

"It feels like nothing."

"It feels like nothing," I agreed, "and it is the entire difference between you and Brandt. Brandt sold everyone and bought himself. You sold one person to buy another, and hated yourself while doing it, and then—" I nodded at his arm, at the bandage I'd cut from his own shirt because there'd been nothing else. "Then you paid over the asking price."

He looked at his arm as if seeing the account for the first time.

"That wasn't payment," he muttered. "That was panic. Those men were going to shoot the general's people in the open and I panicked and stood up and—"

"Tomas." I waited until he looked at me. "I have spent three months learning what men look like when they lie, and I can tell you that no liar alive describes his own courage as panic afterward. Liars describe it as necessity. They keep the receipts. You're giving yours away."

* * *

He was quiet a long time. Out past the fire, one of the officers — a major with frostbitten ears whose name I never learned — shifted in his sleep and murmured something that sounded like a parade ground order, some drill from a life that had ended for him two years before anyone told him.

"When you were small," Tomas said, "you used to follow me to the docks. Every morning. Mother would tell you not to and you'd go anyway, ten years old, walking in my footprints in the snow like a little surveyor. And the men used to laugh and say, there goes Yarrow and his shadow. And I liked it. I liked being followed. I liked being somebody's map." His mouth twisted. "And then you grew up and started drawing better maps than mine, and I stopped knowing what I was to you. Just the brother with the debts. The brother Ferenc found so easy to buy."

"Ferenc found you easy to buy," I said, "because you were standing where he needed a man, holding things he wanted, and because nobody had ever told you what you were protecting. That's not cheapness, Tomas. That's ignorance, and ignorance gets rented out every day in this country by better men than you. Halvard knew the route was worth killing for and he died rather than rent it out. You didn't know. You learned the price the same hour the rest of us did."

"And you?" he asked. "What did you learn the price of?"

I looked into the embers and told him the truth, because he'd earned it and because I was tired of carrying it alone. "That I drew a map to drown men. On purpose. With care. I checked the melt lines twice. I chose the ford that fails. Somewhere under that water right now are men who trusted the plate I inked, and I sit here warm."

Tomas was silent. Then: "Were they good men?"

"Some. Some were just wolves following a wolf. One of them was a colonel who would have let Mother rot in a cell to keep his ledgers clean."

"So you drowned wolves," Tomas said slowly, working it through the way he'd work a stubborn crate loose, "to save a mother and eleven tired men. And you checked your work twice."

"Yes."

"Then we're even," he said. "Because I gave up a date to save a mother and damned near got her killed, and I bled trying to take it back. Neither of us is clean, Ivo. But neither of us is Brandt either, and I'm starting to understand that's the most any man gets to claim this year."

I laughed — actually laughed, out here in the dark with the marsh groaning behind us — because he was right, and because he'd arrived at in one night of pain what I'd needed a whole atlas to learn.

"We're even," I said. "God help us both."

* * *

He slept after that, really slept, arm propped on a rolled coat, breath going slow. I sat up keeping the embers fed and did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding: Elira across the frontier by now or close to it; Mother home or nearly; the shop standing; Halvard's notes in my coat, damp at the corners, worth more than everything Ferenc had ever offered and priced, now, beyond any market.

Near dawn, Tomas woke without warning, the way dockmen do, and looked at the grey light and then at me.

"When we get home," he said, "I'm going to stand in the shop and watch you finish the atlas. The whole thing. Every plate. And you're going to explain each one to me like I'm simple, because I am, a bit, about paper."

"They'll read the blank marshes and call it diligence," I said. "Officials love a blank. Blanks mean nothing to steal."

"And the truth?"

"The truth walks east with eleven officers and a woman who owes me a letter."

Tomas considered this, then nodded once, heavily, satisfied — the nod he used to give when a load sat right on the barrow. Then he frowned, the way he did when a load didn't.

"There's smoke," he said. "Behind us. Far off. On the ridge line."

I turned. Against the paling sky, thin and deliberate, a thread of smoke rose from exactly the place a signal fire would be built — high, visible, patient. Not burning wreckage. Burning wood, stacked by hands, tended by someone waiting to be seen.

Somebody knew where we'd crossed. Somebody was telling the world we were coming.
