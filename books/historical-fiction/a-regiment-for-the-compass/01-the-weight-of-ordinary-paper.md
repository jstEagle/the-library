## 1. The Weight of Ordinary Paper

The river carried us toward Ashmoor the way rivers carry everything in Vell now—slowly, obediently, and without comment.

I stood at the rail of the *Mild Contentment*, a grain barge with a name too peaceful to be trusted, and watched the winter banks slide past: bare willows, drowned fences, a shrine to some saint the empire had already struck from its calendars. The wind off the water had teeth. I let it bite me, because a grain clerk named Tam Weller would stand out in the cold complaining about it, and I had been Tam Weller for eleven days now, and Tam Weller complained about weather, about grain prices, about his feet.

"Cold enough to crack the millstones," I said to the deckhand beside me, and he grunted agreement, and that was the whole conversation. Perfect. Ordinary paper doesn't need many words.

My satchel hung against my ribs under my coat, where it had hung since I left Corren Vale nine days ago. It held nothing but papers—eleven sheets, folded twice, sealed with plain wax a child could have broken. I had not broken them. I would never break them. That was the oath, and the oath was the only furniture left in the house of who I was.

*Carry, never read.*

Four words, taught to me at fourteen by a woman named Mistress Ovel, who had hands like a carpenter's and a voice like a closing door. She'd made me say them back to her forty times on the night I took the badge—the little tin courier's pin they gave us, stamped with a wren in flight. The wren carries what it carries. It does not open the seed.

"You will be tempted," she said. "Not by villains with knives. By boredom. By loneliness. By a long road and nothing to do but wonder. Wonder is the first lockpick. Don't hand anyone your curiosity; it's the only tool they need."

I was fourteen and immortal and I thought she was being dramatic. I am twenty-six now, and I know she was describing the rest of my life.

* * *

The thing no one tells you about being a courier is how much of it is waiting dressed up as work. Waiting for barges, for fog to lift, for checkpoints to change shift. And in all that waiting, the satchel breathes against your side like something alive.

I've learned to think of it as ordinary paper. That's the trick, if there is one. Every document I carry—manifests, ledgers, sealed correspondence, whatever this was—I make it boring inside my own head. Not secret. Boring. A grain clerk carrying grain accounts. Numbers about bushels and moisture content and the price of chaff in the southern markets. Papers so dull that even if I were searched, even if the seal were cracked, whoever read them would yawn and wave me through.

History, I told myself, watching the grey water fold and unfold, happens to other people. To magistrates and colonels and men whose names get printed in broadsheets with black borders. My job exists precisely so that history can happen somewhere else while the rest of us move our little loads from one quiet place to another. I was the gap in the record. The blank margin. And margins are safe. Margins survive every fire.

Nine days I'd been telling myself that.

The trouble with rehearsing a cover story on a barge is that you run out of river before you run out of doubt. So I ran it again anyway, mouthing the words into my collar where the wind couldn't take them.

*Tam Weller. Grain clerk, out of Corren Vale, contracted to the Ashmoor factor's house of Pell & Sons to reconcile their autumn receipts against the granary tallies. Traveling alone. No family worth mentioning—mother dead, father never spoken of, which was true, which was always true. Reason for the papers in the satchel: contracts and tally-sheets, nothing interesting, ask the factor if you don't believe me.*

Every word of it true except the ones that mattered.

That's the craft of it, really. Lies built entirely from true planks rot slower than lies made fresh. My mother *is* dead—fever, when I was twelve, though the record says otherwise and I keep the record's version because it's better armor. I *do* know grain arithmetic; couriers learn three trades minimum, and mine are grain clerking, tallow inspection, and playing a mediocre fiddle badly enough to be forgettable. I could talk chaff percentages until a customs man wept.

What I couldn't explain—not to myself, not to the grey river, not to the wren on my hidden pin—was why the Republic's last couriers were still running at all. Everyone I passed on the water looked conquered. The ferry towns flew the imperial double-eagle where the republic's comet used to hang. Soldiers at the locks wore grey instead of blue and checked papers with a bored thoroughness that was somehow worse than cruelty. The war was over. Everyone knew the war was over.

And yet three weeks ago a woman I'd never seen, in a safehouse that smelled of tallow and ink, had pressed this satchel into my hands and given me a route and a name—*Halvard Brann, Ashmoor*—and told me the delivery was worth more than my life and less than my curiosity. Worth less than my curiosity. I'd turned the phrase over for nine days like a coin worn smooth. What kind of instruction is that? It sounded almost like permission. Or a warning wearing permission's coat.

*Carry, never read,* I reminded myself, and the river said nothing back, and the satchel breathed against my ribs.

* * *

Toward dusk the barge locked through at Tarn Weir, the last checkpoint before Ashmoor water. I stood in line behind two fishmongers and a widow with a crate of hens, shuffling forward in the cold, and I did what I always do at checkpoints: became aggressively unremarkable. Shoulders down. Eyes at the level of the soldier's third button. Face arranged into the mild, put-upon patience of a man who has stood in a great many lines and expects to stand in a great many more.

The soldier at the table barely looked at me. He looked at my papers, which were flawless—Pell & Sons' factor in Corren Vale owed the network a favor going back years—and he waved me through with the weary courtesy of a man who has already decided the empire will outlive him and sees no reason to hurry history along.

Ordinary paper. It passes through soldiers' hands like water through a net.

But as I stepped back onto the barge's cold deck, I caught sight of something at the far end of the weir station that snagged on me like a burr. A small desk had been set up apart from the soldiers' table, and behind it sat a woman in civilian dress—grey coat, neat as a ledger-line—with an open book before her and a pen that moved steadily, unhurried, writing down names. As each traveler passed, she asked them something. Most answered in a few words and moved on. One old man argued; she smiled at him the way a cat smiles, patient and private, and he subsided and answered.

A census. Of course—a census. The empire loved counting things; counting was how it digested what it swallowed. I had been counted a dozen times since the fall, in a dozen towns, under a dozen names, and it had never once mattered. Names go into books. Books sit on shelves. Margins stay margins.

Still, I watched her longer than I should have. There was something in the way she wrote—not the bored scrawl of a clerk filling quotas but the careful, deliberate hand of someone keeping a personal account, as though the empire's forms were merely her rough notes for a truer ledger underneath. When she looked up from her book and scanned the queue, her eyes crossed mine for half a second, and I felt the strangest sensation: not of being suspected, but of being *filed*. A discrepancy noted. A column opened.

I turned away and found a shadowed corner by the grain sacks and became Tam Weller again, thoroughly, all the way down to the ache in my feet. Tomorrow we'd reach Ashmoor. I'd deliver eleven boring sheets of ordinary paper to a printer named Halvard Brann, collect whatever receipt the network required, and be gone on the northbound barge before the census taker's pen could dry.

The wind came off the water and I pulled my collar up and rehearsed it once more, whispering into the wool:

*Tam Weller. Grain clerk. Contracts and tally-sheets, nothing interesting.*

Below my ribs, the satchel pressed warm against my heartbeat, patient as a buried coal.

I did not know then that the woman with the pen would remember me. I did not know that eleven sheets of ordinary paper were the keys to a grave the empire was still digging. And I did not know that before the week was out, the blank margin I had spent my whole life being would catch fire like everything else in Ashmoor.

I knew only the oath, and the river, and the cold—

—and that tomorrow, God willing, I would be nobody again.
