## 2. One Question Too Many

The queue at Ashmoor's gate moved the way queues always move in occupied towns: slowly, and with everyone pretending not to be afraid of it.

I had rehearsed my name until it sat in my mouth like a pebble, smooth from handling. *Merrin Hale, grain clerk, Vellport factor for Corrow & Sons.* I said it to myself in the rhythm of the barge engine, over and over, until Merrin felt less like a costume than a coat I'd worn so long I'd forgotten I owned shoulders. That was the trick of it. A cover story isn't a story at all if you tell it right. It's just Tuesday.

The barge had come downriver through a morning of gray light, the water the color of pewter, mist hanging in the willows along the banks like laundry no one would claim. Ashmoor announced itself first by smell—coal smoke, tallow, the sour-sweet breath of the tanneries downstream—and then by its walls, which were not walls at all anymore but a customs palisade thrown up where the old toll arch used to stand. Imperial timber. Imperial nails. Someone had painted the double-headed eagle on a board wide enough to be seen from the river, and someone else, braver or drunker, had already thrown something at it. The stain was still there, dark against the gilded feathers.

I stood with my satchel and my ledger-case and let the line carry me forward one shuffling step at a time. Around me: a drover with three exhausted sheep; a widow with a coffin-sized crate she claimed was looms; two brothers who looked enough alike that even their mother might have needed the paperwork. Everyone held their documents the same way—too tightly, edges soft with sweat—and everyone wore the same expression, which was no expression at all, carefully maintained, the face of a person thinking about grain prices or boot leather or anything, anything other than what they were thinking about.

I knew the expression intimately. I'd been wearing it since I was fourteen years old, the day Master Corvin pressed his thumb into wax and made me swear.

*Carry, never read,* the oath goes. *Deliver whole what you are given, ask nothing of its contents, and if they take you, let them take you empty.* There were eleven clauses and I could recite all of them, but those were the ones that mattered, the spine of the thing. The couriers' guild dressed it up in ceremony—the candle, the closed box, the words spoken with your hand flat on a blank ledger—but underneath the theater it was simple. We were furniture. We were the riverbed the current passed over. History happened *to* other people, and my entire profession, my entire life, rested on keeping it that way.

A man ahead of me argued about his transit stamp and lost. The soldiers turned him around without malice, the way you'd turn a cart. He went back down the road he'd come, and the line closed behind him as though he'd never existed, and I thought: *there but for a good lie go I.*

Then it was my turn.

The census table had been set up inside the gatehouse arch, out of the wind, and behind it sat a woman perhaps forty years old with ink-black hair pinned in a coil so precise it looked machined. Her uniform was imperial gray but better cut than the soldiers', her collar fastened to the throat, and on the table before her lay an open register, a stoppered inkwell, a blotter worn into a valley by use. She had a pen in hand and did not put it down when she looked up at me. She looked up at me the way an apothecary looks at a specimen.

"Next," she said, though there was only me. Then, with a smile that arrived a half-second late, like thunder after lightning: "Good morning. Name and purpose."

"Merrin Hale. Grain clerk." My voice came out bored, which was the goal. Boredom is armor. "Corrow and Sons, Vellport factors. I'm here to audit the granary tallies for the winter requisition." I set my papers on her table—not thrust, not slid, placed, the way a man does forty times a week—and waited while she read.

She read thoroughly. That was the first wrong note, if I'd been listening properly. Most census clerks skimmed; the empire had processed so many towns that its own functionaries had gone numb, stamping faces through like coins. This woman read every line of my guild letter, every entry in my factor's ledger, and while she read she watched me, and I mean *watched*—not the glancing suspicion of a soldier but the steady, patient attention of someone counting.

"Vellport," she said. "Long way for grain tallies."

"Requisitions don't walk themselves."

"No. They don't." The smile again, that half-second lag. "You'll forgive the questions. Ashmoor is being brought into the register. New town, new forms." She opened a fresh page in her register with a crackle that sounded, in the cold air under the arch, like ice going out on a pond. "Occupation?"

"Grain clerk."

"Years in the trade?"

"Nine."

"Where were you born?"

"Vellport. Saltgate parish." True, all of it, or true-adjacent, which is safer than truth because truth has edges that can be matched against other truths. Merrin Hale had been constructed with care back in the spring, built out of a real clerk who had died of fever, with his real history and his real handwriting habits. I'd studied him the way an actor studies a role. I could have told her the name of Saltgate's sexton.

She wrote. The pen scratched. Under the arch, the drover's sheep bleated somewhere behind me and a soldier told him to shut them up. Odile—I saw the name plate then, brass letters on gray wood, CENSUS TAKER O. VANE—Odile Vane asked me my age, my height, any distinguishing marks, whether I traveled alone, how long I intended to remain. And I answered everything with the flat fluency of long rehearsal, and each answer landed correctly, and I remember feeling the particular cold satisfaction of the well-made lie settling into place, stone by stone, a wall going up between me and the world.

And then she looked up from her register, tilted her head slightly, as if she were listening to something under my voice, and asked:

"And your mother's maiden name?"

The question went through me like a needle finding the gap in armor.

I want to be precise about why. It wasn't that the answer was dangerous—any competent cover carries a mother in it, a birthplace, a plausible little family tree. Merrin Hale's mother was Anneth Hale, born Corrow, a cousin-line to his employers, tidy as a bookbinder's stitch. I could have said it asleep. No—the danger was the question itself. I had crossed nine checkpoints in six years wearing this face. I had filled out imperial forms in four provinces. The census asked your name, your trade, your origin, your length of stay. It had never once, in all the empire's hungry cataloguing, asked for a mother's maiden name.

That question belonged to genealogy. To blood. To a regime that had stopped counting bodies and started counting *lines*.

"You'll want that for the kinship column," Odile Vane said pleasantly, pen poised, as if she were offering to do me a favor.

"Corrow," I said. "Anneth Corrow, before she married." Smooth as poured oil. No hesitation—or none worth measuring. I even supplied the small extra detail, the gratuitous flourish that liars know sells the goods: "Her people were millers, upriver. That's how Corrow and Sons came to hold our account, matter of fact."

It was good work. It was some of the best lying I've ever done, and I say that without pride, because in that moment I understood that the quality of the lie was exactly the problem. She watched me deliver it and something moved behind her eyes—nothing so crude as doubt, nothing I could name, just a shift, the way water shifts when a fish passes under it. She wrote *Corrow* in the register in a small, exact hand.

"Well, Mr. Hale," she said. "Everything appears to be in order." She stamped my transit paper, blew on the ink out of habit rather than need, and handed it across. "Welcome to Ashmoor. Mind the curfew—bell rings at dusk, and the garrison doesn't distinguish between clerks and anyone else."

"I'll mind it."

"One more thing." She hadn't looked down at her register. She was looking at my hands where they rested on her table, taking back my papers. Looking, I realized much later, at the faint shadow of ink in the creases of my knuckles—clerk's ink, honest enough, the stain of a man who keeps tallies. Or courier's ink. Or compositor's. From across a table, one ink is very like another. "If you recall anything further—dates, names, corrections—the census office is in the old assize hall by the square. We're always glad of precision."

"I'll bear it in mind."

"Precision," she said, still smiling that delayed smile, "is the whole of the law now. Everything else is commentary."

I walked through the gate into Ashmoor with my stamp warm in my pocket and my heart going like a drum in a parade I couldn't hear the music for. Behind me, the sheep complained, and Odile Vane called, "Next," to whoever was next, and the line closed over the place where I had stood as completely as water closes over a dropped coin.

The mask held. I kept telling myself that, all the way down the high street, past the ration lines and the proclamation boards. The mask held.

Barely. And I had heard the *barely* in my own ears, and I was almost certain she hadn't.

Almost.

* * *

There is a discipline to discrepancy. Anyone can collect answers; answers are cheap, they lie in rows like eggs. What I collected were the cracks *between* answers, the places where a life failed to join up with itself.

My name is Odile Vane, and before the annexation I kept the parish registers of a town smaller than this one, and before that I audited wool weights for a merchant house, and before *that*—well. A woman is allowed one sealed room in her history, and mine stays shut. What matters is that I have spent thirty years learning that documents confess. People lie; paper only hesitates. You learn to feel hesitation the way a blind man feels a threshold underfoot.

The grain clerk was a hesitation.

Not in his answers—his answers were immaculate, which was itself interesting. Nine years in the trade, born in Saltgate, mother a Corrow of the milling Corrows, and every fact delivered at the same measured tempo, like coins counted out from a purse. Ordinary men don't speak in counted coins. Ordinary men stumble, correct themselves, embellish, forget their own ages. They say *nine years—no, ten, was it before the canal works?* Their lives have texture because their lives have friction. His rolled off the table without a mark on it.

But it wasn't the polish that stopped my pen. Polish I could attribute to a dull man with a good memory. It was the hands.

Ink sits differently on different trades. A tally clerk wears it on the side of the writing hand, a smudge riding the middle finger like a ring. An accountant carries it higher, on the cuff. But this man's ink was *under*—down in the creases, worked into the knuckle-folds, the staining of hands that press and pull at type or at paper for hours, hands that handle ink as a material rather than an accident. I have catalogued the hands of this province for three months. I know what a laundress's knuckles look like, what a rope-maker's palms look like, what a printer's fingertips look like when he thinks he has scrubbed clean for the census.

He was no more a grain clerk than I am.

I wrote *Corrow* in the register and I did not write down the rest, because the rest was not yet evidence—it was instinct, and instinct is a currency my superiors stopped accepting some time ago. Commissioner Hase wants the register complete and the town quiet. He told me so in October, in the phrase I have come to think of as the empire's whole philosophy distilled: *"Vane, we are counting sheep, not wolves. Give me numbers."* He believes accuracy is a virtue of the clerical class, like punctuality, and should stay in its drawer.

But here is what Hase has never understood, what none of them understand: order is not the absence of questions. Order is questions answered honestly. If I record a false grain clerk as a grain clerk, then my register is a fiction, and an empire built on fictions is a fire waiting for a spark. I did not serve the old republic's chaos for six years to build its successor out of polite lies. Whatever this man is—smuggler, spy, printer's runner, nothing worse than a deserter playing at commerce—he belongs in the record *as what he is*. Truth first. Judgment after. That is the whole of my creed, and I have paid for it twice, and I expect to pay for it again.

So I did what I always do with a discrepancy. I gave it a page.

I keep a second book. Not official—unofficial in the way that a knife carried in a boot is unofficial. In it I enter the small things that don't fit, cross-referenced and dated, because discrepancies are like foxes: one sighting means nothing, three sightings mean a den. *Grain clerk, Hale, M.—hands of a pressman or binder. Answers too fluent. Mother's maiden name given with unsolicited detail (miller connection)—rehearsed generosity? Corrow & Sons, Vellport: verify account exists; verify miller link.* And beneath, because I am honest even with myself about my own pleasures, I wrote the sentence I probably shouldn't have:

*A man this careful is carrying something worth the care.*

I finished the morning's arrivals—a drover whose answers matched his grain receipts to the bushel, bless him; the widow with the looms, whose crate I intend to inspect by Thursday—and at midday I sent a runner to the granary with a request for Corrow & Sons' correspondence file. If the account exists, the file exists. If the file exists, a clerk named Hale will appear in it, in his own handwriting, and handwriting is a confession no tongue can retract.

And if the file comes back thin, or late, or smelling of excuse—

Well. Then the fox has a den, and I have time, and Ashmoor is a small town. Small towns hold their strangers the way a sieve holds water: briefly, and visibly. Let him settle in. Let him believe the mask held. Masks always believe that, right up until the moment someone asks to see the face beneath—and I had already seen, under the gatehouse arch, the half-second flicker in his eyes when I asked after his mother.

People think a census taker counts the living. We count the hidden. Every register is a net, and every net catches things the fisherman never stocked the river with.

I blotted the page, squared my pens, and called for the next name.

Somewhere behind me in the town, the grain clerk was walking away with my stamp in his pocket, believing himself invisible.

I let him believe it. The best discrepancies ripen in the dark.
