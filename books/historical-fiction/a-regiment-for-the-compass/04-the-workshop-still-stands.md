## 4. The Workshop Still Stands

I spent the day after the census doing what grain clerks do, or what I imagined they did, since I had never been one and had no intention of failing at it now. I walked Ashmoor with a ledger under my arm and a pencil behind my ear like an ornament I had earned through years of honest drudgery, and I counted things. Sacks at the granary quay. Barrels outside the cooper's yard. The number of steps between the chandler's shop on Tallow Row and the corner where the garrison sentry stood his post with his collar turned up against the river wind.

Counting is a courier's second discipline. The first is forgetting. But counting keeps the forgetting honest — you cannot claim to have seen nothing if you have not first looked at everything and agreed, silently, to call it nothing.

Ashmoor was easy to count because it had already been counted by someone else. Every surface that could hold paste held a proclamation: the new census rolls, the curfew hours, the schedule of grain allotments, the rewards for reporting unregistered presses — *unregistered presses*, as though a press were a wolf that might be licensed. The imperial eagle stared down from every wall in ink so fresh it still shone when the light crossed it, printed over the ghost-edges of older notices beneath, republican notices, their corners showing like bones through thin soil. In one alley off the fish market I found a scrap where the layers had peeled apart, and for a moment three different governments looked out at me from the same wall, none of them entirely dead.

The town wore its occupation the way an old coat wears its patches: without comment, without surprise. Ration lines formed at dawn along the chandlery side of the square, quiet people with quiet hands, each of them holding the little stamped cards Odile Vane's office had issued. They did not talk much. When they talked, they talked about weather, about the price of tallow, about a cousin in Marrow Ford. Nobody talked about the war, which had ended eight months ago, or the republic, which everyone agreed had never really existed except in the minds of agitators.

That was the thing I kept circling back to as I made my rounds of nothing in particular. It wasn't resistance I saw in Ashmoor. It wasn't grief either, not openly. It was something more finished than either — a town that had already concluded its own story and filed it away. A last chapter. I had the unpleasant sense of walking through a book whose ending had been typeset before I arrived, and of being a character who had wandered in from some other volume entirely.

*History happens elsewhere,* my mother used to say, folding her hands on the table as if pressing something flat. *It happens to loud people in capital cities. We are not loud people.* She had died believing it, comfortably, in a house that was never searched. I had built a whole life inside that sentence. I intended to keep living in it for another two days, deliver a sealed case, and leave before the ink dried on anything.

* * *

The chalk sign was on the third pass.

You learn the signs young in the courier service — not from any book, because there is no book, but from the old hands who teach you to see without seeming to look. A chalk mark is a language of small accidents: a double stroke here, a hook there, always placed where a child might have drawn it, always deniable. The mark on the downspout beside the chandler's door on Tallow Row was a courier's *open* — two short strokes crossed by a longer one, low enough to be mistaken for a boy's game, high enough that no boy would have bothered.

I had been past it twice already that morning and seen nothing, which meant it hadn't been there. On the third pass, just after noon, it was there. Someone had walked thirty paces behind me all morning, then, deciding I was worth the risk of a mark, had stepped into the chandler's doorway long enough to draw it and gone on selling candles to the empire.

The protocol was simple and had not changed in the eleven years since the couriers' college in Vell City burned its own records rather than surrender them. You did not knock. You came back after dark, you carried nothing in your hands, and you stood in the shadow of the door and said the words that meant *I am expected and I am alone*: a question about candle prices that no real customer would ask, because real customers bargained and couriers only confirmed.

So I waited for dark. I ate a supper of barley soup at a tavern near the bridge where the garrison officers drank at their own table and nobody looked at anybody. I went back to my rented room above the rope-walk and sat with the lamp unlit, holding the document case against my chest the way you'd hold a sleeping child, feeling its edges through my shirt. It was heavier than paper should be. It had been heavy for nine days, since the man in the grey coat handed it to me at the barge landing in Corren's Reach and said the words every courier hears at every handoff, the words that are supposed to make the weight bearable:

*Carry it. Never read it. What you don't know, you can't give up.*

I had repeated that oath at seventeen with my hand on a press-plate older than the republic, and I had kept it through four wars and two changes of government, through checkpoints and searches and one memorable night in a ditch outside Hallowmere while soldiers walked the road above me discussing, at length, the proper way to gut a suspected smuggler. Carry, never read. It had never cost me sleep before Ashmoor.

But Ashmoor had a census taker with quick eyes and a question about mothers' maiden names, and the case under my shirt seemed to grow heavier by the hour, as though whatever slept inside it were turning over.

At half past ten, when the patrol passed Tallow Row on its circuit — I had timed them; they took eleven minutes to come round again — I stepped out of the rope-walk's shadow and crossed to the chandler's door, and stood in the smell of tallow and smoke, and asked the darkness the price of a dozen dipped candles.

The darkness answered with the counter-sign, voice low and dry as paper dust: *"Twelve, and no fewer."* A bolt drew back. "In. Quickly."

* * *

Inside was not a chandler's shop. Or rather, it was, at the front — racks of dipped tapers, a counter, a ledger open under a shuttered lamp — but the air told the truth before anything else did. Underneath the tallow was another smell entirely, one I knew from childhood better than I knew my mother's voice: oil and solvent, molten metal, and the particular warm, sour breath of a steam press working. My father had smelled like that. For one disorienting moment I was seven years old again, standing in the doorway of a print shop that no longer existed, waiting to be noticed.

A hand closed on my sleeve and drew me through a curtain, down a short passage, and into a room where the floor shook.

Two presses ran. Not the little jobbing platen presses a country town might use for bills of sale and funeral notices — these were cylinder presses, iron-framed, tall as a man, salvaged or smuggled or both, and they were running flat-out in a cellar that had clearly been dug deeper than any cellar needed to go. Lamps hooded with tin threw light only where it was wanted: on the beds, on the stacks of damp sheets coming off the rollers, on the hands of six or seven people who moved among the machines with the wordless economy of a crew that has done this many nights and expects to do it many more. Nobody looked up. Looking up is a luxury for people whose work isn't secret.

And the work was not pamphlets. I could see that from the doorway. Pamphlets run eight pages, sixteen at most, set loose and fast. These sheets were large folio, printed close and dense, and they came off the press in ordered sequence — someone at the delivery board was collating them into signatures with the care of a priest laying out relics. Whatever this was, it had chapters. It had a shape. It had been planned the way cathedrals are planned, by people who did not expect to see it finished.

"Master Brann," said the man who had let me in, to the figure bent over the nearest press, "the courier."

Halvard Brann straightened slowly, the way old men do when they've decided slowness costs them less than speed. He was sixty or thereabout, big through the shoulders in the way of men who have worked presses all their lives, with forearms scarred by hot metal and a face that seemed assembled for the purpose of giving away nothing. His eyes went over me once, thoroughly, the way Odile Vane's had gone over me at the gate — and I felt the same cold slide of being *seen*, catalogued, filed. Printers and census takers, it turns out, share a temperament.

"You're late," he said.

"The river froze at Wether Bend. Three days." It was true; I offered truth the way a duelist offers his weakest hand first.

"Frozen rivers." He said it as though the weather were part of a conspiracy he'd long ago stopped taking personally. "The codes?"

Not *the documents*. Not *your papers*. The codes. I heard the word land wrong in my ear, like a step missing the bottom stair, and before I could ask why he'd chosen it, discipline closed over the question. A courier asks nothing. A courier delivers.

I brought the case out from under my shirt. Nine days of carrying had taught my body its shape; letting go of it felt like setting down a bone I'd grown. I put it on the table between the presses, in the lamplight, and stepped back.

"From the archivist at Corren's Reach," I said. "Sealed. Unread. The seal is intact — you'll want to check the wax yourself. There's a countersign phrase, but I'm instructed to speak it only to the person named on the outer wrapper."

Brann turned the case over once, read the wrapper, and grunted. "That's me." He broke the wax with his thumbnail, efficient, almost indifferent, and lifted out — not papers. A bundle of slender brass rods, each no longer than a finger, each engraved along its length with fine characters I did not try to read. He counted them by touch, lips moving, then nodded once.

"Good. That's good." He wrapped them back in their cloth and tucked the bundle inside his coat with a tenderness he had shown nothing else in the room. Then he looked at me, and something in his face shifted — a flicker of curiosity, or appraisal, or something warmer that I chose not to name. "You'll stay in Ashmoor tonight?"

"I'm instructed to confirm delivery and return."

"To whom?"

"That's not mine to say."

"No," he said softly. "It wouldn't be." He glanced at the presses, at the collator laying out signatures, at the whole sweating, lamp-hooded industry of the room, and then back at me. "You don't know what you've carried."

"I know I've carried it," I said. "That's all I'm paid to know."

Something like a smile touched the corner of his mouth and died there. "Paid." He weighed the word. "Yes. Well. You've done your part, clerk—" my false name, spoken correctly, "—and the road back will be no easier than the road here. Rest tonight. Go tomorrow. And Kestrel—" 

My blood stopped. Not my false name. My true one, spoken quietly, in a room full of strangers and thundering iron, as easily as if he'd read it off the inside of my skull.

"—when you cross the river," Brann went on, as if he hadn't just reached into my chest and turned a key, "keep to the towpath. The ferry master at Wether Bend has been asking travelers questions the ferry master shouldn't know to ask."

He turned back to his press before I could answer, before I could even decide whether there was an answer that wasn't itself a confession. The conversation, apparently, was over. One of the crew — a broad woman with ink to her elbows — jerked her chin toward the passage, and I understood I was being dismissed, escorted out, returned to the ordinary world like a book reshelved.

* * *

Outside, the cold hit like clean water. The patrol was due in four minutes; I counted them off standing in the chandler's doorway, breathing tallow smoke, trying to understand what had just happened.

Delivery made. Oath kept. By every measure I had lived by for eleven years, the night was a success, and relief should have come flooding in behind it like water into a lock — the sweet, empty lightness of a task discharged, a weight set down, a name crossed off a list I would never see. I waited for it. I stood in the doorway waiting for it the way you wait for a train you can hear approaching but never feel arrive.

It didn't come. What came instead, creeping up through the relief like frost through a windowpane, was suspicion. Cold. Precise. Growing outward from a dozen small cracks.

The codes. He had said *codes* before I said anything at all.

Brass rods, engraved, counted by touch — not documents but keys. Keys to what? No one seals decryption rods in wax and pays a courier nine days' dangerous travel unless there is a vault somewhere, and no one builds a vault unless there is something in it worth more than the courier's life and the printer's and everyone else's in that cellar besides.

Four hundred pounds of iron turning below a candle shop in a conquered town, printing folio volumes by night — volumes with chapters, volumes planned like cathedrals — and now unlocked. I had done that. I had carried the keys through three checkpoints and a frozen bend of river with the tender, scrupulous innocence of a man delivering medicine, and I had not read a single character, and my oath was spotless, and something enormous had just been switched on because I delivered it, and I did not know what.

*What you don't know, you can't give up.* The oath ran both ways, I realized, standing in the cold with the patrol's lanterns swinging into view at the corner of Tallow Row. It protected me from them. But it also meant that everything I had just enabled — whatever it was — had happened behind glass, on the far side of my own life, and I had no way of knowing whether I had delivered a vaccine or a plague.

The lanterns passed. I slipped away up the rope-walk with my ledger under my arm, a grain clerk going home to bed, invisible, blameless, sworn.

Halfway there I stopped. Because there was one crack in the frost too wide to ignore, one fact that refused to freeze over no matter how I smoothed the rest:

Halvard Brann had called me *Kestrel*. Not the name on my papers. The name my mother gave me, the name that appeared on no register in any province, the name I had buried eleven years ago along with my father.

Nobody had told him that name. Which meant somebody had. Which meant I had not walked into Ashmoor by chance at all.

I had been summoned.
