## 5. What the Codes Unlock

The chalk mark on the chandler's door had changed.

I noticed it the moment I came down Tallow Lane in the grey of morning, breath smoking, hands buried in my sleeves where the ink stains couldn't be seen. Yesterday the sign had been a simple hook—a courier's shorthand for *the way is open*. Today there was a second stroke beneath it, a short diagonal like a falling leaf. I stood in front of the chandler's window pretending to study his candles, his bundles of rushlights, his barrels of tallow gone amber with age, and read the message three times before my heart agreed to slow down.

*Come after dark. Bring nothing you cannot lose.*

A fine instruction for a man who was, at that moment, nothing but things that could be lost: a false name, a false trade, a satchel of codes sewn into the lining of a grain clerk's coat. I bought two candles to justify my loitering—tallow ones, cheap, the wicks crooked—and walked back out into Ashmoor as though I were exactly what I claimed to be. A clerk. A nobody. A man whose whole life fit inside a ledger and could be balanced by supper.

Behind me, through the frost-furred glass, an apprentice was trimming wicks. He didn't look up. But I had the feeling, walking away, of eyes between my shoulder blades—the same feeling I'd carried since Odile Vane asked her one question too many at the gate. *Your mother's maiden name.* I had lied. The lie had held. And still the town felt different now, the way a house feels different after someone has walked through it touching all the walls.

* * *

Halvard Brann opened the workshop door himself when I knocked the third time—the pattern he'd told me, three slow, one fast, like a man knocking on his own memory. He looked older by lamplight than he had by daylight, or perhaps it was simply that I was looking more closely now, because I already knew I'd been lied to about what this place was.

"You came," he said. Not surprised. That alone should have warned me.

"You left me a sign."

"I left you a choice." He stepped aside to let me in. "That's rarer."

The workshop swallowed sound. The presses were running—I'd heard them from the lane, a rhythmic iron breathing, in-and-out, in-and-out—but inside, the noise became something you moved through rather than heard, a weather of its own. Two apprentices worked the big press near the far wall, feeding sheets and pulling them again, their faces orange in the light of shielded lamps. The air was thick with the smell I had come to know too well for a grain clerk: linseed oil, ink, damp paper, hot metal. Somewhere a kettle muttered on a brazier.

"Where's Sela?" I asked.

"Composing." He nodded toward the corner where a girl sat hunched over a case of type, her fingers moving over the little lead letters like a blind woman reading a face. She glanced up when Halvard said my name—or didn't say it; he said *our courier*—and then went back to her work. She'd been friendly enough yesterday, quick-smiling, ink to the elbows. Tonight she seemed to be somewhere else entirely, someplace deeper in the work.

Halvard led me past the presses to a small room at the back, walled off by shelves of paper stock so heavy the boards bowed under them. A lamp burned on a table there, and on the table lay the thing I had carried four hundred miles without once understanding.

It was not the codes themselves. Those I had delivered three nights ago—a sheaf of thin brass-edged cards, each punched with holes in patterns I'd assumed were some quartermaster's inventory system, because assuming was my profession. No. On the table lay what the codes had opened: stacks of printed pages, fresh from the press, still smelling of oil, weighted at the corners with type cases so they wouldn't drift in the draft.

"Read it," Halvard said.

"No." The word came out faster than I meant it to, harder. I put my hands behind my back like a schoolboy. "Master Brann, I delivered what I was sent to deliver. My part is finished. If you need another run made, if you need anything carried out of here, I'll do it. But I don't read."

He studied me for a long moment. He had that kind of face, wide and patient, a face built for waiting out other people's convictions. Then he turned the top sheet toward me anyway, gently, the way you'd turn a wounded bird.

"You've been carrying these names for eleven days," he said. "Against your heart, against your spine. You just never knew they were names."

I looked despite myself. I will always have looked despite myself, and I think that's what he counted on, and I hate him a little for it even now.

*Testimony of Maret Ostrev, weaver, taken at Vellport, ninth month of the Interregnum.* And beneath the heading, in the plain unlovely type of a working press: *They came for my brother on the feast of Saint Corvin. He had said nothing. He had written nothing. They had a list, and his name was on it, and no one would tell us who kept the list.*

I stepped back as if the page had heat.

"What is this," I said, and it wasn't a question, but Halvard answered it anyway, in the low level voice of a man who has rehearsed this speech to every mirror in his soul.

"The sealed archives of the Republic of Vell. Arrest registers. Tribunal minutes. Prison manifests from the Holding Houses. The ledgers of the Disposition Office—which was the office that decided which prisoners came home and which did not." His hand rested flat on the stack, possessive, tender. "When the government fell, certain clerks sealed everything rather than let the empire burn it outright. Sealed archives, cipher-locked, keys scattered to couriers across three provinces on the theory that no single carrier could be tortured into giving up the whole. Your cards are keys. Fourteen sets arrived here over the winter. Yours were the last."

"And the book?"

"The book is the record itself. Testimony, gathered for two years. Names—every name we could recover, of everyone taken, everyone tried, everyone who went into a Holding House and never came out." He said it plainly, and the presses breathed behind the paper shelves, in and out, in and out, like something sleeping. "Four hundred copies. Bound. Ready to move within the week."

"They'll burn it."

"They'll try."

"They'll burn *you*, Master Brann. This shop. Your daughter." My voice cracked on the last word and I hated that most of all. "You're printing your own death warrant in an edition of four hundred."

"We're printing theirs first," he said quietly. "Empires live on the belief that no one wrote it down. Take that away and every gallows afterward costs them double."

I turned away from him. I pressed my palms flat against the cool paper stocks and breathed the smell of pulp and size, and I tried to find in myself the person who had boarded the barge at Corven's Reach eleven days ago—that careful, bland, well-practiced nonentity who believed, truly believed, that history was a country other people lived in, that a courier's virtue lay in being furniture while empires rearranged themselves around him.

That person was furious. That person wanted to seize the satchel straps off the hook by the door and walk out into the snow and keep walking until Ashmoor was a rumor behind him.

That person was also terrified, and the fury was mostly the fear wearing a coat.

"You used me," I said finally. "Someone chose me. Chose a Dunmore for this run, of all the couriers in the network. You knew what these codes were. You knew what they'd unlock. And you let me carry them believing—" I gestured at nothing, at everything. "Believing they were grain quotas. Believing the oath was enough."

"The oath *is* enough," Halvard said. "Carry, never read. You carried. You haven't read. You're standing there with your back turned and your hands shaking, and you still haven't read." He came around the table slowly, an old man's movement, joints announcing themselves. "Kestrel. Look at me."

I didn't.

"Then listen. I won't pretend I didn't hope you'd stay. Any fool printing sedition hopes his courier has a spine. But I swear to you on my press, on my daughter's head, that I did not choose you. The network assigned you. If someone above me chose a Dunmore deliberately, that knowledge isn't mine—and I'd very much like to know why you flinched just now, because you did. At your own name. As if it were also on a list."

The lamp hissed. Behind us the press exhaled its sheet and drew its next.

"My father's name was Dunmore," I said, because silence would have been worse. "He was arrested when I was nine. My mother told me he'd been a tax assessor who fell afoul of a corrupt magistrate. She made me promise never to ask questions. She said questions were how people disappeared. She built our whole life on that—neutrality like a coat you wore indoors and out." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "She died believing it. Never asked a single question. And here I am, carrying keys to a room full of answers I've spent twenty years not wanting."

Halvard said nothing for a long time. When he spoke, his voice had gentled into something that was almost kind, and kindness, from him, felt more dangerous than threat.

"I can't unmake what you carried," he said. "And I won't apologize that it turned out to matter. But the door is behind you, courier, and it opens as easily outward as inward. Walk out tonight and by morning you'll be a grain clerk again, and none of this will ever have touched you—except that you'll know. That's the price either way. You know now. There's no cipher for forgetting."

I thought about walking out. I want that recorded honestly, wherever this ends up: I thought about it with my hand actually on the door, feeling the cold of the metal through my glove, composing the report I'd send to the network—*delivery complete, recipient compromised, recommend no further contact*—and imagining how clean the words would feel, how neatly they'd seal the whole terrible business shut again.

Then Sela appeared in the gap between the paper shelves. She must have heard everything; the walls were paper, the walls were always paper. She held a single printed sheet in both ink-blackened hands, and she didn't offer it to me, exactly. She just stood there holding it, the way you stand holding a candle in a dark house, letting the light do the persuading.

"It's the list of the disappeared from the Vellport Holding House," she said. "First installment. Papa says I set it truest because I go slowest." Her voice was steady, but her eyes weren't. "There are six hundred and forty names on this sheet alone."

Six hundred and forty names on one sheet. And the stacks behind her went back farther than the lamplight reached.

My father had been taken from a river town smaller than Ashmoor, on a night much like this one. Somewhere in those stacks—if Halvard's archive was half what he claimed—it was possible that a line existed with his name in it. Possible that the reason I'd been chosen, the reason a Dunmore had been routed to this particular door, was not accident at all but arithmetic: the network sending the archive to the man whose family was inside it.

I took the sheet from Sela's hands. I read the first column. Then the second. I read until the letters swam, and among all those strangers' names I found none I knew, and somehow that was worse—because it meant the list went on past the edge of everything I could personally grieve, and grief without a face has nowhere to put itself down.

"I nearly walked out," I told Halvard. My voice sounded strange to me, scraped hollow. "Understand that. Understand I may still."

"I understand," he said. "Anger keeps better than despair. I'll take your anger."

Outside, snow had begun again, fat slow flakes turning in the lamplight of Tallow Lane. I pulled my collar up and became, step by step, a grain clerk walking home through an ordinary occupied evening—past the ration queue, past the proclamation boards where imperial decrees were pasted over older notices like skin grafts over old scars, past the census office with its windows lit and its lamp burning late.

In that lit window, bent over her ledger, sat Odile Vane.

She did not look up. She did not need to. I felt her the way the press felt its next sheet—something incoming, patient, inevitable—and I walked on through the snow carrying, for the first time in my life, something heavier than any satchel: comprehension.

The oath said *carry, never read.*

No one had ever told me what to do once I'd read anyway.
