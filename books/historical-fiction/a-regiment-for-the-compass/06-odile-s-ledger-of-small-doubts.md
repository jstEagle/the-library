## 6. Odile's Ledger of Small Doubts

Odile Vane kept her ledger the way other women kept prayer books: close, constant, and with a certain private tenderness.

It was not an official document. The official document—the town register of Ashmoor, population nine hundred and forty-one souls by her current count—lived in a folio bound in imperial blue, stamped with the double-headed eagle of the Vell occupation, and was meant to be boring. Boringness was its purpose. A census that excited no one was a census that had done its work. Every name entered in a fair hand, every trade and age and household noted without comment or flourish, so that when some clerk in the provincial capital thumbed through it years hence he would find nothing but the smooth, unbroken surface of a town that had accepted its new masters.

The ledger was another matter. In it Odile recorded everything the fair copy left out. Not rumors—she had no patience for rumor—but *discrepancies*. Small frictions between what people said and what the paper said. A widow who claimed to live alone but bought flour in quantities for four. A fisherman whose boat was registered at Larchen Quay but whose nets smelled of a different river entirely. A cooper whose son appeared on no church roll yet answered, when she called his household's names aloud at the door, to a name spoken before she finished it.

She wrote these things down in her small, upright script, dated them, cross-referenced them, and waited. Most discrepancies resolved themselves. People were careless, not criminal; they forgot birthdays, misspoke about harvests, miscounted their own children when nervous. Nerves explained nearly everything, and Odile made a professional discipline of distinguishing nerves from lies. It could be done. Lies had a texture. They arrived too quickly or too slowly. They were too complete. A truthful answer was like a rough-cut board—splintered at the edges, slightly wrong in its dimensions, human. A lie was planed smooth on all sides, sanded, finished, presented for approval.

The grain clerk's answers had been planed smooth on all sides.

* * *

She had interviewed thirty-one residents of Ashmoor since the census wagon rolled through the south gate eleven days ago, and she thought of them now in categories the empire would not have recognized: the weepers, the blusterers, the ones who fed her. Ashmoor, being a river town recently conquered, had produced more weepers than usual—old women who cried into their aprons not, she suspected, for the Republic of Vell, which had been distant and tax-hungry even when it existed, but for some smaller country of habit that had died with it. The blusterers were mostly men who had owned things. The feeders were the dangerous ones: bakers who pressed warm rolls on her, innkeepers who would not let her leave without cider, as if kindness were a form of weather she could be made to stand in until her questions dissolved.

She recorded all of it. She recorded that Marta Kessel, widow, age sixty-one, had said her late husband had been "no politics" three times in one interview, which was either truth or rehearsal. She recorded that the ferryman Bren had given the correct number of children but hesitated half a heartbeat over the youngest's birthday, as though the date had been changed in some ledger of his own. She recorded that the chandler Oskar Pell, whose shop backed onto the tannery lane, had answered every question about his household with cheerful completeness and never once asked why the empire wanted to know—which was itself worth a line, because everyone else asked, even if only with their eyes.

And she recorded, in an entry she had copied out twice because the first copying displeased her:

*Day 4. Grain clerk, name given as Tam Ressler, twenty-six, employed by the Marnholt granary, arrived by grain barge from upriver. Papers in order. Answers fluent, complete, unhurried. States mother's maiden name as Corvane. No Corvane appears in the parish rolls of any district within two days' ride. When questioned casually regarding his route, gave distances that do not reconcile with the barge manifests. Notes: hands ink-stained along the right forefinger and thumb. Clerk's ink is black; this stain is iron-gall brown, old, worked deep into the whorls. Granary clerks use pencil.*

She had noticed the hands immediately. That was the trouble with interviewing people—you saw them. The capital had designed the census forms, designed the questions, designed the courteous gray uniform and the little brass badge of office, and had apparently forgotten that the whole apparatus depended on a woman looking at faces for ten hours a day, and that faces told you things no form asked. Odile had tried, once, early in her career, to write to the provincial bureau suggesting that census takers be trained in observation rather than procedure. The reply had come back in six weeks, signed by a deputy undersecretary, thanking her for her dedication and reminding her that standardized methodology ensured comparable results across districts.

Comparable results. She had put the letter in the back of her ledger, where she kept things that proved the empire did not actually want what it said it wanted.

* * *

Because here was the thing Odile Vane believed, and believed stubbornly, in the face of six years of bureaucratic indifference: order required truth. Not virtue—she had no illusions about virtue, hers or anyone's—but truth, plain and accurate, the way a bridge required sound stone regardless of whose banner flew from the tower. An empire that governed by fiction would eventually walk off the edge of its own map. The Republic of Vell had done precisely that; she had watched it happen from inside a customs office in Drenmark, watching requisition orders describe granaries that were empty and garrisons that existed only on paper, everyone lying politely upward until the whole structure stood on nothing and came down in a season. The republic had not been defeated. It had been *miscounted*.

She would not be party to a second miscounting. If Ashmoor contained nine hundred and forty-one souls, her register would say so. If one of those souls was lying about his mother's maiden name, then the record would bend toward that truth eventually, because Odile would sit across from him again and again, patient as water, until the planed-smooth answers splintered.

Her superiors wanted none of this. She had learned within her first week in Ashmoor what the census was actually for—not knowledge but *coverage*, a great gray blanket thrown over the province so the capital could report the province pacified. Captain Reiss of the garrison had told her as much over a supper she hadn't wanted, in the tone of a man explaining fire to a child. "You count heads, Vane. You don't count opinions." And when she'd pointed out, mildly, that opinions were where the fires started, he had laughed and refilled her glass and said, "Then you'll have a busy winter."

He wasn't wrong. But he had misunderstood her completely, and she had decided not to correct him, because a census taker whom the garrison found harmless was a census taker free to work.

So she worked. Mornings she sat in the rented front room of the Widow Anselm's house on Tanner Lane with the good light, receiving townsfolk summoned by printed notice, asking her questions in her gentle, unvarying order. Afternoons she walked—the church rolls at Saint Ferren's, the granary ledgers by permission of Captain Reiss, the harbor manifests, the burial register with its terrible recent pages. Evenings she reconciled. The ledger grew fat.

And the grain clerk entry grew annotations.

*Corvane. Checked parish rolls: Ashmoor, Larchen, Drenmark proper, the fen chapels. Nothing.* Then, a week later: *Marnholt granary confirms employment—letter arrived suspiciously fast for winter roads. Fast letters are paid letters.* Then: *Saw subject Tuesday at market. Bought turnips, salt, a candle. Counted change correctly and quickly—clerk's hands. But stood in the square four minutes after finishing his purchases, doing nothing, facing the chandler's lane. Clerks have somewhere to be.*

She did not write down what she suspected, because suspicion written down became evidence, and evidence demanded action, and action was the garrison's business, and the garrison's business was burning things. She wrote observations. Let the pattern assemble itself. Patterns assembled honestly could still be disassembled if they proved false; patterns assembled from fear could not.

* * *

On the twelfth day she interviewed the chandler himself.

Oskar Pell came to the Widow Anselm's front room rubbing his palms on his apron, leaving pale streaks of wax dust, and sat where she pointed like a well-trained dog, and answered everything. Wife: yes, Danna, born Hesk. Children: two, the girl married to a cooper in Larchen, the boy apprenticed to him here, sixteen, called Wim. Trade: candles, tallow and beeswax both, sold wholesale to the garrison since the change of flags—"and glad of the custom," he added, and then went red, hearing how it sounded.

"There's no shame in selling candles to whoever pays," Odile said, and meant it. "Loyalty is a luxury. Wax is a necessity."

He blinked at her as though she'd spoken a foreign language kindly. "That's... yes, madam. That's it exactly. Only most folk won't say it."

"Most folk think I'm here to punish them."

"Aren't you?"

"I'm here to count them." She turned a page. "Your apprentice. Wim. He was born in Ashmoor?"

"Yes, madam. Winter of—"

"The roll says the ninth of Frostmonth."

"That's right."

"And he attends Saint Ferren's?"

"When there's preaching. Father Aldous doesn't preach much lately. His voice." Pell touched his own throat apologetically.

Odile nodded and wrote something that was not what she was thinking. What she was thinking was that Oskar Pell had answered nineteen questions without one splinter, without one rough edge, and that no living man was that honest about his own life—and then she caught herself, and smiled inwardly at her own trap. This was the other failure mode, the one she guarded against hardest: seeing lies everywhere, becoming a woman for whom smoothness itself was damning. The world was full of dull, truthful people whose lives simply had no edges. She checked. She checked everything against the rolls and receipts, and Pell's answers matched the rolls and receipts the way a key matches its lock, and she closed the interview satisfied and slightly ashamed.

But as he stood to go, buttoning his coat with fingers gone clumsy with relief, she asked the last question, the one she asked everyone, the one that was not on the imperial form at all and never would be.

"Is there anything in this town I ought to see?"

Pell stopped buttoning.

"It's a large question," Odile said pleasantly. "People usually answer with a bridge or a chapel."

"I—" He looked at the window, at the gray light coming off the river. "Nothing, madam. Quiet town. Quieter than it used to be."

"Than when?"

"Than ever, really." He finished the buttons. "Good day to you."

She wrote afterward: *Pell, O. Interview clean against all records. Final question produced 6-second pause, deflection via generality ("quiet town"). Note: pause preceded by glance toward window, north-facing. Chandler's shop sits on Tanner Lane; north-facing window overlooks tannery yard and the lane behind the chandlery.* She underlined the last clause once, lightly, and hated herself a little for the pleasure the underlining gave her.

* * *

That evening the snow came properly, the first real fall of the winter, sealing the streets in a hush that made Ashmoor feel less conquered than merely asleep. Odile sat at her borrowed desk with the shutters cracked for light and reconciled the day's entries, dipping her pen, blotting, dipping again. Downstairs the Widow Anselm clattered pans and hummed something old in a minor key—a republican hymn, probably, worn down by decades into a tune a body could hum without knowing it. Odile let it run under her thoughts like groundwater.

She reviewed the grain clerk file entire. Eleven annotations now. She laid them out in her mind like cards: the maiden name that existed nowhere; the distances that didn't reconcile; the fast letter from Marnholt; the iron-gall stains; the four minutes facing a north-facing lane; today's addition, fresh from the harbor office—that the grain barges from upriver had carried, per the manifest, forty sacks of wheat to Ashmoor, and that the granary's intake book recorded thirty-eight received, and that no clerk had flagged the difference because the difference was two sacks and nobody counted sacks in wartime except women like her.

Two sacks. It might be pilferage. Pilferage was the river's oldest industry. But it might be payment, or cover, or ballast for something hidden, and she would know which soon enough, because tomorrow she had scheduled the follow-up interviews—the second pass, the one where the questions circled back around wearing different clothes, and liars, who had rehearsed their first answers, discovered they had not rehearsed these.

She had put the grain clerk down for tomorrow, midmorning. Third interview. There would be a soldier present this time—Captain Reiss had insisted after the incident in Drenmark, the thing that had gotten her last posting transferred, though the paperwork called it a promotion—and the soldier's presence changed interviews, frightened people into smoother lies, but it couldn't be helped. Rules were rules, and besides, she wanted witnesses. Whatever happened with Tam Ressler, she wanted it *recorded*, because she had begun to suspect—had been suspecting for days, in the wordless way of a pattern assembling behind her eyes—that the man was not a grain clerk at all, and not quite a smuggler either, and that whatever he actually was sat at the center of a small knot of anomalies that included a chandler who glanced north, an apprentice absent from evening services, and two sacks of wheat that had evaporated somewhere between the manifest and the granary floor.

Small doubts. That was all she dealt in. She was not an inquisitor; she had no interest in confession or punishment. She was a counter of true things in a province full of arranged ones, and if the empire chose to burn what her counting uncovered, then that sin would lie on the empire's account, weighed and itemized in its turn—by someone, someday, with a ledger of her own.

Odile blotted the final page, squared the entries, and blew out her lamp.

Outside, the snow went on falling over the tannery lane and the chandler's roof and the granary with its two phantom sacks, covering all of it equally, the way snow does, making the whole town for one night a single unwritten page.
