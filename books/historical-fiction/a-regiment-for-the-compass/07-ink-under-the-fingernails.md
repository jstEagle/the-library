## 7. Ink Under the Fingernails

The summons came by way of a boy with chapped hands and an imperial seal on heavy cream paper, delivered to the chandler's shop at midmorning while I was ostensibly counting tallow.

*The Office of Census and Registration requests the attendance of one Maren Ostrow, grain clerk, at the second bell past noon. Bring your ledger. This is a routine verification.*

Routine. The word was printed in the same tidy serif as everything else the empire produced, as if tidiness itself could be mistaken for innocence. I read it twice, felt the paper's weight—good stock, the kind that costs more than a laborer earns in a week—and understood that I had been weighed too, somewhere in Odile Vane's careful arithmetic, and found worth a second look.

I burned the summons in the chandler's stove and spent the morning being ordinary. I hauled a barrel of lye up from the cellar until my shoulders screamed. I argued about the price of lamp oil with a fishwife who smelled of brine and grievance. I let the chandler scold me for smudging his invoices, and apologized like a man who had been apologizing all his life. By the time the bells rang, my hands ached honestly, and there was honest grease under my nails to sit beside the ink stains I could never quite scrub away.

The census office had been a countinghouse before the war's end—a merchant's boast of glass windows and carved cornices, now hung with the double eagle of the occupation and the smell of wet plaster where they'd patched bullet holes rather than admit any had been made. A soldier stood inside the door, helmeted, thumbs hooked in his belt, occupying space the way a doorpost does. He did not look at me. Doorposts never do. That is their function.

"Maren Ostrow." Odile Vane sat behind a desk too large for her, in a room too small for the desk, and she said my false name the way she'd said it at the gate—lightly, tasting it, a woman sampling a wine she suspected of vinegar. "Thank you for coming promptly. Sit, please."

There was one chair, placed closer to her desk than comfort allowed. I sat. The soldier remained by the door, a fact I noted and declined to be frightened by, in the way you decline to think about drowning while crossing ice.

"Your papers were in order," she began, opening a folder I recognized as my own life, reduced to four pages. "Trade license, travel writ, ration card. All stamped correctly. All—" she paused, selecting her word with a jeweler's care, "—correct."

"Is there a problem with them?"

"No. That's what interests me." She smiled, and it was a good smile, warm at the edges, the smile of someone who liked people. That was the worst part of her, I was learning. She genuinely liked people. She collected them the way other women collected buttons. "Everything about you is correct, Herr Ostrow. Do you know how rare that is? In three weeks of processing this town, I have found errors in every single file. Misspelled villages. Birthdates that contradict church rolls. A man who claims to be sixty-two but has hands that never held anything heavier than a pen. Errors are how I know a place is real. You—" she tapped the folder once, softly, "—you are seamless."

"Grain clerks keep accurate records," I said. "It's the trade."

"It is the trade," she agreed pleasantly. "So let us talk trade. Your firm. Tell me about them."

This was the ground I had prepared, and I walked onto it gratefully. "Ostrow & Pell, out of Marren Ford. Family concern, thirty years. We broker between the river barges and the mill cooperatives—weighing, tallying, certifying quality. I'm the third clerk they've sent down this stretch since the harvest; the others rotated back north."

"Marren Ford." She wrote nothing. She didn't need to; I watched the town file itself away behind her eyes. "And your route here?"

"By barge to Wexley Landing, then cart. Eleven days total, weather delays."

"Wexley Landing," she repeated. "Lovely in autumn, I'm told. The bridge there—the old stone one, with the shrine on it—still standing?"

I had never seen Wexley Landing. I knew it existed because it appeared on shipping manifests, which listed tonnage, not shrines. Somewhere behind her mild gray eyes I felt a hook drop into dark water.

"I wouldn't know," I said. "We came in on the south road. Cart tracks, no view of the river."

"Mm." She turned a page she did not read. "You understand these questions seem idle. They aren't. A census isn't names on a list, Herr Ostrow. It's a picture. Every answer is a brushstroke. When a man's strokes don't match the painting—" she looked up, and her gaze was gentle and bottomless, "—the picture is wrong, and wrong pictures get repainted. Usually badly. Usually over people."

"I'll try to paint accurately," I said, and heard my own voice do something it had never done in eleven years of couriering: it warmed. It laughed slightly. I gave her the story of the fishwife and the price of oil, complete with her vocabulary, and Odile Vane laughed with me, a real laugh, and wrote *cooperative* in the margin of my life.

That was the trick, and I hated how well I knew it. Fear reads as concealment. Concealment reads as guilt. So you bury your lies in a landslide of truth—real prices, real smells, real grievances—and you give her one flaw to find herself, because a woman who hunts discrepancies must be fed discrepancies or she will go hunting through walls.

"The ledger," she said at last. "May I?"

I passed it across. She turned pages slowly, running one finger down columns of weights and prices I had fabricated with loving tedium over three days in a loft above the chandler's shop. Her finger stopped.

"This entry. Barley, forty sacks, certified on the ninth." She turned the ledger toward me. "But your travel writ says you entered Ashmoor on the eleventh. Who certified sacks two days before you arrived?"

There it was. The flaw I'd sewn into my own coat, right where her fingers would find it. I let my face fall—let confusion bloom, then embarrassment, the small hot shame of a clerk caught in sloppiness. "The barge master signed the certification at loading. I countersign on delivery—it should say so, I—" I leaned forward, squinting, then exhaled. "Ah. No. There. Countersigned the twelfth, see, the ink's different. I transposed the dates when I fair-copied it. I've been doing that all month; my firm will flay me."

She studied the page for a long moment. Then she took out a little bottle of blotting sand, dusted the entry, and nodded, satisfied—not by the explanation, but by the error. A real man, in her taxonomy, made mistakes. I had just become real.

"Sloppy," she said warmly, "and human. Thank you, Herr Ostrow. That's all I need."

I rose. At the door, the soldier opened it for me—or rather, moved aside, which amounted to the same courtesy. Behind me, Odile's voice followed, pleasant as tea:

"One more thing. Your hands."

I stopped. Turned.

"When we met at the gate, I noticed them. Ink under the fingernails. Grain men handle chalk and carbon paper, not ink pens—not daily, not deep like yours." She smiled her warm, collecting smile. "But clerks copy ledgers, don't they. Fair copies. In ink." She waved a hand, dismissing her own observation before it could become a question. "Forgive me. I see patterns everywhere. Occupational failing. Good day."

I walked out into the winter light with my heart hammering against my ribs like a bird against glass, and I did not exhale fully until I'd put three streets between myself and the countinghouse windows.

* * *

That night I sat in the loft with my hands spread in front of the candle, turning them over like documents.

Ink under the fingernails. Eleven years of scrubbing and it lived in me anyway, sunk into the quick, a stain that had stopped being evidence and become anatomy. I thought of Odile dusting my forged entry with blotting sand, satisfied, and felt the twin pleasures of my trade rise in me together: the thrill, bright and filthy, of having lied well to a woman built entirely out of noticing—and beneath it, cold and rising, the cost. Because every lie that works doesn't make you safer. It makes you more invested. Each successful deception is a brick laid into a wall, and one day you look around and realize you've walled yourself into the very fiction you built to survive.

She'd seen the ink. She'd named it. And then she'd folded it away, filed it under *clerk*, because I had taught her, in ninety minutes, what a grain clerk's hands looked like.

Or because she wanted me to believe I had.

I snuffed the candle and lay in the dark listening to the chandler's apprentice shift in his sleep below, and I told myself the mask held. I told myself she'd caught exactly the lie I'd fed her, and nothing more.

But patterns were her trade, and I had given her a beautiful pattern today: a man too correct, corrected by a small mistake, whose hands remembered ink.

Somewhere across the square, I imagined her lamp burning late, adding a line to some private ledger of small doubts.

And in the workshop below the chandler's storeroom, faint as a heartbeat through the floor, the press began its night shift—thump, pause, thump—setting into paper, one name at a time, the true history of us all.
