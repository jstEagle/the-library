## 8. The Compositor's Daughter

I found her by accident, which is how I found everything in Ashmoor that mattered.

It was the fourth evening after my interview with Odile Vane, and I had taken to walking the long way back to my lodging—a clerk's habit of lingering I had cultivated so carefully it had become real. The chandler's yard smelled of tallow and cold iron. Frost had come early that year, silvering the cobbles, and my breath hung around me like something I'd been caught carrying.

The workshop's back door stood open a hand's width, spilling a blade of candlelight across the frozen weeds. And from inside came a sound I couldn't place at first: a soft, rhythmic ticking, like rain on slate, like a clock made of small bones.

I should have kept walking. A courier who lingers is a courier who gets seen. But the oath was already fraying by then—I had delivered Halvard Brann his codes, watched the presses begin their night work, and spent three days telling myself that whatever they were printing was none of my business. The oath said carry, never read. It did not say never listen.

The ticking stopped.

"You're breathing too loud," said a voice from the doorway. "And you've been passing this door for four days. You always stop at the third cobblestone. The cracked one."

She was perhaps sixteen, thin as a composing stick, with ink to her wrists and eyes that looked at me slightly past where I stood—as if I were a shape she could hear better than see.

"I'm the grain clerk," I began.

"No," she said, not unkindly. "Grain clerks don't walk on their toes."

* * *

Her name was Sela, and she was the compositor's daughter in every sense that mattered. Her mother had died of lung fever when she was six; her father had raised her among the type cases until she knew them the way other children know their own hands. She had been born with eyes that dimmed a little more each winter—the miller's daughter said it, and the midwife before her—and so Halvard had taught her to set type by touch, letter by letter, in the dark, so that the day the light failed entirely would cost her nothing but the candles.

"Come in or go away," she said now, holding the door wider. "Standing in the frost makes you memorable, and you don't want to be memorable. I can tell that much about you."

Inside, the workshop held its breath. The great press stood silent under its canvas shroud, but the composing stone was lit by two candles and a shuttered lamp, and across it lay galleys of locked type—columns of metal sentences waiting like soldiers at attention.

Sela sat down at the case without looking for her chair. Her fingers went into the compartments the way a swimmer's go into water.

"What are you setting?" I asked.

"A page," she said. "One page of many." She drew out a piece of type, turned it, seated it in the stick. Tick. Tick. Tick. "You can look if you want. Papa says anyone who comes through that door has already decided to look. He just likes them to admit it first."

I didn't move. The oath sat in my chest like a swallowed stone. Carry, never read. Six words I had recited since I was twelve years old, since the night the woman with grey braids put my hand on a sealed satchel and made me swear it in a cellar that smelled of lamp oil and old paper. I have carried grain manifests, love letters, death warrants. I have carried things wrapped in oilcloth that clinked, and things that were only paper and weighed nothing and weighed everything. In nine years I had not once broken the seal. Not once read what I carried. That was the discipline that kept couriers alive: a man who doesn't know anything can't be made to know anything.

But I wasn't carrying now. I was standing in a warm room beside a girl who set type blind, and the pages were right there, and nobody had asked me to swear anything.

"Read it aloud," Sela said. "Papa always reads them aloud. He says type is meant for voices. Metal remembers better that way." She smiled faintly. "He's sentimental about metal."

I stepped closer. The candle guttered. I bent over the galley, and the mirror-world of type resolved itself, line by line, into words.

It was testimony. I understood that before I understood anything else—the cadence of it, the plainness. Not proclamation, not pamphlet-rage. A voice answering questions. *They came to the house on Vell Street on the ninth of March. My husband asked to see a warrant. There was no warrant. They took the ledger, and they took him, and in the spring the town office told me he had been transferred south. There is no south. I wrote to the office eleven times.*

A name followed. A date. A place of last sighting.

"That's Maret Osten," Sela said quietly, still setting. "Wool factor. His wife walks past our window every Sunday on the way to the shrine. She doesn't know yet that he'll be in a book."

I straightened up too fast. The room tilted.

"This is—" I started, and stopped, because every word available to me was wrong. This is sedition. This is suicide. This is not mine to see.

"It's the census," Sela said. "The real one. The one Odile Vane isn't writing."

* * *

I told myself I stayed for one more page. Then I told myself I stayed because the frost outside had thickened and a clerk loitering in a chandler's yard at night was worth questions I couldn't afford. Both stories were true the way cover stories are true—technically, uselessly.

Sela worked while I read. She had a rhythm to it, a music: the tick of type against the stick, the slide of the quoin key, the soft exhalation when a line locked true. She talked as she worked, in the low, unhurried way of people who live alone with their hands.

"Papa started collecting the testimonies two years ago," she said. "Before the occupation even finished settling in. People would come to order pamphlets—prayer cards, ration notices, a wedding announcement if they could pay—and some of them would stand there and say things. Just say them. Out loud, in a print shop, like saying it near a press made it more real. So Papa began writing it down. Then they began coming on purpose."

"How many?"

"Two hundred and eleven accounts. Four hundred names so far, and we're not done with the southern parishes." She seated another letter. "Some of them are just names. Some people wouldn't give more than a name—they thought even that was dangerous. Papa took it anyway. A name is an anchor, he says. Everything else can be rebuilt from an anchor."

"And the codes I brought—" I heard my own voice go flat, careful. "What do they open?"

"The sealed archives. What was left of them. When the ministry burned, the clerks smuggled out the ledgers and locked them with cipher keys distributed to couriers. Your codes are half the alphabet. Papa had the other half." She glanced up at me—or toward me; with her it was hard to say where the looking ended. "You didn't know."

"I carry," I said. "I don't ask."

"Hm," said Sela, in the tone of someone filing a discrepancy away the way her father filed loose type.

I moved along the stone. Galley after galley, page after page of the book that had no title yet. Testimony of the harbor purges. Testimony of the grain requisitions that starved the river valleys while imperial barges rode low and fat. A list of teachers dismissed, then a shorter, colder list of teachers who had been dismissed and then simply ceased to appear anywhere. The empire had not killed these people in secret, exactly. It had done something worse and cheaper: it had unrecorded them. Struck them from rolls, closed their files, let the ordinary machinery of forgetting do the rest. No massacre to mourn. Just absence, compounding daily, until no one remained who remembered there had ever been a presence.

That was what this book was. An anti-census. A ledger of everyone the new regime insisted had never existed.

I reached the last completed galley—the most recent pages, still smelling of the whetting stone—and began to read the way you skim, the way a clerk skims, fast and shallow, keeping the meaning at arm's length.

Then I saw the name.

Not at arm's length. Not shallow. Not skimmed.

*Dunmore, Corvess.* Courier, second class. *Arrested at the Tollbridge station, autumn of the year the ministries fell. Witness account given by R. Halloway, toll clerk:* *he refused to surrender his satchel. They beat him on the platform. He did not cry out until they opened it. Then he cried out—not for himself. For the papers. He kept saying: those aren't mine to lose. Those aren't mine to lose.*

My father.

The word arrived in my body before my mind consented to it—a physical event, a crack running through timber. My knees knew it before I did. I gripped the edge of the composing stone and the metal was cold and real and the name was cold and real and nine years of careful, practiced blandness split down the middle like river ice in thaw.

Because here is what I had been told, all my life, by the aunt who raised me after: your father was a courier who took a bad route in bad times. Wrong place, wrong season. Neutrality is the family trade, Kestrel—we carry, we don't choose sides, and that is why we survive. History happens to other people. Keep your head down and your seals intact.

And here is what the page said: he died defending documents that were not his. He died the way a courier dies when the carrying becomes a kind of faith. And someone had testified to it. Someone had stood in this warm room, near this press, and spoken my father's last words aloud so that metal could remember them.

"Kestrel."

I hadn't told her my name. I realized that distantly, from very far away. I had given the grain clerk's name at the gate, and Halvard had called me nothing at all.

"Sela," I managed. "How did you—"

"Papa knows your surname. He chose you for this run." She said it simply, without apology, still facing her case. "He doesn't know I'm telling you. But you've gone white as fresh stock, and I don't like watching people break without knowing why."

I should ask her what else Halvard knows. I should be angry—I could feel the anger somewhere below the crack, banked, waiting for oxygen. He had requested me. He had known. All that careful anonymity I wore like armor, and the old printer had looked straight through it and picked me anyway, the way you pick a tool.

But that anger needed air, and right now there was no room for it. There was only the page. Only the name. Only the sentence I read again, and again, the way you press a bruise to confirm it hurts:

*Those aren't mine to lose.*

"Yes, they were," I whispered. "They were yours to lose. That was the whole point."

"They're not lost," Sela said. She had turned to face me fully now, her strange half-sight fixed on some point near my heart. "We're printing them. That's what losing means to Papa—that no one writes it down. As long as the type stands, nothing is lost. It's just waiting."

Outside, a patrol went by on the frozen street—boots, lantern-light sliding under the door gap, the muffled clink of a rifle sling. We both went still, the way workshop people go still, bodies remembering raids their minds haven't witnessed. The light passed. The boots faded toward the square.

When I could breathe again, I asked the question I had been circling since the door.

"Why show me? Why tonight?"

Sela considered her type case for a moment. Then she reached in, selected a single piece of type, and pressed it into my palm—warm from her hand, the letter-face sharp against my skin. I tilted it toward the candle. A D.

"Because tomorrow you'll either walk out that door and report us, or you won't," she said. "And I wanted you to decide knowing whose name is in the book. Not the grain clerk's decision. Yours."

* * *

I walked back to my lodging through streets gone blue with frost, and the town had changed around me, the way a familiar room changes when someone dies in it. The proclamations on the walls—ORDER RESTORED, THE CENSUS PROTECTS YOU—were suddenly legible in a way they hadn't been before. Every blank space in them was a struck name. Every tidy register line was an erasure being enforced. Odile Vane with her charming questions and her meticulous forms was not merely counting Ashmoor. She was finishing what the arrests had started: writing the town as the empire wished it had always been.

And I had answered her questions. Smoothly. Well. I had helped keep her census clean.

In my rented room above the cooper's, I lit no lamp. I sat on the narrow bed with my boots on and my hands flat on my knees, and I did the thing I had trained myself never to do: I looked directly at what I carried.

Nine years. Nine years of sealed satchels and rehearsed blandness, of being furniture, of letting history happen to other people because other people was the safest thing to be. And the whole time, history had been waiting for me in a print shop behind a chandler's yard, set in metal by a blind girl's fingers, bearing my father's last words in its locked lines. He hadn't been neutral. He hadn't been unlucky. He had chosen, at the end, on a freezing platform, with blood in his mouth—to hold on. To refuse the loss. And I had built a life on the polite fiction that he'd simply taken a wrong road.

There was a version of me that got up tomorrow, collected the balance of my fee, and walked to the barge landing. The oath permitted it. The oath demanded it, maybe. Carry, deliver, forget.

But the manuscript was upstairs in the workshop. Locked, Halvard had said, in the cabinet beneath the composing stone. Two hundred and eleven testimonies. Four hundred names. My father's voice, crying out for papers that weren't his to lose.

I lay back on the bed and stared at the dark rafters, and I understood with terrible clarity that I was not going to sleep tonight, and that sometime between now and dawn I was going to walk back through the frost to that door.

A courier does not read.

I had believed that with my whole life. I lay in the dark and felt the belief come apart, seam by seam, like a book dropped in water.

Those aren't mine to lose, my father had said.

No, I thought. They're not.
