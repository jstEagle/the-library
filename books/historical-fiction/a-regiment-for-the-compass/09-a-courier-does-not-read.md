## 9. A Courier Does Not Read

The name would not leave me.

I lay in the loft above the workshop on a pallet that smelled of lamp oil and cold wool, and I stared at the rafters as though they might rearrange themselves into an answer. *Corven Dunmore.* My father's name, set in lead and locked into a page of testimony, three lines down from a woman named Ilse Marren who had been taken from the ferry docks at Vellwater in the spring of the last year of the republic. I had read those lines only once—only the once, in Sela's composing room, by accident or by fate, before my eyes had gone blind with refusal and I had stepped back as though from a well.

Three days now. Three days of carrying the name around inside me like a stone swallowed whole. It sat behind my breastbone when I ate. It sat there when Halvard spoke to me about grain weights and delivery schedules in our careful, coded language. It sat there when I passed Odile Vane in the square and gave her the small polite nod of one clerk to another, and she returned it with her pen already moving in her mind, recording, always recording.

A courier does not read.

I had said the oath at fourteen, kneeling in the cold back room of the waystation at Harrow Ford, my hand flat on a ledger older than I was. *I will carry what is given me. I will not open, fold back, or look within. What I carry is not mine to know.* The words had felt like armor then. They had felt like a promise that history could pass through me without touching me, the way water passes through a pipe without staining it. My mother had taught me the oath's deeper grammar over supper: *We are neutral, Kestrel. We carry for everyone. That is how we survive. That is how we have always survived.*

Survive. The word had a taste to it now, like river water in winter—clean, cold, and empty of anything.

I rose past midnight. The house slept; below me I could hear Halvard's slow breathing through the floorboards, and the tick of the press cooling, iron contracting in the dark like something settling down to dream. I dressed without light. I went down the stairs the way couriers go down stairs, placing each foot where the wood would not speak.

Sela kept the manuscript pages in a flat cabinet beneath the window, wrapped in oilcloth against damp, tied with printer's cord. She had shown me once, laughing, how she hid the knot inside a false seam so that even a searching hand would find nothing. I had admired the cleverness of it the way one admires a lock on someone else's door.

My hands knew the knot anyway. Hands remember things the heart refuses.

* * *

The lamp I lit was small, shuttered, its light falling in a coin on the worktable, and I told myself—I was still telling myself, even then—that I would only look at the first page. Only confirm. Only see whether my father's name appeared where I thought I had seen it, whether grief had simply misprinted itself across my memory. Confirmation, not reading. A courier may verify a seal.

The seal was broken the moment I untied the cord.

The first page was not testimony but a preface, written in Halvard's upright, unornamented hand: *This record is compiled from sealed archives of the Republic of Vell, recovered under warrant of the Provisional Assembly and decrypted under authority of the census codes carried by courier in the autumn following the capitulation. It contains the names of 2,114 persons arrested, deported, or executed by the Imperial Administration between the Armistice and this writing. It is printed here because paper burns more slowly than memory.*

Two thousand one hundred and fourteen names.

I turned the page because my hand turned it. There was no decision in it anymore; decision had happened somewhere behind me, on the stairs, perhaps years ago, perhaps the day my mother pressed a sealed satchel into my arms and said *don't ask, darling, asking is how people disappear.*

The archive was organized by season. Winter first—the winter of the capitulation, when the imperial proclamations had gone up on every wall like frost spreading, and the lists had begun. I read names. That is all I can say of those first hours: I read names, hundreds of them, each with a date and a place and a single line of circumstance. *Taken from his shop. Taken from the queue at the granary. Taken in error, acknowledged in error, not returned.* The empire's arithmetic laid out in Halvard's neat columns, and beneath some entries, in smaller type, fragments of testimony recovered from the sealed files—depositions, letters smuggled out of holding houses, a guard's confession dictated to a priest who had died before he could be made to forget it.

I found Ilse Marren again and read her whole entry this time. She had been a ferrywoman. She had refused, twice, to give the names of passengers she had carried across the Vellwater on the night the garrison changed hands. Her deposition survived because her sister had bribed a clerk to copy it before it was sealed. It was four sentences long. It ended: *I carried them the same as anyone. That is all I did. That is all I will say.*

A courier's answer. An oath-keeper's answer. And they had killed her for it, or let the cold do the work and called it hers.

I put my head down on the table then, beside the lamp, and let the shaking come. Because I understood, finally, with the terrible clarity that arrives only after the thing cannot be undone, what I was reading. Not a book. A mirror held up to my whole life, showing me the shape of the lie I had been living inside.

* * *

My father was arrested on a Tuesday.

I know it was Tuesday because my mother made bread on Tuesdays, and the bread was still rising when the knock came, and I remember thinking—even then, even at nine years old—that the dough would be ruined, that we should punch it down, that someone ought to tend to practical things while the men in grey coats filled our doorway. Four of them. They were polite. That is the detail that has haunted me longest: their politeness, the little bows, the officer's apology for the hour. One of them admired my mother's garden. Another asked if the house had a cellar.

They said my father was needed for questioning regarding documents in his possession. My father was a surveyor. He owned maps. He drew boundaries for farms and argued gently about watershed lines, and he came home smelling of chalk and river mud, and he used to balance a ruler on his knuckle to make me laugh.

He kissed my mother on both cheeks. He crouched down to my height, which he did not often bother to do, and he said, "Mind your mother," and he straightened his coat the way a man does when he wants to look respectable for strangers, and he walked out between the grey coats into a morning that continued, insultingly, to be a morning. Birds went on making noise. The bread rose.

He did not come back. We were told he had been transferred south. Then we were told nothing. Then, two years later, a letter arrived with no signature—a letter my mother read once at the table and then burned in the stove, watching it curl, and after that she began teaching me the courier trade in earnest, as though a door had closed somewhere and she had decided I would live on the safe side of it.

*We are neutral,* she said. *We carry for everyone. We do not read, because reading makes you a party. Parties burn.*

And now, in the lamplight, with Halvard's columns swimming before my eyes, I saw it. Saw the architecture of the myth. My father had been a surveyor the way I was a grain clerk—the profession worn like a coat over the true work. Of course. Of course he had carried too. The sealed archives had not traveled to Ashmoor by miracle. Someone had moved them, piece by piece, year by year, through the network of couriers that the republic's death had left orphaned and running on habit. My father had been one of the movers. And when the lists began, when carrying stopped being invisible work and became evidence, they had come for him on a Tuesday morning with their grey coats and their politeness, and my mother had burned whatever that unsigned letter contained and taught me never to ask.

She had not been teaching me neutrality. She had been teaching me amnesia. She had cut our history out of me the way you cut a brand from a hide, and told me the scar was my birthright.

* * *

Toward the coldest hour, I found him.

Not near the beginning, where I had feared he would be—one of the first, one of the famous, his name a banner. He was deep in the winter entries, among the ordinary thousands, which was somehow worse and somehow better. *DUNMORE, CORVEN. Surveyor, district of Harrow Ford. Arrested [date] on charge of conveyance of proscribed materials. Deposition of arresting officer, recovered from sealed file:* 

And then, in Halvard's small type, the officer's own words, dictated into the record with the bland precision of a man describing weather:

*Subject offered no resistance. Subject stated, quote: I carried what I was given and read none of it, and if that is a crime, it is the same crime as the postman's. Unquote. Subject requested that his family be informed he was proud of his work. Request denied.*

I carried what I was given and read none of it.

My father had spoken the oath to the men who were taking him away to die of it. He had stood in the doorway where the bread was rising and recited the family creed to people who found it amusing—or worse, who found it irrelevant—and they had denied him even the small mercy of a message home, and my mother had waited her whole remaining life for a message that existed, that had been recorded, that had been sealed in an archive and locked away behind codes, and the codes had come to me. To me. Carried in my own two hands, unread, up the river, into this town, into Halvard Brann's waiting press.

That was why I had been chosen. Not for reliability. Not for speed. Halvard had needed a courier whose satchel would unlock the death record of the Republic of Vell, and somewhere in the old network's ledgers there was a note—*Dunmore, available, trustworthy, does not read*—and someone had smiled at the symmetry of it, or wept, or both. They had sent my father's oath back to me so that I could deliver the truth of my father's death. The package had been addressed in blood all along, and I had checked the seal instead of the name.

I sat in the shuttered lamplight until the candle drowned in its own wax, and I did not cry, or rather I cried the way walls cry, invisibly, everything moving behind the surface. Grief for him. Grief for her, my mother, who had carried her husband's last words unopened inside herself for twenty years because opening them would have meant admitting the neutrality was a wound and not a shield. Grief, most of all, for myself—for the boy at Harrow Ford who had knelt and sworn an oath that was actually a cage, and had lived in it so long he had called it freedom.

History happens to other people. That was the first article of my faith. But the archive lay open under my hands, and the other people had my face. The other people had my name.

* * *

Gray light was seeping under the door when I heard the stair.

I did not move to hide the pages. Something in me had gone past hiding. I sat with my father's entry open under my palm as Sela climbed into the workroom, hair loose, eyes swollen with sleep, a shawl dragged over her shoulders—and she stopped when she saw me, and she saw the open manuscript, and I watched her understand.

"You read it," she said. Not accusing. Almost gentle.

"My father's in it."

"I know." She came and sat across from me, folding her hands on the table the way her father did, as though the table were a lectern. "Papa didn't request you by chance. You've worked that out?"

"He requested me by surname."

"He requested you by grief," Sela said. "He lost friends to those lists. When the codes came available and the network offered a courier named Dunmore, he said—" she hesitated, choosing honesty over comfort, which seemed to be the family trade—"he said blood would bind better than pay. I told him it was cruel. He said cruelty was honest, and kindness that concealed itself was just another kind of lying."

I looked at my hands. Ink in the creases now, permanent as stains in oak. "He wasn't wrong."

"No," she said quietly. "He rarely is. It's his most irritating quality."

Outside, Ashmoor was waking into its occupied morning: the ration line forming in the square, boots on frozen mud, a proclamation crier with a voice like tin. Somewhere across the town, Odile Vane was sharpening her pencils and opening her ledger of small doubts, and I understood—with a calm that frightened me more than any fear ever had—that I was finished being a discrepancy in her records. I was going to become a certainty. Everything had shifted in me overnight the way a river shifts in flood: the same water, a different course.

"Sela," I said. "Teach me to set type."

Her eyebrows rose. "Since when do couriers compose?"

"Since the courier learned what he's been carrying." I closed the manuscript, tied the cord, slid it back beneath the window—but I touched the cover once before I let it go, the way you touch a grave marker. "My father asked them to tell his family he was proud of his work. Nobody delivered it. So I'll set it myself. In lead. Where it can't be denied."

Sela studied me for a long moment, and then she nodded, slow, and went to wake the type cases from their cold.

But as we lifted the shutters to let in the thin light, I caught sight of movement across the yard—behind the chandler's fence, a figure pulling back quickly from the gap between the boards, too quick, the particular furtiveness of someone who has watched too long. The chandler's apprentice, or someone standing where the apprentice stood.

And in the pit of my stomach, beneath the grief and the resolve, an older instinct stirred and whispered: *someone else knows the presses never stop.*
