## 10. The Informant in the Loft

The knock came at the hour when even the garrison slept—two bells past midnight, a rhythm I did not know: three soft raps, a pause, two more. Sela heard it before I did. She was already out of her cot in the loft above the pressroom, bare feet silent on the boards, one hand raised to stop me moving.

"That's Halvard's knock," she whispered. "But Halvard never knocks."

Below us, through the floorboards, lamplight bloomed and shivered. We heard the bar lifted from the yard door, voices kept low, and then a sound that turned my stomach to river ice: boots. Not Halvard's uneven shuffle, not the printers' worn slippers—boots with nails in the soles, four pairs at least, moving through the workshop where four hundred forbidden pages lay stacked like kindling.

Sela's hand found my wrist in the dark. Her fingers were cold as type metal.

* * *

I should begin further back, because that is how dread works—it does not arrive all at once but seeps, the way damp comes up through a cellar floor, invisible until everything you stored there is ruined.

For six days after the raid-that-didn't-happen—that is, after the night I am describing—I would have told you things had been going well. That was the trap of it. The workshop had settled into its rhythm: Halvard running the flatbed press through the small hours, Sela composing by candle stub, me learning the collator's trade with hands that were finally beginning to smell permanently of ink instead of only smelling of ink when I was nervous. The Ashmoor Record grew nightly, quires of it, testimony gathered into chapters, names set in roman where the empire preferred them unwritten.

We had rules. No one came or went before dawn or after dusk. No one spoke of the work outside these walls, not even in the cellar where we took our meals. The chandler's shop next door, Tammas Wren's establishment, provided our cover—the workshop was, on every imperial register, nothing more than a job-printing annex, turning out price lists and funeral notices for the chandlery.

And yet someone had been talking.

The first sign was small enough to dismiss. On Tuesday, a garrison patrol walked the alley behind the chandler's shop at an hour when patrols did not usually bother with alleys. They paused at the loading door. One of them held a lantern high enough that its light showed under the gap at the threshold—a thin blade of yellow that crossed the floor of the pressroom and climbed the wall, there and gone in four heartbeats. Halvard killed the lamp without a word. We stood in the dark listening to the patrol's laughter recede, and Halvard said, "Drunk boys," in a voice that meant he believed no such thing.

The second sign was harder to dismiss. Odile Vane appeared at the chandler's counter on Wednesday afternoon, buying candles—not a clerk's ration purchase but a deliberate dozen tallow dips, paid for with exact change while her eyes made a slow circuit of the premises. Tammas served her himself. Through the connecting door, half-hidden behind a rack of dip-rods, I watched her gaze travel along the ceiling beams, up the stairwell toward the loft, and rest—just rest, no longer than courtesy required—on the closed door of the printing annex.

She bought her candles. She thanked Tammas warmly. She wrote something in a small book before she reached the corner.

"Routine," Tammas said afterward, too loudly, wiping a counter that was already clean. "They count stock twice a season. It's nothing."

It is a peculiar property of fear that it makes everyone a poor actor. Tammas's denial had the bright, brittle quality of glaze over cracked clay. And I noticed something else, something that lodged under my skin like a splinter: his apprentice, a boy called Perrin with a cowlick and a stammer and a genius for getting underfoot, had gone very still behind the wax vats, and had not asked, as he always asked, what the soldiers wanted.

Perrin who ran errands for everyone. Perrin who carried messages between the chandlery and the annex a dozen times a day, who saw the deliveries come in, who swept the yard where the pressmen smoked. Perrin whose mother, I remembered suddenly, had taken in washing for the garrison since autumn.

I told myself I was being unfair. I told myself that stillness proves nothing, that a stammering boy goes quiet around authority the way any animal does. But the splinter stayed, and worked, and festered.

* * *

Which brings us back to the knock at two bells past midnight, and the nailed boots below.

Sela and I stood frozen in the loft while the voices resolved themselves. One was Halvard's—low, unhurried, performing surprise badly. The others wore the clipped cadence of garrison officers. Then a fifth voice, and my whole body went cold around the marrow: Odile Vane, apologizing—actually apologizing—for the hour, explaining that the census office had received a report of unlicensed night labor at this address, and surely Master Brann understood that she had no choice but to verify.

A report. Someone had reported us.

Lamplight moved below. We heard drawers opened, the press cover dragged aside, Halvard's voice narrating: funeral notices, yes, price lists for the chandlery, a wedding announcement for the Ferris family, see here, officer, see for yourself. And they did see—they saw exactly what Halvard had spent three days ensuring they would see. He had stripped the press of the Record's forme two nights ago, on instinct I still don't fully understand, and locked the composed pages in the cellar, leaving the bed loaded with the most innocent work imaginable.

The inspection lasted perhaps twenty minutes. Boots on stairs. A hand testing the loft ladder—we heard the rungs creak, felt the vibration through the floorboards, Sela's grip crushing my wrist to numbness. Then Odile's voice again, closer now, directly beneath us, thanking Master Brann for his patience, expressing regret for the disturbance, noting aloud that the census office appreciated the cooperation of Ashmoor's honest tradesmen.

Then silence, except for the sound of my own blood.

We waited until the yard gate clanged and the boots faded up the street, waited another quarter hour on top of that, because Halvard whistled a particular tuneless fragment of a harvest song when the house was clean. Only then did we descend.

Halvard stood in the middle of the pressroom, lamp relit, looking ten years older than he had at supper. His hands, those enormous steady compositor's hands, were not quite steady now.

"They knew the hours," he said, without preamble. "Unlicensed *night* labor. Not suspicious activity, not contraband. Night labor. Someone told them the press runs after dark." He looked at Sela, then at me. "Three people outside this room know that."

* * *

We did not sleep again. We sat at the collating table with the lamp turned low and made a list, because lists are what frightened people make instead of decisions.

Outside the walls: Tammas Wren, who had known about the night shifts since the beginning and had never once objected aloud. Perrin, the apprentice, who saw everything and said little. The grain factor who supplied our paper, the cooper who sold us barrels, the woman at the ferry slip who asked no questions about freight marked as chandler's goods. Any of them might have talked—or been made to talk, or talked without knowing what their words unlocked.

Inside the walls: the five of us who slept under this roof. I will not pretend the thought didn't cross my mind, ugly and unwelcome, that I was the newest arrival, the unknown quantity, the courier with ink-stained hands whom a sharp-eyed census taker had already flagged once at the town gate. If I could suspect myself, anyone could be suspected of anything, which is precisely how terror works on a small community—it doesn't need evidence, only permission.

"Perrin," Sela said finally. She said it the way you pull a tooth.

"He's a child," I said.

"He's sixteen, and he carries washing home to his mother every second day, and his mother works for the garrison laundry." Sela's voice was flat, exhausted. "I'm not saying he betrayed us. I'm saying he's the thread a clever person would pull."

"And if we're wrong?" I heard my own voice sharpen. "If we accuse him and we're wrong, we've done the empire's work for it. Fear splits a house faster than fire."

Halvard raised one hand, and we stopped, because Halvard's silences commanded better than other men's shouts.

"No one accuses anyone," he said. "Not tonight. Tonight we assume the worst about the walls, not the people. Everything that can be moved, gets moved. Everything that can't—" he touched the press, the way you'd touch a dying friend's shoulder, "—gets made ready to die quickly."

* * *

Moving meant the matrices. This needs explaining, because the matrices were the true treasure, more than the printed sheets, more than the press itself.

When Halvard first decoded the sealed archives with the codes I carried, he had not simply read them—he had committed them to movable type. Every page of the Ashmoor Record existed as standing composition: thousands of pieces of lead alloy, letters and spaces and quadrats, locked into iron chases, page by page, chapter by chapter. From those formes he could print a hundred copies or ten thousand. Destroy the formes and the book became nearly impossible to reproduce; the manuscript originals were long scattered, hidden, partially destroyed. The type was the memory of the republic, cast in metal.

Four hundred pages' worth. Perhaps three tons of lead, if you counted the furniture and the chases. You cannot hide three tons of lead the way you hide a letter in a coat lining.

"There's the old ice cellar," Sela said. "Under the chandlery's original foundations. My grandfather dug it before the war—it's brick-lined, dry, and the entrance is behind the wax store. Nobody's been down since I was small."

"Behind Tammas's wax store," I said.

"Behind Tammas's wax store," she agreed, and let the implication sit between us like a fourth person at the table.

We began at three in the morning and finished at dusk the following evening, working in shifts, moving the formes a chapter at a time in wash-baskets lined with sacking, covered over with candle stock. I carried lead until my palms blistered and split, and found—to my own astonishment—that I did not care. There is a strange mercy in physical labor when your mind is gnawing itself. Each basketful was a page of the dead made safe. I carried my father's name down those cellar steps four times without knowing it, and perhaps that was a mercy too.

The last trip fell to Sela and me together, near evening, the yard dim and freezing. Halfway across, the chandlery door opened and Perrin stepped out with a bucket of dip-ends for the scrap barrel.

He froze. We froze. The basket sat between us, sacking slipped just enough to show the edge of an iron chase, the unmistakable architecture of locked type.

For three full seconds nobody moved. Snow ticked against the flagstones. Somewhere down the street a patrol drummed its heels past the square, unhurried, bored, omnipresent.

Then Perrin did the thing that decided everything, though none of us understood it at the time: he stepped forward, picked up the third handle of the basket—heavy, awkward, a boy's grip—and carried it to the ice cellar door for us, stammering, "F-frozen goods, is it? For the w-wax cellar? Cold keeps best low," and set it down inside the threshold, and touched his cap, and went back to his bucket.

Sela watched him go. So did I. Neither of us breathed properly until the chandlery door shut behind him.

"Frozen goods," she repeated softly.

"He covered for us," I said. "Or he'll report it within the hour."

"Yes," Sela said. "Both of those are possible. That's the whole horror of it, isn't it?"

We bricked the entrance that night—loose bricks, mortared with mud that would crack away in minutes if ever we needed speed—and stacked candle crates in front of the wall so that only someone who knew would know. Halvard pressed his palm flat against the new brickwork for a long moment, like a man taking leave of a grave he intended to return to.

* * *

I could not sleep, so I went up to the loft window and watched the square.

Ashmoor under snow is beautiful in a way that feels like an insult. Moonlight on the rooftops, the garrison banner hanging motionless above the customs house, smoke rising straight from chimneys into windless cold. Ordinary life continuing its ordinary shapes. And somewhere in one of those houses, behind one of those windows, a person had written a report about night labor at the print shop, folded it, carried it to the census office, and handed it to Odile Vane. Maybe for coin. Maybe for spite. Maybe for the sincere belief that reporting sedition was a civic duty, that order was worth any betrayal, that a well-run empire was kinder than a burned-out town.

Maybe they even believed they were saving lives—flushing out troublemakers before the garrison did it with fire instead of paper.

That was the thought that kept me at the window until my breath frosted the glass: not that the informant was wicked, but that they might be good. Honest, even. Certain they were the hero of the account they'd give themselves. Odile Vane had taught me that much—she interrogated you with such genuine, patient hunger for truth that lying to her felt like blasphemy. The empire did not run mainly on cruelty. It ran on people convinced that accuracy was virtue.

Across the square, in the upper window of the census office, a light was burning. I knew without seeing who kept that vigil. She had inspected us, found nothing, and filed her report—and Odile Vane struck me as a woman who filed nothing without keeping a copy in her own head. She would turn the discrepancy over and over: a print shop that smelled faintly of fresh work despite producing only funeral notices; a master printer too calm by half; a grain clerk with compositor's hands who had materialized in Ashmoor weeks ago and never left.

She was patient. Patience was her weapon, and she wielded it better than any soldier's boot.

Down in the pressroom, Halvard was already reloading the bed with innocent type, rebuilding the fiction of the humble job-shop, stroke by stroke, letter by letter. Sela sat beside him sorting leads by touch in the dark, her eyes closed, her fingers reading the letters the way other people read prayers.

I went down and joined them. What else is there to do, when the walls have ears and the snow keeps falling and the book must exist? You sort type. You carry lead. You trust the wrong people carefully and the right people completely and you cannot always tell which is which until the knock comes at the door.

Two days later, the knock came again—Halvard's rhythm, three and two—but this time it was real, and the man himself stood in the yard, snow to his knees, his face gray as the dawn behind him.

"The warrant's signed," he said. "Seizure and burning. Three days."
