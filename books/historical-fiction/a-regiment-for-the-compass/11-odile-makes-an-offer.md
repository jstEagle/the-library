## 11. Odile Makes an Offer

The summons came on a Wednesday, folded inside an ordinary envelope sealed with the gray wax of the census office—the wax that meant routine business, nothing worth a second glance. I stood in the doorway of my rented room above the cooper's yard and held it a long moment before breaking it, watching my breath hang in the cold air like something I'd said and wished back.

*Citizen Marrec Voss is requested to present himself at the census office, Ashmoor square, tomorrow morning, ninth bell, for the completion of his registration particulars. Bring proof of employment. This is not a summons. This is a courtesy.*

Not a summons. A courtesy. Odile Vane had a way with paper that made threats sound like favors. I had learned to read her handwriting the way Sela read type in the dark—not the letters but the pressure behind them, the places where the pen had paused. There were three pauses in that single sentence. She had been careful. Careful meant uncertain, and uncertain meant she didn't yet know what I was.

I burned the envelope in the cooper's stove and sat up most of the night rehearsing.

*Marrec Voss, grain clerk, employed by Hessler & Daughters of Corren's Landing, sent to audit winter stores at the Ashmoor granary annex.* I had worn this name for eleven days now. It fit like a coat that had been tailored for someone slightly larger—roomy enough to move in, but you always felt the seams. In the dark of my room I recited the ledger entries I'd memorized, the tonnage figures, the names of Hessler's daughters, one dead of fever, one married to a rope-maker in Vell City. I recited them until they stopped sounding like lies and started sounding like grief, which is the trick of every good cover: you don't invent a life, you mourn one that never existed.

Across the square, through the frost-furred window, the workshop's chimney breathed its thin ribbon of smoke into the gray morning. Still running. Still printing. Four days since I'd learned what the codes unlocked, three nights of type-setting beside Sela until my fingers bled at the corners, and every hour the garrison patrols grew a little thicker, a little slower at the corners, the way dogs circle when they've caught a scent they can't quite name.

Someone was circling. And in the morning, I would walk straight into the center of the ring.

* * *

The census office had been a wool merchant's house once. You could still see it in the bones of the place—the carved lintel over the inner door, the tall windows meant for displaying bolts of cloth, now papered over with imperial proclamations from the inside so that the light came through greenish and strange, like being underwater. The waiting bench was oak, polished by decades of anxious trousers. Two other registrants sat ahead of me: a widow renewing her ration classification, a boy of maybe sixteen come to declare his trade as required by the Occupational Edict. Neither would meet my eyes. Nobody met anybody's eyes in Ashmoor anymore. It saved time and lives.

At the ninth bell exactly, the inner door opened and there she was.

Odile Vane did not look like the empire. That was the first thing I'd noticed at the gate, weeks ago now—or days; time had gone soft and slippery since the workshop—and it was the thing that kept catching me even now. She looked like a schoolmistress, or a well-read aunt: small, neat, her dark hair pinned with plain combs, a smudge of ink on the heel of her right hand that she never seemed to notice. Her voice was warm the way a stove is warm—you could put your hands on it and be comfortable right up until you understood you were being cooked.

"Citizen Voss." She smiled, and gestured me through. "Thank you for coming promptly. I know how busy the grain season keeps everyone."

"It's hardly a season," I said. "It's a famine with paperwork."

She laughed—a real laugh, quick and low—and closed the door behind us.

The office proper was small. A desk, two chairs, a shelf of ledgers bound in government gray. On the wall hung the imperial seal, the double-headed eagle rendered in cheap engraving, and beneath it, incongruously, a dried sprig of rowan berries in a clay pot. Someone's touch of home. I wondered whose, and hated myself for wondering.

"Sit, please." She took the chair across from me rather than behind the desk—an old interrogator's kindness, or a kind interrogator's cruelty; I couldn't tell which, and the not-knowing made my palms sweat against my knees. "This should take only a few minutes. Some clarifications. Your file has—" she opened a folder, thumbed pages I couldn't see, "—a few small inconsistencies."

"Inconsistencies," I repeated. My voice came out steady. Eleven days of practice will do that. "In my experience, Citizen Vane, inconsistencies are how clerks stay employed."

Another laugh, shorter this time. Her eyes stayed on mine. They were brown, unremarkable, and absolutely tireless.

"Let's begin simply." She dipped her pen. "Your employer writes that you arrived in Ashmoor on the fourth of Frostmonth. The gate log says the fifth."

"The barge was delayed at Tarn Lock. Ice in the weir channel. The pilot logged it—I can give you his name."

"You already did, in your first interview. Master Oskell." She wrote it down anyway, though we both knew she'd written it down the first time too. "And your mother's maiden name?"

"Corvene. C-O-R-V-E-N-E." The lie came out smooth as poured oil. I had polished it for hours after the gate, built it a whole family tree, given it a grave in the churchyard at Corren's Landing and a sister who emigrated south. Lies are like type, I had learned from Sela: set them properly, justified, kerned tight, and no eye catches the gaps.

Odile wrote it down. Then she set down her pen entirely, folded her hands, and did something I hadn't seen her do before.

She waited.

Silence in an interrogation is a tool, and I knew all its weights. But this silence had a different grain to it. She wasn't letting me hang. She was listening—to the building, to the street outside the shuttered window, to something beneath the floor. When she finally spoke, her voice had dropped its bureaucratic warmth entirely, leaving something plainer underneath, almost tired.

"There are no soldiers coming, Citizen Voss. No one is waiting in the next room. I dismissed my clerk for the morning." She nodded at the folder. "What I'm about to say is not in any record, and it will never be in any record, because I intend to keep it out of records myself. Do you understand? This conversation is happening off the page. For once in my career, off the page."

My heart was going hard enough that I felt certain she could hear it. I kept my face where I'd trained it to stay: mildly puzzled, faintly bored, the face of a man whose greatest fear is arithmetic.

"I'm afraid I don't follow."

"No," she agreed softly. "You follow very well. That's rather the problem." She turned the folder around and slid it toward me. I didn't look down at it. Looking down is surrender; looking away is flight; holding still is the only ground left. "Your hands, Citizen Voss. Or should I say—your hands, whoever you are."

"My hands?"

"Ink. Under the nails of the index and middle finger of the left hand, in the pattern of someone who sets type by hand, who wipes the forme with a rag held in the left palm. Grain clerks get ink on their right hands, from pens. Compositors get it here." She touched her own left fingertips, briefly, as if greeting an old acquaintance. "I noticed it at the gate, the first day. I told myself it was a clerk who also kept accounts by hand. Then I interviewed the chandler next to your—next to the granary annex, and he mentioned smoke from a neighboring chimney at hours when no granary works. Then the boy who delivers water told me about voices singing hymns at night in a street where nobody sings. Small things. I collect small things. It's my profession."

Every exit in the room had quietly become a wall. I made myself breathe. Outside, a patrol went by, boots striking frost-hard cobbles in that rhythm I now knew better than my own pulse.

"You're mistaken about whatever you think—"

"Kestrel Dunmore."

The name landed like a dropped weight. Not shouted. Spoken almost gently, the way you'd speak a name at a graveside. I felt my whole constructed self go still around it, the way a house goes still when the beam under the floor cracks.

She watched me absorb it. She didn't gloat. That was the worst part—there was no triumph in her face at all, only a kind of grim satisfaction, the satisfaction of an equation balancing.

"The courier registry from the last year of the Republic," she said. "Recovered from the ministry archives in Vell City when we took the capital. Most of it burned, of course. But couriers are thorough people, and thorough people keep duplicates. One entry survived: a courier designated only by oath-name, carrying final census codes, destination Ashmoor. Dispatched by an old colleague of mine in the archive service who turned out to be working for the other side." She paused. "Halvard Brann was a friend of his, years ago. Did you know that? Brann asked him for a courier. He was given a choice of four."

I said nothing. There was nothing to say that wouldn't be a confession, and everything I wanted to say that would be a worse one.

"He chose you," Odile went on, "and I have spent three weeks asking myself why a master printer, a careful man, a man who has survived two purges by being forgettable, would request a courier whose surname appears twice in the sealed testimony he is so painstakingly setting in metal." She leaned forward. "I am not a fool, Kestrel Dunmore. Whatever else I am, I am not a fool. So let us stop performing for each other. It exhausts me, and I suspect it exhausts you more."

* * *

What happened then, I can only set down the way I remember it—as a series of details that refused, at the time, to cohere, and have taken years to assemble into meaning.

She rose, crossed to the shelf, and took down a ledger—but not a government one. This binding was black leather, cracked at the spine, the kind sold in stationers' shops to students. She set it on the desk between us but did not open it.

"Do you know what my work actually is?" she asked. "Not what the proclamations say. The real work."

"Counting people."

"Recording them." Something flickered in her face—pride, old and genuine and terribly misdirected. "Everyone. Every soul accounted for, every name matched to a birth, a trade, a death. Before the empire, the Republic's censuses were theater. Half the districts never filed. People were born and died and vanished without ever having been *counted*, and everyone called that freedom." Her hand rested on the black ledger. "I call it abandonment. A person no one has counted is a person no one will miss. I have spent nine years making sure people are missed. Do you understand? When the plague came through Marrow Vale, I knew the number of the dead before the doctors did, because I knew the number of the living. Order, Mr. Dunmore. Truth, even ugly truth, even empire-shaped truth, is the only thing standing between people and erasure."

"And the workshop?" I made my voice flat. "Is that order too?"

Something moved behind her eyes—there and gone, like a fish under ice.

"The workshop is sedition," she said. "By statute. By statute, the men and women setting type in that cellar are committing a crime against public peace, and by statute I am obligated to report them, seize their materials, and burn the matrices so the crime cannot recur." She opened the black ledger at last, turned it to face me.

Pages of columns. Names, dates, times. *Smoke, chandler's lane, 11th bell, Frostmonth 9. Voices singing, 2nd bell, Frostmonth 12. Cart hired from Ostrey's yard, returned heavy, driver paid double, Frostmonth 14.* Three weeks of small things, gathered with the patience of a tide.

"They are printing a book," she said quietly. "A history of the fall of the Republic, compiled from sealed testimony, from the disappeared, from the ledgers your codes unlocked. Four hundred copies, if they finish. And here is what I know that they do not." She closed the book. "When it is finished, it will be found. Because I will find it, or someone less careful will find it faster. And it will be burned—every copy, every matrix, every man and woman who touched it, and the building with them, and probably the chandler, and quite possibly the whole street as an example. I have read the seizure orders from the last three towns. Ashmoor will not be gentler."

"So report it, then." The words came out of me before I could weigh them, and they came out wrong—too sharp, too much like a challenge. She heard the mistake in my voice the way Sela hears a letter set upside down. I saw her hear it.

"I said I would not put this conversation on the page," she said steadily. "I meant it. Sit down."

I sat. My legs had opinions of their own.

She drew a sheet of paper from the desk drawer, and a second pen, and began to write in a quick, sure hand. As she wrote, she spoke—not looking up, giving the words to the page instead of to me, as if that made them easier for both of us.

"Here is my offer. Immunity. Full registration under a protected classification—clerical witness, which is a real category and carries real protections. Your true name entered into the census, legally, permanently, so that no one can ever make you disappear again—that is what you people are afraid of, isn't it, disappearing? That is the whole religion of the courier oath, carry and never read and never be known." The pen scratched. "In exchange: the master plates. The matrices. Delivered to me before the print run completes. I will see the plates destroyed personally—I will stand at the furnace myself, and no soldier will touch them, and no copy of my report will name a single person in that workshop. They lose their book. They keep their lives. You keep yours. Everyone walks away poorer and alive."

"And the truth?" I asked. "The testimony. The names of the disappeared. What happens to those?"

She set down the pen. For a moment—just a moment—she looked older than she had any right to look, as if the office's underwater light had finally reached her face.

"They burn," she said. "As they would burn anyway. That is what you refuse to understand, all of you in that cellar with your beautiful typesetting and your hymns at midnight. You are not deciding whether the record survives. You are deciding whether it burns *with witnesses*. There is no version of this, Mr. Dunmore—none—in which that book outlives this winter. The empire has taken nine provinces. It has burned libraries that were older than the Republic. Your four hundred copies are four hundred candles in a windstorm." She pushed the unfinished immunity draft across the desk toward me, along with her own pen. "I am offering you the only justice available, which is survival, which is what justice has meant in every age for people who aren't heroes. Take it. Sign it. Walk out of that workshop tonight and let me save six lives in exchange for one book."

"And if I don't?"

"Then I do my duty," she said simply. "Soon. Perhaps sooner than you think." And there it was again, the fish under the ice, the hesitation she thought she'd hidden. "But I would rather not. I have burned enough. I would like, just once, to have counted a thing and saved it instead."

* * *

I walked back across the square in a daze, the unsigned paper folded in my breast pocket like a coal.

Immunity. A legal name. An end to running—the end of the whole long gray apprenticeship of being no one, the rented rooms and borrowed faces and the oath worn smooth as a river stone from handling. She had offered me the exact thing I had wanted since I was twelve years old and learned why my father never used our name in public: permanence. Safety. A line in someone's ledger that could not be erased.

All it cost was four hundred books' worth of the dead.

I stood at my window as the light failed, watching the workshop chimney send up its thin thread of smoke, and I understood, finally, the shape of the trap Odile Vane had built—not for me, but around me. She hadn't lied. That was the horror of it. Every word had been true. The book would burn. The empire had burned greater things. Survival *was* the only justice on offer, if justice was measured in breathing bodies. She believed all of it with the whole force of her tidy, terrible sincerity, and belief like that doesn't bend—it only breaks.

And yet.

And yet she had hesitated. Twice. Once when she spoke of the workshop's hymns, and once at the end—*perhaps sooner than you think*—as if her own timeline embarrassed her. I turned the moments over like coins bitten for counterfeit. Somewhere in that precise, dutiful woman was a seam. Sela could have found it in the dark. I needed to find it now.

Below, in the cooper's yard, the evening watch called the hour. Across the square, the workshop's windows glowed with candlelight, and I thought of Halvard bent over the press, of Sela's ink-stained fingers moving through the type cases blind, of my father's name waiting in the locked drawer where the finished signatures were kept. I thought of Odile's black ledger full of small things, and of the one large thing she had not written down: that she had summoned me alone, dismissed her clerk, and spoken treason-adjacent words off the page. A loyal servant of the empire does not offer bargains. A loyal servant files a report.

Unless the report is already filed. Unless the bargain is bait, and the soldiers are already walking.

Or unless—worse, stranger—Odile Vane was at war with herself, and I had just been shown which side was losing.

There was one way to know, and it meant going to Halvard Brann and telling him everything: the ink under my fingernails, the recovered registry, the offer, the unsigned page. It meant confessing that the net had been around us for three weeks while we sang hymns and set type, that our safety had lasted exactly as long as one woman's patience. He had gambled on my blood once already and never told me. How he would receive this news, I could not guess.

But the raid was coming whether I told him or not. The only question left was who would be holding the truth when the door came down.

I took the unsigned paper from my pocket, held it over the stove, and watched Odile Vane's careful handwriting curl and blacken and go up the flue toward a sky that belonged to neither of us.

Then I put on my coat and went to tell the printer he was a dead man unless we finished the book in three days.
