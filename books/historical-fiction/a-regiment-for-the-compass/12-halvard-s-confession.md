## 12. Halvard's Confession

The cellar smelled of tallow and cold stone, and Halvard Brann stood among the crates like a man standing in his own grave, which, in a sense, he was. He had asked me to come down after the second night of printing—asked in that careful, unhurried way of his, wiping ink from his hands with a rag that had long since surrendered to the black—and I followed because I thought he wanted to thank me.

I want you to understand what happened in that stairwell, in those few seconds between the door closing above us and the lamp catching below. I thought I was descending into gratitude. Instead I descended into arithmetic.

"Your father," he said, "was Tomas Dunmore."

Not a question. A naming. The way a priest names a dead man over his coffin, or a magistrate names a criminal over his sentence. He said it plainly, without softness, and the name went through me like a nail driven flush to the wood—the kind of blow that leaves no head to grip and pull out again.

"You knew," I said.

"I requested you." He set the lamp on a crate. Its light climbed the walls and found the shadows of type matrices stacked in their trays, ranks of silent letters waiting to be made into sentences, into accusations, into the names of the vanished. "When your office assigned this run, I wrote back and asked for the courier named Dunmore. There were three available. I chose you."

* * *

I should tell you what I did then, because I am not proud of it, and pride has no place in an honest record—which is, I suppose, the whole argument of this book.

I hit him.

Open-handed, then not open-handed, and he took it the way old printers take everything: by bracing against the press-frame behind him and letting the blow land where it landed. The lamp guttered. Somewhere above us a board creaked—Sela, listening, or only the house settling under frost—and neither of us spoke until my breath came back.

"You used me." My voice sounded foreign, scraped raw from some depth I hadn't visited since I was nine years old. "You opened a letter with my father's blood in it and didn't tell me. You let me carry it through checkpoints, through Odile Vane's questions at the gate, through all of it, and you never said—"

"I needed you to carry it clean." He straightened, rubbing his jaw, and there was no apology in his face. That was the worst of it—not cruelty, but the terrible honesty of a man who has already priced everything, including himself. "A courier who knows what she carries flinches. Flinches are read. You carried those codes like grain ledgers because you believed they were grain ledgers. Your ignorance was the safest box in the Republic."

"Don't call it the Republic. It's gone. You know it's gone."

"I know exactly what's gone." Something moved behind his eyes then, quick as a compositor's hand pulling a finished form off the stone. "That's why it's being printed."

"The oath—" I was grasping for it now, the old litany, *carry, never read; deliver, never ask*, and it came apart in my mouth like wet paper. "The oath says nothing about being chosen. About being selected like a tool."

"No," Halvard agreed. "It doesn't."

* * *

Let me be exact, because exactness is all we have left.

My father was arrested when I was nine. I have told myself the story so many times it wore smooth as river glass: that he was a clerk, that clerks were taken sometimes, that it was misfortune and not design, that our family's neutrality was a shield and not a costume we wore while the country burned around us. My mother rebuilt her life on that story. I built mine on top of hers, floor by floor, and called the building invisibility.

But Halvard had the testimony. I had read it—I had broken the oath two nights before and read every page, and somewhere in the middle of that long, sickening night I had found my father's name among the depositions of the sealed archives, taken down by men who kept records even of their crimes. He hadn't been a clerk. He had been a courier too, before the oaths changed hands, and he had refused, at the end, to hand over a route book. They had erased him so thoroughly that even I had believed the erasure.

And Halvard Brann knew all of this. Knew it when he wrote my name into his requisition. Knew that if I ever read the manuscript—if I ever broke, as he gambled I would break—my own grief would chain me to the work more surely than any wage or threat.

"You bet on my father," I said.

"I bet on blood." He said it without flinching. "It's the oldest bond there is. Older than oaths. Oaths can be administered by empires, girl. Blood can't."

"My name is not yours to wager."

"No." He looked at his hands, at the ink worked so deep into the creases it would outlive him. "It isn't. But someone had to wager something, and everyone else in this town is wagering their lives. You were only wagering your story. Forgive me for thinking it was worth less than theirs."

* * *

I walked to the far wall of the cellar and put my palm flat against the cold stone, because if I didn't give my hands something to hold they would go for him again, and one of us was too old to survive a second round.

Above us I could hear Sela's footsteps crossing the floor overhead, the small domestic sounds of the workshop settling into its night rhythm—the press oiled, the formes locked, the candle stubs arranged. Four hundred copies, nearly done. Names of the disappeared, testimony of the fallen, the true death record of the Republic of Vell, and underneath all of it, in the colophon space reserved for makers, room for whoever survived to claim it.

"I could walk out," I told the wall.

"Yes," Halvard said.

"I could report the workshop. Buy my own amnesty. Odile Vane would take it—you know she would. She offered."

"I know she offered." He came and stood beside me, not touching, close enough that I could smell the oil and metal of him. "And I'll tell you honestly, since I've spent my last honesty on lesser things: I don't know which way you'll go. I never did. That was the flaw in my wager, and I deserve whatever comes of it."

"And if I stay?"

"Then you're not cargo anymore." He said it quietly. "You never were, really. Cargo gets delivered. This—" he nodded upward, toward the presses, toward the book, toward the town sleeping uneasily around its own secret—"this has to be written. And writing takes a hand that chooses."

* * *

Here is the thing I understood down in that cellar, with my knuckles still aching and my father's name still lodged under my ribs like a swallowed coal:

Halvard had manipulated me, yes. Had reached across twenty years and a grave and moved me like a piece of type into a forme I never consented to. That sin was real, and I would not launder it, and I told him so, and he accepted the verdict the way he accepted everything—without excuse.

But the manuscript was real too. The names were real. The man who had died refusing to surrender his route book was real, and he was mine, and no manipulation could make him less mine or the record less true. Odile Vane had offered me survival. Halvard had offered me nothing but a choice dressed up as fate. And between those two offers—one warm, one cold—I discovered I preferred the cold one, because the cold one left me my hands.

"Halvard," I said.

He turned.

"I'm staying. Not for you. Understand that. If you died tonight I'd finish this book tomorrow." I pushed off the wall and faced him, and for the first time since Ashmoor I felt steady, the way a compositor must feel when the forme locks and the letters hold. "I'm staying for the names. All of them. Including his. Including mine, if it comes to that."

"That's all I ever asked," he said. "Though I had no right to ask it."

"No," I agreed. "You didn't."

We climbed the stairs together, and at the top he paused with his hand on the door and said, almost gently, "Sela will teach you the case layout tomorrow. If we're going to make four hundred by week's end, I need another pair of hands that can find an E in the dark."

So it was settled—not forgiven, never that, but settled, the way a joint is set after breaking: functional, aching, and stronger at the fracture than it had been before.

I slept that night in the loft above the press, and dreamed of nothing at all, and woke before dawn with my hands smelling of ink that wasn't there yet.

Outside, in the square, the frost lay thin and bright on the census office roof, and I saw the lamp in Odile Vane's window burning past midnight, and understood, with the calm of a woman who has stopped being anyone's courier, that whatever patience had been holding this town together was running out—and that we had maybe days, not weeks, to finish the printing and get four hundred books onto the water.

I went downstairs to learn the type cases.
