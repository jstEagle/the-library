## 13. Printing by Candlelight

The first night I set type, I ruined a page.

It was the testimony of a woman named Marta Ilves, who had watched soldiers take her sons from a barley field in the spring of the occupation, and I dropped her words into the composing stick wrong—transposed two letters, *sons* into *sno*, and did not notice until Sela's fingers found the error an hour later, reading the forme by touch the way other people read by light.

"You've made her say something she never said," Sela told me quietly. "That's worse than dropping the page. A dropped page is silence. This is a lie."

I pulled the type out letter by letter and reset it, and while my hands worked I thought about how many years I had carried paper without once considering that every word on it had been placed there by someone's fingers, one character at a time, the way a mason places stones. The oath said *carry, never read*, and I had imagined myself a river—impartial, indifferent, moving what was given me. But rivers don't choose their course. Someone does. And now, for the first time, my fingers were choosing.

The workshop at night was a different country from the workshop by day. By day it was a chandler's back room, smelling of tallow and sawdust, deliberately dull, with a false wall of stacked crates that Halvard had built himself and showed to no one but us. By night, when the shutters were barred and the candle-shields hung so that no glow escaped through even a knothole, it became what it truly was: the last press of the Republic of Vell, running against the clock of its own destruction.

There were four of us at work those nights, and later five. Halvard at the press itself, a big man gone quiet and economical, feeding sheets with the tenderness other men reserve for infants. Sela at the stone, setting type by candlelight and by fingertip both, because she had learned composition in the dark years after her mother died, when Halvard could only afford lamp-oil for the press and not for his daughter's eyes. Odile Vane at the door, or the window, or the loft above the chandler's shop, listening for boots on cobbles—and I will write more of her later, because she deserves more than a sentence about listening. And me: collating, folding, sewing signatures, and learning, slowly, clumsily, to set type beside the girl who could do it blind.

* * *

Patrols thickened the way frost thickens on glass—not all at once, but in slow spreading patterns you only notice when the whole pane has gone white.

On the second night, we heard them pass twice before midnight. On the third night, four times. On the fourth, a sergeant's voice stopped directly outside the shuttered window, close enough that I could hear him chewing, and held there for the length of a pipe while Halvard stood with his hand flat on the press beam, ready to throw the oil-damped cloth over the forme, and none of us breathed until the boots moved on.

"They're not searching," Sela whispered afterward. "They're counting."

"Counting what?"

"Us. The town. How many lights burn past curfew, which chimneys smoke at odd hours, who buys more candle-tallow than a household should." She fed another line of type into the stick without looking up. "My father says a census is just a siege conducted politely."

I thought of Odile Vane at the town gate, her pleasant voice asking after my mother's maiden name, and said nothing. Some knowledge you carry like a coal in your chest, afraid to breathe on it.

We changed our rhythms to match the patrols. The garrison kept its rounds on a schedule as regular as church bells—the empire's great gift to the world, predictability—and Halvard had mapped it onto a chalk circle drawn inside the crate-wall, each patrol route a spoke, each gap between rounds a window of safe working time. When the boots came, we froze; when they went, we moved. Press, freeze, move, freeze. It gave the nights a strange music, a rhythm like rowing against a current, and I began to understand why printers through all the centuries of suppression had been hanged and burned and broken on wheels and had never, ever stopped printing. There is a joy in forbidden work that lawful work never knows. Every sheet we pulled was a small resurrection.

* * *

Sela taught me the case the way her father taught her: not with names first, but with the shape of the work.

"Lowercase lives below, capitals above, because capitals are few and proud and want the high shelf," she said, guiding my hand across the graded rows of type. "E is everywhere, so E sits where your finger falls naturally. Q is a snob nobody visits. Learn the case with your eyes shut and you'll set faster than any man who needs lamplight."

"Why would anyone need to set type in the dark?"

She smiled, and in the candlelight I saw her mother in her face for the first time—a woman I had never met, present only as an inheritance of expression. "Because someday they'll come for the lamps too. Father says a printer should be able to work in whatever darkness they leave him."

So I learned with my eyes shut. I learned the deep bite of the em-quads and the shallow kiss of the spaces, the way a piece of type sits true in the stick when the line is justified and wobbles like a bad tooth when it isn't. I learned to read a galley upside down and backward, mirror-script, until the reversed letters stopped being foreign and became simply another alphabet—one that had to be trusted to turn right way round only at the last moment, under pressure, in the grip of the press. There was a lesson in that I chose not to examine too closely.

By the fifth night I could set a line of body text without errors. By the seventh, Sela let me compose a full paragraph alone: the account of the granary seizures at Vellmouth, names and dates and tonnages, the plain arithmetic of hunger imposed by decree. I checked it four times. I made her check it twice more. When the forme was locked and inked and the sheet came off the press clean, Halvard held it up to the shielded candle and read it, and grunted—which from Halvard was applause—and something opened in my chest that I had no name for yet.

Later I would have the name for it. It was the feeling of carrying something and delivering it in the same motion. Of being, finally, both courier and destination.

* * *

The book grew signature by signature, quire by quire, a body assembling itself on the drying lines strung across the rafters like laundry made of testimony.

I learned its anatomy in those nights. I learned that the sealed archives of the Republic had been more than ledgers—that the census clerks of the old government, in the last months before the collapse, had done the one honest thing available to them: they had recorded the disappearances. Names taken at midnight. Houses emptied. The grain requisitions that were famine wearing a uniform. Testimony smuggled out in laundry bundles and hymnals and the linings of coats, sealed behind cipher locks whose keys had been scattered among couriers like seed corn—and I had carried one such key in the lining of my own coat, pressed against my ribs for eleven days, never knowing I was carrying four hundred people's last words about the people they loved.

Now those words stood in metal, line by line, and I touched them the way you touch a scar.

Page 214: *They took my husband on the feast of St. Aldric. The officer wrote in his book that no one of that name was registered at our address. We had lived there nineteen years.* — Yrenna Coss, Ashmoor.

Page 231: *I counted the wagons. Eleven. I am old and my eyes are good and there were eleven wagons going east, and they were not empty.* — unattributed, per the witness's request.

Page 247: a list. Just a list, three columns of names, the disappeared of three provinces, set in the smallest type Halvard possessed because there were so many of them and the paper was rationed and precious. Sela had composed most of that list herself, working double shifts, and I noticed she never spoke while setting it. Her lips moved sometimes, sounding out each name as she locked it into place, giving every name one breath of air before it went into the forme. A printer's requiem. I started doing it too, after I saw her. It slowed the work. We did it anyway.

Some nights the candle burned low and Halvard refused to replace it until the sheet was done—"waste is a luxury of peacetime"—and we worked in a brown gloom, faces lit from beneath like figures in a fever dream, the smell of ink and hot metal and tallow smoke braided together into something I knew I would smell for the rest of my life and be brought back here, always, to this room, this race, this borrowed time.

* * *

Odile Vane stood watch, and I learned to stop flinching at the sight of her.

It was a strange education. She arrived after her day's official work was done—after she had spent eight hours being the empire's patient, meticulous face at the census office—and she climbed through the chandler's loft with the sureness of a woman who had memorized every board, and she took up her post at the north window with a pocket watch and a notebook, logging the patrols' intervals in her neat clerk's hand.

"You're writing down how to catch us," I said to her the first night she joined us. I hadn't meant to say it. It came out anyway.

"Yes," she said, without turning from the window. "And how to avoid being caught, which is the same information worn inside out. Your father's republic taught its couriers that secrets are safest in the open, disguised as ordinary facts. The empire hasn't learned that yet. Its officers want secrets hidden, so they look for hiding places, and find none, and grow complacent." She tapped the notebook. "A patrol log looks exactly like a census appendix. If they search this room and find it, they will see bureaucracy, and yawn."

"And if they look closely?"

"Then I have committed treason with excellent penmanship." Almost a smile. "Go set your type, courier. You count the letters; I'll count the boots."

I went. But I thought about what she'd said for a long time after—that truth and its opposite could wear the same coat, depending on who was walking in it. Odile believed order required truth, and served an order built on lies, and had begun, somewhere in the ledger of her small doubts, to notice the contradiction. I watched her log the patrols with the same care she'd shown at the gate, and I understood that she was not helping us. She was keeping records. It was the only honesty the empire had left her, and she was spending it on us, and neither of us named what that meant, because naming it would make it a choice, and choices can be punished.

* * *

On the ninth night, Sela fell asleep at the stone.

She had been working eighteen hours; the list of names had run longer than the compositor's estimate, and she refused to delegate a single entry. I found her mid-line, cheek resting on her forearm, fingers still curled around the composing stick, breathing the slow breath of total exhaustion. The candle had burned to a thumb's width.

I should have woken her. Instead I finished her line.

It was a name from the eastern provinces—Davorin Miekka, miller's son, age seventeen, taken— and I set it letter by letter with her stick, in her style, checking each character against the manuscript twice. Then I lifted her gently away from the stone, draped my coat over her shoulders, and sat down in her place, and worked until dawn.

When she woke near morning and saw the completed forme and the stack of printed sheets and me still setting, she looked at me for a long moment.

"You didn't wake me."

"The line needed finishing."

"That's not what I mean." She rubbed her eyes. "You could have left it. You could have walked out any of these nine nights—you keep your bag packed by the door, I've seen it. Why do you stay?"

I looked at my hands. They were stained to the second knuckle now, ink worked so deep into the creases that soap barely touched it, the hands of a printer, the hands I had seen in my mind's eye since the night Odile Vane had looked at them across her census table and known me for a liar.

"Because I spent my whole life believing history happened to other people," I said. "Somewhere else. To someone else. And then I found my father's name in this book, and I understood that 'other people' was a story I told myself so I wouldn't have to hold anything. These pages are the first thing I've ever held that was mine." I turned the composing stick over in my ink-stained fingers. "I'm staying because for the first time, my hands match what I'm carrying."

Sela nodded slowly, the way you nod at something already known. Then she pointed at the forme.

"You set Davorin Miekka's name slightly crooked," she said. "Third line. Fix it before lock-up."

I fixed it. And I laughed—actually laughed, there in the candle-smoke and the ink-smell with the patrols passing outside like a tide we'd learned to swim between—and Sela laughed too, tired and bright, and Halvard looked up from the press and shook his head at us both, and for one whole minute the workshop was not a conspiracy or a target or a countdown. It was just a place where people made something together, and the something was true.

* * *

We passed the halfway mark on the eleventh night: two hundred signatures bound, two hundred to go, with the endpaper stock running short and Halvard stretching the last reams of rag paper by printing on ever-thinner sheets that threatened to ghost through.

That was also the night the patrols changed.

I heard it before I understood it—Odile heard it first, as always, her head lifting from the watch-book like a deer scenting weather. "They've altered the route," she said. "New interval. Shorter." She marked the watch, calculated, and her pen stopped moving. "Someone has reported increased activity in this quarter. The garrison doesn't thicken patrols out of boredom."

Halvard set down the ink roller with great care, the way a man sets down a thing he might otherwise throw.

"How much shorter?" he asked.

"Twenty minutes between rounds. Perhaps fifteen by week's end." Odile closed the notebook. "At fifteen minutes, we cannot pull a full sheet. At ten, we cannot run the press at all."

The candle flame stood perfectly still in the dead air, and I looked around the room—at Halvard's jaw set like a locked forme, at Sela's hand resting flat on the stone as if warming it, at the drying lines overhead hung with the town's whole testimony, fluttering faintly in the draft from the shutter seams—and I did the arithmetic we all did, silently, in the same instant:

Two hundred signatures remained. The patrols were closing like a fist.

Three days of work, at the pace we'd been keeping. Less, if the intervals kept shrinking. And somewhere out there in the dark, walking his rounds with a lantern, a soldier who did not yet know it was carrying orders that would reach him soon enough.

Halvard broke the silence first. He picked up the roller again.

"Then we print faster," he said.
