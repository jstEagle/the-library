## 14. The Census Closes

The register of Ashmoor, in its final form, ran to four hundred and eleven pages.

I knew the number because I had bound it myself—sewed the signatures with waxed thread in my quarters above the customs house, because I did not trust the town's bookbinder not to read what passed through his hands. Four hundred and eleven pages of names, ages, trades, kinships, holdings, oaths sworn and oaths amended. Four hundred and eleven pages that claimed to be a portrait of a town and were, in truth, a portrait of everything a town could be made to confess.

I had written most of it by candlelight. Not from any romance of lamplight and diligence—I wrote at night because the days belonged to Ashmoor itself, to doorways and ration lines and churchyards, to the small theater of being seen asking questions. A census taker who works only from her desk is a clerk. A census taker who works from her desk *and* her feet is something else entirely. I had learned early that the empire's paperwork was believed not because it was accurate but because no one checked it against anything. So I checked. Grain receipts against harvest claims. Church rolls against household declarations. Burial records against the living.

It was astonishing how much a town will tell you if you simply compare one lie to another.

I sat at my desk on the last night of the count, the finished register stacked before me in two thick volumes, and I dipped my pen for the final entry—the anomaly I had been carrying for eleven days like a stone in my boot.

*Grain clerk, arrived by barge on the 14th. Name: Corren Vail. Papers in order. Answers consistent.* And then, beneath all that orderly agreement, the line I had added in my own hand, small enough that only a careful reader would find it: *Hands ink-stained beyond the trade. Left index calloused in the manner of a compositor or courier. Volunteered detail freely; permitted one correction without distress. Recommend routine follow-up.*

Routine follow-up. The phrase was a kind of mercy, or a kind of cowardice; I had not yet decided which. In my experience the two wore the same coat.

I blotted the page, closed the volume, and set my seal into the warm wax. The stamp bit down—the imperial eagle, wings spread over a sheaf of grain, as though the bird had ever once looked kindly at wheat—and I felt the familiar satisfaction of completed work settle over me. It was the best feeling my profession offered. Better than promotion, better than commendation. The feeling of a thing made true. Of chaos rendered legible. Somewhere in the capital there would be shelves, and on those shelves this book would stand beside ten thousand others, and Ashmoor would exist—not as rumor, not as memory, but as *record*.

That was the whole of my faith, if I was honest about it, and I tried always to be honest with myself even when I lied fluently to everyone else. Empires fall. Borders move. But a well-kept register outlives them all. I had seen the archives of three fallen governments in my training years, and what struck me was never the decrees or the treaties. It was the censuses. The humble lists. Somebody, decades ago, had walked a street like mine and asked a farmer how many pigs he kept, and because of that anonymous clerk I could see the farm entire—could smell it, almost, across a century of dust. Order required truth. That was the first principle. Everything else followed.

I wrapped the register in oilcloth and carried it down through the sleeping customs house to where Captain Erren Stavros kept his evening brandy.

* * *

Stavros received the report in his office behind the garrison headquarters, a room that smelled of pipe smoke and wet wool and the particular staleness of men who have been stationed somewhere they dislike for too long. He was not a stupid man. This made him worse company than a stupid man would have been, because he understood exactly what his work was and had made his peace with it years ago, the way one makes peace with weather.

"The count is complete?" he said, taking the oilcloth bundle.

"Complete. Cross-referenced against receipts, rolls, and prior provincial records. Three hundred and forty-one households. Forty-two unaccounted persons since the annexation—mostly young men gone east, I'd judge."

"Deserters."

"Gone east," I said again, mildly. "Whether they deserted anything is a matter of definition." I watched him weigh whether to be annoyed and decide against it. We had an arrangement, Stavros and I. He tolerated my precision because precision made his reports look thorough. I tolerated his indifference because indifference left me room to work.

He unwrapped the register, thumbed pages without reading them, and stopped where I had known he would stop—because I had flagged it, and flagged things are to bureaucrats what blood is to sharks. "Your grain clerk. You recommend follow-up?"

"I recommend it be noted. Nothing more. It may be nothing. Ink-stained hands are common enough in a river town."

"You don't believe it's nothing." He said it without accusation, merely as inventory.

"I believe," I said carefully, "that a man whose answers agree too smoothly is either honest or rehearsed, and that rehearsal leaves traces. His hands leave them. I've noted the traces. The judgment belongs to someone paid more than me."

Stavros grunted and set the register aside. Then he said, in the tone of a man sharing a confidence he has already decided to act on regardless, "There was a communication from the provincial office yesterday. Regarding Ashmoor generally."

"Generally," I repeated. The word sat wrong in my mouth.

"There's a print shop here." He turned a paper knife over on his blotter, not looking at me. "Or so the provincial censor believes. Pamphlets have been moving through the valley for months—seditious material, names in them, things about the annexation that were never authorized for print. The trail goes cold somewhere in this town. Your census, Vane, was never just a census. You know that."

I did know it. I had told myself I didn't, or that it was incidental, but I knew it. "And?"

"And tomorrow the provincial order arrives formally. Seizure and destruction of the premises. Sedition." He said the word the way he said *rain*—a forecast, not a tragedy. "Fire, probably. These old timber buildings burn clean. The garrison will handle it. Your role is concluded; you'll submit your register and move on to Merrowfield by week's end."

"Which workshop," I said.

He looked up then, and something in my voice must have interested him, because he studied me for a moment before answering. "Behind the chandler's. On Tanner's Row. Why? Have you been asking after it?"

"I ask after everything," I said. "That's the position."

"Yes," Stavros said. "It is." He went back to his brandy, and I took my leave with the correct degree of deference, and I climbed the stairs to my cold quarters, and I stood at the window for a long time doing arithmetic I did not want to do.

Tanner's Row. Behind the chandler's. Where a compositor's daughter bought tallow every Thursday and never haggled. Where the light burned late in a window that faced away from the square.

* * *

Here is what I want recorded, since recording things is the only prayer I know: I did not sleep that night, and it was not conscience that kept me awake. Or not conscience alone. It was the register.

I lay in bed with my hands folded on my breastbone like an effigy and thought about the four hundred and eleven pages upstairs in the strongbox. Every name in them had been given to me under compulsion—that is the nature of a census; no one tells an official their mother's maiden name out of love. And yet. And yet the giving had a shape to it, a dignity. When the widow Marta Ossen recited her dead sons' birthdates to me, her voice steady as a metronome, she was not obeying the empire. She was insisting that the boys had existed. When the ferryman lied about his brother's whereabouts and we both pretended to believe him, the lie was a form of shelter he was building with his own hands, and I let him build it, and I noted the lie, and the note was—what? Witness? Theft?

I had built my faith on a simple architecture: order requires truth. The register was true. Therefore the register was good.

But tomorrow an order would arrive, and the order would say: take the building on Tanner's Row, take the presses, take the paper, take the type, and burn it. Not correct it. Not file it. Burn it. And I understood, lying there in the dark with the frost ferning the window glass, that the burning would not be directed at lies. Lies are useful to empires; empires keep their lies in climate-controlled rooms with catalog numbers. The burning would be directed at precisely the kind of thing my register was—a true account, kept against the wishes of the powerful. The difference between my book and theirs was only whose wishes.

I got up and lit the lamp and took out my working notebook, the private one, the one with the discrepancies in it, and I reread the grain clerk's entry for perhaps the fortieth time. *Corren Vail.* Hands ink-stained. One small lie surrendered gracefully, like a duelist offering a point to end the bout honorably. I had caught it—the date of his mother's burial, off by a season—and he had accepted the catch with a flicker of something that was not fear. Relief, nearly. As if a correctly landed question were a comfort.

A clerk does not look relieved when caught. A clerk looks terrified, then obsequious, then forgettable. This one had looked like a craftsman watching another craftsman work.

And now the garrison would go to Tanner's Row, and whatever that craftsman was carrying—papers, codes, a person, I genuinely did not know—would burn along with the press, and my beautiful register would record the aftermath as a gap. Household dissolved. Persons unaccounted. The census's oldest verb: *removed*.

I thought about the widow Ossen then. About her metronome voice. *Insist that they existed.* The empire's answer to insistence was ash.

Somewhere below, a sentry called the hour—two of the clock—and the sound of his boots faded along the quay, and I sat with my notebook open to a blank page for a long time, pen in hand, unable to write anything at all.

* * *

In the morning the provincial order arrived, as promised, under seal: *premises on Tanner's Row, seizure of materials, destruction by fire, execution in three days' time, census officer to certify the property inventory prior to seizure.*

Certify the inventory. My signature on the list of what would be destroyed. They had found a use for my precision after all.

I stood in Stavros's office while he read it aloud, and I heard myself say, "Three days is short for a proper certification. An inventory done carelessly invites disputes later—claims from landlords, from creditors. The provincial office hates disputed inventories."

"So do it carefully," Stavros said.

"Carefully takes longer than three days. There's the chandler's stock to segregate—tallow and oil stores belonging to a separate enterprise. If we burn those, the chandler has a claim. Unless—" and here I produced, from my sleeve, the small gift I had spent the night polishing, "—unless the warrant specifies the printing premises only, by parcel number, and the chandler's adjoining stock is certified separately and exempted. Which requires the parcel survey. Which requires two days."

Stavros stared at me. "Vane. Since when do you slow anything down?"

Since always, I wanted to say. Slowness is the whole art. A census taken fast is a rumor. But I said instead: "Since the alternative is handing the provincial office a flawed document. You may tell them your census officer is being difficult. They already believe it."

He signed the amendment request because it was procedurally impeccable, and because he wanted the chandler problem off his ledger, and because men like Stavros trust paper more than they trust people—which has always struck me as the single redeeming feature of bureaucracy, and possibly the reason I love it, and possibly the reason it is about to break my heart.

Walking back across the square that afternoon, past the ration line and the proclamation boards where new notices covered older ones like bark over wound wood, I saw the boy Sela Brann—no. I saw the girl. The compositor's daughter. She was carrying a basket of tallow blocks home from the chandler's, and she saw me seeing her, and neither of us changed our pace, and that mutual stillness told me everything: she knew what I was. Which meant she knew, or would know within a day, what was coming.

Three days. I had bought three days with a clerical technicality, and I did not yet know why I had done it, and I did not yet know whom I had bought them *for*, and the not-knowing felt like standing at the edge of ice, listening to it speak.

That night I opened my notebook to the blank page again. At last I wrote one line, small, in the margin where discrepancies went:

*Tanner's Row — parcel 9. Certified inventory pending. Discrepancy: none found. Yet.*

Then, underneath, in handwriting I barely recognized as my own:

*What is the census for, if the counted can be burned?*

I had no answer. But I had three days, and a pen, and for the first time in my career—an anomaly I intended to pursue.
