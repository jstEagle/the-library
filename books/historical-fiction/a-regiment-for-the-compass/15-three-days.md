## 15. Three Days

Three days. I kept saying the number aloud, as if repetition could make it smaller, more manageable—a thing that could be carried in one hand instead of crushing both shoulders flat.

Four hundred books. Four hundred copies of a book that did not exist, according to every law the empire had written since it crossed the river. Each one bound in gray boards, sewn with linen thread, heavy as a sleeping cat and twice as dangerous. Halvard had counted them twice, then a third time because Sela teased him about his arithmetic, and the number held: four hundred and six, actually, three of those spoiled by ink blots that Halvard refused on principle to destroy. *A flawed truth is still a truth,* he said, and stacked them back in the press room like bricks of something explosive, which is precisely what they were.

We gathered in the cellar beneath the chandler's shop—the same cellar where we'd hidden the type matrices after the near-raid—and spread a map of Ashmoor across two upturned tallow barrels. The candle guttered. The air smelled of wax and wet stone and the ghost of lye, and beneath all of it, faintly, the iron tang of fresh ink still under my fingernails no matter how I scrubbed.

Halvard stood at the head of the barrels like a general who had never commanded anything larger than a print run, which was true, and whose hands shook slightly when he spoke, which was also true, and neither fact diminished him.

"Three routes," he said, tapping the map with a finger stained permanently brown. "The river, the mill road, and the corpse road."

I laughed before I could stop myself.

"It's not a joke," he said mildly. "The corpse road runs behind the burial ground, north along the ridge. It's called that because it's how they used to bring bodies down from the hill farms in winter, when the mill road drifted shut. Nobody uses it now. Which is exactly why we will."

Sela traced the river route with her fingertip, her eyes half-closed—she knew the town by touch better than by sight, and she drew the streets from memory the way other people recited prayers. "The barge leaves on the morning tide in three days. The *Maren's Daughter*. Old Piet's boat. He owes my father money from before the war and hates the garrison more than he fears them, but hatred is not the same as courage, and we should not ask him to be braver than he is."

"Which is why the barge carries only grain sacks," I said. "And why the grain sacks carry only—" I stopped myself. The oath lived in me still, even broken, even remade. Some habits of silence are load-bearing.

"Only what weighs the same as grain," Sela finished for me, smiling in the dark.

* * *

We spent the first day buying things.

This sounds simple. It was not. Everything we needed was either rationed, watched, or suspicious in quantities too small to fill a wagon: rope, canvas, lamp oil, cart grease, the particular wide-mouthed jars chandlers use for pickled herring, which hold a folded octavo volume perfectly if you seal the lid with wax. We bought in threes and fives, scattered across three days' worth of errands, from three different shopkeepers, none of whom could have assembled our purpose from their separate pieces if you'd tortured them individually—and I confess I thought about that possibility more than once, planning for it, arranging the lies so that each of us could be caught alone without condemning the others.

That was the new arithmetic of my life. Not *how do I get this done* but *what happens to everyone else when I fail*. Every alibi was a small architecture built around another person's survival.

Tam Wrenn, the carter, agreed to haul "chandler's stock" to the river landing on the third morning—for a price that would have bought his whole house twice over, and which he took without haggling, which frightened me more than greed would have. Men who don't haggle have already decided what the risk is worth. I watched his face while we talked, looking for the flicker of a man playing a part, and found nothing but a tired man counting coins, and I chose to believe him, because belief was now a supply we needed as badly as rope.

The widow Osk at the ferry house took our bribe differently. She wouldn't touch the coin. She pushed it back across the table and said, "My husband printed handbills for the old assembly, before they closed the assembly. You tell Master Brann the second crossing is free, and the third, and however many it takes." Then she went back to darning a net, as if she hadn't just handed us the difference between success and the gallows.

I walked out of the ferry house into the gray afternoon light and stood very still on the frozen mud of the landing, overcome—not with hope exactly, but with its dangerous cousin. A town I had entered as cargo was becoming a crew.

* * *

The second day, Odile Vane came to the square.

I saw her from the window of my rented room above the cooper's—my window, the one with the warped shutter through which I had spent so many hours watching the workshop door. She crossed the open space in her imperial gray, unhurried, census ledger under her arm, and she stopped in the middle of the square and simply stood there, facing the chandler's shop, for the better part of an hour. Snow began to fall. She didn't move. A soldier came to fetch her; she waved him off without turning her head.

She was watching the workshop the way I had once watched it. With patience. With the terrible patience of someone who has decided that the truth of a place can be extracted like a tooth, slowly, root and all.

But she wasn't watching the workshop door. I checked, again and again, tracking the line of her gaze against the geometry of the square. Her eyes rested on the upper windows. On my window.

She knew where I slept. Of course she did. She had interviewed me twice; she had my name—the false one—and my trade—the false one—and my mother's maiden name, which I had invented on the spot at the gate and which she had surely already compared against parish rolls in three towns. She knew the grain clerk was a fiction wearing my coat. What she didn't know—or hadn't yet decided she knew—was what to do about it.

That night I couldn't sleep. I sat by the shutter with the lamp unlit and watched her window across the square, where a candle burned until the small hours. Two people keeping vigil over each other, armed with nothing but knowledge.

* * *

Day two's work was rehearsal. We ran the plan the way Halvard proofed a page: twice, then corrected, then twice again.

The barge route first. Four hundred and six books, wrapped in oilcloth, packed into herring jars and grain sacks, loaded onto Tam Wrenn's cart before dawn on the third morning, carted to the landing, transferred to Piet's hold under forty sacks of actual barley that Piet had bought honestly and would lose honestly if we failed. The garrison inspected outbound river traffic at the landing between the sixth bell and the eighth. We would arrive at the fifth bell, when the inspectors were still warming their hands at the brazier and resenting being awake.

"The weakness is the cart," Sela said. She said it flatly, without apology, the way she said everything. "One cart, one road, one checkpoint. If they take the cart, they take everything. There has to be a second way out that doesn't depend on Tam's courage or Piet's memory of debts."

So we built the corpse road route as a fallback: three loads, moved on foot over two nights, hidden in the burial ground's old receiving vault until the barge or—if the barge burned—until some later courier, some future Kestrel, came to carry them the rest of the way. It meant the books might leave in ones and twos instead of hundreds. It meant the record might reach the world as fragments, smuggled in coat linings and hymnals, a truth distributed like seed grain. Halvard hated it. He wanted the book to arrive whole, a single voice. But he signed off on it, because he was honest enough to know that a voice that dies entirely says nothing at all.

The mill road we gave to the lie. I would be seen loading the cart with chandler's stock—openly, in daylight, in my grain clerk's coat, with papers showing a consignment of tallow candles bound for a buyer in Marrow Ford. Let the paperwork be perfect. Let the lie be so boring that boredom itself became the armor. Odile had taught me that, though she'd hate to hear it: the best deception isn't hidden, it's documented.

By midnight of the second day we had mapped every patrol interval, bribed or enlisted six people, forged two sets of shipping papers, and rehearsed the loading sequence until Sela could direct it blindfolded, which was fortunate, since she intended to do it in the dark.

Then Halvard made us stop, and sit, and look at one another across the candle.

"Tomorrow night the presses finish," he said. "The morning after, the books go. Between now and then, every one of you will be asked—in your own heart, if nowhere else—whether you can do your part. Not whether you want to. Whether you *can*. Courage that hasn't been tested is just an opinion." His eyes went around the cellar, resting on each of us. "Test yours tonight. Quietly. And if the answer is no, say so now, and no one will think less of you, and you'll be given a task far from the center of things."

Nobody spoke. The candle flame leaned in a draft from the stair.

Tam Wrenn cleared his throat. "I can haul a cart," he said.

Widow Osk wasn't there, but I knew her answer already. Sela reached over and squeezed my wrist once, hard, which in her language was a paragraph. Halvard nodded slowly, satisfied, and blew out the candle, and we climbed the stairs into the cold with our opinions tested and holding.

Mine held. But I noticed, climbing into the dark, that it held the way a rope holds—with strain visible in every fiber.

* * *

The second night, I wrote letters.

Not real ones—there was no one to write to, no address I could safely give—but the kind couriers write before a hard crossing, to be sent only if they don't come back. I wrote one to Sela, thanking her for teaching my hands to speak. One to Halvard, forgiving him in advance for the surname gambit, though I told him I'd deny it. And one I addressed to no one, which was really a letter to my father, telling him that his name was going to survive him, spelled correctly, set in type, printed on paper good enough to last a hundred years, and that his son—who had spent a lifetime pretending history happened elsewhere—had finally learned otherwise.

I sealed them into the bottom of my document case, beneath the false shipping papers, where a soldier's bayonet would find them long before it found the books.

Across the square, her candle burned past midnight again. I watched it until my eyes ached. I tried to read it like type—she was compositor enough of a soul by now, in my imagination, that I thought I could. A steady flame: deliberation. A guttering one: doubt. Tonight it burned steady, and steady frightened me more than wild, because steady meant she had nearly finished deciding whatever she was deciding, and I could not tell from here whether her decision pointed toward us or away.

Odile Vane believed in order. That was the trap of her, the engine of her. She had spent months building a register of Ashmoor so truthful it cut like glass, and somewhere in that glass she had seen our reflection, and now the empire had ordered her to smash the mirror rather than let it show what it showed. I did not know if a woman like that could disobey. I only knew that if she did, we would need her for exactly one hour, on exactly one morning, at exactly one checkpoint—and that no amount of bribery could buy what only conscience could give.

* * *

The third dawn came up the color of pewter.

I was awake before the bells, dressed, the document case strapped across my chest. Below my window, the square lay blue and empty, breath rising from the chimneys, snow squeaking under the boots of the first patrol. At the chandler's side door, a shadow moved: Sela, checking the street, then waving the all-clear back into the dark. Somewhere beyond the roofs, I heard the creak of Tam Wrenn's cart axle beginning its slow approach, and the soft shifting weight of four hundred and six truths settling against each other in the dark like roosting birds.

Fifth bell. The landing. The brazier. The inspectors warming their hands.

I pulled the shutter closed, took one breath, and opened my door—

—and stopped.

Odile Vane was standing in the square below my window, alone, no soldier, no ledger, her gray coat dusted with new snow. She was looking up at me with an expression I had never seen on her face in all our interviews: not the census taker's pleasant blankness, not the interrogator's patient hunger, but something rawer. The face of a woman standing at the edge of her own life, reading the last line of a page and finding it unfinished.

"Master Quill," she called softly, using my false name like a knife laid flat between us. "You'll want to hear what the garrison ordered this morning. Before you go down to the river."

Behind her, far off toward the chandler's street, a thin thread of smoke was rising into the pewter sky.
