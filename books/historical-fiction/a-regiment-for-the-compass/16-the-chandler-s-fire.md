## 16. The Chandler's Fire

The smoke was not smoke yet. That was the first thing I understood, watching it from the workshop window—a gray exhalation, almost gentle, curling up from the chandler's street the way chimney smoke rises on any winter morning in any occupied town. My mind, trained by years of practiced blandness, reached for the ordinary explanation first: a stove left burning, a kettle forgotten, some housewife's carelessness.

Then the wind shifted, and the smell reached us.

Wax. Tallow. The sweet, cloying reek of rendered fat catching flame, carried across three rooftops and into the loft where Sela was setting type by touch, her fingers moving over the cases with the blind certainty of prayer. She lifted her head like a deer scenting wolves.

"That's not a chimney," she said.

"No," I said. "It isn't."

Below us, the press had stopped. Halvard Brann stood in the middle of his workshop with ink to his elbows and a look on his face that I had not seen before—had never seen, in all the weeks of candlelit shifts and whispered counts—and it took me a moment to name it, because it did not belong on a master printer's face. It belonged on a man watching a coffin being built.

"The chandler's," he said quietly. "Pike's shop."

We went down into the street together, the three of us, and Ashmoor came apart around us as we walked.

* * *

I want to describe the fire honestly, because everything after it happened inside that honesty, but fires resist description. They are too fast for language. One moment the chandler's shop was a building—low, squat, familiar, its windows fogged with the breath of tallow vats—and the next it was a mouth, orange-lipped, exhaling heat you could feel from forty paces. The fire had found the wax stores. Of course it had. A chandler's shop is a bomb that spends its whole life pretending to be a business.

The garrison bells began before the town's own bell did. That detail I will keep until I die: the imperial bells rang first, sharp and disciplined, while old Tomas at Saint Ferren's fumbled at his rope like a man waking from a dream. Soldiers came at a run—not the loose evening patrols we had learned to read like weather, but a column, double-file, boots striking cobbles in unison, forming a perimeter around the burning block with the smooth economy of men who had drilled this. Not firefighting. Containment.

"They're not trying to save it," Sela said beside me. We had pressed ourselves into the doorway of a cooper's shed, her hand locked around my wrist hard enough to leave marks. "Look at them. They're letting it burn."

She was right. The soldiers held their line at the edge of the heat, bayonets fixed, faces lit orange, and behind them the bucket chains that should have formed were forming late, half-hearted, townsfolk passing water hand to hand more from habit than hope. The empire did not waste water on accidents. Which meant—

"Which means they think it's an accident," I said slowly, working it through, "or they think it's theirs."

Neither answer comforted me.

Because there was a third possibility, and it had been sitting in my chest since Odile Vane first spoke the word *informant* without speaking it at all—the suspicion that had fallen, unfairly or not, on Pike's apprentice, young Corren, who had been seen too often near the loft stairs, who had asked Sela once what she printed so late, who had a mother owed three months' back rent to a landlord with garrison connections. If Corren had set this fire—if he had meant it small, a diversion, a little blaze in the tallow stores to pull the patrol away from the workshop for an hour, just long enough for one more wagon run to the cellar—

Then he had miscalculated by every measure a fire can be measured by. Wax does not make a diversion. Wax makes an inferno.

"Where is Corren?" I asked.

Sela's grip tightened. "I haven't seen him since yesterday. Have you?"

I hadn't. And in the orange light, with the heat pressing against our cheeks like an opened oven door, that absence told its own story.

* * *

The fire burned through the afternoon. By dusk, half the chandler's shop was a black ribcage open to the sky, and the other half was a smoking wound that steamed where the bucket chains finally, grudgingly, drowned it. Two houses adjacent were scorched. No one died—Pike himself had been at the market, which I chose, in the absence of evidence either way, to read as luck rather than design—and the town stood in the falling snow-ash and stared at the ruin the way people stare at anything too large to hold.

The garrison did not stare. The garrison counted.

I saw them doing it from the shadow of the cooper's shed: a sergeant with a slate, walking the perimeter, noting doors, noting faces, noting which townsfolk had come to fight the fire and which had merely watched. And among the officers at the corner, wrapped in a gray greatcoat that did nothing to hide her posture, stood Odile Vane.

She was not looking at the fire. Fires were finished; fires were data already collected. She was looking at the crowd, her head tilted slightly, her lips moving as though rehearsing names. And then, as if my attention were a physical thing she could feel landing on her skin, she turned and looked directly across forty paces of trampled snow at the doorway where I stood.

She didn't point. She didn't signal the sergeant. She simply held my eyes for a count of five—long enough to say *I know where you live*, short enough to deny it under oath—and then turned back to her officers, and I understood that whatever came next would not wait for the three days we thought we had.

I ran back to the workshop through streets gone blue with early dark.

* * *

Halvard had already pulled the shutters when I arrived. Inside, the workshop glowed with its forbidden candlelight, and everyone was there—Sela, the two journeymen, old Maren who bound the signatures, a handful of the neighbors who had become, without anyone naming it, conspirators. They stood around the press like mourners around a bed.

Halvard spoke first, and his voice was steady in the way of things about to break.

"Garrison rider went past the square twenty minutes ago. Double-time. Warrant's coming." He looked around the room. "Not in three days. Tomorrow. Dawn, if I know how these men count hours—they'll want it done before the market crowd gives them witnesses."

The words landed on the room like stones into still water, each ripple a face changing.

"Then we move tonight," I said. "All of it. Every copy, every signature, down to the cellar and out to the river."

"Four hundred books," Maren said flatly. "Through patrolled streets. In one night."

"Four hundred books," I agreed, "or none. There's no third number anymore."

And this is the truth I have carried since that night, the truth I could not have understood back when I was only cargo: catastrophe does not ask permission. It arrives, it burns something down, and it hands you the arithmetic of your life with the numbers already chosen. Three days ago we had time enough to be careful. Now we had one night, and one night is a different country with different laws.

They began to move. Not panic—something better and worse than panic, the grim choreography of people who had rehearsed disaster in whispers for weeks and now got to perform it for real. Sela went to the type, wrapping matrices in oilcloth with hands that shook only a little. The journeymen broke down the drying racks and started stacking bound copies into grain sacks, the same sacks that had smuggled me into this story weeks ago. Halvard banked the press, wiped the stones, and for a moment stood with his palm flat on the wood of it like a man saying goodbye to a horse he'd ridden twenty years.

I caught his arm. "There has to be someone at the door."

He knew what I meant. Everyone in the room knew what I meant, because the plan we had built assumed a garrison that kept its schedule, and the garrison had just torn up the schedule. Someone had to stand in the doorway of the workshop tomorrow morning when the soldiers came—someone with papers, a reason, a face worth three extra minutes—because three minutes was the difference between the last sack reaching the boat and the last sack reaching a bonfire.

"I'll do it," Halvard said.

"No." Sela's voice cracked across the room like a type case dropped. "Father—"

"You think I'm leaving this press for them to feed to their furnace?" He wasn't angry; that was the terrible part. He was calm, settled, a man who had done his arithmetic days ago and was only waiting for the sum to arrive. "I'm sixty-one years old, girl. I set my first letter at seven. Everything I am is in those sacks. Let me spend the minutes I have left buying them their passage."

"And what do we tell the book?" Maren asked quietly. "The book with all our names in it? You'd be a name with no ending."

Halvard smiled then, and it was the saddest thing I saw in Ashmoor, sadder than the fire.

"Maren. Every name in that book belongs to someone who didn't get to finish their sentence. Why should mine be different?"

* * *

I volunteered for the door. I want that recorded plainly, without heroism, because it wasn't heroism—it was accounting. I was the courier. The oath said *carry*. Everything else in that workshop—every page, every name, every hour of candlelight—had become freight, and freight needs a road, and roads need time bought at their gates. I had spent my whole life being nobody at gates. Nobody was the one skill the empire had never managed to tax.

But Halvard refused me, and Sela backed him, and the argument that followed lasted until the first wagon rolled out at midnight—until I understood, standing in the cold with sack-ropes biting my shoulders, that we were all lying to each other about who would stand in that doorway, and that the lie was the only way any of us could lift the weight.

Four trips to the river. That was the plan. Four wagons disguised as grain deliveries, running the long way through the tannery lane, timed between patrol changes that Odile's own census schedules had taught me. The first wagon went out into the freezing dark at midnight and came back empty at one. The second left at two.

At ten minutes past two, while the second wagon was still on the road, someone hammered on the shutter.

Three knocks. Pause. Two knocks. The courier sign—but wrong, reversed, the way a stranger gets a handshake almost right. Sela and I froze over the type cases. Halvard blew out two of the three candles and moved to the shutter with a pry bar held low against his thigh.

"Who carries the post for Master Quill?" he called softly.

A pause. Then a voice I knew—young, cracked, terrified.

"Pike. The chandler. He says—I have to talk to the printer. Please. Before they come for me."

Corren. The apprentice. The informant, if informant he was, standing outside in the ash-smelling dark asking to be let into the room he had perhaps sold.

Sela looked at me. Halvard looked at the shutter. Outside, far off toward the square, a patrol lantern swung its slow arc, and somewhere beyond it the second wagon creaked its heavy way toward the river carrying two hundred names apiece in every sack.

"He could be bait," Sela breathed. "Open that shutter and we hang the whole book."

"He could be sixteen years old," Halvard said, "who made the worst mistake of his life and watched it burn down half a street."

The knocking came again, faster now, desperate. "Please. They'll question me at dawn. I heard them. I heard the sergeant say the courier's name."

My blood went cold in a way the winter had not managed all season.

"What name?" I called through the wood, and my voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect.

A pause. Then, small and wretched:

"Quill. But that's not—it isn't right, sir. I checked the garrison list tonight, after the fire. The warrant doesn't say Quill." Another pause, and I heard him swallowing fear. "It says Dunmore. Kestrel Dunmore. Courier of the Republic of Vell. Dead eight years, according to their records."

Inside the workshop, the last candle guttered, and no one moved to trim it.

Halvard's hand found the shutter bar. Sela caught his sleeve. And I stood between them with my father's name in a stranger's mouth and my real name, for the first time in eight years, spoken aloud in the open air of a town that was about to burn.

"Open it," I said. "We haven't got until dawn anymore."
