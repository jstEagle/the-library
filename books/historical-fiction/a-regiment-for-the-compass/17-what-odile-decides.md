## 17. What Odile Decides

The warrant arrived at dusk, folded in oilcloth and smelling of lamp-black and other men's ambition. Odile Vane read it twice at her desk in the customs house, by the light of a candle she had been allotted and had learned to make last. *By order of the Provisional Authority of the Ashmoor District: seizure of premises, printing apparatus, and all seditious material; execution of same by fire; supervision thereof assigned to Census Taker First Class O. Vane.*

Supervision thereof. As if a book were a rat to be drowned.

She set the paper down and listened to the building settle around her. The customs house had been built by republicans who believed in straight lines and tall windows; the empire had filled it with clerks who believed in neither, and now it groaned at night like an old man made to carry a younger man's load. Somewhere above her, a colleague was stamping documents with the slow, contented rhythm of a man who would never be asked what he thought about anything.

Odile had spent eleven years believing that order required truth. She had written that sentence once, as a young appointee, in the margin of her first field ledger, and she had never crossed it out. It had seemed so clean then. The census was a mirror held up to a town; you recorded what was there; the record was the thing itself. She had caught discrepancies the way other people caught colds—faithfully, methodically, with a certain pride. The grain clerk whose hands were ink-stained. The widow who claimed two children and bought bread for four. The chandler's apprentice whose shoes were too good for his wages. Each discrepancy was a thread, and she had followed them because following threads was the only honest work left in Vell.

And now the threads led to a workshop full of names, and the Authority's answer was to burn the names and give her a torch to hold.

She turned the warrant over. On the back, in her superior's cramped hand: *Three days. Do not delay. Do not embellish. Do not think.*

The last instruction was the most honest thing anyone in the Provisional Authority had ever written to her.

* * *

She walked home through the square the long way, past the chandler's shop, past the boarded window where the fire had climbed too far two nights ago and left a scorch like a black wing on the brickwork. The town smelled of wet ash and tallow and the river's iron breath. In the ration line outside the granary, people stood in the particular silence of those who have stopped expecting anything—not hope, not rescue, just bread. Odile knew that silence. She had catalogued it, once, in a report no one had read, under the heading *Civic Temperament, Winter Quarter.*

Her lodgings looked out over the square, which meant they also looked out over the window of the room the grain clerk rented from the widow Corvane. She had watched that window for weeks without admitting she was watching it. Tonight there was a light behind the shutter—a thin blade of it—and beneath it, faintly, if you stood very still on the cobbles and let the wind die, the sound of something rhythmic and patient. Not a press. Presses she knew; she had inventoried three of them in her career. This was smaller, faster, human: hands working. Collating, maybe. Folding. A town quietly making a book while the empire sharpened its match.

Odile went inside and did not light her lamp. She sat in the dark with the warrant on her knees and let herself do the one thing her superior had forbidden.

She thought.

Here was what thinking produced: the record Halvard Brann was printing would burn. That was certain. Warrants of this kind did not fail; the soldiers came with pitch and pikes, the presses went into the street, the type went into the crucible, and by evening there was a warm smell where a truth had been. If she supervised faithfully, she would stand in the doorway with her ledger open and enter into the historical register of the Ashmoor District: *one workshop, destroyed; four hundred volumes, destroyed; persons detained per schedule.* Her handwriting would be neat. It was always neat. And somewhere downstream, in some archive of the new regime, a clerk with her own hunger for pattern would find her entry and feel nothing at all, because the entry would contain everything except what mattered.

Unless the record survived. Unless somewhere beyond the river, in a house she would never see, a copy existed with her name in it—not the name the empire used, *Census Taker First Class*, but her name, the one her mother had chosen, attached to what she had actually done and actually known. She had spent her whole career making other people legible. No one had ever offered to make *her* legible. It was a shameful thing, how much she wanted it. She sat with the shame the way she sat with discrepancies: turning it over, testing its edges, refusing to pretend it wasn't there.

There was also this, and she made herself look at it plainly: delaying the warrant would not save her if it failed. A forged date on a transit slip, a misfiled seal, a "clerical error" requiring re-approval from the district office—these bought a night, perhaps a day. Then the error would be found, because errors were found; finding them was her own profession turned against her. She could see the inquiry already, the tidy room, the man with her file open, asking in a voice of genuine puzzlement how a census taker of her precision could have misdated a document. There was no answer she could give that would not be worse than silence.

So it was not a calculation. She understood that finally, sitting in the dark with the ash-smell coming under her door. Calculations weighed outcomes, and every outcome here ended her career or ended the book. What remained when the calculation failed was choice—the raw, unledgered kind—and she found, examining herself as carefully as she'd ever examined a grain receipt, that she had already made it. Perhaps weeks ago, the night she flagged the ink-stained clerk and then said nothing further. Perhaps years ago, in the margin of her first field ledger, in ink she had never crossed out.

Order requires truth. Very well. Then tomorrow night, when the presses ran and the books went out, she would stand watch at the door—not as the empire's instrument, but as its opposite: a witness. And witnesses, she knew from eleven years of taking depositions, must sign their testimony.

First, though, there was the matter of the date.

* * *

She forged the error at her own desk, before dawn, with her own pen, because using anything else felt like cowardice dressed as caution. The technique was simple and she despised how simple: warrants moved through the district office by relay, and each relay stamped its receipt with a date. She took the third-stage receipt—the one from the garrison adjutant's clerk, a boy who drank at midday and pressed his seals crooked—and she warmed the wax over her candle until it loosened, lifted it, and reset it one day later than it should have read. Then she wrote the annotation that any careful official would write upon discovering such a fault: *Date sequence irregular. Return to district office for re-certification prior to execution.*

Irregular. Re-certification. Words like small doors closing quietly in a corridor. By the time the adjutant's office opened, corrected the slip, and re-stamped the warrant, the day would be gone. One night. Perhaps thirty hours if the river fog delayed the riders, and the river fog owed her nothing but she asked it anyway.

Her hand did not shake. That was the strangest part. Eleven years of exactitude, and it turned out exactitude was simply a skill, transferable in either direction, like a knife.

She sealed the warrant back in its oilcloth, marked it *RETURNED — CLERICAL FAULT*, and left it in the dispatch tray where the morning rider would find it. Then she put on her coat, took up her field ledger—for habit, for cover, for courage—and crossed the square in the grey hour when the lamps died and the gulls argued over the fish market refuse and no soldier with any sense was awake.

The grain clerk's window was dark. But the door below it opened before she knocked, which told her the clerk had been watching her cross the square, which told her the clerk understood exactly what kind of morning this was.

"You're early," Kestrel Dunmore said. Up close, without the gate's distance between them, the disguise had worn thin as old varnish. The eyes were courier's eyes: flat, quick, always measuring the exits. The hands were a compositor's hands now, ghosted blue-black at the cuticles. "Or I'm late. Which is it?"

"Neither." Odile kept her voice level, the voice she used for depositions, the voice that made frightened people believe the world still had rules. "May I come in? Or would you rather we conducted this interview on the step, where the patrol can enjoy it?"

Something flickered across the courier's face—fear, yes, but underneath it a hard little spark of humor, and Odile thought: *there it is, the discrepancy I've been chasing since the gate.* People who lied well were always slightly amused by their own lies. It was the tell beneath all tells.

Inside, the room was bare and cold and smelled of ink. A crate stood against the wall, half-packed with folded signatures of paper, and Odile did not let herself look at the topmost page longer than courtesy allowed. Long enough to see columns of type. Long enough to see a name.

"You know why I'm here," she said.

"I know why you *were* supposed to be here." Kestrel leaned against the crate, arms folded, blocking it with their body without seeming to. "The warrant comes tomorrow. Or it doesn't."

"The warrant is in the dispatch tray with a false date on its seal, awaiting re-certification from a district office forty miles upriver." She said it flatly, watching for the flinch, and got instead a long stillness—the stillness of a person recalculating everything. "I bought you one night. Possibly two, if the fog holds. I will not buy more. I can't."

"Why?" It was not hostile. That was somehow worse. Kestrel asked it the way she asked her questions: wanting the true answer, not the useful one.

"Because I have spent my career writing down what happened to people," Odile said, "and I discovered last night that I am employed by men whose entire art is making sure nothing that happens gets written down. I was ordered to burn a record of the dead. Not seize it—burn it. There is a difference, and the difference is that seizure leaves something to be stolen back." She heard her own voice tighten and let it. "I am not asking your forgiveness. I don't want it. Forgiveness is for people who are sorry, and I am not sorry for anything except how late I've been."

Kestrel studied her for a long moment. Outside, a gull screamed over the market, and somewhere a shutter banged, ordinary sounds, the sounds of a town that didn't yet know it had one night left.

"What do you want, Vane?"

"My name in your book."

The words came out of her more nakedly than she intended, and she stood in the cold room and let them lie there between them, unretouched.

"Not my rank. Not the empire's version of me. My name, Odile Vane, and beside it what I did: I counted this town for the empire, and I found the discrepancies, and I reported the workshop—I did that, write it down, I won't have it prettied—and then, when they gave me the torch, I falsified a government seal to buy you the night you're standing in. All of it. The honest shape of me, which is not a good shape, but it is the true one." She took a breath. "Halvard Brann prints testimony. I have testimony. I am offering to become a witness in a court that doesn't exist yet, run by laws nobody's written, and the only sentence it can pass is to remember me accurately."

"And if the book burns anyway?" Kestrel said quietly. "If the barge is stopped at the checkpoint, if the fog lifts, if I'm dead in the water by Friday—then your name goes down with mine and Halvard's and Sela's, in smoke, and the only record of any of us is your own census, which says we were seditious printers and a lying grain clerk. Is that justice? Because I've carried enough paper to know what archives do, Vane. They outlive everyone and forgive no one."

"Then I'll have chosen wrong," Odile said, "and it will have cost me everything, for nothing, and I'll have done it anyway." She met the courier's flat grey eyes. "You think I haven't run that ledger? I ran it all night. Every column ends the same way. So I closed the ledger." She allowed herself, briefly, the smallest motion of her shoulders—not a shrug, something older than a shrug. "Some things aren't calculations. I'm told you've recently learned this."

Something moved behind Kestrel's eyes—the crack she had seen forming for days, the grief the courier had been carrying like contraband. It didn't open. But it shifted.

"Halvard decides what goes in the book," Kestrel said at last. "Not me. I collate."

"Then take me to Halvard."

"It's not safe—"

"Nothing," Odile said, "has been safe since the day I asked you your mother's maiden name at the gate and you lied to me beautifully. We passed safe a long time ago, both of us. All that's left is true or untrue." She lifted her field ledger, her badge of office, the thing she had served for eleven years, and set it down on the crate of printed pages, deliberately, like laying down a weapon. "Take me to him. Let him decide if my name is worth setting in type. If he says no, I'll go back and lead the raid myself, and I won't sabotage it. That's the bargain. You get my night; he gets my name; the book gets the truth about me or it gets nothing."

Kestrel looked at her for a long time. Then at the ledger on the crate. Then, through the shutter's seam, toward the river, where the fog was doing what fogs do—lying low, keeping its counsel, promising nothing.

"One night," Kestrel said finally. "And you walk behind me, and you keep your hands visible, because Sela sets type by touch in the dark and she'll know you're a stranger before you clear the stair." A pause, and then, grudgingly, almost against their will: "Your seal work was clean. The date. I saw the warrant copy. Clean work."

"I know," Odile said. "It's the only dishonest thing I've ever done perfectly. I intend to make it count."

They went out together into the grey morning, past the scorched wing-mark on the chandler's wall, down toward the river where the fog waited like an unwritten page. Behind them, in the dispatch tray of the customs house, a false date ticked silently toward discovery. Ahead of them, in a cellar behind a chandler's shop, a press stood oiled and ready, and Halvard Brann's candles were already lit—as if the old man had known, the way printers know things, that tonight the book would need every hour the darkness could give them.

Thirty hours. Four hundred copies. One name waiting to be set in type.

It would have to be enough.
