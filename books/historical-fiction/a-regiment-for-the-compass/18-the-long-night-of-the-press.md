## 18. The Long Night of the Press

The candles were lit at dusk, all of them at once, as if the workshop had decided that if it was going to die tomorrow, it would do so in full light.

Halvard had bought out the chandler's remaining stock—every stub and taper, every coil of rushlight—before the fire took half the shop. He stood them along the composing stone in ranks of clay saucers until the room glowed like a chapel, and their light trembled on the walls where forty years of ink ghosts hung: the shadowed outlines of posters long since pulled down, proclamations of the old Republic layered beneath imperial edicts layered beneath older proclamations still. Ashmoor's whole argument with itself, written on the plaster.

"We print through the night," Halvard said, rolling his sleeves to the elbow. His forearms were corded with the particular strength of a man who has pulled a press lever ten thousand times a day for decades. "Four hundred copies. We have perhaps two hundred finished. The garrison will come when they come."

"And if they come before dawn?" Sela asked. She was already seated at her case, fingers moving over the type with a blind man's certainty, though she could see perfectly well by candlelight. She worked in the dark out of habit, she'd told me once—the habit of a girl who had grown up setting type while her father slept, so as not to wake him, and had never stopped.

"Then we will be interrupted," Halvard said, "and we will resume when they leave, or we won't." He said it the way he said everything, without decoration, and I understood then that this was his version of a prayer.

I took my place at the collating table, the one nearest the door because Odile insisted the courier stand where he could run. She was positioned at the shuttered window with a view of the square, dressed not in her census gray but in plain wool, her ledger—her real one, the one of small doubts—locked away somewhere I did not know. Her forged clerical error had bought us this night. Somewhere in the garrison's files, the seizure warrant for Brann's workshop sat misdated by a week, waiting for a clerk who would discover the mistake sometime around midmorning.

"One night," she had said, when she handed me the plan through the cellar hatch. "Don't thank me. I haven't decided what I am yet."

* * *

The press began at full dark.

There is no sound in the world like a hand press working its purpose. The great oak frame creaked and settled like a ship taking sea; the screw turned with a low groan; and then the platen came down with a thump you felt in your sternum, in the floorboards, in the fillings of your teeth—and lifted again, and there lay a page of the Ashmoor Record, wet and gleaming, four hundred years of a republic's testimony pressed into rag paper.

Halvard worked the lever. Sela fed him sheets. I pulled pages from the tympan and laid them in rows on drying lines strung across the rafters, and Odile watched the square.

We fell into rhythm within the hour. That is the strange mercy of necessary work—it takes your body first and asks questions later. My hands learned the collating sequence: folio, gathering, quire, stitch. My back learned the ache of bending to the drying lines. My mind, mercifully, went quiet for the first time since Ashmoor's gate, since Odile's question about my mother's maiden name, since everything.

But the quiet didn't hold. Nothing holds, on a night like that. Between pulls of the press, between armfuls of paper, the words on the pages got into me.

I had read the manuscript two nights ago—I had broken my oath over it by candlelight in the loft above the chandler's, weeping over my father's name in the testimony of a woman who had been arrested beside him—and now the book came off the press in pieces, and each piece passed through my hands. Ledgers of the disappeared. Depositions taken in cellars. Grain requisition figures that proved the engineered famine of Vellmark was policy, not accident. The census codes I had carried across three provinces without reading had unlocked all of it, and here it was, made ordinary, made paper, made *print*.

A courier does not read. A courier carries. But a printer makes, and a collator touches every page twice, and somewhere in the third hour I stopped being a courier and understood that I had become part of the thing itself.

"You're crying onto the folios," Sela said quietly, not looking up from her case. "Stop it. The ink runs."

I laughed—a cracked, exhausted sound—and wiped my face on my shoulder. "How can you tell?"

"Your hands shook when you laid that last gathering down. And you've been breathing wrong for an hour." She slid a line of type into her composing stick, upside down and backward, letters I could never have sorted in a year. "My father says grief is just love with nowhere to put itself. So put it in the book. That's what it's for."

* * *

Past midnight, the candle flames leaned all together toward the window, and Odile's voice came low from the sill: "Patrol. Four men. Crossing the square."

We killed the lights—not all of them; Halvard kept one shielded behind the press—and stood in the dark listening to boots on frozen mud. They paused outside. Someone laughed. A flask passed hand to hand. Then they moved on toward the taverns on Tanner's Row, their voices fading into the ordinary noise of an occupied town drinking against the cold.

Odile exhaled slowly. In the shielded candlelight I saw her face, and it was not the face of a woman standing watch over sedition. It was the face of a clerk doing her job very well.

"Your superiors think you're home asleep," I said.

"My superiors think I'm drafting the final annex to the town register." She turned back to the window gap. "Which I am. In a sense. Every name in that book is in my register too, now. I cross-referenced them myself."

"Why?" I asked it plainly, finally. "You believe in order. In truth. You told me—you told me the empire's sin isn't lying, it's *burning*. So why burn your own career for this?"

She was quiet a long moment. Below us, Halvard pulled the lever; the platen fell and rose; another page joined its brothers on the lines.

"Because I asked a thousand people what their mothers' maiden names were," she said, "and every answer was true, and none of it mattered. I built the most accurate record in the province and it recorded nothing but compliance." Her voice stayed level, clerical, which somehow made it worse. "This book lies to no one. If order is worth anything, it's worth *this* order. Names to facts. Facts to witnesses. Witnesses who signed." She glanced at me. "And because someone has to write down what happens tonight, and I appear to be the only person in Ashmoor with a functioning pen."

* * *

At the fourth bell past midnight, Sela called us to the stone.

"The last page," she said. "It needs deciding. Now. The forme closes in an hour."

We gathered around her case. The last page of the Record was reserved—we had always known—for the names. Not the dead; they filled three hundred pages already. These were the living: everyone who had set, printed, carried, hid, or saved the book. Halvard Brann. Sela Brann. Kestrel Dunmore, which would be the first time my true name appeared anywhere outside my own memory in eleven years. And Odile Vane, whose name I had argued into the list over Halvard's flat refusal, and whose testimony—her account of the census, the registers, the burning orders—was already bound into the appendix.

"There should be one more," I said.

Halvard looked up from the press. "Who?"

"Tomas Ellery."

The chandler's apprentice. The informant—or the boy we had *believed* was the informant, the one whose nervous questions and late-night absences had nearly gotten us raided twice, the one whose diversionary fire had gone wrong and cost half his master's shop and moved the soldiers' timetable up by a day. Nineteen years old. Frightened out of his wits since the fire, hiding in the cellar under the chandler's ruin, certain we would give him to the garrison or worse.

"He informed," Halvard said. Not angrily. Weighing.

"He confessed. He wept. He set the fire to help us, badly, because he panicked—he wasn't selling us out, he was trying to draw the patrol off our scent after he realized what he'd done reporting us. And he's nineteen, and he's terrified, and if this book is what it claims to be—" my voice caught, and I steadied it "—if it's really a record of what happens when fear gets written down as fact, then his name goes in it. As it happened. Forgiven."

"Forgiven by whom?" Halvard asked. "You don't have the authority."

"No one does. That's rather the point." I looked at Sela. Her hands rested on the rim of her case, ink-blackened to the second knuckle, absolutely still.

"He held the door during the last raid," she said softly. "When the matrices were in the cellar and I couldn't move fast enough. He stood in the stairwell and lied to a sergeant with his voice shaking the whole time. He was terrible at it." She almost smiled. "He was terrible at everything. Lying, informing, arson. Maybe forgiveness is just noticing that some people are bad at being enemies."

Halvard stood a long time, the press lever loose in his grip, candlelight moving on his face. Then he nodded once, curtly, the way he approved a proof.

"Set it," he said. "Tomas Ellery. And under the names—" he looked at me "—you wanted words?"

I had wanted them for days without admitting it. I gave them to Sela letter by letter, watching her sort them backward into the stick:

*Carried by hand. Set by night. Witnessed by friend and enemy alike. Let every name here be read aloud, and let the reading be the debt repaid.*

Sela locked the forme. Halvard pulled the first proof of the last page and held it to the candle, reading it upside down and backward as printers do, and something in his shoulders loosened that I had never seen loosen before.

"It's true," he said, as if surprised.

"That's the whole trick," Odile said from the window. "Now make four hundred more."

* * *

What follows in my memory has no hours in it. Only motions.

The press speaking its two-note sentence—groan, thump; groan, thump. Sela feeding paper, her lips moving as she counted gatherings in a whisper. Halvard's forearms shining with sweat despite the cold, his breath smoking in the candlelight. The drying lines filling, page below page below page, the workshop's ceiling becoming a second sky hung with the republic's testimony, and walking beneath it felt like walking under a roof made of voices.

My hands stopped being mine somewhere in the deep of the night and became the book's hands. Collate, gather, square, pass. I touched my father's name so many times that by the hundredth copy the grief had worn smooth, the way a coin wears, not smaller—clearer. I could read his arrest record without stopping. I could set my jaw against the lie my family had lived—*we are neutral, we are careful, history happens elsewhere*—and feel it break cleanly, like ice at thaw, and not drown.

Once, near the fifth bell, I found Sela asleep upright at her case, a piece of twelve-point type still held in her fingers like a relic. I did not wake her. I set her next line myself, badly, checking each character against the specimen sheet three times, and when she woke twenty minutes later she looked at the composed line, looked at me, and said nothing—but she left my line in the forme. It appears in four hundred copies. Somewhere in the middle of the appendix there is a sentence set by a courier's hands, slightly crooked, and only three people alive know which one it is.

Once, near the sixth bell, Odile came away from the window and stood at the drying lines among the hanging pages, reading them in silence, top to bottom, like a woman walking a gallery. When she reached the testimony of the Vellmark survivors—the famine ledgers, the requisition orders bearing imperial seals—she reached out and touched one page, just the once, with two fingers, the way you touch a wound to learn if it's yours.

"I filed requisition reports," she said. "Vellmark district. I verified the figures. I thought—I was taught that accuracy was integrity. That if the numbers were right, the work was clean." She straightened the page on its line, a small clerk's gesture, useless and precise. "The numbers were right. That was the horror of it. Right numbers, all the way down, into the graves."

"Come on the boat," I said. I hadn't meant to say it yet. "When we cross. There's room."

She looked at me for a long moment, and in the candlelight her face was younger than her uniform had ever let it be, and more tired than her youth should allow.

"Ask me again at the river," she said. "If I've earned the asking by then."

* * *

The last copy came off the press as the sky behind the shutters turned the color of dirty pewter—that flat, sourceless light that comes before a winter sunrise.

Halvard pulled the final sheet, examined it against the candle, and nodded. Sela unlocked the forme and distributed the type back into the case, letter by letter, the way you return borrowed things. I collated the last gathering. We sewed the final copy together with waxed thread by the last candle, and Halvard pressed his thumbprint into the wet ink of the colophon beside the printed ones—an old printer's mark, he said, older than any license—and then it was done.

Four hundred copies of the Ashmoor Record. Bound, stacked, wrapped in oilcloth. Hidden beneath grain sacks in the cellar, waiting on carts, waiting on the barge, waiting on the forged warrant to fail and the garrison to come.

We stood in the wreck of the workshop—wax puddles, paper scraps, the sweet reek of ink and tallow—and none of us spoke. There was nothing left to say that wasn't already in type.

Then Halvard began snuffing candles, one by one, methodical as ever.

"Rest," he said. "All of you. Eat something. The barge loads at noon, and the soldiers will come tomorrow morning, and between those two facts there is work that requires steady hands." He looked at me last, and for the first time since the chalk sign on the chandler's wall, since the codes passed hand to hand without a word, he smiled—small, tired, entirely human. "You carried well, courier."

Outside, the sky was going pale over the rooftops, and the frost on the square glittered like a field of unsorted type. I stepped into the street with the cold hitting my face like water, and that was when I saw them.

Soldiers at the far end of the square. A dozen or more, breath steaming, bayonets catching the first light. And at their head, hurrying, furious, a garrison officer with a paper in his fist—the warrant, undelayed, discovered early—and behind him, pale as the frost, marching at bayonet point with her hands tied with signal cord:

Odile Vane.
