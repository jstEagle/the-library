## 19. Burning Bright

We finished at half past four in the morning, when the candles had burned down to their collars and the air in the workshop was so thick with ink and oil and human breath that it tasted like a thing you could chew.

Four hundred copies of the Ashmoor Record, bound in grey board, sewn with linen thread, stacked in grain sacks that smelled honestly of chaff because they had held grain until three days ago. Sela pressed her palm flat against the last stack as though taking its pulse. Halvard stood over the press with his hands hanging at his sides, ink to the elbow, looking like a man who had set down a weight so large he no longer knew the shape of his own body without it.

"It's done," Sela said, and her voice cracked on the second word, the way a voice does when it has been holding a note too long.

"It's done," Halvard agreed. Then he looked at me, and I saw something settle in his face — not peace, exactly. Decision. The particular stillness of a man who has already argued with himself all night and won.

I did not understand what I was seeing then. I want to be honest about that, even now. I thought he was tired. I thought we were all tired, and that tiredness looked different on different faces. I was counting sacks. There were forty-one. I had mapped the route to the quay twice in my head, the turns, the sentry posts, the stretch along the tannery wall where the ice would creak underfoot. I was a courier again, finally, wholly — carrying something that mattered, through a town that wanted it dead.

"Rest," Halvard said. "An hour, no more. Odile says the warrant lands at seven."

We slept where we stood, more or less. Sela curled among the empty type cases with her cheek against a forme of loose letters, and even asleep her fingers moved faintly, setting lines no one would ever read. Odile had gone an hour before, back across the square to her office, to her desk, to the small theater of paperwork she would perform this morning — the forged error, the delayed warrant, the one night she had bought us with her own ruin. I lay down on the floor near the door because someone had to hear footsteps, and because lying near doors is a habit couriers learn the way sailors learn to sleep in storms.

I did not sleep. I listened to the building breathe.

You live inside a place for two weeks and it stops being a place. The workshop had its own weather: the damp cold rising from the stone floor, the dry heat pooling near the drying racks, the smell of linseed oil and hot metal and the sweetish rot of old paste. It ticked and settled like a ship. Somewhere in the walls lived a mouse that Sela had named Compositor, and around four fifty I heard it moving behind the plaster, industrious, setting its own tiny pages.

At five twenty, Halvard got up.

I heard him cross the floor — I knew his step by then, the drag of his left heel — and I heard him doing things I could not immediately parse. Metal against metal. The scrape of the lamp-oil jugs being gathered. The soft, deliberate sound of a man arranging objects in order of use.

"Halvard," I said.

"Sleep, courier."

"What are you doing?"

A pause long enough for me to sit up. In the dark his outline was just a darker dark, but I felt him looking at me, and I understood, in the way you understand a fall only after it has happened, what the jugs were for.

"No," I said.

"The warrant comes at seven," he said, mild as a man discussing bread. "With or without Odile's clerical mistake. She bought us a night, not a pardon. And a press that falls into their hands intact is worth more to them than a press that burns — they'll print from it. Proclamations. Denunciations. Lists." He said *lists* the way other men said a curse word. "This shop has printed truth for eleven months. It will not print lies after I'm gone. That's not sentiment. That's typography."

"You can come with us. Forty-one sacks, there's room—"

"There is room for forty-one sacks and not one gram more, and every gram must be grain if anyone looks." His voice stayed gentle. That was the terrible thing about Halvard Brann; he could say anything gently, as though he were reading it aloud to a child by lamplight. "And there are men in this town whose names are in that book who will hang if the garrison follows that barge too closely. A burning shop draws every eye in Ashmoor to Ashmoor. Smoke is the best alibi ever invented. You'll slip down the river under cover of everyone watching my roof."

"That's not a plan, that's arithmetic with your life in the denominator."

"Yes," he said. "That's what arithmetic is."

Sela was awake. I don't know how long she'd been awake; perhaps she never slept that night at all. She stood between the type cases with her arms wrapped around herself, and she didn't argue either. That frightened me more than anything. Daughters argue. Daughters rage and bargain and swear they'll never forgive. Sela just stood there, already grieving, which meant she had done all of that days ago, alone, while I was learning to set type beside her and thinking we were racing the soldiers together.

"Papa," she said quietly.

"Sela." He crossed to her and took her face in his ink-blackened hands, and I turned away, and then made myself turn back, because some things you have no right to look away from. "Eleven months ago I asked myself what a printer owes the dead. Not the living — the dead. Because the living can run, and hide, and start again in another province under another name. But the dead can't carry their own names anywhere. Someone has to do it for them." He touched her forehead, her mouth, her hands — a printer's blessing, each part named for its work. "You're the compositor now. Whatever survives of this trade in Vell, it survives in your fingers. Don't let them stiffen. Don't let them forget."

"I won't."

"And you—" He turned to me, and I braced for manipulation, for the confession of another gamble placed on my blood, for one more way this old man had used me. Instead he simply bowed his head, briefly, the way a master bows to a journeyman who has passed the trial piece. "Courier. You carried. No oath ever written asks more than that. What you chose to do past the edge of the oath — that was yours, freely given, and I know the difference. Go now. Take my daughter and take the book and don't look back at the fire, because fire makes liars of us all. It makes us think we see faces in it."

* * *

We went out through the cellar, up the stair into the chandler's ruined half, out into streets blue-grey with the hour before dawn. Frost furred the pump handles. Our breath hung around us like gauze. Behind us, through the broken shutter of the chandler's loft, I saw one candle move across the workshop window — Halvard making his last rounds, or lighting his lamps so the garrison would believe the shop still worked, still slept, still planned to be there at seven to be seized.

The sacks went onto the barge in ones and twos, casual as morning errands. Marta the fishwife took two under her shawl-wrapped arms and complained loudly about the price of rye. Old Vesey loaded three and told anyone listening that the miller was cheating him on weight and always had. Sela sat amidships wrapped in sacking like a sack herself, hollow-eyed, silent, her hands buried in her apron. I poled us off the wall into the current with hands that shook only after the boat stopped touching land.

Then I made the mistake Halvard had warned me against.

I looked back.

Ashmoor at dawn is a town built of pale brick and river fog, and the workshop's roofline is nothing — a low slate ridge behind the chandler's gable, a chimney, a hoist arm. You would never pick it out of a crowd. Which is why what happened next needed no landmark. At six minutes past six, while the sky was still the color of skimmed milk, the windows of the workshop lit orange from the inside, all at once, evenly, the way bread browns — and I understood that Halvard had not waited with a torch for the soldiers. He had laid his fire before we left. Oiled the floors, stacked the paper stock, opened every jug. He had arranged his own ending with the same care he gave a signature, and the dawn was merely the moment he had chosen to begin it.

By the time the first shout rose from the garrison gate, the smoke was black and climbing straight up in the windless cold, a column you could read from across the river, and every soul in Ashmoor came out of every doorway to look at it. Ration lines dissolved. Soldiers ran toward the square and were swallowed by the crowd's slow, obstinate, unhelpful gathering — people who did not run so much as drift, drift, drift toward the spectacle, filling the streets the way silt fills a channel. Marta shouted about her fish stall. Vesey quarreled with a corporal about right-of-way. The whole town, wittingly or not, became a wall of bodies between the garrison and the river, and under that smoke, under that noise, our barge slid out into the current with its forty-one sacks of grain that were not grain.

Sela watched the fire. I watched her watching it. Her face was lit orange from two hundred yards away, and she did not cry, and she did not look away, and when the roof beam fell — we heard it even out on the water, a groan like a felled tree, then a roar as the flames found the open air — she closed her eyes once, briefly, the way a compositor closes her eyes to check a line she knows by heart.

"He's still in there," she said. Not a question. She had known since before I did.

"Yes."

"Good," she said, in a voice like breaking ice, and then she wept without any sound at all, tears running straight down off her jaw into the river, and I put my arm around her and poled with the other hand and did not trust myself to speak.

Behind us the workshop stood forever by ceasing to stand. I have tried many times since to find a better sentence for it and I never have. The fire ate the presses, the cases, the formes, the paper, the paste, the eleven months of night shifts — and none of it mattered, because everything that mattered was under sacking in the hold, moving north with the current at the speed of a slow river, which turns out to be faster than any empire.

Soldiers reached the burning shop and milled at its edges, useless, heroic in the way of men hauling buckets to a furnace. Through the crowd, on the quay, I saw a figure in a census office coat standing very still, watching the smoke with her head tilted, and I knew it was Odile Vane, and I knew she was checking her watch against the warrant she had delayed, and I knew that whatever calculation was running behind those sharp eyes, it had ended with her standing on that quay instead of leading that charge. She did not look at the river. That was her mercy: she gave us the dignity of not being seen leaving.

The column of smoke bent east as the wind woke, and Ashmoor shrank behind us into a grey smudge crowned with black, and the sacks breathed their honest chaff-smell all around us, and somewhere under my feet, between the third and fourth sack, lay a book with four hundred names in it — including, now, the name of the man who had chosen to die rather than surrender a single letter of them.

Sela slept at last, mid-morning, her head against the gunwale, her fingers still moving faintly in her dreams, setting type.

I sat at the stern with the pole across my knees and watched the smoke thin to a thread, then to a memory, then to nothing, and I thought: *carry, never read*. Well. I had read. And what I carried now was heavier than paper — it was a town, a father, a girl's grief, an enemy's testimony, a printer's last arithmetic — and ahead of us, where the river narrowed between the white cliffs, the imperial checkpoint waited with its chain across the water and its ledger of names.

Every name in this boat was contraband. And the woman who had written most of them into her census was waiting at the border, and I did not yet know whether she would be holding the chain shut — or opening it.
