## 21. A Name Given Freely

The river gave us up gently.

I had expected the far bank to feel like an ending—the way a held breath ends, all at once, with a gasp—but it did not. The barge grounded itself in the shallows of a reed-choked inlet with the soft complaint of old timber, and the men who had been waiting for us waded out to their thighs in the cold to take the grain sacks down as if they were nothing more than grain. Which they were, and were not. Four hundred books slept beneath the barley, each one wrapped in oilcloth, each one warm from the press, smelling of ink and tallow and the smoke we had not yet stopped tasting.

Sela sat beside me at the tiller rail long after the work was done, her hands open in her lap, palms up, as though she still expected type to fall into them. Her fingers were cracked from the lye we'd used to scrub the workshop's traces from our skin, and there was still a crescent of black under the nail of her left forefinger that no amount of washing would reach. I knew because I had the same mark, on my right thumb. We had compared them by candlelight two nights before, like children comparing scars, and laughed until Halvard told us to be quiet, and then laughed again, quieter, inside.

"You should sleep," she said now.

"So should you."

"I will." She looked back across the river. The smoke had thinned to a gray smudge along the southern horizon, less than a smudge—a stain on the underside of the clouds, the way a bruise shows through cloth. "I keep thinking the fire is still going. I know it isn't. It can't be. There's nothing left to burn."

"There's something left," I said. "It's on this boat."

She turned her head and looked at me, and in the winter light her eyes were her father's eyes exactly: patient, skeptical, lit from underneath. Then she leaned against my shoulder and slept, sitting up, in the way of people who have gone too long without it, and I stayed very still so as not to wake her, and watched the reeds bend and straighten, bend and straighten, keeping time with a river that did not care what either of us had carried across it.

* * *

The house they took us to stood outside a village whose name I never learned, a low stone farmhouse with a roof of blue slate and a barn that smelled of apples and mice. Our hosts were a widow and her two grown sons, and they asked no questions, which was its own kind of eloquence. They fed us barley soup with more salt than anything else in it, and after Sela and the others went down to the barn to sleep among the hay, I sat alone at the kitchen table while the widow's son banked the fire, and I took out the one copy of the book that was mine to carry.

It was smaller than I remembered it being. In the loft above the chandler's shop, with four hundred of its brothers stacked around it, the manuscript had seemed vast—a thing with weight and mass and gravity, a thing that could pull a town into orbit around it. Alone, wrapped in plain paper on a stranger's table, it was just a book. Sewn signatures, pasteboards covered in unbleached linen, the title stamped in black on a spine that would fit in a coat pocket. *The Ashmoor Record.* Halvard had wanted something grander. Sela had overruled him. "Grand titles burn first," she said. "Plain ones get shelved and forgotten about, and forgotten things survive."

I set it on the table. The fire ticked. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard settled, the house breathing in its sleep.

The colophon was the last page but one. Sela had composed it herself, small type, centered, the way colophons have been set since before either of us was born—the printer's signature, the place, the season. It read:

*Set and printed at Ashmoor, in the year the Republic fell. The names of those who made this book follow.*

And then, in the order Sela had chosen—Halvard first, then herself, then the two journeymen who had escaped north, then Odile Vane, whose name she had insisted upon against every argument I made, until I understood that Sela was right and stopped making them—there was a blank line.

A blank line, and beside it, a space where a name should be.

Halvard had left it for me. He had said nothing about it, done nothing to draw my attention to it, simply left the space empty in the forme and printed four hundred copies with a gap where I ought to be, trusting—or gambling, which was Halvard's word for trusting—that I would fill it when I was ready. Or perhaps he hadn't trusted at all. Perhaps he had known that the choice was the point, that a courier who signed his own name to the cargo had become something else entirely, and that no argument of his could accomplish what a blank line and a pen could.

I found the pen the widow kept in a jar with the darning needles. I dipped it in the ink she used for marking linens, ordinary household ink, brown-black, nothing fine. My hand shook, and I was ashamed of it shaking. I had crossed a river under patrol lanterns with forged manifests in my boot. I had lied to a census taker twice and meant it both times. I had stood in a doorway while a man I loved like a father walked back into a burning building and closed the door behind him. And my hand shook over a page of paper, in a warm kitchen, in a country where no one was looking for me anymore.

Because that was what it was. Not fear. It was the shaking of a muscle that has been clenched too long and finally lets go. Since I was nine years old I had been Kestrel-no-surname, the grain clerk, the factor's assistant, the cousin from upriver, a name worn thin as a coin passed through too many hands. My mother taught me the trick of it before she taught me letters: *never say your whole name aloud, never write it, never answer to it in a crowd.* She had learned it from my father, or from whatever remained of my father after the soldiers came for him—the part of him that decided his family would survive by being no one, by being smooth and blank and forgettable, history passing over us like weather. I had built my whole life in the shelter of that erasure. I had been proud of it. I had called it discipline, craft, professionalism, and told myself the oath was sacred: carry, never read, never be read.

And here was the book that proved the lie of it. Here were four hundred copies of everything my father's silence had tried to prevent—not because he was a coward, I understood now, but because he believed, the way Odile had once believed, that survival was the only justice available, and he had spent his life paying for a justice that never arrived. His name was in these pages, testimony given in a sealed archive eleven years ago, unlocked by codes I had carried without reading, spoken by a man who had thought even then that someone, someday, might print them. He had written his truth down and sealed it away and erased himself from the world, and the empire had erased him anyway, and the only difference between his choice and mine was that mine ended here, at a kitchen table, with a pen in my hand and a blank line waiting.

History happens to other people. That was the first lesson of my childhood, and the last lesson of my oath, and it was a lie from beginning to end. History had happened to me in Ashmoor. It had happened in a doorway at sunrise, in the smell of hot lead and lamp oil, in a woman's hand waving a barge through a checkpoint while her career burned behind her. It was happening now, in this kitchen, in this ink.

I wrote my name.

*Kestrel Dunmore.*

Two words. Eleven letters. It took less time than the lie at the gate had taken, less time than any of the hundred false names I had spoken into the air of three provinces, and it cost more than all of them together, and it bought more. The ink soaked in and dried darker than it went on, and I sat looking at it the way you look at a wound you've decided not to hide, and I felt—there is no other word for it—*present*. Located. A person standing in a specific place at a specific time, accountable, findable. Visible. It was terrifying. It was the opposite of everything I had ever been trained to want. It felt like setting type: the letter seated true in its groove, the quoins tightened, the form locked and ready to take the pressure of the press. Ready to make an impression. That was all printing was, Sela had said once, teaching me in the dark by touch. Making an impression that lasts.

I let the ink dry before I closed the cover. Some things you do properly or not at all.

* * *

We dispersed the copies over nine days.

That was Sela's plan, and it was better than any I could have made, because Sela thought the way she set type—in matrices, in repetitions, in systems that could survive the loss of any single piece. No courier would carry more than three copies. No courier would travel the same road twice. No courier would know where the others had gone. The books would move outward from the farmhouse the way water moves through ground: silently, finding the cracks, surfacing where no one expected it.

I went south myself, toward the hill towns where the old republic's assizes had once been held, carrying six copies sewn into the false bottom of a grain pannier—because some ironies you keep, the way you keep a scar, as proof of what you survived. In the market town of Ferren I left two with a schoolmistress who had lost her post for refusing to teach from the imperial readers; she hid them behind a loose brick in her chimney and pressed my hand and said only, "They'll be read aloud. Every winter. I promise you that." In the village of Cold Harbour a defrocked priest took one and buried it in the churchyard under a stone with no name on it, and told me with a straight face that it was the only honest grave in the parish. A widowed ferryman took one wrapped in sailcloth. An innkeeper sewed one into the ticking of a mattress that traveling judges sometimes slept on, which Sela, hearing the story later, called the finest joke in the history of the trade.

By the end of the month, copies of *The Ashmoor Record* lay hidden in lofts and wells and chimneys and graveyards across three provinces, and no two of them within a day's ride of another, and no living person could have said where all of them were—including me, including Sela. That was the design. The empire could burn a workshop. It could burn a man. It could seize a town's register and rewrite a census and paste new proclamations over old notices until the walls of Ashmoor wore five years' worth of lies in layered skins like the rings of a tree. But it could not burn what it could not find, and it could not find four hundred truths scattered like seed. You cannot arrest a book that is everywhere and nowhere. You cannot interrogate a library that has dissolved into the population. Halvard had understood this at the end—I saw it in the way he fired the presses himself rather than let them be taken, denying them not the books but the *means*, forcing the record to live henceforth only in dispersed copies and human memory—and Sela had inherited the understanding whole, the way she inherited his hands.

Odile's testimony traveled too. Her account of the census, the gate, the grain clerk, the warrant she delayed with a forged clerical error—it was bound into the middle of the book between the archive testimonies and the list of the disappeared, typeset exactly like the rest, neither defended nor explained. When I argued, early on, that including a census taker's confession would hand the enemy a weapon, Sela had looked at me with her father's patience and asked one question: "Do you want a record, or do you want a monument?" I didn't answer. There was no answer that favored me. A monument flatters. A record tells everything, especially the parts that complicate the people it honors. Especially, perhaps *only*, those parts.

Some nights, in whatever barn or back room the roads gave us, I took the book out and read Odile's pages by candlelight, and it was like reading a letter from someone who had died, except she hadn't, and that was worse and better in equal measure. *I believed order required truth,* she had written. *I was half right. I intend to spend what remains of my life correcting the other half.* No apology. No plea. Just the ledger of her own small doubts, entered honestly, balanced at last.

* * *

On the last night before we separated—Sela north with the final copies, I west toward the coast and whatever waited there—I sat with her in the farmhouse kitchen, the same kitchen where I had written my name, and we divided what remained of the type matrices between two satchels. They weighed almost nothing, those little bars of lead and tin, each one bearing a single reversed letter, and together they weighed everything: the alphabet entire, packed in straw.

"Wherever you settle," Sela said, turning a capital D over in her fingers, "you'll need a press. Don't tell me where. Just—" She hesitated, which she almost never did. "Just put your mark in the colophon. The same one. So if anyone ever lays two copies side by side, a hundred years from now, they'll know the hands that set them were the same hands."

"The same hands," I agreed.

"And Kestrel." She looked up, and the firelight caught the ink-stain under her fingernail, the mark we shared, the badge neither of us could wash away and neither of us wanted to. "When they ask you your name—and someday, somewhere, someone will ask, that's how it always starts—give it to them. All of it. Freely."

"I will," I said, and discovered that I meant it, and that meaning it no longer frightened me, or rather that it frightened me in the good way, the way standing at the head of a press frightens you before the first pull.

In the morning we parted at a crossroads in the frost, two couriers again, but carrying a different cargo now—one that could not be taken from us without our consent, because it was written into us as thoroughly as into the pages. She went north. I went west. Behind us, in chimneys and churchyards and mattress-ticks across three provinces, four hundred names slept in the dark, waiting to be read aloud.

And in the margin of the last page of my own copy, below the colophon, below the name I had given freely, I added a single line in the widow's brown household ink, small enough that only someone looking for it would ever find it:

*Carried. Delivered. Read.*

Somewhere, in a future I would not see, an archivist would open this cover by lamplight and begin to catalog what survived—and the first thing they would find was not the empire's version of events, but ours.
