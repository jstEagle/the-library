## 1. The Weight of Being Unremarked

The bell at Vellamare's counting-house rang six times before the gulls woke, and Orsen Amberdale was already through the workshop door with his sleeves rolled and his eyes lowered, exactly as he had been for four thousand mornings give or take the ones the fever took. He did not count them aloud. Counting was a thing people noticed.

The workshop smelled the way it always smelled at first light: brine and lamp-oil and the sweetish rot of oyster shells heaped in baskets by the sorting benches, waiting for their pearls to be drawn out like small cold moons. Beyond the open shutters, the harbor was still gray water and gray mist, the masts of the Vellamare barges standing in rows like a congregation at prayer—a prayer, Orsen thought, that no one in this building had ever learned the words to.

He took his place at the third bench from the window. Third bench: close enough to the light that his work could be checked without the overseer crossing the room, far enough back that the light itself never fell on his face. He had chosen it twelve years ago, in his first week, when he was seven years old and already understood that in Vellamare's workshop a boy could be useful or visible, but not both.

The pearls came to him in lots, each lot wrapped in oilcloth and tagged with a lead seal and a slip of paper bearing a name. *Vessin. Corrow. Oduya.* The names of families who had sold—or been said to have sold—their inheritance pearls to the company, diving families whose boats were older than the warehouse walls. Orsen had learned long ago never to read the slips. His fingers knew how to turn a tag face-down on the bench without looking, the way a man's feet know the path home in the dark. He sorted by luster, by size, by the faint rose or green cast beneath the nacre, and he kept his eyes on the pearls themselves, which asked nothing of him except that he see them clearly.

That was the cruelty of it, though he had not had the words for the cruelty until perhaps his fifteenth year: the work demanded perfect sight. A pearl graded wrong by a single degree cost the company coin, and coin was the one thing Vellamare counted more carefully than names. So they had trained him to be an instrument—eyes like a jeweler's loupe, hands steady as tide—and instruments are not permitted histories. An instrument does not wonder whose grandmother wore this one to her own wedding. An instrument does not read the tag.

*Amberdale boy, no kin claimed.* That was what his indenture paper said. He had read it once, the day they signed him over, and never again. No kin claimed. As if kin were a thing you claimed off a shelf, and his family simply hadn't gotten there before closing time.

"Amberdale."

Orsen did not flinch. Flinching was visible. He turned his head the precise quarter-turn that said *I heard you and I am attentive and I am nothing.*

Overseer Doss Kettering stood at the end of the bench row, a broad man gone soft at the edges the way driftwood goes soft, with the permanent squint of someone who has spent thirty years looking for discrepancies. He held a ledger against his chest the way a priest holds a book, which in Vellamare was roughly what it was.

"You'll take the Halvard lot today," Kettering said. "High-grade. Master Quillon wants it graded before the auction season inventories."

"Yes, Overseer."

"The Halvard girl's father drowned last spring. Family's selling out." Kettering said it the way he said everything, flat as slate, but his eyes lingered on Orsen a half-second longer than the sentence required. "You know how it is. Boats come up empty."

Orsen knew how it was. He knew better than anyone in the room, and he knew that knowing it was the most dangerous thing about him. "I'll have it done by midday," he said, and bent back over his bench, and Kettering moved on down the row because there was nothing in Orsen to hold onto. No resentment showing. No curiosity. Nothing but a competent boy-shaped absence with excellent hands.

It had taken years to build that absence. It was, he sometimes thought while oiling the sorting trays at night, the finest work he had ever made, finer than any grade he'd ever assigned a pearl. He had carved himself hollow in all the places where a person shows.

The morning went on around him. The other sorters murmured about the auction season, about buyers coming up from the southern ports with velvet cases and hard mouths. The shell-baskets filled and emptied. Somewhere beyond the wall, a diver's wife sang a hauling song in the old cadence, low and rising, low and rising, and Orsen let it pass through him like weather because letting things pass through him was the whole of his craft.

But the Halvard lot sat under his hands by nine o'clock, and the pearls in it were beautiful in a way that made his chest hurt—deep-lustered, salt-white, matched in pairs the way inheritance pearls always were, bred into a family across generations until mother and daughter wore twins. Two of these were matched twins. He looked at them too long. He caught himself wondering who had worn the sister of this pearl, whether she was alive, whether she had wept when her father's boat came up empty, and he set the thought down the way he set down every thought that had a name attached to it.

Face-down. Don't look. Twelve years.

At midday he ate his ration on the loading step, alone, because eating alone was forgettable and eating in company required conversation and conversation required a self. The harbor lay bright now, and the divers' boats were coming in, hulls riding low, men and women hauling up baskets of shells with rope-burned hands. One of the old women on the nearest boat—gray-haired, broad-shouldered, singing the hauling song herself now—caught him watching and raised two fingers off her oar. Not a wave. A recognition. Maren Oduya had dived company waters forty years and had buried a husband in them, and she was the only person in the harbor who ever looked directly at Orsen Amberdale, which he both treasured and dreaded, the way you treasure and dread a mirror.

He raised his hand a fraction and put it down again.

The afternoon brought the inventory carts, and with them Master Quillon himself, the workshop factor, a thin man whose voice was soft enough that everyone leaned in to hear it, which Orsen suspected was the entire design. Quillon stopped behind the third bench and watched the Halvard grading for a long moment.

"Exceptional pairing," Quillon said softly. "You have a gift, boy. Do you know that? In twelve years you've never misgraded a lot."

"Thank you, Master Quillon."

"Never once." The factor tilted his head. "Funny thing, that. A gift like yours usually comes with questions. Where'd you learn to see like this?"

"My mother," Orsen said, and then heard the word leave his mouth like a bird escaping a cage he'd forgotten he'd left open. His mother. Dead eleven years in company waters. Not to be mentioned, not to be remembered, certainly not to be credited with anything.

Quillon's pale eyes rested on him. "Your mother," he repeated, tasting it.

"She—dived," Orsen managed. "For the company." And then, because invisibility was a muscle and the muscle was strong even when the man inside it was shaking: "She taught me to judge luster by lamplight. I was young. I don't recall much else."

"No," Quillon agreed softly, and something in the softness made Orsen's spine go cold. "No, I expect you don't. Records would show there wasn't much else to recall." He smiled without warmth and moved on down the row, leaving behind him a silence shaped exactly like Orsen's whole life.

Orsen finished the Halvard lot. His hands did not tremble; his hands never trembled; he had spent a decade teaching them not to, and they obeyed him the way good servants obey a kind master, out of loyalty rather than fear. He sealed the graded pairs into their case, turned the name-slip face-down without reading it, and set the case on the dispatch shelf with the others.

And only then—alone at the bench as the light went copper and the gulls settled—he noticed that one pearl remained loose in the tray. A twin without its sister. Small, deep-lustered, with a faint rose cast beneath the nacre that he knew the way a man knows a scar on his own body, because he had seen its match once, long ago, on a cord around a drowning woman's neck before the water took her and the company took everything else.

He should log it. He should call the overseer. He should be, for one more evening, the Amberdale boy with no kin claimed.

Instead, before he understood what his hands were doing, he closed his fist around it—and from somewhere above the drying racks, faint through the floorboards, came a sound no workshop should make after hours: a child, breathing.

* * *

He told himself it was nothing. Rats moved in the loft; cats; wind in the shell-husks. But the breathing had the small hitching rhythm of a sleeper trying to be silent, and silence-that-tries-to-be-silent was a language Orsen spoke fluently, having spoken it since he was seven years old. Someone above the drying racks was doing what he did. Someone was learning his choreography.

Kettering was still in the counting-house. Quillon's cart was still in the yard. If a child—a runaway, a thief, God knew what—was found in the workshop after hours, the penalties would fall on whoever kept the keys, and Orsen kept the keys, because giving keys to a boy with no kin and no history was safe; a boy with no history could not sell them.

He climbed the ladder slowly, and the loft smelled of dust and old oilcloth, and in the corner where the spare sorting cloths were stacked, a girl of perhaps six or seven years lay curled with her knees to her chin, asleep or pretending to be, and clutched in her fist against her chest—

A pearl pendant. Rose-cast. Deep-lustered.

Its sister was in his fist downstairs.

She opened her eyes. She did not cry out, which told him more than crying would have; children who cry out believe someone will come. This one looked at him the way he looked at name-slips—with the practiced blankness of someone who has already learned that being seen is the dangerous thing.

In her other hand, folded small and damp with her sweat, was a note. She held it out to him without a word, the way you surrender a weapon.

Three words, written in a hand he almost recognized from someplace deep and unforgivable:

*Forget her face.*

Orsen stood in the loft of the workshop that had erased him, holding a pearl that matched his mother's, looking at a face he was instructed to forget—and understood, with terrible clarity, that the choreography of his silence had just ended.
