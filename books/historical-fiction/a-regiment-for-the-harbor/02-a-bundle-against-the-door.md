## 2. A Bundle Against the Door

The lamp in Orsen Amberdale's room had a habit of guttering just past midnight, as though even the flame grew tired of being watched. He had learned to work by that dying light—mending nets he was not required to mend, oiling the hinges of a chest that held nothing but his own folded years. Twelve years in the harbor quarter taught a man to keep his hands busy after dark. Busy hands did not think. Busy hands did not remember.

He remembered anyway, tonight, without meaning to.

It was the wind off the water that did it. It came through the gap beneath his door carrying the smell of low tide and kelp rot, and for a moment—the length of a single breath—he was six years old again, standing on the shingle below his mother's boat while she sang the launching song with salt crusted white at her hairline. *Come home before the lamps go out,* she always said, and then she went down into the green dark with a knife and a basket and came up again with light held in her fist.

Orsen set down the hinge. He did not let himself finish the memory. Finishing it meant arriving at the part where she did not come up.

He blew out the lamp, lay down on his pallet fully clothed, as he always did—as if sleep were something he might be called away from—and listened to the workshop settle around him: the tick of drying timber, the far-off knock of hulls against pilings, the sea doing what the sea does whether anyone is erased by it or not.

He was nearly under when he heard it.

Not a sound, exactly. An absence of sound where there should have been one. The gulls that roosted along the workshop's eaves had gone quiet, and in their silence was something else—a small, wet, hitching breath, like a child trying very hard not to cry and failing by degrees.

Orsen sat up. Every instinct he had honed over twelve years told him the same thing: *stay down, stay small, stay unremarked.* Trouble at your door after midnight is company trouble, and company trouble belongs to the company. Let the night watch find whatever it is. Let someone whose name is still written somewhere deal with it.

But his feet were already on the cold boards, and his hand was already on the latch.

* * *

The fog had come in thick off the harbor, the kind that swallowed lamplight whole so that the streetlamps of Gullrow became smears of amber, like bruises seen through skin. The workshop loomed behind him, all shadow and salt-stained plank, and there—at the base of his door, tucked into the hollow of the threshold as carefully as an offering left at a shrine—was a bundle.

A blanket. Gray wool, good weave, company issue or stolen from someone who could afford better. And rising from it, the hitching breath again.

Orsen crouched. He knew, distantly, that he should call out, should wake the night warden two doors down, should do any of the ten sensible things a man in his position would do. A man in his position had no business with found children. Found children were questions, and questions had a way of being answered with ledgers.

Then the blanket shifted, and a face looked up at him.

She was perhaps five, maybe six—small for either, the way harbor children were small, the way hunger drew them down like tide drawing back from shore. Black curls matted flat on one side of her head. Cheeks streaked with dried tears and older salt. Her eyes were open now, enormous in the fog-dark, and they fixed on him with a wariness far too old for her face. She did not scream. She had been told not to scream; you could see it in the way she pressed her lips together, in the way her small fists pulled the blanket up to her chin like armor.

And against her chest, clutched in one grimy hand so tightly the cord had bitten a groove into her palm, was a pearl pendant.

Orsen's breath stopped in his throat.

He knew that pendant. Not this pendant—he had never seen this pendant—but the shape of it, the weight of it, the way the pearl sat in its cage of dull silver wire, wrapped twice at the top the way diving families wrapped their inheritance stones so no clasp could tear free underwater. His mother had worn one exactly like it for as long as he could remember. She had been wearing it the last morning he saw her alive, glinting at her throat above the neckline of her diving shift, catching the dawn like a second moon.

They had taken it from her effects. They had taken everything from her effects. He had signed a paper saying they could.

"Please," the girl whispered. Her voice was sand and rust. "I'm not supposed to talk."

"I know," Orsen heard himself say, which made no sense, and yet felt truer than anything he'd said in years.

There was a note pinned to the blanket. Small, cheap paper, already damp at its corners, ink bleeding slightly. Four words in a hurried, educated hand:

*Forget the face.*

Nothing else. No name. No explanation. Just the instruction, delivered with the same casual authority the Vellamare Trading Company used on every document it ever produced—as though forgetting were a service rendered, a courtesy expected, a line item in some account Orsen had never been permitted to read.

Forget the face.

His hands were shaking. He noticed this the way one notices rain—distantly, with irritation. Because here was the arithmetic, cold and simple, that twelve years had taught him: a child on the doorstep of a company man, with a note instructing silence, was not an accident. Someone had chosen him. Someone knew—or guessed—something about him that even he had never dared examine. And whoever left her might come back, and whoever wanted her forgotten might come looking, and the only safe thing, the only *survivable* thing, was to wake the warden, report the foundling, wash his hands, and return to being no one by morning.

That was the arithmetic. He had lived by it since he was eleven years old and signed away his name for bread and a bunk.

But the girl's eyes hadn't left his face, and in them was something that broke the sum apart entirely. Recognition—not of him, but of *kind*. The look of someone who had already been handed to strangers once tonight and had learned, in one terrible lesson, what hands could do. And her fist, still welded shut around the pendant, opened a fraction. Just enough. Enough that the pearl caught the distant smear of lamplight and threw it back, warm and pale and unmistakable, and Orsen felt twelve years of careful nothing crack down the middle like a shell struck wrong.

*Get inside,* he thought, and didn't know if he meant her or himself.

* * *

The loft above the drying racks was the one place in the workshop no one looked at, because no one looked at anything Orsen touched. That had been the design of his whole life: make yourself the furniture. Furniture isn't searched. Furniture isn't questioned. Furniture survives.

He carried her up the ladder with the blanket bundled tight, and she weighed almost nothing—harbor-nothing, hunger-nothing—and she did not fight him, which frightened him more than screaming would have. Children who don't fight have already learned how the world works.

The loft smelled of brine and cedar and the faint sweetness of shell polish. Below them, rows of harvested pearls rested in their sorting trays, waiting for the auction season, each one catalogued and owned and accounted for. Above them, in the dark, sat the only thing in the building that belonged to no ledger at all.

He settled her into the nest of spare sailcloth behind the stacked drying frames, where the warmth rising off the curing racks below would keep the chill out. She watched him do it with those old eyes, silent as agreed.

"You can't make a sound," he whispered. "Not in the day. Not ever, if you hear boots on the stairs. Can you climb down if you need to?"

She nodded.

"If anyone comes—anyone—you're cargo. You're a bundle of netting. You're nothing." The words tasted like ash and familiarity. He was teaching her his own survival as if it were a lullaby.

Her eyes flicked down—to his hands, he realized, and then to the pendant in hers. Slowly, deliberately, she turned it toward him. Showing him. Asking, in the only language she had left, *do you know this?*

Orsen looked at the pearl turning in the dark, and his mother's voice rose unbidden through the fog of twelve drowned years: *It comes down the mother-line, little fish. Wrapped twice so the sea can't steal it. As long as we wear them, we know who we are.*

He had signed that knowing away. He had signed until the ink ran dry and the clerk smiled and said *there's a good lad.* And here it was, come back to his door in a gray wool blanket small enough to carry under one arm.

"No," he lied softly, because lying to her was the only protection he had left to give. "Never seen one like it."

Something in her face fell—but she nodded, accepting, the way children accept the lies of adults, having no other choice. She burrowed into the sailcloth, curled around the pendant like a snail around its shell, and within moments her breathing slowed into the deep, unguarded rhythm of true sleep. Sleep, Orsen thought bitterly, was a skill he'd surrendered long ago.

He stayed in the loft a long while, sitting on the ladder's top rung, listening to her breathe.

Below him, the pearls waited in their trays. Somewhere across the harbor, in the tall counting house with its lit windows, clerks were writing the names that mattered and striking out the ones that didn't. Somewhere in his own chest, under twelve years of practiced quiet, something had begun to move—slow, tidal, patient as pressure against a hull.

*Forget the face,* the note had said.

But it had not said *forget the pearl.* And whoever wrote that note—whoever chose his doorstep out of every threshold in Gullrow—had known exactly which face they were asking him to forget.

Orsen climbed down without a sound, took up the note, and held it close to the banked coals of the workshop stove until the paper curled and the words lifted off it like smoke. Then he stood in the dark, listening to a sleeping child breathe above the racks, and understood that the life he had built out of being no one had ended tonight, quietly, at his own front door.

Tomorrow the overseer would come with the supply rolls. Tomorrow he would need to explain rations, movement, noise.

Tomorrow he would have to learn to lie in ink.
