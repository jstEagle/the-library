## 4. The Diver's Daughter

The boat had a name but she wasn't allowed to say it.

Sefi knew this the way she knew other things that lived in her body instead of her head: the way to hold your breath so it went long and quiet, like a fish swimming through you; the way salt dried white on your arms and made them itch; the way a song went when two people sang it and one of them was your mother and one of them was the sea. The name of the boat was painted on its side in blue letters that flaked off at the edges. Her finger had traced those letters a hundred times. Her mouth had never been given permission.

*Quiet now,* someone said, in the remembering place. *Quiet, little pearl.*

She didn't know who had said it. The voice came out of the water-smell of her dreams, warm and low, and it always ended before she could see the face.

* * *

The loft above the drying racks was full of smells that were not sea-smells. Oil. Old wood. Something bitter, like leaves boiled too long. Sefi lay on a pallet stuffed with what felt like rope-ends and counted the cracks in the boards over her head until the counting got tired and stopped.

The man — Orsen, he'd said, saying it fast like a word he wanted rid of — brought her bread in the mornings when the light under the door was still grey. He didn't talk much. When he did talk, his words came out small and careful, like he was carrying each one across a slippery deck.

"Eat," he'd say. "Then be quiet."

Being quiet was the one thing Sefi was very good at. She had been told so many times, by so many voices, some kind and some not-kind, that quiet had grown into her like a shell grows around a grain of sand. She could be quiet through hunger. She could be quiet through the night sounds of the workshop below — the tick of cooling metal, the scurry of something small in the walls. She could be quiet even when the questions pressed up behind her teeth like bubbles, all of them wanting out at once:

Where is my mother? Where is the boat? Why does my neck feel empty?

She kept them down. She was good at it. But sometimes, in the dark, one bubble would escape anyway, and she would whisper it into the rope-stuffed pallet where nobody could hear.

"Boat," she whispered. "Blue letters."

That was all. That was the whole prayer.

* * *

In the remembering place there was singing.

It happened most nights. She would be walking somewhere — the remembering place was mostly water and mostly dark, but there were boards under her feet, wet and smooth as glass — and then the singing would start, and she wouldn't be walking anymore, she'd be small, smaller than she was now, wrapped in wool against the wind, and a voice above her would rise and fall like gulls.

The song had no words she could keep hold of. They slid away from her like minnows. But the shape of the melody stayed: up high at the start, like a question asked to the sky; then down, down, deep and slow, like something being lowered into the water on a rope.

*Down, down,* the song went, without words. *Down, down.*

Her mother sang the high part. Sefi knew this without knowing how she knew. There was another voice sometimes, a man's, rougher, laughing in the middle of notes — and once, in the dream, Sefi had turned to look for him, and the looking had woken her up, heart banging, alone in the loft with the oil-smell and the cracks in the boards.

After that she tried not to look in dreams. You weren't supposed to look. Looking made things end.

* * *

There was a day in the remembering place that was not a good day, and Sefi's mind went to it the way a tongue goes to a broken tooth.

They had been on the dock. Not the boat — the dock, where the wood was dry and hot and full of splinters, and men shouted numbers at each other in voices like knives. Her mother's hand held hers so tight it hurt. Her mother smelled of salt and fear, and Sefi hadn't known yet that fear had a smell, but her body had known, and her legs had gone heavy.

"We're only here to sell what we can spare," her mother had said — or maybe that was a different day, maybe mothers said that every day, standing on docks, holding hands too tight. "Only what we can spare. Remember that, little pearl. Whatever they say, our pearls come from our own waters. Our waters. Ours."

And then the hands.

Not her mother's hands. Big ones, dry ones, smelling of ink instead of salt, and they didn't grab her — grabbing was fast, and this was slow, which was worse — they reached and lifted, and the chain at her neck pulled taut, taut, and then —

The snap was very loud. Louder than shouting. Louder than the sea.

Sefi felt it even now, awake, in the remembering place and out of it: the thin burn across the back of her neck where the cord broke, the little weight lifting away from her chest where the pearl had hung, cool and round, warm from her skin. Gone into a stranger's fist. And her mother's voice going high and sharp — not the singing-high, a different high, a breaking high — and then arms around Sefi herself, carrying her, and the world tilting past in slices: boots, crates, a wheel turning, water flashing between boards.

*Quiet now,* said the voice carrying her. It was not a warm voice anymore, or maybe it had never been warm and she had only needed it to be. *Quiet, little pearl, and this will all sort itself out.*

It had not sorted itself out.

* * *

Mornings, she watched Orsen through the gap in the loft floor.

He thought she slept. She let him think it, because watching was not the same as being seen, and being seen felt dangerous in a way she couldn't explain even inside her own head. So she lay still and looked down through the knothole at the man who fed her, and she learned him the way she'd learned the boat: by his parts. His hands, which moved quick and sure over the pearls he sorted, quick like they'd done it forever. His shoulders, which sat crooked, curled inward, like a person trying to take up less room than a person takes up. His eyes, which never rested anywhere longer than a breath — except once.

Once, when she'd come down in the evening and the lamp caught the pendant he wore — a plain pearl on a cord, nothing fine about it, duller than hers had been — his whole face changed. It opened, just for a moment, like a window in a shut house. Then he saw her seeing, and the shutter came down, and he turned away and pretended to count things that did not need counting.

But Sefi had seen. Her mother's pendant had looked like that. Same roundness, same faint oily shine, same way of holding the light even when the light was bad.

*Ours,* her mother had said. *Our pearls come from our own waters.*

Maybe his mother had said it too. Maybe that was why he'd taken her in off the doorstep, why he fed her bread and told her to be quiet, why his face opened when he looked at pearls. Maybe there were more people in the world whose mothers said *ours*, and if she found them, the boat with the blue letters would find her too. Things came back. Pearls came back. Songs came back. Sefi believed this the way she believed the tide: without proof, without argument, completely.

* * *

One night the dreaming took her all the way back to the boat itself, and she stood on the wet boards in her sleep, and the blue letters were right there in front of her, close enough to touch, and she touched them.

Her finger fit into the grooves exactly. Of course it did. It always had.

Behind her, the singing started — high part first, her mother's part — and Sefi opened her mouth to sing the answering line, the low one, the one every diving child learned before they learned their letters. She could almost feel it in her throat. Almost. The shape of it rose in her chest like a bubble rising through dark water toward a surface she couldn't quite reach—

"Boat," she whispered, waking, into the rope-stuffed dark. "Blue letters. I almost had it."

Below her, through the knothole, lamplight moved. Orsen was awake too, sitting at his table with papers spread out all around him — old papers, yellowed ones, and she watched him run his finger down a line of writing and stop. Stop, and go back, and stop again. His lips moved over the words without sound, the way her lips moved over the lost song.

Then he did a strange thing. He took the pendant from around his neck — his dull little pearl, his mother's-or-someone's pearl — and set it down on the table beside a paper, and bent close, comparing, the way you compare two shells picked up from the same beach.

Sefi pressed her eye to the knothole and forgot to breathe.

Because she had seen that paper before. Not that exact paper — she couldn't read, but she could remember, and remembering was better than reading — but that *kind*. Heavy paper. A seal pressed into red wax at the bottom. One had lain on a desk on the day of the dock, the day of the snapping cord, and a dry-handed man had smoothed it flat and said words like *settled* and *all claims resolved*, and her mother had made a sound like something tearing.

The pearl on Orsen's table gleamed. The sealed paper lay beside it. The lamplight made them shine together, the same shine, the family shine, the *ours* shine.

Sefi's heart knocked against her ribs like a small fist against a locked door. She should stay quiet. Quiet was her gift; quiet had kept her alive through strangers' kitchens and strangers' boats and one terrible long walk along a seawall to the doorstep where Orsen found her.

But tomorrow, when he brought the bread, when he said *eat, then be quiet* —

Tomorrow she was going to point at the paper, and point at the pearl, and ask him the question she'd carried since the dock:

*Whose is it?*

And whatever answer he gave, she had the feeling — tide-certain, wordless, huge — that neither of them would ever be able to be forgettable again.
