## 5. Salt and Signatures

The tide was going out when Orsen slipped away from the workshop, and he had learned long ago that the ebb was the only honest hour the harbor kept. Everything the water gave back in those minutes was what it had truly taken: weed torn from the pilings, a broken crate stave, a doll's porcelain arm gone green with salt. The Vellamare Trading Company did its accounting at high tide, when the barges came in heavy-bellied and the ledgers were opened under lamplight. But the ebb told the truth about what had been carried off.

He left Sefi sleeping in the loft above the drying racks, a coil of netting pulled over her like a second blanket, her small fist closed around the pearl pendant even in sleep. He had tried once—gently, casually, the way a man might ask about weather—to take it from her hand to look closer. She had woken screaming before his fingers touched it, a raw animal sound that had no words in it, and after that he did not try again.

*Forget the face,* the note had said. He had not forgotten it. He had looked at it every night for six days by candle stub light, memorizing it the way he had never been permitted to memorize anything of his own: the chipped front tooth, the salt-cracked corner of one eye, the way the child's silence had a practiced quality to it, as if she too had learned that being unremarkable was how you stayed alive.

Maren Oduya lived past the rope walks, in a leaning house whose shingles were the color of old oyster shells. She had been the best diver on this coast before the lung-fever took half her breath away, and she had been retired from the company's rolls for eleven years—which meant, Orsen knew now, that she had been erased for eleven years, her name struck from whatever paper said who owned what, who had sold what, who had been paid and who had been pressed. He had not thought of her in years except as a figure on the strand, mending nets, nodding to no one. That was the thing about erasure, he was beginning to understand. It did not need to be violent to be complete. It could be done with a shrug, an omission, a ledger line left blank.

She was on her porch sorting mussel shells into piles when he came up the path, and she looked at him for a long moment before speaking.

"You're the Amberdale boy," she said. "Grown."

The name went through him like cold water through cloth. Twelve years, and no one had said it aloud. In the workshop he was *you there*, *the sorter*, *boy*, *hands*. His own name had become a coin so old its face had worn smooth.

"Yes," he said, and his voice cracked on it, embarrassingly, like a boy's.

"Sit." She swept shells from a stool without looking up. "You walk like your mother. Like the water's still deciding whether to keep you."

Orsen sat. His hands would not stay still; they wanted to fold, to hide, to do the small busywork of being invisible. He made them be quiet in his lap.

"I need to show you something," he said. "And I need you to tell me if I'm wrong. I've been wrong before. I've been wrong on purpose, most of my life. It's hard to know anymore where the mistakes end."

Maren set down her shell knife and looked at him then, fully, with eyes the pale gray-green of sea glass, and he understood that she had been waiting for someone to come up this path for eleven years.

* * *

He had brought the pendant wrapped in a square of sailcloth, because he could not bring Sefi—he would not put her on the road until he knew what road it was—and because some part of him feared the object itself, feared what it would confirm. He unwrapped it on Maren's table between two candle stubs, and the pearl caught the light and held it, deep and warm, with that faint rose cast at its heart that no farmed pearl in the Vellamare workshops ever had.

Maren did not touch it at first. She leaned over it the way divers lean over dark water, reading what is beneath before committing.

Then she turned it over, and her breath stopped.

"Inheritance mark," she said quietly. "See the setting. Three points, like a trident's crown, but bent inward—that's not fashion, boy, that's a family sign. Each diving family on this coast drilled their heirloom pearls with their own mark before they ever strung them. Oduya pearls carry a wave-knot. The Pellwicks carry a gull's foot. And this—" her thumb hovered over the three bent points without touching them, "—this is the Amberdale mark."

The room tilted slightly. Orsen heard his own pulse.

"My mother wore one like it," he said. "I was four. I remember it around her neck when she came up from the water, shining against her skin, and I remember—" He stopped. He had spent twelve years not remembering; the habit was strong. "I remember hands taking things out of our house. Not thieves' hands. Orderly ones. They made lists."

"They always make lists," Maren said. "Lists are how they steal without breaking anything you'd notice."

She pushed the pendant gently back toward him across the table, and her hand, when it withdrew, was trembling—not with age, he saw, but with something held down for a very long time.

"Your mother dove company waters. You know that much?"

"Everyone knows it."

"Do you know why she drowned?" Maren's voice was flat, careful, the voice of a woman stepping onto thin ice deliberately, testing each inch. "The harbor will tell you she was caught under when the storm came early. Storms come early. Divers die. It's the arithmetic of the trade, they say, and everyone nods."

"I was four," Orsen said. "They told me nothing. They told me I was indentured and that was my settlement. My mother's debt, they called it, though she owed no one. I signed where they put my hand. I didn't even write my own name—I wrote the mark they showed me, because I couldn't write yet." He laughed, short and bitter. "They taught me letters afterward. In the workshop. So I could read their inventories. Do you see the joke of it? They gave me literacy just enough to serve their books, never enough to question them."

"I see it," Maren said. "I've been living inside that joke for a decade."

She rose, stiffly, one hand pressed to her chest where the lung-fever still lived, and took down from the high shelf a clay jar sealed with wax. When she broke the seal, the smell of old paper and camphor rose out of it.

"I was there," she said, "the season your mother died. And the season before it, and the season after. I dove beside her for nine years. And I watched the company change, slowly, the way water changes wood. First they stopped buying outright and started offering 'advances'—coin up front against a family's next seasons' harvest. Then, when the harvests failed—and they failed more often than chance would have it, though I couldn't prove it then—the families owed more than their beds were worth. So they sold the inheritance strands. The grandmother pearls. The marked ones, the ones that said *this water fed us, this blood belongs here.*"

"And the company bought them cheap."

"The company bought them for less than cheap. For forgiveness of debt. For not pressing charges on contracts written in terms a fisherman couldn't parse. And here is the part that matters, boy, the part that has been eating me alive since before you were tall enough to reach a sorting table." She drew papers from the jar—fragments, water-stained, some charred at one edge. "Every purchase was recorded. Every inheritance mark was entered in the company's ownership rolls, because a pearl with a family mark is worth triple to collectors abroad—it's provenance, it's story, it's the whole romance of the trade. They needed the marks documented to sell high."

"So the records exist."

"The records existed." Maren laid a scorched fragment flat. It was the corner of a page, but the corner bore part of a stamped heading: VELLAMARE OWNERSHIP AND PROVENANCE ROLLS. "And every few years, there's a fire. A lamp knocked over in the record room. A chimney fault. Once, a whole warehouse annex, conveniently empty of stock that night, full to the rafters with paper. And after each fire, the debts are unrecoverable, the ownerships are unverifiable, and any family that comes forward claiming their strand is shown the ash and told: prove it. Prove anything. Paper burns, friend. The sea takes what it takes."

Orsen sat very still. Something enormous was assembling itself behind his ribs, piece by piece, and each piece fit a memory he had filed away as noise.

"The silences," he said slowly. "In the workshop. There are lots we're not allowed to inventory. Sealed crates that go straight from the receiving dock to the auction floor, bypassing the grading tables entirely. And when I asked—once, twelve years ago, in my first month—the overseer looked at me the way you look at a crack in a hull. After that I learned not to ask. I learned to sort without seeing names, to weigh without asking weights of what. I thought I was keeping myself safe."

"You were keeping their machine running," Maren said. "That's the cruelty of it. Every diver who didn't count her catch too loudly, every clerk who didn't read the entry above hers, every mother who hushed her child at the company docks—we all thought we were surviving. And we were. But we were also the walls. Silence holds a building up just as well as stone, if enough people keep it." She met his eyes. "You've been load-bearing, boy. All of us have."

The words settled over him with a weight that was almost physical. Twelve years of careful choreography—the angled shoulders, the averted eyes, the competence deployed precisely so that no one would ever think him worth promoting or punishing. He had called it invisibility. He had even been proud of it, in the secret way a survivor is proud. And all along his blindness had been a beam in someone else's architecture.

"Sefi," he said. The name came out rough. "The girl I'm keeping. Her pendant matches mine. Same setting. Same three points, bent inward."

Maren went still in a different way than before—a hunter's stillness.

"There are no other Amberdales," she said. "Your mother was the last of that line on this coast. I'd have known. We all knew each other's marks the way we knew each other's strokes in the water." She leaned forward. "Where is this child? Whose door did she wash up on?"

"Mine," Orsen said. "With a note that says *forget the face.*"

For a long moment neither of them spoke. Outside, the ebb tide ticked against the pilings, patient as a clock counting down something neither of them could yet name.

"If that pendant is what I think it is," Maren said finally, "then either your mother's line didn't end where the paper says it ended—or someone is wearing dead families like jewelry, and a child got loose with the proof around her neck." She began, deliberately, gathering her fragments back into the jar, but her hands had changed; they moved with purpose now instead of grief. "There's a copy ledger, boy. The fires burn the public rolls, but provenance is money, and men like Vellamare never let money exist in only one place. There'll be a working copy. Sealed lots. Graded and marked and waiting for auction season."

"Auction season," Orsen repeated. His mouth had gone dry. "The grand auction. Corvane Halst arrives next week to audit the workshop ahead of it. They call him the man who burns paper."

"Then you have six days," Maren said, "to find where they keep the true registry—before Halst finds it first."

* * *

Orsen walked back along the strand as the light failed, the pendant warm against his chest where he had tucked it inside his shirt like contraband, which, he understood now, it was. Behind him Maren's lamp glowed in her window, the first lit window on the coast that had ever been lit for him.

Ahead, the workshop lamps burned in their orderly rows over the drying racks, and above them, in the loft, a child slept holding a dead family's testimony in her fist.

Six days, he thought, and found that for the first time in twelve years, he was counting down to something instead of merely counting himself lucky to be overlooked.
