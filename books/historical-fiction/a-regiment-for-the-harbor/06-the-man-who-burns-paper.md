## 6. The Man Who Burns Paper

The gull that landed on the drying rack at dawn was the first sign, though Orsen did not know it yet. Gulls came to the workshop every morning to steal the smaller oyster scraps; this one only sat, facing the harbor road, silent as a sentinel, and when Orsen looked up from his sorting tray it lifted off without taking anything.

He had learned, in twelve years, to read the harbor's small weather. The gull was a change coming.

It arrived an hour after first bell, riding in a company carriage too fine for the salt-stained lane, drawn by two gray horses that stepped delicately around the fish guts and broken shell as if the road itself offended them. Orsen saw it from the window over the washing basins, and his hands went still in the water before his mind had finished deciding why.

The overseer, Doss, was already moving down the workshop aisle with the particular quickness he reserved for fear. "Up, all of you. Company inspection. Wash your hands, straighten your trays, and if anyone asks you a question, you answer it short and true and nothing else." His eyes found Orsen's across the room and held there a half-second longer than necessary — not warning, exactly. Plea. *Be forgettable today, Amberdale. Be nothing.*

Orsen dried his hands and thought of the loft above the drying racks, where Sefi slept beneath a tarpaulin among the hanging kelp-bundles, curled as small as she could make herself, her thumb hooked through the loop of a pearl pendant.

*Forget the face,* the note had said. He had kept the note folded inside his boot for eleven days now, until the ink had softened and the paper smelled of him.

The carriage door opened.

The man who stepped out was unremarkable in the way that a knife is unremarkable lying flat on a table. Medium height, medium years, a coat of undyed wool that would have cost more than Doss earned in a season precisely because it refused to look expensive. He carried no ledger himself. He carried instead a small tin case, flat and scarred at the corners, and Orsen knew what was in it because everyone in the harbor trades knew what was in it: oil of vitriol, a glass stylus, and a set of iron punches for breaking wax seals cleanly, so that a document could be read one last time before it stopped existing.

Corvane Halst. The man who burned paper.

They said he had once been a clerk — that he had spent twenty years writing entries before he'd been promoted out of writing altogether and into its opposite. They said he never raised his voice because he had never needed to. They said that when he finished with a company's records, the past fit inside a single locked drawer, and the drawer fit inside his coat, and the coat went wherever the company wanted the past to go.

Halst paused at the workshop threshold and stood there a moment, breathing the air — the brine, the oyster rot, the smoke from the polishing braziers — as though tasting a vintage. Then he removed a pair of plain spectacles from his breast pocket, put them on, and entered.

* * *

"Amberdale."

Orsen had been arranging his tray for the fourth time when the name reached him, spoken evenly, from behind. He turned with the unhurried motion of a man who has been interrupted ten thousand times and expects nothing from any of them.

"Sir."

"You're the sorter." Halst consulted a card he held between two fingers, though Orsen suspected the consultation was theater; the card was blank on the far side. "Twelve years' service. No infractions. No requests. No transfers requested by other departments." A pause, thin as a blade's edge. "No one ever asks for you, do they, Amberdale?"

"No, sir."

"That must be restful."

"It's work, sir."

Something moved behind the spectacles — not warmth, but the acknowledgment of a well-made thing, the way a jeweler might nod at a properly drilled pearl. "Show me your intake method. The auction lots are being assembled next week, and I want the provenance chain clean before the buyers arrive."

*Clean.* Orsen led him to the sorting bench, aware of every step, aware of the loft ladder at the far end of the room, of Sefi up there under the kelp smell and the canvas, awake now — he could feel it, the way you can feel weather through a wall — listening. He laid out the day's intake: river baskets, harbor lots, the sealed consignments that arrived twice monthly under Vellamare seal and were never opened in front of the sorters.

"Where are these weighed?" Halst asked, touching the edge of a sealed lot without lifting it.

"In the back room, sir. Overseer Doss handles the seals."

"And you don't wonder what's inside?"

"No, sir."

"Lie number one," Halst said pleasantly, "though I'll allow it's the required lie. Everyone in this building wonders what's inside. Curiosity is the cheapest commodity in a harbor town." He took out a small notebook and made no note in it, which was somehow worse than writing. "Your mother worked these waters, I'm told. Pearl diver. Amberdale — that's a diving-family name, is it not? Oduya, Amberdale, Ferrow, Sel. The old bottom-folk."

The name landed like a hook behind Orsen's sternum. Maren Oduya's face rose in him — her cracked hands, her certainty about the pendant — and he pressed it flat.

"My mother's dead, sir. Twelve years."

"I know. It's in the file." Halst turned a page he hadn't written on. "Drowned, wasn't she. Company waters. There was a settlement."

"There was an indenture, sir."

"Yes." Halst smiled for the first time, a small, dry movement, like paper folding. "That's what we called it."

Across the workshop, a tray clattered. Doss shot the culprit such a look that the boy went white. Orsen kept his eyes on the pearls, their small wet moons, and felt the old choreography take him — shoulders lowering, breath shortening, face smoothing into the pleasant blankness that had kept him fed and invisible since he was nine years old. He could feel how well it fit. That was the horror of it, the thing he had begun to understand in the eleven days since Sefi arrived on his doorstep: the mask wasn't something he wore anymore. It had grown into the skin. He performed invisibility the way other men breathed, and some traitorous part of him *relaxed* under Halst's gaze, grateful for it, the way a beaten dog is grateful for the familiar shape of the boot.

He hated that part of himself with a new and surgical hatred.

"The pendant," Halst said.

Orsen's hands did not stop moving. Twelve years of practice held them steady over the tray. "Sir?"

"The inventory discrepancy. Lot 44, sealed consignment, received the eleventh. One item short against the manifest." Halst's voice remained conversational, almost bored. "A single pendant on a silver chain. Small lot, low value, which is why it interests me. Valuable things get stolen loudly. Cheap things get stolen quietly, by people who know the difference between noise and loss." He looked up. "You handle the small lots, don't you, Amberdale?"

"No, sir. Sealed lots go through Doss."

"Mm. And the eleventh — you were on the late shift. Alone, part of it."

"For two hours, sir. Standard rotation."

"For two hours alone." Halst closed the notebook. "I'm not accusing you, boy. Accusation is inefficient. I observe, I ask, and the truth arranges itself, the way silt settles in still water. Someone in this building knows where that pendant went. Pendants are personal things. They aren't stolen for value — they're stolen because they mean something, and meaning," he said, tapping the tin case against his palm with a soft rattle of glass and iron, "is what I am hired to remove."

* * *

The audit lasted through the afternoon. Halst moved through the workshop like a tide, asking questions that seemed to have no direction until, looking back, you saw they had all been pointed the same way — toward the eleventh day, toward the small lots, toward the people who asked for nothing and were therefore owed nothing and noticed by no one.

At the fourth bell he requested the storeroom keys.

Orsen watched from the washing basins as Doss unlocked the back rooms, as Halst walked the rows of shelved consignments trailing one finger along the shelf edges like a blind man reading. He watched him pause at the false wall.

Everyone in the trade knew the workshop's back room had a false wall — it was not even a secret, merely furniture, an old smuggler's partition from the previous century that everyone pretended not to see because pretending not to see things was the local religion. What lay behind it, Orsen had learned only days ago, kneeling in the dust with Maren Oduya's lamp: the true rolls. Ownership registers, diving-family marks, purchase records going back forty years — the paper trail of everything the company had bought from drowning men and then declared never to have existed. Sefi's pendant matched a sealed lot in there. So, Maren believed, did half the pearls currently strung for auction.

Halst stood before the false wall for a long time.

Then he took out his notebook, wrote something — actually wrote this time — and turned away.

"Overseer," he said. "This partition. Load-bearing?"

"Old construction, sir. Harbor authority's plans list it."

"Harbor authority's plans," Halst repeated, tasting the words. "How convenient, that the past keeps leaving us paperwork we can hide behind." He snapped the notebook shut. "I'll want it opened tomorrow. I'll want a mason, a witness from the company office, and—" his gaze traveled the room, unhurried, and snagged, briefly, on Orsen, on the empty space where Orsen was so carefully standing— "a sorter who handles small lots. For inventory verification. Amberdale. You'll attend."

"Yes, sir."

"Early bell. Bring steady hands." He replaced his spectacles in his pocket, tucked the tin case under his arm, and paused at the door on his way out, looking back at the workshop — at the racks, the braziers, the loft ladder rising into shadow above the drying kelp.

"You have a draft up there," Halst observed mildly, to no one in particular. "Above the racks. Canvas flapping. You should see to it. Paper hates damp, and damp hides smells, and smells—" he smiled his paper-fold smile, "—well. Smells tell me things."

The carriage took him away at dusk. The gray horses stepped daintily through the fish guts, and the gull that had sat sentinel at dawn returned to the drying rack and screamed once, harshly, at the darkening water.

Orsen climbed the ladder before full dark, moving on the loose third rung so it wouldn't creak. Sefi was pressed into the corner under the canvas, eyes enormous, the pendant gripped so hard in her fist that he could see the chain's mark printed into her palm.

"He's coming back," she whispered. It wasn't a question. Children always know.

"Tomorrow. Early." Orsen knelt, keeping his voice below the sound of the tide. "And he's opening the wall, Sefi. Behind the wall there's a book with names in it. Your name might be in that book."

Her brow furrowed — the same expression she wore when he tried to explain what an indenture was. Then she said, in the small clear voice that had already rearranged his entire life:

"Then you should read it before he burns it. Mama says—" She stopped. Her face did the thing it did sometimes, the shutter coming down over whatever boat, whatever song, whatever tearing hands lived behind it. "Mama said names keep you."

Orsen sat in the dark beside her while the workshop settled below, and found that the dread in his chest had changed its shape overnight without his permission. It had sharpened. It had edges now. It had, he realized, been sharpening for eleven days, and what he had mistaken for terror was something closer to a whetstone, and tomorrow at early bell Corvane Halst would open a wall, and Orsen Amberdale — forgettable, unasked-for, twelve years erased — would be standing right there beside him with steady hands.

Names keep you.

He touched the folded note inside his boot and began, silently, to rehearse what he would remember.
