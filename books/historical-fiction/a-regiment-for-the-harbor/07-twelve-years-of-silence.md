## 7. Twelve Years of Silence

The lamp in the loft guttered low, and Sefi slept the way children sleep who have learned that sleep is a skill—curled small, one hand still closed around the pendant as though it might be taken in the dark. Orsen sat with his back against the drying racks below, where the salt smell rose thick enough to taste, and let himself do the thing he had not done in twelve years.

He remembered his mother on purpose.

It was a dangerous practice. He had trained himself out of it the way a man trains a limp out of his walk—not because the limp hurt, but because limping made people look. Grief was the same. Grief drew eyes. And so for twelve years he had carried his mother the way he carried everything: flat, folded, filed under a name no one asked after.

But Sefi's pendant had cracked something open, and now the remembering came whether he willed it or not.

* * *

She had hands like knotted rope and she sang when she worked, always, even at the grinding tasks—even shucking oysters in the cold shed behind their house, her knife clicking a rhythm under the words. The songs were diving songs, old ones, in a dialect the harbor had mostly forgotten. *Down is not dark if you carry your own light,* one went. He could hear her voice still, low and rough and certain, and he could see her in the shallows at dawn, standing waist-deep to test the water before she committed herself to it, because his mother never committed herself to anything she hadn't measured.

Her name was Ilsette Amberdale. He said it to himself now in the dark of the workshop like a man testing a floorboard. Ilsette. When had he last spoken it aloud? Three years ago, maybe four, to a harbor clerk who needed a next-of-kin line filled in for some form, and the clerk had written it down wrong—Ilsbeth, Ilse—and Orsen had not corrected him. Correcting cost attention. Attention cost everything.

The pearls had come from her mother before her, and from her mother's mother before that—a strand of them, gray-pink, sea-lit, the kind divers called *heirloom water* because they held the color of every dawn their keepers had ever watched from a boat. They were not for sale. That was the whole point of them, the entire architecture of the inheritance: they were proof. Proof that the family had stood on this harbor since before the Vellamare Trading Company had drawn its first charter, proof kept in the only currency the harbor respected. Other families had deeds; divers had pearls.

Orsen had been seven the first time he understood what the strand meant. A company man had come to the door with an offer so large his mother laughed—not cruelly, just with a kind of tired wonder, the way you'd laugh at a tide pool offering to buy the ocean. The man came back each season. Each season the offer grew. Each season she sent him off with tea and courtesy and a door that closed like a verdict.

"They want the strand," she told Orsen once, letting him hold it, letting its weight settle into his small palms. "But a thing isn't worth what someone pays. It's worth what it proves." She fastened it back around her neck. "As long as we wear it, we were here."

* * *

In the present, above him, Sefi turned over in her sleep and murmured something in that fragmentary child-language of hers—a word that might have been *boat*, might have been *song*. Orsen's chest tightened.

The company waters lay past the second buoy line, the deep beds where the oysters grew fat and slow and full. Divers were paid by weight there, and the weights were good, and everyone knew the risks, and everyone dove anyway, because poverty does arithmetic that courage doesn't get to audit.

His mother refused the company beds for years. She had her own grounds, shallow ones, meager ones, grounds that yielded barely enough—but they were hers, marked by her family's stake, and she would say only: "I don't dive where my air belongs to someone else."

Then came the winter the herring failed, and the rent came due, and Orsen's father had been two years drowned already, and the offer from the company man was not for the strand this time. It was for labor. Her labor, in the company beds, at company rates. She looked at the paper a long time. Orsen remembered her thumb moving over the signature line the way she used to check an oyster shell for cracks.

She signed for the work. Never for the pearls. Even starving, she kept those two things separate with a precision that Orsen, at eight years old, did not yet understand was heroism.

She dove the company beds through spring. On the third day of the neap tides in early summer—the water cold enough to cramp, the light poor—she went down and did not come up.

They told him it was an accident. Of course they told him it was an accident. The other divers told him other things, quietly, in the way divers talk, faces turned toward the water: that she'd been seen arguing with a bed-master that morning about the tally, that her catch that week had been suspiciously fine, that a woman who could hold her breath through a hymn did not simply forget how to surface. But whispers are water too; they find no shape unless something holds them. Nothing held them. No one claimed her body's cause. The company paid for the funeral—company generosity, the clerk called it, writing the figure into a ledger—and then, because an orphan boy on the harbor rolls was a liability, the clerk wrote something else as well.

*Settlement: one indenture, workshop division, term of fourteen years.*

Fourteen years. His mother's life, priced out in candle-oil and sorting tables. He had signed where the X was because he was eight and because the alternative was the parish workhouse, and the overseer had smiled and said, "There's a sensible lad," and that was the last time anyone connected to the Vellamare Company had called him by anything warmer than *boy*.

Amberdale boy, no kin claimed. He had read his own entry in the old roll just days ago and felt nothing, which was the trick of it, which was the design. Erasure doesn't feel like violence when it's administered in ink. It feels like weather.

* * *

Now, in the dark beneath the loft, Orsen laid it all end to end the way he laid pearls on the grading cloth, and the sequence was so clean it appalled him.

A family that would not sell. An inheritance that proved prior claim. A death in company waters with no inquiry. A child converted, by paperwork, into company property. And around it all, the same pattern he had spent twelve years not looking at: the desperate families Maren Oduya had described, selling strands to eat, the records of ownership burned, the names struck, the harbor's memory scraped smooth as a tumbled shell.

His silence had not been neutral. That was the understanding arriving in him now, cold as deep water, unwelcome as surfacing too fast. Every day he had sorted pearls without reading the lots they belonged to, he had been holding a wall up. Not with his hands—with his blindness. The whole trade ran on men like him: competent, careful, unremarked. The company had not merely erased him. It had *employed* his erasure. It had taken the son of the woman it drowned and set him to polishing the very stones of the wall that buried her.

And it had nearly worked. That was the worst of it. There had been years—he could count them, five, six—when he had felt genuinely nothing, when the choreography of invisibility had felt not like survival but like peace. He had mistaken numbness for safety, and safety for contentment, and contentment for a life.

Then a child had arrived on his doorstep with his mother's inheritance knotted at her throat, carrying a note that said *forget the face*—the company's oldest instruction, the first commandment of Vellamare, the law he had obeyed since he was eight years old.

Forget the face.

"No," he said aloud, softly, so as not to wake her. The word hung in the salt-smelling dark and seemed, absurdly, to be the first true sentence he had spoken in twelve years.

Ilsette Amberdale. Say it. Keep saying it until the harbor has to hear it.

He understood now what he had inherited. Not the strand—it was gone, sold or stolen or strung around the necks of strangers in cities he'd never see. Not the grounds, not the boat, not the name on any roll the company kept. What passed to him was smaller and heavier: the debt of remembering. His mother had worn her proof against her skin every day of her life, and the company had killed her for it, and the interest on that killing compounded daily in every ledger line, every burned page, every child like Sefi taught to be quiet.

Debts, in Orsen's experience of the workshop, were never forgiven. They were only collected—or transferred.

He would collect this one.

* * *

Above him, the loft boards creaked. Sefi's small face appeared over the edge, hair wild, pendant swinging free of her collar.

"You're talking to nobody," she accused.

"To somebody," Orsen said. "You just couldn't see her."

Sefi considered this with the gravity of a harbormaster weighing cargo. Then: "Was it the singing lady?"

His breath stopped. "What singing lady?"

"The one in the boat." She hugged the rail, chin on her stacked hands, eyes enormous in the lamplight. "Before. I only remember the boat part and the singing part. And the bad hands." Her fingers went to the pendant without her seeming to notice she'd done it. "The singing lady sounded like—" She frowned, searching. "Like warm bread smells."

Something in Orsen's chest broke and reassembled itself in a different shape.

"Go back to sleep, Sefi."

"Will you sing?"

"I don't remember the songs."

"Liar," she said comfortably, the way children say it, meaning *you're sad, I'll allow it*—and slid back down out of sight.

Orsen sat a long while after, watching the lamp burn. Then, almost without deciding to, he began to hum—one of his mother's diving songs, half-remembered, the melody rising out of him like something surfacing after twelve years underwater, lungs burning, refusing to stay down.

*Down is not dark if you carry your own light.*

When it ended, the workshop was silent, and he knew two things with diver's certainty: that Corvane Halst would return before auction season, and that this time, when the man came asking his quiet questions, Orsen Amberdale would have answers ready—

—and behind the workshop's false wall, sealed in candle oil and old ink, waited the papers that would tell him exactly whose face he must never forget again.
