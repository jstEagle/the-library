## 8. Copies in Candle Oil

The false wall revealed itself the way most lies do: by being too perfect.

Orsen had walked past that stretch of the workshop's back corridor ten thousand times—a paneled stretch between the drying racks and the salt store, unremarkable, unadorned, the kind of surface a man trained in forgetfulness would never look at twice. It was Sefi who looked at it twice.

She had been three days in the loft, learning the rhythms of his hiding her—when to breathe loudly and when not to, which floorboards announced visitors—and she had grown restless in the way of children kept too long in small spaces. On the fourth evening, while Orsen sorted the season's first graded lots below, he heard her bare feet on the ladder, then silence, then her voice, thin with wonder.

"Orsen. The wall's warm."

He nearly told her walls weren't warm. Then he climbed up to see what she meant.

The paneling was warm because behind it, pressed against the workshop's chimney flue, something was being kept dry on purpose. Paper. He knew the feel of company paper now the way he knew the grain of pearl—the faint waxy slickness of it, the way it drank candle oil rather than light. When he pressed the panel at its lower corner, it gave with a click so soft he might have imagined it, and swung inward on a hinge older than his indenture.

The cavity beyond was narrow as a coffin stood upright, and it was full of ledgers.

Not the company ledgers he knew—those lived in the front office under lock, their spines stamped *VELLAMARE TRADING CO.*, their contents the tidy fiction of honest trade. These were different. Older bindings. Hand-sewn. And on the spine of the nearest one, in faded iron-gall ink, a word Orsen had only ever seen crossed out or burned: *Registry.*

* * *

Maren Oduya came at low tide, when the harbor's noise dropped to a mutter and the workshop emptied for the evening meal. She brought two candles, a flask of lamp oil, a bundle of clean fish-paper dried stiff for writing, and a face like a woman going to war with whatever weapons lay to hand.

"You're certain," she said, though she was already pulling off her boots at the door, already moving toward the corridor with the certainty of a diver who has decided where the bottom is.

"I matched the lot number." Orsen kept his voice low out of habit, though the workshop was theirs until morning. "The pendant's clasp is stamped. A diving-family mark and a registry number—lot 114, sealed inventory, entered the year my mother drowned. The pendant in that lot is listed as *unclaimed heirloom, ownership lapsed.*" His jaw ached from clenching. "It wasn't lapsed. It was taken."

"And the ledger that says who it belonged to?"

"That's what we're going to find out."

They worked by candlelight in the coffin-narrow space, Maren seated on an overturned crate with the oldest volume open across her knees while Orsen knelt beside her with the fish-paper and a nib cut from gull quill. The plan was simple and terrible in its simplicity: copy everything. Names, dates, lot numbers, signatures. Every family the company had bought from in desperation and then erased. Every record Halst would burn if given the chance.

The candle oil was Maren's idea. "Ink can be questioned," she said, dipping the quill. "Ink has authors. But oil—" She tipped the flask over the finished pages, one by one, letting each drink its measure before folding them into waxed cloth. "Oil-soaked paper keeps. And if they come for us before we finish, you drop these in the water tank, and no fire on this earth touches them."

Outside, the tide ticked against the pilings. Inside, the dead spoke.

"*Oduya, Tessa,*" Maren read aloud, and stopped. Her thumb pressed flat against the page as if holding down something alive. "My sister-in-law. Drowned the same season as your mother. They bought her string of seed pearls off my brother for half its weight, and I always thought—" Her voice did something Orsen had never heard it do; it went young. "I always thought she'd sold cheap because grief makes fools of us all."

"She didn't sell at all." Orsen turned the ledger toward her. Beneath the entry, in a different hand, smaller: *Consideration waived. Heirloom surrendered in settlement of harbor dues.* Harbor dues that, he saw as he read on, had been assessed the week after Tessa Oduya's boat was denied mooring rights—rights the company itself controlled.

"They made the debt," Orsen said slowly, understanding arriving like cold water filling a boot. "Then they collected it in pearls. Then they burned the proof anyone ever owned them."

"Not burned." Maren tapped the wall around them. "*Hidden.* Why burn what you can keep? Burned paper invites questions. Hidden paper lets you sell the same inheritance twice, once as salvage and once as stock. Corvane Halst doesn't come to destroy evidence, boy. He comes to move it somewhere even we can't find."

* * *

They copied through the night watches. Orsen's hand cramped; Maren read in a voice gone flat with discipline, because if she let feeling in they would never finish. There were more names than either of them could bear to speak aloud—whole pages of diving families, three generations deep, each entry ending in the same bloodless formula: *ownership lapsed*, *heirloom surrendered*, *no kin claimed.* No kin claimed. Orsen wrote those words four times before he understood he was copying his own indenture entry over and over, that the phrase was a stamp the company pressed onto anything it wanted to be true.

Sefi slept in the loft above, and once, near the deepest hour, he heard her turn in her sleep and murmur something—a snatch of song, three notes rising. A boat song. He froze with the quill in the ink, listening, and thought: *someone sang to you. Someone on a boat.* He wrote faster after that.

It was Sefi, too, who saved them.

Dawn was still an hour off when Orsen heard it—not from the loft but from the front of the building: the scrape of the outer door. Not the workshop's morning key. A key turned with a stranger's patience.

"Maren." He pinched out the candle between finger and thumb, and darkness closed over them like water. In the black he felt her fold the last oil-soaked pages into her bodice with the calm of long practice, and he heard—God help him—he heard himself think, with the old treacherous relief: *I know how to be invisible. We'll stand still and this will pass.*

But they were in the corridor, not the loft, and the false wall stood open behind them, and there was nowhere in this narrow dark to be nothing at all.

Boots in the front office. The strike of a match. And then Halst's voice, mild as milk, speaking to someone Orsen hadn't heard arrive: "No, don't fetch him yet. Let me see what our diligent sorters have been about after hours."

Light bloomed at the far end of the corridor. Shadows of shelving stretched toward them like fingers.

And then, from above—from the loft—a floorboard creaked, deliberate as a knock.

Halst's footsteps stopped.

A small voice floated down the stairwell, sleep-rough and sweetly confused: "Hello? Is somebody there?" A pause, perfectly timed. "I'm not supposed to talk to strangers. Are you a stranger?"

"Sefi—" Orsen breathed, horror and admiration arriving in the same instant.

"What have we here." Halst's voice changed register, warming into the pleasantness he wore like gloves. Boots moved away from the corridor, toward the stairs. "A child. Alone in a locked workshop. That's a curious thing, little one. Curious things are my whole profession."

"My da said wait here and don't touch nothing." A sniffle, artful. Orsen realized she'd rehearsed this—had practiced innocence the way harbor children practice knot-tying, and he couldn't imagine where. "But I got lonely."

"Did he. And where would your da be?"

"Gone to the auction warehouses. He works for the company." A beat. "Do you work for the company?"

"I do indeed."

"Then maybe you know him. He's got a beard."

Orsen was already moving, Maren's hand steady at his back, easing the false wall shut behind them—click, soft as a swallowed word—as Halst's attention hung entirely on the loft stairs and the small liar performing at the top of them. They slipped along the shadow of the drying racks, out through the salt store's side hatch, into the gray breath of pre-dawn, and did not stop walking until the harbor wall took their weight and the open water lay before them like a witness that could not be bribed.

* * *

They sat on the stones below the sea-stairs, the waxed packet of copies between them, Maren breathing hard not from exertion but from the effort of not laughing—or crying; with her it was hard to say.

"A beard," she managed finally. "She gave him a beard."

"He'll check the loft within the hour. He'll find the blanket, the rations, everything." Orsen stared out at the water going from slate to silver. "She bought us minutes. Maybe less. Once he understands what she is—what she carries—"

"Then we don't give him hours. We give him nothing." Maren pulled the packet open and counted the pages with her fingertips, a diver counting her catch before surfacing. "Forty-one families. Forty-one names with heirs still living, scattered along this coast like seed pearls in silt. Your mother among them. My sister-in-law among them." She looked at him, and in the growing light her eyes were the flat gray of deep water, which is to say: bottomless, and moving. "The auction opens in three days, boy. Halst will want these ledgers moved before the buyers arrive—which means he'll empty that wall tomorrow night, when the workshop stands empty for the loading."

"Then we've got one night left in that room."

"No." Maren folded the packet closed and pressed it into his hands, closing his fingers over it the way she'd once closed a drowned man's jaw. "You've got one night left in that room. I've got divers to wake. Forty-one names, Orsen. Some of them have sons with boats and grievances with interest accrued." Something moved through her face that was not quite a smile. "You hold the testimony. I'll gather the witnesses."

She went up the sea-stairs without looking back, a broad gray figure dissolving into the working dawn.

Orsen stayed on the stones a moment longer, the oil-soaked pages heavy against his chest, heavier than any pearl he'd ever carried. Behind him the workshop rose against the brightening sky, its windows catching the first light, and somewhere inside it Corvane Halst was asking a small girl questions she was answering in lies stitched from truth—and winning.

Three days, then, to the auction. One night to the wall.

For twelve years Orsen Amberdale had made himself nothing to stay alive. Now the nothing had a name, a child, forty-one families, and a packet of stolen truth against his ribs, and he found—to his own astonishment—that he was no longer afraid of being seen.

He was afraid only of being too late.
