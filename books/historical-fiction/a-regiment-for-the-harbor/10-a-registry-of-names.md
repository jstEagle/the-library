## 10. A Registry of Names

They reached the workshop before the clerk did.

Orsen had run the harbor road a thousand times without ever running it like this—not to arrive unnoticed, not to slip through a gap in the day, but to beat another man to something worth keeping. His lungs burned with salt air and twelve years of habit telling him that running drew eyes. He let it burn. Let them look.

The workshop door was still locked; the key had lived around his neck since he was nine years old, because no one had ever thought to take it from him. That was the whole of his inheritance until now: a key nobody bothered to steal. He got the door open, lit no lamps, and went straight for the false wall behind the drying racks, where Maren Oduya waited in the dark with a lantern turned so low it was more memory than light.

"You felt it coming," she said.

"I saw his colors."

"Then we're quick." She handed him a bundle already wrapped in oilcloth—the true registry, every page they'd copied and every page they'd saved, tied with cord and sealed with candle wax gone soft at the edges. "Halst's men will come for what's left of the originals. Let them have ash. Ash proves nothing anymore." Her voice was steady, but her hands were not, and Orsen understood: this woman had spent thirty years watching paper die. Tonight paper was going to live.

He heard the clerk's boots on the boards outside—hurrying, then slowing, then stopping altogether. A knock. Then, absurdly, a second knock, as though the man expected courtesy.

Orsen did not answer. He pressed his palm flat against the false wall one last time, feeling the grain of wood that had hidden three erased people's testimony, and then he stepped back and left it standing, empty, like a shell after the creature has gone.

The knocking stopped. Boots retreated toward the harbor road, toward whatever report Halst would write tonight and whatever ruin would be written over Halst tomorrow.

Maren blew out the lantern.

"They'll come at dawn with the harbor master's writ," she said into the dark. "And when they open that wall, they'll find nothing but old salt smell."

"Good," Orsen said, and found that he meant something larger by it than escape. The wall had been built to hide ownership. It would stand now only as proof that hiding had happened—and proof, kept openly, was called history.

* * *

Dawn came gray and then gold, the way dawn does over water when the wind has worked all night to scrub the sky.

They came, as Maren promised: two clerks of the harbor authority, the harbor master himself—a broad, sun-cured woman named Ilsa Venn whose voice could carry across a fleet—and, trailing at the edge of the group as if unsure of her invitation, Sefi, scrubbed pink, wearing a coat that had belonged to some diver's daughter forty years gone and fit her like a tent.

The harbor master read the writ aloud on the workshop threshold. Words like *seized*, *dissolved*, *reconstituted under public authority*. Words like *fraud* and *theft* and *restoration*. Then she looked up at Orsen, who stood with his back straight and his heart going like a gull in a storm, and said, "You're the one who read the names."

"Yes, mistress."

"At my auction floor. Without leave. Without voice." She studied him the way she might study a pearl held to lamplight, turning him, checking for flaw. "An indentured boy has no standing in this harbor. You knew that."

"I knew it," Orsen said.

"And you stood anyway."

"I stood anyway."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth—not quite a smile; harbormasters are careful with smiles—but close enough. "Then you'll do for a keeper. Sign here."

The document was long and full of words Orsen had spent his life on the wrong side of: *indenture*, *voided*, *compensation*, *title*. He signed where she indicated, and his hand shook, and he did not care who saw. When he finished, Ilsa Venn took the pen back, struck a line through the word *indenture* wherever it appeared, and wrote above each strike, in her own blunt hand, the same word: *void*.

Twelve years, canceled in four strokes of ink.

"There's one more line," she said, turning the page. "The registry needs a keeper's name for the rolls. Full name. Kin, if claimed."

Kin, if claimed.

Orsen looked at the blank space. All his life the paperwork had written itself against him: *Amberdale boy, no kin claimed.* No mother entered, no trade inherited, no name beyond the one the company found convenient. He had believed, somewhere deep beneath the competence and the quiet, that this was simply what the world was—that erasure was weather, and you dressed for it.

But he had stood on an auction floor and read names aloud, and the sky had not fallen. The company had fallen instead.

"My mother," he said, and his voice came out steadier than he had any right to expect, "was Ilva Amberdale, pearl diver, licensed out of this harbor, owner by inheritance of the Amberdale bed and its registry mark. Restore her name to the rolls. And mine—" he touched the page, just below where hers would go— "mine is Orsen Ilvason. If she can't claim me now, then I'll claim her. Kin goes both ways, or it should."

Ilsa Venn wrote it down. *Ilva Amberdale. Restored.* And beside it: *Orsen Ilvason, keeper.*

The letters looked small on the page. They did not feel small. They felt like a boat coming about in heavy water—slow, laborious, and then suddenly all sail and open sea.

* * *

The reopening took eleven days, and Orsen remembered every one of them the way he had once remembered nothing—deliberately, gratefully, keeping accounts.

The drying racks were rebuilt with their doors off, so anyone walking past could see the work inside. The sorting tables were moved to face the windows. The scale was re-hung by a weights-man sent from the customs house, and calibrated publicly, twice, while half the harbor watched from the quay with the particular delight harbors take in watching institutions weigh themselves honestly for once.

And in the front room, where the false wall had hidden the true registry, they raised a new fixture: a long case of glass and harbor oak, and inside it, the book.

Not a copy. Not a sealed inventory lot. The registry itself—open, always, to its current page, where anyone could walk in off the street and read who owned which beds, which pearls, which inheritances, and who had been paid fairly and who still awaited settlement. Maren Oduya sat at the desk beside it most days, ostensibly to verify diving-family marks, actually to argue with anyone who lingered too long without buying anything, which was nearly everyone, which she seemed to enjoy enormously.

On the first morning of the new workshop, before the doors opened, Orsen stood alone in the front room with the book.

He turned back through the pages to the restored entries. There were dozens now—Maren's divers, the families of the drowned, the children scattered and the grandchildren returned. And there, near the top of the first new leaf, in ink gone brown with age but legible as a heartbeat:

*Amberdale bed, registered to Ilva Amberdale, diver. Inheritance mark: single pearl pendant, drilled silver. Status: settled in full, restitution paid to heir.*

*Heir: Orsen Ilvason.*

His mother's pendant lay against his chest under his shirt, where it had hung since the auction, since the moment he'd pulled it from the evidence case and the harbor master had looked at him and said, quietly, *take what's yours.* It was warm from his skin. It had been warm from his skin for eleven days and he suspected it would be warm from his skin for the rest of his life, and that seemed correct. Some things should stay warm.

The floor creaked. Sefi stood in the doorway in her enormous coat, hair braided by Maren's impatient hands, holding a slate against her chest the way she'd once held the note that told him to forget her face.

"Am I allowed in yet?" she asked. "Maren said before doors. But Maren also says that about everything."

"Maren is usually right about doors."

Sefi came in slowly, the way she still entered rooms—testing, ready to be small again if smallness was wanted. Orsen had learned to watch for that readiness and hate it. Twelve years he had made himself forgettable on purpose; this child had been made forgettable by other people, and neither of them should ever practice it again.

"I have to enter you in the book today," he said.

Her chin came up. "My real name?"

"We're still learning it."

"We know most of it." She set the slate down on the keeper's desk and turned it toward him. In chalk, in a child's uneven, triumphant hand: *Sefa Odune Vell.* "Grandmother Sefa. Mama said Odune was for the tide-side family. And Vell—" she paused, and her voice went careful, the way voices go when they carry something breakable— "Vell was our own. Our bed. Before."

Before the company bought pearls from desperate families and burned the paper proving they'd ever owned anything. Before a boat and singing and hands tearing a pendant from a small neck. Before *forget the face*.

Orsen knelt beside the registry and opened it to the apprenticeship page. The heading was simple, meant for trades passed father to son, mother to daughter, diver's lung to diver's lung. He dipped the pen.

"Sefa Odune Vell," he wrote aloud as he wrote it down, letter by letter, so she could hear her name exist. "Apprentice, first of the new registry. Entered freely, by her own saying, under the hand of the keeper, Orsen Ilvason."

"Read it back," she commanded, because Maren had taught her that paper means nothing until someone reads it aloud.

He read it back.

She listened with her whole body, absolutely still, the way divers' children listen—to weather, to water, to the sound of a world deciding to hold together. When he finished, she climbed onto the stool beside the desk, put her hand flat on the open page, and said, "Now it's true."

"Now it's true," Orsen agreed.

Outside, the harbor bell rang the hour. Through the windows they could see the quay filling—divers with their marks strung on cords, buyers with their purses drawn tight, the curious, the skeptical, the families who had come to look at a book and find, impossibly, themselves in it. Maren's voice carried above all of it, arguing with a customs man about the proper hanging of a sign.

Sefi hopped down and went to the door and put her hand on the latch, and then hesitated, looking back at him with an expression he was beginning to know very well—the expression of a person about to ask for something they have only recently learned they're allowed to want.

"When people come in," she said, "and they ask who keeps the book—"

"The keeper's name is on the first page."

"No. I mean—" she frowned, working at it, this child who had been taught that names were dangerous and was learning, stubbornly, that they were load-bearing— "do I get to say it? Yours? So they know who answers for the truth of it?"

Orsen stood. He straightened his collar, and underneath it his mother's pendant lay warm against his heart, and outside the bell rang on, and the harbor waited, and for the first time in his life he was not waiting with it.

"Yes," he said. "Say it loud enough that they hear it over the water."

She grinned—quick, fierce, wet-eyed, hers—and threw open the doors, and the morning came in with the crowd behind it.
