## 1. The Ink That Would Not Dry

The ink would not dry.

Marguerite had learned this about winter work: the cold crept into the studio through the river stones and settled in the inkwell like a patient guest, and every line she laid down stayed wet longer than it should, glistening, vulnerable, ready to smear at the touch of a careless sleeve. She blew on the plate a fourth time, held it to the lamp, watched the lamplight swim across the wet black of the river's course — the Marne bend, the town, the bridge rendered in her master's precise hand because she was copying his hand now, stroke for stroke, as though he might still reach out from the grave and correct her.

He could not. Three weeks dead. They had pulled him from the canal on a Tuesday morning and called it an accident, and everyone in town had nodded the way people nod when they have agreed not to look too closely at anything ever again.

She set the plate down and flexed her fingers. The lamp guttered. Outside, the wind came off the flooded fields with a sound like cloth tearing, slow and continuous, and beneath it she could hear the river working at its banks — that deep, muscular grinding she'd grown up hearing every November and never quite gotten used to. The water was high this year. Higher than anyone remembered. Old Bastien at the ferry house said the bridge would go under within the week, and when the bridge went under, the town sealed itself like a jar, and nothing moved in or out until the thaw.

Which meant she had perhaps six days to finish what should have taken six months.

Marguerite turned back to the desk. Forty-one plates lay finished in their tissue wrappings, stacked in the order her master had specified in his last coherent letter: coastlines first, then rivers, then roads, then the towns, each one drawn twice — once as the world knew it and once as the war had remade it. It was beautiful work. It was the best thing either of them had ever made, and if she were honest with herself, which she tried not to be after midnight, it was the only thing standing between her and a life spent mending other people's maps for other people's money in a town that had already forgotten her name once.

*The Winterbourne Atlas of the Reclaimed Territories.* Her name would not appear on it. His would. That had been the arrangement since she was nineteen years old and still grateful enough not to argue.

She dipped the pen. Plate forty-two: the eastern approaches. She drew the road first, then the hedgerows, then the little church with its leaning tower, all of it innocent, all of it the kind of geography any schoolchild might need. Then she paused, pen hovering, and looked at the door.

It was locked. She had locked it. She always locked it now.

She drew the fortifications.

They were already penciled in — his hand, his measurements, done sometime in the spring before the armistice, before whatever bargain he had been trying to strike, before whoever had decided he knew too much. Battery positions marked in red. Sight lines in blue. The old Roman earthworks outside Saint-Aubin that the army had re-cut and nobody official had ever acknowledged re-cutting. A supply depot disguised, in the government's own survey, as a granary. She had spent two nights copying them exactly, hating every line, feeling the way she imagined a forger feels — complicit in the truth rather than the lie.

The atlas was supposed to be finished by now. The commission wanted it in the spring, clean and complete, a monument to orderly peace. What the commission did *not* want — what no one official had asked for, what her master had died, probably, for refusing to surrender — were these final four plates. The ones charting fortifications the armistice was never meant to see. Fortifications that were, according to the letter she'd found sewn into his coat lining, still manned. Still armed. Still pointed, quietly and without declaration, across borders that existed now only on paper.

She finished the last sight line and sat back. The ink gleamed up at her, wet and black and patient.

*Inheritance,* she thought. *And indictment.*

Her master had used the phrase once, years ago, over a plate showing the old salt roads — "Everything worth inheriting is also something you can be hanged for" — and she had laughed then, because she was twenty-two and he was alive and the sentence had sounded like wit instead of prophecy.

Now she lifted plate forty-two, tilted it so the ink caught the lamplight, and understood that she was holding both halves of her future in one sheet of paper. Deliver the atlas complete, all forty-six plates, and the commission would hail it as definitive — and someone in some ministry would read those four final plates and understand that a dead cartographer in a river town had documented what they'd sworn didn't exist. Or deliver it incomplete, burn the four plates, keep her hands clean and her name small, and spend the rest of her life knowing she had let the truth die in a fire because the truth was dangerous.

There was a third option. There was always a third option. Her master had taught her that too: *the map is never the territory, Marguerite; the map is the argument.*

She just didn't yet know what argument she was prepared to make.

A floorboard creaked upstairs.

She went very still. The studio occupied the back half of the ground floor of the master's house — her house now, though the deed said otherwise and the garrison's ledger said otherwise-er. Above her, the old rooms where he had slept and eaten and argued with correspondents stood empty, furniture under sheets, and empty houses made their own noises. Wind in the chimney. Beams contracting in the cold. She had heard all of it before, dozens of nights, and told herself each time that it was only the house settling around its grief the way she was settling around hers.

But this creak had weight behind it. This creak had boots.

She pinched out the lamp.

Darkness took the room whole. She stood with plate forty-two in her hands, ink still tacky against her fingertips, and listened. Below the wind, below the river, there it was again: the front gate, the hinge she'd meant to oil, swinging. Then gravel. Two sets of footsteps, maybe three, unhurried, the walk of men who had every right to be wherever they were walking.

The knock, when it came, was polite. Three raps on the studio's outer door, evenly spaced, the knock of a man who expected the door to open.

Marguerite's heart was doing something loud and stupid in her chest. She crossed the dark room by memory, lifted the loose board near the hearth — the one over the cavity where her master had stored his brandy during the occupation years, dry, deep, and smelling faintly of oak and better times — and slid the finished fortification plates into the dark. All four. Her fingers shook so badly the third one scraped the stone, and the sound seemed to fill the house like a shout.

She replaced the board, dragged the hearth rug square over it, and opened the inner shutters just as the knocking started again, harder now.

"One moment," she called, and made her voice annoyed instead of terrified, which was the single finest piece of cartography she had ever performed.

Sergeant Delacour stood on the step with a lantern held high and a younger soldier behind him, both wearing the flat expressions of men doing arithmetic they'd been ordered not to show. Snow had begun to fall, fat and slow, catching in the sergeant's mustache.

"Mademoiselle Winterbourne." He peered past her into the dark studio. "Late hours."

"Ink doesn't respect curfews, Sergeant." She let the irritation sit in her voice like sediment. "My master is dead three weeks. Someone has to finish his work or the commission will have all our heads."

Something flickered behind his eyes at the word *commission*. He shifted the lantern. "We've had reports of lights here. Nightly. And movement."

"You're looking at the movement." She leaned against the doorframe, arms crossed, letting the cold bite through her shawl so her breath would fog convincingly. "I work when the house is quiet. Unless the garrison intends to regulate lamplight now. In which case I'll want that in writing, because my master's solicitor will enjoy it almost as much as I will."

Delacour studied her for a long moment. Snow gathered on his shoulders. Behind him, the young soldier's lantern swung slightly, throwing light along the studio wall — along the drying rack, the stacked tissue paper, the long table where forty-one wrapped plates waited like bodies under sheets.

"The captain wants a list," Delacour said finally. "Of everything the deceased left. Papers, instruments, correspondence. For the inventory."

"Then the captain can send me the form," Marguerite said, "and I will fill it out in triplicate, as is proper, when I am not racing a flood to finish a commissioned atlas. Good night, Sergeant."

She closed the door. Not slammed — closed, with the deliberate softness of a woman who has nothing to hide. She stood with her forehead against the wood and listened to the gravel crunch away, the gate hinge complain, the silence pour back in.

Then she laughed, once, short and graceless, because her legs had gone watery and laughing was better than sitting down on the floor of a dead man's studio.

The inventory. Of course. An inventory meant soldiers handling his papers, his letters, his drawers — and somewhere in this house, sewn into a coat lining or folded into a folio, were documents that would answer every question she'd just refused to ask. If they inventoried thoroughly, they would find them. If they found them, they would find the four plates' reason for existing, and then they would find the four plates, and then—

She lit the lamp again. Its flame steadied, and by its light she saw the hearth rug, square and innocent, and beneath it the boards that held the most dangerous papers in the province.

Six days until the flood closed the bridge. Perhaps less. Whatever she was going to do — deliver, destroy, or something in between that hadn't yet occurred to her — she would have to do it before the water rose and the town became a sealed jar with her inside it.

She sat back down at the desk, dipped the pen, and began plate forty-three.

Outside, the snow thickened, and the river kept grinding at its banks, patient as a debt coming due.
