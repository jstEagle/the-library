## 2. A Promise Made in Peacetime

The ink on the atlas's title page would not dry.

Marguerite had written the same line four times now — *A Regiment for the Lantern, being a survey of the river country* — and each time the letters bled slightly at their edges, as though the paper itself were reluctant to hold them. She blotted it, lifted the blotter, and the ghost of the line rose up at her from beneath, doubled, like a thing seen through ice.

She set down the pen. Outside, the garrison's boots went past on the cobbles in their evening count, twelve pairs of feet striking in time, and she found herself holding her breath until the sound faded toward the bridge.

Her master had hated that bridge.

*Old Arnaud*, she corrected herself. He had asked her, in the last spring of his life, to stop calling him master. *We are colleagues, Marguerite. The atlas will outlive both of us; it does not need a master, only hands.* But he was dead now, and the dead are difficult to argue with, and in her grief she had reverted to the old word the way a hand returns to a scar.

She turned the title page over and looked at the blank verso — the space where, by all the conventions of the trade, a dedication should go. Her pen hovered. She thought of the words she might write, and of the words she could not, and of a promise made five years and one war ago, in this same room, with the windows open and the river running quiet and no one in Europe believing that anything would ever change.

* * *

August 1914. The studio smelled of linseed and river damp, and the windows were thrown wide because Arnaud Pellier believed a map drawn in stale air was a map drawn in bad faith.

The young lieutenant stood at the long table in a uniform still creased from its packing, holding his cap in both hands as if it were something he'd been issued by mistake. He was perhaps twenty-three. He had the kind of face that had not yet decided what it would become — a soft jaw, an intent gaze, the particular earnestness of a man who has been told all his life that he is promising. His name, written in the visitors' ledger in a hand that pressed too hard, was Elias Voss, and he had come across the border on a survey exchange that both governments had approved and both governments were, at that very hour, quietly regretting.

"Three days," Arnaud said. He said it the way he said everything, flat and final, like a contour line. "In three days they will close the frontier, and after that it will not matter what either of us approves. You should be on the morning train."

"I know."

"And yet you are standing in my studio."

"I wanted to ask you for something," the lieutenant said, "and I did not want to ask it in a letter. Letters can be read."

Marguerite was grinding pigment at the side bench — Prussian blue, for the river plates — and she did not stop grinding, because apprentices did not stop grinding, but she listened with her whole spine.

"Ask, then," said Arnaud.

"If the war comes — when it comes — there will be men on both sides of this river who want the same thing, which is to be on the other side of it. Soldiers who've had enough. Men who slip away in the dark." The lieutenant's voice was steady but his knuckles had gone white around the cap. "Your town is the crossing point. Your atlas will know every ford, every ferry, every stretch of bank where a boat can be put in without being seen from the road. I've seen the preliminary plates. You've mapped this river more intimately than either army ever will."

"Flattery," said Arnaud, "is a poor foundation for an oath."

"It isn't flattery. It's an inventory." The young man set his cap on the table, squared it to the edge with a precision that made Marguerite think of her master's own hands at the drafting board. "I am asking you to keep that inventory for the right purpose. If a man comes to this river trying to get home — any man, from either side — and he has no other way, I'm asking you to let the atlas be a door and not a wall."

The room was quiet. Down at the wharf someone was singing, a barge song, the kind of song that assumes tomorrow.

"And who vouches for this man?" Arnaud asked. "This hypothetical deserter. Who is he?"

"I am," said Lieutenant Voss.

Marguerite's pestle stopped against the mortar.

"Not now," he went on, and there was something almost like a smile, quickly gone. "Now I am a lieutenant with papers and a return ticket. But wars have a way of rearranging a man. I've read the histories. I know what armies do to the men they no longer need. If that day comes, I will not be asking as an officer. I will be asking as the man I actually am, and I will have nothing to offer but my word that I am still that man."

Arnaud looked at him for a long time. Marguerite had seen her master appraise maps that way — turning a suspect plate toward the light, checking its watermarks, weighing what it claimed against what it was. Then he crossed to the shelf where he kept the atlas's master folio, the one object in the room more guarded than the strongbox, and he laid his hand flat upon it.

"Here is what I can promise, and it is not small," he said. "This atlas will be finished, whatever the war does. And when it is finished, any man who comes to this door in good faith, having lost his war, will be shown the plates. The fords at the willow bend. The ferryman's channel below the mill. The gravel bar that surfaces in August. I will teach him the river well enough to save his life, and I will ask him nothing but that he never use that knowledge against this town."

"And if the town objects?"

"Then the town can hang me," Arnaud said, "and the atlas will still be true."

The lieutenant bowed — not the parade-ground bow but a shorter one, the kind a man gives in a church. "Then I hold you to it."

"You hold a dead man to it, more likely," Arnaud said, without any particular gloom. "Wars eat the old and the careful first. But a promise is a promissory note, Lieutenant. It passes to the estate." He glanced at Marguerite, and it was the briefest glance, the kind that assigns a debt without discussion. "My colleague will witness it. She has a better memory than I do, and she will likely outlive us both."

Marguerite set down the pestle. "I witness it," she said, because there was nothing else to say, and the lieutenant looked at her then — really looked, the first time — and said, "Thank you," in the tone of a man accepting a weight he intended to carry a long way.

Three days later the frontier closed. The trains stopped running. The barge song at the wharf was replaced by the sound of pilings being driven for the gun emplacements, and the river, which had always been a road, became a wound between two countries.

* * *

Marguerite came back to the present the way one surfaces from cold water.

The lamp had burned low. On the table, the title page had dried at last, and the line sat there crisp and black and finished: *A Regiment for the Lantern.* Below it, the verso still waited, blank as the river in fog.

She had thought of the promise perhaps twice a year since 1914 — at odd moments, on feast days, when the ice went out of the river — and each time she had filed it away as Arnaud's debt, Arnaud's oath, a note in a ledger she kept out of sentiment. He was the one who had sworn it. He was the one who had said *my colleague will witness it* and then, being dead, had left the witnessing to stand for the whole.

But debts did not work that way. She understood that now, sitting in his chair, in his coat, with his pen drying in her hand. A promise made in peacetime was a promissory note, and war was the collection agent, and the estate paid. She had inherited his studio, his unfinished atlas, his name on the town's register of persons of interest — and his oath, which was the only item in the inventory that could not be locked in a strongbox or burned.

*If a man comes to this door in good faith, having lost his war.*

She thought of the garrison's evening count, the twelve pairs of boots. She thought of the reward notices that would surely come, now that the armistice commission's letters were arriving at the mayor's office with increasing frequency. She thought of the plates beneath the floorboards — the true plates, the fortification plates, the ones that could hang a cartographer as easily as clear her — and of how a man who knew this river's fords and channels might also know what those plates meant, and what they cost, and who had died over them.

It was a foolish thought. She dismissed it. She dismissed it again. It returned a third time, the way thoughts do when they are not thoughts but premonitions wearing the clothes of thoughts.

Marguerite dipped the pen and, on the verso of the title page, wrote nothing. She blotted the nothing carefully and set the page aside to dry.

Then she heard it — a sound from the street, wrong for the hour. Not boots. Not the garrison's count. A single set of footsteps, uneven, dragging, stopping outside the studio door. A pause long enough to hold a whole war.

And then, softly, three knocks — spaced like a man who had learned that urgent knocking gets doors opened by soldiers instead of friends.
