## 3. The Bridge Closes

She stood with the pen still in her hand and did not move.

Three knocks, spaced like a confession. Marguerite counted her own heartbeats between them — four, five, six — and told herself that if it were the garrison they would not knock. They would come in. They had a key to every door in Sainte-Aubin-sur-Brume except the ones people kept locked out of pride, and pride had died in this town two years ago with everything else.

She opened the door on the chain.

Odette Marchand stood in the lamplight, hood up against the drizzle, one hand pressed flat against the doorframe as though the street were tilting under her. Her breath came in white plumes. Behind her the lane ran dark and empty, the cobbles shining.

"You're awake," Odette said.

"I'm working."

"At this hour." Odette's eyes went past her shoulder, into the studio, to the lamp and the scattered plates and the smell of ink that never quite left the air. She looked back at Marguerite with an expression that was careful the way ice is careful. "The river's up. Did you hear? The ferryman came through at nine shouting it. The water's over the causeway already and the bridge master's closed the gates."

Marguerite unhooked the chain. "Closed for how long?"

"Until it drops. Weeks. Maybe the month." Odette stepped inside without being asked, out of habit more than invitation, and pushed back her hood. Rain had beaded in her gray hair. She was fifty-one and had buried a husband and a son and had stopped apologizing for taking up space. "You know what that means."

"It means no post. No commission. No garrison rotation."

"It means," Odette said, "the town is sealed. Whatever comes in stays in. Whatever's here when the water rises is here until spring thaw lets it leave." She said it plainly, but her eyes stayed on Marguerite's face a moment too long, reading it the way she read weather. "You look terrible, Marguerite."

"I've been finishing the atlas."

"The atlas." Odette turned her hands palm-up toward the warmth of the stove, though there was no fire lit; the studio held its heat like a held breath. "Auguste worked on that thing his whole life and it killed him, and now you work on it, and I wonder what it will do to you."

"Finish me properly, I hope."

It was meant as a joke. It came out sounding like something else, and Odette heard the something else, and neither of them corrected it. Outside, faint under the rain, came a sound that did not belong to rain — a long, low groan from the direction of the river, timber complaining under strain, the bridge announcing its grievances to anyone who would listen.

"There it goes again," Odette said quietly. "They say the middle span shifted last night. The ferryman says by morning the water will be through the arches like teeth through bread." She pulled her hood back up. "I only came to tell you because you're down here alone by the water, and if anything happens in the night—"

"Nothing will happen."

"Something already happened," Odette said. "To Auguste. In October. And nobody has been arrested, and nobody has explained, and the garrison asks its little questions and shrugs." She paused at the door, one hand on the latch. "Lock your chain. Even from friends."

Then she was gone, and the lane swallowed her, and Marguerite stood listening to the river groan in the dark like something large turning over in its sleep.

* * *

The water took the bridge at dawn.

Marguerite watched it from her window with a cup of coffee going cold in her hands. The Brume had risen all night — she had heard it even in sleep, a sound beneath sound, the town's floor humming — and by first light the river was no longer a river but a brown sea with a current in it, moving with the patient violence of something that had all the time in the world.

The old bridge took until seven o'clock to die. First the water went through the arches and stood level with their crowns, and then it began going *over* them, a smooth brown sheet where the parapets had been, and then the middle span — the one Odette had spoken of — gave a shudder visible even from this distance and settled, and after that it was not a bridge anymore. It was a reef. Gulls came down immediately, the way they do, to stand on the drowned stonework and complain.

By eight the garrison had posted the notice on the church door, four copies, nailed at heights suitable for reading by officers:

*BY ORDER OF THE OCCUPYING COMMAND — THE BRIDGE AT SAINTE-AUBIN IS CLOSED TO ALL TRAFFIC UNTIL FURTHER NOTICE. THE TOWN IS HEREBY CLOSED FOR THE SEASON. MOVEMENT OUTSIDE THE PERIMETER IS FORBIDDEN.*

Marguerite read it twice. Around her, the small crowd murmured and dispersed with the practiced fatalism of people who had survived worse than inconvenience — who had survived occupation, conscription, hunger, and the death of nearly everyone they loved, and who regarded a flooded bridge as a rude but familiar guest.

*Whatever's here when the water rises is here until it drops.*

She thought of the atlas hidden under her floorboards, forty-one finished plates and six that could hang a regiment. She thought of the armistice commission that was rumored to be making its rounds of the river towns before the new year. Let it come, she thought, or let it wait for the thaw — either way, the water bought her time. Time to finish. Time to decide.

She walked home along the levee path, close to the edge, watching the current carry things south: fence rails, a drowned hen paddling its own strange funeral, a cartwheel turning over and over like a coin spinning forever without landing.

And on the far bank, half-hidden in the reeds where the water had climbed into the fields, a rowboat lay overturned. Empty.

She noticed it without noticing it, the way you notice a face in a crowd you'll see again later in different circumstances. The current was too strong for any boat to cross now. Whoever owned that boat had crossed before the water rose — or had tried to cross since, and failed, and was somewhere on this side of the river now.

Somewhere inside the seal.

* * *

The chapel of Sainte-Aubin had lost its roof to a shell in '17 and never regained it, so the townspeople had moved their devotions into the nave of the larger church and left the old chapel to pigeons and memory. Its crypt, though, was dry, and deep, and cool, and in the years of the war it had served the town in ways no one wrote down: a cellar for contraband brandy, a hiding place for two deserters whose names Marguerite had never learned, once, briefly, a schoolroom.

Her master had kept the key.

She went at night, after the garrison's count, carrying a shuttered lantern and the excuse of grief. It was a walk she made often — Auguste had loved the crypt's cold silence, had gone there to think, and she went there to feel closer to him than the grave allowed. The stairs descended in a spiral worn hollow by centuries of feet. At the bottom, the dark was total and smelled of stone and tallow and time.

She heard him before she saw him.

A breath. Not a sigh — breaths taken through pain have a particular architecture, drawn carefully around an injury like water flowing around a stone. Then a shift of weight, cloth against stone, and a sound he could not suppress, low in the throat, bitten off instantly.

Marguerite raised the lantern.

He was sitting against the wall beneath the empty niche where the reliquary had once stood, in a uniform stripped of insignia, and he had his hand inside his coat pressing his own side, and there was blood on his fingers — dried black at the edges, wet at the center, which meant recent, which meant hours, not days.

For one full second neither of them moved. The lantern flame leaned in a draft from the stairwell.

Then he said, in French accented by the north, in a voice roughened by fever or by the road: "I knocked three times. On your door. Last night. You didn't answer."

The world tilted very slightly, the way the bridge had shuddered, and settled.

"I thought it was Odette," Marguerite heard herself say.

"I know. I waited until she left." He tried to straighten and failed, and the failure cost him; sweat stood on his forehead in the lantern light. "Your master promised me passage. Auguste Winterbourne. Before the borders closed — 1914, the last week of July. He swore it on his work." A ghost of something crossed his face — not a smile, nothing so simple. "On the atlas itself. He put his hand flat on it and swore."

"You're Voss," she said.

"Lieutenant Elias Voss." A pause. "Formerly. Now, I'm told, nothing. A deserter with a price." His eyes were bright with fever, and steady anyway. "The bridge was my way out. I crossed before it fell. I've been in these woods three days waiting for the patrols to change, and then the water rose, and now—" He gestured weakly at the crypt, the dark, the sealed town above them, and the gesture said everything the sentence couldn't afford to finish.

Marguerite stood at the foot of the stairs with a dead man's lantern in her hand and felt the trap close around her with the softness of rising water. Every instinct she had honed since October said *walk away, climb the stairs, blow out the light.* The garrison wanted this man. The reward notice hadn't been posted yet, but it would be; she could already see it nailed to the church door beside the flood order, four copies, a number printed beneath his description.

But Auguste had sworn. On the atlas. With his hand flat on the pages she had spent three months finishing in secret, grieving, hiding.

And promises outlived the men who made them. That was the whole cruelty and whole point of promises.

"How bad?" she asked, coming closer, setting the lantern on the step.

"It's a bullet graze. I think. Or I hope." He lifted his hand from his side and she saw the tear in the coat, stiff and black. "I cleaned it as well as I could. There's a fever coming. I can feel it coming like weather."

She knelt. Up close he was younger than his rank had sounded — thirty perhaps, hollow-cheeked, with the particular thinness of men who had eaten poorly for years — and he flinched when she touched the wound through the cloth, and apologized for flinching, which was somehow worse.

"I can't take you to my studio tonight," she said. "There are eyes on my street."

"Then I'll stay here."

"You'll die here."

"Then I'll die somewhere your name isn't attached to it." He said it without drama, the way a man states a fact of terrain. "I only need to reach the border. Your master knew a route — he described it to me once, when we thought there'd be time. The mill weir, the ford above the vineyard terraces—"

"The ford is under four meters of water."

"Then I'll need it to drop."

"The season won't drop it. You'd be here till spring." She heard her own voice go quiet. "And so, Captain, would I. We're sealed in together, you and I. Whatever's here when the water rises stays until it falls."

He looked at her then — really looked, fever-bright and searching — as if hearing in her words something more than geography, some echo of the conversation they would inevitably have to have, about the atlas, about the commission, about why a woman spent her nights alone with a dead man's maps.

"Help me," he said simply. "Not for the promise. For whatever reason you decide to invent afterward. People are better at helping when they're given permission to explain it to themselves later."

Marguerite sat back on her heels. Above them, muffled by stone and earth, the town slept its guarded sleep, and beyond the town the river ground the bridge to gravel in the dark.

"Tomorrow night," she said at last. "Bring nothing. Trust no one on the stairs. And if Odette Marchand finds you before I do — God help us both."

She picked up the lantern and climbed toward the thin gray line of the doorway, and behind her, in the dark, the wounded man lay back against the cold stone and let himself, at last, begin to shake.
