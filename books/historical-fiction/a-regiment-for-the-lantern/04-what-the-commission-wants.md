## 4. What the Commission Wants

The candle guttered twice before she spoke.

Elias Voss counted the flickers the way he'd once counted rifle volleys across a field at Verdun — not because counting helped, but because a mind had to be given something or it would give itself to panic. The stone floor of the crypt was cold through his coat. The wound in his side had soaked through the second bandage. Above them, through the chapel floor, he could hear the town settling into its winter silence: a dog somewhere, the groan of ice forming along the quay, and once — distinctly — boots. Garrison boots, with the hard heel-strike of men who expected obedience.

Marguerite Winterbourne stood at the foot of the crypt steps with her lantern raised, and she looked at him the way people look at something they have already decided to drown.

"You're bleeding on consecrated ground," she said.

"I'm bleeding on your master's tomb," Voss answered. "He'd have found that funny."

"He found nothing funny after 1916."

"No," Voss said quietly. "Neither did I."

She came down the last three steps anyway. That was the thing about her — he'd seen it in the studio, years ago, when she was an apprentice grinding pigment and pretending not to listen while two officers talked maps: she always came down the last three steps. Curiosity in that woman wasn't a weakness. It was a discipline, honed and dangerous, like everything else she did with her hands.

"Give me one reason," she said, "why I shouldn't walk back up those stairs and let Sergeant Brac do his duty."

"Because Brac will hang me by Thursday," Voss said, "and you'll still be sitting in that studio with a dead man's atlas and no idea why he died."

The lantern didn't waver. He gave her credit for that. But her breath changed — a small catch, quickly swallowed, the sound of a woman hearing aloud the thing she had been refusing to say to herself since they pulled Aubert's body from the canal.

"I know why he died," she said. "He was old and stubborn and he argued with soldiers."

"He died because of what he drew."

* * *

She sat down eventually. Not near him — near him would have been surrender — but on the third step up, where the lantern light reached her face and his, and neither could read the other's hands. Voss took it as progress. In interrogation rooms he'd learned that where a person chooses to sit tells you more than anything they say.

"Start at the beginning," Marguerite said. "And understand that I will know if you lie. I've spent six years reading men's faces for cartographic corrections. Lying is just bad drafting."

"The beginning," Voss said, "is a letter dated the eleventh of November."

"The armistice was signed the eleventh of November."

"Yes. And four days later, before the ink on that agreement was dry in Paris, a commission was formed. Not a peace commission. An audit commission." He shifted against the wall and paid for it; the pain sharpened him nicely. "Its mandate is to inventory everything the occupied territories hold that might be useful in the negotiations to come. Rail gauges. Rolling stock. Coal reserves. And maps."

"Maps are public documents."

"Some maps are public documents." He held her eyes. "Madame Winterbourne, do you know what's in the final plates of your master's atlas?"

The pause lasted three heartbeats. He counted those too.

"You don't," he said. "You've been finishing it and you don't know."

"I know it's an atlas," she said, too evenly. "River towns, roads, bridges. A commercial work. He sold subscriptions to booksellers in three countries."

"And the last twelve plates? The ones he kept locked?"

Her jaw tightened. She hadn't seen them. Or she had, and understood them, and was deciding right now how much a wounded deserter deserved to know. Voss decided to spend his best coin and see what it bought.

"The fortifications," he said.

Nothing moved in her face. Good. That meant she'd suspected.

"Aubert spent the war doing survey work under commission — everyone knew that. What nobody knew is that he never stopped. Bridges with load ratings. Ford depths by season. The heights above the river road, with firing angles worked out to the degree. Twelve plates that constitute, as far as I've been able to determine, the most complete military assessment of this stretch of frontier in existence. Better than anything my own army ever produced. Better than anything *any* army produced, because Aubert was here before the war, during it, and after, and he was the finest technical draftsman either side ever employed and then wasted."

"Why are you telling me this?" Her voice had gone flat, careful. "You're handing me reasons to burn the whole thing."

"Because in nine days — perhaps fewer, if the flood doesn't slow them — that audit commission arrives in this town. Six men, military escort, authority to seize records. They have a list of holdings to inspect, and Aubert's studio is on it. His death wasn't random, Madame. He wasn't robbed and he wasn't killed in a quarrel. Someone told the commission what the atlas contains, and someone else decided the safest atlas was a finished one in commission hands rather than a half-finished one in a widow-less apprentice's studio. Your master was killed because of what he drew. The question you should be lying awake asking is why *you* weren't."

* * *

The silence after that went on long enough that Voss heard his own blood moving.

"They think I'm nobody," Marguerite said at last. It wasn't self-pity. It was arithmetic. "An apprentice. They think whatever's unfinished in that studio is worthless without him."

"Which buys you time and nothing else. When they arrive, they'll inventory everything. Every plate, every sketchbook, every marginal note in his hand. And if the fortification plates are there—"

"They're not there."

Voss stopped. For a moment the cold, the wound, the damp rot-smell of the crypt all receded, and there was only the cartographer's reflex: new data, replot immediately.

"Where are they?"

"That's not your business."

"It became my business the moment I crawled through a flooded culvert to reach this town instead of walking to the garrison and turning myself in."

"Then you're a fool as well as a deserter."

"I'm a man who made a promise to keep a promise." He said it before he could decide whether to. Some cards you play early precisely because they cost you. "Your master swore me safe passage. August of 1914, in that studio, with the mobilization notices already going up. I was twenty-three years old and I'd shown him a survey error on the border charts that would have gotten a column of our own men shelled by their own artillery, and instead of arresting me for having seen his working papers, he shook my hand and said: *Lieutenant, whatever comes, this house will get you home.*"

"He couldn't have known what was coming."

"Nobody knew. That's what makes it worth keeping." Voss leaned forward, and the motion tore at his side enough that he had to breathe through it, slowly, the way the field surgeons taught. "I don't want your atlas. I want passage. There's a neutral border sixty kilometers north. Get me to it, and I disappear into a country that has no use for me, and your master's oath dies honored instead of hanging over this town like the rest of his debts."

"And what do I get?"

"Testimony."

"Testimony to whom? You're a deserter. Your word is worth—" 

"My word is worth exactly what yours is," Voss said, "which is nothing to a commission and everything to us. Listen to me. I served four months on the general staff liaison before I walked away from it. I know which of the commissioners is honest, which is ambitious, and which one signed off on the requisition order that put Aubert's name on a watch list in October. I know the audit schedule. I know the escort strength. I know that the commissioner who'll actually read the plates is a man named Delorme, who has a reputation for thoroughness and a habit of arriving two days before he announces himself." He let that land. "If the commission finds those plates, they take them, they take the studio, and they take you in for questioning about why an apprentice hid military intelligence under her floorboards. If *I* testify that Aubert's survey work was commissioned, documented, and legally civilian — if there's a paper trail showing he reported to a ministry office — the plates become bureaucratic refuse. Seized, stamped, filed in a warehouse in the capital, and forgotten for fifty years."

"You can forge a paper trail."

"I can *produce* one. There's a difference. Aubert kept correspondence — I saw the file reference myself, in the liaison office, before I ran. Somewhere in that studio is a letterhead with a ministry stamp on it. Find it, and we make the atlas boring. Boring is the only safety there is."

Marguerite looked at him for a long time. The candle had burned down to a stub; shadows climbed the saints' niches in the wall until the carved figures seemed to lean in and listen.

"You want me to believe," she said slowly, "that you deserted your post, took a bullet getting here, and hid in a freezing crypt beneath a chapel — all to honor a promise my master made to you five years ago."

"I want you to believe what's useful."

"And what's useful?"

"That I need you alive and free more than I need anything else in the world. Because you're the only person who can finish the atlas well enough to fool Delorme, and I'm the only person who knows how to make Delorme stop looking once he starts." He spread his hands, palms up, the oldest gesture of disarmament there was. "You need me. I need you. Neither of us trusts the other. That's not an obstacle, Madame. That's the most stable arrangement two people in this town can have right now."

* * *

She rose. He watched her weigh it — he could see the scales working behind her eyes, grief on one pan, fear on the other, and something else he couldn't name pressing down on both.

"If I bring you food," she said, "and bandages, and nothing else — if I say nothing yet about passages or testimony — will you be here tomorrow night?"

"I have nowhere else to bleed."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only honest one I have left."

At the bottom of the steps she paused, lantern raised, and for a moment the light caught the tomb behind her — AUGERT-HENRI, MASTER CARTOGRAPHER OF THE GUILD, and below it the dates of a life that had ended face-down beside a canal with his satchel empty.

"He told me once," Marguerite said, not turning around, "that a map is a promise the land makes to whoever reads it carefully enough." She looked back at him then, and her eyes were dry and very bright. "I used to think that was beautiful. Now I think he meant it as a warning. Tomorrow night. If the garrison hasn't taken you."

She went up the stairs, and the dark closed over the crypt like water.

Voss lay back against the cold stone and began, methodically, to count the sounds of the town above him — and stopped, because underneath the dog and the ice and the wind he heard something else. Boots again. Not passing this time. Stopping, somewhere on the chapel square, two men's voices, low and unhurried.

Then the knock at the chapel door, and Odette Marchal's voice — Marguerite's friend, the lacemaker, the woman who noticed everything — saying pleasantly to the sergeant beyond it:

"At this hour, Sergeant? The chapel's been shut since the flood. Come back when God's awake."
