## 5. Neighbors, Informants

The notice went up on the market cross on a Tuesday, in the hour between the fish carts and the bread.

Marguerite saw it from across the square, and for a long moment she could not make her feet move. The paper was fresh, the ink still sharp against the gray morning, and it had been nailed up by a boy in garrison blue who looked pleased with himself, the way boys do when they are given a hammer and told they matter.

She crossed the square because not crossing would have been worse.

*FIVE HUNDRED FRANCS*, the notice read. *For information leading to the apprehension of Captain Elias Voss, deserter of the —th Infantry. Last seen wearing civilian clothes. May be wounded. Approach with caution. Report to the town garrison.*

Five hundred francs. She did the arithmetic without wanting to. Five hundred francs was a year of coal, two years of rent, her master's debts settled and a new press bought besides. It was the kind of money that turned neighbors into clerks and friends into accountants, everyone tallying what they knew about everyone else and wondering what their knowledge might fetch.

The town had been hungry before. It had been hungry through four winters of occupation, through requisitions and ration cards and the slow moral erosion that comes of standing in line. But hunger made people desperate; this was something else. This was *prosperity* being dangled over them like bait on a line, and Marguerite had learned enough about human nature in her master's studio to know that prosperity corrupted faster than want ever had.

She read the notice twice, committing it to memory — the phrasing, the description, the small cruel detail that he *may be wounded* — and then she walked home at an ordinary pace, past Madame Roux sweeping her step, past the cooper's boy hauling staves, nodding to each of them exactly as she always had. Her face felt like something she was wearing. Inside it she was screaming.

In the studio, Voss was asleep on the cot behind the drying racks, his fever down but not gone, his breathing the shallow careful breathing of a man who had learned to sleep the way animals do — ready to wake. The wound in his side had closed at the edges and gone angry red at the center. He needed a doctor. What he had instead was Marguerite, a bottle of carbolic, and a book of surgical diagrams she'd found in her master's effects and studied until three in the morning with her heart hammering like someone else's fists on the door.

She stood in the doorway now and watched him sleep and thought: *I have hidden a fugitive beneath my floor, and the whole town is being paid to find him.*

It occurred to her, with a clarity that felt almost like calm, that she had already made her choice. Somewhere between the crypt and the carbolic, between the first night's lie to the patrol and this morning's arithmetic, she had stopped being a woman who might betray him and become a woman who wouldn't. There was no going back up that river. There was only the question of how far downstream it carried her.

* * *

Odette came on Thursday.

Marguerite heard her at the street door — the particular double knock, brisk then soft, that Odette had used since they were girls — and she took the stairs with her pulse ticking in her throat, arranging her face into something ordinary as she went.

"You look terrible," Odette said, kissing her on both cheeks. She smelled of lavender water and the bakery where she worked, flour dust still on her sleeve. "You look like you haven't slept since the flood."

"I haven't. I'm finishing the atlas. You know that."

"I know you're *always* finishing the atlas." Odette set a wrapped loaf on the table, unasked, unrefused — the old currency of their friendship, bread for company, company for bread. "That's rather what I've come to talk about."

Marguerite poured coffee. Her hands were steady. She had been practicing steadiness all week, and it showed, and she hated how much it showed.

"Go on," she said.

"The lights." Odette wrapped both hands around her cup, and her eyes came up over the rim, direct and brown and impossible to lie to — which Marguerite knew from twenty years of trying. "There are lights in your studio at night, Marguerite. Two o'clock, three o'clock. Madame Fournier across the lane sees them. The Vermeulens' boy sees them. Everyone sees them, because everyone is awake these nights, because everyone is counting everyone else's windows."

"It's work. It's always been work."

"Your master kept those lamps burning till dawn for thirty years and no one ever said a word about it." Odette's voice stayed gentle, which somehow made it worse. "But that was before there was five hundred francs pinned to the market cross. Before there were soldiers walking the lanes at night asking after strangers. Before—" She stopped herself. Marguerite watched her stop herself, watched the sentence die behind her teeth, and understood that Odette had rehearsed this visit and was now improvising off the page.

"Before what?"

"Before people started saying things." Odette set the cup down. "Marguerite. I'm not accusing you of anything. I'm telling you what's being said, because I'd rather you hear it from me than from a sergeant. They say the German officer who used to come to your master's house before the war — the young one, the one who liked the maps — they say a man answering his description was seen near the chapel the night the bridge flooded. They say the garrison has a list, and your master's name was on it, and names on lists don't get struck through by accident."

The fire ticked in the grate. Somewhere upstairs a door slammed; the wind off the flood had been rising all day, and the whole town creaked under it like a ship.

"And what do you say?" Marguerite asked.

"I say you're grieving and stubborn and working yourself half-blind on a dead man's atlas, and grief makes strange hours." Odette reached across the table and took her hand. Her grip was warm and flour-roughened and it nearly undid her. "That's what I say. That's what I'll keep saying, if anyone asks me, which they will, because Odile Vermeulen asked me yesterday at the bakery whether I thought you'd taken in lodgers, and I laughed at her, and she didn't laugh back."

Marguerite held her friend's hand and lied to her face.

"There's no one here, Odette. Only me and the dead."

"I know," Odette said. And then, terribly, softly: "That's what frightens me. Because if there *were* someone here, you'd tell me, or you'd be lying to me, and I don't know which of those I could bear less."

She left soon after. At the door she paused, half-turned, silhouetted against the gray light of the lane, and for one moment she looked older than her thirty years — older than the war, older than the floods, older than everything that had happened to all of them.

"The commission comes within the fortnight," she said. "Everyone knows it. Whatever you're carrying in that house, Marguerite Winterbourne, carry it lighter."

Then she was gone, and Marguerite stood in the middle of the room with two cups of cooling coffee, feeling the vise close another turn.

* * *

That night she moved Voss.

Not far — there was nowhere far to move him, the town being sealed by water on every side — but out of the studio proper, down into the old map vault beneath it, the low brick room where her master had stored plates too valuable to trust to the ground floor. She'd cleared a corner of it days ago and told herself it was precaution. Now it was necessity. If the garrison came, they would search upward and outward, rooms and attic, the way men always searched; they would stand in the studio among the drying racks and see nothing below their boots but flagstones.

Voss descended the ladder badly, one-armed, biting back sound. On the dirt-brick floor he leaned against the plate racks and looked around the vault with a cartographer's eye, cataloguing exits that did not exist.

"How long?" he asked.

"Until the water drops. Weeks, maybe. Longer if the rain holds."

"Weeks." He said it without inflection, the way he said most things, and she had learned to hear what lived underneath: not complaint but calculation. A man measuring weeks against a wound. "And the notice. Five hundred francs."

"You saw it?"

"Madame Fournier reads aloud to her cat in the evenings. Loudly. By the window." Something that was almost a smile touched his mouth and died there. "You should know that I considered turning myself in. Briefly. When the fever was highest."

"And?"

"And I decided the francs would be wasted on me." He shifted, winced, settled. "Madame Winterbourne. The people downstairs — your neighbors, the baker, the boy with the staves. In a week, someone will sell me. Not from malice. From arithmetic. Five hundred francs buys a great many loaves."

"I know the price of bread here better than you do."

"Then you understand my point."

She did. That was the unbearable part — she understood it completely, and understanding changed nothing, because the river was up and the roads were drowned and there was nowhere in the world for him to go except deeper into her house.

"I'll bring food at nine," she said. "Lamp out before then. And Voss — if they come—"

"If they come, you never met me. You found the crypt open, you took in a stranger out of charity, and you've regretted it since. Practice it. Say it until it bores you." His eyes caught the lantern light, flat and steady. "Boredom is the only alibi that works."

She climbed the ladder and pulled the trapdoor shut over him, and stood a moment in the dark studio with her palm flat on the boards, listening to the silence below, which was either a sleeping man or a secret, and was both.

* * *

Friday brought snow, thin and mean, sifting down through the smoke of the town like ash returning home. Friday also brought Sergeant Delacour.

He came to the door at midday with two privates and a clipboard, polite as a tax collector, and Marguerite's entire body went cold while her voice stayed warm, and she marveled distantly at the machinery of it — the way terror ran along its rails and the train above it kept moving.

"Routine inquiries, madame. Deserters in the district. We're visiting every household on the lane." He consulted his clipboard. "You live alone?"

"Alone, sergeant. Since Monsieur Aubry died in October."

"A loss to the town. His maps were famous." Delacour's eyes went past her shoulder, into the hall, with the idle thoroughness of a man doing sums. "We'll need to look through the house. Nothing personal. Ten minutes, perhaps."

"Of course."

They went through the studio first. She watched a private lift the drying racks' cloth and peer at half-finished charts of a coastline that existed only in her master's head; watched Delacour run a finger along the drafting table and examine the dust; watched him pause at the floorboards — *at the floorboards* — and stand there for three seconds that lasted a season.

"Cellar?" he said.

"Vault," she corrected lightly, opening the trapdoor herself, holding the lamp down into the dark so the light fell where she chose it to fall — on empty brick, on plate racks, on the shadowed corner where a man lay folded flat behind the largest of her master's copperplates, still as stone, his wound seeping slowly through rags she would have to burn. "Plates and presses. My master was possessive of his cellar. I confess I barely go down."

Delacour looked. The lamp trembled — she let it tremble; a trembling lamp was honest — and its light slid across the racks and the brick and the dark, and found nothing, because light only finds what it's aimed at.

"No lodgers, then."

"Do I look like a woman who could feed lodgers?"

He almost smiled at that, and wrote something on his clipboard, and thanked her, and went away down the lane to the next house, and Marguerite closed the door and stood with her back against it while her knees remembered how to hold her.

Below, in the dark, she heard nothing at all. For a moment that frightened her more than anything else — more than the clipboard, more than the finger on the floorboards — and she lifted the trapdoor and called down softly, and after a silence long enough to grow roots in, a voice came up out of the earth:

"Boredom," it said. "As discussed."

* * *

Saturday evening, Odette came again, and this time she did not knock her knock. She scratched — twice, quick, low on the door — and when Marguerite opened it, her friend pushed inside without waiting, cheeks bright with cold and something hotter than cold.

"They've doubled it," she said, breathless. "A thousand francs, effective tomorrow. And Marguerite — there's more. I heard it from the mayor's clerk, who owes me his mother's bread recipe and his discretion besides." She gripped Marguerite's wrists. "The armistice commission isn't coming in a fortnight. They're coming Thursday. Three days. And they're not coming for the deserter."

Marguerite's blood went very quiet.

"They're coming," Odette said, "for your master's records. Every chart, every plate, every scrap of paper in that house. And the garrison will be at their side the whole time, helping them look."

Behind her, faintly, from below the floorboards of the studio — too faint for any ear but a listening one, tuned to it now the way a mother is tuned to a child — came the small deliberate sound of a man shifting his weight against a rack of copperplates.

Three days.
