## 6. The Plates Beneath the Floor

The cough gave him away before the fever did.

Elias Voss had spent four days in Marguerite Winterbourne's cellar learning to be quiet. Quiet was a skill a soldier learned early and forgot slowly, and he had thought himself good at it — but the body betrayed its own arithmetic. A cough held down at night came up in the morning like a debt called in. The wound in his side, a bayonet's parting gift from a patrol outside Verdun-sur-Rive that had decided he was a deserter rather than a courier, had gone from hot to angry to something quieter and worse: a deep, patient throb that woke him before dawn with his heart slamming and the taste of copper on his teeth.

So it was that on the fifth day, while Marguerite was out buying bread and pretending not to be watched, Elias climbed the cellar stairs to the studio because lying still had become more dangerous than moving.

Not for discovery. For warmth.

That was the lie he told himself, anyway. The truth was uglier and simpler: he could not spend another hour staring at the ceiling joists cataloguing the ways this could end. Firing squad. Rope. The river, if they were merciful about it. A man could bear the dying; it was the waiting that rotted him from the inside. And upstairs there was work. Her master's work. The atlas.

He told himself he would only look at what she allowed him to see.

He was already past that by the time he reached the top of the stairs.

* * *

The studio smelled of her now, not of old Aubry Marchand. That surprised him. Four years since Voss had last stood in this room, a young lieutenant with a sealed letter and an oath extracted from a dying man's better judgment, and he had expected the room to be a shrine — pipe smoke and turpentine and the particular dust of a man who had lived alone with his obsessions. Instead it was Marguerite's smell: ink and cold tea and the faint coal-smoke of a stove fed just enough to keep the plaster from cracking. She lived here the way a garrison lives in a captured fort. Efficiently. Without illusion.

The atlas itself lay open on the great drafting table under a linen sheet, and he did not touch it. He had some honor left, or the ruins of it. He looked instead at the room.

He looked at the floor.

It was nothing, at first. A loose board near the north wall, the kind any old house grew. But he had spent the war reading rooms the way other men read maps, because rooms were where men hid things, and hiding places had a grammar. This board sat wrong — not warped, not lifted, but *seated*, pressed back into place with care, and the candle soot on the joists above it was disturbed in a fan shape, as if something flat and wide had been slid through the gap many times, wearing a path in forty years of grime.

He crouched. His side screamed. He ignored it the way you ignore weather.

The board came up without protest. It had been designed to come up quietly. Whoever fitted it back had oiled the edges.

Beneath: oilcloth. Beneath the oilcloth: plates.

Copper would have been too heavy, too valuable, too likely to be melted by a hungry town. These were zinc, hand-engraved, wrapped in three layers against damp, and when Elias lifted the first one into the grey window light his breath went out of him in a long, slow exhalation that had nothing to do with the pain in his ribs.

He knew what he was looking at. He had been trained to know. Two years with the cartographic section, then the commission liaison work that had ended with his name on a list and a price on his head — he could read a fortification plate the way a priest reads scripture, and these were scripture indeed. The river batteries at Pont-Neuf-du-Roi. The redoubt lines east of the marshes, the ones both armies had sworn in writing did not exist. The gun emplacements cut into the quarry face above Saint-Aubin, angled not outward toward any border but inward, downriver, toward towns like this one. Towns full of people who had been told the war was over.

The armistice commission had never seen these. He knew that too, because he had been the one tasked with confirming their absence. He had signed a paper saying the final plates of Marchand's atlas were destroyed in the bombardment of the archive at Amiens. He had signed it because his superiors told him to sign it, and because the alternative to signing papers in 1917 was not refusal but a different kind of signature, on a different kind of paper, in front of a firing post.

And here they were. Under a loose board. In the studio of a dead man whose murder everyone in town politely declined to discuss.

Elias lowered himself onto the drafting stool, which was a mistake his body punished immediately, and laid the plates out one by one with the tenderness of a man handling the evidence at his own trial. Because that was what they were. Not evidence of fortifications. Evidence of Marguerite.

* * *

She had known. All winter she had known.

He ran it backward through the days since he'd crawled bleeding into the chapel crypt and she had found him there, lantern shaking, and made the choice — God help them both — to bring him home instead of running for the garrison. Every careful answer she'd given him. Every question she'd deflected. When he'd told her about the audit, about the commissioners coming to inspect the town records, about how Marchand had died because someone in Berlin or Paris or wherever orders were written these days had decided an old mapmaker knew too much — she had not been shocked. He had taken her stillness for grief. It had been calculation. She had already known what was in her floor, and she had been weighing him against it the whole time, the way you weigh a stranger's coin between your teeth.

Betrayal arrived first, hot and stupid. *She let me bleed in her cellar knowing she held the thing I crossed a country to find.* He had walked four hundred kilometers on a leg that still remembered every step, had traded his uniform for a corpse's coat, had let a farmwife burn his papers in her stove on the strength of a promise a dead man made in peacetime — and all the while the answer had been sleeping beneath the feet of a woman who served him soup and asked nothing.

Then the anger turned itself over, the way anger does if you let it sit long enough, and showed him its underside.

She hadn't used them either.

That was the fact that would not go away. She could have sold the plates a dozen times over. To the commission — immunity, a pension, her master's name cleared and her own future secured. To the other side — enough money to leave this smoldering town forever and never look back. Even to the town itself, in a way: proof, leverage, a shield against whatever officer next decided the bridge needed guarding. An atlas of secret fortifications is power, and power is the one currency that never floods out with the season.

Instead she had kept them under the floorboards and finished the atlas around them like a woman setting a bone. Publishing the survey without the fortification plates. Delivering the science and withholding the weapon. Carrying her dead master's life work across the finish line while amputating the part of it that would get people killed — or get her hanged.

He thought about the oath. October 1914, this very room, old Marchand's hand shaking as much from rage as palsy, swearing safe passage to a twenty-three-year-old lieutenant who had come to requisition his maps and left having promised to protect them instead. *When the borders close, Captain, remember that a promise made in peacetime is the only kind that counts. Wartime promises are just strategy with better manners.*

Marchand had kept his promise the only way left to him after the borders closed: by dying for it. And Marguerite — Marguerite who had been a girl of nineteen when Elias last saw her, holding the lamp steady while her master swore — had inherited the debt along with the studio, and had paid it in the coin nobody sees: fear, daily, unbankable, for months.

She wasn't hoarding the evidence. She was standing between it and the world.

Elias looked at the plates spread across the table — the inward-facing guns, the redoubts that existed on no treaty map, the whole silent architecture of a peace built to fail — and understood at last why the commission wanted Marchand's archive audited and burned, and why someone had wanted Marchand dead before he could be audited, and why a deserter with a bounty on his head might be the most dangerous man in this town for reasons that had nothing to do with desertion.

Because he had seen the absence report. He had written half of it. If the commission ever learned the plates survived — if they ever learned a decorated liaison officer had falsified their destruction and then gone looking for them — it would not be Marguerite they came for first.

The floorboard was back in place. He didn't remember doing it. His hands had learned things during the war his mind preferred not to know.

He was re-wrapping the last plate in its oilcloth when the bell over the street door rang downstairs, and Marguerite's voice came up through the floorboards, light and perfectly pitched for an audience:

"Odette! What a surprise — no, don't take your shoes off, I've just mopped—"

And beneath the lightness, pitched for no audience at all, a second sentence, spoken low and fast, aimed straight up through the boards at the man she had never once invited upstairs:

"The garrison posted the reward this morning. They're searching house to house tomorrow."
