## 7. Odette's Choice

Odette Fournier had a way of arriving that made the doorbell sound like an accusation. Three sharp pulls, no hesitation, the kind of ring you gave when you wanted whoever was inside to know you'd been counting.

Marguerite heard it through two walls and a closed door and felt her stomach drop like a stone into the flooded river.

She had left Voss in the crypt twenty minutes ago with a basin of water, a candle stub, and strict instructions not to die loudly. The wound in his side had been going bad — she'd smelled it under the camphor when she changed the dressing that morning, that sweet rotten edge that meant the flesh had begun to argue. She needed laudanum. She needed carbolic. She needed things a woman could not buy without explaining why.

And Odette was at the door.

"Coming," Marguerite called, and took the stairs too fast, and opened the door with what she hoped was the face of a woman who had nothing in her cellar but turnips.

Odette stood on the step in a coat the color of wet slate, her breath smoking, her eyes already moving past Marguerite's shoulder into the hall the way they always did — cataloguing, weighing. She was thirty-one, widowed at twenty-six, and she ran the pharmacy on Rue des Tanneurs with the brisk mercy of someone who had learned that grief was a thing you did standing up. Half the town owed her for medicines they hadn't paid for since '15. The other half owed her secrets.

"You look terrible," Odette said.

"I've been working."

"At four o'clock in the afternoon? In February?" She held up a paper-wrapped parcel, small and dense. "Your ink order came from Lyon. I signed for it because the boy couldn't find your name in his book." A pause, delicate as a scalpel. "He said he's never once found your name in his book. He said deliveries here are always signed by Monsieur Aubry."

The cold came up through the floorboards then, through Marguerite's shoes, through everything.

"Monsieur Aubry is dead," Marguerite said. "I told you."

"I know what you told me." Odette stepped inside without being asked, which was also her way. "I know what everyone told everyone. I also know that dead men don't place orders from Lyon, and that someone has been buying lamp oil in quantities suitable for a lighthouse, and that Madame Roux swears she saw a light burning in your studio until three this morning — *two* lights, she says, though Madame Roux also swears the Germans ate her piano, so we may discount one of those."

"Odette—"

"And," Odette went on, unwinding her scarf with terrible patience, "there is a notice nailed to the garrison door as of yesterday. A deserter. Captain Elias Voss, of their engineering corps. Ten thousand francs." Her eyes came up and fixed on Marguerite's face and stayed there. "Ten thousand francs, Marguerite. You could rebuild the whole studio. You could leave this town forever."

The silence between them had weight, and texture, and a smell like snow about to fall.

"You should sit down," Marguerite said.

"No," said Odette. "But I think you should."

* * *

She would not go down to the crypt at first. She stood at the top of the cellar stairs with her arms crossed while Marguerite went ahead with the lamp, and only when Marguerite looked back and saw her friend's face — pale, jaw set, hands trembling in a way her voice never did — did she understand that Odette wasn't refusing out of caution.

She was refusing because she was afraid of what she'd feel.

Then she came down anyway.

Voss had propped himself against the wall behind the ossuary shelves, where the old bones of the town's first dead kept their patient company. Fever had gotten into him during the day; his skin had a shine to it, his breathing a rasp. But his eyes were clear enough, and they went straight past Marguerite to the woman in the doorway and did the arithmetic instantly.

"The pharmacist," he said.

"You know me?"

"Everyone knows the pharmacist. Everyone knows who feeds them." He tried to straighten, winced, gave it up. "Madame Fournier. Your husband was Lieutenant Henri Fournier, Third Chasseurs. He surrendered at—" He stopped himself, but too late.

"Sedan," Odette said. Her voice didn't change at all, which was how Marguerite knew how much it cost her. "You were there?"

"I drew the maps for it. The ones nobody used." He laughed, or something near it. "That was my entire war, madame. Maps. I can tell you the depth of every ford on the Meuse and I could not save one man's life with any of it."

"Ten thousand francs," Odette said.

"Yes."

"For you. Alive or—" She let it hang.

"They'd prefer alive. Deserter trials are better theater." He looked at his own bandaged side with mild disgust. "Though at the rate this is healing, they may get the other option regardless."

Marguerite lit the second candle because her hands needed something to do. The light doubled itself against the damp stone, and the three of them stood in it like figures on a stage set for a play no one had agreed to be in.

"You should report him," Marguerite said quietly. It had to be said; it was the only honest thing in the room. "You know what happens to people who harbor deserters. You know what the garrison does to women who—"

"To women who what? Get caught?" Odette turned on her, and there it was at last, the thing beneath the briskness, surfacing all at once like a body coming up through floodwater. "Henri died in September of 1914. Do you know what I got? A printed card. A printed *card*, Marguerite, with a stamp on it, telling me he fell for the glory of France. No body. No grave. I buried an envelope." Her voice cracked and she hated it, Marguerite could see her hating it. "Four years. Four years I have sold bandages to the wives of men who inform on their neighbors for coffee rations, and smiled, and wrapped their purchases in brown paper, and thanked them kindly. So do not stand there and tell me what the garrison does. I have watched what the garrison does every single day since I was twenty-six years old, and I am very tired of pretending it is anything but murder wearing a uniform."

The candles guttered in the draft off the stairs. Down among the shelves the old bones did not comment.

"So no," Odette said, steadier now, pressing her palms flat against her coat as if to iron herself back into shape. "I will not report him. Not because he deserves it. Because I am done doing the arithmetic their way." She looked at Voss. "You. How bad is the wound, honestly? Not the brave answer. The pharmacist answer."

"Fever's been climbing since morning. The edge of it smells wrong."

"It is wrong. You've got rot starting." She knelt beside him without ceremony, pulled the lamp close, peeled back the bandage with hands that had done this ten thousand times and had stopped asking permission of anyone years ago. Whatever she found there made her mouth thin. "You need carbolic and proper drainage, and you need laudanum tonight or the fever will take the last sense you have. Which means I need to bring these things here, which means someone in this town will see me carrying them, which means I am in this now." She sat back on her heels. "Well. That was efficient."

"I'm sorry," Marguerite started.

"Don't apologize. Apologizing is how people talk themselves into betraying each other — you apologize and apologize until the betrayal starts to seem like tidiness." Odette began repacking her satchel with quick precise movements. "Instead you'll both listen, because I didn't come here only to play nurse. I came because of what I heard at the garrison this morning, delivering their quinine like the good little collaborator I am."

Voss lifted his head.

"There's a train coming Thursday," Odette said. "Special carriage. The armistice commission — records audit. They're sweeping every municipal archive between here and the border, and this town is on the list because of the river crossing and because of—" she glanced at Voss, "—well. Because of whatever it is you two have been whispering about down here. They arrive Thursday. Three days. And there's an officer attached to the commission, a civilian really, some professor type, who has been asking about Aubry's atlas by name. Asking who inherited it. Asking whether the final plates were ever recovered."

The crypt seemed to contract around them. Marguerite felt the floorboards overhead — the loose one, the one over the north corner where the true plates slept in their oilcloth — press down like a hand.

"By name," she repeated. "Who's asking?"

"A commissioner named Delacroix. Sharp eyes. The garrison captain doesn't like him, which tells you everything." Odette rose, slung her satchel across her body. "Three days. If he finds what you're hiding, it won't matter whose promise whose master made to whom. They'll burn this building with both of you in it and call it administrative necessity." At the foot of the stairs she paused, one hand on the rail, and looked back at them — at the wounded deserter and the woman with ink on her fingers — and something in her face softened by a degree, no more.

"Henri used to say the war would end and the lying wouldn't," she said. "I thought he was being clever. He was just early." She climbed three steps, stopped again. "I'll come tomorrow evening with the medicine. Use the front bell twice — twice means me, once means anyone else, and if it rings once you put that man behind the bone shelves and you open the door looking bored. Can you look bored?"

"I've had practice," Marguerite said.

"I imagine you have." And then, quieter, almost to herself, almost to the dark: "Thursday, then. God help us, Thursday."

Her footsteps went up and away, and the street door closed with its ordinary click, and Marguerite stood in the candlelight listening to the ordinary sounds of a town that had no idea what was sealed beneath its floor.

Voss was watching her. "Why?" he asked. "Why would she risk that? For me?"

Marguerite thought about a printed card with a stamp on it. About an envelope in the ground.

"Because she lost more than either of us," she said, "and she refuses to lose politely anymore."

Above them, muffled by stone and timber, the church clock struck five. Three days. Somewhere east of the flooded river, a train was already being scheduled, a sharp-eyed man was already packing his files, and under the floorboards of the studio the atlas waited with its two truths — the one that would make her name, and the one that could hang them all.
