## 8. The Audit

They came on a Tuesday, in three grey automobiles that could not cross the river and so had to be ferried over, one at a time, while the town stood in the market square and pretended not to count them.

I counted them. I was carrying crates at the time — hired porter's coat, cap pulled low, hands wrapped in sacking against the cold — and a porter counts what he carries. It is the only way to be invisible. A man who stares at the commission's motorcars is a curious man; a man who grunts under the weight of a crate of surveying instruments is furniture.

Marguerite had drilled me on it for two days. *You are Henri Aubry, from upriver, deaf in the left ear since Verdun.* Deafness was her idea. "Deaf men are forgiven everything," she said. "They can't answer questions they didn't hear, and no one repeats themselves twice for a stranger." She had even put wax in my left ear, warm and foul-smelling, and I confess it worked better than any uniform I ever wore. The moment I couldn't fully hear, I stopped listening like an officer. Officers listen for threat. Porters listen for the next instruction.

The commission was four men and a woman. I catalogued them as they stepped off the ferry onto the flooded landing stage: two clerks with leather document cases, soft as dough; a major named Brissac whom I knew by reputation only — logistics, not combat, a man who had spent the war counting other men's boots; and then the fourth, the one Marguerite had warned me about without knowing she'd seen him.

*Watch the thin one,* she'd said. *He reads like you do.*

His name was Delorme, and he was not army. Civilian auditor, seconded from some ministry whose name would take an hour to say and mean nothing when said. Grey coat, no decorations, spectacles he cleaned constantly with a cloth that was already too dirty to help. He stepped off the ferry last, looked at the bridge wreckage downstream where the flood had chewed through the central span, looked at the water, looked at the town — and I understood immediately why Marguerite feared him. He wasn't looking at things. He was reading them.

And he read me. For perhaps two seconds, across forty meters of wet cobblestone, his eyes passed over a porter holding a crate and stopped, the way a finger stops on a line of text that doesn't scan. Then the major called his name and the line moved on, and I stood very still among the crates and let my heart do its work quietly, behind the ribs, where no one could audit it.

* * *

The atlas was presented in the town hall at ten o'clock, and I carried it there myself.

That was Marguerite's decision, and a cold-blooded piece of genius I still don't fully understand. "No one guards the atlas like the man who knows what it should weigh," she told me the night before, in the studio, with the true plates already swapped out and the forged ones drying flat between blotters. "If a stranger carries it, strangers will ask him questions. If you carry it, everyone will assume you're mine."

She'd made the substitutions over four nights. The fortification plates — the ones Ishikawa had died with his body across, the ones the armistice commission was never meant to see — were gone from their folio, replaced by plates she had drawn herself, faithful to the master's hand down to the tremor in the coastlines and the particular brown-black of his ink, which she had learned to mix from watching him and which she now mixed from memory with her sleeves rolled up and her face lit by one shaded lamp. What she gave the commissioners was a beautiful, finished, honest atlas of roads and rivers and elevations and grain routes. What she kept beneath her floorboards was the truth, folded in oilcloth, waiting for me.

I set the atlas on the table before the commission and opened the clasp with hands that did not shake, because I had ordered them not to, and hands obey orders if you've been giving them long enough.

Major Brissac did the talking first. He wanted production figures, transport ledgers, the state of the quays. Marguerite answered everything in a voice pitched exactly between deference and boredom — the voice of a woman who has explained her dead master's work many times to men who barely listened. And mostly they didn't listen. Mostly they turned pages with the reverence of men who have been told a thing is valuable and have decided to agree.

Delorme read.

That was the difference. Brissac flipped pages. Delorme read them, plate by plate, and took notes in a small black book, and every few minutes he cleaned his spectacles and looked at nothing, thinking. I stood against the wall with the empty carrying case under my arm and watched him read my death warrant and found, to my own disgust, that part of me was admiring the penmanship.

* * *

It went wrong at half past eleven.

Not loudly. Nothing about it was loud. Delorme simply stopped turning pages, went back three, and laid his finger on a plate showing the river approaches — the forged one, Marguerite's forgery, good enough that I had stared at it for an hour the night before trying to find the seam and failed.

"Madame Winterbourne." His voice was mild. "Your late master began this plate in March of 1917?"

"According to the logbook, yes. He worked on it through the summer."

"And completed it when?"

"In the autumn. September, I believe."

"Mm." Delorme turned the plate toward the light. "Then it interests me that the water level here—" his finger moved to the river's edge, to a line of hatching so fine I could not see the problem from where I stood, though my whole body had gone cold and attentive, the way it used to go before an artillery barrage lifted— "is drawn from a survey taken in the spring. There's a notation in the margin, very faint. Spring survey marks. If the plate was begun in March and finished in September, why does the waterline match the spring?"

Silence. The stove ticked. Somewhere outside, floodwater moved against the quay with a sound like slow breathing.

"I couldn't say," Marguerite said. "My master's methods were his own. He sometimes reworked old surveys into new plates. Perhaps—"

"Yes," Delorme said. "Perhaps." He cleaned his spectacles. He looked at the plate a long moment, and then — this was the terrible thing, the thing I will remember longer than anything else about that morning — he looked up and looked directly at me.

At the porter against the wall. At Henri Aubry, deaf in the left ear, holding an empty case.

"You there," he said. "You brought this in. Did you notice anything odd about the weight? Documents that have been rebound often sit differently in the binding."

I let the silence stretch exactly as long as a deaf man's would — too long, apologetic, one hand going to the wrong ear — and shook my head and said, "Beg pardon, monsieur?" in the flattest river-town accent I could manage, and Marguerite, without missing a beat, said, "Henri is hard of hearing, monsieur. Since Verdun."

"Ah." Delorme smiled, and it was not an unkind smile, which somehow made it worse. "Verdun takes its toll on all of us. Different currencies." And he turned back to the plate.

Brissac, bored, declared the morning's session concluded and the records satisfactory pending review, and the clerks began repacking their cases, and I carried the atlas back to its case and buckled it shut with fingers that felt borrowed. Delorme watched me buckle it. I am nearly certain he watched me buckle it. There is a specific way a man handles something precious, and there is a specific way a porter handles cargo, and I had chosen porter because it was safer, and in that moment I understood I might have chosen wrong.

* * *

We got away with it. That is the sentence I keep returning to, the one that tastes strange every time I say it even in my own head.

By one o'clock the commission had withdrawn to the Hôtel du Commerce to draft findings, and Marguerite dismissed "Henri" with a coin and a nod, and we met an hour later in the crypt where the candles wouldn't show through shuttered windows, and she asked me, in a whisper, whether I thought he suspected, and I told her the truth: that I didn't know, and that not-knowing was worse than knowing.

Because here is what I learned that morning, standing against a wall while another man read a lie: getting away with something is intoxicating in a way I hadn't anticipated. For two hours I was nobody — a deaf porter, a piece of furniture, beneath notice — and it was freedom of a kind I hadn't felt since before the war, since before the borders closed, since a novelist's apprentice swore an oath to a young lieutenant who believed promises were the kind of thing nations kept. For two hours the bounty on my head might as well have been printed in a language I couldn't read. I walked out of that room carrying the most dangerous documents in the province and no one saw anything but a laborer going home to his soup.

But intoxication fades, and what remains is Delorme's finger on that waterline, moving slowly, and his eyes lifting, once, deliberately, to the porter in the corner.

He had noticed something. Maybe not what it was. Maybe just that the room contained one object that didn't fit its label — a porter with an officer's posture, or a deaf man who flinched at a sound from the wrong direction, or simply a face he would see again and eventually place. Auditors like Delorme don't catch lies. They catch inconsistencies, and they file them, and they come back.

At dusk Odette came to the chapel door with bread and a look on her face that I had learned to dread more than garrison patrols.

"The thin one," she said, shrugging off the rain. "Delorme. He's been asking questions in the market. Not about the atlas." She set the loaf down and looked at us both. "About who works at the studio. About the woman who keeps lights burning past midnight." She paused, and her voice went quieter. "He asked me, Madame Ishikawa. By name. He asked whether the porter who carried the atlas this morning was a regular hire."

The crypt was very cold, and somewhere above us the flood pressed against the town like a patient animal, and Marguerite reached out and steadied herself against the stone wall, and I heard myself ask the question neither of us wanted answered:

"And what did you tell him?"
