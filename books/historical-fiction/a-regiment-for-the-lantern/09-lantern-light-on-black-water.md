## 9. Lantern Light on Black Water

*Voss*

He heard the boots before he saw the lanterns — the particular cadence of them, unhurried, official, a man walking because he could afford to.

Voss was at the window of the studio's back room, where Marguerite had moved him two days after the audit, when the knock came at the street door below. Three raps. A pause. Three more. The knock of a man who did not expect to be kept waiting and had never once been made to wait in his life.

Commissioner Aubry. He'd known it would come back to this. The man had lingered over plate forty-one — the forged one, the one Marguerite had inked in eleven hours of desperate work while Voss stood in the corner wearing a porter's coat, holding a crate he didn't need to hold. *The river's course is wrong here,* Aubry had said, tapping the paper with one gloved finger. *Or the river has changed its mind.* He had smiled when he said it, and the smile had stayed with Voss like a splinter working toward the heart.

"Marguerite." His voice was low, but she was already in the doorway, dressed, her hair pinned as though she hadn't slept — which she hadn't, but she wore it well. "He's come back."

"I know." She listened to the knocking. "Through the yard. The boat's already at the wall."

"You said three days."

"The river doesn't keep appointments and neither does he." She crossed to him, and for a moment her hand hovered near his sleeve without landing — the whole of their intimacy measured in that half-inch of air. "Odette will let him in. She'll keep him talking over the inventory. That gives us twenty minutes if we're lucky and ten if we're not."

"And if she keeps him longer than that?"

"Then she's a better liar than either of us." She pulled the floorboard up with both hands, and there they were: the true plates, wrapped in oilcloth, forty-one through fifty-eight, the fortifications no armistice commission was ever meant to see, the reason her master had died face-down on his own studio floor with his spectacles still folded neatly beside him. Voss looked at them and felt the old hunger rise — the officer's hunger, the map-hunger, the belief that truth in the right hands saves lives.

Then he remembered whose hands they'd be in, and how many hands there were between here and anywhere.

"Take them," Marguerite said. "Careful of the corners. The ink's still soft on the last ones."

They went down through the yard, past the dead rose canes, over the wall where the stones were slick with thaw. The flood lay below them like a country that had drowned its own borders — black water running fast where streets used to be, the church roof standing out of it like an island's peak. The rowboat waited, tied to the wall's iron ring, riding so low it seemed already halfway to surrender.

Voss lowered the plates in first, then himself, then reached up for her hand.

She took it. That much, at least, was no longer measured in inches.

* * *

*Marguerite*

Above them, behind them, Odette opened the door.

Marguerite couldn't hear the words from the water, only the music of them — Odette's voice pitched high and bright, the voice she used on men who thought themselves clever, all apology and charm and *of course, Monsieur le Commissaire, what an honor, forgive the mess, the flood took our cellar and my nerves along with it.* Aubry's answering voice was silk drawn slow over wire. Somewhere in the exchange Odette laughed, and Marguerite felt something crack open in her chest that was not quite grief and not quite gratitude.

Row, Elias. Don't listen. Row.

But you cannot row a flooded river and not listen, and so she listened too, pulling against a current that wanted them sideways, downstream, into the collapsed tannery or the drowned orchard where the apple trees stood to their shoulders in black water like drowned men still standing at attention. The lantern sat in the bow, hooded, throwing a coin of light no wider than a dinner plate. Beyond it: nothing. The town's lights behind them, small and warm and treacherous. Ahead, the far bank, a rumor.

"He'll ask about the lights," Voss said between strokes. "The neighbors reported them weeks ago. He wrote it down himself."

"He asked me about them at the audit. I told him I work nights. Artists do."

"He believed you?"

"He believed what it cost him nothing to believe." She shipped her oar to turn them past a fence line just breaking the surface, barbed wire strung with dead leaves like a garland. "That's the arithmetic of men like Aubry, Elias. They don't pursue truth. They pursue whatever costs them the least to carry."

Voss grunted — the sound of a soldier conceding a point to a civilian and hating the taste of it. Then: "The plates. When?"

Midriver. They had agreed on midriver, where the current ran deepest and fastest and anything that sank would sink beyond finding. But agreement was one thing and striking fire over moving water in February was another, and her hands were shaking now — not from cold, or not only from cold.

Because these were her master's last true work. Because under her name, or his, or any name, those plates would have made her. A career. A reputation. A place in the world that no woman cartographer had ever held and few men either. She had told herself for months that keeping them hidden was protection; she knew now it had also been hope, hoarded like coins in a mattress.

"Elias," she said. "If I strike this match, I want you to know what I'm burning."

He stopped rowing. The boat swung lazily in the current, the lantern light rocking across his face — the new scar at his temple, the eyes that had been young lieutenant's eyes in 1914 and were something older now.

"Tell me," he said.

"My future. My name in the histories. Everything he—" Her voice caught. "Everything Auguste died believing mattered more than his life. And yours too, possibly. If the commission ever learns what was in this boat tonight, desertion won't be the charge they hang on you. It'll be theft of state documents. It'll be espionage."

"It was never going to be anything else." He said it gently, the way you tell a patient a thing the patient has long known. "Marguerite. I watched those maps kill a man who loved them. I have spent six years watching maps kill everyone who loved them. Burn them."

She struck the match.

It flared once, absurdly small against the dark, and she touched it to the oilcloth's edge, and the fire took with a sound like a held breath released. She lifted the burning bundle clear of the boat and the water rose up to meet it, hungry, orange-lit, and for three seconds the whole river wore the plates' reflection — fortification lines written in flame on black water, bastions and battery positions blazing and gone.

Then the river closed over them, and there was nothing.

No evidence. No inheritance. No indictment. Only ash spiraling on the current and the smell of scorched linen and the two of them in a twelve-foot boat on a drowned road in the middle of winter, poorer by everything except each other.

Marguerite found she was laughing. Quietly, helplessly, the laugh of a person stepping off a ledge and discovering flight. Voss took up the oars again, and he was smiling too, that rare unguarded smile she had seen perhaps four times.

"What?" she managed.

"The atlas," he said. "You signed it with both our names."

"Yes."

"It needed a title page worth forging. Now it has one."

* * *

*Voss*

Behind them, a lantern kindled at the studio's upper window. Two figures crossed the glass — Odette, and a taller silhouette that could only be Aubry, moving from room to room with the patience of a man searching a house he intended to buy.

"They're in the studio," Marguerite whispered.

"Let them look. There's nothing left to find." But his arms ached from the rowing and his heart counted every second backward toward the shore. The far bank was close now — he could see the willows, the ferry landing drowned to its posts, the mud smell of the reed beds. Twenty minutes of water between them and the border road. Ten, if the current was kind.

It was not kind. Nothing in this war's aftermath had been kind; why should its rivers differ? The current took the boat and swung it downstream, and Voss dug the oars in and fought, and Marguerite leaned her weight against the tiller-side gunwale, and together they made the crossing the way people had made everything in the last five years — badly, barely, together.

The keel ground into mud and gravel. Voss stepped out into shin-deep freezing water and pulled the boat up the bank until it grounded for good, then turned to offer her his hand a second time.

From the far shore, faint across the black water, came a sound that stopped his blood: a bell. Not the church bell — the garrison bell, struck three times, the signal he had heard on a hundred nights for a hundred reasons, and most recently for one.

Deserter sighted.

"They know," Marguerite breathed. "Odette—"

"No." He scanned the opposite shore. The studio windows were dark again. No lanterns moved toward the river landing, no boats put out. Only the bell, ringing and ringing, and then, cutting through it, thin with distance: Odette's voice, high and carrying, calling something to unseen men in the town square.

"She's pointing them upstream," Voss said slowly. "Toward the mill. She's sending them the wrong way."

The bell stopped. Boots and voices receded into the town's interior, hunting a deserter who was, at that moment, standing hip-deep in mud on the border side of the river, holding the hand of the woman who had saved him twice — once with a promise inherited from a dead man, and once with a match.

Marguerite squeezed his fingers hard, once, and let go.

"Go," she said. "Before the current changes someone's mind about mercy."

He climbed the bank. At the top, at the edge of the willow dark, he turned back. She stood in the shallows beside the abandoned boat, the hooded lantern raised so he could see her face, and she called across the water — not softly, not secretly, but clearly, the way you say a thing you intend to have said forever:

"Two names, Captain! Remember the title page!"

And Voss, declared enemy, deserter, thief of nothing now that nothing remained to steal, raised his hand in salute and stepped into the trees, carrying eastward the only document that had survived this night: the memory of fire on black water, and a promise, at last, fully paid.

Behind him the river ran on, keeping its secrets, all of them burned.
