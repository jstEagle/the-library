## 1. The Weight of Small Silences

The trick to being invisible in a counting-house is arithmetic. Not the kind you do with figures—the kind you do with yourself. You must be useful enough that no one questions your presence, and dull enough that no one remembers it. I had spent three years calibrating the exact weight of myself at Corvane Trading, and I could tell you to the dram what it came to: slightly more than the furniture, slightly less than the ink.

I arrived each morning before the clerks, because a woman who arrives early is assumed to be eager, and an eager woman is assumed to be simple. Simplicity was my uniform. I wore it the way the directors wore their seals—every day, without thought, until it became part of the body.

The counting-house occupied the ground floor of the Corvane offices on Saltwharf Street, all high windows and higher ceilings, built by men who wanted their ledgers to feel like cathedrals and their workers to feel like dust motes. By half past seven the shutters were open and the light came in long grey bars across the trestle tables, and I was already three pages deep in yesterday's entries for the *Kestrel's Fortune*, which had come in from the spice islands with a cargo manifest that read like a confession nobody had bothered to write down properly.

"Jude." Master Ellery, the head clerk, didn't look up from his own desk when he said it. He never looked up. Looking up would acknowledge that he relied on me, and reliance between a master clerk and a bonded girl was something neither of us could afford to name. "The Vellamare customs copies for the *Fortune*. Where are they?"

"In the second drawer of your left-hand table," I said. "Beneath the harbor dues, where you put them Tuesday and then spent a quarter hour blaming the sweep-girl."

A pause. The quill above his ledger stopped moving.

"They were misfiled," he said.

"Yes, sir."

Another pause, longer. Then the quill resumed, and the matter was closed forever, because that is how silences work in this building—they are not empty, they are full, packed tight with everything everyone has decided not to say, and if you shook one hard enough the whole house would come apart.

* * *

I should explain what I am to Corvane, since Corvane itself would struggle to.

My father sold me into indenture the winter after he drowned—or rather, the company bought the debt my mother owed them, and my father's death made me the collateral that walked. Seven years of service against pearl-diving debts I never incurred, in a city I couldn't leave, doing work I was never formally trained for because formal training would have meant acknowledging what I was. On the books I am listed as a copying assistant. In practice I am the person who finds the errors before the auditors do, who reconciles the manifests against the customs copies, who knows which director marks his expenses twice and which captain waters his cargo reports—and none of it appears anywhere, because appearing anywhere would mean being someone, and being someone means being accountable, and accountability is the one commodity Corvane never trades in.

So I read faster than the clerks. That was the first thing anyone noticed about me, back when anyone noticed anything: I'd finish a page in the time it took Master Ellery to wet his pen. It wasn't cleverness, exactly. It was childhood. I learned letters from tide tables and cargo sheets on my father's pearl boat, sitting cross-legged on nets while the grown men argued about weights, and a girl who learns to read on a rocking deck learns quickly or not at all. But I kept the origin quiet. A diver's daughter who reads like a trained scrivener raises questions, and questions are holes in the hull, and I had already watched one boat go down.

By nine o'clock the counting-house was full—quills scratching, Ellery dictating figures to young Pem Halloway, the sweep-girl hauling ash buckets past the door with her eyes down. And I sat at my corner table with the *Kestrel's Fortune* spread before me, and I did the thing I did every day, which was notice.

Here is what I noticed that morning: the *Fortune* carried forty-one casks of indigo out of the spice islands according to her manifest, but the duty was paid on thirty-eight. Three casks unaccounted—not smuggled, mind; the numbers were too clean for smuggling. Smuggling hides. This didn't hide. It sat there in the margin like a bruise, three casks' worth of duty simply not paid, as though someone had decided the crown wouldn't look, or hadn't needed to be paid, or both. I copied the figures faithfully into my reconciliation sheet, exactly as instructed, and beside the discrepancy—in pencil, faint enough to erase—I wrote nothing at all, which is its own kind of entry.

Then I turned the page and found the same pattern again, and again, and by the time the noon bell rang I had found it eleven times across four ships, always three casks, always the same neat little wound in the arithmetic, and I understood two things. First: whatever it was, it wasn't new. Second: whoever was doing it trusted the books never to be read closely, which meant they didn't know about me, which meant—for one bright dangerous moment—I mattered more than I was allowed to.

I erased the pencil mark. Some findings you keep behind your eyes, where even you can't quite prove they exist.

* * *

Luncheon was bread and small beer in the yard behind the counting-house, shared with Odile Fenn, who kept the company laundry in the cellar level and who was the closest thing to a friend that a bonded girl can safely have. Odile was sixty if she was a day, forearms like mooring rope, and she had survived Vellamare by the opposite strategy from mine: she was so thoroughly seen—loud, opinionated, everywhere—that no one ever actually looked at her.

"You've got your thinking face on," she said, tearing her loaf in half and giving me the bigger piece, which she did every day and which I accepted every day because refusing kindness draws attention.

"I don't have faces."

"Everyone has faces. Yours is just quieter than most." She chewed, watching the gulls fight over the fish-market scraps beyond the wall. "Ellery shouted at you again."

"He thanked me, in his way."

"That man thanks people the way the sea drowns them. Slowly, and pretending it's a favor." She brushed crumbs from her apron. "You know he takes credit for your reconciliations. The quarterly report to the directors had your work in it signed with his name."

"I know."

"And that sits fine with you?"

I considered how to answer. The truth was that it sat fine with me the way a stone sits fine in a shoe—you learn to walk on it, and eventually you stop noticing, except on days when you take the shoe off and remember what feet were for. "It keeps me invisible," I said. "Invisible is safe."

Odile snorted, a sound like a kettle deciding against boiling. "Safe." She said it the way sailors say a wind direction they don't trust. "My girl, I've buried two husbands and a son in this city, and I'll tell you what I learned: invisible isn't safe. Invisible is just dying somewhere no one thinks to look."

I didn't answer that. There was a whole country inside me that didn't answer things—my father's face going blue-grey under a wool blanket while the pearl money drained away to Corvane, my mother selling her strand of pearls to pay the debt and then selling herself to the mainland within the year, the promise I'd made over a deathbed to a captain with salt-cracked hands who gripped mine and said *remember this family, Jude, whatever comes*. All of it sealed below the waterline where the inspectors don't check.

But Odile saw me anyway. That was the trouble with her. Three years of careful calibration, and this one old woman with her laundry kettles had somehow gotten a reading on the true weight of me, and I couldn't decide whether it was a comfort or a catastrophe.

* * *

The afternoon brought the customs men.

They came at two o'clock, two of them, grey coats and crown buttons, wanting the duty schedules for the quarter. Ellery received them with the bowed obsequiousness he saved for anyone who could hurt him, and I kept my head down over my copying while they stood at the master clerk's table and spoke in the low bored voices of men whose power lies entirely in what they might choose to notice.

And here is the thing I will carry to my grave: while they talked, one of them mentioned—offhand, to Ellery, in the tone of a man discussing weather—that the crown would soon be sending someone proper to reconcile Corvane's accounts. An auditor. Not the usual rubber-stamp men from the port office, but a real one, sent from the capital, with authority to open any book in the building.

"With the Marchetti business," the customs man said, "the ministers want everything looked at. Everything. Traitors leave fingerprints in strange places, Master Ellery."

Marchetti. Captain Ilya Marchetti, who had taught me knots on my father's boat, who had held my father's hand closed around a promise, who had stood at the graveside in the rain and said nothing, because he was a man who understood silences too. Declared an enemy of the crown eight months ago. Hunted now, his name spoken in this counting-house only in the flat careful voice reserved for plague ships and burning districts.

Ellery murmured assurances. The customs men left. The quills resumed their scratching as though the air hadn't changed temperature.

But I sat very still at my corner table, hands flat on the *Kestrel's Fortune* and its eleven neat wounds of missing duty, and felt the floor of my carefully calibrated life shift beneath me like a deck in weather. An auditor with authority to open any book. A hunt for traitors with instructions to look at everything. And somewhere in these ledgers, threaded through the columns like a vein of ore, discrepancies that somebody powerful had decided no one would ever find.

I told myself it was none of my affair. I told myself my business was copying figures and keeping my head at the correct angle of invisibility, and that in seven years—six now—my debt would clear and I would walk out of Corvane with nothing but my name, which was more than I'd walked in with.

That night I locked the office door behind me, as I did every evening, last one out because last one out is another form of unseen.

And on the company steps, in the blue hour before true dark, something was lying on the cold stone that had not been there that morning.

It was wrapped in sailcloth. It was small. And it was crying.
