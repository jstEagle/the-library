## 2. Left at the Door

The Corvane Trading Company taught me three things before it taught me arithmetic: that doors are heavier than they look, that silence is a currency with better exchange rates than coin, and that nothing good arrives before dawn.

I was up before dawn anyway. The counting-house never truly sleeps; it only dozes in shifts. Someone has to bank the stoves so the ink doesn't freeze, someone has to fetch the night-soil bucket from the clerks' room before the junior men wake and pretend they never used it, and someone has to be me, because being me means being the person nobody thinks about until the moment they need something carried, fetched, or forgotten.

So when I pushed open the great brass-studded door at the hour the gulls start their arguments, I was already thinking about coal and buckets, which is why my first thought on seeing the bundle on the steps was not *a child* but *someone has left laundry*.

My second thought corrected the first with a violence that knocked the breath out of me.

* * *

It was a good bundle. That's what I noticed, absurdly, while the world tilted: whoever had wrapped this infant had done it like a sailor, tight and weatherproofed, wool over linen over wool, tucked the way you'd tuck cargo you expected to survive a crossing. The knot at the top was a bowline. You don't tie a bowline unless you've spent years trusting your life to knots.

The baby slept. Small face, red-rimmed eyelids, a frown of profound professional displeasure, as though she had been consulted about none of this and intended to file complaints. A pearl hung at her throat on a cord of braided silk — grey, imperfectly round, the kind of pearl divers bleed for and traders lie about.

I knew pearls. I'd grown up with them under my fingernails, in my mother's hair, in the bottom of our boat where they rolled loose like teeth. I knew them well enough to know that one.

I also knew handwriting, because reading other people's ledgers is my trade and my sin, and the note pinned beneath the bowline was written in a hand I would know drowned, burned, or hanged.

*Jude.* Just my name first, as if we were still two people who could stand on a deck and speak plainly. Then, in that slanting, patient script:

*Forget her face. Forget you ever saw it. If anyone asks, she is no one's. That is the kindest thing I will ever ask of you, and the last.*

No signature. He didn't need one. Captain Ilya Marchetti had written my father's letters for him for twenty years — my father could haul a net all day but his letters came out like a man drowning in his own alphabet — and I had read every one of them pressed flat between my palms while the ink was still smelling of salt.

Captain Ilya Marchetti, whose name had been read aloud in Vellamare's market square eight months ago with the word *traitor* nailed to it like a price tag. Captain Marchetti, whose face was chalked on every customs wall from here to Saltreach, whose capture was worth more than my indenture cost to buy, multiplied by a number I didn't want to calculate.

I looked up and down the street. Empty, except for a cat conducting its own audit of the fishmonger's refuse, and the grey light coming off the water the way it does in Vellamare, like the whole city is lying at the bottom of a clean glass bottle.

Nobody saw. Nobody ever sees me. For three years that has been the entire architecture of my survival.

For the first time, I understood it could cut both ways.

* * *

Here is what I should have done, if I were the woman the Corvane Company believed it owned: I should have gone inside, woken the house steward, and reported an abandoned infant left on company property. The Company has procedures. The Company loves procedures the way a spider loves webbing. An abandoned child becomes a registry entry, then a parish entry, then a foundling-hospital entry, and by noon she'd have stopped being a person and started being paperwork, which in Vellamare is how most things stop being people.

I stood there with the cold eating through my skirts and did the arithmetic anyway. Foundling hospitals in this city take seven children in ten past their third winter. And a foundling with Marchetti's jaw — and it *was* his jaw, even on a face that small, that stubborn set that I'd watched across my father's deathbed — a foundling with that face, in a city where bounty clerks are paid per name matched... Paperwork can be searched. Paperwork can be seized. The crown had recently developed quite an appetite for seizing Corvane's paper.

The baby stirred and made a sound like a hinge that wanted oiling, and something behind my ribs that I'd kept locked and inventoried for three years rattled hard against its door.

I picked her up. She weighed less than a ledger. She fit into the crook of one arm as though she'd been measured for it, which, knowing Marchetti, she possibly had.

"Stupid," I told her quietly, because it was safer than saying anything true. "This is stupid, and I'm going to be hanged, and you have your father's chin."

She opened one eye, considered me, and closed it again. Wise. It's what I'd have done.

* * *

Odile Ferreza washes for half the counting-house and knows more about the Corvane directors than the directors' own mothers, because there is no document in this world more honest than a man's shirtsleeves. She's sixty if she's a day, forearms like mooring rope, and she has buried a husband, two sons, and every illusion she ever had about the sea, which she lists cheerfully whenever anyone suggests the water is romantic.

Her washing-yard sits behind the clerks' row, walled in with drying lines strung like rigging. I came to her the back way, through the gap in the wall that I suspect Odile maintains deliberately, since she uses it herself when she wants to buy brandy without the neighborhood pricing it into gossip.

She took one look at the bundle in my arms and put down the shirt she was wringing.

"No," she said.

"I haven't asked yet."

"You're breathing like a woman who's asked." She wiped her hands on her apron, which was already wet, which told me she'd been working since before dawn too, and came closer. "Jude Sterling. What is in that blanket."

"You know what's in the blanket."

"I know what it generally is. I want to hear you say it."

"A baby," I said. "Left on the steps. With a note."

Whose note, she didn't ask, because Odile asks questions the way surgeons make incisions — precisely, and only where necessary. She looked instead at my face, and whatever she saw there made her go very still.

"The captain," she said. Not a question either. Word travels in the servants' quarters the way water travels in a hull — everywhere, silently, and finding every weakness.

"There's a bounty," I said.

"There's always a bounty. There was a bounty on my husband once. Fish don't pay taxes and neither did he." She held out her arms, and I swear the baby transferred over like she'd been waiting for exactly those hands. "Well? Are you going to stand there drowning or are you going to tell me what you need?"

"Milk. Warmth. And for her to not exist."

"The milk I have. The warmth I have." Odile settled the infant against her shoulder with the ease of long practice, and the baby latched onto a fold of apron and began to gnaw it with tremendous seriousness. "The not existing — that's harder. That costs."

"How much?"

"Not money." She fixed me with a look I'd seen her give clerks who tried to pass off stained linen as merely *aged*. "That kind of debt, girl, gets paid in years. In sleep. In always looking over your shoulder and teaching everyone around you to look over theirs. I'm old enough to afford it. Are you?"

I thought about my cot in the attic, my spotless conduct record, the careful grey person I had built out of three years of never being noticed. I thought about my father coughing seawater into a cloth while Marchetti held his hand, and a promise I'd been too young to understand was binding.

"Yes," I said, and heard my voice come out steadier than I had any right to expect.

"Then she stays in my yard until dark, and after dark in my room, and we say she's my sister's grandchild come down from the coast, because my sister's grandchildren are numerous, unattractive, and nobody checks on them." Odile bounced the baby once, expertly. "And Jude — the note. Burn the note."

"I—"

"Burn it. Tonight. Ink remembers longer than people do, and yours is a memory someone might search." She turned away toward the house, the bundle bobbing against her shoulder, then paused. "You won't burn it."

"No," I admitted.

"No," she agreed, without judgment, which is Odile's rarest quality. "But fold it small and keep it somewhere a man wouldn't think to look, and pray to whichever saint handles fools that you never need it."

Then she was gone through her door, and I stood alone in her yard among the drying shirts, listening to a baby cry briefly and then settle, feeling like a woman who had just stepped off a dock in the dark and hadn't yet hit the water.

* * *

I went back to the counting-house and opened the ledgers and was brilliant and invisible for nine hours, as trained.

But here is what I could not stop seeing, in the margins of the shipping manifests, in the columns I reconcile without thinking: the word *pearl*, appearing more often than it should. Cargo entries for pearl-weight, marked and priced and routed through ships whose names I knew — knew from a childhood spent rolling them off my tongue like prayers. One ship above all.

*The Meridian*, out of Vellamare, master I. Marchetti.

And beside the newest entry, in the auditor's advance correspondence that had arrived by fast packet that very morning — the crown's man announcing he would come to reconcile the books — a line I read twice, then a third time, my finger resting on the page like a diver hovering over something glinting below.

He wasn't coming for the ledgers, that letter suggested.

He was coming for names.

I closed the book. Somewhere behind the drying lines, a baby who officially did not exist was learning to exist in hiding, and I had until the crown's auditor arrived to decide whether I was the invisible woman Corvane bought —

or my father's daughter.
