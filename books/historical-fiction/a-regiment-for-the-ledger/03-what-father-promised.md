## 3. What Father Promised

There is a particular kind of arithmetic that happens at three in the morning, when the counting-house is dark and the baby two floors up has finally stopped crying, and the sum you arrive at is: *I am committing treason.*

Not the romantic kind. Not the kind with trumpets and a scaffold and your name shouted by crowds. The other kind — the quiet kind, committed in a laundress's linen closet with a wet nurse's borrowed milk and a pearl on a cord that I had no right to keep and less right to return.

I sat on my cot with the note unrolled across my knees for perhaps the hundredth time since dawn.

*Forget the face.*

Captain Ilya Marchetti's handwriting. I would have known it blind. He wrote his *g*s like little anchors, because he was the sort of man who thought jokes should be embedded in everything, even penmanship. He had taught me to write my letters on the back of cargo manifests when I was seven, steadying my hand with fingers thick as mooring line. *Press hard, little fish,* he'd say. *The sea erases soft things.*

The sea had erased him too, or the crown meant to. Three weeks ago the proclamation went up on the customs house: ILYA MARCHETTI, ENEMY OF THE CROWN, WANTED FOR TREASON AGAINST THE REALM'S SHIPPING. The reward figure beneath it was generous enough to buy a small ship or a large conscience. Half the port had spent the intervening days developing sudden memories of having seen him everywhere.

And now here was his handwriting, delivered to my door with his daughter in its arms, instructing me to do the one thing I had spent three years learning never to do: be seen holding something that mattered.

* * *

My father died on the deck of the *Perla Viva*, eleven miles off Vellamare, with a ruptured lung full of seawater and a captain's coat draped over him because Marchetti insisted a diver deserved better than canvas.

I was fourteen. I had been below, wringing out my own hair after the third dive of the morning, when the shout came down. By the time I reached the deck, they had already laid him against the mast, and the whole crew had arranged itself into that terrible circle sailors make — close enough to witness, far enough to pretend it isn't happening.

"Jude." My father's voice sounded like someone else wearing it. "Come here, little fish."

I came. Of course I came. There is no version of that moment where I don't come.

He gripped my wrist — not gently; he hadn't the strength left for gentle — and pressed something into my palm. A pearl. Not one of the company's graded stock, but an odd one, grey-rose, misshapen, the kind the graders discard. "Your mother's," he said. "She wanted you to have the ugly ones. Said the perfect pearls belong to everyone and the strange ones belong to us."

"I don't want it," I said, because grief makes children honest. "I want you."

"Well." A bubble of blood at his mouth, which he wiped away with the dignity of a man brushing off crumbs. "Can't be helped."

Marchetti knelt on his other side. I remember being struck, even then, even at fourteen, by how wrong it looked — a captain kneeling, his fine coat soaked to the knee in another man's blood. Marchetti who joked about everything. Marchetti who wasn't joking.

"Tomas," he said. "Hold on. We'll make harbor, we'll get you—"

"You'll listen," my father said, and it was astonishing, the authority in it, a dying deckhand commanding a captain, and the captain obeying. "If ever my child can shelter yours — she will. Swear it."

"Shelter mine?" Marchetti laughed, or made a sound shaped like a laugh. "Ilya, you great fool, I haven't so much as—"

"Not yet," my father said. His eyes had gone somewhere past all of us, past the rigging, maybe past the world. "But a man like you will. And a girl raised on ledgers and pearl-beds learns to keep things hidden. Swear it on the boat. On the water. On her mother's grave if she had one, God rest her."

And Marchetti — I can still see this — looked at me. Not at my father. At me. As though asking permission of the person actually being conscripted into the bargain.

I nodded. What else does a child know to do?

"On the water," Marchetti said. "On my name. If ever your child needs shelter from mine, Jude Sterling, she'll find it. And if ever mine needs shelter—" he squeezed my father's hand once, hard, "—she will. Both ways. It cuts both ways or it's worth nothing."

"It's worth nothing anyway," my father said, with the ghost of his usual grin. "Worthless promises are the only kind a poor man can afford to keep." And then, because he was my father and sentiment embarrassed him worse than drowning did: "Jude. The ugly pearls. Don't let them grade you."

He was gone before the *Perla Viva* turned for home. Marchetti carried him ashore himself, over the objections of every man aboard, and stood at the pauper's burial with his hat off, and afterward pressed coin into my landlady's palm and told me, crouching to my height the way nobody else ever bothered to, "You write to me, little fish. Anything you need. Anything. That promise runs both directions, remember."

I was fifteen before I stopped writing. Not because he stopped answering — he answered every letter, always, in those anchor-bottomed g's — but because the indenture papers came, and Corvane clerks read outgoing correspondence from their charges, and I had learned already what silences keep people safe. The last letter I sent him said only: *Doing well. Don't wait on word. Some doors are best kept shut from both sides.*

He sent back a single line: *Doors open, little fish. That's what they're for.*

* * *

Now I sat in the dark doing the sums.

Here is what treason costs in Vellamare, per the current schedule posted outside the assizes: harboring a traitor, death. Harboring a traitor's child — the law is vague on infants, which means it defaults to whatever the magistrate had for breakfast. Concealing knowledge of treason, transportation to the colonies. Aiding and abetting, whatever the crown decides it feels like that week.

Here is what keeping a promise costs: everything above, plus the certain knowledge that I had been warned. Nobody had tricked me. A bundle appeared on the steps and I picked it up. Free choice, exercised at four in the morning by a woman who prided herself on making no free choices at all.

*It cuts both ways or it's worth nothing.*

That was the part that kept catching in my chest like a fishhook. Because the promise had been symmetrical, once — shelter flowing either direction between two families too poor to own anything but each other's loyalty. But look at the ledger now. Marchetti's side of the account: hunted, attainted, his name nailed to every post in the harbor. My side of the account: three years of invisibility, a cot, a wage, and now an infant.

He hadn't asked me for anything in six years. Hadn't written, hadn't summoned, hadn't spent the promise the way a man spends credit. And then the one time he drew on it — the *only* time — he'd done it with a note telling me to forget the face. As though he expected me to refuse. As though he'd wrapped his daughter up and set her on the steps of a trading company half-hoping the tide would take the decision out of his hands.

Which meant he believed he might be caught. Which meant he believed the danger was real enough to spend his child on.

Captains don't abandon daughters on doorsteps over ordinary trouble. Ordinary trouble buys passage abroad. This was the kind of trouble where ports close and names get watched and a man with a child moves slower than a man without one. Whatever Marchetti had done or been accused of doing, he'd judged the doorstep safer than his own arms.

And the pearl on the cord — I took it out again, turned it in the moonlight through the window. Grey-rose. Misshapen. From the same bed as the one my father pressed into my palm, or near enough that the difference was a matter of fathoms. He hadn't just sent me his daughter. He'd signed the message. *You know whose this is. You know why you're the one.*

Somewhere overhead, the baby stirred and began to fuss — a thin, determined sound, nothing like crying yet, more like negotiation. Odile would have her. Odile had taken one look at the bundle on the steps, one look at my face, and said, "Well. Bring her up before the street wakes, then, and don't tell me anything I shouldn't know until you must." The women of the laundry rows have been running an unofficial government of Vellamare for longer than the company's charter, and they ask fewer questions than any auditor alive.

But questions were coming. Questions always came. A child appears; a child must eat, sleep, cry, be registered or explained. The company counted everything — that was the entire point of the Corvane Trading Company, that existence itself could be reconciled against a master ledger. Sooner or later some clerk with neat hands would notice an extra mouth in the laundry wing, the way I noticed discrepancies, because noticing discrepancies was the whole trade of the place, and I had taught half the junior men how to do it better.

I had hidden my father's trade for three years. Divers' daughters don't get hired into counting-houses except by clerical error, and errors get corrected when discovered. I had buried the accent, the pearl-scarred fingertips, the way I could hold my breath through a long recitation without thinking. All of it folded flat and packed away like cargo.

Now Marchetti had put a living, breathing discrepancy into my arms and dared me to reconcile it.

*Forget the face,* the note said.

"No," I said aloud, to the empty room, and heard how small my voice sounded and hated it. Then, louder, testing the weight of it: "No."

The promise wasn't memory anymore. That was the thing I understood, sitting there in the dark with a traitor's pearl in my fist. For six years it had been a keepsake, a story, a dead man's whimsy — safe because it demanded nothing. Now it had come due, the way debts do, all at once and with interest, and I could feel it changing shape inside me: from something remembered into something owed.

My father had taught me the pearl-beds. You don't take the shell that's ready to give; you take the one that fights you, because the fighting ones hold the pearls worth keeping. He'd also taught me, in the same breath, when to surface. Every diver knows the moment. The chest starts to burn, the edges of the world go silver, and you choose — rise easy, or push deeper for one more shell and risk never rising at all.

I had been surfacing easily for three years.

Below me, faint through the floorboards, the baby fussed again, and Odile's voice murmured something low and soothing, and I thought: *the sealed holds are in the east cellar, and the manifests from the southern routes pass through reconciliation in the spring, and Cassian Roe arrives Thursday.*

I didn't know the last part yet. But the first parts were already turning over in me, smooth and cold as shells in a current — and by Thursday, when the crown's auditor stepped off the packet boat with his immaculate cuffs and his crown commission, the current had already decided for me which direction it ran.

I only hadn't told myself so yet.
