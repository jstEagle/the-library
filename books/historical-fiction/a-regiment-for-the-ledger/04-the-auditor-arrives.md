## 4. The Auditor Arrives

Jude

The crown arrived on a Tuesday, which was fitting. Tuesdays were the day Corvane's clerks did their worst arithmetic, and the company had always believed that if you were going to be ruined, it should be done efficiently.

I heard him before I saw him—the front doors opening at an hour when even the gulls hadn't finished arguing—and then the particular quality of silence that follows a man who expects rooms to rearrange themselves around him. I didn't look up from my column of tallow weights. Looking up is how you become a person, and being a person in the counting-house of the Corvane Trading Company was a luxury item I could not afford.

"Which one keeps the seizure ledgers?" the voice asked.

Director General Hask's answer came back smooth as oiled rope. "Merrit handles those."

"Merrit," the voice said, and I heard pages turning—someone else's pages, turned by someone else's hands, which in a counting-house is roughly equivalent to someone touching your teeth. "Merrit transposes columns like a man copying scripture he doesn't believe in. Who actually reconciles them?"

The silence that followed had a shape, and the shape pointed at me.

I kept my pen moving. Ink dries crooked if you flinch, and my father taught me that much before the sea took him: *steady hands, Jude-girl, steady hands or the pearls go to the bottom.* He'd meant diving. He'd meant everything, though I only understood that later.

"You," the voice said, arriving beside my desk like weather. "Name."

"Sterling." I gave him my eyes for exactly as long as politeness required and no more. "Ledger clerk, third class."

He was younger than I expected, the crown's man. Grey coat cut well enough to be expensive but not well enough to be noticed, which told me he understood invisibility too—he just wore his as a uniform instead of a shroud. His face had the terrible patience of a fisherman watching a line.

"Third class," he repeated. "And how long have you been third class?"

"Three years."

"Doing third-class work?" He set down the ledger he'd been carrying—Corvane's outbound shipping record, the one I'd spent two years learning to read the way other people read faces. "Because this entry here, the *Meridian*'s cargo manifest from March, carries a correction in the margin that no third-class clerk would dare make. Someone flagged a weight discrepancy against the customs house figures. Quietly. In pencil."

Pencil, I thought. Of all my sins, the pencil would be the one to find me out.

"Mistakes get corrected," I said.

"Mistakes do." He smiled. It was not an unkind smile, which made it worse. Kindness from a hunter is just bait with better manners. "Thank you, Clerk Sterling. I'll want you available."

He moved off toward Hask's office, and I looked down at my column of tallow weights and discovered my hands were not steady after all. My father would have forgiven me. The sea forgives nothing, but fathers forgive their daughters most things.

In the laundry yard behind the counting-house, under a basket of sheets that smelled of lye and lies, Odile's charge slept on, unaware that the crown had come to count noses. I had counted them myself that morning. One nose. One small fist gripping a cord with a single pearl on it. One note in a dead man's best friend's handwriting, folded into my apron pocket where it sat like a coal.

*Forget the face,* Marchetti had written. As if faces were things you could put down. As if I hadn't spent three years memorizing every face in this building precisely so I'd know which ones to fear.

Cassian Roe

They always think the clerk is furniture.

I have audited eleven houses of trade in six years, and in every one there is a person the directors walk past as though walking past a chair—a copyist, a tally-keeper, a woman with ink-stained cuffs—who knows more about the company than its own board. The directors pay these people almost nothing and trust them with everything, because trust, unlike salary, costs nothing to give.

Clerk Sterling was the best example of the species I had yet encountered.

I watched her for three days before speaking to her again. This is my method: watch first, speak second, arrest last. On the first day she processed forty-one entries without a single error while simultaneously correcting, in pencil, a transposition error made by the aforementioned Merrit—who, I confirmed, had signed her work as his own for the better part of two years. On the second day she anticipated the requisition form Hask would demand before he demanded it, and had it ready, and received no thanks, and showed no expectation of any. On the third day, when a junior clerk dropped a folio and loose pages scattered, she reassembled them in correct order in nine seconds flat, by sight, the way a card sharp reads a deck.

A woman like that does not remain invisible by accident. She remains invisible by discipline. And discipline of that quality is usually built over something. A cellar. A grave. A secret.

My commission from the Admiralty court was plain enough: reconcile the Corvane Trading Company's ledgers against the crown's record of seizures arising from the treason proceedings against Captain Ilya Marchetti. The official theory, the one printed in the gazettes, was elegant. Marchetti, sworn friend of the late diver Tomas Sterling and hero once of the pearl fleet, had turned pirate-traitor, running arms through Vellamare under cover of legitimate cargo, and Corvane—poor, wounded Corvane—had cooperated fully with the crown in seizing his shipments and exposing his network. The directors had testified beautifully. The director general had wept, I was told, at the appropriate moment.

But elegant theories are what men build when they want a story to close neatly, and I have never once closed a ledger that closed neatly. Ledgers do not lie so much as they cough—small irregularities, marginal corrections, pencilled doubts—and if you listen long enough, they cough up the truth.

Sterling's pencil-marks were the finest coughs I'd ever heard.

There was something else. The registry of indentures listed her father: *Tomas Sterling, diver, drowned in service of Corvane's pearl fleet.* And the pearl fleet had been captained, until four years ago, by Ilya Marchetti. Sympathizers of the traitor were to be identified and reported—that clause sat in my commission like a hook sits in bait, and I confess I did not examine it too closely. A career is a career.

On the fourth day I brought her tea.

This is not kindness. It is technique. People hold warm cups and tell you true things; I have seen hardened smugglers confess over chamomile. I set the cup at the corner of her desk, where it would not interfere with her work, and observed that she noted it, catalogued it, and assigned it a threat level—all in the space between two pen-strokes.

"Clerk Sterling," I said. "Do you know why I'm here?"

"To reconcile the books against the seizures," she said. "You said so Tuesday. In front of everyone."

"I did. And yet the books have been reconciled twice already, unofficially, in pencil, in your hand—or a hand very like yours—in the week since I arrived. You've been auditing the auditor." I let that sit. "I'm curious whether you found anything."

She looked at me then, properly, and I will record honestly what I saw: not fear. Calculation wearing fear's clothing, the way a spy wears a servant's coat. "I found that Merrit can't add," she said. "I found that the customs house rounds down and Corvane rounds up, and the difference buys Director Hask a new coat every quarter. I found nothing about Captain Marchetti, sir, because I keep cargo ledgers, not history books."

"Captain Marchetti," I repeated. "Not 'the traitor.' Not 'that man.' *Captain* Marchetti."

Her pen paused—one heartbeat, perhaps half of one—and resumed. It was the finest half-heartbeat of self-betrayal I have witnessed in eleven houses of trade, and I have witnessed some remarkable ones.

"Titles are habits," she said.

"So are loyalties." I drank my tea and left the cup with her, because a returned cup ends a conversation and a kept cup extends it. "I'll want the seizure cross-references by Friday. The ones in pencil are acceptable. The ones in ink should be true."

Jude

He knew. Not everything—thank the deep water, not everything—but he knew the shape of me the way you know the shape of a keyhole before you've found the key.

That night I sat in Odile's kitchen with the baby asleep against my shoulder and turned the day over like a stone, looking for what lived underneath.

"He gave you tea," Odile said, wringing a sheet hard enough to crack walnuts. "Crown men don't bring tea unless they mean to stay for supper."

"He's hunting Marchetti's sympathizers. That's the commission. Everyone in this building who ever shared a drink with the captain is a name on his list, and I—" I stopped.

"You're worse than a name," Odile said. She had a laundress's gift for stating facts at the temperature they occurred. "You're his daughter. The diver's girl. If he pulls the registry threads far enough—"

"He already pulled them. I saw him with the indenture book yesterday." I shifted the baby, whose breath came fast and hot against my collarbone, feverish-sweet. She'd been restless all day, and Odile had brewed willow bark and muttered about wet nurses and discretion, and I had thought: *this is what drowning feels like from the inside. Calm surface, everything pulling down.*

"Then decide quick, girl," Odile said. "Because a fever doesn't wait for your courage, and neither does the crown."

I looked down at the small face—Marchetti's jaw, or so I imagined; I'd never met the child's mother, never learned her name, knew only the note and the pearl and the promise made on a pitching deck while my father coughed seawater into a cloth. *If ever my child can shelter yours, she will.* My father had traded his last honest hour for that sentence, and I had spent three years pretending the debt was buried with him.

Now the auditor wanted my pencils. Now the registry wanted my bloodline. Now a fever wanted the child.

And underneath it all, in the margins where I'd marked discrepancies no one asked me to mark, something else was surfacing—shipments funded by money I knew the smell of, pearl money, *my mother's* pearl money, entered under fronts and struck through by seizures and signed away by men who had wept beautifully at the right moments.

Friday, Roe had said. He wanted my cross-references by Friday.

He thought he was setting a trap. He didn't understand that he was handing a drowning woman a rope and asking her to please mind where she tied it.

I kissed the feverish forehead, tucked the note deeper into my apron, and began, in my head, to draft the most dangerous ledger entry of my life.
