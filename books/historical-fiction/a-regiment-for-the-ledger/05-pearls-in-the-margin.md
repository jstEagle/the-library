## 5. Pearls in the Margin

The trouble with a lie, my father used to say, is that it has to be fed. He said it about the sea mostly—about how the tide chart you fudge to please a harbor master will drown you two seasons later—but it applies just as well to ledgers. A false entry is a hungry thing. It eats its neighbors. And Corvane's books had been eating for years.

I found the first tooth on a Tuesday.

The auditors had left the counting-house at dusk, Cassian Roe among them with his coat folded over one arm like a man who owned nothing worth stealing and wanted everyone to know it. The clerks had gone with him. For three years I'd stayed after hours to true up columns, because a girl who works late is invisible twice over—once for being a girl, once for being tired—and because I liked the counting-house empty. In the day it hummed with men performing competence. At night it was just arithmetic, and arithmetic never lied to make itself look better.

Mostly.

The entry was in the spring volume of the coastal manifests, page 214, a shipment of dyewood out of Vellamare aboard the *Kestrel's Grace*. Ordinary. Except the freight charges were paid in pearl script—the little chit-marks the company used when a buyer settled in gemstone rather than coin—and the lot number of those pearls matched an entry I knew by heart before I ever learned to read a ledger.

Lot 9-11. My mother's last dive.

You think grief fades. It doesn't. It just learns your handwriting. My mother went down off the Serpent Reefs when I was eleven, one of the free divers the company contracted before they decided freedivers drowned too expensively and switched to dredges. Her pearls came up in a basket, her body didn't come up at all, and my father signed the receipt for both with a hand so steady it frightened me more than crying would have. Lot 9-11. Forty-one pearls, graded second-clear, sold to the Corvane Trading Company. I was eleven years old and I memorized the number the way other children memorized prayers.

Now it sat in the margin of a dyewood shipment, dated eight months after she died.

Pearls don't rot. That's the cruelty of them. Coin gets spent, grain gets eaten, but a pearl waits in a vault as patient as sin until somebody moves it. So either the company was selling my mother's pearls over and over—which happened, everyone knew it happened—or this particular entry was something else. A marker. Money moving through the books wearing a costume.

I copied the entry onto scrap, then sat very still in the dark and listened to the building settle, the way a ship settles, groaning through its joints.

Here is what I should have done: closed the book, burned the scrap, gone home, and thought very hard about nothing. Three years of indenture had taught me that curiosity in a counting-house is a disease with one cure. But my father also used to say that a diver who surfaces too fast dies of her own breath, and some part of me—eleven years old again, holding a receipt for everything we owned—had been holding that breath for nine years.

I turned back one page. Then ten. Then I pulled the winter volume off the shelf and worked backward, and the pattern opened under my fingers like a reef at low tide.

It took me four nights to map it properly, working after hours while Odile minded—well. While Odile minded what Odile minded. I'll come to that. The pattern itself was simple once you saw it, which is the mark of good fraud: it doesn't hide, it just wears so many coats you stop looking at its face.

Six shipments over eighteen months. Dyewood, salt fish, canvas—dull cargo, honest cargo, the kind nobody inspects. Each one paid for wholly or partly in pearl script, and each pearl lot traced back, if you followed the chain far enough, to the same source: a private account kept under a name that meant nothing. *Marrow & Sons, factors.* No Marrows existed. No sons either. I checked the personnel registry, the guild rolls, even the parish records Odile's cousin kept for the church of Saint Ferren. Marrow & Sons had never bought bread in Vellamare.

But Marrow & Sons had paid for six ships' cargoes, and all six ships had been reported seized by crown patrols within weeks of sailing. Seized for carrying contraband. Seized on intelligence. Seized, inspected, condemned, and their cargoes auctioned—and here was where my hands started to shake, because the auction records weren't in the coastal volumes at all. They were in the seizure annexes, the ones only senior clerks and directors touched. I couldn't read those from the floor. What I could read was the insurance.

Every seized ship had been insured through Corvane's own underwriting desk. Every claim had been paid in full, promptly, to a single beneficiary: Marrow & Sons, factors.

The company insured a phantom against losses on ships it had apparently loaded, informed upon, and condemned, and then paid itself back through a name that had never drawn breath. Six times. I counted the sums twice, then a third time standing up and walking around the room, because the total made me dizzy. Enough money to buy a ship outright. Enough money to buy several. Enough money to outfit a fleet and call it smuggling and never once dirty your own hands, because every crime wore the uniform of an accident.

And then there was Marchetti.

Captain Ilya Marchetti, my father's sworn friend, the man who'd taught me to splice line and swear in three languages, stood accused of treason: of running arms to the crown's enemies, of using Corvane hulls to do it, of betraying the company that had employed him since he was a boy no older than I was now. The evidence was shipping manifests. His signature on lading documents for the seized vessels. His handwriting, his name, his neck.

I found one of those manifests on my third night. The *Amity*, seized eleven months past, manifest signed *I. Marchetti, master*. Except the ink was wrong. I've handled Marchetti's writing my whole life—he wrote letters to my father every season, big rolling letters like a man carving oars—and Marchetti pressed hard enough to emboss the page. This signature floated on top of the paper like a leaf on water. The date was wrong too; he'd been laid up in port with a broken wrist that month, and a man with a broken wrist signs nothing, not even his own ruin.

They hadn't even tried very hard. Why would they? Who reads a dead diver's daughter's memory against a director's word?

I put the manifest down and discovered I was angry in a way I had no practice being. My anger before this had always been small and useful: anger at short rations, at a foreman's wandering hands, at the world's general opinion that girls like me were furniture. Manageable anger. This was a different animal. This was grief that had been sitting in a cellar for nine years, waiting, and had finally found a door.

My mother's pearls. Forty-one of them, graded second-clear, hauled up from the dark by a woman whose lungs gave out before her courage did—and the company had taken them, moved them through the books like contraband themselves, used them to dress a theft, and spent the profit hanging my father's friend for it. She had died for those pearls. Literally, plainly, without poetry. And they'd become a costume for fraud.

*Forget the face,* Marchetti had written on the note left with the baby at our door. As if I could forget anything. As if forgetting were a thing this family had ever been offered or had ever managed.

I sat down at the long table in the empty counting-house and did what I do best. I made lists. Which ships, which lots, which dates, which signatures. Where the chain could be proven from the open volumes alone, and where it ran into the sealed annexes I couldn't touch—the original manifests, unaltered, locked in the company hold, the ones that would show what the *Amity* actually carried before someone's careful pen got to it. Proof lived behind a lock. Of course it did. Lies are expensive to keep; they get rooms of their own.

Somewhere above me a shutter banged, and I flinched like a thief, which is what I supposed I now was—a thief of truths, the worst kind, because stolen truth wants witnesses and witnesses want protection and I had neither. I had a laundress with a sick infant sleeping behind her kitchen, a promise to a dead man, and a head full of numbers that could hang me faster than they'd hang any director.

And then, folding my papers small enough to fit inside my sleeve, I understood the shape of the thing entire, and it stopped me cold on the stairs.

Marchetti hadn't sent the baby to me because I owed him. He'd sent her to me because I was the only person alive who could read these books and *feel* them—who had lot numbers for inheritance instead of gold, who would recognize her mother's pearls the way another orphan recognizes her mother's shawl. He hadn't trusted my loyalty. He'd trusted my grief. Somewhere in hiding, hunted for crimes built out of my own mother's bones, he had gambled the last thing he had on a drowned woman's daughter doing exactly what I had done these four nights: reading backward, diving deep, refusing to surface fast.

He'd known the company would frame him. Maybe he'd known with whose money they'd pay for the rope.

I blew out the lamp and let the dark have the room. Tomorrow Roe would be back at the long table with his folded coat and his gentle questions, and the auditors would reconcile, and somewhere in the sealed hold lay the original manifests like a heart waiting to be cut out. I had a child to protect, a name I'd spent three years keeping blank, and now a list.

Grief, I decided on the stairs, makes poor company but excellent ballast. Kept low in the hull, it keeps you upright in weather.

I went home the long way, past the harbor, where the *Amity* sat at anchor under crown seal with her holds padlocked—waiting, like everything else in this city, for someone brave enough or stupid enough to open her.

I intended to be one or the other.
