## 6. A Name in the Registry

The trouble with ledgers is that they are honest even when the men who keep them are not. I have spent eleven years learning this truth in port offices from here to the Salt Reaches, and it has never once failed me: a clerk can lie with his mouth all day long, but his hand grows tired of it by the second column. Numbers drift. Margins fill with little confessions. Somewhere in every counting-house there is a page where someone wrote the true figure and then thought better of it.

I had come to Vellamare to reconcile Corvane Trading's books against the crown seizures of Captain Ilya Marchetti's fleet, and to do a quieter piece of work beneath that one — to find out who in this city had loved the traitor enough to help him. The Director General's writ gave me authority over the first task and plausible deniability for the second, which is the usual shape of such documents.

I will confess something now, since no one is grading me but posterity: I expected Corvane to be easy. Companies like Corvane are always easy. They commit their crimes in the same handwriting as their virtues, because they cannot be bothered to hire a second penman for villainy. By my third evening in the guest chamber above the counting-house I had already found four shipments entered twice under different flags, and a warehouse rent paid on a building the company did not officially own. The directors were not criminals so much as accountants who had discovered ambition.

But it was not the smuggling that kept me at my desk past midnight. It was the girl.

* * *

You notice her the way you notice a well-set typeface — only after reading ten pages without stumbling. Jude Sterling, junior reconciler, third desk from the window in the long room. A woman doing reconciliation work in a house that otherwise employed women to carry ink and carry children, which was itself an anomaly worth a note. And she was good. Better than good. I watched her work through the glass partition on my second morning, ostensibly reviewing procedures, actually watching her hands.

She read columns the way other people breathe — without appearing to do it at all. A ledger would go into her hands closed and come out open, corrected, annotated in pencil so faint it might be mistaken for dust. Twice I saw her flag an entry that the senior clerk had signed off on, and twice the senior clerk changed the figure without argument, without even looking at her, as though correcting himself had been his idea all along. She let him believe it. That was the part that interested me. Most clever people cannot resist letting you know they are clever. This one had built a career out of being underestimated, and she maintained it with the discipline of a woman maintaining a disguise.

People maintain disguises for two reasons, in my experience. Either they are hiding something they've done, or they are hiding something they are. I find the second kind far more interesting, and considerably more dangerous.

So on my fourth night I sent for the indenture registries. All of them. Five years of contracts, apprenticeships, and bonds, the great dull census of everyone in Vellamare who worked for their bread under another man's seal. It is tedious work, cross-referencing, and I do most of it myself because clerks see names and I need to see shapes — patterns, repetitions, the way certain surnames cluster around certain ships like barnacles around a hull.

*Sterling.* The name surfaced around midnight, in a registry seven years old, and I set down my pen and sat very still for a while, the way you do when a hunt stops being abstract.

Jude Sterling. Indentured to the Corvane Trading Company at age fourteen, term of five years, extended by mutual agreement — and I wrote *mutual* in my notes with some skepticism, because nobody extends an indenture mutually unless the alternative frightens them more. Listed next of kin: Edric Sterling, pearl diver, deceased. Occupation of kin: crew, *Perla*, master I. Marchetti.

There it was. Sitting in the public record for seven years, plain as a coin at the bottom of clear water. Daughter of a drowned man from the traitor's own boat, working quietly inside the company that had testified against him, reading numbers faster than any clerk in the building.

Either it was coincidence, or it was the most patient revenge ever staged, or it was something stranger than both. I have learned to bet on stranger.

* * *

I want to be honest about what I felt when I found the name, because honesty is the one luxury my profession allows me in private. I felt pleased. There is a particular pleasure in the hunt, in watching a shape resolve out of noise, and I am not ashamed of it. But underneath the pleasure there was something else, something I noticed only later, and it was this: I hoped she wasn't guilty.

That is not professional. I noted it, filed it away, and resolved to watch it. A hunter who starts hoping is a hunter who starts missing things.

* * *

I arranged the confrontation the way I arrange most of them: casually, in daylight, on territory she knew better than I did. Nothing frightens a witness into silence faster than being summoned. You learn nothing from a frightened person except what fear sounds like.

She was at her desk just after dawn, before the other clerks arrived, working by lamplight with her collar buttoned to the throat against the harbor damp. I set a stack of registries down beside her lamp — not her registry, just registries, a prop — and asked her opinion on a discrepancy in the grain tariffs, which was real, because I prefer even my misdirection to be load-bearing.

She answered correctly, concisely, and without looking up. Good start.

"The Corvane records are unusually clean," I said. "For a company this size."

"Company policy," she said. "Mistakes get corrected the same day they're found."

"By you, mostly."

That got her eyes up. Grey, level, and giving away exactly nothing — the eyes of a woman who has practiced being looked at without being seen. "It's my work," she said. "I do it."

"You do it remarkably." I pulled out the chair across from her desk, uninvited, and sat. Up close there were details the glass partition had softened: ink stains worked deep into the cuticles, a small scar through one eyebrow old enough to have faded white, and hands that were wrong for a clerk — broad-palmed, callused in a way that comes from rope and cold water rather than pens. A diver's daughter who had never quite stopped being a diver's daughter. "Seven years with the company."

"Three years' indenture. Four total."

"Extended your term."

"The company asked. I agreed." Flat. Practiced.

"It's unusual," I said, "for a reconciler to be so loyal to an employer who keeps her on bond rather than wage."

"It's unusual," she said, "for a crown auditor to care about the employment terms of junior staff." And there it was — not fear, not yet, but a quickening behind the stillness, a current under ice. She knew why I'd come, or she suspected, and she was deciding right now, in front of me, how much to show.

I decided to show her mine first. It is a technique I use perhaps once a year, because it only works once per target: give them the whole board and watch which piece they reach for.

"Sterling," I said. "Edric Sterling. Pearl diver. Lost off the Serpent Shoals eight years ago, aboard the *Perla*." I laid the registry page between us, turned to face her. "Master listed as Ilya Marchetti. Your father's captain."

The lamplight caught her face and I watched it happen — the small involuntary movements, the ones no one can fully train away. Her breath stopped for half a second. Her hand moved toward the page and then deliberately didn't. Her jaw set, not in defiance but in the way a person sets a jaw when they're swallowing something large.

"I was fourteen," she said finally. "I don't know much about his work."

"That's not what I asked."

"You didn't ask anything."

Fair. I allowed her the point; generosity costs nothing early in an interview. "Captain Marchetti," I said, "is attainted for treason. His assets are seized, his officers are under warrant, and anyone found sheltering him, his family, or his interests is liable to the same. Those are the facts of your situation, Miss Sterling, whether or not you acknowledge them."

"And if I acknowledged them?"

"Then I could offer you something better than acknowledgment." I leaned forward, keeping my voice low, though the long room was still empty. "The crown protects those who help it. Testimony given freely, protection formalized in writing, your indenture voided, wages owed, passage anywhere you like afterward. A new name, if you wanted one. A life where no one owns your labor or your history." I let that sit a moment. "I know what indenture is, Miss Sterling. I've read enough registers. I'm offering you the door out of it."

And here is what I learned about Jude Sterling in the silence that followed: she was not tempted. Or rather — she was tempted, visibly, humanly, the way anyone bound to a company seal would be tempted by freedom — but the temptation moved through her and left nothing behind. I watched it pass like weather. What remained was something harder and older, and I recognized it with a hunter's sinking certainty: she was protecting something. Not herself. Herself she would have traded by now, or lied about more fluently. She was protecting something she considered worth more than herself, and whatever it was, it had a heartbeat, because her eyes went — just once, quickly, involuntarily — toward the courtyard door. The laundry. Then back to me.

I should not have followed her glance. A better interrogator would have missed it. But I am honest with myself if no one else, and so I record that I saw it, and that it told me everything: whatever Marchetti had entrusted to this city before he ran, it was here, it was close, and this quiet woman at the third desk from the window was standing between it and the entire apparatus of the crown.

"I don't have testimony to give," she said. "My father drowned when I was a child. I barely remember the captain. He used to bring oranges." A pause, and then — and this was the move that made me sit back — a small, tired, entirely convincing smile. "If you're looking for sympathizers, Mr. Roe, look at the ledgers. Money remembers what people forget. That's the only testimony I can offer, and you don't need my permission to read it."

Money remembers. She said it lightly, a clerk's proverb, and then went back to her columns as though I had asked her about grain tariffs, and dismissed me more thoroughly than any director in this city had managed.

I thanked her and left. In the doorway I paused and said, "The offer stands. Doors have a way of closing, Miss Sterling. Mine stays open a while longer." She didn't answer. The pen scratched. The lamplight held steady over her bowed head, and I walked out through the courtyard into the grey morning feeling, for the first time in eleven years of this work, like the man being hunted.

* * *

Back in my chamber I made my notes and then sat looking at the last line of them for a long time. I had written: *Subject protecting unknown party. Glance toward laundry courtyard. Investigate.*

Investigate. Two words, routine, procedural. Any competent auditor would write them.

But I found I was hesitating, and I examined why, because hesitation in my trade is either corruption or conscience and it pays to know which one you've caught. The truth, I think, is this: I have spent my career pulling people out of ledgers and into courtrooms, and most of them deserved it, but I have never once been offered a door out of anything by anyone. I know what it costs to be a name in someone else's registry. I had watched her flinch at the word *indenture* and I had meant my offer — that was the trouble. Some part of me, the part that grew up watching my mother count other people's money for bread, had meant it completely.

And some other part of me, colder and better trained, noted that the best traps are baited with exactly what the quarry wants most.

I dipped my pen and added a line beneath *investigate*: *Laundry courtyard. Discreetly. Tonight.*

Somewhere below my window, the wash-line pulleys creaked in the wind off the harbor, and a woman I could not see was folding sheets that might have been hiding anything at all.
