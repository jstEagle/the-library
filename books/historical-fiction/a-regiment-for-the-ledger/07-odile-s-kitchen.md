## 7. Odile's Kitchen

The fever came on a Tuesday, which I have since decided is the universe's idea of a joke. Tuesdays at the Corvane counting-house are reconciliation days—the day the clerks argue with each other's arithmetic until someone weeps—and so I spent nine hours proving that two hundred barrels of salt cod had never existed while a child I was not supposed to know about burned quietly in a laundress's attic.

I knew she was sick before Odile told me. That's the thing about learning to read silences: you start reading them everywhere. Mrs. Halloway, wife of the second clerk, had been watching me for six days with the particular patience of a woman who has nothing to do but notice things. She'd seen me carry a basket of laundry up the back stairs twice when once would do. She'd heard—God help us all—a baby's cry on the night of the storm, muffled by wool and wind but not quite enough. And Tuesday morning she stood in the courtyard holding her own infant son and said, too pleasantly, "Your laundress has been buying milk for two."

"Odile takes it in her tea," I said. "She's Vellamare born. We put milk in everything. It's a failing of the entire coast."

Mrs. Halloway smiled the way auditors smile, and I walked into the counting-house with my heart hammering out a rhythm no ledger could reconcile.

* * *

I went up to Odile's attic at dusk, through the rain, past the yard where the wet sheets hung like surrendering ghosts. The baby—Marchetti's daughter, my father's sworn friend's daughter, a child with no name in any book—was flushed red and crying in that thin, exhausted way that means the crying hurts more than the reason for it.

"She took the milk wrong," Odile said. She was sixty if she was a day, built like a mooring post, with hands that had washed the company's linens for thirty years and could wring truth out of anyone. "Or she caught something off the damp. Or she's punishing us, which would be fair." She rocked the bundle against her shoulder. "There's an apothecary on Cooper Lane who asks no questions. Costs double."

"I'll pay it."

"With what? Your indenture wages?" Odile didn't look at me. She looked at the baby, which was somehow worse. "Jude. Sit down."

I sat on the crate by the window. Rain ran down the glass like it was trying to escape too.

"You've been carrying this child like a parcel you mean to set down eventually," Odile said. "A week now. Maybe two. You bring me the baby, the pearl, the note, and then you go back downstairs and become invisible again, and I'm left holding the treason." Her voice wasn't cruel. It was tired, which is crueller. "Well. The parcel's getting heavier, and Mrs. Halloway is getting curiouser, and the auditor's clerks have been asking the street children who sleeps where. So you'll decide tonight. Either we find this child a proper way out—wet nurse, smuggler, some honest criminal with a boat—or you commit. All the way. No more half-hiding."

"And if I commit?"

"Then you stop being invisible," Odile said. "That's the price. You've spent three years learning how not to be seen. This child will cost you every lesson you ever learned." She finally turned those grey eyes on me. "So. What did your father promise, and what does it weigh?"

I hadn't told her. Of course I hadn't. But Odile washed the company's sheets, and sheets talk, and apparently so do I, in my sleep, in moments of weakness, or perhaps she'd simply read the silence I carried around the same way I read ledgers.

"He promised Marchetti," I said, "that if ever his child could shelter Marchetti's, she would."

"On his deathbed?"

"On a pearl boat, with him drowning slowly from the inside. Marchetti held his hand. I was eleven. I thought promises made over water were just stories sailors told." I looked at the baby. "It turns out they're invoices. They come due."

Odile snorted—the closest thing to approval in her repertoire. "And the crown says Marchetti's a traitor."

"The crown also says the Corvane Trading Company has never lost a shipment it didn't choose to lose," I said. "I've been reading their books for three years. I know what the crown believes is worth."

* * *

Here is what I did that night, and I want it recorded properly, because for three years everything I did was recorded improperly, by other people, in books I kept but did not write.

The company keeps a registry of deaths among its indentured households—not from mercy, but from accounting. A dead clerk's widow stops drawing rations; a dead child releases a cot, a blanket allocation, a line in the provisioning ledger. Everything ends somewhere in ink, or it doesn't end at all.

There had been a baby in the registry eight months ago. Daughter of Tamsin Reeve, a laundress before Odile, gone back inland to her sister's after the winter fevers took the child. I remembered the entry because I had copied it: *infant, female, died of lung-fever, interred parish ground, 14th of the Grey Month.* The parish sexton keeps no cross-reference with company records; the parish keeps no records at all beyond a stone list that rain has been erasing for a decade.

I waited until the night clerk, Pellam, went to his brandy—which he did at the striking of the ninth bell, regular as tide—and I opened the registry under the good lamp, the one whose flicker excuses any smudge, and I wrote a death.

I wrote it in Pellam's hand. I have been imitating Pellam's hand for three years the way other people learn songs. His capital R leans like a drunk against a wall; his fives look like sevens; he crosses his t's too high, a man perpetually reaching for something above his station. *Infant, female, ward of the household of O. Ferrant, laundress, died of lung-fever.* Today's date. *Interred parish ground.*

A child who dies on paper stops existing to the Corvane Trading Company. No more milk drawn against the household account—I'd enter the reduction myself tomorrow, tidy as arithmetic. No name in any column. No face in any record. If Cassian Roe's bounty clerks came matching faces to registries, they would find only a neat line of grief, dated, witnessed, closed.

My hand shook on the last stroke. Not with fear exactly—or not only fear. It was the shaking of a lock turning, of a door opening onto a room I'd been standing outside for twenty years. I had altered figures before. Everyone alters figures; the whole company breathes through small falsifications, and I'd merely declined to breathe. But this was different. This was me, choosing the lie, writing it in my own steadiness, putting a death into the world to keep a life in it.

I thought of my mother's pearls going into ships' holds I'd found traces of in the margins of seized manifests. I thought of my father's hand going cold in a traitor's warm one. I thought: *they forged first. They've been forging since before I could hold a pen. I am only answering correspondence.*

When Pellam came back smelling of brandy and self-pity, I was copying grain tariffs, my lamp trimmed low, my face arranged into its usual polite blankness. He grunted at me, the entirety of our relationship, and I grunted back.

* * *

I paid the apothecary out of the tin I kept nailed under my cot—three years of small thefts against my own wages, skimming the difference between what the ledger said I earned and what I actually touched. Fitting, I suppose, that embezzlement should fund treason. My father would have laughed himself sick. My father laughed at funerals; it's why Marchetti loved him.

The fever broke on Thursday night. Odile sent word via a basket of returned linens—one sheet folded in a particular way meaning *she slept*, another fold meaning *she ate*. We had developed a whole grammar of linen by then, which I suspect Odile invented partly for efficiency and partly because watching me decode napkins amused her.

Friday morning, Mrs. Halloway stopped me in the courtyard again.

"Pity about your laundress's little boarder," she said. "Such a shame. Lung-fever, they say."

"Yes," I said. "Terrible season for it."

"Mm. Though the milk deliveries haven't stopped." She said it lightly, tossing it, a woman skipping a stone across still water to see how deep it went. "Odd, that."

"Odile buys ahead," I said. "Grief makes people strange with money. Ask any widower in this city."

Something moved behind her eyes—not belief, but the decision to postpone disbelief, which in Vellamare passes for diplomacy. She moved along. And I stood in the courtyard understanding, with total clarity, that postponement was all I'd bought. Sooner or later Roe's clerks would ask her, or she'd grow bored of waiting, and the stone would sink.

So Friday afternoon I did something I had never done. I walked—not drifted, not filtered, *walked*, at a clerk's pace with a clerk's purpose—to the sealed hold on the sub-level where the original manifests were kept under triple wax and the director general's personal seal. I didn't touch the door. I stood in front of it for the length of four bells, memorizing the locks, the guard rotation, the lamplighter's schedule, the drainage grate in the floor that some previous generation of smugglers had surely used and some future one might again.

Because a burial entry buys time. It does not buy truth. The child was erased from the record, but Marchetti was still a traitor in it, and my mother's pearls were still financing his ruin in margins no auditor had bothered to read closely—no auditor except one, with careful hands and kind eyes and a noose woven gently into every question.

Roe wanted the truth. Well. So did I, now. The difference between us was that I intended to reach it first.

That night, walking home through the rain with my collar up and my heart, for the first time in three years, loud enough for anyone to hear, I passed the harbor watch posting new bills on the customs house wall. Fresh ones. Fine paper, fine printing.

A reward, they said, for information regarding persons harboring enemies of the crown.

Below it, smaller: *Inquiries directed to C. Roe, Auditor.*

He was close. I could feel it the way divers feel a change in pressure—something in the water shifting, deep and patient, coming up toward the light.

Good, I thought. Let him come up. I'd be waiting in the dark, and I'd already learned to breathe down there.
