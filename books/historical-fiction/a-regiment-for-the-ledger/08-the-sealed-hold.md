## 8. The Sealed Hold

The trouble with secrets, my father used to say, is that they're like ballast. You can carry a great deal of them if you distribute the weight properly, but stack them all in one corner and they'll capsize you in fair weather.

I had been stacking mine in one corner for weeks now: an infant sleeping behind Odile's drying linens, a forged burial entry cooling in the registry like a knife left in a wound, and the growing certainty that Captain Ilya Marchetti—traitor to the crown, enemy of Vellamare, ghost of every wanted poster on the customs house wall—had been made into a traitor by men who signed their names with gold nibs and never once got ink under their skin.

What I needed was proof. And proof, in the Corvane Trading Company, lived in one place.

I found it the way I found most things: by listening to men talk about themselves.

"Sealed hold," Master Quillon was saying to the second auditor that evening, in the tone of a man performing reluctance. "Company charter requires originals retained seven years. Crown law, crown seal. We keep the key, the magistrate keeps the wax." He said it twice more over the following days, each time with less reluctance, which told me he wanted someone to ask about it. Men always advertise the rooms they'd rather you didn't enter; it's the empty rooms they guard in silence.

The sealed hold sat beneath the counting-house proper—a stone throat of a room at the bottom of the stairs behind the director general's private office, where the original manifests, bills of lading, and shipping papers from the past seven years lay in iron-banded chests. The working ledgers upstairs were copies, and copies could be improved upon. I knew this because I had improved upon several myself. But somewhere below my feet lay the true record: every cargo the *Meridian's Wake* carried out, every shipment entered as *seized* that had never been seized at all, and—God forgive her—every payment drawn against my mother's pearls.

She hadn't known. I had to believe she hadn't known. A widow selling her dead husband's pearl money into company hands because a charming factor told her it would be *held in trust* for her daughter's future—and then watching that same daughter indentured to the very company that had eaten the trust whole. Either she had been deceived or she had been desperate, and I had loved her too long to let her be anything but deceived.

The hold opened for inventory once a quarter. The last inventory had been eleven days ago. The next would not come before month's end.

Which meant I had perhaps two weeks to find a way through a locked door guarded by a man who slept above it, watched by clerks who owed me nothing and auditors who suspected me of everything.

I began, as any sensible criminal does, with the laundry.

* * *

Cassian Roe had been in Vellamare nineteen days, and he had learned three things about the Corvane Trading Company: its books were beautiful, its directors were courteous, and its courtesy had the specific texture of a hand placed gently over your mouth.

He had also learned about the laundress.

It was nothing he could have written in a report. A woman named Odile Ferrant, fifty-one years of age, employed fourteen years, wages unremarkable, character vouched for by everyone—which itself was remarkable, because servants who were vouched for by *everyone* had usually learned to be useful to everyone. She bought more bread than a woman living alone required. She had taken in her late sister's boy, everyone said, though the boy was seldom seen. And three times in recent weeks, when the night porter made his rounds, he had noted—without noting, the way porters note things—a light in the laundry yard that went out a heartbeat after his boots hit the gravel.

Roe did not believe in coincidence. He believed in patterns, and patterns were how the crown paid him. Somewhere in this city of salt and ledger-paper, Captain Ilya Marchetti had left something behind—or someone—and the pattern said it was small, and hidden, and tended by women who bought too much bread.

He had also found the name. It had cost him four days in the indenture registries, cross-referencing crew lists of Marchetti's last commanded vessel, and there it was in faded clerk's copperplate: *Sterling, Jude — daughter of Tomas Sterling, diver, d. at sea — bound to Corvane Trading Company, term seven years.* A drowned diver from Marchetti's crew. A daughter who read ledgers faster than men with ten years' seniority. A daughter who had looked at him across the reconciliation table with eyes that measured him even while pretending to be measured.

Tonight he meant to knock on doors.

* * *

Odile put the baby down at dusk, fed her, wrapped her in the good blanket, and then stood over the cradle with her arms crossed like a woman arguing with God.

"You've decided," she said. Not a question.

"I need one night in the hold."

"And if you don't come back up?"

"Then you've never seen me in your life and the child goes to your cousin in Saltrow, as arranged."

Odile snorted. "My cousin drinks. Give her to the widow Brann instead." She turned away, then turned back, and what was in her face made me stop pretending to fold linen. "Three years I've watched you make yourself small, girl. Invisible as smoke. Tonight you go down those stairs and become something else, and whatever comes of it—" she jabbed a finger at me, "—don't you dare apologize for it after."

It was, I think, the first blessing anyone had given me that didn't come attached to conditions.

The plan was simple, which is to say it depended entirely on luck and a borrowed key. The night porter took his brandy at the Anchor's Rest between the hours of ten and eleven—it was in the ledger of his habits, and I keep excellent books. His ring held six keys, only one of which he ever thought about. The stair door behind the director general's office was locked, but the office itself was not, because Director General Ambrose Corvane trusted locks the way he trusted people: only when they were his own.

I waited until the house settled. I waited until the clock in the hall gave its tenth stroke and the porter's chair stood empty. Then I took the copied key Odile's nephew had pressed from wax—God rest whichever apprentice had lost his post over that missing impression—and I went down into the dark.

* * *

Roe started with the laundry, because you start with the weakest thread and pull until the garment tells you everything.

Odile Ferrant met him in her doorway with a candle and the expression of a woman interrupted at prayer. Behind her, the room smelled of lye soap and steam and—underneath it, unmistakable to a man raised above his father's chandlery—milk.

"Madam," Roe said pleasantly, "a routine inquiry regarding household provisions. There have been irregularities in the company stores."

"There are irregularities in everything," Odile said. "Ask your questions."

And he might have—he had questions enough, prepared like surgical instruments—but it was then that the boy appeared. The sister's son, fourteen perhaps, all elbows and panic, and behind the boy, half-visible past the drying lines, a cradle. Empty now. Recently empty. The blanket inside still holding its shape.

Roe thanked her kindly for her time and walked back through the yard with his heart beating in a way he did not entirely care for. He had hunted smugglers, forgers, and one memorable bigamist, and he knew the difference between guilt and fear. That room had been full of fear, and fear was the smell of something worth protecting.

Which meant either the laundress was hiding the child of a traitor—

Or someone else already had it, and had moved it tonight, and the person most likely to move a child on the precise evening an auditor came asking was the person who knew he was coming.

Jude Sterling.

He turned toward the counting-house, walking faster.

* * *

The hold smelled of tar, cold iron, and paper left alone too long—the smell, I thought absurdly, of my entire life kept in a basement.

The key turned. The door swung. And there they were: seven chests in a row, labeled by year in Ambrose Corvane's own immaculate hand, each one crowned with the crown's dull red wax.

I broke the seal on the chest marked for the year my mother sold her pearls, and I will confess that my hands shook—not from fear. From the nearness of it. Three years of being invisible, and here at last was the truth, lying in the dark waiting for someone to read it.

The manifests told it plainly, the way original documents do when no one has yet taught them to lie. The *Meridian's Wake*, Captain I. Marchetti, cleared harbor with silk and specie. Two months later, a seizure entry in the copy ledgers upstairs—cargo lost to pirates, insurance claimed, Marchetti cited for negligence. But the original bill of lading showed the cargo had never left the hold. It had been off-loaded at a company warehouse upcoast, three days before the *Wake* ever sailed, under a permit signed by A. Corvane himself. The piracy was theater. The seizure was theft. And the insurance money, traced line by line through disbursement entries, flowed outward in payments that included—there it was, in black and white that smelled of dust and lamp-oil—*Sterling, per arrangement re: pearl holdings.*

My mother's pearls. Bought with stolen goods. Financing the ruin of the man who had promised my dying father he'd watch over me.

I copied until my fingers cramped, copying fast and small the way I do everything, and I was sealing the last page inside my bodice when I heard the stair door open above me.

Not the porter. The porter hummed. Whoever this was descended in silence, and silence in that house belonged to exactly one man.

I blew out the lamp and stood in the dark among the chests, doing sums. One exit. One intruder. No weapon but a letter opener in my sleeve and a head full of other people's crimes.

The footsteps stopped at the top of the stairs.

"Miss Sterling." Cassian Roe's voice, quiet as turning a page. "I know you're down there. The lamp oil is still warm, and frankly, the wax seals were easier to find than you seem to have believed." A pause. "Bring the light. We should discuss what you've discovered—and what I'm willing to trade for it."

I stood in the dark with the evidence of everything against my ribs, and I understood that the corner where I had stacked my silences had finally, finally capsized.

I struck the match.
