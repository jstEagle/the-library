## 9. Ledger of Debts

There is a particular sound a sealing-wax stamp makes when it cracks under pressure — a small dry snap, like a knuckle popping — and I heard it three times before I understood that the noise was coming from my own hands.

The sealed hold smelled of tar and old rope and the particular staleness of paper that has been kept from the sun on purpose, because sunlight is honest and this room was not. I had picked the lock with a fishhook from my father's tackle box, the last thing of his I owned that still did useful work, and I will not pretend my hands were steady about it. They were not. But they were quick, which is what mattered.

The manifests lay in a chest marked *Corvane Trading Company — Vellamare Station — Years 12 through 14*, and they were beautiful in the way that only original documents are beautiful: uncorrected, unsigned-over, bearing the true weights and true ports and true names before anyone had decided those truths were inconvenient. Here was the *Meridian Rose* listed as carrying forty chests of pearl-shell out of Vellamare. In the copy ledger upstairs, the same ship carried salt fish to a port she never visited. Here were three entries initialed by Director General Ambrose Corvane himself, routing cargo through Marchetti's ship without Marchetti's knowledge — cargo that, when the crown's customs men found it, would hang a captain very neatly.

And there, threaded through it like pearls on a string, my mother's money. Small sums, entered quarterly for six years. *M. Sterling — subscription.* My mother had thought she was investing in her husband's future. She had been buying rope for someone else's gallows.

I copied until my wrist ached. I copied the way I had learned to copy at seven years old, hunched over my father's knee, matching his hand stroke for stroke so he could send letters home from deep water. I copied until the candle burned down to a coin of wax, and then I made a mistake.

I heard the footsteps too late.

* * *

Cassian Roe did not burst in. Of course he didn't. Bursting is for men who need drama to feel powerful; Roe needed only a doorway and the patience of a man who has already won.

"Third drawer of your desk," he said from behind me, "holds a stub of red sealing wax that doesn't match any stick issued by this office. The laundress Odile bought candles in quantities inconsistent with a household of two. And you, Miss Sterling, have ink on your left cuff that predates tonight, which means this isn't the first night." He stepped into the candlelight. "You're better at invisibility than most spies I've caught. You're simply worse at arithmetic than you think."

I set down the pen. There was no point hiding the pages; they were in my bodice, against my ribs, and we both knew it, and pointing at my own chest seemed undignified.

"How long," I said.

"Since the registry. Since your father's name surfaced next to Marchetti's like a body surfacing in a harbor." He came closer, and I noticed he had brought no lantern, no guards, nothing that announced itself. "You could have run when I offered you protection. Instead you've spent four nights in a locked room committing treason against your own employers. I confess I find that more interesting than the ledgers."

"Then let's trade," I said. "You're interested. I'm armed."

His eyebrows rose a fraction. "With what?"

"With the truth," I said, and even I winced at how it sounded, so I pressed on before either of us could laugh. "Not silence. I know how much silence is worth; I've been paid in it for three years. Silence is a currency that degrades. Truth holds its value." I pulled one page free and laid it flat between us: the *Meridian Rose*, forty chests, Ambrose Corvane's initials. "Your crown wants Marchetti. Your crown is going to get a director instead."

Roe looked at the page for a long moment. He had the kind of face built for long moments, all planes and patience, and I understood suddenly why the company had hired him: he was their mirror image, a man who also believed that people were entries to be reconciled.

"You'd testify," he said. "Against Corvane."

"I'd do more than testify. I'd give you the originals. Every altered manifest, every forged seizure order, every entry where my mother's pearl money became smuggled silk. And in exchange—" I made myself say it plainly, because bargains mumbled are bargains broken— "the child leaves on the first neutral-flag ship out of this harbor, with papers that name no one. And when Marchetti stands trial, you put me on the stand and I swear to everything in these pages. Clemency testimony. On the record. In my own name."

"In your own name," he repeated, softly, as though tasting it. "Jude Sterling. Diver's daughter. Indentured clerk. You understand that once you sign that testimony, you stop being invisible forever. Corvane's directors have longer memories than the crown does."

"I've been invisible for twenty-three years," I said. "It hasn't kept anyone I love alive."

Something moved behind his eyes — not warmth exactly, but recognition, the look of an auditor finding the entry that balances the column. He took out a small notebook. "Terms," he said. "In writing."

And that is precisely when the door came off its hinges.

* * *

They were the director general's men, not the auditors' — I knew them by the grey sashes, by the smell of the counting-house pomade Ambrose Corvane favored, by the fact that they did not announce themselves or show papers but simply arrived the way weather arrives. Four of them. The leader had a face I had passed a thousand times in corridors without ever once seeing, which is the compliment we invisibles pay our masters' instruments.

Roe said something official-sounding about crown authority, and the man with the pomade said something back about the crown's authority being a matter currently under review, and then it stopped being words.

I got two pages deeper into my bodice and one elbow into the first man's throat before they took me down. I am not brave; I want that recorded somewhere. I am merely a person who has been struck before and knows that flinching costs more than standing. Someone wrenched my arm behind me until the copied pages spilled across the floor like startled birds, and the man with the pomade crouched, gathered them, and read one with the leisurely pleasure of a man confirming what he'd been paid to confirm.

"Well," he said. "Little Miss Ledger."

That was when the second set of footsteps came up the hold stairs — fast, uneven, unmistakable — and Odile appeared in the doorway with the baby wrapped inside her shawl and a boat-hook in her hands, because of course she had followed me, because of course she had, because that woman had been keeping other people's secrets since before I was born and had apparently decided to die of it rather than reform.

"Odile, *no*—" I said, and the man nearest her swung, and she turned — not away, *into* it, curling her whole body around the bundle in her arms the way my father once told me a diver curls around a stone when the current means to take it — and the boat-hook clattered, and Odile went down against the chest of manifests with the baby screaming inside the shawl and blood already darkening her collar where her ear met her jaw.

The room changed. I felt it change, the way you feel a ship change its mind beneath you.

Because here is what three years of indenture taught me and what I had somehow forgotten: survival is a ledger too, and I had been keeping mine in the wrong column. I had balanced invisibility against safety, obedience against bread, silence against shelter, and every line had cleared, every year, perfectly — and the sum total of all that perfect bookkeeping was a woman bleeding on a floor for a child who wasn't hers, protecting a promise I had been too careful to keep loudly.

I stopped calculating.

I don't remember deciding anything. I remember the taste of blood — mine, from a split lip — and I remember Roe shouting something about crown witnesses and the pomade man laughing, and I remember picking up the boat-hook because it was nearest, and swinging it not skillfully but *completely*, the way you swing when you have finally stopped trying to be small. It caught the pomade man across the shoulder and he dropped the manifests, and I dropped to my knees on top of them, arms spread, cheek to the cold boards, a woman lying prostrate upon the truth of the matter.

"Arrest us all," I said, and my voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect. "But count the pages first. Count them in front of your auditor, in front of the harbormaster, in front of God if he's taking attendance. Forty chests, Captain. Signed by your master's own initials. You're not holding a thief. You're holding the evidence."

Silence. The good kind — the rare kind — the kind where everyone in the room is doing arithmetic at the same speed.

It was Roe who broke it, rising slowly, drawing a document from his coat with the unhurried motion of a man playing his strongest card last. "By authority of the Crown Commission on Seized Shipping," he said, "this station and all persons within it are remanded to audit custody, effective now. Anyone touching these papers answers to me. Including—" and here he glanced down at me with something that might, in a better-lit world, have been respect— "*especially* the witness."

The pomade man's jaw worked. Somewhere above us, the company bell began to ring the hour, indifferent as ever.

Odile groaned. That sound decided more than any manifesto. I crawled to her, pages crumpling under my knees and I no longer caring — let them crumple, let the whole cursed archive crumple — and pressed my hand below her ear where the blood ran warm, and the baby's fist emerged from the shawl and closed around my finger with the ferocious grip of the very young.

"Hold on," I told Odile, uselessly, the way people do.

She opened one eye. Even concussed, the woman managed an expression of profound editorial judgment. "You," she said thickly, "owe me a kitchen."

"Madame," I said, "I owe you a fleet."

Roe was directing his clerks-in-arms to secure the hold, and the director general's men were discovering, with the dawning resentment of bullies everywhere, that the rules had changed while their backs were turned. Above us the bell finished striking, and beneath the ringing came another sound — boots on the quay, many of them, moving with purpose toward the Corvane station doors.

Roe cocked his head, listening, and for the first time since I'd known him, the auditor looked uncertain of his figures.

"That'll be the harbormaster's watch," I said. "Or it'll be Ambrose Corvane burning his evidence. We have perhaps ten minutes to find out which."

He was already moving for the stairs. "Bring the woman," he called back. "And the child. And, Miss Sterling—" a glance over his shoulder, ink-stained, blood-stained, entirely visible— "bring yourself. You're the only one of us who can read those pages."

So I gathered the manifests to my chest, lifted what I could of Odile's weight onto my shoulder, and carried my entire life's accounting up into the noise and the torchlight — no longer hidden, no longer silent, owed and owing, and free for the first time to find out what all of it was worth.
