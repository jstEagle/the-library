## 10. Signed in Her Own Hand

Spring came late to Vellamare, the way it always did—sideways, off the water, carrying salt and the smell of tar and something almost like forgiveness. I stood at the window of Odile's kitchen on a morning in April and watched a navy cutter come up the channel with her colors wrong for a merchantman and right for everything else: the crown's white over blue, flying at last with honor instead of menace.

The scandal had broken in February, the way scandals do—not with thunder but with paperwork. A junior magistrate with more ambition than sense had gotten hold of copies of the manifests I'd made, and once one copy exists, others breed like mice in a granary. The Corvane directors had been arrested in their own counting-house, which I confess gave me a satisfaction I'm not proud of but won't pretend I don't still taste. Director General Halloway wept when they took him. I'd have expected rage. The weeping was worse, somehow. It made him human, and I found I preferred my monsters monstrous.

The company's seal was struck from its door in March. Struck—literally, with chisels, by two men on ladders while a crowd gathered to watch, the way crowds gather for executions and ship launches, having never learned to tell the difference. Odile took me down to see it. She stood in that crowd with her arms crossed and her chin up, her arm still bound from where a director's man had swung at the baby and caught her instead, and she watched the letters fall one by one like teeth.

"Good riddance," she said.

"Good riddance," I agreed, and meant it, and discovered that meaning things out loud was a habit I was going to have to practice.

* * *

The indenture had been voided by writ in January—one of the first acts of the new admiralty court, before it even had furniture. The clerk who read the writ to me stumbled over his own formality, and I stood there in the magistrate's anteroom realizing that for three years I had belonged, on paper, to an entity that no longer existed, and that the paper had been the only thing holding me in place all along.

"What will you do now?" the clerk asked, kindly enough, the way you ask a question of someone whose answer doesn't matter to you.

I said I didn't know yet. That was true. What I did not say was that I knew exactly where I would be every morning anyway—at Odile's stove, or at the window, or bent over a cradle that had migrated from the laundry loft to the corner by the hearth because the baby had decided, somewhere along the way, that she lived here now, and babies are tyrants about their living arrangements.

Her name, as far as anyone official was concerned, was nothing. She was the unnamed infant recovered from the Corvane matter, ward of the crown pending disposition. As far as Odile was concerned she was "little miss," delivered with the same gruff tenderness Odile gave to scalded linen and burned onions. And as far as I was concerned—

As far as I was concerned she was the reason I got up, which was more than the ledgers had ever been, though I'll admit the ledgers had been a decent second.

She had Marchetti's eyes. I hadn't known that until I'd seen his face again—in a broadsheet, during the worst weeks, when the papers ran his portrait beside the word TREASON in letters tall enough to read across a street. Then the manifests came out, and the seizures were shown to be staged, and the smuggling routes traced back through three shell companies to the directors' own pockets, and the same papers that had called him traitor ran a retraction in smaller type, because retractions are always smaller type; shame is shy that way. His portrait appeared again, this time beside EXONERATED, and I cut it out with scissors meant for thread and kept it folded in my pocket like a schoolgirl with a crush, until I caught myself doing it and stopped, mostly.

The pearl on its cord I had kept around my own neck since the night he left her. It seemed right that she should have it back, eventually. It also seemed right that I should keep it until then. Both things were true. My father taught me that two true things can share a boat without capsizing it, though he learned it the hard way, at the bottom of the sea.

* * *

The cutter anchored at midday. I knew it was his before the launch put off, because I had spent the morning pretending not to watch the harbor and failing completely, and because Odile finally took the spyglass out of my hands and said, "Either go down there or stop breathing on my window."

The baby was on my hip. She had opinions about the stairs and expressed them loudly, and so we went down together, the two of us clattering through Odile's kitchen and into the street, past the fish stalls and the rope-walk, down to the quay where the launch was grounding in the shallows and a man was stepping out into water that came only to his knees.

He was thinner than the broadsheet. Broadsheets flatter everyone; they'd flattered me too, once, in the account of the trial testimony—they'd called me "a clerk of unusual diligence," which is the kindest lie ever printed about me. He was thinner, and greyer at the temples than his years accounted for, and he walked up the shingle like a man who had forgotten how land moves underfoot, too fast, then too slow, then stopping altogether ten feet away because he had seen the child on my hip and whatever speech he'd prepared drowned on the spot.

"You kept her," he said.

"I promised someone I would." My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "He was a diver. Stubborn man. Dying took him three days and he spent all three making bargains."

"Jude Sterling." He said my name like he was testing whether it would hold weight. "Your father saved my life twice. Once from drowning, once from marrying badly. I only ever managed to repay him in secrets."

"Well," I said, "you've repaid him in an infant now, and I've paid myself in sleepless nights, so I'd say the accounts are square." Wryness is armor; I've told you that. But underneath the wryness my arms were shaking, and he saw it, because he stepped forward and put one weathered hand over both of mine where they gripped the baby, and none of us said anything for a long moment while the tide sucked at the shingle and gulls argued overhead about something neither of us would ever understand.

Her name was Lucia. He told me that at last, quietly, the way you tell someone a thing you've been carrying alone too long. Lucia Marchetti, born in a storm off the Sable Isles, mother dead of fever six months after. He'd run because staying meant watching the crown take her to be raised by people who'd already decided what her father was. He'd sent her to me because—here he faltered, this captain, this man who had sailed through blockades—and finally said, "Because your father told me if ever I needed shelter, your house would be it. I thought he meant a roof. He meant you."

* * *

There was a register. There is always a register; Vellamare runs on them the way other cities run on bread. The harbormaster's office kept a book of transfers of wardship, and since the crown's disposition of the Corvane matter required formal documentation of the child's custody—which is to say, since some clerk somewhere needed a signature—the transfer had to be entered and signed.

We walked there together in the late afternoon, the three of us. Lucia rode against my shoulder, drowsing, her fist closed around the cord of the pearl, which I had put back on her that morning and immediately regretted and immediately un-regretted, because keeping things is a habit too, and I am trying to learn which habits to keep.

The harbormaster's clerk—a young woman, sharp-eyed, ink-stained to the elbow, the kind of clerk I would have been proud to be—turned the great book toward us and dipped the pen.

"Custodian relinquishing wardship signs here," she said, tapping a line. "Receiving party below."

Marchetti signed. His hand shook slightly; I pretended not to see, out of courtesy, the same courtesy he'd extended me on the quay.

Then the clerk turned the page to the custodian's line and looked at me expectantly, pen extended.

And here is the thing I want to get right, because I have written it in my head a hundred times since and I refuse to let the memory be grander or smaller than it was. There was no trumpet. No crowd. No sudden swelling music. There was a smoky office, a bored harbormaster in the next room complaining about pilings, a line ruled on a page awaiting ink, and me—Jude Sterling, formerly of the Corvane counting-house, formerly invisible, formerly owned—standing before a space exactly wide enough for a name.

Three years I had signed *J. Sterling*, or nothing, or let others sign for me. My father's whole strategy for my survival had been smallness. Be quick, be quiet, be useful, be unseen. And it worked. God forgive me, it worked—it kept me alive through the audits and the bounty clerks and Cassian Roe's gentle, terrible kindness, and I do not regret it. But a strategy is not a self. Smallness had been the shape of the cage and I had mistaken it for the shape of me.

I took the pen.

I wrote *Jude Sterling*, full, unhurried, every letter given its due weight, the way you write when you are writing for a record that might outlast you. The J with its honest hook. The name my father gave me, which was also the name the sea tried to take and failed. Below it, because the clerk offered and because I have decided I am done letting silences speak for me, I added: *custodian, ad interim.*

"That's not standard," the clerk said.

"No," I agreed. "Neither am I."

She looked at me a moment longer—clerk to clerk, one keeper of records recognizing another—and then, instead of striking it out, she initialed the margin and blotted the page. Some acts of rebellion are just good filing.

Marchetti watched all this from the door with Lucia awake now in his arms, blinking at the lamplight, and when I turned around he was looking at me the way men look at a coastline they'd given up for lost and found charted after all.

"They'll want you aboard, you know," he said. "The admiralty. After your testimony. They don't let witnesses like you drift."

"I know."

"And I'd—" He stopped. Started again, plainer: "I'd not ask you to come. You've paid enough for my debts. But there's a berth, if ever you want it. And there's a daughter here who I suspect has decided you're permanent, and I've learned not to argue with women in this family."

"This family," I repeated.

"Yours and hers," he said. "I'm only the part that comes and goes."

Lucia reached for me then, the way infants reach—imperious, certain of being caught—and I took her, pearl cord and all, and stood in the doorway of that smoky office with my name drying in the great book behind me and the harbor going gold outside, and I understood at last what my father had actually left me. Not money. Not even safety. He'd left me the knowledge that a person can be small and survive, and the choice—mine alone, earned in blood and ink—to be otherwise.

Odile was waiting at the top of the street when we came out, arms crossed, pretending she hadn't been watching the harbormaster's door for an hour. She looked at the baby, at Marchetti, at me.

"Well?" she said.

"We're getting supper," I said. "All of us. And then tomorrow I'm going to find work that pays, because I am done being useful for free."

Odile snorted—the highest compliment in her vocabulary—and fell into step beside me, and the four of us went up into the city together, past the fish stalls closing for the day, past the chiseled scar on the wall where CORVANE used to be, into the evening, visible, accounted for, and owed nothing to anyone.

Almost nothing. The sea keeps its own ledger, my father used to say. And somewhere out past the breakwater, I suspected, an entry had just been opened with my name on it—new hand, new terms—and I found, to my considerable surprise, that I was eager to read what came next.
