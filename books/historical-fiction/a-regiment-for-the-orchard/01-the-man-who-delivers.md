## 1. The Man Who Delivers

The trick to being invisible, Edmund Hartwell had learned, was not to hide. It was to be useful in the most boring possible way. Nobody looked twice at a driver. A driver was furniture with a cap, part of the carriage, part of the street. A driver was the man who existed so that other men could arrive.

He had been practicing invisibility for six years, and he had gotten very good at it.

On the morning of March 14th, 1911, he stood at the curb outside the Voss house on Pembroke Row, checking the harness buckles for the third time though they needed no checking, and watched the light come up grey over Bristol like a sheet being drawn slowly off a body. The horse — a bay mare named Consequence, because Adeline Voss enjoyed naming things ironically — shifted her weight and blew warm air into the cold.

"You're talking to her again," said Adeline from the doorway. "People will think you're mad."

"People already think I'm mad," Edmund said. "I talk to horses and carry letters for ladies who shout at ministers. Mad is the charitable interpretation."

Adeline Voss came down the steps the way she did everything, at a pace that suggested the world would simply have to keep up or get out of the way. Forty-three years old, small and straight-backed, dressed in a coat the colour of wet slate, with a hat that announced she had opinions before she opened her mouth. She carried a leather satchel stuffed with correspondence, and she handed it to him without ceremony, the way you'd hand a man a shovel.

"Three deliveries, two pickups, and this—" she tapped an envelope at the top of the pile "—goes to Mrs. Aldous on Queen's Road before noon. She'll pretend not to be home. Wait until she pretends."

"Understood."

"And we're stopping at the printer's on Wine Street first. If Mr. Gedge tries to charge us again for the pamphlets he spoiled, remind him that spoiling them was his own fault and his own politics."

"Shall I remind him loudly?"

"Moderately loudly." She settled into the seat and arranged her skirts with two brisk motions. "There's a meeting at the Guildhall at four. We'll want to be early. Last time they gave our seats to the aldermen's wives."

Edmund took his place at the reins and clicked his tongue, and Consequence pulled them out into the morning traffic, into the river of carts and trams and men in bowlers, all of them flowing toward work like water finding its level.

Here is what nobody in Bristol knew about Edmund Hartwell: nothing at all, which was precisely how he liked it.

* * *

The letters were the thing.

He hadn't planned it, at first. In his first month of driving for her — hired off a reference from a livery stable that no longer existed, with papers that said he was Edmund Hartwell of Clifton, born 1881, parents deceased, occupation whatever the moment required — he had delivered her mail unopened, sealed as scripture, untouched. A man had his ethics. A man had lines.

But letters are heavy things, even when they weigh an ounce. You feel them in your hand. You feel the thickness of them, the intention, the way a sealed envelope is a room with the door shut. And Edmund had spent his whole life standing outside rooms with the door shut.

The first one he opened was an accident, or close enough to an accident that he could keep calling it one. An envelope stuck, gummed badly, and his thumbnail went through the flap, and there was the letter, right there, half-exposed, and it would have been stranger — more suspicious — to reseal it than to read what was already showing. So he read it. Two lines about a subscription to *Votes for Women*. Nothing. A locked door opening onto a broom cupboard.

But the second time wasn't an accident. And the third time wasn't either.

By the spring of 1911, Edmund had read perhaps three hundred letters that were not addressed to him. He knew the suffrage movement in Bristol the way a clergyman knows his congregation — by its confessions. He knew which district organizers were quarrelling with headquarters in London. He knew that Mrs. Aldous's husband didn't know where her money went. He knew that a young woman named Ruth Cavanagh wrote to Adeline every fortnight in a hand that started controlled and ended ragged, as if the words outran the pen. He knew the movement's whole private weather, its fronts and pressures, its storms brewing over Manchester and London, and he knew none of it was his.

That was the arrangement. That was the creed. History happened to other people. It happened to Adeline Voss, who would be remembered; it happened to the women who chained themselves to railings and fed themselves through tubes and had their names printed in newspapers their fathers burned. It happened out there, beyond the driver's box, in the bright loud world where names mattered. Edmund's business was the space between events. He carried history from house to house and set it down on hall tables, and then he drove away.

It should have been enough. Most days it almost was.

* * *

At the printer's on Wine Street, Mr. Gedge, a small damp man with ink permanently worked into the creases of his knuckles, tried to charge for the spoiled pamphlets exactly as predicted.

"They were spoiled because your compositor set the wrong date," Adeline said, not raising her voice, which was worse than raising it. "Two thousand pamphlets announcing a meeting on the ninth when the meeting was on the sixth. Do you imagine anyone came? Do you imagine the police didn't find that amusing?"

"The error was regrettable—"

"The error was convenient, Mr. Gedge, and we both know whose convenience we're discussing. You'll reprint at cost or I shall take my business to Stokes & Croft, and I shall also mention to certain parties that your politics shift depending on who's holding the purse. Now — are we reprinting?"

They were reprinting. Edmund stood by the door holding his cap and watching the exchange the way he always watched exchanges between Adeline and tradesmen: with admiration and a small cold feeling underneath it, like a coin dropped down a well that never hit bottom. Because the truth was he loved these moments and hated that he loved them, loved being adjacent to consequence, to a woman who said things aloud that other people only thought, and the cold feeling was the knowledge that adjacency was all he would ever have. He was the wall the light fell on. He was not the light.

Outside, back in the seat, Adeline exhaled and laughed once, shortly.

"You enjoy that," Edmund said.

"I despise it. That's why I'm good at it." She passed him the day's new letters to add to the satchel. "You're quiet this morning. Quieter."

"I'm always quiet."

"Yes, but usually it's a comfortable quiet, like a cat's. Today it's a held-breath quiet." She looked at him directly, and her eyes were pale grey and missed nothing, which was the great trial of working for Adeline Voss — that behind the performance of bluntness was an instrument of unusual sensitivity. "Is something wrong?"

"No, ma'am."

She held the look a moment longer, then let it go. "Very well. But when it is something, you'll tell me. I don't pay you to be stoic. Stoicism I can get free from my family."

She didn't know how funny that was. He turned the mare toward Queen's Road and kept his face arranged into the mild blankness of a man thinking about harness buckles.

* * *

Mrs. Aldous pretended not to be home. Edmund waited eleven minutes on the step until she pretended otherwise, accepted the envelope with trembling hands, and murmured the code phrase — "Compliments of Miss Voss, and she says the roses are coming along" — which meant *the money is inside, burn this*. Mrs. Aldous's eyes went glassy with relief, and she pressed a shilling into his palm that he later dropped into a beggar's hat on Park Street, not out of virtue but because keeping it felt like being paid twice for the same theft.

Because that was the thing he circled and never landed on, the thing he avoided looking at directly the way you avoid a loose tooth: reading the letters was theft, and he had made his peace with it, and his peace was thin as communion wine.

He told himself the old story. *I'm nobody's correspondent. No one writes to me. These words were never meant for me, but they're passing through my hands, and hands notice what they hold.* He told himself he was a student of history, an amateur, self-taught, attending lectures through the keyhole. He told himself that a man who has no name worth writing — whose papers say one thing and whose memory says another, whose mother's face he could barely reconstruct from the ruin of it, whose father was a question mark — that such a man must live somewhere, and if he couldn't live his own life, he might live along the edges of other people's, the way ivy lives on a wall. The wall doesn't mind. The wall doesn't even know.

But some mornings, handing over envelopes that he'd steamed open at dawn over the kitchen kettle and resealed with a paste stick and a steady hand, he felt the ache come up in him like water in a cellar. Not guilt, exactly. Guilt would have been simpler. It was more like hunger. He read three hundred letters a year and not one of them contained his name. He knew everything happening to everyone and nothing was happening to him. He was the most informed invisible man in England, and the sum total of his knowledge was the proof of his erasure: history, vast and burning, going on everywhere except in the place where he stood.

Consequence clopped along the Hotwells road, the harbour spread out below, masts and cranes and the smoke of the Ireland boats. Edmund looked at the ships without knowing why, the way you touch a bruise without meaning to. Something in the smoke over the docks snagged at him, faintly, like a hook catching a sleeve. He shook it off.

* * *

The Guildhall meeting ran long and ended in shouting, which Adeline called a success. By the time Edmund brought the carriage round, the light was failing and the gas lamps were coming on in the streets like a slow infection, and Adeline climbed up beside him instead of inside, which she did sometimes, when the day had gone well or badly and she wanted the air.

"Mr. Gedge reprinted at cost," she reported.

"A victory for civilization."

"Don't be arch." But she smiled. Then, after a while: "There's a letter in the satchel I need you to take tonight, not tomorrow. To number fourteen, Charlotte Street. Knock twice, wait, knock once. Give it to whoever answers, and don't wait for an answer back."

"Secret letter," Edmund said. "How exciting for me."

"It isn't exciting. It's necessary, and necessary things done quietly are just work." She was quiet a moment, watching the lamps go by. "Do you ever wonder what's in them, Edmund? The letters?"

The question landed with a strange precision, like a needle finding a vein. He kept his eyes on the horse's ears.

"Can't say I've thought about it, ma'am."

"No?" She almost laughed. "A man carries other people's mail for six years and never wonders? You're either a saint or you're lying, and I've seen you cheat at cards, so."

"Cheating at cards keeps the ladies' morale up. Losing to them does no good at all."

"Mm." She looked out at the street. "Whatever's in them, most of it's grief, if you want to know. Grief and hope and money, mostly money. Women hiding what they spend and where they go and sometimes who they are. Everyone in this movement is carrying a sealed envelope, Edmund. Even the ones who shout in public. Especially those."

*Everyone in this movement is carrying a sealed envelope.* He felt the words settle onto him like a hand on the shoulder.

"Even you?" he said.

"Especially me. But mine will open in its own time, and probably not pleasantly." She gathered herself as the house came into view, lit warm against the dusk. "Knock twice, then once. And Edmund — if anyone asks you about that delivery, you took a parcel to a dressmaker. Boring as bread. Can you manage boring as bread?"

"Boring as bread is my native language, ma'am."

She patted his arm once, briefly — the closest thing to tenderness she permitted on weekdays — and went inside.

* * *

He didn't steam that one open. He wanted to note that, for the record, for the ledger he kept in himself, the one where credits and debits balanced against the theft of three hundred letters. This one he carried sealed, through the lamplit streets, to number fourteen Charlotte Street, and he knocked twice and waited and knocked once, and a woman opened the door on a chain — a young woman, dark-haired, tired around the eyes, with a look that went through him and past him, cataloguing the street behind him before she catalogued him.

"From Miss Voss," he said. "Compliments, and the roses are coming along."

She took the letter. Her fingers brushed his, and they were cold, and for half a second her eyes focused on him properly — on his face, actually on him — and something crossed her expression too fast to name. Recognition, maybe, or its opposite: the shock of seeing a stranger where she'd expected a kind of person rather than a person.

"Thank you," she said, and closed the door.

He stood on the step a moment, in the smell of coal smoke and someone's boiling cabbage, and found his heart going faster than the walk warranted. It was nothing. People looked at each other on doorsteps all the time. He turned up his collar and went back to the carriage.

But driving home through the dark, the letter satchel empty beside him, he couldn't shake the sensation of having been — for half a second, by a stranger, on an unlit doorstep — *seen.* Not glanced at. Not categorized as driver, as furniture, as function. Seen the way you see a face in a crowd that belongs to someone you used to know.

And beneath the satchel, when he got back to his room above the stable, he found the morning's last letter still there — one he'd missed, wedged under the leather flap, addressed in a cramped hand he didn't recognize. Addressed not to Miss Voss, but to *the household*, which meant it was fair game, or near enough, or that was what he told himself as he slit it open with the letter knife and unfolded the single page.

He read it twice. Then a third time, standing motionless in the cold room, the lamp hissing.

It was a short letter. It concerned a woman who was not who she claimed to be — a woman living under a name that was not hers, sheltered among respectable people who did not know, and the writer urged discretion above all, because there were men asking questions, official men, and a census was coming.

*The census,* the letter said. *In April they will count every soul under every roof, and names will have to match papers, and papers do not lie quietly forever.*

Edmund stood holding it a long time, the lamp hissing, his own false name folded away upstairs in a drawer with his false life.

Then, because he was who he was — a man who lived along the edges of other people's histories — he folded it small, hid it under the floorboard with the others, and told himself, firmly, in the voice he used for horses and for himself:

None of your business.

The word *census* stayed with him anyway, all night, ticking like something buried in the wall.
