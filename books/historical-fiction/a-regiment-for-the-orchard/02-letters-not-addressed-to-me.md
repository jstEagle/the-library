## 2. Letters Not Addressed to Me

The letter was addressed to Mrs. E. Voss, and Edmund read it anyway.

He had a system, refined over eleven months of driving Adeline Voss through Bristol's wet streets. He collected the post at eight, sorted it in the front seat while the engine ticked and cooled, and set aside anything that looked private — which meant everything. Bills he skimmed. Invitations he barely glanced at. But letters with handwriting that leaned hard into the paper, letters sealed twice or sent care of the stationer on Park Street instead of the house itself — those he read properly, holding them low against his knees, one eye on the gate.

It wasn't theft. That was the argument he'd settled on, the one he returned to the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. A letter in his hands was already out of its owner's keeping. He didn't take copies. He didn't sell what he learned to anyone, though he'd been offered — twice — by men who thought a driver heard everything and could be bought for the price of a pint. He simply read, and then he delivered, and then he forgot. Mostly.

His creed was simple: history happened to other people. It happened to Mrs. Pankhurst in her prison cell, to the strikers in the papers, to the names printed above the fold while he ate his supper standing up. It did not happen to Edmund Hartwell, who drove a motorcar for a living and kept his opinions where they couldn't be subpoenaed. Reading other people's mail was as close as he cared to come to the front lines of anything. He was a spectator who'd found the best seat in the house, and the ticket cost him nothing but a small, regular transaction with his own conscience.

He'd made that trade so long ago he'd stopped feeling it.

* * *

This letter was different before he even broke the seal.

For one thing, it came care of the tobacconist on Queen's Road, which meant the sender hadn't wanted it passing through any sorting office. For another, the handwriting was disguised — not badly, but deliberately, the letters standing too straight, like a man wearing a false moustache. And for a third thing, which Edmund noticed only after he'd opened it there in the car outside the house, with the rain ticking on the roof and the smell of petrol and wet wool all around him: it began *Dear Eleanor*.

Adeline Voss's name was Adeline. Everyone knew it. The newspapers knew it; the constables who loitered outside suffrage meetings knew it; the greengrocer knew it, and charged accordingly. Whoever Eleanor was, this letter was not meant for the woman whose mail Edmund carried every day of his working life.

*Dear Eleanor*,

*You will forgive the roundabout route of this note, but you taught me caution and I have tried to be your student. I write to say that the arrangement stands. The rooms are ready whenever you need them, and no questions asked — you were clear on that point, and I honour it.*

*I confess I do not know what name you go by now. I have decided this is a mercy rather than an injury. The woman I knew was brave past the point of sense, and if she has had to become someone else to survive, then I hope whoever she has become is sleeping well and eating enough. Both were never her strong points.*

*Do not reply. Do not come to me. When it is safe, you will know how to find me, because you found me before when it was less safe, and I am still at the same window most evenings.*

*Your friend, always,*
*M.*

Edmund read it three times.

The first time, he read it the way he read everything — quickly, hungrily, a spectator taking in the scene. Someone hiding under a false name. Rooms waiting somewhere, no questions asked. A fugitive, then, or near enough. His pulse did its small traitor's jump, the one it always did when other people's history brushed against his sleeve. So the Voss circle had its secrets. Of course it did. Half of Bristol's respectable drawing rooms had secrets, and the half that didn't were only better organized about it.

The second time, slower, he noticed the details that didn't sit right. *You taught me caution.* Whoever Eleanor was, she'd trained someone else in tradecraft — false addresses, coded silences. That wasn't the amateur stuff of drawing-room committees and petition-signing. That was the grammar of people who expected to be hunted.

The third time, he read it as a man reading his own sentence, though he didn't know that yet. Because the third time, he stopped being a spectator.

The letter was addressed to the Voss household. Care of the tobacconist, yes, but the tobacconist held mail for the Voss house — Edmund had collected from him before, small parcels, nothing marked. Which meant *M.*, whoever he was, believed Eleanor received post through Adeline Voss. Which meant either Adeline was forwarding letters to some hidden woman — or Eleanor was someone inside the Voss circle, hiding in plain sight, under a name that wasn't hers.

Under a name that wasn't hers.

Edmund sat very still in the driver's seat. Rain ran down the windscreen in long silver threads. Somewhere behind the house, the kitchen door banged; Marta the cook was arguing with the milk boy again, her voice carrying the way it always did, untroubled by secrets.

Here was the thing about being invisible: you told yourself it was a gift. You told yourself the world's business was none of yours, and yours was none of the world's, and between those two mercies you lived comfortably, lightly, untouched. But invisibility had a second face, and Edmund had spent eleven months not looking at it. Invisibility was also complicity. The invisible man saw everything and reported nothing, and called his silence neutrality, and slept well on the word.

He folded the letter along its original creases, precisely, the way a surgeon closes.

"None of my business," he said aloud, to hear how it sounded.

It sounded exactly like what it was.

* * *

He delivered it, of course. That went without saying — or it should have gone without saying, and yet he stood in the hall for a full minute afterward, turning over whether it had gone without saying, whether he might have done otherwise, whether "otherwise" was even a category available to a man like him.

Adeline Voss took the morning's post in the study, standing at the window, because sitting down meant staying, and staying meant thinking, and thinking was for after midnight. She sorted without ceremony: bills to the left, correspondence to the centre, the weekly circular from the WSPU dropped onto the pile that would be answered by Miss Quill when she came Thursday.

The letter from *M.* she read last. He watched her face do something complicated and quick — a flicker, a stillness — and then it was gone, banked like a fire raked over with ash, and she set the letter flat on the desk and put a paperweight on it and said, in a voice entirely level:

"The car wants oiling, Mr. Hartwell?"

"It does, ma'am."

"See to it after luncheon. And Edmund—" She almost never used his Christian name; it landed oddly, warmly, like a coin dropped in a beggar's cup. "If anyone comes asking about the household's correspondence, you'll recall that we receive bills and invitations and nothing else."

"I don't recall receiving anything else," Edmund said.

It was smooth. It was perfectly smooth, and he hated it, because it was true and untrue at once — he *hadn't* recalled anything else, officially, and both of them knew what the sentence was doing, and neither of them flinched. Two liars confirming each other's alibis across a writing desk. She gave him the ghost of a nod and turned back to the window, and he let himself out, and in the hall he discovered his hands weren't quite steady.

Not fear. He examined the feeling carefully, the way he examined engines — listening for the knock. Not fear. Something adjacent. The unease of a man who has just realized the play he's been watching from the gallery has been watching him back.

She knew he read the letters.

Or she suspected. Or she simply understood that any man who handled her post handled it with eyes, and had priced that risk into her arrangements years ago, the way she priced everything. Either way, she'd just recruited him — silently, deniably — into the conspiracy of the household. *Nothing else.* His silence was now a service rendered. His invisibility was now an asset on her books.

He should have felt used. Instead he felt, horribly, included, and inclusion was a currency he had no practice spending.

* * *

That evening he walked home the long way, up through Clifton, because walking was where he did his forgetting and today the forgetting wouldn't take.

The city did its usual evening work around him. Lamplighters moving down the hill with their poles. Tram sparks jumping blue off the overhead wire. Boys selling late editions — *PARLIAMENT SITTING AGAIN ON CONCILIATION BILL*, the headlines shouting about other people's history, the great machinery grinding on in London while Bristol bought its fish and queued its trams. None of it touched Edmund Hartwell. That had been the whole design of his life: to be the man the machinery never reached.

But the letter had gotten grease on his hands, so to speak. *No questions asked.* *Whatever name you go by now.* He turned it over and over on the walk, and each turn produced a new splinter.

Eleanor. Who was Eleanor? He inventoried the Voss circle the way he inventoried a toolbox. Miss Quill, the secretary — fortyish, competent, a Yorkshire accent she'd sanded down to respectability. Marta, the cook, foreign in some direction nobody pressed. The women who came Thursdays: Mrs. Aldous, loud and fearless and transparent as glass; the two Pemberton sisters, who finished each other's sentences and everyone else's secrets. And Adeline herself — but no, Adeline was Adeline, stamped and public, her name in the papers monthly. Nobody hid a woman that visible.

Which left the edges. Visitors who came at odd hours. The woman who'd stayed a week in September, "a cousin," tall and quiet, who'd left before dawn and never written. The gentleman caller, once, whom Adeline had met at the door herself and shown upstairs, and who had departed looking over his shoulder.

Any of them. All of them. Or none — perhaps Eleanor was nobody in the household at all, merely someone who used the address, a stranger sheltering in the gap between the Voss name and the law's attention. Perhaps the entire matter concerned Edmund not at all.

Yes, he thought. Let that be true. Let it be none of my business, and let me return tomorrow to bills and invitations and the weather, and let this be one more story I watched from the gallery and closed the programme on.

He got home to his two rooms above the stable mews, lit the lamp, and ate bread and cheese standing up, per the creed. History happened to other people.

Then why, he asked the ceiling, am I the only one who knows?

Because knowledge was the poison of it. Before today, his reading had been harmless — a spectator's vice, victimless, the letters' contents dying quietly in his head. But now he *knew* something. Knowing changed the chemistry. There was a woman alive somewhere in England going by a name that wasn't hers, and Edmund Hartwell knew it, and if ever the law came asking — and the law, he'd noticed, had lately developed a taste for asking, what with the census coming and the government's new appetite for counting every soul in the kingdom — then his knowledge would stop being private. Silence would become a choice, made fresh each time, and choices left marks.

The census. April, they'd said. Every man, woman, and child accounted for, by name, by birthplace, by relation. The state sharpening its pencil over every household in Britain, and here was the Voss house with a phantom in its walls, and here was Edmund, the invisible man, holding a secret that a single pencil line could drag into the light.

He washed his plate, set it to dry, and stood at his window watching the lights of the city do their honest, countable business below.

"None of your business," he told himself again, and this time he heard it for what it had become: not a creed but an alibi.

He slept badly. In the grey hour before morning he dreamed of a letter addressed to himself — his own name on it, in handwriting he almost recognized — and woke with the odd, formless dread that the world was preparing to stop letting him watch from the gallery.

Downstairs, the post had already begun to arrive. Most of it was ordinary. One envelope was not.

It was addressed to the house, in careful copperplate — not to Mrs. Voss, not to Eleanor, but to *The Occupier*. And the franking on it, when he turned it over in his hands, bore the crest of the census office.
