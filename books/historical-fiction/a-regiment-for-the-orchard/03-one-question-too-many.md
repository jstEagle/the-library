## 3. One Question Too Many

Census day came in on a grey wind off the Avon, the sort of morning that made Bristol look like a photograph of itself—soot and slate and gulls wheeling over the water like punctuation marks no one had bothered to read.

Edmund Hartwell woke before the house did, as he always did, in the room above the carriage house that Adeline Voss paid for out of a purse she pretended not to keep accounts of. He lay still for a moment and listened to the building settle: the tick of the boiler, the groan of a pipe somewhere in the wall, the muffled sounds of Cook lighting the range in the kitchen below. A house was never silent. It only seemed silent to people who weren't listening.

He had been listening for eleven years. That was the whole of his profession, if he was honest about it, and he tried to be honest about it in the privacy of his own skull, where honesty cost nothing. He drove a motorcar for a woman who was, depending on which newspaper you held, either a menace to the British family or the only sane person in England. He carried her letters. He waited outside halls while she spoke to rooms full of women who applauded with their hats on. And in the hours between, he read what passed through his hands—not all of it, not greedily, but enough. Enough to feel, briefly, like a man who had been somewhere.

History happened to other people. Edmund had built a life on that principle the way other men built lives on God or drink. It was a comfortable creed. It asked nothing of you except that you stay small, stay quiet, stay useful. The world burned and voted and marched and wept, and Edmund Hartwell stood at the kerb with the engine running, invisible as weather.

He dressed, splashed water on his face, and went down to check the car. The Vauxhall sat in its damp brick bay like a patient animal, and he walked around it once, touching nothing, satisfying himself that it was where he'd left it, unvexed by the night. Then Cook's boy brought him tea and the day began to assemble itself around him, piece by familiar piece.

It should have been an ordinary Sunday. That was the joke of it, he thought later. He'd have remembered even an ordinary Sunday, after.

* * *

Adeline appeared at half nine with her hair pinned up and a list in her hand and the particular brightness she carried on days when there was work to be done. She was fifty-one years old and moved through the world like a woman perpetually late for something important, though she was never actually late for anything.

"Edmund. Good, you're upright." She handed him the list without ceremony. "The census man comes today. Mr.—Prowse, I'm told. He'll want everyone accounted for, so make sure the staff know he's coming and don't let him wander. Last time they counted us, 1901, the fellow helped himself to sherry and opinions in equal measure."

"Everyone accounted for," Edmund said. "I'll tell them."

"You'll be here? You're on the schedule?"

"I live above the carriage house, Mrs. Voss. I'm very much on the schedule."

"Good." She was already turning away, then stopped, the way she did when a thought caught her by the collar. "Answer him plainly, Edmund. These census men are civil servants, not detectives, but they write things down, and things written down have a way of outliving their usefulness." Her eyes rested on him a half-second longer than the remark required. "That's all. Plainly."

"Plainly," he said, and smiled, and she went off toward her study trailing purpose behind her like a scarf.

He told himself the look meant nothing. Adeline looked at everyone that way; she had the habit of suspicion the way other women had a habit of humming. He told himself this while he polished the mudguards, and again while he checked the tyre pressure, and a third time while he failed entirely to think about anything else.

Because here was the thing about being invisible: it required paperwork. Not much—a birth certificate bought secondhand from a clerk in Cardiff eleven years ago, a reference letter written in a hand that wasn't anyone's real hand, a National Insurance card issued to a man named Edmund Hartwell who had been born in Taunton in 1884 and who had, conveniently, died of nothing at all because he had never existed. The name Edmund Hartwell was real. It belonged to whoever wore it. Edmund had worn it so long now that some mornings he woke up inside it completely, and only in the gap between sleep and the kettle did he remember that it was a coat, not skin.

His mother's face he remembered truly. His mother's name he had left behind in a town he would not name even to himself, along with a father who drank and a childhood that ended the way a rope ends—frayed, then cut.

Plainly, he thought. Yes. He could do plainly. He'd been doing plainly for eleven years.

* * *

Mr. Prowse arrived at two o'clock precisely, which was the first thing wrong with him.

Census men, in Edmund's limited experience, were tired minor clerks doing a fortnight's extra work for extra pay, men who wanted their supper and their bed and as few complications between the two as possible. This one came up the drive at a measured pace, umbrella furled though there was no rain, and rang the bell exactly twice, and when Cook opened the door he said, "Good afternoon. Census enumerator for the district. I wonder if I might trouble the head of household," in a voice like a man reading a menu aloud to himself.

He was perhaps fifty-five, grey at the temples, with the neat, bloodless hands of a lifelong penman. He carried a leather portfolio under one arm, strapped shut, and he took off his hat in the hall and held it against his chest like a choirboy.

Adeline received him in the drawing room, because she believed in conducting official business on her own territory, and Edmund stationed himself by the door in his capacity as furniture-with-wages. The household was marshalled in due order: Cook, the maids Ada and Ruth—Ruth, quiet Ruth, who had come to the house fourteen months ago with a reference so glowing it read like an apology—and the gardener's lad, whose name changed according to need. Names were given. Dates were given. Places of birth were given. Prowse wrote them all down in a long, ruled book with a nib that scratched like a mouse in a wainscot.

Edmund watched him write and felt the particular boredom of the safe man watching procedure. Nothing here touched him. He was a line among lines. He was—

"And your name, sir?"

Prowse had turned in his chair. Not toward the maids. Toward Edmund.

"Hartwell. Edmund Hartwell."

"Occupation?"

"Driver. Motorcars."

"Mm." The nib moved. "And where were you born, Mr. Hartwell?"

"Taunton. Somerset."

The scratching stopped.

It was a small thing, the stopping. Half a second. But Edmund had spent eleven years learning to hear the sounds a room made when it was about to turn on you—the intake of breath before bad news, the chair pushed back before an accusation—and he heard it now in the silence between one scratch of the pen and the next. Prowse looked up. His eyes were pale, unhurried, the eyes of a man who had found a coin on the pavement and was deciding whether to pick it up or note its position for later.

"Taunton," Prowse repeated pleasantly. "Somerset. Born in '84, was it? You'll forgive me—I do like my columns tidy."

"January 1884."

"January 1884." The nib resumed, and Edmund's heart resumed with it, and he told himself, firmly, that he was a fool. Half a second of silence. Clerks paused all the time. Clerks' pens ran dry, clerks' minds wandered, clerks thought about their suppers. There was nothing in a pause.

But Prowse lingered over the next questions too. Age at last birthday—confirmed. Personal description—he didn't ask for one, since the form didn't require it, but his gaze travelled over Edmund's face anyway, cataloguing, the way a tailor measures a customer he intends to remember. And then, folding the form closed with a crispness that sounded almost satisfied:

"That's the household complete. Mrs. Voss, you've been most helpful. Most helpful indeed." He stood, took up his hat, and paused at the door with the air of a man remembering an afterthought. "You'll find these things go smoother, you know, when everyone answers plainly. It's the little discrepancies that cause trouble later. Birthplaces especially. People move about, forget themselves. The paper remembers, though." He smiled at a point slightly to the left of Edmund's face. "Paper always remembers."

Then he was gone, umbrella swinging, footsteps crunching away down the gravel, and the drawing room breathed out, and Adeline said, "What an odd little man," and poured herself a sherry, and life went on.

Edmund stood by the door and discovered that his hands were cold.

* * *

He told himself he was imagining it through the evening. He told himself while he put the car away, while he ate, while he climbed the stairs to his room above the carriage house and lit the lamp. He was good at telling himself things; it was practically a profession too.

But the phrase would not leave him alone. *The paper remembers.*

He knew why Prowse had paused. He worked it out lying on his narrow bed, staring at the ceiling, going back through the arithmetic of his own fiction like a man recounting stolen money. In 1901, the last census, he had been entered somewhere else—in Bristol, lodging over a livery stable, under the same name but a different birthplace. Plymouth. He'd given Plymouth in 1901 because the forged papers he'd bought that year said Plymouth, and the papers he'd bought in 1905 said Taunton, because the forger in Cardiff had sold him whatever origin suited the batch of documents that week. Two censuses. Two counties. One name.

A man checking—one careful, unhurried man with access to the schedules of 1901, if schedules were ever compared, and who was to say they weren't—might notice that Edmund Hartwell had been born in two places at once, which is a trick even the Church of England declines to perform.

The floor dropped.

Not literally, of course. The floorboards stayed where they were, solid under the rug, and the lamp stayed lit, and the night stayed ordinary. But Edmund felt it go—that vertiginous lurch of a man who has walked confidently across what he took for stone and finds it is a stage set, canvas over trestles, and beneath the canvas, nothing. Eleven years of invisibility, and it had taken one grey little man with a leather portfolio forty seconds to put a pin through it.

*Being seen.* He had forgotten what it felt like. It felt like this: cold starting at the spine and radiating outward, the pulse loud in the ears, the mind offering helpful images—cells, docks, a ship, handcuffs warming to the temperature of his wrist. He sat up and put his feet on the cold boards and breathed, slowly, the way you breathe when panic is a luxury.

They didn't hang you for a wrong birthplace. They fined you. Possibly. Probably. Unless the wrong birthplace led someone to ask *why*, and the why led to the name, and the name led to the papers, and the papers led backward along eleven years to whatever it was he'd been fleeing when he first decided history should happen to other people instead of him.

He got up. He paced. Above the carriage house there was not far to pace—six steps, turn, six steps—and he did it until his legs tired of the argument.

And underneath the fear, unwelcome as a guest arriving wet, was another feeling, one he examined the way you examine a tooth that's started to ache. Relief. A horrible, thin sliver of relief. Because if the paper remembered, then he existed somewhere outside his own vigilance. If a stranger could catch the discrepancy, then Edmund Hartwell was a fact in the world, verifiable, contestable—*seen*. For eleven years he had been a rumour in his own life. Now he was a discrepancy. It should not have felt like anything at all. It felt like being spoken to after a long illness.

He hated himself for the relief. He banked the fear on top of it and blew out the lamp.

* * *

Two miles across the city, in a rented room above a stationer's shop on Stokes Croft, Mr. Prowse unlocked the leather portfolio and laid out his evening's work.

There were thirty-one households in his district. Thirty-one schedules, thirty-one sets of names, ages, occupations, places of birth, marital conditions, infirmities. He transcribed each into his enumerator's book in a fine, upright, immaculately legible hand, the hand of a man who had spent thirty years making records that other people would have to read, and who intended them to suffer for it.

Household nineteen: VOSS, Adeline M., head, widow, 51, suffrage organiser (he wrote the occupation with a small, private satisfaction; let Whitehall choke on it). COOK, Margaret. ADA—. RUTH—. HARTWELL, Edmund, servant, driver (motor), single, 27.

At the Hartwell line he paused, and dipped his pen, and in the margin, beside the place of birth, he made a mark. Not a correction—a notation. A small, neat cross, four millimetres tall, meaning nothing to anyone but himself.

Then he turned to the back pages of the portfolio, past the schedules, to a folded sheet of cheap paper on which he had begun, in the same immaculate hand, a list. It was not an official document. It had no heading and no authority. It was simply a record of observations, kept in the manner of a man who had learned, over a long and undistinguished career, that observation was the one currency that never devalued.

Item the first: HARTWELL, E., driver, residence Clifton. Place of birth returned as Somerset (Taunton). Cross-reference: 1901 schedule, district 14, entry 62—same name, same age, place of birth returned as Devonport. Discrepancy noted. Value: probable.

Probable. He liked the word. It left room for the possibility that he was wrong, which he was not, and for the pleasure of confirming it, which he anticipated.

There were three other names on the folded sheet already. A lodger on Jacob Street who had aged nine years in ten. A widow in Redland whose "deceased" husband had been seen buying tobacco on Tuesday. And one more, further down, in fresher ink—a name from a large house, a young woman whose references glowed a shade too brightly, whom he intended to look into at his leisure.

He blotted the page carefully, closed the portfolio, strapped it shut, and set it by the door for the morning.

Below him, the stationer's dog stirred in its sleep, and a tram sang past on the wet rails, and the city settled into its Sunday-night hush, unaware that it had just been weighed and entered into a column.

In the margin of the sheet, at the bottom, Prowse added a final line, small as a footnote:

*Voss household: watch. Servant of interest. Approach gently — value improves with patience.*

He blew out his candle, satisfied. Paper remembered. Paper was patient. And Monday was coming, as Mondays did, bringing with it the ordinary machinery of the world—which had lately, and pleasingly, begun to run on information.

Above the carriage house in Clifton, Edmund Hartwell slept badly, and dreamed, for the first time in years, of a town he would not name, and woke before dawn with the certain knowledge—cold, specific, undeniable as gravel underfoot—that someone had written his name down, and that the writing was not finished with him yet.
