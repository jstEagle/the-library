## 4. The Orchard Behind the House

Edmund found her by accident.

He had come around the back of the house on a Tuesday morning to check the Daimler's tires — the rear left had been soft for a week and Adeline noticed everything about the motorcar while noticing nothing about him, which was exactly how he liked it. The carriage house stood at the end of a gravel path, and beyond it, past a wall he'd never thought to wonder about, there was a gate.

It was open. And through it, in a long rectangle of grass gone wild and apple trees grown leggy and strange, Adeline Voss stood with a pruning knife in one hand and her skirts pinned up like a woman preparing to wade a river.

He should have turned back. He was a driver; drivers did not wander into their employer's private grounds uninvited. But she had seen him — of course she had, she saw everything — and she lifted the knife in something between greeting and summons.

"Mr. Hartwell. Do you know anything about trees?"

"Nothing whatsoever, ma'am."

"Neither do I." She said it cheerfully, as though confessing a crime. "Come here anyway."

* * *

Adeline had not expected him to come.

She had expected him to make his excuses — the motorcar, the errands, the small machinery of avoidance that servants deployed so fluently — and she had already composed the gracious nothing she would say when he declined. That was the arithmetic of her days now: every request measured against refusal, every alliance provisional. The movement taught you that. People said yes to your face and no to the Home Office, or yes to the Home Office and no to you, and you learned to hold both truths without flinching.

But Edmund Hartwell came through the gate.

He came the way he did everything — carefully, quietly, as though the gravel might report him. He was a thin man, her driver, thirty perhaps, with the kind of face you forgot while looking at it. She'd hired him on Mrs. Pankhurst's cousin's recommendation and had wondered since whether the recommendation was a favor to him or to her. He drove beautifully. He spoke only when spoken to. He was, in short, exactly what she required, and she had learned to be suspicious of things that were exactly what she required.

"The trees were her project," Adeline said. "My sister's. Margaret."

She hadn't meant to say it. The name simply arrived, the way names do when you stand in a place that belongs to them.

Edmund said nothing, which was correct. Servants did not ask questions. But he didn't retreat either, and after a moment she found herself talking anyway, because the orchard had a way of pulling words out of you like splinters.

"She bought this house for the garden. Everyone assumed it was for the drawing room — good for meetings, good for hiding pamphlets in the piano bench, all of that. But Margaret wanted the trees. There were nine then. She counted them like children." Adeline cut away a dead branch; it came off too easily, rotten at the heart. "She died four years ago. Before any of it mattered. Before I became—" she gestured with the knife at herself, at the whole ridiculous apparatus of her public life, "—this."

"I'm sorry," Edmund said. It sounded true. Most condolences sounded like coins dropped in a plate.

"She'd have hated what the house has become," Adeline went on. "The comings and goings. The women crying in my parlor at midnight because they've been force-fed and can't bear to swallow tea. She'd have hated it, and she'd have been here every night anyway, holding the basin." She laughed, and it came out wrong, and she stopped. "I don't know why I'm telling you this."

"Orchards do that," he said.

And that was all. He picked up a fallen branch and began dragging it toward the wall without being asked, and she watched him go, and thought: *there is something wrong with that man*, in the way you think *it might rain* — a fact noted, filed, not yet acted upon.

* * *

A sister. Of course. Everyone had a dead sibling or a lost brother or a father who'd vanished into some colonial somewhere; grief was the common coin of the age and Edmund had learned to accept it without making change. He stacked branches against the wall and kept his eyes down and let her talk, because talking was easier than questions, and questions were dangerous.

He knew about dangerous questions. Mr. Prowse had asked him three last week, standing in the hall with his ledger open like a mouth: *Where were you born, Mr. Hartwell? And where precisely? And your mother — was she born there also?*

He had answered. He'd answered well. He'd given the name he'd worn for six years and the town that belonged to it and the smooth lie about his mother that he kept oiled like an axle. And Prowse had smiled and written something down, and the sound of the pencil had stayed with Edmund for days afterward, a small dry scratching, like a mouse in a wall.

Now he dragged another branch across the grass and tried to think about nothing at all.

"You've done this before," Adeline called. "Worked land."

"No, ma'am."

"Liar. You hold that branch like a scythe."

He looked down at his own hands as if they belonged to someone else. They did, in a way. Six years ago they had been someone else's hands entirely — a boy's hands, in another county, under another name, hauling hay for a farmer who'd paid him in bread and suspicion. He flexed them and said, "Perhaps I read about it."

"In a book?"

"There are books about everything."

She laughed — properly this time — and something in his chest moved in a way he did not authorize and did not examine. This was the peril of the Voss household: the walls were thin with humanity. He had taken the position because it was invisible work for an invisible man, and instead he had spent eight months in the current of other people's lives, reading their letters, hearing their arguments, watching history happen to everyone but himself.

And now the letters had started saying things. Last week, a note in a hand he recognized from a dozen envelopes, unsigned, mentioning "our friend in the attic rooms" — except the Voss house had no attic tenant that Edmund had ever seen, and the letter had been burned within the hour by Adeline herself, at the drawing-room fire, while he stood in the doorway with the post tray.

He had told himself it was none of his business. He was still telling himself that. It was becoming a full-time occupation.

"Margaret would have liked you," Adeline said suddenly.

He nearly dropped the branch. "Ma'am?"

"She liked people who listened. She said most people were just waiting for their turn to talk, and the ones who weren't were worth knowing." Adeline wiped her hands on her apron and looked at him directly, and Edmund understood, with a lurch, that she was not being kind. She was being careful. Kindness was free; care cost something. "Where did you learn to listen, Mr. Hartwell?"

"A large family," he said. The lie was smooth. He hated how smooth. "You learn to wait."

"How many?"

"Seven." His mother had seven children by her first marriage, before Edmund, before the flight, before everything scattered like seed from a shaken head. It wasn't even a lie, exactly. It was inventory.

"Seven," she repeated, and something flickered across her face — not suspicion, he decided. Recognition. The look of a woman who had also built a person out of spare parts and set him loose in the world. Then she turned back to her tree, and the moment passed over him like weather.

* * *

The tree was worse than Adeline had let herself believe.

She worked at it with the knife, opening the bark here and there, and found what she'd feared: canker, deep, running the length of the trunk like a bad sentence. The whole row was the same. Four years of neglect and the trees had begun quietly dying from the inside, still leafing out each spring out of habit, still producing small bitter apples nobody picked, dying all the same.

*You could say the same of me,* she thought, and then laughed at herself for the melodrama, and then stopped laughing, because it was true.

Margaret had planted hope in this ground. Not the loud hope — not the window-smashing, hunger-striking, headline hope that Adeline had inherited and made her own — but the quiet kind, the patient kind, the kind that put a tree in the earth expecting nothing for five years. Margaret used to say that the movement was all storm and no season. *Anyone can shout, Ade. Growing something takes character.*

Well. Margaret had grown trees and died at thirty-four of a fever that took her in nine days, and Adeline had shouted, and the trees had rotted, and here she was with a knife in her hand trying to decide which failure to address first.

She glanced back at Hartwell. He had cleared half the fallen wood already and was working without hurry, without expectation, a man entirely comfortable in silence. She had employed dozens of people in her life and could count on one hand the ones who were comfortable in silence. Most filled it the way children fill a cellar — with noise, against the dark.

"Mr. Hartwell."

"Ma'am."

"If I asked you to help me bring these trees back — proper pruning, manure, the lot of it — would you think less of me? A woman of my reputation, playing at farming?"

He considered this with what appeared to be genuine seriousness. "I think," he said, "that a reputation is like a tree, ma'am. It matters what it's rooted in."

She stared at him. "Did you just make that up?"

"Yes, ma'am."

"It's dreadful."

"Yes, ma'am." But he was almost smiling, and so was she, and the morning opened around them like a gate.

They worked until noon. He hauled and dug and held things; she pruned and swore and learned, slowly, where to cut so the tree would heal rather than bleed. At some point she talked about Margaret again — the way she sang badly, the way she'd once smuggled a fugitive coachman's daughter to Wales inside a hay wagon, the way she'd said *we are a regiment, Ade, we just haven't mustered yet.* At some point Hartwell began to talk, a little, in his flat careful voice — about soils, about grafting, about a farm he'd worked at, briefly, years ago, in a county he didn't name. Each fact was a door closed before she could see the room behind it. But the doors kept opening. A man like that gave you facts the way a miser gave coins: singly, reluctantly, each one costing him visibly more than it appeared to be worth.

By the time the church bells rang the quarter-hour she was filthy to the elbows and lighter than she had been in months, and she caught herself thinking the dangerous thought — *I could trust this one* — and put it away where she kept all such thoughts, in the drawer that had gotten Margaret's friends arrested twice.

"Thank you," she said. "Same time Thursday?"

"If you wish, ma'am."

"I wish." She unpinned her skirts and looked at the ruined trees, half-revealed now from their tangle, wounded but salvageable. All but two. Those would have to come out entirely, cut to the stump, and something new planted in their place. "Some of these won't survive," she said aloud, not entirely about the trees.

"No, ma'am," said Hartwell. "But the ground remembers what was planted. If you get the roots right, it comes back."

She looked at him sharply. He was studying the nearest trunk with great attention, as if the answer to everything were written in its bark.

*There is something wrong with that man,* she thought again. And then, because she was tired and the sun was warm and trust was the one crop she'd never managed to grow: *Whatever it is, I find I don't much care.*

* * *

Edmund walked back around the house with dirt under his nails and terror in his throat.

Not the orchard — the orchard had been fine, better than fine, an hour in which he had almost been a person. No: it was the question she'd asked, the light one, thrown away like change. *Where did you learn to listen?*

*A large family.*

He had said it easily. That was the horror of it. Six years of practice and the lies came out polished as apples, and today, for the first time, he had felt the polish crack — felt Adeline Voss look straight through the fruit at whatever was rotting underneath, and choose, generously, not to look closer.

She would look closer eventually. They always did. Prowse was already looking; his pencil had said so.

That evening Edmund carried the post tray into the drawing room as usual. Three letters. He sorted them with his eyes down, the invisible man, the man to whom history happened to other people — and at the bottom of the tray his thumb found the fourth envelope, the one that hadn't been there an hour ago when he'd left the tray in the hall.

No stamp. Hand-delivered. Addressed to *Mrs. Voss* in a cramped, urgent hand — and beneath the address, in smaller letters, as though the writer could not stop himself:

*Burn this before the census man returns.*

Edmund carried it upstairs. He told himself he would deliver it unread, unread, unread —

and his fingers were already loosening the flap.
