## 5. Inspector Calloway Calls

The knock came at half past nine on a Tuesday, which was wrong on two counts. Tradesmen came before eight and after five, when the light was low and the streets were theirs. The Voss house did not receive callers before ten, and it never received them on Tuesdays, because Tuesdays were when Adeline Voss wrote her column for *The Clarion* and would not be interrupted by God himself.

So when Mrs. Trench appeared in the kitchen doorway with a card held between finger and thumb like something dead she'd found in the drain, Edmund knew before he read it that the day had turned.

"Inspector Calloway," Mrs. Trench said. "Bristol Constabulary. He's got a constable with him. Standing on the step like a pair of hat stands."

Edmund dried his hands. He had been polishing the brass fittings on the Darracq, an hour of small meditative work that kept his mind off the letter folded into his breast pocket — the third one this week, the one he'd told himself he'd stop reading and hadn't. He put the cloth down carefully, as if the care he showed the cloth might transfer to what came next.

"Did you tell him Miss Voss is writing?"

"I told him Miss Voss sees nobody on Tuesdays." Mrs. Trench's mouth went thin. "He said he'd wait. He said—" she paused, and Edmund watched her decide whether to soften it, and watched her decide not to, "—he said he'd wait as long as it pleased him."

The card was cream stock, good stock, better than most inspectors could afford. *Inspector T. Calloway. Criminal Investigation Department.* Beneath the printed name, in pencil, in a small precise hand: **Regarding census irregularities.**

Not a question. A statement with the question mark amputated.

"Where's Miss Voss?"

"The morning room. Door shut."

"And Ruth?"

"Gone out. Marketing." A beat. "With Ellen."

Good. Whatever this was, let it land on him first. He was, after all, the irregularity. He had known it since Prowse's pen had hovered over the ledger three weeks ago, since the man's wet eyes had lifted from the page to Edmund's face with the particular satisfaction of a fisherman feeling a tug on the line. He'd told himself the moment had passed. Men like Prowse collected small discomforts; they didn't cash them in.

Apparently they did.

"I'll answer the door," Edmund said.

"You're not staff for door-answering."

"I'm whatever the house needs me to be this morning." He took off his apron. Underneath he was wearing his second-best suit, because Tuesdays he drove nowhere and dressed like a man with nothing to do, and there was no time to change and no point pretending otherwise. Let the inspector see a driver in his good coat. Drivers wore their best on their days off. It meant nothing.

It occurred to him, crossing the hall, that he had rehearsed lies for this exact moment without ever admitting he was rehearsing them. In bed at night, in the grey space before sleep, his mind ran drills: *Where were you born? Who is your father? Why does your reference from Bath not exist?* And each night he had answered smoothly, easily, in the dark where answers cost nothing. He had thought of it as prudence. Standing now with his hand on the latch, he understood it for what it was: practice.

* * *

Calloway was not what Edmund expected, which he supposed was the first trick. He'd expected bulk, or bluster, or the theatrical sadness policemen wore like mourning bands. What stood on the step instead was a lean man of perhaps fifty, silvering at the temples, in a coat cut by someone who understood shoulders. His face was open, friendly, almost kind — a face built for parlours and confidences, a face you'd tell your troubles to on a train.

Behind him, the constable held a leather notebook and the expression of a man who wished he were anywhere else.

"Mr. Hartwell?" Calloway said, before Edmund had spoken. "The driver. Yes — I can see it. The hands." He smiled. "Inspector Thomas Calloway. May I trouble the household for a few minutes?"

"That depends what you're troubling it about."

"Direct. Good. I get so tired of servants who pretend not to understand English." He produced the census schedule from inside his coat — not a copy, the original, Prowse's fair copy with its ruled lines and its little pencilled annotations, and Edmund felt the floor tilt a half-degree under him before he steadied it. "May we speak inside? The wind off the river does nothing for my chest."

There was no wind off the river. It was a still, grey morning smelling of coal smoke and last night's rain. But you didn't refuse a man his chest. Edmund stepped back and let them in, and heard behind him, faintly, the scratch of the constable's pencil already moving.

They settled in the front parlour, the good room, the room with Adeline's father's portrait and the cold fireplace. Edmund did not offer chairs. Calloway took one anyway, unhurried, arranging himself as though the room had been prepared for him.

"Now." He laid the schedule on his knee. "You'll know the census has been causing a certain amount of correspondence. Clerical matters mostly — names spelled oddly, ages disputed, the usual comedy of a nation asked to count itself in an afternoon. But some entries attract more attention than others." He turned the sheet around and tapped one line with one finger.

**HARTWELL, EDMUND J. — Coachman (domestic). Born: Clifton, Bristol. Age 29.**

"You were born in Clifton," Calloway said pleasantly.

"Yes, sir."

"That's what the paper says." He smiled again, and the smile did all the work his voice refused to do. "Only here's my difficulty, Mr. Hartwell. I've been making inquiries — routine, purely routine — and Clifton keeps a very good register. Very good. Baptisms, births, the parish books are models of their kind. And there's no Hartwell born in Clifton in any year near yours. No Hartwell baptized, no Hartwell buried, no Hartwell married. The name simply doesn't occur." He tilted his head. "Which is odd, isn't it? Because names usually occur. Names are the one thing people leave everywhere, like footprints."

Edmund felt the drill begin, the night-drill, the dark-space answer rising smooth as water. *My family were not churchgoers. My father was dissenting — Baptist — we were never on the parish rolls.* All true-shaped. All ready.

"It's the father," he said. "He was a chapel man. Strict Baptist. He wouldn't have the parish near us — thought registration was Romish meddling. Half the congregation's missing from those books. You'll find Hartwells in the Baptist records, if you look there, though Father would've called even that vanity."

Calloway listened with the attention of a man being handed a gift. "Baptist," he repeated. "And which chapel?"

"Zion Chapel, off Jacob's Wells Road. It closed — the minister died, the congregation scattered. That's why you won't find anyone to ask."

"Mm." Calloway looked at the ceiling as if the chapel might be written up there. "Dead ministers and dissolved congregations. You do meet a great many absent witnesses in my line of work, Mr. Hartwell. The living ones are such a disappointment." His eyes came down. They were pale grey, and they did not blink enough. "But you see how it looks. A man whose birthplace can't be verified. Employed in a household that—"

He stopped. Delicately. The stopping was louder than the sentence.

"—that?" Edmund said.

"That has come to notice." Calloway set the schedule aside, and produced instead a slim notebook of his own, and did not open it. "Let me be frank, since you're a frank man. I'm not here about you. You're a footnote, Mr. Hartwell, and I've nothing against footnotes. I'm here about a name. A woman who may have passed through Bristol in the last year under other names than her own. A woman whose friends believe her to be in London, or Manchester, or safely across the water, and whose enemies believe otherwise. Miss Voss's circle is — active. Active circles collect strays. Strays need feeding, housing, papers." He spread his hands. "I count papers. It's my whole occupation. And this house, according to the census, holds four souls: Miss Voss, yourself, a cook, and a general maid named Ellen Byrne. Yet the neighbours report more. Lights in the attic after midnight. A woman seen at an upper window in a green shawl, twice, in February. Laundry — well, you know what laundry tells you. Sheets for beds nobody officially sleeps in."

"The attic's storage," Edmund said. "Trunks. Furniture in oilcloth."

"Of course it is." Warmly. As if Edmund had confirmed everything. "And the green shawl?"

"Mrs. Trench owns a shawl. She's sixty-one and wears it against the damp."

"And Ellen Byrne." Calloway opened the notebook at last, and read aloud as though tasting each word. "Ellen Byrne, general servant, aged twenty-two, born Cork, Ireland. Entered service — when was it? — March last year. No prior position recorded. No reference. A girl appears out of the air with a Cork accent and takes up residence in the house of a woman whose newspaper columns have been called seditious by two separate members of Parliament." He closed the book gently. "Tell me about her."

"She's a good worker. Quiet. She came through an agency."

"Which agency?"

"A placement office on Park Street. It's gone — the woman who ran it retired to Weston."

The smile again, wider now, genuinely delighted. "Retired. Dissolved. Dead. Do you notice, Mr. Hartwell, how everyone connected to this house has vanished like breath off glass? Your minister. Your agency. Your birthplace. If I believed in patterns I'd think the house swept its history behind it like a broom." He rose, buttoned his coat, and became suddenly, terribly courteous. "I'll take up no more of your Tuesday. Give my regards to Miss Voss — no, don't give my regards, she'd find it presumptuous. Tell her only that I admire her writing. Truly. There's force in it. Force frightens people, and frightened people write to me." At the door he paused, and the friendliness dropped away for exactly one second, the way a mask lifts, and underneath was something patient and cold and very sure of itself. "One more thing. The schedule requires correction where entries are found false. Mr. Prowse will call again within the fortnight to amend the record. When he does — think carefully about what you'd like your life to say. Paper is patient, Mr. Hartwell. Paper always wins."

Then the smile returned like weather, and he was gone, and the constable with him, and the street door shut on the smell of the river that wasn't there.

* * *

Edmund stood in the hall until his legs remembered their business. Then he went to the kitchen, where Mrs. Trench was waiting with the rigid stillness of a woman who had heard every word through the door and would die before admitting it.

"Well?"

"He knows about the attic."

"He suspects about the attic. Knowing and suspecting are different animals." She wiped a counter that was already clean. "What did you tell him?"

"The truth, mostly. Truth travels further than lies — lies need carrying."

She glanced at him then, sharply, and something moved behind her eyes that might have been respect or might have been alarm. "Miss Voss will want to see you."

"Not on Tuesdays."

"On Tuesdays especially," Mrs. Trench said, "when the writing won't go."

He climbed the stairs slowly. Through the morning-room door he could hear it: the small furious tick of her pen, stopping, starting, stopping again. He raised his hand to knock, and stopped.

Because here was the argument, the one that had begun the moment Calloway's finger touched the ledger line and would not now stop arguing. *Loyalty says lie. Loyalty has been lying for years — it's what loyalty is made of, in houses like this one. But truth — truth says you built this. You read the letters. You learned the trade of other people's secrets, and you got good, so good that a policeman couldn't tell your lies from daylight, and you hate it, you hate how easy it was, how the words came out polished, Zion Chapel, Park Street, retired to Weston, three inventions in ninety seconds like a man dealing cards.*

*And Calloway saw it. That's the worst of it. He watched you lie and he enjoyed it, the way a man enjoys watching a horse he means to buy.*

Through the door, the pen stopped altogether.

"Edmund." Adeline's voice, muffled. Not a question. She'd heard the bell, or Mrs. Trench had already been up, or she simply knew — she had a genius for knowing when the weather of the house changed. "Come in. And bring whatever it is you're going to tell me, because I can hear you deciding whether to."

He opened the door. She sat at her desk with her pen capped and her hands flat on the blotter, and behind her the window showed the orchard, the ragged trees, the ground gone wild with spring. On her desk lay a letter, unopened, addressed in a hand he recognized — the same hand, or near enough, as the letter burning its quiet hole in his own breast pocket.

"Start anywhere you like," Adeline said. "But start today."
