## 6. Nell on the Quay

The parcel was heavier than it looked, which was Adeline's way of telling me she hadn't packed it herself. She had a gift for making other people's labor look effortless, including mine.

"Byrne," she said, pressing it into my arms. "Quay Street, the chandler's near the dry dock. A Miss Nell Byrne. You'll know her by her hands—she'll be mending nets or wishing she were."

"And what do I say if she asks who sent it?"

"Tell her a friend remembers Cork."

That stopped me. Not because of the words, but because of the way she said them—lightly, the way you'd mention weather, while watching me the whole time. Adeline Voss missed nothing. It was one of the many reasons I found her exhausting and could not have quit her for any money in England.

"Cork," I repeated.

"You were born there. Or so your papers say." She smiled and turned back to her desk, dismissing me with the particular kindness of a woman who has decided not to press. "Go on. The tide won't wait for parcels, and neither will I."

I carried the parcel down through the city like a man carrying something live. The morning was grey and smelling of river—that thick Bristol smell of tar and weed and coal smoke settling on water—and by the time I reached the bottom of the hill my collar was stuck to my neck and the parcel had grown a second weight entirely: the word *Cork*, sitting in my chest like a stone swallowed whole.

I had not thought about Cork in eleven years. That was not an accident. That was policy.

* * *

You have to understand something about the docks, because I didn't, not really, until that day. I had driven past them a thousand times—the Prince Street bridge, the sheds, the cranes standing like gallows against the sky—but driving past is its own country. You see shapes. You don't see people.

On foot, with a parcel under my arm, I fell into the current of Quay Street and the current took notice of me the way water notices a stick. Dockers coming off night shift, faces grey with fatigue and coal dust. Women with shawls and baskets, arguing prices at the fish stalls. Children running messages barefoot in April, which told you everything about the arithmetic of those households. A man selling hot eels from a cart, crying his wares in an accent I recognized before I understood that I recognized it—an Irish accent, but not any Irish accent. *That* accent. The vowels my mother had smoothed out of herself over years of careful practice, the ones she'd made me practice too, until I could pass.

The parcel grew heavier.

The chandler's shop was where Adeline said it would be, wedged between a sailmaker's loft and a public house called the Anchor, its window full of rope ends and tarred twine and lamps with glass gone yellow as old teeth. Inside smelled of hemp and paraffin. A man behind the counter looked at me, looked at the parcel, and jerked his thumb toward the door behind him.

"Yard," he said. "She'll be in the yard."

The yard was a courtyard stacked with crates and coils of line, open to the sky, and half of it given over to a long trestle table where three women sat mending nets, their needles going fast and blind, the way hands work when they've done ten thousand repetitions. Two of them were older, broad, brown-faced. The third was—

The third one.

I want to get this right, because I have turned it over so many times since that the edges have worn smooth, and I don't want them smooth. I want the rawness back.

She was perhaps nineteen. Small, the way my mother had been small—not slight, but compact, built like someone who'd learned early that space is a thing you take only as much of as you're given. Dark hair pinned up carelessly, escaping in curls at her temples. Sleeves rolled to the elbow. And she was laughing at something the older woman said, throwing her head back to laugh, and when she threw her head back the light came down into the yard and hit her face full on.

And I stopped walking.

Because it was like looking into a mirror that some careless god had tilted twenty years backward and handed to a stranger. The jaw. My jaw, the one I shaved every morning without thinking about it, sitting on a girl's face. The brow. The set of the eyes—not the color; hers were grey where mine are brown—but the *set* of them, slightly wide apart, slightly ironic even at rest, as though the world were a joke they'd heard the first half of and expected the rest to disappoint.

My mother's face. My mother's face, young again, wearing a net-mender's apron in a Bristol shipyard, laughing.

The parcel slipped. I caught it against my hip, hard enough to bruise, and the crack of it against bone was loud in the yard. Three heads came up.

"Lost something?" the oldest woman asked, not unkindly.

"I'm looking for—" My voice came out wrong, pitched too high, and I dragged it down. "Miss Byrne. Nell Byrne."

The girl set down her needle. And here is the thing—I watched her do it, watched her lay the needle across her knee with a precision that was somehow familiar, the way my mother used to lay down her sewing, squaring it, finishing the motion completely before looking up. Then Nell Byrne looked at me.

She looked at me for a long moment. I watched her do the same arithmetic I was doing—the jaw, the brow, the eyes—and I watched her arrive nowhere, because why would she? She had no reason to go looking for ghosts in a delivery boy's face. Her family, whatever it was, was hers.

"That's me," she said. Her voice had Cork in it too, unsmoothed, unashamed. "Who's asking?"

"A friend. In Clifton." I held out the parcel. "A friend who remembers Cork."

Something moved behind her eyes then. Not recognition—something before recognition, something older, the way a dog goes still before thunder arrives. She wiped her palms on her apron and took the parcel, and her fingers brushed mine, and her hands were cold and rough and exactly, exactly the shape of hands I had held crossing a street in Manchester when I was six years old.

I did not say anything else. I am not sure I could have. I touched my cap, turned, and walked out of the yard on legs that belonged to somebody else.

* * *

Behind the sailmaker's loft there was a stretch of quay wall where the dockers ate their dinners when it rained, sheltered under a wooden lean-to, and I walked there without deciding to and stood looking at the water with my heart going like a pump handle.

Let me tell you what I knew, in that moment, before I knew anything.

I knew that my mother had come from Cork, and that she had left it in 1889 with a husband and a baby and a story about starting fresh. I knew that she never spoke of her own family except to say they were dead, all of them, quickly, as if she'd rehearsed it. I knew that she died in '98 of the cough, having spent her last years teaching me two things above all others: how to speak like we'd never been poor, and how to be nobody. *Be the furniture, Edmund,* she'd say. *Furniture lives longest.*

And I knew—one fact, kept in a drawer so deep I sometimes went years without opening it—that once, drunk on gin after my father's funeral, she had wept and said a name over and over. *Nellie. Nellie, Nellie.* And when I'd asked, she'd said it was nobody, a girl from home, dead these years, God rest her, and she'd never said it again, and I had been fifteen and eager to be lied to, and I had let it lie.

Eleven years. Eleven years of being the furniture, and history happening to other people, and reading other people's letters because my own life had nothing written in it—and now the past had walked into a shipyard wearing my mother's face and asked me *who's asking* in my mother's voice, and I had said *a friend in Clifton* and touched my cap like the furniture I'd trained myself to be.

I was furious. At her, mostly. At myself. The fury had nowhere to go, so it went into my legs, and I walked the length of the quay twice, and then I did the thing I am least proud of and most honest about: I went back.

* * *

I told myself it was professional caution. A parcel delivered to a name connected—however impossibly—to my mother's drunken grief, in a household Adeline Voss corresponded with, on a day when inspectors were already sniffing around irregular census entries. Any reasonable man would verify. I was being reasonable. I have always been excellent at this particular trick: dressing curiosity as duty, hunger as prudence.

The truth is simpler. I went back because she had my mother's face and I could not breathe right until I saw it again.

She was alone at the trestle table now, the older women gone off somewhere, eating bread and dripping from a twist of paper, reading—a book, propped against a coil of rope, holding the page open with her elbow while she ate. Reading. Of course she was reading. My mother had read everything she could steal, and hidden it inside the covers of prayer books, and here was the habit, surviving, in a yard in Bristol.

She heard my boots on the cobbles and didn't startle. That too was familiar—my mother had never startled at anything; she said startling was for people whose lives were quiet.

"You again," she said, not looking up. "Did I shortchange your friend?"

"No. The parcel was correct."

"Then?" Now she looked up, and the irony in her eyes was fully operational, and God help me, it was my own expression looking back at me from another person's skull. "Because men don't walk back across town for the pleasure of my company, and if yours does, tell him he can save his boots."

"I wanted to ask—" I had prepared a question. I had prepared several. They were all gone. What came out instead was: "Where did you learn to hold a needle like that?"

She frowned. "Like what?"

"Squaring it. Laying it down square before you look up."

For a moment she just studied me, chewing, and I braced for her to tell me to clear off. Instead she said, slowly, "Me mam taught me. Well—" and something flickered, quick as a fish turning, "—not me mam. A woman who raised me. Sister Agnes said it was me mother's habit, taught to her by her mother, passed down the line like a curse." She shrugged. "Why?"

"It's a habit I know," I said. "From Cork."

The bread stopped halfway to her mouth.

We looked at each other across the trestle table with the nets hanging between us, and the yard noises went on—the winches, the gulls, a man swearing at a crate—and none of it touched us. We were in a different weather altogether.

"My name's Byrne," she said carefully. "Nell Byrne. But Sister Agnes always said it wasn't my first one. Said the woman who left me at the convent gate in Cork had a different name for me, wrote it on a card, and the card was lost in a flood the winter of '89." Her chin came up, and there was fight in it now, old fight, the kind you grow around a wound. "So if you're one of them genealogy men, or a journalist, or worse, I'll thank you to say your business plain, because I've had fellows come round before with soft voices and questions, and every one of them wanted something."

"I'm not a journalist."

"What are you, then?"

And I opened my mouth to say *a driver*, which is true, and safe, and furniture—and then closed it, because she was waiting, and because for the first time in eleven years the lie stuck in my throat like a fishbone.

"I don't rightly know," I said.

That surprised her. It surprised me more.

She put the bread down. Squared it on the paper, edge to edge, precise as a sacrament. Then she looked at me with my mother's eyes and said:

"You've got the look of a man I've seen before. Can't place it. But there's a chapel on St. Augustine's Back does evening service, and I sit outside it Thursdays when the weather's kind, because the acoustics carry into the street and it costs nothing." She picked her book back up, ending the audience. "If you find out what you are, you could come tell me. Thursday. I'll be the one mending nets."

I walked away along the quay with the gulls screaming and the tide going out, and I thought: *Thursday.* Four days. Four days to decide whether to be a man who delivers parcels, or a man who opens drawers.

And underneath the thought, quieter, colder, arriving like water under a door: Adeline Voss had said *tell her a friend remembers Cork*. Adeline Voss, who misses nothing, had sent me—the one man in her household born in Cork—to deliver a parcel to a girl named Byrne.

Adeline Voss knew something.

I climbed the hill back toward Clifton with the parcel's weight gone from my arms and settled permanently into my chest, and by the time I reached the top I had stopped pretending I didn't know what I had to do next.

I was going to search my mother's papers. Every drawer. Everything she buried.

God help whatever I found.
