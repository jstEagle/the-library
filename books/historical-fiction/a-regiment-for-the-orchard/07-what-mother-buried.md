## 7. What Mother Buried

The tin box lived at the bottom of Edmund's wardrobe under a folded army blanket and a pair of boots he no longer wore because they reminded him of a man he was pretending not to be.

He had carried it through four addresses in eleven years. It had survived two landladies' inspections, one flood, and the long grey winter of 1907 when he'd considered burning everything twice—once out of grief and once out of fear. He had never once opened it sober in daylight. Tonight, with Nell Byrne's face printed on the inside of his eyelids like a photograph left too long in the sun, he hauled it into the lamplight and set it on the floor of his room above the mews with the deliberation of a man opening a grave.

The lock had rusted years ago. A flat screwdriver persuaded it.

Inside: a christening gown gone yellow as old tallow. A marriage certificate. A prayer card for a saint whose name had been worn to a smudge by a thumb that must have rubbed it ten thousand times. A bundle of letters tied with green ribbon, addressed in his mother's hand to an address in Cork he did not recognize. And beneath all of it, flattened nearly translucent, a photograph he had never seen before—a woman standing outside a stone cottage with a child on her hip.

His mother. And a child who was not him.

Edmund sat down on the floor because standing had stopped being an option. The child in the photograph had dark hair and his mother's chin and eyes that looked up at the camera with an expression he knew intimately, because he saw it every morning in the shaving glass: the expression of someone waiting to be told what they were worth.

*No,* he thought. *That's mine. That's my look. She can't have it.*

He picked up the bundle of letters. The ribbon untied with a whisper, like something exhaling.

* * *

He was nine years old, and the house smelled of boiled cabbage and beeswax, and his mother was crying again but quietly, the way she did all her crying, folded over the washboard with her sleeves pushed up and her shoulders making small movements like a bird ruffling feathers in rain.

"Mam." He stood in the doorway with his cap in his hands. "Mam, why do you cry on Tuesdays?"

She didn't stop scrubbing. "Because Tuesday is washing day."

"That's not why."

"Edmund." His name in her mouth could be a door closing or a door opening, depending on how she said it. This time it closed. But then she looked at him—at his stubborn jaw, which she always said came from his father, though she never said anything else about his father—and something in her face went soft and terrible. "Some things are washed and never come clean," she said. "That's all you need know."

He had filed it away with the other mysteries: why she crossed herself when the Cork boat came in, though she claimed no religion; why she kept a tin box she never opened; why, once a year on a date she never named, she got drunk alone in her room and sang a song in Irish so low it was barely breath.

He had thought, all his life, that the box contained his father. That was the story he'd built—the absent man, the soldier or the sailor or the scoundrel, the reason his mother's eyes went to the sea. He had constructed whole biographies for this phantom father, and in each one the father was the wound and his mother was the wounded, and Edmund himself was the bandage she applied, the good boy, the only story.

He untied the first letter and read the date and understood, with the slow vertigo of a man stepping off a kerb he didn't see, that he had built his entire childhood on the wrong ghost.

* * *

*15 March 1893*

*Mrs. Byrne,—I enclose the half-crown as agreed and will continue same quarterly while I am able. You will tell me true how she does. I ask nothing else. Do not write to me again at this address; we are moved and it is better so.*

*You understand why.*

*—E.H.*

Edmund read it three times. The initials meant nothing. The address meant nothing. But *she*. *How she does.* Quarterly payments, like rent on a secret.

The next letter was dated eight months later.

*20 November 1893*

*Mrs. Byrne,—Your letter reached me against instruction but I read it entire. A cough is not a small thing in a child that age. Take her to Dr. Flannery on the North Mall and send me the account direct. Do not spare expense. If the quarter's money runs short, say so plainly and more will come.*

*Tell her—*

The sentence stopped there. Not crossed out. Just stopped, the pen lifting from the paper mid-thought, leaving a small ink blot like a full stop after a word that never arrived.

Edmund's hands were shaking. He was twenty-six years old and his hands were shaking over paper that smelled of lavender and mildew, and somewhere below him in the mews a horse stamped and a cart rattled off toward the city, and the ordinary world went on being ordinary, indifferent to the fact that his mother had been sending money to Cork for a daughter. For a *she*. For a child with a cough, in 1893, when Edmund himself had been—

He did the arithmetic twice because the first time the answer refused to be true.

1893. He had been born in 1885. He would have been eight years old, eating bread and dripping in a kitchen in Bristol while his mother cried over wash on Tuesdays, while across the water in Cork another woman's kitchen held another child. A girl. His sister.

*Half*-sister, said some careful clerk in his head. Half. As if the word softened anything.

* * *

There were nineteen letters. He read them all, in order, sitting cross-legged on the floorboards until his legs went numb, and the lamp burned low, and the night outside the window thickened from blue to black. The letters told a story in the way that ledgers tell stories—all the feeling pressed out, all the love converted to sums—but a story nonetheless.

Her name was Ellen. Born February 1891, Cork, father unnamed in any document Edmund could find, though the letters made plain enough who had paid for her keeping: a man called Edward Hartwell, initials E.H., the phantom father at last given flesh, and the flesh was worse than the ghost because the ghost had at least been Edmund's alone.

Edward Hartwell had gotten a child on a woman in Cork, married Edmund's mother within the year, died—fever, 1894—before either woman could confront him with the arithmetic of his life. And Edmund's mother, Brigid Hartwell, née nobody, a Bristol girl with Irish parents and Irish guilt, had taken on the dead man's debt as a penance. Quarterly. In secret. For seventeen years.

Until 1910. When the letters stopped.

The last one was dated January 1910, and it was not in her hand.

*Dear Sir,—Mrs. Hartwell having passed, and no further remittance being looked for, I have placed the girl out to service with a respectable family in Blackrock. She is a good worker and knows her letters. She asks after her mother sometimes. I have told her what I judged proper.*

*I remain, Mrs. Alice Byrne (widow)*

Edmund sat with that for a long time.

*What I judged proper.* Somewhere in Cork a fourteen-year-old girl had asked about her mother and been handed a lie shaped to fit, and somewhere in Bristol a fifteen-year-old boy—he had been fifteen when she died, fifteen and furious at her for dying, fifteen and certain he was the whole of her story—had stood at a graveside and heard the priest say *taken too soon* without knowing the half of what was taken.

He laughed. It came out wrong, a single bark that frightened him in the empty room. Because here was the joke, if you wanted it: he had spent his whole adult life reading other people's mail, feeding himself on other people's secrets, telling himself that history happened to other people—and the biggest secret in his own house had been addressed to a stranger in Cork, and he had carried it unopened for thirteen years. He had been the invisible man standing guard over a letter that was, all along, about him.

Nell.

Ellen. *Nell* was what happened to Ellen when strangers shortened it. He remembered the quay, the parcel sweating under his arm, the girl turning with her mother's chin and his own wary eyes, and the name she'd given the foreman—Byrne, Byrne, the name on every envelope in the tin box, the name of the woman paid to raise his mother's abandoned daughter.

She hadn't been fleeing Ireland. She'd been following the money backward. Following the only thread she had: a name, a city, a ship's manifest, a hope.

And she was saving for a berth. One berth. He had watched her count coins into a tin of her own—everyone keeps a tin, he thought wildly, everyone has a box under the floor of themselves—and he had felt the pull of recognition and called it nothing, called it coincidence, called it none of his business, because *none of my business* was his creed, his uniform, his armor.

* * *

Flashback, unwelcome, insistent:

He is eleven, and his mother has taken him to the docks to watch a ship leave for America. Emigrants crowd the rail—whole families, cardboard cases tied with string, a baby held up to wave at relatives already going grey on the quay. His mother grips his hand too hard.

"Why are we here, Mam?"

"Watching," she says.

"Watching what?"

"Other people going places." Her voice is strange, flat as the water. Then, catching herself, brightening with an effort that hurts to watch: "One day that'll be you, son. America, or Canada, or wherever the world wants you. You'll go where I never went."

"I'll take you," he says, because he is eleven and generous.

Something crosses her face then—something he had no vocabulary for at eleven and only names now, standing in his room among her papers. Grief. Guilt. The specific anguish of a woman looking at the child she kept and thinking of the child she couldn't.

"You're a good boy," she says, and holds his hand harder, and the ship pulls out, and the people on the rail grow small, and she watches them until the water takes them, and he thinks she is dreaming of his future when she is mourning her own decision, made years ago, made in a different harbor, in the opposite direction.

He had thought she was watching other people's history.

She had been watching hers, sailing away without her.

* * *

The lamp guttered. Edmund fed it more oil with hands that had steadied, mostly, and laid the letters out on the floorboards in chronological order, nineteen pale rectangles like a hand of cards dealt by fate with a very poor sense of drama. Nineteen quarters of a year. Four years and three months of half-crowns and coughs and accounts sent direct. Then silence, then death, then a girl put out to service with her questions answered by strangers.

He thought: *I have a trade. I drive a woman who is making history, and I carry her correspondence, and I read it because my own life is a waiting room. And the whole time, my own history was sitting in Cork, growing up hungry, learning her letters from a paid stranger.*

He thought: *She has my mother's eyes. She has my mother's chin. She counted coins on a quay forty miles from the house where those coins used to arrive, and she doesn't know me, and I walked past her.*

Not past her. He hadn't walked past. He had stopped. Had stared. Had felt the vertigo and fled it, delivered the parcel, tipped his hat like a tradesman, and left her standing among the fish crates with her borrowed name and her half-saved fare.

*Only one berth left*, the booking clerk had said, when he'd gone back that evening on a pretext he hadn't let himself examine—to check prices, he'd told himself, purely to check prices. One berth on the morning tide, Friday. After that, nothing for a fortnight.

Friday. Two days.

He gathered the letters, retied them—not with the green ribbon but loosely, so they could be found again, so he would never again be a man guarding a locked box—and put the photograph on top. Mother and child. The child looked at the camera with his eyes, and he let her keep them.

Then he sat by the window with the lamp turned low and did the thing he had spent eleven years training himself never to do: he made a plan that involved himself. Involved his name, his money, his face, his risk. The plan had exactly two lines, and the second line erased the first, and both of them ended at the docks.

Because there was a problem, and the problem wore an inspector's coat. Calloway had been at the Voss house twice now, smiling his careful smile, asking his careful questions, and today there had been a plainclothes man loitering by the tobacco shop on Queen Street, and tomorrow—Edmund had heard Adeline say it to Ruth over supper, low, urgent—tomorrow there would be watchers on the quay itself. The net wasn't tightening. It had tightened. Everything that moved toward that harbor now moved inside it.

And somewhere in that net, a girl with his mother's face was counting coins toward a berth that sailed Friday morning, believing herself alone in the world, believing her mother had simply died and left nothing, believing the worst lie of all—that no one was coming for her.

Edmund stood. He took his coat from the hook, and the tin box went back under the blanket, but lighter now, emptied of its power to ambush him. What remained was simple and terrible and his: a name he might have to give away, a berth he might have to surrender, a truth he owed a girl in Cork whether or not the police were watching the gates.

He blew out the lamp. In the dark he said it aloud, testing the weight of it, the way you test ice before you trust it:

"Nell. My name is Edmund, and your mother was my mother, and I'm sorry, and I'm coming."

Outside, the mews slept on. Inside, a man who had spent his whole life as a passenger in other people's stories took his hat off the hook, and behind his ribs, quiet as a fuse, the morning tide began to pull.

But between him and the quay lay the Voss house, and the Voss house lay under the eye of Inspector Calloway—and Calloway had sent word that afternoon, polite as poison, that he would like a further word with Mr. Hartwell. Tomorrow. Ten o'clock. About irregularities.

Edmund pulled the door shut behind him and stood a moment in the cold, doing the new arithmetic. One berth. Two days. One inspector. And a letter in his coat pocket, still warm from Adeline's desk, that he had read twice on the stairs and knew could hang her.

First, the inspector. First, survive the morning.

Then the tide.
