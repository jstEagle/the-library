## 8. The Price of Silence

The summons came by a boy in a cap who didn't wait for an answer, which told Edmund everything before the note did. *Inspector Calloway requests Mr. Hartwell's attendance at the Bridewell station, Tuesday, eleven o'clock. A matter of some administrative tidiness.* Administrative tidiness. The phrase had the smell of a trap that had been oiled and set with care.

He read it three times in the stable, standing among the smell of harness oil and straw, while the mare watched him with her patient dark eye. Then he folded it into quarters and put it in his breast pocket, where it lay against his heart like a small cold coin.

*Requests.* Not demands. That was worse. Calloway was a man who understood that a man summoned is a man on guard, but a man invited—invited he must decide to come, and the deciding itself is a confession. You could refuse an inspector's request. Only a guilty man would.

Edmund fed the mare an apple from Adeline's orchard—the small hard apples, more promise than fruit—and thought about how many days it had been since Prowse's pen had scratched across the ledger and made him visible. Nine days. He had counted. He had never counted days before; counting them was what men did when they expected something to arrive.

Adeline was at her desk when he went up through the back stair, dictating a letter to Ruth in her quick clipped voice, and she looked up at him with those level grey eyes and said nothing about the note because he hadn't shown it to her. He had decided not to show it to her. He told himself this was protection. He was beginning to suspect it was also hoarding—the same instinct that made him read other people's letters. He kept things. He kept secrets the way misers kept coins, not to spend them but simply to have, to be a man of property in at least one currency.

"I'll be out until two," he said. "The Vauxhall errand."

"Take the long way," Adeline said, returning to her page. "The roads are crawling with constables since the census business. Apparently we're all smugglers now." She smiled as she said it, the smile of a woman who had been called dangerous so often it had become a kind of joke between her and history itself. "Mind you don't get arrested, Hartwell. I'd hate to lose a good driver."

*You'd hate it,* he thought, *or you'd write a pamphlet about it?* She would do both. She would mourn him and use him in the same breath, and she would be right to, and he would forgive her for it, because that was what the people around Adeline Voss did: they forgave her for making their lives into material.

Outside, Bristol was doing its grey Tuesday best. Gulls over the Frome. A drizzle fine as sifted flour. He drove the Daimler slowly along the Vauxhall road without any errand in mind, letting the engine idle low, and at twenty minutes to eleven he turned down toward the Bridewell because there was nowhere else in the world for him to go.

That was the thing about being seen, he was learning. Once a man has looked at you properly, every street becomes a corridor leading to his door.

* * *

Calloway received him in a room that wasn't an interrogation room, which was its own kind of message. It was an office—a real one, with a window onto the yard, a filing cabinet, a framed photograph of the Chief Constable looking like a man photographed against his will. Two chairs. A pot of tea, already poured, steam rising. Calloway sat behind the desk in shirtsleeves, waistcoat unbuttoned, reading glasses pushed up into his thinning hair, and he looked up with the mild pleasure of a country vicar greeting a parishioner.

"Hartwell. Sit down, sit down. Tea?"

"Thank you."

"Milk?"

"Just as it comes."

Everyone in that office understood that no one cared how anyone took their tea. Calloway poured anyway, unhurried, and slid the cup across, and Edmund held it in both hands because his hands wanted employment.

"You've driven for Mrs. Voss," Calloway said, "how long now?"

"Three years."

"Three years." He said it as though tasting it. "And before that?"

"Various positions. Livery work, mostly. Bath. Before that—" Edmund let the sentence die naturally, the way a man does whose early life is too dull to recount.

"Bath." Calloway opened a folder—not a thick folder, a thin one, which frightened Edmund more than a thick one would have. Thick folders meant suspicion. Thin folders meant certainty. "Bristol born and bred, according to the census."

"That's right."

"Prowse is thorough. Writes a good hand, too. Very good hand." Calloway removed his glasses and polished them on his tie, an operation of tremendous delicacy, as though the glasses were evidence and he was destroying it. "Funny thing about Prowse. Funny thing I've noticed. He's got entries in his ledgers for houses nobody lives in. Names for families that don't exist. And he's missing entries altogether for certain addresses I could mention. Clifton, mostly. Addresses with money behind the doors." He looked up. "Doesn't that strike you as odd, Hartwell?"

"It sounds like slovenly bookkeeping."

"It sounds like commerce." Calloway smiled. He had a good smile, warm, used often, worn like a coat that fit. "But that's neither here nor there. I didn't ask you here about Prowse."

Edmund drank his tea. It was scalding and he drank it anyway, because the pain gave his face something honest to do.

"The Voss household," Calloway said. "I'll come at it plainly, because I respect plainness and I think you're a plain man, Hartwell, underneath the livery. There's a woman named Ruth Aldous living at that address under circumstances the law takes an interest in. There are letters passing through that house that I would very much like to read. There is money moving in directions money shouldn't move. And there is you—you, who were born in Bristol according to one document and somewhere else entirely according to the way you flinched when I asked." He folded his hands. "Now. Here's what I can do."

Here it comes, Edmund thought, and found himself strangely calm, the way he'd been calm once watching a horse break its leg in the ring at Bath—horror arriving too large for feeling, leaving only observation.

"Immunity," Calloway said. "Full immunity. Whatever your irregularities—and I'm not saying they're criminal, I'm saying they exist—whatever they are, they go into a drawer and stay there. Your name becomes yours again, clean paper, clean slate. In exchange: testimony. What letters you've carried. Where they went. Who signed for them. Whether Mrs. Voss knew the contents." He leaned back. "You've been invisible for years, son. I'm offering to make you a citizen again. All it costs is telling the truth—which, I'm given to understand from your census form, is something you've always struggled to do."

The silence stretched. Somewhere below in the yard a constable laughed at something, the laugh bouncing off stone.

"And if I refuse?"

Calloway spread his hands, regretful, generous, terrible. "Then I look closer. That's all. I don't threaten you, Hartwell—that's not my method. I just keep looking. Looking is free." He stood, indicating the audience was ending, and held out his hand, and Edmund shook it because refusing to shake it was information too. "Think about it. No hurry. A week. But do think about what a man is, when everything he calls his own belongs to a lie."

In the doorway Edmund stopped. He didn't turn around.

"Inspector. If I testified—would it save Mrs. Voss?"

"No," Calloway said pleasantly. "It would save you. That's rather the point."

* * *

He drove for an hour afterward without destination, up through Clifton where the houses stared down their noses at the gorge, out along the Downs where the wind came off the water flat and smelling of salt and weed. His hands were steady on the wheel. That surprised him. Inside, something was moving around like a animal in a burrow, turning and turning.

*It would save you. That's rather the point.*

The trouble was—and he admitted this to himself honestly, driving into the wind—the offer was good. It was better than good; it was the first honest transaction anyone had ever offered him. Tell the truth, walk free. His whole life he had traded in falsehoods and gotten nothing but invisibility for his pains, and here was a policeman offering to buy his lies at full price, cash on delivery.

And the price was Adeline.

Not even Adeline, quite. Adeline could survive Calloway; she'd survive worse; she had arranged her life like a fortress with herself as the garrison and the martyrdom clause already drafted. No—the price was the orchard. The price was the way she'd stood among the crooked apple trees talking about her sister. The price was Nell, whose existence Calloway didn't yet know about, and wouldn't, unless Edmund handed him the thread and let him pull.

Because that was the thing about testimony. You never testify to only what they ask. They ask about letters and you give them a household, they ask about a household and you give them a life. He had read enough correspondence to know how stories worked: one fact pulls another, and another, until the whole garment comes apart in your hands and everyone standing near you is suddenly dressed in nothing.

By four o'clock he was back at the house. Adeline had gone to a committee meeting in Redland; Ruth was upstairs, behind her door, doing whatever fugitives did with their afternoons. The hall clock ticked. The post had come at two, and there on the hall table lay the day's letters, waiting, and Edmund stood over them the way a drunkard stands outside a pub with his wages in his fist.

There were four. Three were ordinary—one from the WSPU office, one a bill from the coal merchant, one with handwriting like spiderwork that meant nothing to him. The fourth was heavy cream stock, addressed in a small precise hand, and the return corner bore a name Edmund had seen before, in March, in a letter he should never have read: the name of a man who organized the movement's money, and who, per that March letter, had been advised to leave the country by a route that passed through certain sympathetic households.

Households. Plural. This letter was going to name one.

He took it upstairs to the kitchen, and he told himself he would only check the address, only confirm it was routine, only—

He slit it open with the bone-handled knife from the dresser drawer, the knife Adeline used for apples.

*Dear Miss Voss,—Forgive the brevity; I write from a place where brevity is wisdom. The arrangements stand as discussed. I shall come north through Bristol on Thursday week and would ask that your man drive me to the station at dawn, no luggage, nothing marked. I am told your household remains discreet, which in these times is the rarest virtue. Burn this.*

*I remain, faithfully, J.*

Burn this. Everyone wrote burn this. Nobody burned anything; burning was a courtesy, a fiction of discretion, like milk and sugar. The letters accumulated in drawers and hatboxes and the false bottoms of writing desks, whole archives of burn this, and any one of them was enough to hang the household twice over.

Edmund sat at the kitchen table with the letter in front of him and the teakettle ticking on the hob, and he understood—with the clarity that arrives only after a decision has already been made somewhere deeper than the mind—that this letter was different. This one was live. This one named a day. Thursday week. Dawn. No luggage. Your man.

Your man. Himself.

If Calloway kept looking—if he looked at the mails, at the station porters, at the dawn trains—he would find this letter or its consequences, and this letter would find Adeline, and Adeline would give up Ruth before breakfast to protect the network, because that was arithmetic too, and Edmund had learned arithmetic recently and hated the subject.

Or the letter could stop existing.

He thought about immunity then. Clean paper. His own name, his own life, handed back across a desk with a handshake. All he had ever wanted, priced exactly at the cost of everyone else. And he heard his own voice in Calloway's office saying *just as it comes*, polite, evasive, nothing—and realized that he had been stalling since eleven o'clock. Stalling the way he stalled at doors, at questions, at mirrors. A lifetime of stalling. And the stall was itself an answer; Calloway had known it; probably Calloway had known it before Edmund walked in, and the week's grace was bait, and men who took a week to decide what they already knew always decided wrong.

The kettle began to sing.

Edmund rose, crossed to the range, and held the letter over the flame. For a moment he hesitated—the paper trembling in the heat like a living thing, J.'s small precise hand curling black at the edges—and some part of him screamed that this was destruction of evidence, that this was the act of a guilty man, that Calloway would know, Calloway always knew—

He dropped it into the grate and watched it go.

It burned fast, cream stock taking the fire greedily, the words going to a brightness and then a black lace of ash that collapsed inward when he stirred it with the poker. *Thursday week. Dawn. No luggage.* Gone. The first thing in his life he had ever destroyed instead of kept, and his hands shook afterward, badly, and he sat back down at the table and put them flat on the wood until they steadied.

The kettle sang on. He took it off the heat and made tea he didn't drink.

So. Now he knew what he was. Not an invisible man. Not a reader of other people's histories. An actor. He had acted, finally, irreversibly, and the strange thing—the thing he would carry to whatever end was coming—was how light it felt. How much lighter than lying. Ash didn't need maintaining. Ash asked nothing of him except that he never speak of it.

Which meant, he understood, sitting alone in the warm kitchen as the light went blue at the window, that he was now keeping a secret from Calloway worth more to the inspector than all his census fictions combined. The noose hadn't loosened. It had changed rope—moved from his neck to the household's, and his own hands had tied the knot tighter by cutting one strand loose.

Upstairs, a floorboard creaked. Ruth, moving behind her door. Or Adeline home early. He couldn't tell, and for the first time in three years of listening at keyholes of the mail, not knowing frightened him.

Thursday week, said the ash in the grate. Dawn.

He had eight days to learn how to be a man who existed.

Out in the orchard, wind moved through the crooked trees, and somewhere beyond them, past the city and the water, a girl with his mother's eyes was saving coins toward a berth on a ship he hadn't yet begun to think about—and would think of little else, soon, though he didn't know it yet.
