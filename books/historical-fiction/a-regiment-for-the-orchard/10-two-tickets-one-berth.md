## 10. Two Tickets, One Berth

The shipping office on Prince Street smelled of tar and tea, and the clerk behind the grille had the face of a man who had spent thirty years saying no.

"White Star, third class," I said. "Bristol to New York. Two berths."

He licked his pencil without looking up. "For the *Majestic*?"

"The next sailing."

"That'd be the *Cymric*, Thursday tide." He turned a ledger toward himself and ran a finger down a column of figures the way a priest runs a finger down a list of sins. "Third class, two berths, that's seven pounds ten for the pair. Names?"

I opened my mouth and found nothing in it.

It is a strange thing, to have spent years being nobody and then be asked twice in one breath who you are. I had papers that said Hartwell. I had a voice that said Hartwell. I had, by now, almost a life that said Hartwell — Adeline Voss's driver, her man of errands, invisible as a lamppost. But the name I wanted written on that ticket was not mine, and the name beside it belonged to a girl I had known for six days.

"Hartwell," I said. "Edmund Hartwell. And—" The pause lasted half a second too long. The clerk's pencil hovered. "Byrne. Nell Byrne."

"Relation?"

"My sister."

The word went out of me like something escaping a cage I hadn't known I'd built. The clerk wrote it down without ceremony, which was somehow worse than if he'd looked up and stared. To him it was nothing. A sister. A line in a ledger. He could not know that I had learned the word again only last week, standing on a quay with my mother's eyes looking back at me out of a stranger's face.

"Seven pounds ten," he said.

I counted it out in sovereigns and half-sovereigns, coins I had been hoarding against a rainy day I could never quite imagine. It turned out the rain had been waiting all along; it had simply been waiting for someone else to fall on.

"Thursday morning tide," he said, sliding the tickets under the grille. "Be at the quay by seven. After that she sails whether you're aboard or not."

I took the tickets. They were flimsy things, cheap card, and they trembled slightly in my hand because my hand was trembling and not because the paper had any opinions of its own.

Two berths. I walked back up Prince Street holding them like a man carrying two live coals, and it did not occur to me until I reached the corner — until I saw, across the road, a bowler hat I recognized and a coat cut a little too well for a man pretending to loiter — that I had never once asked myself which of us would fill them.

* * *

Inspector Calloway was leaning against the rail at the head of the dock stairs, watching the water with the patient interest of a fisherman who has already baited his hook.

He didn't turn when I approached, which meant he'd heard me coming, which meant he'd heard everything. There were perhaps forty people between the shipping office and this spot, and I would have sworn I'd threaded them cleanly. I would have sworn wrong.

"Mr. Hartwell," he said pleasantly. "Buying a ticket, were you? Lovely day for travel."

"I'm often sent on errands."

"For Miss Voss." He said the name the way a man lays a card face-down on a table he intends to win anyway. "She sends her driver to book American passages. Curious household, hers. Curiouser every week." Now he turned, and his eyes were mild and grey and missed nothing, the eyes of an accountant auditing your soul. "Who's Byrne, then?"

"A family name. Old friends of the household."

"Mm." He produced a notebook — a small black one, different from Prowse's ledger but somehow its cousin — and made a show of consulting it though I'd have bet my last shilling there was nothing in it yet about Nell. "You'll find this amusing, Mr. Hartwell. Or perhaps you won't. Since census night I've taken a certain interest in irregularities. Households where the ink doesn't sit right. Birthplaces that wander about like stray dogs." He snapped the notebook shut. "Yours wandered from Kilkenny to Somerset between one question and the next, if I recall Mr. Prowse's careful hand."

"Prowse makes mistakes."

"Prowse makes notes." A barge went past below us, low in the water, stacked with crates stamped with names of firms that existed mostly on paper. Calloway watched it go with what looked like genuine pleasure. "You know what I like about the docks, Mr. Hartwell? Everything leaves from here eventually. People, letters, money, lies. All of it funnels through these few hundred yards of mud and rope, and a man who stands still long enough sees the whole world pass in front of him and can count it." He tipped his hat brim toward me. "I've begun counting."

"And what have you counted so far?"

"One berth short," he said, "on the Thursday *Cymric* — though I expect that will correct itself. One fugitive organizer unaccounted for within a mile of this spot. One household of suffragists with more comings and goings than a railway station. And one driver whose mother tongue slips out when he's tired — you said *grand* yesterday, to the greengrocer, thinking no one listened. Nobody ever thinks anyone listens. That's the whole art of my work, Mr. Hartwell: everybody believes themselves invisible."

The word landed in me like a needle finding the exact bruise.

"You're mistaken about me," I said, and heard how smoothly it came, how polished, and hated the polish, hated the smoothness, hated the man I had built out of other people's names.

"No doubt," said Calloway cheerfully. "I'm often mistaken about people. That's why I keep notebooks instead of trusting my heart." He pushed off the rail and straightened his coat. "Enjoy the sunshine. It won't last — weather or otherwise. And give Miss Voss my regards. Tell her I do hope everyone in her house is quite, quite regular."

He strolled off toward the Custom House whistling something tuneless, and I stood at the head of the stairs with two tickets in my pocket and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that the net had not been closing around the Voss house at all.

It had been closing around the quay.

* * *

Nell was waiting where I'd told her, on the bench behind the salt warehouse, the one sheltered from the wind that came up the Avon smelling of sulphur and herring. She had a way of sitting that reminded me of photographs of our mother — spine straight, hands folded, chin lifted as if daring the world to make itself worth her attention. Six days of knowing her, and already I caught myself doing arithmetic with her gestures, subtracting the years, adding the hunger, trying to make the sums come out to a childhood I might have prevented if I had known she existed.

"Well?" she said. "Have we got them?"

"We've got them." I sat down beside her and gave her both tickets before I could think better of it. "Thursday tide. Seven o'clock."

She read them slowly, tracing the printed letters with one cracked fingernail, the way she must once have traced letters in a Cork schoolroom before the schoolroom and everything else was taken away. "*Edmund Hartwell and Nell Byrne.*" She looked up. "Sister. You told them sister."

"It's what you are."

"It's what I am," she agreed, "and it's a word I got on without for twenty-two years, so forgive me if I hold it a bit carefully." She handed one ticket back to me. Mine. She held hers against her chest the way children hold bread. "New York," she said, tasting it. "Ellis Island. They say they change your name there if they can't spell it. I don't mind. I've had a name given by strangers before. Byrne itself was given by strangers, did I tell you? The nuns couldn't spell whatever my mother left with me, so they chose the nearest thing." She smiled, and it was our mother's smile exactly, and I had to look away at the water.

"There's a complication," I said.

"There's always a complication. You're the sort of man complications follow. I knew it the first minute I saw you — you walk like a man carrying something breakable."

"Calloway was at the docks."

Her hands went still on the ticket. "The inspector."

"He knows about the passage. He may not know about you specifically, but he counts things, and he's begun counting me." I told her the rest — the notebook, the *grand*, the berth short on the manifest, the way he'd said *one berth short* as though reading it aloud from a page he'd already written. When I finished, Nell was quiet for a long moment, watching a gull hang motionless over the masts.

"So he'll watch the gangway," she said at last. "Thursday morning. He'll stand there with his little book and look at every Irish face going up the plank, and he'll see mine, and he'll ask who I am, and I'll say Nell Byrne, traveling with my brother, and he'll say *which brother*, Mr. Byrne, and there you'll be." She turned to me. "Unless there isn't a Mr. Byrne. Unless there's only a girl traveling alone under a clean enough name, going to a cousin in New York, sad and ordinary and not worth a second glance."

"Nell—"

"How much is the ticket alone?"

"Don't."

"How much?"

"Three pounds fifteen," I said, "and I've paid for both, so it's settled, so we needn't discuss—"

"Settled." She laughed, and there was no humor in it at all, only a hard bright edge. "You've known me six days, Edmund. Six days, and you're settling things over my head like I'm a parcel. I spent my whole life being a parcel — passed from a workhouse to a laundry to a family that wanted a scullery maid and called it charity. I will not be settled. I will be asked."

I looked at her, and the truth was a stone in my throat, and I swallowed around it and failed. Because here was the arithmetic I had been refusing to do since Prince Street: two tickets, one berth remaining after Calloway's men began their counting — no. That wasn't even true yet. The ship had berths enough. What remained single was the safe way out. One clean passage, one forged or borrowed identity, one face that could pass the gangway unremarked — and two of us. Every plan I turned over in my head came apart the same way, like wet rope, and always the same strand held fast while the other slipped free, and I could not yet bear to look at which strand was which.

"If anything happened to you—" I started.

"Nothing will happen to me that hasn't already happened twice," Nell said. "Hunger, I've done. Strangers' names, I've done. What I haven't done is be somebody's sister for longer than a week, and I find I've developed a taste for it, and I'll thank you not to end it early out of some notion of nobility." She stood, folding her ticket away inside her glove. "Thursday, then. Seven o'clock. Both of us. You can argue with me again tomorrow if it keeps you warm."

She went off along the warehouse wall, small and upright, and I watched her until the corner took her, and then I sat alone on the bench doing sums.

One berth. Two of us. And somewhere behind me, in a house with an orchard going wild, a fugitive woman sleeping in an attic whose existence I was now complicit in, and an inspector with a black notebook growing fatter by the day. I tried to imagine a version of Thursday in which everyone got out — Nell to New York, Ruth to anywhere, Adeline above suspicion, me back behind the wheel of the car, invisible again, history happening to other people.

The sums wouldn't come right. They wouldn't come right in any arrangement I tried, and I understood finally that they weren't going to, because the problem was not numbers. The problem was that I had spent thirty-one years believing I could pass through the world without leaving a mark, and the world had begun, gently, bureaucratically, with pencils and ledgers and polite grey eyes, to invoice me for all those unmarked years at once.

I went home the long way, past the orchard wall. The apple trees stood black against the evening, heavy with fruit nobody harvested, a whole regiment standing at attention behind rusted iron, waiting for orders.

Inside the house, a lamp burned in the upstairs room that was supposed to be empty.

And below it, at the gate, a police whistle sounded — short, sharp, twice — and a lantern swung up the lane toward the orchard door.
