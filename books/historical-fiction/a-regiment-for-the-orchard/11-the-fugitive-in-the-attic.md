## 11. The Fugitive in the Attic

Ruth had learned to count her days in floorboards.

The attic above the Voss house had a loose plank near the north window, and when she pressed her ear to it she could hear the kitchen below—Adeline's voice rising and falling through the arguments she rehearsed for no one, the clatter of the kettle, once, memorably, Edmund Hartwell laughing at something. She had been up here nineteen days. She knew this because she scratched a small mark into the beam each morning with a hairpin, and because time in an attic behaves differently than time anywhere else. Time in an attic is a held breath.

Her name was Ruth Ellery. It had been Ruth Ellery in Manchester too, before the warrant, which was either a great stupidity or a point of pride—she hadn't decided which. The organizers had begged her to take another name, and she had refused, and Adeline Voss had looked at her across the tea table with something like respect and something like pity and said, *Then we'll keep you as you are, and God help anyone who comes asking.* Ruth thought about that sentence every day. It was the first time in her life anyone had risked anything on her behalf, and it sat in her chest like a coal that wouldn't cool.

She heard the motorcar before she saw it—the particular heavy idle of a police vehicle, unmistakable now to her ear the way a mother knows her own child's cough. She went to the dormer window on her knees and lifted the curtain one inch.

Two constables were hammering on the door of the Hollis house, two doors down. Then more of them, spilling out of a second car like ants from a kicked nest. A third car idled at the corner, and beside it stood a tall man in a grey overcoat who did not move at all, who simply watched the street with his hands clasped behind him, and Ruth understood without being told: that was Calloway. That was the man whose name moved through the movement's safe houses like weather.

They were working down the street. House by house.

Ruth did not scream, though every nerve in her wanted to. Screaming was a luxury. She pulled on her boots, took the canvas satchel she kept packed beneath the eaves—papers, a change of clothes, forty-one shillings—and went to the trapdoor. And there she stopped, because the plan, such as it was, ended at the trapdoor. Down through the linen room, along the back corridor, into the orchard, over the wall into the tanner's yard. Adeline had walked her through it twice. But the plan assumed warning. The plan assumed someone downstairs would have the wit to stall men with warrants while a woman came down through their ceiling like a ghost.

She could hear voices below now. Not the police—not yet—but Adeline's, sharp, calling for Edmund.

*Please*, Ruth thought, pressing her palm flat against the cold planks, *please be worth the coal.*

* * *

Edmund was polishing the windscreen when the cars came.

He counted them the way he counted everything now—with the detached arithmetic of a man who had been doing sums about his own ruin for weeks. Three vehicles. Eight men visible, maybe ten. They started at the Hollis house, and he had perhaps four minutes, maybe six, before they reached the gate of number nine.

"Adeline." He said it quietly, then again, louder, and she appeared in the doorway with ink on her fingers and the particular stillness of a woman who has been waiting for this knock for years. "They're coming. All of them. Working up the street."

She didn't ask how he knew. She was already moving—up the stairs two at a time, and Edmund followed because following Adeline Voss had become, somewhere in the last months, simply what his body did.

In the attic, Ruth Ellery stood by the window with her satchel already on her shoulder, and Edmund had exactly enough time to register three things: that she was smaller than he'd imagined; that her hands were steady; and that she looked at him not as a driver, or furniture, or a servant, but as a comrade—which was so foreign an experience that it nearly buckled him.

"The orchard," Adeline said. "Not the lane—they'll have a man on the lane. Through the trees, over the wall, down to the tanner's yard, and then God go with you."

"They'll search the garden," Ruth said. "If I'm caught crossing open ground—"

"You won't be," Edmund said.

Both women turned to look at him, and he heard his own voice as if from outside himself, calm and certain, belonging to some braver version of Edmund Hartwell who had apparently been waiting all this time for permission to exist.

"I'll walk the lane," he said. "Front of the house. When they arrive, I'm the driver, I'm the staff, I'm boring. I'll keep them looking at me. You cross behind the apple trees where the shadow falls after four o'clock. Go now."

Adeline gripped his arm—briefly, hard, her fingers leaving marks he would feel for days—and said only, "Edmund." Just his name, the way you'd say a word you'd only just learned meant something.

Then Ruth was down through the trapdoor and gone, quick as water, and Adeline went with her as far as the linen room, and Edmund straightened his collar, picked up his chamois cloth, and walked out the front door to meet the law.

* * *

Inspector Calloway climbed out of the third car at a pace that suggested he had all the patience in the world and intended to use every second of it.

"Mr. Hartwell," he said pleasantly.

"Inspector."

"Busy day for us. Warrants for several properties on this street. Irregularities, you understand. Census matters, mostly. Though one does find all sorts of things when one starts opening cupboards." He smiled with genuine warmth, which was the worst thing about him. "Is Mrs. Voss at home?"

"She is."

"And the household is quite alone otherwise? No lodgers? No guests?"

"Just myself and the cook, and she's gone to her sister's in Clifton until Friday."

"Mm." Calloway produced a paper from his coat and unfolded it with the ceremony of a man unwrapping a gift. "Your entry, Mr. Hartwell. Do you know what I admire about the census? Its honesty. Every soul in England, asked the same questions, on the same night, under penalty of law. Birthplace, for instance. Yours says Bristol." He glanced up. "But the 1901 return for a household in Bath lists an Edmund Hartwell born in—where was it—Cork. Ireland. Same name, same age within a year. Curious thing, coincidence."

The ground did not drop away this time. Edmund noticed that. Somewhere in the last weeks he had grown a floor.

"Census clerks make errors," he said. "So do census takers. I believe there's a Mr. Prowse working this district whom several households might describe as thorough to the point of invention."

Something flickered behind Calloway's eyes—annoyance, quickly banked. "I'll want to speak with Mrs. Voss."

"She's in her study. Correspondence. I expect she'll receive you when she's ready."

"I expect," Calloway said softly, "she will."

And then, because God or the devil had a sense of theatre, one of the constables came around the side of the house at a jog, and Edmund felt his heart stop against his ribs like a bird hitting glass.

"Sir—" the constable began, and then the back door opened and Adeline Voss stepped out onto the garden path with a pair of pruning shears in one hand and an expression of mild irritation that deserved its own portrait in the National Gallery.

"Is there a reason your men are trampling my rhubarb?" she demanded. "Because if there is, I'd like it stated plainly, and if there isn't, I'd like them off my property before I write to the Chief Constable, whose wife, incidentally, is a subscriber."

Calloway turned toward her with the smooth pivot of a man who enjoyed this part most of all, and Edmund understood—he saw it whole, in a single frozen second—that the constable had been about to report movement in the orchard. A figure among the trees. A woman climbing a wall. And that Adeline, whether she knew it or not, whether by design or by instinct, had just stepped directly between the law and the fleeing shape of Ruth Ellery.

He did the only useful thing left to him.

"Inspector," Edmund said loudly, "if you're here about the census, you should know Mrs. Voss keeps meticulous records of everyone who's stayed in this house. Boarders, correspondents, the lot. She'll want her solicitor present before any names are discussed—she's very firm on procedure—but I can fetch the guest book itself, if that would speed things along. It's in the hall."

It was a lie wrapped in a truth wrapped in a lie, and Calloway weighed it, visibly, against the fading light and the long street still left to search and the fact that a woman with pruning shears was making him feel, absurdly, like the intruder he legally was not.

"Fetch it," he said.

Edmund walked into the house without hurrying, because hurrying would have killed him, and through the hall window, as he lifted the leather-bound book from its shelf, he saw—in the last slant of four o'clock light—a figure drop from the orchard wall into the tanner's yard, roll once, and vanish.

He carried the empty guest book out into the gathering dusk and handed it to the Inspector of Police like a man delivering nothing at all.

* * *

They searched the house anyway. Of course they did. Two constables through the rooms while Adeline sat perfectly straight in her study chair and answered questions with questions, and Calloway went through the guest book page by page looking for a name that wasn't in it because Edmund had spent the previous hour tearing out the relevant leaf and feeding it to the kitchen range.

Ruth was gone. Gone over the wall, gone down through the tanner's yard, gone—to the next safe house, to the station, to the ends of the earth, Edmund didn't know and couldn't ask. She was a hole in the world shaped like a person, and the house felt the absence of her the way a mouth feels a missing tooth.

At half past seven the cars finally pulled away, and Calloway paused at his own door and looked back at Edmund standing on the step.

"You're better at this than you let on, Mr. Hartwell," he said. "That worries me more than if you were bad at it."

Then he was gone, and the street was quiet, and Edmund stood in the dark listening to his own heartbeat slow down like an engine cooling.

* * *

Adeline found him in the orchard.

He didn't know why he'd gone there. Perhaps because it was the route she'd taken, the path of her escape, and walking it made the escape real—made it something that had happened rather than something that had almost gone wrong forever. He stood among the apple trees in the dark with his hands in his pockets, and when Adeline's hand touched his elbow he didn't startle, because some part of him had been listening for her the whole time.

"You tore out the page," she said.

"Yes."

"You lied to an inspector of police. Twice. To his face."

"Yes."

There was a long silence full of leaves moving. Then she laughed—a short, disbelieving sound—and pressed her hand over her mouth, and when she took it away her eyes were wet.

"Do you know what I thought, all these months?" she said. "I thought I'd hired a careful man. Careful drivers are worth their weight in gold, Edmund, and you were the most careful I'd ever seen. Invisible. Unremarkable. I used to think, *there is a man who will never betray anyone, because he will never risk anything.*" She shook her head slowly. "I was wrong about you. Thank God I was wrong about you."

"It was Ruth I lied for," Edmund said. "Not the cause. I don't—I've never been sure about causes."

"No," Adeline agreed. "But you were sure about her. About a stranger in your attic. That's rarer, and it counts for more." She looked up through the black branches at the few stars the city allowed. "Do you know what we are, those of us who shelter people? We're not soldiers. Soldiers get to believe in victory. We're just people who've decided that some doors stay open, whatever comes through them, whoever's watching." She squeezed his arm. "Tonight you were a door, Edmund."

He wanted to tell her then. Standing in her orchard, in the smell of apples going soft on the grass, with her gratitude warm on his skin—he wanted to say, *I am not who my papers say I am, I am a lie with a driving licence, and Prowse has found the seam in me, and Calloway is pulling it thread by thread, and every kindness you've shown me is built on a foundation of fraud.* The words climbed his throat like water climbing a well.

What stopped him was arithmetic. Cold, familiar, hateful arithmetic. If he confessed tonight, he became a liability she'd have to cut loose—or worse, protect—and Calloway would use either outcome. His secret was only survivable as long as it was his alone. So he swallowed it, again, and felt it settle deeper into him like a stone into a riverbed.

"Go inside," he said gently. "It's freezing. I'll lock up."

"You'll come in too. There's soup. There is always soup after a raid; I don't know why, but there is."

"In a minute."

She went, and he stayed among the trees a while longer, and it was there, in the dark orchard, that the thought arrived—the one that had been circling him for days and finally landed, talons first.

Nell sailed on the morning tide in five days. One berth. One berth, and a sister who didn't yet know she had a brother, and an inspector who had just watched him lie fluently and liked him less for it, and a census taker with a ledger full of other people's sins—including, very possibly, Edmund Hartwell's invented birthplace, sitting in Prowse's neat copperplate like a loaded gun.

Five days. He put his hand against the oldest tree, bark rough under his palm, and made the orchard a promise he had no right to make.

*I will get her on that ship. Whatever it costs. Whoever I have to stop being.*

Behind him, up at the house, a lamp went on in the attic window—the trapdoor open, the loose plank lifted, Adeline checking the hiding place out of habit, out of grief, out of love. And far down the street, parked where no police car needed to be at nine o'clock at night, a dark saloon sat with its lights off, and a man inside it lit a cigarette, and the ember glowed once, twice, and went out.

They weren't done with number nine. Edmund knew it the way he knew rain was coming—by the pressure, by the ache, by the smell of the air.

Five days.
