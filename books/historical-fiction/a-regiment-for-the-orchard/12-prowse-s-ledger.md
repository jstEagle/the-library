## 12. Prowse's Ledger

I found Prowse where I knew he'd be, because a man who sells his evenings to the census office is a man with no life worth going home to. The Bristol and Clifton District Registry kept late hours in the weeks after enumeration, checking schedules against the rate books, hunting the discrepancies that made men like Calloway sit up straight at their desks. Prowse worked the back room on Marsh Street, second floor, a window that looked out onto nothing but brick.

I had rehearsed three openings on the walk over. All of them collapsed the moment he opened the door and saw me standing there with my hat in my hand like a penitent.

"Mr. Hartwell." He said it slowly, tasting it, the way you'd taste wine you suspected of being vinegar. "Come about your schedule entry?"

"May I come in?"

He stepped aside without answering, which was answer enough. The room was small and smelled of dust and sealing wax, lit by one gas bracket turned low. Ledgers everywhere—stacked on the desk, stacked on the floor, stacked on a chair that had clearly been evicted from its original purpose. A census taker's whole world was other people's households, reduced to columns and figures, and Prowse sat in the middle of it all like a spider who'd grown fond of the web.

"I'll say what I came to say plainly," I told him, "because I think plainness will interest you more than anything else tonight."

He lowered himself onto his stool and folded his hands. Waiting. He was good at waiting; I could see that now, see the shape of the patience that had let him linger over my name for two full minutes while I stood in Adeline Voss's hallway feeling the floor tilt under me.

"You noted discrepancies in my entry," I said. "Birthplace given as Bath in one record, Bristol in another. You wrote it down twice. You underlined it."

"Standard procedure."

"No," I said. "It isn't. I've asked. Standard procedure is a query slip to the registrar, not a private note in a taker's own book, and certainly not a visit from an inspector within the week. You flagged me. You chose me. And then you sent Calloway to the Voss house like a man pointing a torch at a haystack he wants searched."

Something moved behind his eyes—not guilt, exactly. Calculation. He was deciding how much I knew and how much it would cost me to learn the rest.

"And if I did?" he said. "What business is it of yours? You're a driver, Mr. Hartwell. Or so the schedule says. It says very little else, actually. That's rather the trouble with you."

"It's my business because Inspector Calloway has decided the Voss household harbors fugitives, and he's using your note as his warrant for suspicion. Whatever game you're playing with my papers, there are people under that roof whose liberty depends on how it plays out. So either tell me why you marked me, or I'll go to the registrar myself tomorrow and ask why a census schedule with irregular entries was never forwarded but instead found its way into an inspector's hands ahead of procedure."

The threat was thinner than it sounded. I didn't know if any of it was true. But Prowse's hands unfolded, and I understood I'd guessed right about the shape of him: a small man, a precise man, a man who lived by rules until the rules became inconvenient—and then lived by something else.

"You'd burn yourself doing it," he said. "You understand that. Go to the registrar and the first thing they'll want is your proper particulars. Full name. Birthplace. Parents' names." He smiled without warmth. "Can you supply them, Mr. Hartwell? Can you supply them *consistently*?"

Prowse's POV

The trouble with Edmund Hartwell was that he wasn't afraid enough.

Most of them came to Prowse afraid. That was the design of it, though he'd never written the design anywhere—it lived in the way he kept his duplicate books, the clean set for the office and the working set for himself, the working set with its marginal annotations in pencil that could be erased or elaborated depending on how conversations went. A discrepancy was a lever. Every household had one somewhere—a lodger unlisted, a birth year shaved, a wife entered as a sister, a servant who appeared on no wage book at all. The census had been sold to Parliament as an instrument of statecraft. In practice it was an instrument of *leverage*, and leverage was the only currency available to a man earning thirty shillings a week counting other people's beds.

Hartwell stood in his room radiating the wrong emotion entirely. Not fear. Anger, banked and controlled, the anger of someone who had recently discovered he had something left to lose.

"You've done this before," Hartwell said suddenly. "Not just me. Others."

It wasn't a question, and Prowse didn't insult them both by denying it.

"The Quay Street house," Hartwell pressed. "The Widow Kesselman. Her nephew's entry—age given as nineteen, but she told the school board he was twenty-one last autumn. You called on her twice. She paid her rates late this quarter for the first time in eleven years."

Where had he learned all this? Prowse felt a coldness move through him. The invisible man, reading letters. Of course. Of course—the Voss house ran on correspondence, and the driver carried it, and a man who reads other people's mail learns to read everything.

"And the family on Guinea Street," Hartwell went on, stepping closer, "the ones who emigrated in March, sudden as anything, leaving furniture behind. Their schedule had an annotation too. I've seen your pencil work now, Mr. Prowse. You have habits. You underline twice when you mean to return."

"Careful," Prowse said quietly.

"Why?" Hartwell's voice didn't rise at all, which was somehow worse than shouting. "What will you do—write me down again? I'm already your best entry. A man with no consistent past is a man who can't complain to anyone. Isn't that the beauty of it? You don't pick strong households. You pick the crooked ones, the frightened ones, the ones who can't go to the authorities because the authorities are the knife already hovering. Widow Kesselman can't report you. Her nephew isn't supposed to exist in that house. Neither, apparently, am I."

The room was very quiet. The gas hissed.

"So here is what happens now," Hartwell said. "You're going to show me the working ledger. All of it. Because I think there are more names than mine, and I think some of those names touch people I care about, and I think—" he paused, and for a moment the anger flickered into something rawer, something almost pleading, "—I think you keep records because records are power, Mr. Prowse, and I need power right now, and you need someone to hold yours where Calloway can't reach it."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then tomorrow morning a letter arrives at the registry office describing, in detail, the duplicate books and their marginal annotations. Unsigned. They'll investigate everyone you've ever enumerated. Including me—but I've stopped being afraid of that particular fire. It's already burning my house down. I'll just make sure it takes yours with it."

Prowse looked at him for a long moment. Then he got up, crossed to the shelf behind him, and took down a ledger bound in grey cloth, identical to fifty others in the room except for the worn corners, the soft spine, the signs of a book handled nightly by lamplight.

He set it on the desk between them.

"You understand," he said, "that showing you this makes us partners. Not friends. Partners. There's a difference, and the difference is that partners can't betray each other without destroying themselves."

"I know exactly what we are," Hartwell said.

Edmund's POV

The ledger opened like a wound.

Page after page of households in Prowse's small ruled hand, each entry annotated in that same double-underlined pencil. Some were innocent—a corrected age, a misspelled street. But a dozen pages in, the nature of the thing declared itself. Sums. Dates. Small amounts, mostly—a pound here, ten shillings there—entered beside initials, beside the words *settled*, *arranged*, *no further query*. Payments. A dozen households in Bristol paying a census taker a quiet quarterly rent on their own secrets.

Widow Kesselman. The Guineas Street family before they fled. A dentist on Park Row whose second wife predeceased a first marriage he'd sworn dissolved. A boarding house keeper whose Irish lodgers were listed as English. Page after page, the same architecture: a discrepancy found, a visit paid, an arrangement reached, an annotation kept—not as evidence, but as *collateral*. If a client ever grew brave, the margin note was there to hang them.

"You've built a gallows out of paperwork," I said.

"I've built a living out of observation," Prowse replied. "There's scarcely a difference in this city. Everyone is hiding something smaller or larger than they can afford. The census simply asks the questions people can't survive answering honestly. Someone was always going to do what I do. Better a careful man than a careless one."

"Better you than Calloway, you mean."

That landed. I watched it land—he didn't flinch, but his hands went still on the desk, and stillness in Prowse was the equivalent of another man's wince.

"Yes," he said finally. "Better me than Calloway. Calloway doesn't want payments. Calloway wants convictions. When he finds a discrepancy, someone goes to prison. When I find one, someone pays ten shillings and sleeps soundly. Ask yourself which system the Kesselmans of this city prefer."

"I'm asking myself what Ruth Ellery's name is doing nowhere in this book," I said, "when you enumerated the Voss household yourself, and Calloway seems to know precisely how many people sleep under that roof."

The silence stretched. Prowse closed the ledger, slowly, keeping his palm flat on the cover as though holding something down.

"That," he said, "is a different matter entirely."

"How?"

"Because the Voss house isn't in the working ledger. I never put it there." He looked at me with something I hadn't seen in him before—not cunning, but a kind of weary candor, the look of a gambler showing another gambler his losing hand. "Because Calloway approached *me* first, Mr. Hartwell. Three weeks before enumeration day. He came to the registry office and asked for the takers assigned to Clifton and Redland, and he gave us instructions—quietly, off the books—about certain households worth attending to. Certain questions worth lingering over. The Voss house was on his list before I ever knocked on its door. Your discrepancies weren't my discovery. They were my assignment."

The floor did its tilting trick again. I gripped the edge of the desk.

"He used you."

"He uses everyone. That's what inspectors are *for*." Prowse's mouth twisted. "And here is the part that should interest you most, since you've come all this way to be angry at me. When Calloway gave me the list, there were four houses on it. Four households he wanted scrutinized. The Voss house. A printer on Stokes Croft. A lodging house near the terminal. And a dockside address on Wapping—rooms above a chandler's shop, occupied since January by families off the boats from Cork."

Cork.

My mother's city. My half-sister's city. The word went through me like a splinter finding its depth.

"What dockside address?" I heard myself ask, and my voice sounded far away, belonging to some other man in some other story.

"Wapping," Prowse said, watching me with those patient, terrible eyes. "Number nine. Why, Mr. Hartwell? Do you know it?"

I thought of Nell Byrne on the quay, saving her coppers for a berth, wearing my mother's eyes in a stranger's face. I thought of Calloway watching the docks—I didn't know yet how I knew it, only that the knowledge had been gathering in me for days like weather. And I thought of the arithmetic that had begun forming in my chest, cold and exact: four houses on a list, one berth on a ship, and every exit costing somebody.

"Take out your pencil," I said.

Prowse raised an eyebrow.

"You're going to write down what Calloway asked you to find," I told him. "All of it. The instructions, the dates, the list. In your own hand, in your own book, signed. Because if I'm going to be your partner, partner, then you're going to be useful—and the only thing Calloway fears more than a discrepancy is his own handwriting coming back to him in someone else's testimony."

For a long moment I thought he'd refuse. Then he drew the pencil from behind his ear, opened a fresh page, and began to write—and watching him, I felt the strangest sensation of my invisible life: that for once, the record was being made for me, about my enemies, and I was standing close enough to read it.

He was halfway down the page when he stopped, pencil hovering.

"There's something else," Prowse said. "About the Wapping address. Calloway didn't just want it scrutinized. He wanted it *watched through April*—and there's a sailing on the morning tide next week that he specifically asked to be reported. Passenger lists. Berth bookings. Everything." He glanced up. "Someone's planning to leave Bristol on that ship, Mr. Hartwell. And Calloway means to know who, before the gangway comes down."

The whistle I'd been hearing in my dreams all week blew silent in my head. One berth. One manifest. One inspector already leaning over the quay.

"Now," Prowse said, laying down the pencil, "shall we discuss terms?"
