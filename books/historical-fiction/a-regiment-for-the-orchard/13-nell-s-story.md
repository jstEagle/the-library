## 13. Nell's Story

She had rehearsed it on the walk over, in the way you rehearse a lie—except that everything she meant to tell him was true, and somehow that was worse. True things needed no rehearsal. They simply sat in the chest like ballast and waited for someone foolish enough to ask.

He'd asked.

Not in words. Edmund Hartwell didn't seem to be a man who did anything in words when he could do it in silence instead. He'd asked by standing on the quay three days ago with his face gone white as gull-bone, and he'd asked again last night at Mrs. Kettle's door with a paper bag of apples held out like an offering to a priest, and he was asking now, sitting across her brother's—her *brother's*—small rented room in Hotwells, hat on his knee, hands folded like a schoolboy waiting to be caned.

"Go on," he said. "Tell me."

Nell Byrne—*Byrne*, a name some matron had plucked out of the air because her mother's surname was too dangerous to keep, too much a thread leading back to a woman who had walked onto a boat and off the face of the earth—looked at him and felt the strangest thing. Not anger. She'd carried anger across the Irish Sea like a bundle she couldn't set down; she knew its weight better than her own name. But looking at him now, this pale English gentleman with his careful cuffs and his guilty eyes, she found the anger had gone strange and soft in her hands, like fruit kept too long.

"You'll want tea first?" he said. "I could—"

"I don't want tea." She heard how hard her voice came out and made herself gentler, the way you gentle a horse. "Sit still, will you. If I stop I won't start again."

He sat still. He was good at that, she was learning. Good at waiting, good at watching, good at making himself small. She wondered who had taught him that trick, and suspected the answer was: the same person who had taught her its twin, which was how to make yourself loud enough that nobody noticed you were alone.

"My earliest memory," she said, "is the smell of a workhouse laundry."

His face didn't move, but something behind it did. She watched it happen—the flinch he swallowed, the way his throat worked. He wanted her to stop already. She could see it. And she found, to her own surprise, that she didn't want to stop. She wanted him to sit there and hold every word in his lap like hot coals, because he'd asked for them and they were hers and she had carried them twenty years alone.

"Cork. You know that much, maybe. The North Infirmary lane, then the union itself when the money went. Mam—" the word caught, as it always caught, on the smallness of it, *Mam*, one syllable, the whole world once—"Mam worked washing for the garrison families before that. My father, whoever he was, was gone before I had memory enough to miss him. It was her and me and a room with a hearth that smoked sideways. I remember her singing. That's what people always say about their mothers, isn't it? But I mean it literal. She sang while she worked, all the old ones, and I thought the whole world sang while it worked. I thought that was what work was. A song and another pair of hands."

Edmund's eyes had gone bright. He didn't blink it away. Credit to him for that.

"And then one morning there was no song." Nell looked at the window, at the grey Bristol light lying on the sill like something washed up. "I was four. Maybe five—you lose count early when nobody's counting for you. There was a woman from the parish, and a bag packed, and my mother kneeling down in front of me in her good shawl, the green one, and she said—I remember it exact, because I've said it to myself ten thousand times trying to hear something different in it—she said, *'You mind Sister Agnes now, a stór, and I'll come back before the apples.'*"

"The apples," Edmund said. Barely a sound.

"She left in September. Apple season." Nell smiled, and hated the smile even as she made it. "So either she lied, or she meant the next year, or the year after, or she meant some orchard so far away the seasons took a lifetime to turn. I used to think about that. Every autumn, in the laundry, in the mill, in every place after, I'd see the apples come in and think—this is the year. This is the year she meant. You can build a whole religion out of a sentence like that. I did. Twenty years of it. And every year God answered *no*, and every year I prayed it again."

The room was very quiet. Down in the street a cart went by, iron-shod wheels on cobbles, and neither of them moved until the sound had faded entirely, as though noise would break whatever thin ice they stood on.

"Sister Agnes gave me the name," Nell went on. "Byrne. She said my mother had asked for it to be so—that if anyone came asking after a child called Villalobos, or Hartwell, or anything else, there was no such child. I thought for years that was protection. Then I got older and understood it was erasure. She didn't hide me from danger, Mr. Hartwell. She hid herself from me. Gave me away twice over—once at the dock, and once in a parish book."

"Edmund," he said quietly. "If we're doing this, you should call me Edmund."

"We're not doing anything yet," Nell said. "I'm talking. You're listening. That's the arrangement, and it's the only one I'm minded to make."

He bowed his head slightly. Obedient. She almost liked him for it.

The workhouse years she gave him plainly, without ornament, because ornament would have been a kindness and she wasn't sure yet whether he deserved kindness.

"There were two hundred of us children in the Cork union, give or take the dying. We slept in wards of forty beds, and the beds were iron, and the sheets—if there were sheets—had been boiled so often they were more hole than cloth. We rose at six. Chapel, porridge, work. The little ones picked oakum till their fingers bled, and the bleeding was counted a fault, mind—not the work, the blood. Blood on the oakum spoiled it for sale." She said it evenly, and watched him grip his own fingers as though checking them for blood. "At seven I was sent out to service in a farmer's house outside Midleton. Eighteen hours a day, and the master's wife beat me for dreaming. That was the actual charge. *Dreaming.* Standing at the window looking west, she said, like I had a choice in where my head went."

"Nell—"

"You asked. Let me finish." Her voice cracked on the second word and she let it. "There was a girl in the ward before me, Brigid Halloran, who used to say the workhouse was a school for forgetting. First thing you forget is your birthday. Then your mother's face. Then your own name, if you're not careful, because the name they call you by isn't yours and the one that is gets rusty from disuse. Brigid forgot herself inside two years. Used to answer to anything. Used to cry at nothing. They sent her to the asylum at eleven." Nell met his eyes. "I decided I wouldn't forget. That was my one rebellion, d'you see? Not escape—there was no escaping, girls tried and were brought back and the story of what happened to them traveled faster than any of us ran. No. My rebellion was memory. I kept everything. Every beating, every winter, every night I lay on that iron bed doing sums in my head to stay sane—how many days since September, how many apple seasons come and gone. I built myself a warehouse of grievance and I stocked it floor to ceiling and I called it keeping faith with her. Because if I remembered it all, then somebody in the world remembered her leaving, and the sin of it couldn't just dissolve into the sea the way she had."

Edmund had put his hat aside at some point. His hands were flat on his knees now, pressing down, as though holding himself to the chair.

"What did you think she'd done?" he asked. "When you were young."

"I thought she was dead," Nell said. "That was the merciful version, and I clung to it longer than was sensible. Dead mothers are tragic. Dead mothers leave you an ache and a halo and everyone pities you proper. It was only later—twelve, thirteen, when I was back in the union after the farmer's wife turned me out for growing too like my mother, which was apparently also a fault—that I started doing sums of a different kind. She'd have reached England in a day and a half. Boats went daily. If she lived, why no letter? Letters cost a penny. I checked. A penny, Edmund. I stood in the Cork post office at fourteen years old and asked what a letter to England cost, and the man told me a penny, and I stood there with fourpence in my pocket—four letters' worth—and understood that in twenty years my mother had never once found a penny's worth of wanting me."

The words came out harder than she'd intended, and she saw him take them like blows, one after another, shoulders absorbing each. Good, some cold part of her said. Then a warmer part said: *he's a stranger to you, and you're flaying a stranger,* and the cold part answered: *no, he's not a stranger, that's the entire trouble.*

"So I stopped praying for her return and started praying for her address," Nell said. "That's a colder prayer, but it's a working one. I wanted a door to knock on. I wanted to stand in some fine hallway with my bundle in my hand and watch her face do whatever it was going to do. I practiced the speech, d'you know that? Years. On the quays, in the mills, walking the roads between jobs. *My name is Nell. You knew me as Eileen. You left before the apples.* I must have said it ten thousand times to nobody." She laughed, short and unamused. "And then last Tuesday a man delivers a parcel to the fish stall on St. Patrick's quay, takes one look at me, and goes the colour of candle wax. And I look at him and I think—he has her eyes. Nobody in Cork had those eyes. Grey-green, with the lid coming down low at the corner, like a curtain half drawn. I'd have known them anywhere, because I shave the same ones every morning in whatever mirror a lodging house bothers to provide. So I followed him. Halfway up the town like a fool, heart going like a drum, and he turns around and says, 'What was your mother's name?'"

"And you said—"

"'Mary Villalobos,'" Nell said, "and watched him come apart right there in the street. And then he said, 'She was my mother too.'"

Silence again. The light had shifted while she talked; afternoon was leaning toward evening, and somewhere below a neighbor was frying onions, and the ordinary smells and sounds of living seemed almost obscene against the thing laid open between them.

"She told you she was an only child," Nell said. "Or no children at all. One of the two."

"She told me almost nothing," Edmund said. "She told me stories with the middle cut out of them. That we were Spanish on her side—hence the name—a sailor grandfather out of Cork, shipwrecked ashore, stayed for love. That her parents died when she was young. That she'd been married before my father and it ended badly and we weren't to speak of it. I was a boy. Boys don't press. Boys are grateful for any story at all." He rubbed his jaw. "After she died I found a bundle of letters in her trunk. Cork postmarks. I read them—they were addressed to a Mrs. Hartwell, but the hand wasn't writing to a Mrs. Hartwell, if that makes sense. It was writing to somebody else wearing the name. Money mostly. Small sums, regular, for years. And one line I've never forgotten: *'Does she ever speak of me?'* No name on the *she*. I assumed a sister. Some cousin. Someone left behind in the old country whom she was ashamed of owing."

"For years," Nell said. "It stopped coming when I was eleven. The matron said my guardian in England had fallen on hard times. We ate bread and dripping for a month and called it Lent."

"I'm sorry." He said it fast, like a man stepping into cold water. "God, Nell, I'm sorry. Not for her—for me. For the—she sent me to school. Do you understand that? She sent me to school. I had a piano teacher. I had a coat for winter and a coat for summer and a governess who taught me French, and the money for all of it came out of the same purse that couldn't spare a penny for a letter, and I never once asked where the purse was filled, because asking questions wasn't—wasn't what I did. I made myself invisible. It was my creed. History happened to other people; I carried it around in other people's bags." His voice frayed. "You were in the history. You were in it up to your neck, and I was riding past it in a motor car reading other men's letters."

"Yes," Nell said.

He blinked. Whatever speech he'd been building toward, expecting to be argued down from, her plain assent knocked it sideways.

"That's all? Just—yes?"

"What would you have me say? That it wasn't your fault?" She leaned forward. "Listen to me, Edmund, because I've had twenty years to learn this and you've had three days. It wasn't your fault. You were a child being fed out of the same hand that starved me, and a child can't audit the hand. Blaming you for the school fees is like blaming the rain for the flood—it fell where it fell. *She* chose. She chose the quiet life and the clean slate and the boy she could remake from scratch, because a son with no past was worth more to her than a daughter with one. That's the truth of it, and it's ugly, and I've had to swallow it in installments my whole life. But I'll not have you swallow my portion for me. Guilt taken on behalf of the guilty doesn't ease them. It just makes two people carry what one deserves."

He stared at her. "How can you forgive me so easily?"

"I'm not forgiving you," Nell said. "There's nothing to forgive. I'm forgiving *her*—or I'm trying, some days, in a low dishonest way, the way you forgive weather. And that—" she stopped, and made herself finish, because he deserved the finish—"that hurts worse than any blame I ever carried. Anger keeps you upright. Forgiveness is what knocks you down. Because anger says *she owed me*, and forgiveness says *she was a frightened woman who did a terrible arithmetic and chose the sum that kept her safe*, and both things are true, and neither one gives me back twenty years of oakum and lice and a name off a matron's tongue."

"A name given by strangers," Edmund said softly.

"Byrne." She shrugged. "It fits me now. Like the workhouse boots fit—badly, but you walk anyway, and after a while the shape of the wrongness becomes the shape of your foot. I've been Nell Byrne so long that Eileen Villalobos is a stranger I get letters about."

"Letters," he said sharply. "From whom?"

"Ah." She sat back, studying him. He really was quick, under all that stillness. "Now that's the part I came here to settle before I told you anything else, and I've gone and told you everything else first. There's a woman in Cork. Old now. Sister Agnes's niece, who kept the parish papers after the sister died, and who wrote to me two winters ago—found me through the mill registry—to say that among the papers was a note in my mother's hand. Undated. Probably written the week she sailed. It said that if anything ever prevented her from sending, I was to know the fault was circumstance and never love, and that she'd left something for me with the one person in Bristol she trusted above all others."

"Trusted," Edmund repeated. "Who?"

"I don't know," Nell said. "The niece thinks the rest of the note was torn away—or cut away. Deliberate. Whoever kept it wanted the name kept." She watched his face carefully as she laid the last of it down. "But I'll tell you what I know besides, since we're trading truths tonight. Two days ago, a man came asking after me on the quay. Well-dressed, civil, spoke soft. Asked the stall women where the Byrne girl lodged, said he was from the shipping office, that there was a query on my berth money. And the stall women, bless their loose tongues, told him Mrs. Kettle's in Hotwells. So either your shipping office has grown a policeman's shoulders, or someone in this city is very interested in a girl from Cork who suddenly came into a brother."

Edmund had gone still in a new way—not the stillness of guilt now but of alarm, and she recognized it, because it was the stillness of a man who has seen something behind her shoulder that she cannot see.

"Nell," he said slowly. "The man who came asking. Was he tall? Grey at the temples? Soft-spoken, with a way of smiling that never reached—"

"You know him."

"He was in my mother's circle," Edmund said. "Before she died. He knew her. And yesterday he offered to tear up every charge against me if I'd testify against the woman I drive for—and when I refused, he told me that refusing wouldn't save you. That the berth you're sailing on is already a matter of record." He stood, snatched his hat off the chair, and for the first time since she'd met him he looked less like a gentleman than like a man deciding to run. "Get your things. Not tomorrow. Tonight. Because I think the informant Calloway's been hunting isn't in the Voss house at all—I think he means to make it you."
