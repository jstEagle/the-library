## 14. The Betrayal He Didn't Commit

The knock came at half past seven in the morning, which was how Edmund knew it was serious. Policemen kept office hours. Friends and tradesmen came when it suited them; the law arrived punctually, like a train.

He was in the kitchen with a pot of tea going cold when Ruth came down the stairs two at a time, her face pale under its freckles. "Front door," she said. "Two of them. Not Calloway."

"Not Calloway is worse," Edmund said, and set down his cup.

It was Sergeant Doyle and a constable whose name nobody would remember, and they had a paper in Doyle's hand that made the hallway feel suddenly narrow. Adeline appeared in her dressing gown with her hair not yet pinned, which she did deliberately—Edmund had learned this about her, that she wore disarray like armor, forcing men to look at her as something other than what they expected.

"Miss Voss," Doyle said. "We've received information regarding persons harbored at this address."

"Information." She let the word sit on the floor between them like something dropped. "From whom?"

"That's not for me to say, miss."

"No. It never is." She folded her arms. "You may search. You'll find a cook, a gardener who comes Tuesdays, myself, my driver, and my housekeeper's niece, who is visiting from Frome. If your informant counted more than five of us, he counted shadows."

Doyle's eyes went past her shoulder to Edmund, and stayed there a moment too long.

They searched for two hours. They went through Adeline's correspondence with the delicacy of men who could read but not quickly, through the linen press and the cellar and the attic where Ruth had slept three nights running before being moved to the widow Cartwright's next door. They found nothing, because there was nothing left to find—the house had been scoured so often by fear that fear itself had become the housekeeper. But before they left, Doyle paused beside Edmund in the hall, close enough that Edmund could smell the tobacco and carbolic soap of him.

"Hartwell, isn't it," Doyle said. Not a question.

"It is."

"Do you know you're down in the census book twice?" Doyle smiled without warmth. "Once as Hartwell of Bristol, once as Hartwell born in Cork. Mr. Prowse flagged it himself. Thorough man, Prowse."

Edmund felt the floor tilt the way it had on census day, the whole house listing toward some cold sea. "Clerical error," he said. "I corrected it the same week."

"Mm." Doyle put on his hat. "Errors have a way of travelling, Mr. Hartwell. Like bad pennies. We'll be seeing you."

The door shut. In the silence after, Adeline turned and looked at him—not with accusation, not yet, but with the terrible neutral attention of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't want to finish.

"Cork," she said.

"My mother was Irish. I told you I drove for a family there before Bristol."

"You told me you were born in Clifton." Her voice was quiet, which was worse than loud. Quiet meant she'd been turning things over in the dark hours, fitting them together, and the picture wouldn't lie flat. "You've told me a great many things, Edmund. I believed them because I wanted to. A woman in my position learns to believe people—it's cheaper than checking."

"And now you're checking."

"Now the police are checking, which is the same thing done with less courtesy." She pressed her fingers to the bridge of her nose. "Ruth was in this house two nights ago. If they'd come with a warrant instead of a fishing line—"

"They'd have found nothing. I'd have driven her to Cartwright's myself if you'd asked."

"I shouldn't have needed to ask." She lowered her hand and looked at him straight, and it was the looking that wounded him more than any word. "Someone informed, Edmund. Someone who knows this house knows Ruth was here. And the only person in this house the police can already place at the center of a lie is standing in front of me wearing his collar crooked."

He straightened the collar. His hands were steady, which surprised him; inside, something was coming apart quietly, the way ice goes off a pond—not cracking, just thinning, going dark, becoming water you can't stand on.

"I have never lied to you about anything that mattered," he said.

"Then you've been very careful about what matters." She turned toward the stairs, then stopped. "The letter you burned. In March. I saw the ash in the grate and said nothing, because everyone burns letters, God knows I do. But you burned it *quickly*, Edmund. Like a man drowning a cat."

She went up. The stair door closed with a sound like a period at the end of a sentence.

* * *

He drove her to a meeting that afternoon because she asked him to, formally, in front of Mrs. Quill, using the voice she used for servants—which was its own verdict, delivered in public. He sat outside the hall in Redland with the engine ticking and understood, for the first time, what Nell must have felt every day of her life in Cork: the particular loneliness of being suspected by the very people you'd have died for.

He reviewed it all while the rain came down on the windshield. Who could have informed? Prowse—he had the census discrepancies, the blackmail ledger, the appetite. But Prowse didn't know about Ruth; blackmailers sold secrets they had, and Prowse's secret was Edmund's own, and selling Edmund would burn the goose that laid his envelopes. Unless the goose had stopped laying. Unless Edmund's stalling had taught Prowse that fear was better harvested than patience.

Or Doyle was bluffing entirely. There might be no informant at all—just a net thrown wide, watching what fell into it. He thought of Ruth moving between houses after dark, thought of the widow Cartwright's nervous son, thought of the meeting lists Adeline kept in a code so simple a clever schoolgirl could break it. Any of a dozen threads, pulled by any of a dozen hands.

And yet. *Down twice in the census book.* Prowse had shown him that page himself, had tapped it with one dry finger, smiling like a man showing a card trick. Prowse had wanted him to know it existed. Which meant Prowse wanted him to know it could be *used*. And now it had been used—or dangled—and Edmund couldn't tell whether he was holding the leash or wearing the collar.

At four o'clock a boy in a cloth cap knocked on the car window and handed him a note with no name on it. He knew the hand. Dry, small, precise as an insect's legwork.

*Your sister sails on the morning tide Saturday, or she doesn't. The manifest wants correcting. My study, eight o'clock tonight. Bring nothing you value.—P.*

Edmund read it twice and fed it to the cigarette lighter, watching the corner curl and blacken. So. Prowse hadn't sold him. Prowse had *invested* in him. That was almost worse—an enemy you could hate was a clean thing; an enemy who owned shares in you got inside your arithmetic.

But it also meant Prowse wasn't the informant, or not only. Because Prowse's note said nothing about Ruth, and whoever had informed had spoken of Ruth.

There was someone else. Someone who watched the house.

* * *

He went back at dusk and found Mrs. Quill waiting in the kitchen with her arms crossed, a woman shaped like a keg and about as easy to move.

"She's asking herself questions about you," Quill said, without preamble. "I'll not pretend otherwise. But I'll tell you what I told her. That girl has been in six safe houses since February. Six. And the only one that's been named to the police is this one—the one where you sleep over the coach house." She uncrossed her arms. "Now, I don't believe you did it. But I'll say this plain, driver: belief isn't a thing you can pay wages in. You want her trust back, you bring her the name of who did."

"And if I can't?"

"Then learn to live in a house where the doors are polite and the hearts are locked." Quill went back to her sink. "That's how we all lived before her anyway. Some of us remember."

He climbed to his room above the coach house and sat on the narrow bed with his elbows on his knees. Below, the orchard was a dark tangle against the last grey light, the trees Adeline loved and neglected in equal measure, and he thought: *that's what I am to this household now. An unpruned thing. Bearing fruit nobody trusts.*

He had given up his name once already—in the sense that mattered, the true one, the one on the ship's manifest that would carry Nell away clean. He had lied to Calloway, stalled Prowse, burned a letter, covered for Ruth in the orchard with a policeman's lantern swinging thirty feet away. Every one of those acts had been loyal. Every one of them had looked, from the outside, exactly like treachery.

That was the trap he finally saw, sitting alone in the dark with the rain starting again: innocence was no defense, because his innocence was made entirely of guilt. To clear himself he would have to confess things that would destroy the people he'd protected. To protect them he would have to remain, forever, the man who looked guilty.

Somewhere below, the kitchen door opened and closed. Footsteps crossed the yard—not Adeline's quick stride, not Quill's heavy tread. Lighter. Hurried. Going out toward the lane, where the gas lamp didn't reach.

Edmund was down the stairs and at the window in time to see a figure pause at the gate—a young man, slight, collar up, and in the instant the lamplight caught him, Edmund knew the face. The widow Cartwright's nervous son. The boy who carried messages, who knew which houses held which fugitives, who had seen Ruth come over the wall on Tuesday night.

The boy glanced back at the house once—once, quickly, the way people do when they're ashamed—and slipped out into the lane.

Edmund stood in the dark with his heart going like a hammer, and understood two things at once, each cancelling the other's mercy: the betrayal had a name, and proving it would mean telling Adeline that her own network had leaked—that the rot wasn't him, and wasn't only him, and might run further up than either of them dared to look.

He took his coat off the hook. Outside, the rain had stopped, and the lane lay empty in both directions—but he knew which way led to the telegraph office, and he knew the last collection was at nine.

He went after him.
