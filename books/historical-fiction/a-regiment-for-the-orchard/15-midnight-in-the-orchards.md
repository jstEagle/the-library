## 15. Midnight in the Orchards

The orchard at midnight was a different country. By day it belonged to Adeline's grief and her pruning shears; by night it belonged to the wind off the Avon and the black shapes of apple trees that had gone unpruned so long they'd grown wild, their branches crossing like people arguing.

Edmund stood among them with his collar turned up, waiting for Nell, and understood that he had never once in his life waited for anyone. He had delivered, driven, carried, departed. Waiting was what other people did—wives on doorsteps, mothers on quays. Now here he was, doing it himself, and finding that it hurt in an unfamiliar place, somewhere below the ribs.

She came through the gap in the wall with the ease of someone who had grown up climbing over things, her shawl dark against the darker grass, and when she saw him she didn't smile. That was fair. He hadn't smiled either.

"You came," he said, and immediately regretted how it sounded—as though he'd expected her not to, as though she were the unreliable one.

"You said you'd something to tell me." She stopped three trees away, keeping the distance between them like a border. "You could've said it at the docks."

"Not there. There are eyes on the docks now." He'd seen them that afternoon—a man reading a newspaper upside down, two others who asked the same questions of every sailor's wife and got the same nothing. Calloway's men. The net was drawing in, knot by knot. "And this isn't dockside talk."

"Then say it."

He'd rehearsed it all day while driving Adeline through Clifton, while carrying parcels up Park Street, while standing in Prowse's smoky office watching the old man alter a manifest with hands that shook from gin or conscience or both. He had rehearsed it as a gift. Standing in the dark with his sister's face—his mother's face—turned toward him, every rehearsal collapsed.

"There's a ship," he said. "Thursday morning tide. The *City of Cork* out of Bristol, bound for New York. There's one berth left on her. One. And I've arranged—" He stopped. Started again. "I've arranged for a name to be on the ticket. A clean one. Not Byrne, not Hartwell. A name nobody's looking for."

Nell said nothing. The wind moved through the orchard, and somewhere overhead a night bird called once and gave up.

"The berth is yours," Edmund said.

"No."

"It's yours, Nell. It's paid for. It's—"

"I heard you the first time." Her voice wasn't loud. It was worse than loud; it was flat and certain, the voice of a woman who had been offered things before and had learned what they cost. "I'm asking who's meant to take it."

Edmund looked at the ground, at the grass silvered with the first frost of the season. "There's only one berth. I told you."

"That's not what I asked."

* * *

Nell had learned long ago that men gave gifts the way they threw coins to beggars—from a height, without looking down, wanting the transaction finished before gratitude became obligation. Her father had been like that, before the fever took him. The nuns had been like that, with their bread and their sermons served together, so you couldn't tell which was which.

So when the man in the orchard—who was her brother, though the word still sat strangely in her mouth, still tasted of Cork and workhouse bread—when he said *the berth is yours*, she did not hear a gift. She heard a transaction with the price hidden.

"Answer me," she said.

He raised his head. Even in the dark she could read him; they shared whatever it was that made faces legible to each other. He was going to lie. She watched him decide against it, watched the decision cost him something visible even by moonlight.

"I'm staying," he said.

"Staying where? In this house? With these people whose letters you steal?" She'd known about the letters for weeks—he'd confessed it one terrible afternoon on the quay, as if unburdening himself were a kindness—and she had not forgiven it, because forgiving it would have meant admitting she understood it. She did understand it. That was the unbearable part. She too had spent her life living in other people's stories because no one had written one for her. "You're staying to testify. I know why you're staying, Edmund. The inspector's man followed me yesterday. Did you know that? A little fellow with a notebook. He knows my face now. He'll know yours soon enough, and then there won't be any testifying, there'll just be a cell."

"They won't touch me before Thursday."

"And after?"

"After doesn't matter."

The words hung in the cold air between them, and Nell felt her whole body go hot with anger, anger that had nowhere to go because it wasn't really anger—it was terror wearing anger's coat. "After doesn't matter," she repeated. "That's what our mother must've said. When she put me on the step of Saint Brigid's with a blanket and a rosary. *After doesn't matter.* Look what after made of me, Edmund. Look what after looks like."

"Nell—"

"Don't." She held up a hand. "Don't say my name like that. Like it's holy. You didn't earn it."

* * *

She was right, and that was the worst of it.

Edmund stood in the orchard taking the truth like a blow and knew he deserved every ounce of it. He had rehearsed this meeting as generosity, but it had never been generosity. It had been arithmetic—the same cold arithmetic Calloway dealt in, the same arithmetic Prowse scratched into his ledger. One berth. Two siblings. Subtract Edmund. The sum worked, and he had mistaken a solution for a sacrifice.

"You're right," he said. "I didn't earn anything. But listen to me—please listen, because I haven't much time and less courage than I need." He stepped toward her, and she didn't step back, and that small mercy undid him more than her anger had. "If we both stay, we both hang. You understand that. Calloway doesn't want me for what I've done; he wants me for what I know, and what I know can bury Miss Voss, and burying her buries everyone near her—including a girl named Byrne who shouldn't exist on any paper in this country. If you sail, you live. If I sail, we both live and she goes to prison alone and I spend my whole life in America knowing it. I can't do that. I won't."

"So you'll do it here instead. Spend your whole life in a cell knowing it."

"If it comes to that—yes."

"And what am I meant to do with that?" Her voice cracked finally, the flatness breaking open, and beneath it was the child on the church steps, forty years younger than her body. "What am I meant to do in America knowing my brother rotted for me? You think that's a life? I had a life made out of someone else's leaving already. I'm not doing it twice. I'm not building a new self on top of another person's grave."

"It's not a grave—"

"It will be!" She was crying now, furiously, wiping her face with the heel of her hand like a girl fighting in a schoolyard. "Everything you give away, you give away dead. Don't you see it? A berth you don't use, a name you don't claim, a testimony that gets you locked up—that's not giving, Edmund, that's dying politely. You're so used to being nobody that you think vanishing is love."

The words went into him and found the exact center of everything. He stood very still among the apple trees, the frost beginning to whiten his shoulders, and let it land.

"You're right," he said again.

"Stop saying that!"

"Then let me say something else." His voice came out low and steady, steadier than he felt. "I'm not offering you a berth. I'm asking you to do something. I'm asking you to live—not for me, not instead of me, but because somebody in this family has to finish the story, and it can't be me. My story ends here. Yours doesn't have to. Take the ship. Write to me from New York. Be insufferable about it. Tell me about the streets and the work and the men who ask you to dance. Fill pages. Because if I'm going to sit in a cell, I'd rather do it reading letters addressed to me than knowing there were none coming."

Nell stared at him. The fury drained out of her slowly, leaving something rawer underneath.

"That's blackmail," she said at last, and her mouth twitched despite itself. "That's the first honest thing you've done. Blackmailing your own sister."

"I learned from professionals."

* * *

They sat down together under the oldest tree, the one with the split trunk that Adeline said had been struck by lightning before the war, before any war, before anyone now alive could remember. They didn't touch. They sat a foot apart, shoulder blades against the bark, two people discovering the strange physics of blood—that you could share a face and a fury and twenty years of separate hunger and still not know how to reach for each other's hand.

"I used to make up a family," Nell said quietly, after a long time. "In the workhouse. I'd lie awake and invent them. A mother with kind hands. A brother older than me who'd come for me someday, riding something—I didn't know what, a horse, a cart, I'd never seen one up close. He'd come through the gate and the matron couldn't stop him because he had papers. Papers saying I was his." She laughed softly, without humor. "Papers. Even in the dream it came down to papers."

"I'm sorry about the papers."

"I'm not. Not tonight." She turned her head and looked at him, and in the moonlight the resemblance was almost unbearable—his own eyes set in a stranger's history. "Here's my price, Edmund. Hear it, because I'll only say it once. I'll take the ship. But not as charity, and not as escape, and not because you're buying yourself a clean conscience at the price of a ticket. I'll go because going is the fight I can win and staying is a fight you'd lose us both. That's not obedience. That's strategy. Do you understand the difference?"

"I think I'm starting to."

"And when you stand up in that courtroom—if it comes to that—you tell the truth. All of it. Not the version that protects you. The whole ugly thing, the letters, the lies, the census, all of it. Because I spent my childhood being lied about by strangers filling in forms, and I will not have my brother become one more form-filler with a comfortable falsehood." She jabbed a finger at his chest. "Swear it."

"I swear it."

"On what?"

He thought. Everything else had been taken or given away; what remained to swear on?

"On the orchard," he said. "It's the only ground I've got."

She nodded slowly, satisfied or close to it, and pulled her shawl tighter. Above them the apple trees creaked in the wind like old rigging, and somewhere beyond the wall, far off toward the city, a dog began to bark—short, sharp, alarmed.

Neither of them moved at first. Then the barking was joined by a second dog, nearer, and beneath the trees Edmund heard what he had trained himself years ago to hear: footsteps. More than one pair. Moving along the lane outside the wall, unhurried, thorough, the pace of men searching rather than passing.

"Calloway's raiding," he breathed. "Tonight. He's working up the street house by house." He took her arm. "Not the way you came. The low wall, past the dead pear tree—there's a gap behind the compost heap, I've used it. Go back to Mrs. Kavanagh's and don't leave till morning. Whatever you hear, don't come back."

"What about you?"

"I'm going inside." He was already moving, pulling her with him through the trees toward the wall, his mind running ahead of his feet, cataloguing: Ruth in the attic, Adeline asleep with her evidence scattered across her desk like a spread deck of cards, the burned letter's ashes still in the study grate where any fool with a sieve could read them. "Whatever happens tonight happens to me. Not to you."

"Edmund—"

"Go." He pushed her gently toward the gap in the wall, and for one moment her hand caught his sleeve and held it, hard, her mother's—*their* mother's—grip, strong as rope.

"Thursday," she said. "You swore on the orchard."

"Thursday," he said. "Now run."

She vanished through the gap, and Edmund turned back toward the sleeping house, where lights were already blooming wrong—two windows, then three—and the first fist was hammering at the front door.
