## 16. Ruth Is Taken

The knock came at half past six in the morning, and it was not a polite knock.

Edmund was in the kitchen with the kettle already on—he'd taken to rising early since the docks, since Nell's face had rearranged the furniture of his mind—and he heard boots on the gravel before he heard the fists on the door. Four men, by the sound of it. Maybe five. He set down the kettle with a care that surprised him, as if noise itself could be evidence.

"Miss Voss is not receiving visitors," he called through the door.

"We're not visitors, Mr. Hartwell."

Calloway's voice. Of course. Edmund stood in his own kitchen in his waistcoat and shirtsleeves and felt the morning tilt beneath him like a deck. Through the window over the sink he could see the orchard, the crooked apple trees Adeline refused to let anyone fell, their branches still bare from winter, holding up nothing but sky and a few blackbirds.

He opened the door because there was no version of this morning in which he didn't.

"Inspector." He kept his voice level. He had become very good at level. "It's early for official business."

"It's early for most things worth doing." Calloway stepped past him without waiting to be asked in, and behind him came two uniformed constables and a plainclothes man Edmund hadn't seen before, young, nervous, writing nothing. "We've a warrant for the premises. And an arrest warrant for one Ruth Kavanagh, also known as Ruth Ellison, also known as—well, we'll see what she calls herself today."

The name went through Edmund like cold water through cloth. He'd known, of course. He'd carried her letters, read them standing under gas lamps with his collar turned up, learned her handwriting the way you learn a face. But hearing it said aloud in Adeline's hallway, in front of the coat stand and the umbrella pot, made it real in a way his own knowledge never had.

"You'll find no one here by that name," Edmund said.

"I expect not," Calloway said pleasantly. "I expect we'll find someone else entirely. That's rather the point."

* * *

Ruth was awake. She'd been awake all night, in fact, in the attic room above the stables where she'd lived these four months as a ghost with excellent penmanship. She heard the boots too, and she did what she had rehearsed: she took the packet of documents from beneath the floorboard—the lists, the names, the routes out of the city—and she fed them into the little iron stove, one page at a time, feeding the fire slowly so it wouldn't roar, watching forty women's safety curl into ash that smelled faintly of ink.

She was calm about it, mostly. There was a strange liberty in knowing the thing had finally come. For months she had lived folded up small, moving between rooms when the household slept, eating what Edmund left on the stairs, existing as a rumor even to herself. She had been a organizer once—a woman who stood on platforms, whose voice carried across halls full of people who'd walked miles to hear it. Now she was a floorboard creak. Now she was someone else's secret.

The last page she hesitated over. It wasn't a document. It was a letter, unsent, addressed to a sister in Manchester, three lines long, saying only: *I am well. Don't come looking. Whatever they print, remember I chose this.* She held it over the stove until the heat began to blister the paper, then she couldn't do it. She folded it into her bodice instead, against her heart, which was either sentiment or stupidity and probably both.

Then she sat on the narrow bed and waited, hands in her lap, listening to the search of the house below—the drawers pulled open, the doors tried, Calloway's voice moving room to room with terrible patience.

When the boots came up the attic stairs, she stood.

* * *

They brought her down through the kitchen, and that was how Edmund saw it: Ruth Kavanagh, wrists already bound, hair coming loose from its pins, walking steadily with her chin up like a woman descending to a dinner party. Her eyes found him immediately. Not fear in them—something worse, something like apology.

"Don't," she said quietly, before he could speak. "Whatever you're going to say. Don't say it in front of them."

"She's right," Calloway said. He was examining a bookshelf in the hall with two fingers, tilting volumes out as though checking for hollowed-out Bibles. "Save the farewells for the dock, Mr. Hartwell. Or better yet, save them for the trial. You may be asked to repeat them under oath."

Adeline came down the main staircase in her dressing gown, grey hair unbound, and stopped one step from the bottom so that she looked down on the whole scene—the constables, the inspector, the bound woman, Edmund frozen at the stove with the kettle still ticking beside him. She had put herself on that step deliberately; Edmund understood it the way he understood everything about her now, late and completely.

"Inspector Calloway," she said. "You are arresting a woman in my house at dawn without letting her finish dressing."

"She can dress at the station, ma'am."

"She can dress here, or she can walk out in her nightclothes and every photographer in Bristol will have it on the evening edition. Is that the image you want? The WSPU martyr paraded half-naked?" She descended the final step. "Because I promise you, Inspector, that image will do more for our cause than a year of my speeches."

Something flickered across Calloway's face—not doubt exactly, but the professional annoyance of a man realizing the trap works both ways. He gestured, curtly, and the young plainclothes man went back upstairs with a spare blanket from the carriage.

While they waited, Ruth looked around the kitchen. At Edmund. At the orchard visible through the window, bare trees, blackbirds.

"The trees need pruning," she said to him, conversational, absurd. "The apples were poor last year. Too much growth on the top, nothing at the bottom. Someone should cut it back hard."

"I know," Edmund said. "I've been meaning to."

"That's what's wrong with everyone," Ruth said. "Meaning to."

The constables shifted. Calloway pretended not to hear. And Edmund stood there with the kettle cooling behind him and understood that she was handing him instructions disguised as small talk—that somewhere in her arrest there was a message meant for whoever came after her, and that he was whoever came after her, and that she trusted him, this stranger, this driver, this invisible man, with the whole orchard of it.

Ruth dressed quickly—Adeline insisted on supervising, closing the kitchen door on the men—and when she came back down she was in her good coat and hat, composed, and she paused at the threshold and looked back at the house for just one second. One second, taking inventory. Then she walked to the Black Maria on her own feet and climbed in before they could touch her again.

* * *

They did not take Adeline. Not that morning. That was the part Edmund couldn't stop turning over afterward, sitting alone in the kitchen with the burnt smell still in the air.

Calloway lingered after the van pulled away, standing in the hall, turning his hat in his hands like a man at church.

"You could have gone with her, Miss Voss," he said. "There are those in my office who'd have welcomed it."

"I'm aware of what your office would welcome."

"A ship leaves for France Thursday. I mention it as a matter of arithmetic, not sympathy."

"How kind." Adeline folded her arms. "And if I sail away, what happens to the movement? What happens to every frightened girl who reads that Mrs. Voss ran when the police knocked? No, Inspector. If you want me, you'll have to come to a public meeting and take me in front of witnesses and newspapers. I intend to be extremely available."

"You're describing your own arrest as a strategy."

"I'm describing it as an advertisement. It's the same work, done properly." She smiled at him, and Edmund saw the smile land on him like weather. "You think you're catching me, Inspector. But martyrs are not caught. They volunteer."

Calloway put his hat on. At the door he turned, and his eyes found Edmund over Adeline's shoulder—found him and held him, one long second, with the expression of a man filing a document he intended to retrieve later.

"Mr. Hartwell," he said. "We'll speak soon."

The door closed. The gravel crunched. The car went.

* * *

That afternoon Adeline did the most alarming thing Edmund had ever seen her do, and he had seen her heckle a bishop.

She wrote a letter to the papers announcing a public meeting in Queen Square on Saturday night. *The Case of Ruth Kavanagh,* it was headed. *What the Government Does in Your Name While You Sleep.* She dictated it to Edmund while pacing the drawing room, and her voice never wavered, and her hand never shook, and when it was done she read it aloud twice and changed one word—*cowardice* to *fright*, which was somehow more damning—and sent him to post it.

"They'll take me Saturday," she said, almost lightly, when he returned. "At the meeting, in front of the cameras. It's the only way this stops being a police matter and starts being a scandal."

"You could be sentenced. Transportation isn't off the table for harboring—"

"Edmund." She stopped pacing and looked at him, and for a moment the strategist fell away and he saw underneath: a tired woman of fifty-eight who missed her sister and whose feet hurt. "I buried one person I loved by keeping quiet about her. I'm not doing it twice. If my arrest buys Ruth a fair trial and buys the movement a hundred new members, then Saturday night is the cheapest ticket I'll ever purchase."

He wanted to tell her. Standing there in the drawing room with the lamp lit and the letter posted, he wanted to say: *I read your mail. I've read it for years. I know about Ruth because I opened her letters, and I know about Prowse, and I lied to Calloway in this very hallway, and there's a girl on the docks with my mother's eyes who needs a berth I can't give her because I gave my name to a census taker that doesn't exist.*

He wanted to confess everything and be absolved and be, for five minutes, not the invisible man but a man seen and forgiven.

What he said was: "Shall I drive you to the square Saturday? Front entrance?"

She smiled. "Back entrance first, front entrance after. Let them chase us round the block—it photographs beautifully."

* * *

Saturday night came down cold and clear, and Queen Square was full past counting.

Edmund had never seen anything like it. They came out of Redcliffe and Totterdown and the terraces by the gasworks—women mostly, but men too, dockers with their caps in their hands, clerks, a scatter of students from the college—and the platform had been built at the far end near the statue, and the lamps were lit, and the press were there, dozens of them, tripods planted like artillery.

Adeline spoke for twenty minutes and did not once raise her voice. That was the terrible power of it. She told them about Ruth Kavanagh, about the charge of conspiracy, about the difference between the law and justice, and the crowd made a sound Edmund felt in his ribs, low and continuous, like the sea deciding something.

He stood at the edge of the platform with his cap off, and he scanned the crowd out of habit—the habit of the invisible man, always reading exits, always reading faces—and that was when he saw them.

Two constables by the railings, trying to look casual. A sergeant near the tram stop. And beyond them, at the corner of the square, a familiar figure in a good overcoat, not watching the platform at all.

Watching Edmund.

Calloway lifted one hand. Not a wave. An acknowledgment. *I see you. I've always seen you.*

Edmund's mouth went dry. On the platform, Adeline reached the end of the speech—"and if they come for me tonight, let them come in front of you all"—and the crowd roared, and the constables moved at last, unhurried, up the platform steps, and Adeline Voss extended her wrists to them like a woman accepting flowers.

The flashbulbs went off like lightning. The crowd surged. Somebody began to sing, and then everybody was singing, and Edmund stood in the middle of it with his heart hammering and thought, with terrible clarity: *This is what it looks like when history happens to people. This is what I've been hiding from my whole life.*

A constable touched his elbow. "Mr. Hartwell? Inspector would like a word. Now, if you please."

* * *

They walked to the end of the square, away from the singing, and Calloway stood under a lamp with the crowd's noise washing around them like surf.

"Well," he said. "Your mistress has made her choice. Have you made yours?"

"I don't know what you mean."

"Come now. We both know your papers are fiction. We both know you've been carrying letters for that organization for—how long is it? Two years? Three?" He said it kindly, which was the worst way. "Here's what happens next, and I'll say it plainly because I think you're a man who respects plainness. Tomorrow I interview Ruth Kavanagh. If she talks, she hangs herself and takes Miss Voss with her, and you were never involved. If she doesn't talk, I need testimony from inside that house, and you're the only soul inside that house who isn't a true believer. Do you understand what I'm offering you? You're the only one who can walk away."

"And if I won't testify?"

Calloway looked at him for a moment with genuine curiosity, the look of a man studying a specimen that keeps behaving contrary to type.

"Then I start asking why a driver with a false name was seen talking to an Irish girl on the quayside last Tuesday," he said. "The Byrne girl. Nell Byrne. Sailing Monday on the morning tide, or so her booking says." He straightened his coat. "Funny thing about manifests, Hartwell. They're such simple documents. So easy to check."

He walked away into the crowd, toward the Black Maria where Adeline Voss was climbing in with her head high, and Edmund stood alone under the lamp with the singing going on around him, and felt the two halves of his life close over his head like water.

One berth. Monday tide. His sister.

And now a man who knew it.
