## 17. Calloway's Bargain Renewed

The note came by a boy in a cap too big for him, who pressed it into Edmund's hand outside the bakery on Stokes Croft and was gone before Edmund could ask who sent it. The paper smelled faintly of pipe smoke. The hand was neat, unhurried, the hand of a man who never had to hurry because everyone came to him eventually.

*The Anchor public house, Redcliff Street. Four o'clock. Come alone, Mr. Hartwell. Or should I say—*

And there it stopped, as if the writer had grown bored of his own sentence and left the blade of it hanging.

Edmund read it twice on the pavement while carts clattered past him and a woman scolded a child and the city went on being a city, indifferent to the small detonation in his chest. *Or should I say.* Calloway didn't need to finish sentences. He let you finish them yourself, which was crueler, because whatever ending you supplied would always be worse than his.

He thought about not going. He actually stood there for a full minute composing the refusal—a man like him could vanish; he had done it before; he had a talent for it that had once felt like freedom. But Nell's face rose up, unbidden: her jaw set at the same angle as his own, her voice in the orchard at midnight saying *I won't take what you won't give freely*, and behind her face, fainter, Adeline's, standing in her doorway with the light behind her, trusting him with things that could hang her.

There were only two people in the world whose ruin would cost him anything, and both of them lived inside Calloway's reach.

So he went.

* * *

The Anchor was the kind of public house that existed to be forgotten—low beams, a fire burning peat even though it wasn't cold, three men playing dominoes who never once looked up. Calloway sat in the back corner with his back to the wall, which told Edmund everything about how the man worked. He never put himself where he couldn't see the door. He wore no uniform today, just a grey suit going soft at the elbows, and he had two glasses of stout on the table before him, one pushed slightly toward the opposite chair, an invitation arranged in advance so that refusing it would require an act of hostility rather than simple manners.

"Mr. Hartwell." He said the name pleasantly. He said it the way a man reads a label on a bottle he intends to test for forgery. "Sit down. You've had a walk."

"I've had a tram."

"You've walked the last quarter mile. I watched you decide whether to come in." He smiled without warmth. "You have a walk, Mr. Hartwell, when you're thinking. I've noticed it about you. You pace when other men fidget. It's why you make such a good driver. Hands steady, mind moving."

Edmund sat. He did not touch the stout. "You wanted something from me. Say it and let me get home."

"Home." Calloway tasted the word. "Which home is that this week?"

The dominoes clicked. Somewhere behind the bar a tap ran and stopped. Edmund kept his face still—he had spent years learning stillness, stillness was his trade, stillness was the coat he wore over the man nobody knew—and Calloway studied that stillness with the patience of a fisherman watching a float.

"Here's where we are," Calloway said at last. "I'll be plain with you, because I find plainness loosens people faster than threats. Threats make them brave. Plainness makes them arithmetic." He turned his glass a quarter turn on the wood. "Ruth Ellery is in Horfield Prison. Mrs. Voss will join her within the fortnight—the warrant's drawn, I'm only waiting on a signature that likes to take its Sundays. The fugitive question is settled. What remains open—" he lifted one finger, "—is the conspiracy question. Who helped. Who carried. Who knew."

"And you think I knew."

"I think," Calloway said, "you are the most interesting irregularity in my file. A census entry that doesn't hold water. A man whose birthplace changes depending on who's asking and when. A driver with no history before 1904 that any registrar can find. And now—" the finger came down gently, almost kindly, on the tabletop, "—a sister. Ellen Byrne, of Cork, booked passage out of Bristol on Saturday's tide under a name that cost someone money to arrange. Manifests are government documents, Mr. Hartwell. Forged manifests are a crime. Harbouring fugitives is a crime. And conspiracy binds all its participants together like wet rope—one pull and every knot shows."

Edmund's mouth had gone dry. He made himself reach for the stout anyway, because a man who wouldn't drink looked like a man who needed courage, and he drank slowly and set it down and said, "I don't know what you're describing."

"No," Calloway agreed. "You describe it beautifully. That's what I'm telling you. You're very good at not knowing things. Better than anyone I've met in nineteen years of policing, and I once worked with a man who genuinely did not know he'd murdered his wife until we showed him the photographs." He leaned forward. "Here is the arithmetic. Sit still for the arithmetic."

Edmund sat still. His heart was not still. Nothing in him was still except the surface, and he understood suddenly—with the clarity of a man falling who finally sees the ground—that this was the moment his whole creed had been walking toward. History happens to other people. He had believed it for thirty years, built his life inside it like a hermit builds inside a wall, and here was Inspector Calloway informing him, with two glasses of stout and a soft grey suit, that history had been happening to him all along, had been happening through him, through his hands and his wheels and his silence, and the bill had arrived.

"Plead against Mrs. Voss," Calloway said. "Testify to what she harboured, what she financed, what she asked of you. Give me Ruth Ellery's network, the names in Cork, the names in Liverpool. Do that, and your census irregularity becomes a clerical error. Your manifest becomes someone else's forgery. Your sister sails on Saturday a free woman, and you remain a man with no history—which, between us, seems to suit you."

"And if I don't."

"Then I arrest you Thursday morning for conspiracy and document fraud, and I arrest Miss Byrne at the gangway on Saturday, and she goes into custody as an alien under a false name, which means deportation at best and at worst—" he shrugged, delicately, "—she goes back to Cork, where there are people who remember her, and questions they'd like answered about where she's been and who paid for her. You've seen the state of the dockside Irish, Mr. Hartwell. You know what a girl alone with a mark against her name can expect. Workhouse wards don't turn girls away. That's the tragedy of them."

The room seemed to tilt very slightly, the way a deck tilts. Edmund heard his own breathing and made it slower.

"You'd use a child's hunger," he said. "That's what this is. She was hungry when she was nine years old and you'd feed her to the machinery to make me talk."

"I'd do nothing," Calloway said. "That's the whole art of it. I simply tell you what the machinery does, and then you operate it. I've found people prefer it that way. They can sleep after, telling themselves the lever was their own idea or somebody else's fault, whichever gets them through the night." He finished his stout and set the glass down with a click that sounded final, a door latching. "You have until Thursday. Not because I need the time. Because you do. Men like you always want one more day to become the kind of person who does what they were going to do anyway."

"What makes you think I'll do anything?"

Calloway stood, buttoning his jacket, and for a moment the lamplight caught his face fully and Edmund saw—not cruelty exactly, but a kind of exhausted professional tenderness, the look of a vet with a difficult animal. "Because I've read your letters," he said.

The floor dropped. Prowse's ledger had been bad; this was worse; this was the bottom of the world opening under his feet a second time in one season.

"Not all of them," Calloway went on, mildly, collecting his hat. "A few. Enough. A man who reads strangers' mail for years, who lives inside other people's histories because he has none of his own—that man isn't loyal, Mr. Hartwell. He's hungry. And hungry men negotiate." He set a coin beside the untouched second glass. "Thursday. The Anchor, or my office at Bridewell, whichever feels less like surrender to you."

He paused at the edge of the lamplight.

"One thing more, since you'll lie awake composing speeches. Whatever you decide, decide it knowing this: I don't want Mrs. Voss ruined. Ruin makes martyrs, and martyrs make more of them—I've enough work. Testify to the small truths and I can charge her lightly, fine her, embarrass her, done. Lie to me wholly and I must charge her wholly. The truth, sized correctly, is mercy. Think of it as—" he smiled his bloodless smile, "—a regiment for the orchard. Prune, and the tree lives."

Then he was gone through the low doorway, and the dominoes clicked on, and Edmund sat alone with two glasses of stout and the wreckage of every exit, examining each one and finding a person impaled on it.

* * *

He walked. Not toward home—toward nothing, along the Floating Harbour where the cranes stood against the dusk like gallows that had learned to lift instead of drop. The gas lamps came on one by one along the quays, each with its small stutter before it caught, and he counted them because counting was a way of not deciding.

Testify against Adeline. He turned it over. Adeline, who had given him trust the way other people gave alms—not because he'd earned it but because she could not imagine the world otherwise. Adeline in the orchard saying *my sister died waiting for me to be brave*. If he testified even the small truths, the sized-correctly truths, he would save himself and Nell and hand Adeline to the machine piece by piece, a finger, an ear, a name. And she would stand in the dock and look at him—she would know, she would understand instantly, she had spent her life reading men's silences—and that look would be the last thing history ever wrote about either of them.

Refuse, and Nell went back to Cork, back to the hunger he'd only just learned had made her, back into the story their mother had buried and he had dug up too late. Nine years old in a workhouse ward. He tried to picture it and could only picture his own mother's hands, which meant he was picturing wrong, which meant the picture would keep him awake forever.

And there was a third door, thin as paper, painted on the wall. He had known it was there since the quay, since the arithmetic of berths and tides. One berth. One name on a manifest that could be made clean. One morning tide, and Calloway watching the docks—or believing he watched them, if Prowse's pen could be persuaded to blur the record one more time.

If Edmund Hartwell ceased to exist on Friday night—if the man with no history finally surrendered the only thing he had, which was nothing—

Nell sailed free. No testimony required. No betrayal purchased. The bargain Calloway offered was a trap with two doors, but a trap only holds what walks into it, and Edmund had spent thirty years learning how to be invisible. He could disappear so completely that the case against everyone else lost its keystone. A conspirator who cannot be produced cannot be sworn. Let Calloway keep his Thursday appointment with an empty chair.

But staying—staying meant the trial, meant the witness box, meant telling the truth at last in the one place built for lies. Staying meant Adeline might yet be saved by what he knew about Prowse, about the blackmail, about the ledger that had started all of this. Two roads, and both of them ended with him giving up his name; the only question was which road let Nell sail, and only one did.

He stopped at the railing above the water. Below him the harbour lay black and patient, holding the reflected lamps like coins at the bottom of a well. Saturday's ship would ride at anchor somewhere out there in the dark, taking on cargo, indifferent, its berths filled and emptied by paperwork.

Every exit costs a person, he thought. So choose which person you are willing to be.

It was nearly ten when he reached the house. Light still burned in Adeline's study—she kept late hours now, writing letters he sometimes carried and no longer read, because some trusts you stop testing. He stood in the yard a long moment, looking at that lit window, memorizing it the way you memorize a face at a station platform.

Then he went around to the kitchen door, took down the lantern, and walked out into the orchard to find Nell before the doubt could finish dressing itself as prudence.

She was already there—of course she was; she slept badly these nights, she'd told him, listening for boots. She saw his face by lantern-light and went still, the way animals go still.

"They've offered me a bargain," he said. "And I'm going to refuse it. But refusing it costs more than they think, and less than you'd fear, and I need you to hear the whole of it before Saturday, because after Saturday there's no version of this where we both stay." He held the lantern higher, so she could see he was not lying, that whatever else was true of him the eyes were true. "Nell. There's one berth. And I've decided whose name goes on it."
