## 18. The Forged Manifest

Prowse kept his ledger in a satchel that smelled of pipe smoke and vinegar, and he guarded it the way other men guarded their wives.

Edmund found him at half past seven in the morning, in a chandler's office near Prince Street where the census man did his private business—private business being, as far as Edmund could tell, the business of knowing things about people and letting them know he knew. The office was narrow and smelled of tar. A window looked out on the water, grey and moving, and beyond it the masts of the *City of Cork* rose like a forest of gallows poles.

"You've got nerve," Prowse said, not looking up from his papers. "Coming here."

"You've got nerve," Edmund said, "keeping a second book."

That made him look up. He was smaller than Edmund remembered from the doorway in Clifton, a soft-bodied man with ink-stained fingers and the eyes of someone who counted everything twice. His gaze went to the satchel under his arm, then back to Edmund's face, doing arithmetic.

"I don't know what you mean."

"Household on Jacob Street. The widow Kearns. You wrote her name down twice—once for the census, once for yourself." Edmund had spent two evenings with Adeline's files, with the lists of women whose husbands had vanished into prisons or emigration or graves, cross-referenced against what he'd seen of Prowse's rounds. "She pays you four shillings a month so her son's desertion doesn't reach the right ears. I'd wager there are others."

The silence stretched long enough that somewhere below them a ship's bell rang the hour.

"There are others," Prowse said finally. His voice had gone flat, defensive. "Eleven households in my district alone. Men who don't want certain names read aloud in certain rooms. Do you think I invented this trade? Every clerk in this city keeps a second book. The police keep one. Your mistress keeps one—I'd bet my pension she does, all those letters going out under three different hands."

"She's not my mistress. She's my employer."

"Is she." Prowse smiled without warmth. "And which name is yours today, Mr. Hartwell?"

The question landed exactly where he meant it to. Edmund felt it like a hook behind the ribs. He sat down across the desk because his legs suggested it, uninvited.

"I want to change an entry," he said. "In your book."

* * *

They argued for an hour. It went the way arguments go when both men are frightened and neither will show it—low-voiced, circular, punctuated by the sounds of the harbour waking up beneath the window: crates dropped, gulls screaming, the groan of a hull taking cargo.

"The manifest isn't mine to alter," Prowse said. "It goes through the shipping line, through the purser, through—"

"But you copy it. You told me yourself, when we spoke outside the Voss house—you transcribe the passenger lists for your own records. Names, ages, ports of origin." Edmund leaned forward. "Because names are your stock in trade. And because a man who knows everyone leaving England is a man who can sell that knowledge to anyone asking—including Inspector Calloway."

Prowse's hand moved to the satchel. Not consciously; Edmund watched him do it anyway.

"He's already asked," Prowse said quietly. "Calloway. Tuesday last. Wanted to know if any Irish women of a particular age had booked passage recently. Wanted descriptions." He rubbed his jaw. "I gave him nothing yet. But he'll come back, and he doesn't leave empty-handed, ever. That's the thing about Calloway people don't understand. He's not clever. He's patient. Patience is worse."

"So you'll give me Nell Byrne instead."

"I'll give you—" Prowse stopped himself, disgusted. "Listen to yourself. You're learning my language. Give, take, trade. This is what we are now, you and I. Two men haggling over a girl's name like it's a bolt of cloth."

"It is a bolt of cloth. It's whatever we write it to be. That's the whole filthy truth of it, isn't it? You proved that with eleven households. A name on a page, and a person becomes someone else entirely. Someone safe."

"A person becomes someone else," Prowse said slowly, "and the someone they were is left holding nothing. That's the part nobody writes down. The widow Kearns paid me four shillings a month and her son still drinks himself blind every night in Cardiff, because changing a name doesn't change a man. It only changes what the world can do to him." He looked out at the water. "Sometimes that's enough. God help us, sometimes it is."

He opened the satchel.

* * *

The manifest lay between them on the desk, ruled lines neat as piano keys. Third-class passengers, the *City of Cork*, sailing on the morning tide Thursday. Forty-one names. Edmund read down the column and saw the future arranged in pencil: forty-one lives reduced to surname, Christian name, age, occupation, last parish.

Nell's name wasn't on it. She couldn't afford the berth—not fully—and the berth she'd been saving toward had been sold out from under her by a family who'd arrived with cash and no history. She had eleven pounds and some shillings in a tin sewn into her coat lining, and the agent had told her Thursday morning was her last chance before prices rose with spring traffic.

One berth remained. One. And Calloway's men were walking the quays in pairs since Monday.

"Here," Prowse said, tapping the pen against a blank line at the bottom. "You understand what this costs you. If this comes undone—if anyone pulls the thread—the forgery points back to whoever handled the manifest. Me. And you, if you're fool enough to be standing next to me when it unravels."

"I'm fool enough for most things lately."

"That's not courage. That's just a man running out of ways to be careful." Prowse wrote nothing yet. He turned the pen in his fingers. "There were two berths yesterday. Did you know that? Two. I checked, because after our conversation I thought—I thought maybe you'd want to send yourself off too. Clean name, clean start. America's generous that way. It asks nothing about your past except that you bring it with you and shut the door."

Edmund said nothing. The harbour light came through the dirty window and lay across the manifest in a long pale bar, illuminating half the names and leaving half in shadow.

"One berth," Prowse repeated. "So before I write anything, you tell me plainly: whose name goes in this space?"

* * *

Edmund had rehearsed the answer on the walk down. He had rehearsed it on the walk home from the orchard three nights ago, when Adeline had stood among the bare trees with frost on her collar and said, *Whatever you're carrying, Hartwell, put it down or put it to use.* He had rehearsed it lying awake in his room above the mews, listening to the city breathe.

But rehearsal is a liar's craft, and the truth came out clumsy, unrehearsed, in his own voice:

"Hers. Nell Byrne. Age twenty-two. Last parish—" he faltered, because he didn't know it, because he'd known his sister for nine days and his mother for twenty years and had never once asked the right questions of either. "Queenstown. Queenstown, County Cork."

"And your name?"

"Goes nowhere. Stays off the page. Stays in Bristol." He heard how it sounded—the flatness of it, the finality, like a door closing in a house he was still standing inside. "If Calloway wants me, he knows where I am. If the law wants a name for what I've done, it can have the one I was born with. But Nell sails Thursday under a clean name, and no thread leads from her to this room or that ship or any of it."

Prowse studied him for a long moment. Then he bent over the manifest and began to write, the nib scratching in the quiet like something small and determined chewing its way out of a wall.

*Nell Hartwell,* he wrote. Edmund flinched.

"Not Byrne?"

"You want a clean name, give her a true one. Half of forging a life is choosing which truths to keep." Prowse didn't look up. "Byrne is the name strangers gave her—that's what you told me. Let her carry the one her mother gave her blood. Nobody in New York will know the difference, and someday, if she ever wonders, she'll know it was hers all along."

Edmund stared at the wet ink. *Nell Hartwell. 22. Domestic servant. Queenstown, Co. Cork.*

His mother's name, travelling to America inside his sister.

"Prowse." His voice cracked and he let it. "Why are you helping me? Truly."

The census man capped his ink bottle with great care, as if the answer might spill otherwise.

"Because in nineteen years of keeping books," he said, "every entry I've altered has been for money or fear, and every one of them has sat in me like a swallowed stone. Eleven stones. Maybe twelve after Thursday." He closed the satchel. "You're the first man to ask me to write something down that might actually save somebody. I find I'd like to try it once, before the stones get too heavy to carry."

He held out the pen.

"Counter-sign. Bottom of the page. If we hang, we hang together—it's only fair."

Edmund took the pen. His signature came out steadier than he deserved.

* * *

He walked back along the quay with the tide turning below him, brown water sliding seaward, dragging at the moored hulls like a slow patient hand. Thursday morning. Thirty-six hours.

He should feel lighter. He had done the thing—he had bought his sister's freedom with the only coin he had left, which was his own name, his own safety, his own place on the page. Somewhere in the arithmetic of the past week he had accepted the exchange without noticing the moment of acceptance, the way a man accepts cold water by degrees until suddenly he is swimming and cannot say when he decided.

But walking past the customs shed he saw one of Calloway's men—Sergeant Doyle, broad and unhurried, notebook in his pocket—standing at the gangway of a moored steamer, talking to the purser. Not hurrying. Taking notes.

Patience is worse.

Doyle looked up as Edmund passed. Their eyes met for a half-second, ordinary as weather, and Edmund nodded the way a driver nods, the way an invisible man nods, and walked on with his heart hammering against the forged name in his pocket.

At the corner by the tobacco warehouse he stopped. Turned back. Looked at the *City of Cork*'s black funnel smoking faintly against the grey sky, getting ready.

Thirty-six hours. One berth. One name on a page that was his mother's and his sister's and, as of this morning, his own sacrifice written in another man's hand.

All he had to do was get Nell to the gangway without Calloway reading the truth off either of their faces.

And then, from behind him, a voice he knew—Adeline's voice, breathless, urgent, calling his name across the cobbles.
