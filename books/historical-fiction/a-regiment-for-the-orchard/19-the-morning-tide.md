## 19. The Morning Tide

The tide turned at ten past six, and the fog with it, peeling back from the harbour in grey ribbons like a bandage coming off a wound. Edmund stood at the foot of the gangway with his hat pulled low and the ticket burning a hole through the lining of his coat, as if paper could generate its own heat. Perhaps it could. He had carried enough secrets to know that documents were never as weightless as they claimed.

Nell walked beside him, and he was aware of her the way a man is aware of a lit match in a powder store—every step she took, every breath of her beside him, felt like a thing that might go off.

She wore the coat Adeline had given her, a good wool coat two seasons out of fashion, with the collar turned up against the river damp. Her hair was pinned under a borrowed hat. She looked, Edmund thought, like a woman who had never been hungry, never been workhoused, never been given a name by strangers. She looked like what the manifest said she was: Miss Eleanor Hartwell, twenty-two years of age, spinster, of independent means, bound for New York.

His name. His surname. He had given it up on the forged page with a strange lightness, as though it had always belonged more to her than to him anyway. What use had he ever made of it? Twenty-nine years of being nobody in particular, of standing behind motor cars and carriage doors and other people's conversations, and now the name went to America in a girl's mouth where it might finally mean something.

"You're doing that thing," Nell said.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you look at me like you're memorising me for later."

"I am memorising you for later."

"Then stop it." Her voice cracked in the middle of *stop* and she covered it by adjusting her glove. "It makes it feel like a funeral."

"Isn't it? A small one."

"No." She stopped walking, and he had to turn back to face her, and there she was—his mother's eyes set in a stranger's face, or perhaps not a stranger's at all anymore. Three weeks. It had taken three weeks for the word *sister* to stop sounding foreign in his head. "Funerals are for things that are finished. This isn't finished. You'll write."

"I'll write."

"And I'll write. And when I've saved—" She did the arithmetic she always did, the sums she performed aloud the way other people prayed. "Eighteen months. Maybe less if the wages hold. Then you come over. There's room in America, Edmund. That's the whole point of America. There's *room*."

"There's one berth this morning."

"That's not what I mean and you know it."

The whistle gave a single short blast across the water, testing itself, and Nell flinched. Edmund took her elbow and steered her toward the gangway before she could plant herself properly. He had learned that about her already: if you let her stop moving, she would argue; if you kept her walking, she would grieve quietly and save her arguments for the letter she would certainly write him from mid-Atlantic, listing every reason he was a fool, every one of which would be correct.

At the bottom of the gangway a man in an oilskin checked tickets with the bored efficiency of someone who had sent ten thousand people away and would send ten thousand more. Edmund handed over the passage paper—the forged one, Prowse's careful hand, the ink still faintly smelling of the candle it had dried beside—and watched the man glance at it, glance at Nell, find nothing worth finding, and nod her through into the machinery of departure.

She got three steps up the plank before she turned.

"Edmund."

"Nell—"

"If Calloway asks you anything, anything at all, you tell him the truth. Do you hear me? Not the clever truth. The plain one." Her knuckles were white on the rail. "You're too good at the other kind. It's going to be the death of you."

"The truth is expensive."

"So is everything else worth having. Go on." She said *go on* the way their mother must once have said it—to a child, to a husband, to a country—meaning *leave me*, meaning *I can't watch*, meaning both.

He stepped back from the gangway. He watched her take the steps upward, one hand trailing the rope rail, and he thought absurdly of the orchard, of Adeline saying that trees planted crooked spent their whole lives leaning toward the light trying to fix it, and he wanted to call out something about staying straight, staying level, but his throat had closed around all the words he owned and would only release the ones he didn't.

At the top she turned again. The crowd was thickening along the rail now—emigrants hanging over the edge like laundry, calling down last instructions, last loves, names of streets and mothers and dead fathers. Somewhere a fiddle was playing badly. A child was crying in the flat mechanical way of children who have cried so long the crying runs on without them.

Nell didn't cry. She gripped the rail and searched the crowd below until she found him, and when she found him she went very still, the way she'd gone still the first day on the quay when she'd seen her own face wearing his expression.

He mouthed it. He didn't trust his voice, so he shaped the words with his lips alone, slow, deliberate, the way one speaks to the deaf or the dying: *Go and be somebody.*

She shook her head. She mouthed back: *Already am.*

And then the whistle blew long and full and terrible, drowning whatever else either of them said or tried to say, and the gap between ship and quay widened by inches, then feet, then a distance no voice could cross without shouting, and Nell shouted. He heard his name tear out of her above the engines and the fiddle and the weeping child—*EDMUND!*—one syllable of it cracking clean off, and half the quay turned to look, wondering who this Hartwell was who was loved that loudly.

He raised his hand. He did not wave, exactly. He held it up, palm out, the way you'd hold it against glass, against fire, against the last inch of something, and he held it there until the smoke came between them, and then after the smoke, because he knew exactly where she would be standing, starboard side, third rail from the stern, and he looked at that empty patch of air and held his hand up to it as though the ship were still there, as though she could see him, until the tugs took her and the whole grey length of her swung out past the point and the gulls rose screaming off the water like torn paper.

Then the quay was just a quay. Crates and hawsers and men with barrows. The ordinary morning resuming around the hole where his entire future had been.

Edmund stood for a while longer than was sensible. A porter asked him twice if he was waiting on someone. The second time, Edmund said, "No one now," and the porter gave him the particular nod men give the recently bereaved and moved off.

He put his hands in his pockets. One of them contained the return half of nothing—a tram token he wouldn't use, a stub of pencil, and the letter. Nell's letter, unsent, undeliverable, written in her fierce slanted hand the night before and pressed on him at the door of the house with instructions to read it only when the ship was gone. He had obeyed her, God help him, because she was the only person alive whose orders he had never once thought to question.

He unfolded it now, standing among the crates, the fog lifting off the water in the first weak gold of morning.

*Edmund,* it began. *If you're reading this the ship has gone and you've done the stupid brave thing, which is the only kind you know how to do.*

*I'm not going to thank you properly because if I start I'll ruin the ink, so instead I'm going to tell you something true. All my life I thought I was left. Left at Cork, left in the workhouse, left by a woman who chose another child over me. And then you turned up on the quay looking like the inside of my own head, and I understood she didn't choose you over me. She just lost us both, differently. You were left too, Edmund. Left in a big cold house full of other people's letters. Well. No more.*

*History happened to you this month. I watched it happen. Don't you dare go back to being invisible now.*

*Nineteen months. Count them. I will be counting them louder than you.*

*Your sister,
Nell*

He read it twice. On the second reading his vision blurred, and he blamed the sea air, and no one was there to contradict him.

* * *

From the deck of the *City of Cork*, Nell Byrne—who was, for the length of this crossing and every crossing after, Eleanor Hartwell—watched Bristol shrink to a smudge and then a rumour and then nothing at all.

She had done her weeping early, privately, wedged against the rail in the press of bodies with her face turned into the wind so no one would see, and by the time the Irish coast fell away astern she had finished, and something harder had set in its place. Grief, she had learned in the workhouse, was like weather: you waited it out or you froze. Waiting it out required motion. So she moved.

She learned the ship the way she learned everything—fast, silently, by watching. Where the warm vents were. Which stewards could be charmed into extra bread. Which of the hundred and forty souls aboard had money sewn into their coats and which had nothing but nerve. By evening she had a place in the second-class saloon by right of conversation rather than ticket, telling stories to a homesick cork-cutter's widow who needed someone to talk to more than she needed tea, and the widow decided within the hour that Eleanor Hartwell was a fine girl from a good family, and Nell let her believe it, because the name fit better each time she said it. *Hartwell.* Heart-well. Some clerk's invention, some stranger's guess at a family, and yet it sat against her skin like a coat cut to measure.

Third night out, in the black middle of the Atlantic with the ship groaning through a swell, she stood at the rail under a sky so crowded with stars it seemed less like night than like evidence—evidence of how much room there really was, room beyond all arguing—and she took out the pencil stub Edmund had slipped into her coat pocket without her noticing, because he was always doing things like that, hiding kindness in linings, and she wrote in her head the opening of the letter she would post the moment they made land:

*Dear Edmund. Nineteen months is too long. I have revised it to fourteen. Argue with the ocean if you disagree.*

Below her, the water ran black and endless and patient, carrying her west at eleven knots toward a country that had never heard of census ledgers or inspectors named Calloway or orchards behind houses in Clifton. She touched the collar of the wool coat. She thought of Ruth in a cell, of Adeline in the dock, of her brother ashore with his hat pulled low, walking back into the teeth of it all with nothing but a truthful tongue, which was the most dangerous weapon a poor man could carry.

"Hold on," she said to the wind, to the water, to the boy-shaped absence three thousand miles astern. "Hold on, and count."

The lights of a distant steamer blinked twice on the horizon, like an answer, or like a summons.

Behind her, at the far end of the deck, a man in steerage clothes detached himself from the shadows and began, unhurried, to walk her way.
