## 20. Testimony

The Old Crown Court on Small Street had a smell I would carry for the rest of my life: dust and beeswax and the sour breath of a hundred strangers pressed into benches built for sixty. I sat in the public gallery with my hat in my lap like a man at a funeral who wasn't sure he'd been invited, and watched Adeline Voss stand trial for sedition, harbouring a fugitive, and conspiracy to incite.

She wore grey. She had chosen it herself, Ruth told me later — not black, which was mourning, and not the green and violet of the movement, which the papers would have called provocation. Grey, she said, was the colour of evidence. Of things seen plainly.

I had come to testify against her. That is what Inspector Calloway believed, what his men believed, what the clerk of the court wrote in his book when I presented myself that morning and asked to be entered as a witness for the prosecution. My name went down in ink: Edmund Hartwell, driver, of no fixed address worth recording.

It was the last lie I intended to tell.

* * *

Calloway found me in the corridor before ten o'clock, when the courtroom was still filling and the air smelled of wet coats. He looked better than the last time I'd seen him — rested, or perhaps merely satisfied. A man close to closing a file carries himself differently. He clapped me on the shoulder as though we were colleagues, as though the past three months had been an arrangement between professionals rather than a slow strangling.

"Hartwell." He lowered his voice. "You'll be called third, after Prowse and the landlady from the neighbouring house. Keep it simple. You drove her. You saw letters. You saw the woman Ruth Ellery come and go. Let Mr. Mowbray draw the rest."

Mowbray was the prosecutor, a long grey man with a voice like a filing cabinet. He had spent the first hour of the trial establishing — through the census rolls, through Prowse's ledger, through the testimony of neighbours who had watched the orchard gate — that the Voss house on Pembroke Road was not a home but an engine. Adeline sat with her counsel and listened to herself being dismantled with the expression of a woman reading someone else's obituary and finding it inaccurate but not entirely unwelcome.

"She'll be bound over regardless," Calloway said. "But your evidence makes it certain. Two years, maybe eighteen months if the judge is merciful. And you walk out of here clean. Your irregularities—" he tapped his notebook, where my name lived alongside the word *discrepancies*, "—are forgotten. Considered settled. That's the bargain, Hartwell. It hasn't changed."

"I know," I said.

"Then why do you look like a man attending his own trial?"

I didn't answer. Down the corridor, a door opened and closed, and somewhere beyond it Nell was sitting on a bench in the witness waiting room, because I had asked her to be there — asked her, God forgive me, without telling her yet what I meant to do. She had sailed eleven days ago. I had stood on the quay and watched the ship take her out past the lightship until her face was smaller than a coin, and I had thought: now there is one person in the world who knows my mother's eyes, and she is safe, and whatever happens next happens only to me.

That thought had lasted four days. Then Calloway's letter came — not a summons, an invitation — reminding me that a brother's passage could be traced, that manifests could be re-examined, that a girl sailing under a forged name was still, in the eyes of the law, a girl who did not exist, and nonexistent girls could be fetched back across an ocean by men with warrants.

The berth had saved her once. It could not save her twice unless I made sure no one ever looked again. And the only way to make no one look was to give them something else to look at.

Me.

"Calloway," I said. "When I go up there. Whatever I say. Will you promise me one thing?"

He smiled the smile of a man who thinks he has already won. "You're in no position to negotiate."

"I'm the only position there is." I held his eye. "Leave the manifest alone. Leave the girl alone. She's nothing to you — she never was. Your case is Voss. Take your case."

Something moved behind his face then, quick as a fish under ice. He was not a stupid man; that had always been the trouble with him. "What are you going to say up there, Hartwell?"

"The truth," I said, and watched him understand it one heartbeat too late.

* * *

They called me just before noon. The usher said my name — *Edmund Hartwell* — and I heard it spoken aloud in a court of law for the first time, and understood that I had spent thirty-one years borrowing it.

I took the oath with my hand on the book and felt nothing holy in it, only the weight of paper. Mowbray rose, smoothing his papers, already halfway into his opening question — *You were employed as a chauffeur and courier by the defendant during the spring of this year?* — when I spoke over him.

"Before we begin," I said, "there are things the court should hear, and they are not what anyone expects."

Mowbray paused. The judge — old Justice Fenner, whose spectacles kept sliding down a nose like a parsnip — peered down at me. "Witness will answer questions put to him."

"Yes, my lord. But I'm asking leave to make a statement first. Because if I wait to be asked, I'll be asked the wrong questions, and everyone in this room will be comforted by the wrong answers."

A murmur went along the benches. In the dock, Adeline turned her head and looked at me for the first time all morning — really looked — and I saw her understand, in that instant, that whatever I had walked in here intending, it was not the script Calloway had written. Her face did not soften. Adeline's face never softened when it mattered; softness was for orchards and dead sisters. She gave me one small nod, the kind she used to give when handing me a letter she knew I shouldn't read, and I took it as permission.

Fenner sighed the sigh of a man whose lunch was receding over the horizon. "Make your statement, witness. Briefly."

And so I told them.

I told them about the letters. How I read them — every one, for two years, steaming them open on the kitchen range and sealing them again with a thumb warmed on the iron. I said it plainly, without decoration, because decoration was the thing I had hidden behind all my life and I was finished hiding. *I read other people's mail because history happened to everyone but me, and their envelopes were the only windows I had.* I heard myself say it and realised how small it sounded, how shabby, how true. Somewhere in the gallery a woman laughed — one short note, contemptuous — and I let it stand. It was an honest laugh. It deserved company.

I told them my name was not entirely mine either — that I had let discrepancies stand, that I had lied to a census taker named Prowse in April, that I had lied since to an inspector of police more times than I could count, and that I had become so good at lying that lying had begun to feel like breathing, which is the most dangerous condition a man can develop.

Then I told them about Prowse.

* * *

Here is what Prowse had told me on the night I went to confront him, in the back parlour of a public house on Jacob Street where he drank his supper alone among the chalk-smell of ledgers. Here is what I laid out before the court now, piece by piece, while Mowbray's objections piled up and were swept aside by a judge who smelled blood and wanted to know whose.

Mr. Ambrose Prowse, census enumerator for the Clifton district, had discovered in the course of his rounds that certain households had reasons to keep their true circumstances off the record. A widow taking in lodgers above her legal number. A family sheltering a son who should have been deported. A doctor whose qualifications predated a scandal in Leeds. Small secrets, ordinary secrets — the secret tax paid by anyone poor enough or frightened enough to live slightly outside the lines. And Prowse, going door to door with his leather satchel and his government pencil, collected those secrets the way other men collected stamps.

He showed me the ledger himself. I described it to the court: a plain exercise book, marbled covers, half its pages filled in his neat hand with names, addresses, sums. Sums received. Sums promised. Dates going back four years. He hadn't even been ashamed of it — that was the detail I gave them slowly, letting it sit in the room. He had been proud. He considered himself a practical man, a collector of rents on the shadow economy of fear. *Everyone's got a page in somebody's book,* he told me, drinking his mild ale, *the trick is making sure yours stays cheap.*

And one page in that book belonged to the house on Pembroke Road. Not because of Ruth Ellery — Prowse had known nothing of Ruth. Because of me. Because of the discrepancy in my entry, the birthplace that didn't match, the man who claimed to be nobody in particular living in a house full of women who signed petitions. Prowse had priced my silence at two pounds a month and raised it to five after Calloway began sniffing round, because scarcity raises value, and I was becoming scarce.

"And did you pay?" Fenner asked.

"For a time, my lord. Then I stopped paying and started counting instead. There are nineteen entries in that ledger besides mine. Nineteen households paying blackmail to an officer of His Majesty's census. I can name twelve of them. I have copies of the pages — taken without Mr. Prowse's knowledge, which I expect makes them improperly obtained, but there they are." I drew the folded papers from inside my coat, and the ushers came for me the way ushers do, and Fenner waved them off and had the papers passed up instead, and for a moment the whole court held its breath around a handful of stolen arithmetic.

Mowbray was on his feet. "My lord, this is— the witness has been called to speak to the defendant's conduct, not to mount some—"

"Some what, Mr. Mowbray?" Fenner said mildly. "Finish the sentence."

Mowbray did not finish the sentence. He knew, as everyone in that room was rapidly coming to know, that the prosecution's star witness had just turned the trial inside out like a pocket.

* * *

They broke for luncheon. In the cells below the court, where they held me once it became clear I was less a witness than a co-defendant waiting his turn, a warder brought me bread and cheese and a cup of tea the colour of varnish, and Calloway came down the stairs two at a time looking like a man whose house had burned down with him inside it.

"You absolute fool." His voice was low and shaking. "Do you know what you've done? You've handed Mowbray a reason to collapse the whole case. Blackmail! A census man! The jury will spend the afternoon wondering if anything out of that house is credible, including the letters, including the testimony, including—"

"Including the truth?" I said. "Good. Let them wonder. Wondering was what I did for years, Inspector. It's overrated."

"You had immunity. You had a clean walk. All you had to do was say what you saw."

"What I saw," I said, "was a woman keeping faith with people the world had thrown away. What I did was read her post, lie to your officers, buy a forged manifest, and burn a letter that might have hanged her. If you want testimony about the Voss household, that's the testimony. I'm not giving you the version where she burns and I stroll."

He gripped the bars. Up close his eyes were bloodshot, and I thought — absurdly, in the way thoughts arrive at useless moments — that he looked tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep. "Hartwell. The manifest. If you say its name in that courtroom, I will have that girl dragged off whatever pier she's standing on within the month. Do you understand me? I don't care about New York. I care about order. And order means the record stands as written."

"The record," I said, "stands as written. That's rather the point of today."

He stared at me, and I watched him try to find the threat behind the sentence and fail, because there wasn't one. There was only a man who had decided, finally, at cost of everything, to stop editing himself.

"The judge will have you for perjury," Calloway said at the stairs.

"Probably," I agreed. "Along with everything else. It's a long list. I wrote most of it out myself last night, to save the court time."

For one strange half-second, something that might have been respect crossed his face — the look a huntsman gives a fox that has doubled back through its own trail — and then he climbed the stairs and was gone, and I ate my bread and cheese, and it was the best meal I had eaten in years, because I had earned it honestly.

* * *

The afternoon belonged to cross-examination, and Mowbray came at me like weather.

Had I not, he asked, admitted under caution in June to burning correspondence? I had. Did I deny it? No. Was the court to believe that a self-confessed thief of private letters, a liar on oath-adjacent documents, a man who had accepted immunity from the prosecution — he let the word *immunity* fall like a coin on marble — was suddenly a reliable narrator?

"No," I said.

Mowbray blinked. "No?"

"No, sir, you are not to believe I'm a reliable narrator. You are not to believe anyone is. You are to believe the ledger, which you may inspect; the twelve households, whom you may summon; and Mr. Prowse, whom I note you have not recalled to the stand, though he sits in your witnesses' room this very minute, because you know what he'll say if you put him under oath twice." I turned, deliberately, and looked toward the bench where Prowse sat in his good coat with his satchel on his knees, grey as porridge, sweating like a jug in summer. "Go on, Mr. Mowbray. Ask him about the sums."

The judge asked him about the sums. Prowse lasted forty minutes on the stand, and by the end of it the marbled exercise book was in evidence, and two of the blackmailed households had been sent for, and Mowbray's case against Adeline Voss had developed a hole amidships that no amount of bailing would mend.

Through all of it she watched me. When I stepped down — not dismissed, detained, the difference explained to me gently by the tipstaff — our eyes met across the well of the court, and she mouthed one word. Not *thank you*. Adeline did not trade in thanks; thanks implied debt, and she hated debts between friends.

She mouthed: *Finally.*

* * *

They remanded me overnight in Horfield, in a cell with a slop bucket and a window the size of a hymn book, and I lay on the plank bed listening to the city breathe beyond the wall — trams, a dog, the far-off clang of the dry dock — and I took inventory of myself the way a man counts coins by touch in the dark.

Name: compromised, but mine. Liberty: gone, probably for a year, possibly two. Money: eleven shillings and a watch that ran fast. Family: one sister, eleven days at sea, carrying a name I had forged for her onto a continent that had never heard of any of us.

It was the strangest accounting of my life, because by every measure I had ever used — safety, invisibility, the smooth running of other people's histories — I was ruined. And I lay in the dark smiling like an idiot, because for the first time since I was a boy watching my mother pack a trunk and lie about Cork, history had happened *to me*. I had walked into it on my own feet. I had signed my own page.

Sleep took me like a tide takes a swimmer who has finally stopped fighting.

In the morning they brought me back to Small Street, and the usher called my name again, and Justice Fenner peered down over his parsnip nose and said the words I had been waiting for since the quay:

"Edmund Hartwell, you will stand up and hear the charges against you."

I stood. And as I rose, the door at the back of the court opened, and a warder came down the aisle with a folded paper in his hand, moving with the particular urgency of a man carrying news that outranks the proceedings, and the whole room turned to watch him come — and I felt, before he reached the bench, before anyone spoke, the shape of what he carried.

A telegram. Transatlantic. Addressed to a prisoner awaiting sentence.

To me.
