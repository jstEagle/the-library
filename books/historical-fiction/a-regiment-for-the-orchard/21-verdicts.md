## 21. Verdicts

The courtroom smelled of wet wool and lemon polish, and Edmund Hartwell sat in the dock listening to the sound of his own life being weighed out in other people's sentences.

It was the third day. The benches were full — suffragists with their buttons hidden under coats, a scattering of reporters who wrote *militant circles* when they meant *women*, and one man in the back row whom Edmund had clocked on the first morning and never stopped watching since: Mr. Prowse, the census taker, hat on his knee, hands folded, wearing the face of a man attending a funeral for someone he'd helped along.

Edmund had learned, over three days, to read the judge the way he once read letters not addressed to him. Justice Marlowe had a habit of tapping his pen twice before he decided against you and once before he decided for you. He had tapped twice at the mention of the attic. Twice again at Ruth's arrest. But this morning, when counsel had raised the matter of the warrant — that Calloway's men had entered through the orchard gate without a second signature, that the search of Adeline Voss's private papers had therefore been conducted on ground the law did not properly stand upon — the pen had tapped once.

Once.

Edmund let himself breathe out slowly and kept his face still, because he had learned that too. In the dock you learned everything. You learned how long a man could stand without shifting his weight. You learned which constables chewed mint and which drank gin before their shift. You learned that your own name, spoken aloud by strangers in a formal room, sounded like a stranger's name after all — *Edmund Hartwell* — and that hearing it did not hurt anymore, because it had stopped being a lie somewhere between the orchard and the witness box, and had become simply what he answered to.

The foreman of the jury stood up at twenty past two.

He was a grocer from Redcliffe with flour still ghosting the creases of his collar, and he read the verdict in the flat voice of a man reciting a delivery order, which somehow made each word land harder than any oratory could have.

*Not guilty.*

The word went through the room like current through wire. Somewhere behind him a woman made a sound — half sob, half laugh — and there was a rustle of movement as people half-rose, remembered themselves, sat back down. Adeline did not move at all.

She stood very straight in her place, chin level, hands folded, and she looked — Edmund searched for the word and found it and disliked it and used it anyway — *vindicated*, which she would hate, because vindication implied the trial had been legitimate, and Adeline Voss had spent four days demonstrating with terrifying courtesy that the whole apparatus was illegitimate, root and branch, and now the apparatus had agreed with her about her own innocence and thereby proved her point better than she ever could.

Her eyes found his across the courtroom. She did not smile. What she did was worse and better: she gave him a single slow nod, the nod of a woman acknowledging a debt she intended to repay in full, in kind, at a time of her own choosing. Then the usher touched her arm, and she turned and walked down through the crowd like a ship parting water, and the women closed around her, and Edmund lost sight of her green scarf, and something in his chest that had been clenched since the raid finally, cautiously, unclenched.

*Not guilty* on a technicality, the newspapers would say tonight. Calloway would say worse. But Adeline would say — Edmund knew exactly what she would say, he could hear the voice already, dry as autumn leaves — *"A technicality is only a truth the government forgot to outlaw yet."*

Then the clerk said his name, and the room's attention swung to him like a lighthouse beam, and the clench came back.

* * *

They had offered him the bargain twice more while the trial ran, and both times he had refused the shape of it they wanted.

Calloway had come himself, the second time, standing in the corridor outside the cells with his hat held correctly and his voice pitched for reasonableness. "You cooperated on Prowse. The Crown remembers cooperation, Hartwell. It also remembers ingratitude. Testify to the conspiracy — the meetings, the letters, the fugitive in the attic — and you walk out of here with a caution and your name clean."

"My name is the one thing I've got left," Edmund had said. "And it isn't for sale."

"You haven't got a name," Calloway had replied, not cruelly, which was the worst way. "That's rather the point of all this. You're a man who borrowed one. I'm offering to make the loan permanent."

And Edmund had looked at this tired, patient, dangerous man — a man who had spent months pulling threads and found, at the end of every thread, either a woman protecting another woman or an official extorting one — and had felt something he hadn't expected: pity, thin and unwelcome, like finding a coin in the gutter.

"Inspector," he had said, "when I testify, I'll testify about everything. That's my offer. Take it or put me in the dock next to an empty chair."

Calloway had studied him for a long moment. "Everything," he repeated.

"Everything. Prowse's ledger. The households he bled. My own papers. All of it, on the record, where no one can trade on it quietly again."

"That's not cooperation. That's confession."

"It's the same thing," Edmund said, "if you mean it."

He meant it. That was the strange discovery of the last month — that meaning it changed the weight of it entirely. When he had lied to Prowse on census day, the lie had been a coat worn lightly. When he had lied to Calloway in the parlour, the lie had been armor. But standing in the witness box with his hand on the book, telling the truth in order — the false name, the read letters, the burned page, the forged manifest, Nell — the truth had come out of him steady and plain, and it had cost nothing at the moment of speaking, and everything afterward, and he had walked down from the box lighter than he had climbed into it.

The prosecution had wanted him as a blade against Adeline. He had declined to be sharp in that direction. So they had used him as a broom instead, sweeping the Prowse affair into daylight, and the scandal had done its work sideways: two magistrates recused, one inquiry opened, and the case against Adeline quietly rotting from below even before the warrant technicality felled it outright.

Now, at the end, the judge peered down at him over his spectacles.

"Edmund Hartwell." A pause; the pen hovered. "You have pleaded guilty to forgery of a shipping document and to making false statements to officers of this court. You have given evidence which this court has no reason to doubt and considerable cause to regret having needed." Another pause. Edmund watched the pen. "In the circumstances — your assistance to the prosecution, the coercion documented in the matter of Prowse, and your evident desire to make restitution — the sentence of this court is six weeks' imprisonment, time served to be reckoned, and a fine of ten pounds."

Six weeks. Ten pounds. The words drifted down like snow. Behind him, someone exhaled audibly. Six weeks for a man who might have drawn two years, and everyone in the room understood the arithmetic, and most of them, Edmund suspected, thought he had gotten away with it.

Only Adeline, if she were still in the building, would know that he hadn't gotten away with anything — that the sentence was the cheapest item on the bill. The real price had been paid at the quay, in the space between one whistle blast and the next, and it had been paid by a girl on the deck of a steamer screaming a name at a shrinking shoreline.

The bailiffs took his arms. He didn't resist. There wasn't anywhere to go that wasn't this.

* * *

Bristol Gaol in March was cold in a way that seemed personal. His cell was eight feet by six, with a straw tick, a stool, a slop bucket, and a high barred window through which he could see, if he stood on the stool and tilted his head, a slice of grey sky and, on clear days, the tops of masts beyond the wall — ships going places without him, which he had decided to find funny rather than otherwise, because the alternative was not funny at all.

He had been inside nine days when the letter came.

Letters to prisoners were rare and scrutinized. The warder brought it at exercise hour, held it up to the light as though treason might be printed between the lines, read the address aloud with suspicion, and finally handed it over with the grudging air of a man surrendering contraband.

Edmund turned it over in his hands and did not open it right away.

Because it was addressed to him.

Not to *the prisoner Hartwell*. Not to *E. Hartwell, care of the governor*. To *Mr. Edmund Hartwell*, in ink, in a hand he knew — a hand that had learned its letters late and never quite forgiven them, the capitals too proud, the small letters crowded together like people huddling against weather. He knew the hand because he had seen it once, filling out the landing card on the quay, her fingers shaking, writing the name he had given her.

*Hartwell,* she had written. Not Byrne. He had told her to take whatever name kept her safest, and she had looked at him across the gangplank rail with their mother's eyes and written *Hartwell*, and the purser had stamped it, and that had been the last thing he saw before the smoke took the deck.

Now the same hand had carried a letter across an ocean and delivered it, via God knew what chain of cousins and countrymen and paid carriers, to a cell in Bristol.

He sat down on the stool. He opened it carefully, because some things deserved care.

*Dear Edmund,* it began, and he had to stop there for a moment, just look at those two words, because in thirty years no one had ever put his name at the head of a letter meant for him alone. He had read thousands of letters. Every one of them had been somebody else's news, somebody else's love, somebody else's grief, arriving through his hands like cargo. This was the first one that was his. The first piece of mail in the whole history of the post that existed because someone, somewhere, wanted Edmund Hartwell specifically to know a thing.

*Dear Edmund. I am in Fall River, Massachusetts, which is a mill town and smells like a mill town, so don't imagine I'm living in a palace. Mrs. Doherty from the passage committee met me at the boat and I have work in the spinning room and a bed in a house with eleven other girls and I have never in my life eaten so much as I eat here, three times a day, whether I'm hungry or not, which took some getting used to, I can tell you.*

*I am sending this with Sean Boyle's cousin who goes back and forth on the cattle boats, so it may reach you in June or it may reach you at Christmas, and either way I'll have written you six more by then, so keep the warder friendly.*

*I want to tell you what the sea was like but I'm saving it, because I'm going to tell you in person someday and I don't want to spend it on paper. I'll just say this. When land went down behind us I cried until I was sick, and then I stopped, and then I stood at the rail and I made myself look at where we were going instead of where we'd been, and do you know what I thought? I thought: he gave me his name and his berth and his country, and the least I can do is use all three well. So that's what I'm doing. I'm using them well. The name fits. It turns out I was always a bit of a Hartwell, whatever the registers say.*

*The money you sewed into my coat bought the winter boots and the rest is in the bank at the corner, drawing interest, which is a thing I have learned exists. When you come out — and you will come out, because you told me once that you'd learned to count exits and this one has your number on it — write to me care of the address below, and I will write back faster than any letter has ever traveled, and we will argue about Mother the way we argued on the quay, which is to say thoroughly and without finishing, because some arguments are how people stay family.*

*Adeline writes to me. Don't ask how. She says you planted the orchard and that the trees are in and that she has taken to calling it yours, which I believe, because she doesn't give things away lightly. Plant one more tree than you need. That's an order. It's for me, and I'll be checking.*

*Your sister,*
*Nell*

*P.S. The man at the bank asked me my father's name for the ledger. I said Hartwell. He wrote it down and the world did not end. Imagine that.*

Edmund read it four times. On the fourth reading he discovered he was laughing, quietly, in a cell, at the postscript — at the sheer ordinary miracle of a clerk's pen scratching a name and the world declining to end — and the warder glanced through the grate with the expression of a man deciding some prisoners were best left unexamined.

Then he folded the letter along its creases and held it in both hands, and the laughter went out of him, and what replaced it was quieter and would last longer.

Six weeks. Time served to be reckoned. Which meant — he worked the arithmetic the way he'd once worked fares and tide tables — perhaps three weeks more, if the fine was paid, and it would be paid, because Adeline did not acknowledge debts without settling them. Three weeks of grey sky and mast-tops. And then the gate, and the street, and the walk up to the house behind Clifton with its new-turned earth, where forty apple trees stood in rows with his boot prints between them, waiting for spring to decide whether they'd live.

And one more tree to plant. Her order. He was already composing the argument he'd make about variety — a russet, obviously, hardy, good keeper, like herself — knowing full well he'd lose the argument to a letter written months ago, and being glad of it in advance.

He stood on the stool and looked out at his slice of sky. Beyond the wall, faintly, a ship's horn sounded, coming in on the afternoon tide, carrying someone home to someone else.

Three weeks, then. He pressed the letter flat and put it inside his shirt, against his chest, where the warder's searches would find nothing but paper, and where it would stay anyway, because that was where you kept the first letter ever addressed to you — the first proof that you had, all along, been a person to whom history happened, and who had finally, at cost, happened back.

He began counting days. For the first time in his life, he counted toward something instead of away from it.
