## 22. A Regiment for the Orchard

They let him out on a Tuesday in March, when the air still had teeth in it but the light had begun to forgive.

Edmund stood at the prison gate with a paper bag containing everything he owned—a spare shirt, the copy of his sentence, and one letter he had read so many times the creases had gone soft as cloth. *Dear Edmund,* it began, in a hand that was nothing like their mother's and entirely like his own. He didn't take it out. He knew it by heart. He had four months of knowing it by heart.

The road to Clifton was longer than he remembered, or perhaps he was simply walking it without hurry for the first time in years. He had spent so much of his life moving through streets like water through a pipe—present, functional, unnoticed. Now a boy selling papers looked up at him and held his gaze a half-second too long. A woman at the corner of Park Street narrowed her eyes, then said, to her companion, "That's him. The one from the trial."

He was, then. Someone. It sat strangely on him, being someone—like a coat cut for a taller man.

Adeline's house came into view the way it always did: too late, all at once, the grey stone rising out of its own garden. But something was different, and it took him a dozen steps to understand what. The orchard behind the house—the neglected one, the ragged one she had tended out of grief rather than hope—was visible from the lane now because the wall around it had been partly taken down. Bricks stacked neatly at the edge of the property. A gap wide enough for two people abreast.

She had opened it. She had opened it toward the city, toward anyone passing, and Edmund stood in the lane feeling his chest do something complicated, because he understood exactly what she was saying. *Nothing hidden here. Nothing hidden anymore.*

He went through the gate and down the side path and there she was, kneeling in the cold mud with her skirts ruined past saving, a trowel in one hand and a bare-root apple sapling across her knees like a sleeping child.

"You're early," Adeline said, without looking up. "I told them Thursday."

"They let me out Tuesday." He set down his bag. "I've been let out on Tuesdays before. It doesn't stick."

"It'll stick this time." She looked up then, and her face was older than it had been in September—he supposed everyone's was—and there was mud on her cheekbone and no apology anywhere in her expression. "Ruth's inside making tea she'll insist is coffee. Prowse sent a crate of grafting stock with a note I'm not to show you, which means you're absolutely going to see it. And—" she hesitated, and he watched her decide not to manage him, which was new, "—there are letters. Three of them. They came while you were inside. I kept them in your room."

"My room."

"The blue room. It was always your room. We were just slow about saying so."

He crouched beside her in the mud. The sapling between them was a whip of a thing, barely thicker than his thumb, roots pale and startled in the open air.

"Where do you want it?" he asked.

"South end. Where the light comes last but stays longest." She wiped her hands on her apron and left worse marks than before. "My sister used to say an orchard is a regiment—you plant them in ranks, you drill them, you lose some every winter, and if you do it properly they outlive every single person who ordered them about." Her voice caught, just slightly, on *sister*. "I never did it properly. I planted this one out of mourning and then abandoned it out of the same. That's over."

"Over," Edmund agreed.

And because they were who they were now—two people who had testified, who had been doubted and believed, who had watched each other tell the truth in a courtroom and survive it—he said what he would once have swallowed whole:

"I'm sorry I burned the letter. In October. When Calloway offered me immunity. There were things in it that would have helped you, and I chose you over the truth, and then I had to spend four months learning that the truth was the better choice anyway."

Adeline studied him for a long moment. Then she laughed—not the drawing-room laugh he'd driven her to a hundred meetings with, but the other one, the real one, rough at the edges.

"I know you burned it. Ruth saw you. We decided it proved you were worth trusting, which tells you something about how desperate we were." She pressed the trowel into his hands. "Dig. Confessions can wait until the hole is deep enough to bury them in."

* * *

The orchard, by April, was a worksite.

Prowse arrived twice a week in a cart that smelled of manure and ambition, having discovered—somewhere between blackmail and bankruptcy—that he possessed an almost religious knowledge of fruit trees and no one left willing to hear about it. He had sold his ledger, he claimed, to a newspaper, though Edmund suspected the newspaper was a drawer and the drawer was under Prowse's bed, where such men keep their consciences.

"Spencer," Prowse said now, straightening from the third row with his hand pressed to his back, "if you plant that Bramley six feet from the Cox, you will be committing murder in approximately 1919. Twelve feet. Trees need room to be strangers before they become neighbors."

Edmund moved the sapling twelve feet. He had learned, these past weeks, that being named was a thing you could practice. *Spencer.* His own chosen name, given honestly to the court, printed in the papers, spoken aloud by a census taker turned reluctant ally. Edmund Spencer Hartwell—the middle name his mother had never used, dredged up from the papers in a biscuit tin, proof that somewhere in her history she had imagined him as more than one word. He was trying it on. Some days it fit.

Ruth worked faster than both of them and complained less than either, which she announced was a condition of her employment and also her entire personality. She had a scar on her wrist from the arrest in November and a habit of touching it when she thought no one was looking, and she had refused, flatly, to discuss the three weeks she'd spent in Horfield except once, briefly, at night, standing at the orchard wall with a cigarette she never lit.

"They asked me who else was in the house," she'd said. "Same question eleven days running. And on the twelfth day I understood that the answer didn't matter anymore, because whatever I said next would be true, and true things don't protect anybody, they only cost what they cost." She'd looked at him across the dark. "You taught me that. Watching you refuse Calloway. I stole it. Don't ever ask for it back."

Now she drove the stakes and tied the young trees to them with soft cord, murmuring to each one something he wasn't meant to hear. Once, passing behind her, he caught it: *"Grow sideways if you have to. Nobody's grading you on straight."*

At noon Adeline called a halt and they sat on the old stone bench against the south wall, eating bread and cheese off a cloth, and Edmund took out the day's letter.

It was the sixth since January. Nell wrote the way she spoke—in bursts, in lists, with the punctuation of someone who'd taught herself to write at nineteen and was making up for lost decades. *Dear Ed* (she had shortened his name without asking; he had loved her forever for it). *The passage was rough and I was sick as anything and I would do it again tomorrow. The land here is so big it makes you feel either free or frightened and some mornings both before breakfast. Mrs. Halloran took me in, she knew Mother's people, small world even when it's an ocean wide. I have work. I will not describe the work, you would worry. Enclosed find a photograph, cost me two bits, don't laugh at my hat.*

He passed the photograph around. Nell stood on a boardwalk in front of nothing in particular, squinting into American sun, wearing a hat that could reasonably be described as architectural. She was laughing at whoever held the camera. At him. Their mother's eyes, aimed at the future instead of away from it.

"She looks happy," Ruth said.

"She looks tall," said Prowse. "Sea air. Or the hat."

Adeline said nothing for a moment, and Edmund watched her hold the photograph with both hands, carefully, the way she held everything that mattered.

"When I was seventeen," she said, "my sister and I swore we'd go to America together. We had it planned down to the ship. Then Father died and someone had to stay, and she married a man from Bath, and I stayed too, and we both forgot we'd ever promised each other an ocean." She handed the photograph back. "Your sister kept the promise. For all of us who didn't. Tell her that, next time."

"She knows," Edmund said. "She says it back to me. Every letter ends the same way." He could recite it; he did recite it, silently, the way you touch a stone in your pocket. *You gave me the boat, Ed. Everything after is mine. Write me about the trees.*

* * *

May brought the first blossom, and with it, uninvited, Inspector Calloway.

He appeared at the orchard gate on a Saturday morning in civilian clothes that fooled nobody, hat in hand like a parishioner, and for one bad second Edmund's body relived every conversation they'd ever had—the polite voice, the questions shaped like kindness, the arithmetic of who costs what.

"Calloway," Adeline said coolly, not rising from her row.

"Mrs. Voss." He looked at the ranks of young trees, the turned earth, the three workers with dirt to their elbows, and something in his face shifted that might have been envy. "Retired, as of February. The service and I disagreed about the Prowse matter. Specifically about how many years it should have been, and whose superiors signed off on ignoring it for a decade." He cleared his throat. "I've taken a cottage in Leigh Woods. I'm told—" and here he looked, briefly and unbearably, like a man asking to be let into a room he'd spent his career locking, "—that you accept volunteers."

The silence lasted long enough for a blackbird to finish an entire opinion.

"Third row," Adeline said finally. "There's a spade with a cracked handle. If you can stand working alongside a man you tried to imprison, and a woman you arrested, and a census taker you threatened, then the regiment takes all sorts."

Calloway nodded once, precisely, and went to find the spade, and Edmund watched him go and found—to his own surprise—that the sight caused him no pain at all. Just a kind of tired wonder. People were capable of arriving. That was the whole discovery of his life, arrived at late: people could arrive.

"He'll be unbearable within a month," Ruth said.

"Within a fortnight," Edmund agreed, and handed her the next sapling.

* * *

In June, a letter came that wasn't from Nell.

It was addressed to *Mr. E. S. Hartwell, care of the Voss residence, Clifton Down*—his full name, correctly rendered, in handwriting he didn't know—and it took him until evening, sitting alone on the orchard bench with the light going gold, to open it.

*I hope you'll forgive a stranger writing. My name is Margaret Byrne, and I was a friend of your mother's, in Cork, before either of us had sense. I knew Nell in the workhouse years—I did what little I could, which I have regretted was little ever since. Nell writes to me from New York. She speaks of you the way people speak of weather that broke a drought. She says you gave up your name and your berth for her, and that you stayed to tell the truth, and that neither act made you proud, which she says is how she knew they were good acts.*

*Here is why I write. Your mother, before the end—she wrote to me. One letter, near the last. She asked me to tell you, if I ever found you, that the name she gave you—Hartwell—was her mother's name, and her mother's mother's, and that it belonged to women who kept orchards in County Armagh before anyone in the family could read or write. She said she took it from you when she left, and she wanted it returned.*

*I am an old woman and I may be remembering wrong. But I think a name is a small thing to return, and a large thing to receive.*

*With respect, M. Byrne*

Edmund sat on the bench for a long time.

Behind him the young trees stood in their ranks, staked and tied, each one a small stubborn argument that next year existed. Ahead of him the wall was down and the city went about its noise, and somewhere beyond the noise, beyond the water, his sister was laughing under a bad hat in a country that had room in it for strangers becoming neighbors.

His mother had taken things from him his whole life—the truth, the family, the simple dignity of being known—and in one paragraph, posthumously, through the hands of a stranger, she had given something back. Not forgiveness. He didn't want forgiveness; he suspected it would have been worthless currency anyway. Something better: a lineage. Women who kept orchards. A regiment stretching back further than any lie he'd ever been told or told.

He folded the letter along its worn lines and put it in his breast pocket, over the heart, where he kept everything now instead of hiding it in drawers.

"Ed." Ruth's head appeared around the tool shed. "Adeline wants a decision on the damson row before she loses the light, and Prowse has opinions, and Calloway has already broken the spade."

"Already?"

"He says the handle was compromised. He says it with great authority. Come arbitrate before there's a schism."

Edmund stood. He brushed off his trousers, out of old habit, then stopped and let the dirt stay—it was his dirt, from his rows, on his knees—and walked back into the orchard where his strange, improbable family argued about drainage in the last of the sun.

He was Edmund Spencer Hartwell. He had a name that had crossed an ocean and a sister who wrote to him and a place where history, at long last, had happened to him personally, and continued happening, season upon season, in ranks, in blossom, in the patient grammar of roots.

The damson row went in where the light came last but stayed longest.

It was, everyone agreed, exactly right.
