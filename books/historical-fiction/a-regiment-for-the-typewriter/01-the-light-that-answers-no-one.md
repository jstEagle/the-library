## 1. The Light That Answers No One

The wind arrived before the dark did, the way it always did in November, coming up from the southwest with a smell of salt and something older beneath it—kelp rot, cold stone, the mineral breath of the sea itself. Petra Marchetti stood on the gallery of the lighthouse at Isola del Corvo and let it push against him like a hand testing a door.

"Early this year," he said aloud. His voice went nowhere. Seven years he had lived alone on this rock, and seven years he had kept up the habit of speaking to no one, which is to say to Lucia, which is to say to himself. The words did not need an answer. That was the point of them. A man who talks only when someone might reply learns to say very little; a man who talks into wind can afford to be honest.

Below him the island lay black and wet, all nine hectares of it—a spine of basalt running east to west, a stunted grove of wind-bent pines huddled in the lee of the tower like sheep behind a shepherd, the keeper's cottage with its one lit window, the boat landing where the sea spent its days trying to unmake what his grandfather's generation had built. Beyond that, nothing but water until Genoa, thirty kilometers of open gray water, invisible now, present always. He could feel the city the way an old scar feels weather: as a pressure, a dull insistence at the edge of things.

He went inside. The stairwell smelled of lamp oil and brass polish and, faintly, of bread—the loaf he'd baked that morning still cooling on its cloth in the cottage below, though the scent had climbed the ninety-one steps to find him, the way good smells do in a small house, the way bad ones climb faster.

The lamp room was warm. It was always warm; the great lens threw heat like a hearth even when the flame was trimmed low for daylight maintenance. Petra took off his coat, hung it on the iron peg that had held his father's coat before his, and set to work.

This was the hour he loved best, though he would never have said *loved* out loud, because love was a word that had cost him enough already. Four o'clock in the afternoon in late November, when the light outside went from pewter to slate to nothing, and the work began: wicks first.

There were five of them, concentric, fed from a common reservoir of colza oil, each with its own brass collar and its own temper. The easternmost wick always burned unevenly—he had never determined why, had replaced it twice, had finally accepted it as a fact of the world, like the tide tables or the dishonesty of barometers—and so he trimmed it first, while his hands were steadiest. The scissors were old, English-made, their handles worn to the shape of his thumb. He snipped the charred crown away in small, patient bites, rotating the wick a quarter turn with each cut, until the top presented a perfect dome to the flame that would soon sit upon it.

"You'd have hated this," he told the empty room. "All this fuss over a piece of string."

Lucia had been impatient with ritual. She had eaten standing up, read three books at once, finished none of them, laughed at priests, and once—in the first year of their marriage, when he had shown her how he oiled the clockwork that turned the lens—she had said, *Petra, you talk to machines more gently than you talk to me,* and he had not known then whether it was a complaint or a compliment, and she had died before he could ask her which.

That was the wound. Not her dying—dying was the sea's oldest trade, and everyone on this coast paid its tariff sooner or later—but the not-asking. Seven years, and there were questions stacked inside him like cordwood, dry and ready and burning nothing.

He lit the wicks in order, east to west, using the long brass taper, and watched the flames take: blue at the base, gold above, steadying. Then he pumped the reservoirs, checked the oil gauges, wiped each lens panel with a chamois dampened in spirits of wine—forty-eight panels, each one polished until the room's lamplight passed through it without a smudge, because a smudge meant a shadow in the beam, and a shadow in the beam meant a gap in the warning, and a gap in the warning meant, someday, a ship on the rocks with men aboard who had trusted this light with their lives.

He did not think of it that way most nights. Most nights it was simply the work, the way kneading is simply the work of bread. But some nights—cold clear nights, when the beam went out over the water and seemed to reach the horizon itself—he thought of it exactly that way, and stood very still among the turning glass, and felt the strange weight of being the last small human argument against the dark between here and everywhere.

The clockwork needed winding. He took the crank in both hands and turned, counting the revolutions under his breath—one hundred and twenty, the number his father had taught him, the number his father's father had used—and the great mechanism gathered itself and began its slow rotation, and the beam swung out through the storm glass and began its long patient sweep of the water. One flash every seven seconds. Corvo Light. On the charts since 1841. In the logbooks, in his handwriting, every night of the last seven years.

* * *

At six he went down to the cottage to eat.

The cottage was two rooms and a kitchen, stone-walled, low-ceilinged, built to shrug off weather rather than to please anyone. He had furnished it badly at first, in the raw years after the funeral, buying nothing, moving nothing, letting Lucia's things stay where they were as if the rooms were a museum and he only the caretaker. Then, around the third year, something had shifted—not grief easing, exactly, but grief settling, the way silt settles in a harbor, and he had begun to live in the house instead of beside it. He had moved her sewing table to the window where the light was good, though no one sewed there. He had put his own books next to hers on the shelf, spines touching. He cooked at her stove, slept in their bed, drank his evening wine from her family's glasses, the heavy green ones with the chip on the rim that she had always turned away from guests.

Tonight: bread, anchovies packed in salt from the mainland, a soup of beans and wild fennel that he made the way she had taught him in the second autumn, when she had stood behind him at this same stove and put her hands over his on the knife and said, *No, smaller, like this—you chop fennel the way you trim your wicks, too careful, life needs rougher cuts than lamps do.* He chopped roughly now. It was as close to prayer as he came.

He ate at the table by the window, facing the water, because facing the wall felt like hiding. Through the glass, beyond the black shoulder of the headland, he could see the light sweeping—flash, seven seconds, flash—and beyond it the sea, which tonight was not yet angry but was thinking about it. The swell had come up through the afternoon with a long, deliberate rhythm, eight seconds between crests, then seven, then six, the intervals shortening the way a man's footsteps shorten when he has decided to cross the room. Old keepers said you could read a gale hours before the barometer admitted it, just by watching how the water walked.

Petra opened the logbook.

The logbook was a ledger bound in gray canvas, one volume per year, forty-one volumes on the shelf going back to his grandfather's hand. He wrote in the fair, upright script the maritime office demanded: wind direction and force, barometric reading, state of sea and sky, lamps lit and extinguished, any vessels sighted, any irregularities. Tonight's entry began ordinarily enough.

*18 November 1913. Wind SW freshening, Force 6 by 1800. Barometer falling steadily, 29.4 inches and dropping. Sea moderate, swell increasing from SW. Sky overcast, no stars. Lamps lit 1645, all burning well.*

Then, because the office never read past the columns and because the columns were not where he kept his real records, he added the line he added every night, in the same small letters, at the bottom of the page:

*Spoke to L. She did not answer. Same as yesterday.*

It was not entirely true, and he knew it. Some nights she answered—or something answered, some movement of his own mind wearing her voice, some phrase she had actually said once, resurrected by loneliness and applied to the moment at hand. Tonight, trimming the eastern wick, he had heard her clearly: *You always did talk to machines more gently than to me.* And he had answered her, out loud, in the empty lamp room: *Machines don't leave.* And then had stood appalled at himself, scissors in hand, because that was cruel, and she hadn't left, she had died, and cruelty toward the dead was a luxury he permitted himself perhaps twice a year and always regretted.

He blew on the ink, closed the book, and sat listening to the house.

A house on an island teaches you its vocabulary. The pine beam over the stove ticked as it cooled. The shutter on the north window worked its single loose nail back and forth, a small wooden pulse. Under everything, the sea on the landing rocks, sorting and re-sorting its stones, a sound like slow applause from an audience that never leaves.

Seven years. He had built this contentment the way he built anything: deliberately, plank by plank, checking each joint. Wake at five. Trim, light, log. Bake on Tuesdays. Row the circumference of the island on calm Fridays whether he wanted to or not, because wanting was a weather and the body needed a climate. Speak to Lucia. Do not drink past the second glass. Do not look longer than a minute at the photograph of her on the mantel, because a minute became an hour became a day lost to the country of her face, and the lamps did not trim themselves.

It was a small life. He knew that. The supply boat came twice a month with flour and oil and newspapers three days old, and Bruno Ferretti, who piloted her, teased him about becoming a fossil, and Petra laughed and meant none of it and meant all of it. But smallness, he had learned, was not the same as emptiness. A lighthouse was small too, against the sea. What mattered was whether the thing you built could hold its light.

The barometer fell another tenth while he washed the dishes.

* * *

By nine the wind had found its voice.

It came in gusts now, ragged, testing the tower the way a boxer tests an opponent in the first round—two hard blows, a pause, two more from a different quarter. Rain followed, fine at first, then in ropes. Petra climbed the stairs again at ten for the midnight watch's preliminary check, and stood in the lamp room with the storm shutters half-closed, watching the water.

He had seen forty winters on this rock, first as a boy, then as assistant to his father, then as keeper in his own name. He knew the sea's repertoire the way a priest knows his parishioners: the ordinary sins, the occasional miracles, the rare and memorable catastrophes. This was shaping toward catastrophe. Not tonight, maybe, but soon—the swell had come around fully to the southwest, and the barometer stood at 29.1 and trembling, and the gulls had gone inland hours ago, all of them at once, which the fishermen called *the birds' vote* and never argued with.

"Early," he said again, to the glass, to Lucia, to whoever kept the accounts of such things. "The big ones don't come until the new year. Someone forgot to tell this one."

The beam swept out and swept back. In its light he could see rain moving across the island in silver curtains, could see the white water starting to show at the reef line east of the landing, a mile of submerged rock announcing itself in bursts of foam. Somewhere out in that darkness were ships. There were always ships—the coastal steamers running between Genoa and the southern ports, the fishing fleet hugging the shallows, the tall ships that still came down from the north with canvas and pride, the smugglers, though no one official mentioned smugglers anymore, the trade having supposedly died with the last century. All of them threading this coast blind, trusting the little chain of lights—Corvo, Portofino, the Camogli mole—that some solitary man kept trimmed and turning.

He checked the clockwork. Checked the oil. Wiped the spray from the lower storm glass with his sleeve. Routine. Ritual. The planks of the small life, holding.

On his way down, he paused on the landing of the stairwell, as he did every night, at the small niche in the stone wall where Lucia's photograph stood in a frame of black walnut. It was the only photograph of her on the island; the rest he kept in a drawer he did not open, for reasons he had never examined closely. This one showed her at twenty-six, on the landing rocks, squinting into sun, hair escaping its pins, one hand raised either against the glare or in greeting—it was impossible to tell, and he had decided long ago that the ambiguity was the truest thing about the picture. She had been laughing a moment before the shutter clicked. He knew because he had been the one holding the camera, and he remembered the laugh, and the way she had grabbed his elbow right after, and said, *Well? Did you catch me?*

*Every day,* he said to the photograph, as he said every night. *Caught every day since.*

The wind hit the tower then, harder than any gust before it, hard enough that the whole structure hummed—a deep note in the masonry, felt in the teeth more than heard—and somewhere below, a shutter broke its moorings and began to bang. He stood in the dark stairwell with his hand flat against the cold stone, feeling the building speak, and thought, with the calm of long experience: *This is the front edge of it. By tomorrow night we'll know what kind of storm this is going to be.*

He secured the banging shutter, weighted it with the spare block of stone kept for the purpose, and went back up.

* * *

Midnight. The watch change that consisted of himself, relieving himself.

He logged the readings: wind SW, Force 9 in the gusts, barometer 28.8, sea high, spray over the lantern gallery. The pen scratched in the lamplight. Outside, the world had been reduced to sound and violence—the roar of the swell detonating on the reef, the shriek of wind through the ventilators, the drumming rain—and inside, the small golden order of the lamp room, the lenses turning, the flames steady, the ink drying straight.

He was halfway through writing *vessels sighted: none* when he stopped.

Because there was a vessel. Far out, southeast, right at the edge of visibility—a light. Not a star; the sky was solid cloud. Not the Portofino light, which bore northwest. A single white light, low on the water, appearing and disappearing behind the swell, and moving—slowly, laboriously—northward. Toward the coast. Toward the worst water on this stretch of shore, with the storm still building and hours yet before its peak.

Petra set down the pen and watched, counting flashes of his own beam against the movement of that distant light, doing the arithmetic every keeper knows: speed, bearing, drift, the set of the current that ran hard along this coast on southwest gales, pushing everything, gently and irresistibly, toward the rocks.

A fishing boat caught out too long, most likely. They happened; the sea collected its tariff. Usually they ran for the lee of Sestri or made the harbor at Camogli. Usually.

But the light was not making for the harbors. It was holding a course that no fisherman would hold in this weather, straight up the coast, close inshore, as if the man steering it knew these waters intimately and feared something on land more than he feared the sea.

Petra reached for the signal lamp—the powerful Morse lamp mounted on the gallery rail—and began, out of seventy years of habit, to call: *What vessel? Where bound?*

He flashed it three times, four, five, the shutter clicking under his fingers, the beam stabbing out into the rain. No answer. The distant light held on, north, north, and then a swell intervened, and when the sea lifted again the space where it had been was empty.

He waited, watching, for the better part of an hour. The wind rose. The barometer fell. Nothing appeared.

At last he returned to the logbook, and sat for a long moment with the pen poised, and then wrote, in the column for irregularities, the plain truth of what he had seen:

*0100. Single white light sighted SE, low, standing N close inshore. Signaled, no answer. Lost in swell. Weather deteriorating.*

He did not write what else he had felt, because there was no column for it: that the light had looked wrong somehow, hurried, hunted; that a man does not steer a small boat up a lee shore in a rising gale unless he is carrying something worth drowning for.

Outside, the great storm of November 1913 came on in earnest, earlier than any storm in living memory, and the light answered no one, and swept, and swept, and swept.
