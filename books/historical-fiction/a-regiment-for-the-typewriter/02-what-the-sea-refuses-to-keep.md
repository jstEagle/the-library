## 2. What the Sea Refuses to Keep

The storm died the way a great animal dies—not all at once, but in long shuddering intervals, each gust weaker than the last, until by dawn there was only the ordinary breathing of the sea and the extraordinary wreckage it had left behind.

Petra had not slept. He did not count that as hardship; sleep was a country he visited rarely now, and always alone, and he had learned to make do with its borders. He had spent the night as every night: trimming wicks, winding the clockwork that turned the lens, logging wind and swell in his careful hand, speaking aloud once or twice to Lucia because the silence of the tower was not the same as solitude, and he had never quite learned the difference between them.

"The worst I've seen," he told her, standing at the gallery rail while the first grey light came up over the water like something being reluctantly surrendered. "Worse than '06. Worse than the night we met, if you remember, which you would, being dead and having nothing else to do."

He said such things without shame. The dead were owed honesty; it was one of the few debts a man could still settle.

Below him the cove, which at low tide showed a crescent of grey sand and at high tide nothing but black water against black rock, had become a museum of the sea's bad temper. A fishing skiff lay on its side among the boulders, split along its keel like a loaf. Driftwood had been thrown twenty meters inland, past the high-water line, past even the line the oldest storms had drawn. Kelp hung from the rocks in ropes and tresses. And everywhere, everywhere, the flotsam: barrels, planks, a ship's door swinging gently on its hinges where it had lodged between two stones, an entire staircase from some vessel whose name he could not guess, tarred rope coiled on the sand like something alive that had given up.

He went down after breakfast, because duty required it and because seven years of practice had made duty into habit, and habit into a kind of peace. There might be bodies. There were often bodies after such nights, though usually they belonged to fishermen who had gambled and lost, men he could carry up himself and lay out and write letters about. He took the handcart, the sacking, the flask of grappa. He took, out of ritual rather than reason, the coil of good line.

The rocks below the tower were treacherous even in fair weather—slipweed, wet stone, channels that filled and emptied with a rhythm you had to learn the way you learned a language. Petra knew them the way he knew his own hands. He worked southward from the base of the tower, methodically, checking the gullies where the sea deposited things it could not digest: a hatch cover stamped with letters worn to ghosts, half a crate of oranges rolling and bobbing in a pool like a head, a child's shoe that stopped him for a full minute with his hand pressed flat against cold rock until he could breathe again. No body attached to the shoe. He made himself believe that meant something.

It was nearly midday when he found the man.

He was wedged in the third gully—the deep one, the one Lucia had called *la bocca*, the mouth, because at high tide it opened and swallowed and at low tide it gave back whatever it had taken. The sea had given him back now. He lay face-down in the shallows of the gully, jammed between two slabs of stone, and the waves came over him and withdrew and came over him again, rocking him with a horrible gentleness, as if the sea were trying to decide whether it wanted him after all.

He had been young. That was the first thing Petra noticed, before the wrongness of the posture, before the smell. Young, and well-dressed for a drowned man: a coat of good wool, city-made, soaked through but sound, boots that had been resoled recently and expensively. Not a fisherman. Fishermen did not drown in coats like that.

The satchel explained everything and answered nothing.

It was strapped across the man's chest—a flat case of oiled canvas with brass buckles gone green at the edges, the kind of thing a courier wears, the kind of thing designed to survive exactly what its wearer had not survived. It had held. The straps had held. The sea, having spent the whole night trying to tear everything else apart, had left this one object cinched to this one dead man as carefully as a bandolier.

Petra stood in the wash and looked at him for a long time.

He should have covered the face first. That was the order of things: cover the face, then secure the effects, then carry the poor devil up, then write the report. But the face was turned away, pressed into stone, and so it was the satchel his eyes kept returning to, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth.

*Single white light sighted SE, low, standing N close inshore. Signaled, no answer.*

A small boat. A man alone, steering up a lee shore in the teeth of the worst storm in living memory. Carrying something worth drowning for.

"Lucia," he said quietly. The wind took the name and gave nothing back.

He knelt and unstrapped the buckles. His hands were steady—he noted this about himself with a kind of detached surprise, the steadiness of a man doing a job—but the buckle-greens came off on his fingers and stayed there, brass-green stains he would be unable to explain later and would not try to.

Inside the satchel: oilskin wrapping, three layers of it, tied with waxed cord. Inside the oilskin, a packet. Papers, several of them, dry as autumn leaves—the waterproofing had done its work with a fidelity the courier's own lungs had been denied. And on top of the papers, one envelope, sealed, addressed in a fine, upright hand.

The address read:

*Al Custode del Faro — P. Marchetti — Isola di San Venerio*

Not *to the keeper of the lighthouse*. Not *to whom it may concern*. To a name. His name, spelled entire, the whole six syllables of it, arriving out of the sea strapped to a drowned stranger like a verdict.

Petra sat back on his heels in the washing water and let a wave break over his boots and did not move.

* * *

He carried the man up first. Duty has an order, and he clung to the order the way the drowning cling to spars.

It took him the better part of two hours, hauling the body up the goat path on the sacking, resting twice, sweating in the November cold until his shirt stuck to his back. He laid the courier out on the table in the store room beneath the tower—the same table where he cleaned the lamp glass—and closed the man's eyes, which the sea had left open, full of a question it had never gotten around to answering. Young. Twenty-five at most. There was ink on the fingers of his right hand, the stain of a man who wrote for a living.

Petra washed his own hands, then stood over the boy for a long moment and found he could not say the usual words. He said instead, "I don't know who sent you. I don't know what you were carrying. But you carried it here." And then, because the sentence frightened him: "That can be someone else's problem now."

The authorities. That was the obvious thing. The obvious thing had the weight of law behind it: a drowned man is reported to the carabinieri post at Portovenere; the effects are inventoried; the letter is handed over unopened, sealed as it is, sealed as it must remain, and some clerk opens it under lamplight and decides what Marchetti needs to know about it, which is nothing, which is precisely nothing, which is—

Which is why, said a voice in him that sounded uncomfortably like Lucia's, you have not yet written the report.

He made lunch. He ate none of it.

* * *

The afternoon light came low and gold through the store room's seaward window, laying bars across the table, across the dead boy's folded hands, across the packet of papers Petra had set down beside him like evidence in a trial he hadn't been summoned to.

He told himself he was only examining them to know what to write in the report. That was the lie he chose, and he chose it consciously, deliberately, the way a man chooses which coat to wear, and knowing he chose it did not make it fit any better.

The seal on the envelope was red wax, pressed with a device he did not recognize: a circle broken by a vertical stroke, like an eye half-closed, or a lamp seen edge-on through fog. Beneath the seal, no signature—only a single word in that fine upright hand, the same hand that had written his name:

*Custode.*

Keeper.

He did not open it. He wants that recorded somewhere, in some column with no heading: *he did not open it.* He turned it over four times. He held it to the light, which was the oldest trick and told him only that the paper inside was folded in three. He set it down and walked to the window and looked at the sea, which had recovered its manners entirely, blue-grey and innocent, giving nothing away, keeping nothing, refusing everything.

Here is what he could not have explained to any inspector, and so explained to no one, not even Lucia, especially not Lucia:

Seven years ago he had buried his wife on this island because she had asked to be buried here, and he had asked her once—only once, near the end, holding a hand gone light as paper—*Lucia, is there anything I should know? Anything at all?* And she had smiled at him with the smile she used for hiding things, which he had mistaken then, and for seven years since, for the smile she used for loving him, and she had said, *You know everything about me that matters, tesoro.* And he had believed her because believing her was easier than the alternative, and because a lighthouse keeper learns to trust the instruments he cannot check.

And now the sea had delivered an instrument he could not ignore: a sealed letter bearing his name, carried by a dead boy with ink-stained fingers, through the worst storm in living memory, to the one man on the Ligurian coast least equipped to receive it.

If he handed it over sealed, he would sleep tonight. He was almost sure he would sleep tonight.

If he opened it—

He picked it up again. The wax seal caught the low sun and glowed like a coal.

There was a column in his logbook for anomalies. He thought of the entry he would have to write: *One male body recovered, approx. 25 years, no papers. Effects: one satchel, contents sealed, forwarded to authorities.* And beneath it, in the space where anomalies went, the truth he could not enter: *The letter knows my name.*

Because that was the thing. Not curiosity—curiosity he could have mastered, curiosity was a cat and could be put out. It was the name. Someone, somewhere, had known that P. Marchetti, keeper of the light on San Venerio, widower, seven years alone, was the correct recipient of a document a young man had drowned rather than surrender. Someone had trusted him—or condemned him—with perfect confidence, and had never asked his permission, and had used the sea as a postman because the land routes were watched or bought or both.

You do not hand a thing like that to a clerk at Portovenere. You do not know that, and yet you know it, the way you know weather coming off the water before the barometer moves.

"Tell me what to do," he said to the empty room, to the dead boy, to the woman buried on the point where the wind came around the north. "Just once, tell me plainly."

The light through the window shifted. Somewhere above him the lens turned on its bath of mercury, sweeping its patient white arm across the afternoon sea, asking its endless question of no one.

Petra broke the seal.

He would spend the rest of his life unable to say whether the sound it made was small or enormous. Inside, the paper was covered front and back in that fine upright hand, dense, hurried, the writing of a person afraid of running out of time—and it was, it was addressed to him, it began *Custode Marchetti, se questa arriva a te...* If this reaches you...

He read it once standing up. Then he sat down heavily on the cleaning stool, the stool Lucia had painted green the first summer, and read it again slowly, and the bars of afternoon light crawled across the floor while he did not move, and above him the light swept, and swept, and swept.

When he finally looked up, the sun was nearly gone and the dead boy's face had gone blue in the dusk, and Petra Marchetti understood three things with terrible clarity.

First: that Aldo Ferrante, the shipping magnate whose name was spoken in Genoa the way weather is spoken of—respectfully, and with an eye on the exits—had built his fortune on rigged wrecks, on ships sent deliberately to the bottom with their crews aboard, and had bought the officials who should have counted the drowned.

Second: that the papers in this packet could prove it, and that the letter named him—named *Marchetti*, named *custodian by marriage*—as the one bound to carry them to a journalist in Genoa before the courts convened in spring.

Third: that his wife, Lucia Marchetti, who had trimmed lamp wicks beside him for eleven years and sung off-key and lied about the size of the fish she'd caught, had been born into a family whose business had been moving dangerous truths along these coasts long before she had ever moved to a lighthouse—and that she had never told him, or perhaps she had told him constantly, in the language of smugglers' wives, and he had never learned to hear it.

The letter ended with a line that seemed written directly against the marrow of him:

*She trusted you to be the kind of man who asks no questions. Forgive her. Now be the other kind.*

Outside, full dark had come. The great storm was three hundred kilometers away and dying over Corsica, but inside the tower the light swept and swept, and Petra sat in the store room with the papers in his lap and the dead boy on the table, and did not sleep, and did not write the report, and heard—for the first time in seven years—the sea begin, very faintly, to say his family's name.
