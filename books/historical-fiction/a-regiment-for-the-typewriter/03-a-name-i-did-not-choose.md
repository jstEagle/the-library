## 3. A Name I Did Not Choose

The dead man's satchel lay on my table like a thing that had been invited.

I had set it there beside the lamp and then, for a long while, I did not touch it. The oil in the reservoir ticked as it warmed. Rain came against the east glass in handfuls, thrown up from the sea below, so that the tower seemed to stand inside the weather rather than beneath it. I made tea I did not drink. I trimmed a wick that did not need trimming. These are the things a man does when his hands wish to be busy while his mind stands at a door, afraid of what is knocking.

The canvas was dark with salt and swollen with water, but the flap was waxed and buckled tight, and when I finally worked the buckles free — brass gone green, the leather of the straps cracking like old bread — the interior was dry. Dry as a drawer. Someone had loved this bag carefully. Someone had sewn its seams twice.

Inside: a bundle wrapped in oilcloth, tied with tarred string, and on top of it a single envelope. Paper, good paper, the kind that costs more than a fisherman earns in a week. It had been protected inside a second sleeve of waxed linen, and though the corners were soft with damp, the face of it was legible enough to stop my breath.

*To the Custodian, Marchetti, keeper of the light at Punta Scuro.*

Not *Petra Marchetti*. Not even a Christian name. Just the surname, and the light, as if the sea itself kept an address book.

I am not an imaginative man. Seven years alone will cure a person of imagination faster than any priest. But standing there with that envelope in my hands, water dripping off my coat onto the flagstones, I felt the island tilt beneath me — the same sensation as the great swells that come out of deep water without warning, that lift everything you thought was fixed and set it down somewhere else.

Lucia's name was nowhere on it. Lucia's name was everywhere in it.

I knew this the way you know weather coming — not by seeing it but by the ache in old scars. My wife had been dead seven years, drowned off the north point in a squall the barometer never saw coming, and still her name could arrive uninvited, riding on a stranger's corpse, and make my chest tighten as if she'd slipped into the room behind me.

I sat down. I opened the letter.

* * *

The hand was small and precise, a clerk's hand or a schoolteacher's, every letter formed as though the writer expected it to be read under interrogation. Perhaps he had. Perhaps that was the joke of it, that he wrote for the reader who would one day hold it over his body.

*I write this knowing it may reach you soaked, or late, or never,* it began. *If it reaches you at all, then Matteo failed, and I am sorry for the manner of its arrival.*

Matteo. The man in my cellar, wrapped in sailcloth, waiting for the ground to soften enough to dig. I looked toward the stairwell as though he might object to being discussed.

*You do not know me. My name is Tommaso Viali, and I was for eleven years a clerk in the offices of the Ferrante Line in Genoa. For eleven years I copied manifests that lied. I have come to know lies the way a compositor knows type: by weight, by shape, by the sound they make when they fall.*

*In the winter of 1906, the steam collier Assunta went down off Capo Mele with the loss of nine men. You may remember it; the coast remembers its wrecks. What the papers did not print is that the Assunta was insured for three times her worth, that her sea valves had been opened before she cleared the harbor roadstead, and that the man who ordered them opened sits today in a marble office above the Porto Antico and is spoken of in the Chamber as a future minister.*

*Aldo Ferrante does not merely sink ships. That would be crude, and Aldo Ferrante is never crude. He sinks ships that deserve sinking — old hulls, tired crews, men from villages too poor and too far away to ask questions. He buys the inspectors who certify them seaworthy, the magistrates who close the inquiries, the editors who decide what the grief will be called. An act of God, the newspapers say. Heavy seas. A tired captain.*

*I have spent four years assembling the proof. Manifests in Ferrante's own hand. Letters between his office and the harbormaster of Savona. A ledger of payments, initialed, dated, cross-referenced — eleven years of bought men, itemized like cargo. It is all here, in this satchel, in duplicate. One copy goes to Elena Sarti of the Gazzetta di Genova. The other must go where no magistrate can be paid to find it.*

*Which brings me to you, custodian, whoever you are.*

Here the handwriting changed. Not the letters themselves but their pressure — as if the writer had paused, dipped his pen, and pressed down harder, wanting these words to survive rough handling.

*I did not choose you. Lucia Marchetti chose you, six years ago, and I confess I argued with her. A lighthouse keeper, I said. A widower on a rock. She told me I understood nothing. She said: the best hiding place for a truth is in the keeping of a man who has already lost everything and wants only to be left alone. She said you would guard it without knowing what you guarded, because guarding is what you do.*

*By your marriage to Lucia, you are custodian by right and by trust to the enclosed evidence. Not by knowledge — she kept that from you, deliberately, for your protection and for the work's. If you are reading this, she is dead, and I am sorry, and also I am not sorry, because she built better than she knew. She built you.*

*Deliver the satchel to Elena Sarti, Gazzetta di Genova, Via Garibaldi. Do not post it. Do not give it to any officer of the law, any priest, any friend. Tell no one what you carry. Men have died for less than knowing this, and men have died for less than delivering it. If anyone asks how it came to you — and someone will ask — you found a drowned man. That much is true. Let the rest stay true by staying silent.*

*Burn this letter once read.*

*Tommaso Viali*

*Genoa, October 1913*

*P.S. — Lucia said you would hate her for this. She said: he will forgive me eventually, or he won't, and either way the light will keep burning. She spoke of you the way sailors speak of a fixed star.*

I read it twice. Then a third time, standing, because sitting had become unbearable, my legs carrying me around the table in a slow orbit like a gull working a wind. The rain kept its rhythm against the glass. Below, in the cellar, Matteo Viali waited under sailcloth for a grave, having carried across half the Ligurian Sea a message addressed to a man who did not exist.

Because that was the truth of it, wasn't it? The custodian, Marchetti, keeper of the light — that was a person Lucia had invented, the way you invent a character in a story you tell yourself at night. A widower who has lost everything and wants only to be left alone. Guarding without knowing what he guards, because guarding is what he does.

She had described me exactly. That was the wound of it. Not that she'd hidden a life from me, but that she had known me well enough to hide one — known precisely which parts of myself I would supply without being asked, the way a lock supplies the shape of its key.

* * *

I put the letter down and the tower seemed suddenly full of her.

Not ghostly — I don't believe in ghosts, or I didn't; I'll come back to what I believe now. No. It was worse than ghosts. It was evidence. Every ordinary evening of our marriage stood up out of its place and arranged itself differently, the way a room rearranges itself when you move one piece of furniture. Her habit of taking the mail boat to San Remo twice a year "for cloth." Her insistence that we learn no neighbors, keep no guests, that our few friends be chosen as carefully as a man chooses which doors to leave unlocked. The way she would sometimes stand at the north window at dusk, watching the horizon with an expression I took for homesickness, and when I asked what she was looking for she would say, *nothing, amore, nothing*, and kiss me, and I would let myself be kissed instead of answered.

I remembered — God help me, I remembered and had filed it away as nothing — the autumn she came back from the mainland with salt crusted in the seam of her boot, though she swore she hadn't been near the water. The crate of books she kept locked in the cedar chest, telling me they were her mother's, in French, dull devotional works, and how once I caught the chest open and glimpsed ledgers, columns of figures, and she laughed and said her mother had been a grocer's daughter and never stopped counting.

A grocer's daughter. I had believed it because believing was easier than asking, and asking was easier than knowing, and knowing would have required me to admit that my wife was a whole country whose borders I had never crossed.

There was a night — I can date it, the third of February, 1907, because the logbook records a clear sky and a moderate westerly, the kind of entry a man writes when nothing happens, which is when everything happens — there was a night she woke me past two o'clock, sat up in bed with her hair loose and her eyes wide open in the dark, and said, *Petra, if I ever asked you to carry something for me, without questions, would you?*

And I — sleep-stupid, fond, incurious — had said, *What sort of something?*

*Does it matter?*

*No,* I said, which was a lie, because of course it mattered, but I wanted her back against my shoulder and the warmth of her and the morning, and questions are cold things to bring into a warm bed. *No, it doesn't matter. Yes. Anything.*

*Good,* she said, and lay down, and was asleep within minutes, breathing evenly as a tide, while I lay awake wondering what I had agreed to and deciding, in the end, that it had been a dream, or a whim, or a woman talking in her sleep. I decided it was nothing because nothing was what I wanted it to be.

Seven years later a drowned clerk had arrived to collect on that promise, and the interest had compounded terribly.

* * *

Toward dawn the storm eased to a long gray grumble, and I went down to the cellar because the dead deserve tending and because I could not bear the upper rooms a moment longer.

Matteo Viali lay where I had laid him, on boards across two trestles, wrapped in the spare sailcloth. In the lamplight his face had settled into something almost peaceful, the sea having finished its argument with him. Younger than I'd first thought. Thirty perhaps. There was ink on the side of his right hand — a clerk's stain, the same stain Lucia used to scrub at, I realized now, every Sunday evening, scrubbing and scrubbing until her fingers were pink, and I had teased her about it. *What do you write, wife, the names of all your sins?*

And she had laughed, and looked at me a moment too long, and said, *Only the accounts, Petra. Only keeping the accounts straight.*

I knelt by the boards. The sailcloth smelled of salt and of the cellar's cold stone.

"You knew her," I said aloud. My voice sounded strange after days of speaking only to wind and machinery. "You knew her, and she sent you to a dead man's house, and now we're both here, and neither of us chose this."

The dead are excellent listeners. It's the living who fail at it.

I thought about burning the letter. Viali had instructed it, and instruction from a corpse carries a certain authority. The stove was right there; paper burns in four seconds; and with the letter gone I could tell myself the whole affair was a stranger's misfortune — drowned man found, buried, reported to the carabinieri when the boat next came, life resumed, the light kept, the world left to its rotting.

But the satchel remained. And the satchel held the second copy, and the ledger, and the manifests, and whatever else four years of a careful man's courage could fit into oilcloth. Burning the letter would be closing my eyes and calling it darkness.

And underneath everything — beneath the dread and the bewilderment and the hot new anger at Lucia that I kept having to set down like a tool too sharp to hold — underneath all of it was the sentence I could not stop hearing:

*She spoke of you the way sailors speak of a fixed star.*

I had spent seven years believing I was the one left behind. Standing in the cellar with a dead courier and a live letter, I understood for the first time that Lucia had left me something after all — not money, not property, not even an explanation, but a task, and tasks are the only inheritance that keeps a man upright.

I climbed back to the lamp room as the sky went the color of pewter and the sea lay down at last, exhausted, its white mane smoothed. Eastward, far off, a smudge on the horizon that might have been weather and might have been smoke: a steamer making for the coast, or for Genoa, carrying men with questions.

The schedule book said the supply boat was due Thursday. Bruno Ferretti, forty years on the same run, who brought flour and oil and paraffin and gossip in equal measure. Who had known Lucia — had known her longer than I had, I saw now, since before she was mine. Whose eyes had lingered on me last month a beat too long when he mentioned, casually, folding a crate, that there were strangers asking after shipping records in San Remo, officials maybe, very polite, very patient.

I had noted it the way you note a change in the wind — filed, unfelt.

Now I took Viali's letter and folded it along its original creases, and unpicked the stitching inside the lining of my storm coat — the coat Lucia had sewn, double-seamed, her own strong little stitches — and slid the paper flat against the canvas between the layers, and sewed it shut again with waxed thread from the stores, my fingers steady, my heart anything but.

Then I sat by the window with the cedar chest open on my knees, her locked box of French devotional works and grocery ledgers in front of me, and the second key — the one I had found years ago taped behind her drawer and never asked about, never, never — turning slowly in my hand.

The light turned overhead. The sea breathed. Somewhere out there, men were already asking questions, and I had three days to decide whether I was a widower or a custodian.

The key clicked. The lid rose.
