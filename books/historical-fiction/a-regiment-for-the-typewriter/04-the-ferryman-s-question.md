## 4. The Ferryman's Question

The storm spent itself in the small hours of the third morning, the way a man spends his last coin—recklessly, and then all at once there was nothing left but silence and the shame of what had gone before. Petra woke to a sea like poured pewter, flat and dull and pretending innocence, and he stood at the gallery rail for a long time watching it lie.

He had not slept properly since finding the man in the rocks. He had done everything correctly—he knew that, had checked himself against his own procedures as against a printed list. The body had been covered with tarpaulin against the gulls. He had searched it thoroughly, twice, by lantern light, finding nothing beyond the satchel and its sealed letter, a rosary of dark wood worn smooth by someone else's thumb, and a brass watch stopped at twenty minutes past eleven on a night when Petra had been trimming wicks not two hundred meters away, unaware that another man was dying within earshot of his lamp. He had noted the discovery in the logbook in his plainest hand: *One male, drowned, approx. 40 yrs., no papers. Remains secured pending authorities.* The ink had dried while his hand shook, and he had watched it dry and hated himself for shaking, because there was nothing in the log to shake about. A dead man washed ashore. It happened. The sea gave back what it could not keep, and keepers received what the sea delivered, and that was the whole of the law and the liturgy.

Except for the satchel. Except for the letter that lay now in the third drawer of his desk beneath a folded chart of the approaches to Savona, wrapped in oilcloth because he could not stop thinking of water even indoors, even on dry land, even in a house where every seam had been caulked against the weather by seven years of his own hands.

*To the keeper of the Punta Vecchia Light, and through him to the Marchetti name.*

He had read it four times now. Each reading left him colder and more certain of nothing. He did not know what frightened him more—the words themselves, or the fact that someone had known enough to write them, or the third thing, which he kept pushing down below the waterline of his attention like something trying to surface: the fact that he had not yet carried the letter to the authorities in Portovenere, as duty plainly required, and that three days had passed, and that he had begun, without deciding anything, to construct reasons.

The supply boat would come today. Weather permitting—and the weather, having emptied itself, permitted everything. Bruno Ferretti ran the *Santa Chiara* out of Portovenere twice monthly in winter, weekly in season, bringing flour and paraffin, lamp oil and salt fish, newspapers three days old, and conversation measured out like medicine. Twenty-two years Bruno had made this crossing. Twenty-two years he had watched Petra grow from the young keeper who blushed when Lucia sang to the gray widower who spoke to gulls. There was no man on the coast who knew Petra Marchetti better, and no man Petra trusted more, which meant, precisely, that there was no man from whom the letter would be harder to hide.

He heard the engine before he saw her—a laboring, asthmatic thump-and-hiss that carried across four miles of calm water—and went down to clear the landing of storm wrack, mostly because his hands wanted work and partly because he did not want to be standing at the top of the stairs when Bruno looked up at him. There are things a face does at height, under an open sky, with nothing to interrupt it, that it cannot do at the bottom of stone steps with crates between two men.

The *Santa Chiara* came around the point at half past nine, riding low, her hull striped with a tide line of scum. Bruno stood at the wheel in his ruined cap, one hand on the spokes, the other holding a mug he never seemed to drink from. His son's boy—no, wait; the grandson, little Matteo, eight years old and made entirely of elbows—crouched at the bow to take the mooring line, and Petra caught it and made fast, and the boat kissed the stones of the landing with a sound like a closed book.

"Three days of that," Bruno said, nodding at the sky as though the sky were a neighbor who'd let his dog loose. "Worst I've seen since 'ninety-eight. You keep your glass?"

"Barely. It took a shingle off the store roof and half my beans."

"Beans." Bruno said the word the way other men said prayers—without expectation, but faithfully. He stepped onto the landing, and here was the thing Petra had been bracing against without knowing its shape: Bruno did not look at him the way Bruno always looked at him. There was no joke in it, no shorthand of twenty-two years, no *you look thinner, Marchetti, is that any way to honor Lucia's cooking*. Instead the old man studied him—there was no other word for it—studied him openly and at length, the way a customs officer studies a manifest, and over the old man's shoulder the gray water lay flat as a ledger page waiting to be written on.

"You look like a man who hasn't slept," Bruno said.

"I haven't. We had a drowning."

And there it was, said aloud for the first time, and the saying of it changed the weight of the thing, moved it from the drawer into the air where it had mass. Bruno set down his mug on the bollard.

"A drowning."

"Washed up after the gale. In the rocks below the tower. No papers." Petra heard his own voice going flat and official, the logbook voice, and could do nothing to soften it. "I've got him covered. The carabinieri will have to come out for the body—I'll want to send word back with you."

"Yes," Bruno said. But he didn't move toward the boat, didn't reach for the manifest, didn't do any of the hundred practical things that were his nature and his armor both. He stood with his hands hanging at his sides, an old man on wet stones, looking at Petra with an expression Petra had never once seen on that face in twenty-two years, and the expression was fear.

Not fear *of* him. That was the strange part, the part Petra turned over later, again and again, examining it like a coin found in the sand. Not fear of Petra, and not quite fear for him either—or rather, yes, fear for him, but of a specific and practiced kind, the fear of a man who has seen a particular trap spring before and recognizes the ground being laid.

"Petra," Bruno said, and used the Christian name, which he almost never did, "come and sit in the wheelhouse. Matteo—go and see if you can find us any of those beans."

"There aren't any beans, nonno, you just—"

"*Vai.*"

The boy went, elbows and indignation, scrambling up the path toward the tower, and Bruno led the way aboard and shut the wheelhouse door behind them both, which he also never did, because Bruno held that a man who shuts doors is a man hiding something, and had said so for two decades in exactly those words.

Inside, the wheelhouse smelled of diesel and coffee and old rope. Bruno lowered himself onto the bench beneath the fog bell and looked at his own hands for a moment, and then at the window, and then, finally, at Petra, who had not sat down because sitting down felt like agreeing to something.

"Men have been asking questions," Bruno said.

"What men?"

"On the mainland. Portovenere first, then La Spezia—I hear things, you know how it is, a ferryman hears everything, that's the trade, we're priests without the collar." He rubbed his jaw. "Well-dressed men. Not police—police wear their questions like uniforms, you can see them coming. These ones dress like clerks but they pay like princes. Buying drinks. Asking after the islands, the lights, which keepers live alone, which keepers get visitors. Asking—" and here the old man paused, and Petra watched him choose, deliberately, whether to say the next part, "—asking about families. About names. Whether anyone on the water still carried letters for people who paid in silence."

The engine ticked as it cooled. Somewhere above, a gull argued with the wind about nothing. Petra became aware that his heart was going hard and slow, like something being carried downstairs.

"What kinds of letters?"

"The kind that don't go through the post." Bruno met his eyes. "Petra. Who was your drowned man?"

"I don't know. I told you. No papers."

"No papers." Bruno nodded slowly. "In forty years on this water I've pulled six bodies out of the sea, and every one of them had papers, or a tattoo, or a saint's medal, or a sweetheart's ribbon stitched into a pocket—something, always something, because the sea strips a man of everything except what he loved enough to strap down. So either your drowned man loved nothing—" the old man's voice dropped, gentle and terrible, "—or whatever he loved is sitting right now in your tower, and you haven't decided yet whether to give it up."

The wheelhouse seemed to contract around them. Petra heard himself say, "Bruno—"

"Don't answer me." Bruno raised a hand. "Don't say a word, because I don't want to know it, and you don't want me to know it, because what a man doesn't know he can't be made to tell. I'm telling you only this, as one keeper of old debts to another: those well-dressed men are working the whole coast, and they're not working it blind, they're working it off a list. And yesterday in Portovenere I saw a photograph being shown around the taverns—not of your dead man. Of a living one. And the men showing it weren't asking *have you seen him*, they were asking *when will he cross*, which means they already know where he is and they're simply waiting for him to move."

The floor of the world tilted very slightly. Petra put his hand flat on the chart table and felt the paper under his palm, cool and smooth, covered with soundings and hazards, all the places where the water was deep enough to drown in marked with patient little numbers.

"They showed you the photograph?"

"They tried." Bruno's mouth twisted. "I'm old and half-blind and I forget faces the way I forget insults. But Petra—" and now the gentleness went out of him altogether, leaving something harder and older underneath, something that had been forged somewhere Petra had never thought to ask about, "—if it's you on that paper, then hear an old fool who knows more than he's ever told you. Whatever you're carrying, decide quickly, because hesitation is the only cargo that sinks every ship."

They unloaded in near silence. Flour, paraffin, the mail sack with its three-day-old papers, a crate of oranges from Bruno's cousin that had survived the crossing bruised but alive. Matteo came back down the path having found no beans and forgiven nothing, and helped with the lines, and Petra worked beside them with his sleeves rolled and his mind moving along some cold bright channel far beneath the surface of his ordinary thoughts.

Because here was the arithmetic he could no longer refuse to do. If the well-dressed men were asking about letters and names—if they were working from a list, off a photograph, with patience and money—then they were not looking for a drowned courier. A drowned courier was a closed account. They were looking for what the courier had been carrying, and they believed it had reached shore, and there was exactly one island, one tower, one keeper between that water and the mainland. He had told Bruno he would send word to the carabinieri. He should send word to the carabinieri. Every honest instinct of thirty years of service said: *log it, report it, hand it up the chain, let the law carry it from there.*

And every honest instinct had also, seven years ago, buried a wife without ever once asking her why she flinched at the sound of certain engines, why she read by lamplight with the shade turned to the window, why her hands, so soft on his face, were scarred across the knuckles in ways she called girlhood accidents until the year she died, when she stopped calling them anything at all.

He burned nothing. He told nothing. Those two facts he fixed firmly in place, like pins in a chart. But that afternoon, alone in the tower with the body still lying decently covered below and the light unlit in the daylight hours, he took up his sewing kit—the one Lucia had packed for him, with its bone-handled awl and its waxed thread—and he unpicked the lining of his good coat at the left breast, over the heart, where a man keeps the things he means to protect, and he sewed the oilcloth packet flat against the canvas between the lining and the wool.

It took him eleven minutes. He counted them, because counting kept his hands steady. The stitches were ugly; Lucia would have mocked them; he did them anyway, and then a second row over the first, and then he put the coat on and stood in front of the little mirror by the door and saw—nothing. A thin man in a salt-worn coat. A keeper. Nobody.

That was the terrible discovery of the day, the one he carried up to the lamp room at dusk while he trimmed the wicks and lit the mantle and set the great lens turning over the mended water: that hiding the letter had taken less effort than hiding his hands had taken all afternoon. That he was, apparently, good at this.

At full dark the *Santa Chiara* crossed back toward the mainland, her single riding light dwindling down the black miles, and Petra stood at the gallery rail and watched her go and understood, with the particular clarity that comes only after a decision has already become irrevocable, that he had just chosen. Not with words, not with a signature, not even consciously—but chosen. The letter was in his coat. The coat would go where he went. And somewhere across that flat, innocent-looking water, men dressed as clerks were buying drinks and showing photographs and asking *when will he cross*.

He spoke aloud to the empty gallery, to the woman-shaped absence that had lived there for seven years.

"Lucia," he said, "what did you leave me?"

The lamp answered, as it always answered, with its steady white pulse sweeping the dark—and for the first time in his life, Petra Marchetti felt the light go out ahead of him like a warning, touching the far coast where the roads began, and thought: *they will come from there.*
