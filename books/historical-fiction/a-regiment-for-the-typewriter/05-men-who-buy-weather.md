## 5. Men Who Buy Weather

The rain came to Genoa the way Aldo Ferrante came to everything: early, unannounced, and certain of its welcome.

He stood at the window of his office on the fourth floor of the Palazzo Doria—a building he did not own yet but would, within the year, once the widow who held it finished convincing herself she had no better option—and watched the harbor dissolve into gray wool. The masts of his ships stood out of the mist like the ribs of some drowned animal, forty-one of them now, each one a vertebra in the spine of an empire that had begun, twenty-two years ago, with one leaking brig and a debt he'd repaid in ways the creditors still did not discuss.

Behind him, the clock on the marble mantel ticked with the patience of a thing that had outlived three generations of men who thought themselves important.

Ferrante did not turn when the door opened. He never needed to. There were only four people in Genoa who entered this room without knocking, and two of them were dead.

"Read it to me," he said.

Captain Riva crossed the room with the soundlessness that was his single most marketable quality. Ferrante had acquired him eleven years ago from the carabinieri of a mountain province where Riva's talent for finding things—men, ledgers, bodies—had made him simultaneously invaluable and unwelcome. He was not a large man. He had the build of a church warden and the face of one too, mild and slightly apologetic, as though perpetually about to request a donation. Women trusted him on sight. Old men offered him chairs. This, Ferrante had long ago concluded, was worth more than any amount of visible menace. Dogs barked at brutes. Nobody barked at Riva until it was far too late to matter.

"The report from Portofino," Riva said, unfolding the telegram with a small dry sound, like a leaf in November. "The steamer *Aurelia* reports heavy weather off Punta Chiappa beginning at approximately nine in the evening. Winds from the southwest, force ten by midnight. She took shelter in the roadstead and rode it out."

"And the *San Gennaro*."

Riva paused for exactly as long as a man pauses when he is choosing which truth to hand over first. Ferrante had learned to read these pauses the way farmers read clouds.

"The *San Gennaro* has not reported."

"Since when?"

"Since Tuesday, sir."

Ferrante watched a fishing boat fight its way across the harbor mouth, tilting like a drunkard against the wind, and felt nothing in particular. That was the secret no biographer would ever capture, he suspected—the utter absence of drama at the center of things. The newspapers, should they ever come for him, would want passion: rage, greed, the fevered arithmetic of a villain counting his gold. What they would find instead was a man of sixty-one years whose hands did not tremble, whose digestion was excellent, who slept six hours without exception and rose at five to take his coffee standing up, because sitting down to coffee invited conversation and conversation before seven in the morning was a tax he had stopped paying decades ago.

Power was not a fire. Everyone imagined fire—consuming, dramatic, hungry. Power was habit. It was the same desk at the same hour, the same pen dipped in the same ink, the same names arriving on schedule to be weighed and filed or discarded. The Republic ran on sentiment; Ferrante's corner of it ran on punctuality.

"Three days," he said. "The *San Gennaro* has missed three scheduled contacts."

"She was carrying cargo, sir." Riva said it flatly, the way one notes that it is raining. "And a passenger."

"A passenger." Ferrante turned from the window at last. The room was vast and deliberately underfurnished—two chairs, the great desk of black walnut, shelves of shipping manifests bound in green calfskin that no clerk had ever been permitted to open. Marble floors, marble columns salvaged from a demolished convent. People expected wealth to be warm. He preferred it cold. Cold things kept longer.

"The courier," he said.

"Yes, sir."

"Describe him again."

"Matteo Viali. Thirty-four. Employed through the notary Cravetto, who does not know your name and believes his client is a grain merchant in Savona. Viali carried documents twice monthly between Genoa and points east. He was careful. He used the ferries, the coast roads, the third-class compartments. He dressed poorly on purpose."

"And now he dresses for the bottom of the sea."

"That," said Riva, "remains to be established."

Ferrante crossed to his desk and sat, drawing toward himself the morning's stack—not the public correspondence, which his secretaries triaged with religious thoroughness, but the private ledger, the one kept in his own cramped hand, in a cipher his late wife had devised and which he had never bothered to change because grief, properly managed, could also be a lock. He opened it to the page marked with a small brass clip.

Viali's name was there. Beneath it, the route. Beneath that, the item: *documents re: Vigliano arbitration — recover or destroy.*

The Vigliano arbitration. Even the name had a bureaucratic dullness to it, a deliberate grayness, and Ferrante permitted himself a moment's professional appreciation for whoever had chosen it. Fourteen months ago, a captain named Vigliano—one of his own, loyal in the way of men who had been paid so long they had forgotten there was any other arrangement—had begun, in the manner of aging instruments, to drift. He had talked. Not much, and not loudly, but to a lawyer in Chiavari who represented families of the crew lost when the *Prosperina* went down with her holds full of insured sand instead of insured grain. Rigged wrecks. Bought inspectors. Policies written on ships designed to die. The words existed somewhere, in someone's handwriting, in a lawyer's careful files—and words, Ferrante knew, were the only cargo that could not be bought back once delivered.

So the documents had to travel. And so Viali had traveled with them. And now the sea, which Ferrante had spent thirty years bribing and insuring and scheduling around, had done what seas do.

"You believe he drowned," Ferrante said.

"I believe the storm was real. I believe the ferry from San Fruttuoso never made its crossing. I believe a body matching Viali's description may wash ashore somewhere between Camogli and Portovenere in the coming days, if the currents behave as they usually do." Riva folded his hands behind his back. "I also believe that a careful man does not carry fourteen months of evidence in a single satchel unless he has been told to, and that whoever told him to may have arranged for the storm to do what we were considering arranging ourselves."

Ferrante looked up sharply. It was rare that Riva volunteered an opinion. It was rarer still that the opinion was correct.

"You think it was taken from him."

"I think the sea loses things, signore, but it rarely loses precisely the thing everyone is looking for. I think if Viali drowned by accident, the documents are at the bottom of the gulf, and we may sleep. But I think—" and here Riva's mild face achieved an expression of genuine, almost tender regret— "that we should proceed on the assumption they did not."

The clock ticked. Somewhere below, in the street, a cart driver cursed his mule in the rich blasphemous poetry of the port, and the sound rose up through the marble like smoke.

Ferrante considered. This was the part outsiders never understood—the actual labor of it. They pictured decisions as lightning strikes. In truth each decision was a stone wall built one course at a time, mortared with information, and the mortar was the whole craft. Who knew about Viali? Six people. Which six could be verified? Three, as of this morning. Where would documents go if recovered? To a buyer—to one of perhaps four buyers on this coast with the money and the malice to pay for them. A journalist. A rival shipper. A deputy with ambitions. Or—

Or a family.

He turned the brass clip idly with one finger. There was a second page in the file, one he had not consulted in over a year, because consulting it admitted that it mattered. The old network. Lucia's people—the coastwise runners, the shrine relays, the fishermen who had moved more than sardines through two wars and a dozen governments. Most of them were scattered, aged, dead, or respectable. But networks were like roots under pavement: cut them, pave them, and still, some spring, a green shoot came up through a crack where no seed had any business being.

And the Marchetti lighthouse stood directly on the current line between Camogli and Portovenere. He had always known that. He had simply never before had reason to care.

"Who keeps the light at Punta della Fede?" he asked, though of course he knew, and knew that Riva knew he knew, and understood that the question was really another question entirely.

"A widower," Riva said. "Marchetti. Quiet man. Seven years at the post, no incidents, no politics, pays his taxes through the agent in Rapallo. By every account a ghost with a lamp."

"Every account." Ferrante smiled faintly. "Accounts are written by people who weren't looking. Have you ever been looked at, Captain, by a man who truly wasn't looking? It's the most dangerous gaze there is. A curious man you can buy or frighten. An invisible man you have to find first."

"You think the courier reached him."

"I think the currents reach him. I think the storm reached him. I think the coast of this entire gulf consists of rocks and witnesses, and that the rocks keep better records than we do." Ferrante closed the ledger and pressed it flat with both palms, feeling the cool leather under his skin, the way other men might touch a rosary. "Sweep it."

Riva waited. He was a man who never accepted an order until it was complete; incomplete orders produced incomplete obedience, and Riva dealt only in the finished article.

"Sweep the coast from Camogli to the Cinque Terre," Ferrante said. "Quietly. No carabinieri—we'll use the harbor police at Rapallo and our own men dressed as salvage crews. Every fisherman, every ferryman, every priest who hears confessions near a landing beach. Find the body. Find the satchel. If a fisherman found either, buy it—if he's honest, buy him; if he's dishonest, buy him cheaper. If anyone has already passed the documents inland—" he paused, and the pause had weight, like ballast shifted in a hold— "then find who received them. I want names, Captain. Not suspicions. Names."

"And the keeper?"

Ferrante rose and returned to the window. The rain had thickened; the harbor was nearly gone now, just shapes and lamplight bleeding into wet dark. His ships rode at anchor with their riding lights lit, patient and obedient, and beyond them the open water hammered at the mole with the blind persistence of something that could not be bribed.

"Watch him," he said. "Nothing more, yet. If he's merely a keeper, watching costs us a week. If he's a keeper who suddenly takes a journey inland in winter, then we will know what he is, and where he's going, and who taught him the route." He turned. "Dead men can't testify, Captain. But living men can be made to lead you to the paper. Paper burns. Men walk. Follow the walking."

Riva inclined his head, a small precise bow, and moved toward the door with his unhurried step. At the threshold he paused—a habit Ferrante tolerated because the pauses always paid rent.

"One more item, signore. The minister's office telephoned this morning. The deputy Bianchi wishes to dine Thursday. He used the word *gratitude* twice."

"Accept. Order the wine yourself—he drinks Barolo and pretends to prefer the local vintage, and letting him drink what he actually wants will unsettle him more than any flattery." Ferrante sat back down and drew the public correspondence toward him, the day's true work beginning, the empire resuming its respiration. "Gratitude is the cheapest currency in Italy, Captain. We mint it ourselves."

The door closed. The clock ticked. Outside, the storm worked the coast with methodical violence, prying at the rocks of a small island where a man was, at that very moment, doing something ordinary and fatal—though neither Ferrante nor anyone in the marble room could yet have said what.

Ferrante read three letters, answered two, and burned one without finishing it.

Then, because order was order and habit was armor, he unlocked the lower drawer of the black walnut desk and looked, for eleven seconds, at the photograph that lived there: a younger woman with sharp gray eyes and her hand resting on the shoulder of a fishing boat, unsmiling, unbowed, unmistakably in command. Lucia's sister. The last of the old network's founders, dead now eight years—or officially dead, which in Ferrante's long experience was a distinction the sea itself refused to honor.

He shut the drawer.

By nightfall, Captain Riva would be on the evening train to Rapallo, reading a newspaper he had no interest in, wearing the face of a man to whom strangers confessed things.

By dawn, the sweep would begin.

* * *

On the island called Punta della Fede, the storm had spent itself by morning, and Petra Marchetti walked down to the rocks below the tower to see what the sea had left him.

But that, as yet, was known to no one in Genoa—no one but the gulls, the tide, and a dead man strapped to a satchel, waiting with the patience of the drowned to change the weather of every life on the coast.
