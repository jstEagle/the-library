## 6. Three Checkpoints

Matteo arrived with the morning tide and a face like weather coming.

Petra saw the boat first—a small lateen-rigged thing, patched at the gunwale, riding the grey swell toward the island's only landing—and then he saw how it came: too fast for the chop, too straight for a man who usually tacked in lazy loops just to show off his canvas. By the time Matteo's boots hit the stone jetty, Petra had already decided to be busy with a rope that did not need coiling.

"You got my signal," Petra said.

"I got your signal." Matteo was sixty if he was fifty, hands like knotted hawsers, a beard salted white at the chin. He did not look at the tower. He looked instead down the length of the island, both directions, the way a man checks a street before saying something dangerous. "And I brought bread, oil, the lamp wicks you asked for. And I brought my opinion, which you didn't ask for but will get anyway."

"Then come inside where the wind can't hear us."

The keeper's cottage clung to the tower's flank like a barnacle, two rooms and a kitchen, everything scrubbed and ordered and smelling of paraffin and dried thyme. Petra put water on for coffee because his hands wanted something to do that wasn't shaking. Matteo lowered himself onto the bench by the window—the visitor's seat, Lucia's old seat—and accepted the cup without tasting it.

"Tell me again," Matteo said. "Slowly."

So Petra told it slowly. The storm. The body in the rocks below the tower, wedged where the sea had hammered him for hours and then, in some final caprice, set him down almost gently among the stones. The satchel strapped to his chest, waxed canvas, military clasps. The letter inside, sealed, dry as a sermon, bearing a name that had not been spoken aloud on this island in seven years.

*Marchetti.*

"And the dead man," Matteo said. "You know who sent him?"

"The seal on the letter had an anchor and three stars. I don't know it. But whoever wrote it knew Lucia's maiden name, knew she married me, knew I kept this light." Petra turned his cup in its saucer. "That's more than I knew about her, apparently."

Matteo drank then, all of it at once, like medicine. Outside, the gulls were working the tide line, screaming their small greeds. Somewhere beneath them, wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of the flour bin, lay a letter that weighed nothing and pulled like an anchor.

"Here is my opinion," Matteo said, setting down the cup. "Two nights ago a launch I didn't recognize anchored off Punta Chiappa. No fishing gear aboard, no nets, men ashore asking questions at the taverna there—asking after a courier lost in the storm. Asking whether anything washed up on the islands." He met Petra's eyes. "They described him. Tall, dark coat, a satchel. They said he carried papers worth more than a man's life, and they said it the way you'd say the price of something."

Petra felt the cold arrive from the inside. "The authorities—"

"The *authorities*," Matteo repeated, and spat the word out like a fishbone. "Petra. Who do you think sent them?"

* * *

After that they stopped pretending there was a version of events where Petra could simply wait, or hand the letter over, or be a man to whom nothing had happened. Matteo spread his big hands flat on the table, and between them, in spilled sugar from the bowl nearby, he began to draw.

"Say you go. Say you're fool enough to go." His finger traced the coast southward through the sugar. "Ferry to the mainland, fine—that part's mine, that part's safe, Bruno owes me and I owe him and neither of us asks why. You land at Camogli before dawn. And here—" the finger stopped, pressed hard enough to dent the table's soul through the sugar, "—here is where your trouble begins. The harbor checkpoint."

"There's always been an inspector at Camogli."

"There's always been a sleepy old man who waves through fish and wine and doesn't look up from his newspaper. That man has been replaced. My cousin saw the new one Tuesday. Young, sharp, going through every bag. And behind him, not in uniform, two others doing nothing—which is worse. Men doing nothing are watching something." Matteo looked up. "They'll have your description by now. They'll have your name. What papers do you carry?"

"My lightkeeper's permit. Issued at La Spezia, seven years back, renewed twice."

"Under what name?"

Petra's jaw tightened. "Marchetti. It's my name, Matteo."

"It's a name that will be circulating in every prefect's office from here to Ventimiglia within the week, attached to words like *seditious* and *wanted* and *reward*. Your permit won't survive it. It's not a passport, it never was—it says you tend a lamp, nothing more. At Camogli it buys you nothing. They ask one question you can't answer well and you're finished on the dock with the fish guts."

The sugar-map stretched inland now. Matteo's finger climbed away from the sea, up into the white space where the mountains began.

"So you don't take the coast road. Coast roads have gendarmes every ten kilometers and no shadows to hide in. You go up. There's a mule track from Camogli into the hills, old smugglers' paths—don't look at me like that, half of Liguria made its living on those paths before any of us were born, moving salt, moving tobacco, moving things nobody remembers anymore. Two days' walk, maybe three in winter, and you come down to the pass at the Croce d'Oro." He paused. "Which is the second checkpoint."

"A pass in the middle of nowhere has a checkpoint?"

"A pass is the *only* place you need one. It's a throat, Petra—everything going north to Genoa or coming south must squeeze through it, so the government built a barracks there and staffed it with soldiers who are bored and cold and grateful for anything interesting. In summer they wave people through. In winter, when the snow closes the road, they turn everyone back entirely." Matteo's voice went flat. "And this year, with the storms we've had? It'll be closed. Which means either you find another way over mountains in February, or you convince armed men that a mourner, a pilgrim, a fool—someone, anyone who isn't Petra Marchetti of the San Venerio light—has business crossing a closed pass in the worst season God ever invented."

Petra stared at the sugar. The granules had drifted where Matteo's finger dragged them, and now the whole route lay printed there in white: harbor, mountain, city, each marked by a small crater where a fingertip had pressed too hard.

"And even if the pass lets you through," Matteo went on, softer now, which was somehow worse, "even if you walk down into the Po valley and ride the train along the coast and come up on Genoa from the east—Genoa is a city of gates. Not walls anymore, not since the old days, but gates all the same. The Porta Soprana, the eastern entrances—they post inspectors there whenever there's political trouble, and brother, there is political trouble, whatever kind you've got folded in your flour bin. Third checkpoint. Papers again. A name again. Your face again." He sat back. "Three doors between you and whoever it is you think you're delivering this letter to. Three doors, each one locked, and the key to all of them is a face that's about to be pasted on every wall in the province."

* * *

When Matteo had gone—taking the sugar-map with him, wiping the table clean with his sleeve as though erasing evidence—Petra stood a long time at the window.

He had lived on this rock for eleven years, seven of them alone. He had chosen it the way other men choose monasteries, and for the same reasons. An island was a sentence with clean edges. On an island, a man's world could be measured exactly: one hundred and forty steps around the base of the tower, four rooms, one horizon. Nothing entered that the sea didn't bring, and the sea, whatever else it took, had never once taken his privacy. He had thought himself safe the way a stone at the bottom of a pool is safe—unseen, unmoved, untouched by the currents above.

Now he understood that the pool had a shape, and the shape had a mouth, and everything that entered or left passed through it.

Camogli. The Croce d'Oro. The gates of Genoa. He turned the names over like stones, looking for the soft underside of each, finding none. Three checkpoints, and each demanded the one thing he could not produce: a self that did not exist. Seven years ago he had buried his public life with Lucia—had surrendered his mainland work, his family's shipping interests, the whole noisy inheritance of the Marchetti name, and retreated behind a lighthouse permit like a man hiding behind a visiting card. He had thought he was retiring from the world. He had not understood that he was also retiring from any identity the world would recognize. Now, when he needed to be someone, he found he had spent a decade becoming no one—and no one, precisely, was what they were hunting.

He tried to imagine the inspector at Camogli, tried to rehearse a voice, a story, a shrug. *I am a widower traveling to see his wife's grave.* True, in a way; everything true was in a way, these days. But the permit would come out of his pocket with Marchetti printed across it in a clerk's indifferent hand, and the inspector's eyes would flick from the paper to the notice board beside his desk—to a notice that did not yet exist but would, soon, Matteo was sure of it, days at most—and the flick would become a stare, and the stare would become a whistle, and the whistle would bring men running down a sunlit quay while fishmongers paused mid-cry to watch.

Claustrophobia was the wrong word for an island; there was too much sky. But Petra felt it now anyway, pressing inward from all sides—not the walls of a room but the walls of a country, the borders and checkpoints and registries and permits, the great patient machinery of a state that remembered everything and forgave selectively. The island had protected him by isolating him. Now isolation was the trap. Every way off this rock led through a door, and every door had a man behind it with a list, and his name was on the list, or would be, and the sea that had guarded him for eleven years had also, all along, been keeping him.

He went down to the landing after dark and stood where Matteo's boat had been. The water slapped the stones in the rhythm it always used, indifferent, ancient. Across the strait, the lights of the mainland glittered—Portofino, Rapallo, the long necklace of lamps climbing toward the pass—and for the first time in seven years they did not look like civilization. They looked like teeth.

Behind him the great lamp turned and turned, sweeping its beam across the water, answering no one, useful to no one now except ships that steered by newer lights. He thought: *I am the last manned light on this stretch of coast, and I am the only thing on this island that cannot leave.*

He thought: *Lucia crossed these waters for years without me knowing. She had routes. She had names. She had ways through doors.*

He climbed back up to the cottage, and instead of logging the weather, instead of speaking to the empty chair as he did each night, he knelt at the bottom of the wardrobe where her clothes still hung in their camphor-sweet order, unworn, unforgiven, and pulled open the drawer he had not opened since the funeral.

It was time, he told himself, to learn what else his wife had hidden besides a name.

Inside, beneath a shawl, something metallic caught the lamplight and glinted like a small, deliberate eye.
