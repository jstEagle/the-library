## 7. Lucia's Drawer

The letter had been in his coat for three days now, folded against his ribs like a second heartbeat, and Petra Marchetti could no longer pretend he was merely keeping it safe.

On the fourth morning, with the storm gone and the sky scrubbed to a hard, indifferent blue, he stood at the foot of the stairs that led up to the bedroom he had not entered properly in seven years—only to change the linens, only to dust, only to perform the small mortuary offices that grief assigns—and told himself, in the voice of a man reading a weather log aloud to an empty room, that this was duty too. The letter named him custodian by marriage. Custodian of what? He could not guard a door he had never opened.

Lucia's things were behind that door.

He climbed the stairs the way a diver descends: slowly, deliberately, letting the pressure build.

* * *

The room was smaller than he remembered. That was always how it went with rooms you avoided; they shrank in your absence, gathering their shadows close like animals in winter. Her side of the bed was still made in her way—corners tucked sharp as folded letters, the pillow dented faintly where seven years of his own sleeping had never quite dared to press. Her shawl hung on the peg by the window, gray wool gone silver at the fold-lines, smelling still—if he pressed his face into it, and he did not, he would not—of lamp oil and rosemary and the particular salt-sweetness that had been hers alone.

The dresser stood beneath the small square window. Oak, dark, bought secondhand in Genoa the year they married. Three drawers.

He opened the top one first because it was easiest. Hairpins in a tin. A comb with two teeth missing. A ribbon, blue, faded to the color of evening water. Ordinary relics. He handled them like an archaeologist, like a thief, like a priest distributing relics he did not believe in but could not set down.

The middle drawer held stockings, a chemise, a folded apron. He closed it again. His hands were not steady.

The bottom drawer was locked.

He had known this, of course, in the way one knows a thing without admitting it to oneself—the way he had known for seven years that the key on her chain, the small iron key she wore beside her wedding band, had opened something more than the sea chest she'd claimed. She had worn it always. Even sleeping. Even ill with fever that last winter, delirious, calling names he didn't recognize—*Bruno, Bruno, tell him the tide is wrong*—her fist closed around it until the doctor pried her fingers loose and Petra, without thinking, had threaded the chain back around her neck himself, because it seemed hers, because it seemed private, because he had spent their whole marriage practicing the art of not asking.

She had died with the key against her heart, and he had buried her with it, because taking it would have felt like theft.

So the drawer had stayed locked. Seven years. And now here he stood with a dead man's letter against his ribs naming him custodian, and the lock suddenly looked less like a boundary than an invitation—or worse, an accusation.

There were tools enough downstairs. A lighthouse keeper is a mechanic whether he likes it or not: brass, glass, clockwork, the endless small tyranny of things that must be fixed or people die. He fetched a fine awl and a thin blade and knelt before the dresser like a man before an altar, and it took him perhaps four minutes, and when the lock gave with a sound like a knuckle cracking he flinched as though he had broken something in his own hand.

* * *

*They had been married eleven years,* he thought, kneeling there. *Eleven years and I never once asked what she kept.*

But that wasn't true either. He had asked once.

It was their third summer, and he had come up from the oil store to find her at this same dresser, the bottom drawer open, papers spread across her lap—papers she swept closed so fast the motion had a violence to it, a soldier's motion, nothing like his slow, deliberate wife who took a full minute to butter bread. He had laughed, then. Made some joke about love letters from a secret admirer. Watched her smile and say, "Receipts, tesoro. From Genoa. You know how I hate waste."

And he had believed her. That was the thing he could not get past, the stone in the shoe of every memory since: not that she lied, but that he had *wanted* to believe her, had been grateful for the lie, because the lie let him keep the marriage exactly as constructed—a quiet man, a quiet woman, a tower, a light, a life with no locked drawers in it anywhere. He had built his contentment on her silences the way the tower was built on rock, never asking what the rock itself rested on.

*You perfected being overlooked,* he told himself now, kneeling in the cold room. *And she let you practice.*

* * *

Inside the drawer: a lacquered box he had never seen, black with gold corners, Chinese or pretending to be. Under it, flat bundles wrapped in oilcloth. And at the back, wedged upright against the wood, a bundle of letters tied with string—not her handwriting on the addresses, though several bore stamps from ports he'd never heard her mention: Bastia, Ajaccio, a smudged postmark that might have been Tunis.

He sat down on the floor. It seemed wise. The room tilted gently, the way the tower swayed in the worst gales, and he waited for it to settle before he touched anything else.

The oilcloth bundles came open under his thumbs. Receipts—so she hadn't lied entirely, which was somehow worse. Dozens of them, years' worth, from chandlers and shipping agents and wine merchants in Genoa, Savona, Porto Maurizio. But the amounts were wrong. No household ran on sums like these; no island keeper's wife had cause to remit four hundred lire to a warehouse firm called Speranza & Figli, month after month, year after year. And the receipts were written in columns of figures that resolved, when he stared long enough, into something other than arithmetic. Certain numbers recurred like refrains. 7-3-11. 19-2-4. Groups of them, separated by dashes, marching down the margins beside the honest sums like ants beside a road.

He was fifty-one years old and he had kept a light for nineteen years and he understood codes the way any man does who has read of them—but understanding that these were codes was not the same as breaking them, and he sat among the receipts feeling the specific humiliation of a man who finds a letter in a language he cannot read addressed, unmistakably, to someone he loves.

*7-3-11.* He said it aloud. The room did not answer. The sea below the rocks did not answer. Even Lucia, wherever she kept herself these seven years, declined to answer, and anger came up in him then for the first time—real anger, hot and ungrateful and alive—not the pale grief he had nursed like a pilot flame all this while, but anger with teeth. *You let me talk to you every night for seven years. You let me log the weather to you. You let me believe we had said everything there was to say, and the whole time you had a language I wasn't given the grammar for.*

*Was our whole marriage a hiding place? Was I the hiding place?*

* * *

The photograph was in the lacquered box, along with the second key.

He found the key first: small, brass, newer than the iron one she'd been buried with, stamped on its bow with a mark like a bird over waves. He turned it in the light. It meant a lock somewhere. A room somewhere. A door standing open all this time in some city he had visited twice in his life, waiting for a woman who was never coming, holding whatever it was she had died rather than explain.

Then the photograph. Cardboard-mounted, the kind itinerant studios sold, cracked along one corner. Six people on a quay, taken—he guessed—fifteen years ago, maybe more, the fashion of the coats placing it before their marriage. A crane loomed behind them; crates lay stacked like a barricade. Five of the faces meant nothing to him.

The sixth was Lucia.

Younger by a decade, hair loose instead of pinned, wearing trousers—*trousers*, on a dock, in daylight, unashamed—one arm slung familiarly around the shoulder of a heavyset man with a beard and a boatman's cap, the other raised in mid-gesture, mouth open, caught mid-word. She was laughing. He had never seen her laugh like that. In all their years she had smiled the way one smiles across a quiet table—warm, contained, a lamp turned low. This woman in the photograph laughed like someone who had just gotten away with something magnificent.

He read the faces of the others slowly, memorizing them, because they were strangers and yet they had known her—known this version of her, this laughing, trousered, gesturing woman—and because one of them, he understood with a cold settling in his stomach, might be the drowned man whose body the sea had brought to his rocks four nights ago. The courier had been young. Too young for the photograph, probably. But grief makes scholars of everyone, and he studied each face anyway, committing them, as if one day he might stand in a crowd in Genoa and know his enemies and allies by sight.

On the back, in pencil, in a hand that was not Lucia's but that he recognized anyway—she had received letters in this hand; he'd carried them in from the supply boat for years, and she'd always read them standing, at the window, facing the sea:

*La Compagnia — Quarto Congresso — non dimenticare chi siamo.*

The Company. The Fourth Congress. Do not forget who we are.

*Who we are.* Not who I am. Who *we* are.

Petra sat on the floor of his dead wife's bedroom holding a photograph of her other family, and above him the great lamp turned in its daytime rest, and below him the sea worked patiently at the rocks, and between those two eternal motions he felt himself suspended—neither the man he had believed himself nor yet the man the letter required, just a widower with ink on his fingers from old receipts, angry in a way that felt dangerously like waking.

* * *

*One more memory came, unasked, the way the worst ones do.*

The night before their wedding, in her mother's kitchen in the village, she had gripped his hands across the table—hard, harder than tenderness required—and said, "Petra. Whatever I was before, I'm finished with it. Whatever comes after, you'll have all of me that matters." And he had kissed her forehead and said something easy and foolish, something about how the past could keep itself, and she had looked at him for a long moment with an expression he had spent eleven years misreading as gratitude and now, finally, correctly, identified as farewell. She had been burying herself that night. He had helped her dig the grave and called it a trousseau.

And yet—this was the thought that stopped the anger short, that left him hollowed and shaking—*she had chosen this.* Chosen the tower, the wind, the man who trimmed wicks and spoke softly. Whatever the Company was, whatever the receipts and the congresses and the laughing woman on the quay, she had looked at all of it and at him, and she had picked the small hard life on the rock. People do not hide themselves in places they despise. She had hidden herself somewhere she loved.

That did not forgive her. But it complicated the forgiving, and Petra was beginning to understand that complication was going to be the whole labor of the rest of his life.

* * *

He worked until the light went amber and then gray, and by lamplight he made order of it, because ordering was the one prayer he trusted: the receipts bundled by year, oldest last. The photograph wrapped again in its oilcloth. The letters—he read three, all in the same coded figures, all signed with initials he did not know—retied with their string. Everything except two items.

The brass key he put on his own chain, next to his watch, where it lay cold against his chest like a small verdict.

And the letter from the drowned man he took from his coat lining and laid on top of Lucia's papers, seal uppermost, and looked at for a long time. Custodian by marriage. Whoever wrote those words had known about the drawer. Had known about the network, the receipts, the laughing woman. Had known that the quiet keeper on the island was not a destination but a link in some chain stretching back through fifteen years of sealed compartments—and forward, now, to a journalist in Genoa whose name the letter held and whose face Petra could not picture.

Three checkpoints between here and that name. Harbor, pass, city gate. Bruno's supply boat coming Thursday, tide permitting, and men—Riva's men, Ferrante's men—asking questions on the mainland already.

He put the letter inside the hollowed base of a spare Fresnel lens component, wrapped it in wool, and packed it with the legitimate supplies he'd need to carry ashore: lamp parts due for refurbishing, requisition forms, a jar of Lucia's rosemary honey he could not explain even to himself.

At the door he paused. The drawer stood open, empty, its lock sprung forever. It looked smaller now, and older, and very much like a coffin he had finally gotten around to opening.

"You could have told me," he said aloud, to the room, to the sea beyond it, to the woman in the photograph laughing on the quay. His voice cracked on the last word, and he was too tired to be ashamed. "You could have *told me*."

The lamp above turned. The sea answered no one.

Thursday the boat would come. And with it, whether he was ready or not, the harbor at dusk, and the first pair of official eyes between him and Genoa.
