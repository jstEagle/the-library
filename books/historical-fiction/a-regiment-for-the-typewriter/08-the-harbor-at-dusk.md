## 8. The Harbor at Dusk

The mainland smelled of woodsmoke and tar, and beneath it all, of people — a hundred small human smells braided together into something Petra had not breathed in seven years. He stood at the edge of the fish market square in Camogli with his collar up and the wind off the water pressing his coat flat against him, and he let himself feel it: not fear exactly, not yet. More like the sensation of stepping off solid rock into water whose depth you cannot see.

Bruno's boat had put him ashore before dawn at a cove two miles east of the harbor, far from the customs shed and its lanterns. They had not spoken much on the crossing. Bruno had gripped the tiller with both hands and stared at the black water as though it might answer questions neither of them wanted asked aloud, and when Petra stepped over the side onto the shingle, the old man had said only one thing.

"Harbor checkpoint opens at dusk. Inspector named Fassio. He counts crates and dreams of retirement." A pause, and then, quieter: "He was there the night Lucia — " He stopped. Shook his head. "Go before dark, Marchetti. Dusk is honest light. Night is where men like Riva do their arithmetic."

Now dusk was coming, and Petra had spent the whole day learning that everything Bruno told him was true and insufficient.

* * *

The letter rode against his ribs in a hollowed lamp part, and he had built that hiding place the way he built everything — slowly, with his hands, out of materials that lied about their purpose.

It was a brass oil reservoir, the kind that fed the mantle of a harbor beacon, one of three genuine spare parts he'd bought from a chandler in Rapallo with coins counted out one by one while the chandler's boy watched him with the incurious stare of the young for the old. The reservoirs were dented, salt-stained, ordinary. In the bottom of one, packed around a false floor of soldered tin, the sealed letter lay curled like something sleeping. Above it, an inch of actual lamp oil sloshed when he moved — enough to make the thing smell right, weigh right, glisten wrong if anyone thought to open it. He had practiced the soldering twice on the island, burning his thumb, muttering to Lucia's photograph that she'd better be worth this, knowing even as he said it that she would say the same to him.

Around the reservoir he'd stacked the rest of his cover like masonry: a lighthouse keeper's requisition order, genuinely stamped, for wick trimmers and glass panes and a crate of mantles; a coil of rope; a canvas bag of tools worn soft from years of use — because no inspector alive suspects a man whose tools look loved. And on top, folded into oilcloth, the permit itself, bearing the name P. Marchetti, Keeper, Isola della Vedova Light.

That name was the whole problem now. He knew it the way a man knows weather coming in his bones. Somewhere between Genoa and the coast road, papers were being printed with his face on them, or soon would be. Riva's men would carry them. And a lighthouse permit was the most scrutinized document in Liguria precisely because nobody ever checked lighthouse keepers — which meant that when someone finally did check, they checked thoroughly, out of boredom if nothing else.

So he had done what he could. He had shaved the beard he'd worn since Lucia died, and the man in the cracked mirror of the pensione room had looked back at him like a stranger — thinner, older around the eyes, the face of a man who has been talking to the sea for too long. He'd rubbed coal dust into the gray at his temples so the reflection matched the permit photo less badly than it otherwise might have. He'd rehearsed a story until it stopped sounding rehearsed: supplies for the winter refit, delayed by the storms, traveling alone because the island budget allowed no assistant this year, going home tonight if the boat could be found.

All of it true except the part that mattered. That, he was learning, was how smuggling worked. Lucia must have known it the way he knew wick-trimming.

* * *

By half past five the sky had gone the color of pewter, and the harbor checkpoint had lit its lanterns — two of them, hung on either side of the customs shed door, throwing cones of yellow light across a queue of perhaps forty souls: fishermen hauling their day's catch toward the weighing table, porters with bales of wool, a widow with a basket of lemons, two friars arguing softly about the price of candles. The queue moved the way queues move everywhere — in small reluctant increments, punctuated by the scratch of the inspector's pencil.

Petra joined the end of it and made himself study the inspector through lowered eyelids.

Fassio was exactly as Bruno had described him: a heavy man past fifty with a waxed mustache gone gray at the edges and the posture of someone who had surrendered to his chair decades ago. He sat behind a plank table under the shed awning, sheltered from the drizzle that had begun, and examined documents with the air of a man reading letters from creditors. Occasionally he glanced at cargo. Mostly he glanced at the clock on the church tower, visible above the rooftops, and sighed.

Beside him, doing the actual lifting and looking, was a younger clerk with a carbine slung carelessly and the restless energy of a dog kept indoors.

*Queue*, Petra told himself. *Be queue.* It was advice he gave himself the way he'd once given himself instructions for trimming the great lens: don't think about the flame, think about the cloth, the angle, the breath. Don't think about the letter against your ribs. Think about your boots. Think about the requisition order. Think about nothing so hard that nothing is all anyone sees.

He watched the people ahead of him instead, and found, to his surprise, that watching them steadied him. The lemon widow haggled over her duty allowance with the fluency of long practice and won. The friars were waved through with a nod and a blessing the younger clerk didn't return. A fisherman's crate was opened, inspected, closed again, the fish already stiffening in the cold. Ordinary life, moving through an ordinary gate, and every soul in that line carrying some small secret — untaxed brandy, an undeclared letter, a lie told to a wife — and none of them dying of it.

When his turn came, Petra stepped forward and set down the canvas tool bag, the rope, and the three reservoirs with the unhurried economy of a man performing a task he'd performed a thousand times.

"Evening," he said. His voice came out lower than usual, gravelled by a day of disuse, which suited him fine.

Fassio looked up. Looked down. Held out one hand without a word.

Petra passed him the permit and the requisition order.

The inspection took four minutes, and Petra lived each of them separately, like four different lives. Fassio read the permit. Fassio read the requisition. Fassio looked at Petra's face, then at the permit, then at the face again — and here Petra did the thing he had decided on that morning, the thing that had come to him unbidden while he'd been staring at the sea from Bruno's deck: he let himself look tired. Not suspiciously tired, not hunted-tired. Just the deep, bone-level weariness of a workingman at the end of a long day, the weariness that says *ask your questions quickly, friend, I've a boat to catch and a lamp to light*. He let his shoulders sag. He rubbed one eye with the heel of his hand, smearing coal dust slightly, and yawned — actually yawned, his body cooperating with a completeness that almost frightened him.

"Lamp oil in these?" Fassio asked, nudging a reservoir with one boot.

"Charged and ready," Petra said. "Beacon's due a full service before the equinox gales. You want me to open one?"

Something flickered across the inspector's face — the ghost of a man who once cared — and was extinguished. Opening reservoirs meant oil on the table, oil on his papers, oil smell in his coat until Christmas. "They're sealed," he said, waving it off. "Sealed's fine."

"The crate of mantles needs checking against the manifest," the young clerk said. "Regulation."

"Open it, then," Fassio said, already looking at the clock.

The clerk opened the crate, counted mantles against the manifest — twelve of twelve, because of course it was twelve of twelve; Petra had packed real mantles, real wick trimmers, a real requisition, because the best hiding place in the world is a shipment that is entirely what it claims to be, plus one thing more — and closed it up again. His hands moved with the brisk competence of routine, and Petra understood suddenly, viscerally, why Lucia's network had run for years through ports exactly like this one. Not because the smugglers were clever. Because the inspectors were human, and humanity is a tide that lifts all boats quietly out past the breakwater.

"Purpose of travel?" Fassio asked, stamping the requisition without reading it.

"Returning from supply run. Boat's waiting beyond the mole." A lie with a boat in it. Boats made lies respectable.

"Where's your crew?"

"Budget." Petra shrugged, and let the shrug carry seven years of solitary winters in it. "Island lights get what the ministry remembers to send."

And there it was — the smallest miracle of the evening. Fassio looked at him with something that was almost sympathy. A fellow servant of neglectful institutions. The stamp came down. The clerk waved him through with a jerk of his chin.

"Next," said Fassio, and Petra gathered his bag and his rope and his three lying lamps and walked through the gate into the lamplit street beyond, his heart beating slow and enormous, like a bell tolling underwater.

He had done it. He was through.

He was ten steps past the shed when the horses came.

* * *

He heard them before he saw them — the particular wet slap of hooves on cobbles, three riders moving fast, and then the sound every animal in the market square responded to before any human did: dogs first, then the fishwives, then a general lowering of voices, the way a room goes quiet when a doctor enters.

Petra did not turn around. Turning around is the first confession, and he knew this with a certainty that seemed to arrive from somewhere outside himself — from Lucia, maybe, or from seven years of standing watch alone and learning what vigilance actually was. Instead he kept walking, unhurried, a tired keeper heading for his boat, and he used the reflective glass of a shuttered shopfront the way he used the horizon at sea: one glance, held in peripheral vision, taking in everything.

Three riders. Dark coats, no insignia he could read at that angle, but the way they carried themselves said *paid* louder than any badge. The lead rider swung down in front of the customs shed and spoke to Fassio, and even at forty paces Petra could see the change that came over the inspector — the spine straightening, the mustache twitching, the weary bureaucracy suddenly remembering it was also an arm of the state. The rider handed over a paper. Fassio read it. Then Fassio's head came up and turned, slowly, scanning the street — scanning the backs of departing travelers — and Petra felt the paper's words land on him from across the distance like a hand on his shoulder.

He couldn't hear what Fassio said. He didn't need to. He saw the young clerk grab the carbine properly, sling it forward, and start down the queue of remaining travelers, pulling people aside. He saw the lemon widow clutch her basket. He saw the rider unfold a printed sheet and hold it up beside the face of a bewildered porter.

His face. Or near enough. A sketch, probably, from a description, from the ferryman's ledger, from somewhere — it didn't matter where. What mattered was that it was moving down the line toward the gate, and the gate was behind him, and the street ahead was filling with evening crowds who had no reason to help him and every reason to point.

*Don't run,* said the voice that wasn't quite his own. *Running is a story. Walking is a chore.*

There was a fish warehouse at the end of the street, doors open for the night's packing, lamplight spilling out over trestles stacked with crates. Men worked inside, backs turned, packing ice and hake. Between the trestles ran an alley — the kind of gap that exists in every harbor town in the world, too narrow for carts, smelling of brine and cat — leading, if the geometry held, to the lanes behind the waterfront.

Petra walked toward the warehouse the way a man walks toward his own workplace. At the doors he paused, set down his bag, and became briefly, magnificently, a keeper of lights fussing with his kit: he shifted the rope to his other shoulder, adjusted the tool bag's strap, bent as if to retie a bootlace. In bending, he slid the bag and the rope and two of the reservoirs beneath the trestle nearest the door — abandoned supplies, someone will collect them or they'll rot, either way they're honest trash now — and straightened up holding only the third reservoir, the one with the letter sleeping inside it, tucked under his arm like a workman carrying his own tool home.

Then he walked into the warehouse.

Nobody looked at him. That was the astonishing thing, the thing that would keep him awake for years afterward: nobody looked. He was a big man in work clothes in a working space at working hour, and he moved among the packers like current moves among pilings — felt, perhaps, noticed by none. One worker called out, "You lost, nonno?" and Petra answered, "Looking for the foreman — Vittorio sent me about the ice for the island run," and the worker shrugged and pointed deeper into the building, and Petra nodded and walked deeper, past the second trestle, to the alley door standing open for ventilation.

Behind him, faint but unmistakable, he heard the clerk's voice in the street, rising with excitement: "*Keeper — the keeper with the lamp parts — stop him—*"

They'd found the abandoned supplies. Of course they had. Good. Let them find them. Let them pull those reservoirs apart in the lamplight and find nothing but honest oil and honest brass, and let that nothing cost them twenty minutes of confidence. By the time they understood the trick, the trick would be a mile away and changing shape.

He stepped through the alley door into the dark and did not look back.

* * *

The lanes behind Camogli's waterfront were a maze designed by centuries of paranoia, and Petra blessed every paranoid generation of it. He moved uphill, always uphill, away from the harbor and its lanterns, through passages barely shoulder-width, past kitchen doors exhaling onion and garlic, past windows glowing amber behind shutters, past a church where vespers had just begun and the sound of singing spilled out into the stone street like warm water. Twice he heard boots behind him and twice he melted into doorways and let the boots pass — once a pair of lovers, once a drunk singing to himself with tremendous sincerity.

At the top of the hill, where the lanes opened onto the coastal road, he stopped in the shadow of a shrine — a little marble niche holding a chipped Madonna, her face worn featureless by salt wind — and permitted himself, for the length of ten breaths, to shake.

It was not fear of death, he realized. He had made his peace with the sea years ago; the sea took whom it took. It was fear of *uselessness* — of being seized now, emptied now, the letter confiscated and Lucia's last testament locked in an evidence drawer forever. He pressed the brass reservoir to his chest and felt the oil shift inside it, a small living weight, and thought: *she carried things through gates too. She did this. She did this for years, and came home to me smelling of lamp oil, and I never once asked why.*

"I'm sorry," he whispered to the Madonna, or to Lucia, or to whoever was keeping the books tonight. "I'm sorry I didn't ask."

The shrine offered no reply, but the singing from the church below swelled briefly, and he chose to take it as one.

Then he straightened, wiped his face with his sleeve, and made himself think like a smuggler instead of a keeper. The coastal road was dead — checkpoints at both ends by morning, his description spreading through every carabinieri post between here and Genoa. The trains were worse; stations had doors, and doors had men. Which left the sea, always the sea, and one name Bruno had given him at the cove that morning along with the warning about dusk:

*"If the harbor goes sour — and harbors go sour, Marchetti, it's what harbors are for — walk up the hill past the shrine, follow the wall till you find the fig tree growing through it, and knock at the green door. Tell them Bruno sends his debt."*

Petra turned uphill. The fig tree was easy to find, a dark knuckle of branches punching through old stonework, and beneath it, just visible in the starlight, a door painted the green of deep water. He knocked — softly, twice, the way one knocks when one does not wish to be remembered knocking.

Footsteps. A bolt drawn back with the oiled silence of long practice.

The door opened a hand's width. An eye appeared in the gap, old and sharp and unafraid, and looked him over from coal-dusted hair to salt-stained boots.

"Bruno sends his debt," Petra said quietly.

For a moment, nothing. Then the eye crinkled at its corner, and a woman's voice — dry as driftwood, amused as sin — said:

"Seven years that debt sits gathering interest, and he pays it with a lighthouse keeper carrying my old lamp trick under his arm. Come in before the whole hillside sees you, fool. And give me that reservoir — you've sealed it wrong, the oil will rot the paper."

The door opened wide enough to let him through, and behind him, down in the harbor, the search lanterns were still swinging.
