## 9. Bruno's Debt

Bruno Ferrando had ferried the waters between the island and the Ligurian coast for forty-one years, and in all that time he had learned one thing above every other: the sea tells you what it wants, and if you argue with it, you drown arguing.

Tonight the sea wanted fog.

It came down off the mountains in a slow grey shroud while he was still three miles from his mooring at Cala d'Oro, swallowing his bow wake, muffling the putt-putt of his engine until it sounded like a heartbeat heard through a wall. Bruno killed the lamp and steered by the feel of the water — the long swell from the southwest, the counter-eddy where the current bent around the headland — and he thought, as he always thought in weather like this, of Lucia.

Lucia Marchetti had loved fog. *The best smuggler's weather God ever made,* she used to say, standing at his tiller with her hood back and her face turned up into the wet grey like a woman greeting rain after drought. That had been twenty years ago and more, when his boat was younger than he was and carried more than fish on its return runs, when crates marked *sardines* held pamphlets and crates marked *oil* held ledgers, and nobody aboard asked questions because asking questions was how boats sank.

He hadn't thought about those years in a long time. He had gotten very good, over two decades, at not thinking about them — the same way a man gets good at not thinking about a tooth that aches only when he bites down.

Then this evening, at the island jetty, Petra Marchetti had stood in the failing light with a packed bag at his feet and a look on his face that Bruno recognized the way you recognize your own handwriting: the look of a man carrying something heavier than luggage.

*Take me across tonight,* Petra had said. Not asked. Said, in that flat keeper's voice, the voice of a man who trimmed wicks and logged winds and spoke to no living soul for weeks at a stretch.

*Tonight? In this?* Bruno had squinted at the sky, where the clouds were stacking themselves like cordwood. *First light is soon enough.*

*No.* And then, quieter, almost to himself: *I don't think I have first light.*

So here Bruno was, running dark through fog because a lighthouse keeper with a dead wife's eyes had told him to, and because Bruno Ferrando owed the Marchetti name a debt so old and so deep that he had stopped counting interest decades ago.

* * *

Petra sat in the stern with his arms wrapped around himself, watching the fog eat everything. The island was already gone behind them — the tower, the light sweeping its patient circle, seven years of his life reduced to a smudge and then to nothing. He should have felt fear. He had expected, packing in the dark, to be eaten alive by fear.

What he felt instead was stranger: a kind of weightlessness, as though by leaving the rock he had stepped out of his own life and into some other man's story. The letter pressed against his ribs inside the coat lining, sewn there that afternoon with sail thread and shaking hands. It rode against him like a second heart.

"You can sleep," Bruno called forward over the engine noise. "We've hours yet."

"I'm not tired."

"Nobody crossing water at night with a secret is tired." The old ferryman said it without turning around. "Tired comes later. First comes the part where you keep checking that it's still there."

Petra's hand went to his chest before he could stop it. He saw the old man's shoulders shift — not quite a laugh, but the shape of one.

"How did you know?"

"I've run this crossing forty years, son. I know cargo." Bruno adjusted the tiller a degree, feeling the current take hold. "You carry it like Lucia used to carry hers."

The name went through Petra like cold water through cloth. He gripped the gunwale. "You knew my wife."

"I knew her before she was your wife." Bruno let the engine drop to an idle as they entered the sheltered water behind the point; the sudden quiet was enormous, ringing. "Everybody on this coast knew her, one way or another. They just didn't all know it was her."

* * *

Bruno tied up at Cala d'Oro just past eleven, at a stone slip below his house that didn't appear on any chart — a landing he had built himself, unofficially, thirty years ago, with stone "borrowed" from a quarry whose owner owed him money. Some habits outlive their reasons. His house sat above the cove with its back to the road and its windows toward the sea, and he had chosen it that way deliberately, because a man who watches the sea never has to explain what he's watching for.

Inside, the house smelled of woodsmoke, salt, and the dried herbs his daughter hung from the beams every autumn. Bruno put soup on without asking whether Petra wanted any. Hunger, in his experience, followed terror the way dawn follows night, reliably and without effort.

Petra sat at the table with his hands around a cup of wine he wasn't drinking, and watched the old man move through his kitchen with the economy of long habit, and felt something loosen in his chest that he distrusted immediately. Kindness was dangerous now. Kindness created witnesses.

"Eat," Bruno said, setting the bowl down. "You'll want your strength tomorrow, and you won't want to ask me for anything else tonight, but you will anyway. Men always do." He lowered himself into the opposite chair with the groan of bad knees and studied Petra across the steam. "Now. You can tell me nothing, and I'll put you on the morning cart to the coast road and we'll both pretend this was about fish prices. Or you can tell me what's in that coat, and why men from Genoa have been at my slip twice this month asking after the Marchetti light."

Petra looked up sharply. "Men have been *here*?"

"Twice. Polite men. Well-dressed men. The kind of polite that costs somebody money." Bruno sipped his own wine. "They said they were insurance assessors. Insurance assessors don't travel in pairs and don't pay cash for gossip, and they certainly don't ask whether the keeper ever receives letters from the mainland." He set the glass down. "So. Nothing, or everything?"

Outside, the fog moved against the windows like something breathing. Somewhere down in the cove, a hawser creaked against the slip.

Petra found that he was tired — truly tired, tired in the marrow — and that the exhaustion had burned away whatever caution remained. Perhaps it was the wine. Perhaps it was the smell of the soup. Or perhaps it was simply that Bruno had said *Lucia* in a particular way, the way a man says the name of someone he grieved.

"It's a letter," Petra said. "A dead man brought it to my island. It names me custodian of evidence against Aldo Ferrante. Rigged wrecks. Bought officials. Seven ships, maybe more, sent to the bottom with crews aboard so that men in marble offices could collect the insurance and buy themselves a government." His voice came out level, which surprised him. "And it says my wife's family were the ones who gathered the evidence. Twenty years of it. Smuggled out of Genoa crate by crate, the same way Ferrante's people smuggled everything else. Only they moved truth instead of contraband."

The kitchen was very quiet. The fire ticked. A log settled.

"And you didn't know," Bruno said. Not a question.

"I knew she came from the mainland. I knew she had no family left and didn't like talking about why. I knew she read everything — newspapers, manifests, anything printed — the way other women read prayer books." Petra stared into the wine. "I knew she cried once, years ago, when a ship went down off Portofino. Forty drowned. She cried like it was a funeral in our own family, and I held her, and I never once asked the right question. Seven years married and I never asked."

Bruno nodded slowly. Then he got up, went to the dresser, and took down a bottle of grappa that was older than Petra's marriage. He poured two fingers into each cup, replacing the wine.

"If I'm going to tell you what I'm going to tell you," he said, "we're past wine."

* * *

"My son Matteo was twelve years old in the winter of '92," Bruno began. "Stupid age. Old enough to want to help, too young to understand that helping meant staying out of it. One night I let him come along on a run — not a smuggling run, a supply run, or so I told myself, though the crates were the usual crates. Fog came down off the mountains faster than it ever has since. I lost the channel markers. We hit the rocks off Punta Chiappa."

Petra went still.

"Hull opened like a melon. Water came in faster than any pump I owned. And Matteo — Matteo couldn't swim. Never learned. His mother died young and I'd kept him off the water out of superstition, which is a fine joke on a ferryman." Bruno turned the cup in his thick fingers without drinking. "There was another boat out that night. Small, fast, running dark like us, and I remember thinking in the moment before we struck: *they'll think we're customs, they'll run.* Instead they turned. They came straight at a wrecking boat in open fog, and a woman stood at the bow with a lamp in one hand and a line coiled in the other, and she threw that line across thirty feet of black water like she'd done it a hundred times, and maybe she had.

"She got Matteo across. Then she got me across. Then she went *back* — back onto my sinking boat, mind, in December water — and came out with the crates. All of them. Because she said the crates mattered more than either of us, and she was laughing when she said it, and I have never in my life been so frightened of anyone."

Petra's throat had closed. "Lucia."

"Lucia." Bruno drank at last, grimacing. "I tried to thank her. She wouldn't have it. She said, *You owe me nothing, ferrante's people will sink you someday if you keep running their water, so get out while your boy still has a father.* And I did. I got out. Sold my silence along with my boat and bought this one, honest and slow, and I told myself the debt was paid because I'd taken her advice."

"It wasn't paid."

"No." The word dropped like a stone into a well; they both listened for it to land. "Twenty-eight years, keeper, and it isn't paid, because a debt like that doesn't get paid — it gets *carried*. Like you're carrying that letter. And I have carried it badly, because I knew men were asking questions this month, and I said nothing to you. I let you live alone on that rock thinking the world had forgotten the Marchetti name." His eyes, watery and red-rimmed, were suddenly fierce. "That's my guilt, and you can have it, because guilt shared weighs less and yours looks heavy enough already."

* * *

Petra slept in the loft above the kitchen, under a wool blanket that smelled of lavender and decades. He did not expect to sleep, but sleep took him the way the fog had taken the coastline — completely and without negotiation — and he dreamed of Lucia standing at a bow in the fog with a lamp raised high, and in the dream her face was clear for the first time in seven years, and she was not looking at him. She was looking ahead, at some horizon he couldn't see, and she was saying something he couldn't hear over the water.

He woke before dawn to voices below.

For one blind instant he was back on the island, reaching for the weather log — and then memory arrived all at once, the letter, the crossing, the coat hanging on the nail by the door with its precious lining, and he was down the ladder fast enough to crack his elbow on the beam.

But it was only Bruno, at the door, speaking quietly to a boy of perhaps fifteen who stood dripping in the grey early light, having clearly rowed through the cove in the dark.

"—two of them at the harbor office since yesterday," the boy was saying, low and urgent. "Not assessors. The harbor inspector's been locked in his own back room since suppertime, and there's a car from Genoa parked outside with the engine running. And they've got papers, signor Bruno. Papers with a drawing on them. Tommaso saw one through the window — a face, and a name underneath."

"What name?" Bruno's voice was very calm, the calm of a man standing in shallow water knowing the drop-off is somewhere near.

The boy swallowed. "Marchetti. First name Petra. There's a reward."

Behind them, in the kitchen doorway, Petra Marchetti stood barefoot on cold flagstones with the firelight at his back, and understood that the sea had refused to keep the courier, and the mountain roads would refuse to keep him, and that from this hour forward the only place his face existed safely was nowhere at all.

Bruno turned. For a long moment the two men looked at each other across the warm kitchen, across twenty-eight years of unpaid debt.

"The coast road's finished," Bruno said quietly. "You'll go the other way."
