## 10. Riva's Method

Captain Riva did not believe in cruelty.

This was not a matter of conscience but of arithmetic. Cruelty was expensive. It bruised the meat of a man and then the man became useless — weeping, broken, telling you anything at all, which meant nothing at all. A beaten man told you what he thought you wanted to hear, the way a cracked bell makes whatever sound the crack prefers. No: Riva preferred men whole. He preferred them sitting upright in a comfortable chair with a glass of water within reach and a window that showed ordinary life going on without them — carts in the street below, a woman shaking a rug from a balcony, gulls wheeling over the harbor at Rapallo as though the world had never once required anyone to be afraid in it.

Ordinary life was his best instrument. Not the dark cell. Not the strap or the pincer. The window.

He arrived at the harbor office at four in the afternoon, when the light came low and amber off the water and made everything look gentle, even the ledgers stacked on the inspector's desk like small gray tombstones. He came alone. He always came alone. Two men at a door announced trouble; one man at a door announced paperwork, and paperwork was something every official in Liguria had learned to receive the way soil receives rain.

"Signor Bardi," he said, removing his hat. "I hope I find you before the fish do."

The inspector — a soft man with ink-stained fingers and the complexion of someone who had spent twenty years indoors counting other people's mistakes — laughed too quickly. They always laughed too quickly. Riva noted it the way a physician notes a tremor: not yet diagnostic, but suggestive.

"Captain Riva. This is an honor."

"It is an inconvenience," Riva said pleasantly, taking the chair across the desk uninvited, settling into it as a man settles into his own kitchen. "I have come about last Tuesday's ferry. The evening crossing from the island. The one with the lighthouse keeper."

Something moved behind Bardi's eyes — a shutter drawn halfway down a window. Riva catalogued it without appearing to look. The shutters of the face were more honest than the mouth; most men did not know they owned them.

"The crossings are all logged," Bardi said. "Of course. If you'll allow me—"

He reached for the ledger with hands that were almost steady. Riva watched the almost. He let the man find the page, let him run a finger down the column of names and cargoes, let him arrive — as Riva knew he would — at the entry for the keeper of the Punta Nera light. *P. Marchetti. Lamp oil, two tins. Glassware, one crate.* Legitimate. Unremarkable. The kind of entry a thousand inspectors had skimmed past a thousand times without a second thought, because the sea keeps its own records and no one audits the weather.

"A quiet man, this Marchetti," Riva said conversationally, while Bardi read. "The supply boatmen say he speaks to no one. Pays in exact coin. Never drinks." He smiled, and the smile was a door left deliberately open. "You spoke with him, I understand. That evening. Helped him carry the crate down."

Bardi's finger stopped on the page.

"I — helped him, yes. Or he helped me. The cart was heavy and my porter was drunk, as usual." A small laugh, seeking company. Finding none. "A courteous man. Quiet."

"Courteous," Riva repeated, tasting the word. "Yes. The boatman said the same. Isn't it strange how everyone remembers the same thing? Courteous. Quiet. Paid exactly." He leaned back and looked out the window at the harbor, at the fishing boats coming home with their lamps already lit against the early dark, at the gulls quarreling over something thrown from the market hall. "When everyone remembers a man identically, Signor Bardi, it is usually because no one actually saw him. A man who is truly seen is remembered unevenly. One person recalls his hands, another his voice, another the way he stood. Memory is a crowd, and crowds disagree. Agreement is a kind of silence."

The room had grown quieter around them. Outside, a bell buoy clanked its slow iron note. Bardi's hand lay flat on the ledger now, pressing the page down as though the wind might turn it.

"What is it you're asking me, Captain?"

"Nothing." Riva spread his own hands, palms up — an honest gesture, he had found, precisely because it was so rarely honest. "I am asking you nothing. I am telling you that a courier drowned off that coast ten days ago carrying letters that certain people in Genoa very much wish to read. I am telling you that the same week, a hermit who has spoken to no one for seven years suddenly crosses to the mainland carrying lamp parts heavier than lamp parts should be. And I am telling you—" he paused, and let the pause fill the room the way water fills a hull, quietly, from below "—that I will find out what was in that crate whether or not you help me. Your help determines only how pleasant the finding-out is. For everyone concerned."

* * *

Riva had learned his method young, in the customs service, from an older man named Ferrero who drank himself to death by degrees and taught, between glasses, the only lesson worth teaching: *Never ask a question you don't already know the answer to. Ask questions whose answers you can verify. Then the lying becomes visible, and visible lying frightens a man far worse than any threat, because it proves you know more than he thought you did.*

So he asked Bardi about the tide tables. About the duty roster for the east pier. About a shipment of anchovies and the stamp fee owed on foreign glassware — trivial things, verifiable things — and each answer Bardi gave, Riva compared silently against what he had gathered in three days of patient listening: the boatman paid for his opinions over wine in Rapallo, the porter sobered up just enough to remember being sobered up, the clerk who kept duplicate manifests and kept them, Riva suspected, against the day they might be worth something.

Bardi lied twice. Both times about small things — once about where he'd been standing when the ferry docked, once about whether he'd opened the glassware crate to inspect it. Small lies. But Riva watched them happen, watched the little shutters come down, and after the second one he set down his glass of water with great care, the way one sets down a chess piece to signal the endgame, and said:

"You inspected the crate."

"I didn't—"

"Your manifest says glassware, six pieces, packed in straw. Your duplicate ledger — the one your clerk keeps in the bottom drawer, third folder, because you believe the bottom drawer is safe — says glassware, five pieces." He had read it that morning while Bardi took his lunch. He had worn gloves. It had taken four minutes. "One piece missing. A lamp housing, by the description. Hollow. Brass. Weighs perhaps two kilos empty." He turned his gaze from the window at last and gave Bardi the full weight of his attention, which those who had felt it described afterward as a kind of cold, and which was not cold at all but simply total, undivided, patient. "You opened the crate, Signor Bardi. You counted. You found the extra weight odd — brass lamp housings don't travel with their lenses, and this one traveled with something inside it, something that shifted when you lifted it. You heard it shift. And then you closed the crate and stamped it through, because whoever pays for this office to stay warm pays for it generously, and because curiosity is a luxury for men with pensions."

Bardi's face had gone the color of the harbor in February. His lips moved twice before sound came. "I never—"

"Sit down," Riva said gently, because the man had half-risen, knees buckling, and it was important — essential — that he remain comfortable. That was the whole method. Comfort. A frightened man standing feels cornered; a frightened man sitting feels merely visited. "Please."

Bardi sat.

"Now. Here is what happens next, and I want you to listen carefully, because I will only arrange it once." Riva drew a folded paper from inside his coat and laid it on the desk between them, still folded, and did not touch it again. Inside it was nothing. It was a blank sheet. But Bardi could not know that, and the paper sat there growing heavier by the minute, the way blank paper does in the imagination of a guilty man. "Tomorrow morning you will write a report. In it you will state that on the evening of the twelfth you inspected a consignment belonging to the keeper of the Punta Nera light, and that the consignment contained a brass lamp housing of unusual manufacture — double-walled, recently soldered — which you retained for further examination. You will date it the thirteenth. You will file it in the ordinary way. You will tell no one, including your clerk, especially your clerk."

"And if I don't?"

Riva considered the window again. Below, the woman was still beating her rug; the gulls still wheeled; the world went on performing its innocence. "Then the report writes itself anyway," he said, "and it names you as the man who passed contraband through a government checkpoint and took payment for it. There is evidence of the payment. There is always evidence of the payment; there has never once in history been a bribe without evidence, only bribes without courage." He rose, retrieved his hat, settled it with both hands. At the door he paused, and turned, and his voice dropped into something almost kind. "You are not a bad man, Signor Bardi. You are a tired one. Tired men keep warm however they can. I understand this completely." A beat. "But the man in that crate — the keeper, the quiet one, Marchetti — he is carrying something that belongs to people who burn warehouses to warm themselves. When they come asking, and they will, I would prefer they find your report already filed. It is much better to be the man who helped than the man who knew."

He left the blank paper on the desk.

In the doorway, framed a moment against the amber harbor light, he added, as if remembering: "The boatman said Marchetti's coat was old. Navy wool, horn buttons. He wore it on the crossing. Do you recall the coat?"

Bardi shook his head — too fast, too smooth.

"No," Riva said. "Neither do I. Yet."

* * *

The coat was the thing he could not stop turning over, that evening, in the bare room above the trattoria where he stayed whenever business brought him down the coast. He kept rooms in Genoa, of course — a good address near the port offices, chosen for its respectability the way one chooses a coat — but he thought better here, in cheap rooms with thin walls, where the fishermen argued below about prices and the smell of frying sardines came up through the floorboards like a reminder of what all this machinery was ultimately for.

Ferrante wanted the letter. That was the instruction, and instructions from Aldo Ferrante arrived pre-shrunk, pressed flat of all feeling, like laundry done by a machine: *Find the courier's cargo. Discreetly. The election is in spring.* Seven words of intent wrapped in forty words of reassurance that no scandal need touch anyone if everyone behaved sensibly. Riva had worked for passionate men earlier in his career — men who shouted, men who broke things, men whose rage was a kind of confession that they had lost control of the situation. Ferrante never lost control of anything. Ferrante signed papers. Ferrante raised an eyebrow and a shipping lane closed, a magistrate transferred, a journalist's press lost its lease. Working for him was like working for the tide: impersonal, total, and utterly without malice, which was somehow worse.

And so the hunt for one widowed lighthouse keeper proceeded not as a chase but as a filing exercise. Riva had notices drawn up — a likeness rendered from the boatman's description, decent enough, the face generic in the way of faces that have practiced being unseen: *Wanted for questioning regarding stolen correspondence. Answers to P. Marchetti. Last seen Rapallo district.* They would go up in the morning, in three provinces, on the boards outside the carabinieri posts and the paste walls of the railway stations, among the opera posters and the quack medicines. By noon every stationmaster, every gate guard, every village priest with a taste for gossip would be an unpaid employee of Aldo Ferrante. That was the modern way. Why send ten men when a printed face could stand watch on a thousand walls, sleepless, unfed, incorruptible?

Almost incorruptible. There were always exceptions. Riva budgeted for them.

But the coat bothered him.

He sat by the window with the lamp turned low and turned the problem the way one turns a stone to see what lives beneath. A smuggler's housing — double-walled, recently soldered; the harbor clerk's duplicate ledger had given him the dimensions and the smith in Chiavari, when visited, had gone pale and remembered everything — such a housing does not fall open. Soldered means sealed. Sealed means the contents were meant to survive water, impact, inspection. A careful cargo. And a careful cargo implies a careful carrier, and a careful carrier does not leave traces.

Yet traces were all anyone had. The boatman's memory of courtesy. The clerk's missing kilo of brass. The paleness of a locksmith. And now, tomorrow, a face on a wall.

It was nearly midnight when he went down for a glass of the house wine he did not drink, purely to hear the room, and found the trattoria nearly empty: two fishermen arguing about nets, the owner stacking chairs, a drizzle beginning against the windows with a sound like sand poured slowly on paper. He was turning to go back up when he saw it.

On the bench by the door, half under the edge of a fish crate some porter had left there, catching the lamplight along one curved edge.

A button. Horn, four holes, navy thread still knotted through two of them. Old wool fibers caught in the thread — navy wool. It sat there in the yellow light with the modesty of all small objects, asking nothing, accusing no one, and Riva crouched beside it for a long moment without touching it, the way one crouches beside a track in soft ground.

The bench by the door. Where a man waits for a boat, or shelters from rain, or sits with his back to the wall watching who comes in. Where a coat brushes wood, and a loose button — loosened by salt, by years, by the working of cloth against a body that lived alone with no one to mend it — gives way.

He took out his handkerchief, lifted the button, and held it up to the lamp. Four holes. Horn, aged to the color of dark honey. He had seen buttons like it on a hundred coats. That was the point of buttons like it; they were designed by God or by the garment trade to belong to everyone.

But everyone had not sat on this bench on the night the ferry came in from the island. The boatman had told him that much, unprompted, over his paid wine: *the keeper waited an hour inside the trattoria for the late tide, drank nothing, spoke to no one, sat by the door.*

Riva closed his fist around the button and felt it press its small hard shape into his palm, and allowed himself, for the first time in days, something adjacent to satisfaction.

Not the letter. Not yet. But a thread — and threads, pulled slowly, in the right order, with the patience of a man who had never once needed to raise his voice, led inevitably to the hand that sewed them.

He finished closing the shutters, checked the street both ways out of habit, and stood a moment in the rain-smelling dark, looking south toward the mountains, toward the pass road that climbed inland toward Genoa — the only road a man without papers would dare.

"Where are you walking, keeper?" he said quietly, to no one, to the drizzle, to the night that kept its records better than any clerk.

Then he went up to write his reports. By dawn, the face would be on the walls; by dusk, he calculated, watching the black shapes of the hills through the wet glass, he would be waiting at the mountain pass — ahead of the man he hunted, the way one is always ahead of weather once one has learned to read it.
