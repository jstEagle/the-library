## 11. The Mountain Pass

The road stopped being a road an hour before dawn.

Petra felt it more than saw it—the change from packed earth to something softer, treacherous, that gave beneath his boots with a sound like wet bread tearing. Snow had come down all night in the high country, and it kept coming, not falling so much as hanging there, a grey suspension of itself, so that the world ended three arm-lengths from his face and began again whenever the wind chose to part the curtain. Somewhere ahead was the pass. Somewhere behind him was the harbor town where he had left a trail he could no longer measure. And somewhere below all of it, invisible and absurd, was the sea he had spent seven years watching, which now would have looked like mercy.

He had slept—*slept*, the word was generous—in a hay barn belonging to no one he'd asked, curled against the warm flank of a cow who had regarded him with the flat tolerance livestock reserve for creatures too pitiful to be worth alarm. His coat smelled of hay and cattle and his own unwashed fear. Inside its lining, sewn back into place with crooked stitches by candlelight, lay the letter. He touched the lining through his coat every hundred paces or so, the way a man touches a tooth that has begun to ache, confirming that the hurt is still there, still his.

The letter was heavier than paper had any right to be. It had been heavy since Bruno's kitchen table, since the old ferryman's hands had trembled around a cup of wine while he said, *Your Lucia once pulled my boy out of the water at Chiavari, signore, when I was too much of a coward to do what she asked me to. You carry this for her.* Petra had not known how to answer that. He still did not know. He walked because walking was the only verb left to him.

Ahead, muffled by snow, came the sound of voices.

He stopped. Wind moved through the pines on either side of the track with a long exhalation, like the mountain deciding whether to bother with him at all. The voices resolved—not soldiers, he thought first, then corrected himself: he had no way of knowing what soldiers sounded like in the mountains, and assuming they sounded friendly was exactly the kind of stupidity that put men like him in places like Riva's custody. He stood very still among the trees, breathing shallowly, and watched the white.

They emerged as shapes first: dark figures strung along the track in a loose line, moving with the deliberate, patient gait of people who have surrendered to weather rather than fighting it. A dozen of them, perhaps fifteen. At their head someone carried a lantern whose light did almost nothing—a pale coin of glow that the snow swallowed whole—and someone else, further back, was singing.

Singing. In this.

Petra knew the melody before he placed it, the way one knows a smell from childhood. It was an old hymn, a pilgrim's song, the kind sung at wayside shrines all down the coast, the kind Lucia had hummed sometimes while shelling beans, not out of devotion but out of habit, the way she did everything. *Santa Maria del Monte, ora pro nobis.* The words drifted to him across the snow in fragments, worn smooth by centuries of mouths.

A pilgrimage. Of course. The feast day. He had lost the days somewhere between the island and here; they had slipped off him like wet gloves. But if the shrine at the pass was expecting pilgrims, then the road above was full of them, and full of pilgrims meant—

It also meant soldiers. Pilgrims meant a crowd, and crowds meant checkpoints, and the second checkpoint was the pass itself. Bruno had drawn it for him on the back of a tobacco packet: *Here the road narrows, here the barracks, here they will ask you who you are, and signore, pray God you know by then.*

The column drew level with his hiding place. He counted them properly now: fourteen, mostly women, two old men, a boy of perhaps ten leading a donkey loaded with bundles. Their clothes were poor but clean, mended with the bright thrift of the very devout—ribbons on hats, a painted banner furled against the wet, wax and incense and damp wool. They moved like people carrying something precious through dangerous country, and Petra understood, with a lurch, what that something was: themselves. They were bringing themselves up the mountain to give away.

One of the women saw him. He must have stepped wrong, or the wind must have shifted, because her head turned and her eyes found him in the trees as easily as if he'd been standing in a doorway. She did not cry out. She did not alert anyone. She simply looked at him—at his ruined coat, his unshaven face, his boots leaking snowmelt—with an expression he could not read until he could: recognition. Not of him. Of his condition. She had seen men running from something before. Perhaps she had been married to one. Perhaps she had been one.

She slowed her step, let the column draw ahead, and waited for him in the middle of the track like a gate that had decided to open.

"You'll freeze up there alone," she said, when he came to her, because what else could he do. Her voice was smoke and authority. "And you'll die down here alone. Either way you're alone, unless you walk with us."

"I'm not—" He heard himself reaching for the lie and abandoned it halfway, astonished at his own exhaustion. "I'm going over the pass."

"Everyone going over the pass is going to the Madonna." She said it without judgment, as one states the price of bread. "That's what the pass is *for*. The soldiers know it, we know it, the Madonna knows it." Her eyes were quick and dark and missed nothing, including, he was certain, the shape of whatever was sewn into his coat. She decided, visibly, not to see it. "Can you carry the banner?"

"What?"

"The banner." She nodded toward the boy with the donkey. "Giacomo's arms are short and the wind is spiteful. A mourner carries things. That's what mourners are for."

"I'm not a mourner either," Petra said, and then heard the sentence arrive in the world and understood that it was both false and unbearably true. Seven years of mourning, and he had never once carried anything for it. He had trimmed wicks and logged weather and spoken aloud to a dead woman in an empty tower, and called that mourning, when all it had ever been was hiding with better lighting.

The woman's name was Assunta. She took his silence for grief—which it was, though not the kind she meant—and pressed a wool scarf into his hands, black, smelling of woodsmoke and another person's life. "For your head," she said. "Mourning black. You'll thank me at the checkpoint."

* * *

The pass revealed itself gradually, the way a face reveals itself to someone recovering from fainting. First the trees thinned, giving up their argument with altitude. Then the rock showed through the snow in black shoulders and ribs. Then, quite suddenly, the world opened on both sides—valleys dropping away north and south, filled with cloud like basins of milk—and Petra, who had lived seven years at the top of a tower, felt vertigo anyway, because this height had no lamp in it, no purpose, only space.

The road narrowed to a shelf cut into the cliff. Along it, the pilgrim column wound in single file, and at its far end, where the shelf pinched to a throat between rock walls, stood the checkpoint: a low stone barracks with a slate roof sagging under snow, a barrier of pine poles, and four soldiers in grey-green greatcoats whose breath rose above them in little private clouds.

Assunta's plan was simple enough to be honest. They would approach as they were—as what they were. The Madonna's feast brought hundreds over the pass each year; the soldiers' orders were surely to stop solitary men and suspicious cargo, not to interrogate grandmothers and donkeys. But orders bent less easily for a man traveling alone in a borrowed scarf with no papers worth the name and a face, by now, printed on walls from here to Genoa.

"So you'll walk in the middle of us," Assunta murmured as they closed the last bend, her breath warm against his ear. "And when they look at you, you'll look at the ground like a man with grief, which you are, and you'll hold the banner, which is heavier than it looks, and you will *pray*, signore, even if you've forgotten how. Kneel with your heart if nothing else. Soldiers can tell a praying man from a performing one. It's the only thing they can still tell."

"And if they can't?"

"Then the Madonna will have to manage," Assunta said, "and so will you."

His turn came faster than he wanted. The soldier at the barrier was young—younger than the uniform made him, twenty at most, with chapped red hands and the particular boredom of a boy stationed where nothing happens, guarding snow from other snow. Behind him, in the barracks doorway, an older sergeant sat warming a tin cup between his palms, watching the column with the flat patience of a man counting sheep.

"Papers," the young soldier said, to Assunta, to the old men, to the women, to the boy with the donkey. He barely glanced at the documents held out to him—parish letters, travel permits, the humble passports of the poor. Then his eyes came to Petra.

Petra held out the banner pole with both hands and looked down.

He had prepared a story. He had prepared several; he was, after seven years of speaking to no one, alarmingly good at inventing conversations. But standing there, feeling the young soldier's gaze move over his face—the face from the notices, the face that was now the only passport he lacked—he found that the stories had all gone out of him like heat from an opened door. What remained was the truth wearing mourning clothes: a man on a mountain in winter, carrying something for a dead woman because he had failed to carry anything for her while she lived.

"Papers," the soldier repeated.

"He hasn't any," Assunta said smoothly, before Petra could speak. "His wife died in the autumn. Fever. He's walking her to the Madonna because he promised her he would, and a promise like that doesn't wait for paperwork." She said it with such settled certainty that Petra's throat closed. It was not even a lie. It was a translation.

The soldier hesitated. His eyes went from Petra's bowed head to the banner, and something shifted in his young face—not suspicion but recognition, the memory of some funeral of his own, some grandmother, some promise. For a moment the barrier, the barracks, the whole grey machinery of the state balanced on the chapped red hands of a boy deciding what he believed.

"Go on, then," he said, and waved the column through. "Mind the ice past the chapel. Two fell last week."

Petra walked through the throat of the pass with his heart hammering so loudly he was sure the whole mountain could hear it. On the far side, where the road widened and descended toward the valley and the shrine, Assunta took his elbow briefly, steering him around a patch of black ice, and said nothing at all, which was somehow the kindest thing anyone had said to him since the sea gave up its dead.

* * *

They reached the shrine as the light failed.

It was less a church than a stubbornness: a stone chapel grown onto the mountainside like a limpet, ringed by the huts and lean-tos of those who lodged there, its small bell tower crowned with snow. Candles burned in every window, so that from the path the whole building seemed to breathe light. The courtyard was full—pilgrims unpacking bundles, sharing bread, arguing gently about blankets—and above them all hung the smell of woodsmoke, tallow, wet wool, and underneath it something older: cold stone and incense and time.

Petra stood at the edge of the courtyard holding a banner he no longer needed to carry, and experienced something he had not felt in seven years and had not missed.

He felt surrounded.

On the island, solitude had been a discipline, a craft, a regiment of one drilled daily until obedience was instinct. Here there was nowhere to stand that was not within reach of another human voice, another stranger's kindness offered without being asked for, another hand pressing bread into his own. A girl laughed somewhere near the well, and the sound went through him like a splinter. An old man asked him twice if he'd eaten. Assunta appeared at his side with a bowl of soup and the imperious tenderness of a woman who had decided he belonged to her now, at least until the feast was done.

"Eat," she commanded. "Then sleep in the east stable, it's warmest. And tomorrow—" she fixed him with those quick dark eyes, "—tomorrow you'll make your confession, because I've seen your face, signore, and whatever you're carrying, it isn't only sewn into your coat."

He ate the soup. It was the best thing he had tasted since Lucia died, or possibly since Lucia lived; grief and hunger had long ago merged into a single appetite he fed carelessly. He slept—or lay awake—in the straw of the east stable listening to strangers breathe around him, dozens of them, the rustle and sigh of the innocent, and he thought about what Assunta had seen in his face, and about the priest he had glimpsed that evening moving among the pilgrims: a small, bent figure with a white beard and eyes that went through a man like lamplight through glass.

Outside, the storm returned. He heard it coming up the valley before he saw anything—a pressure change, a note in the wind like a struck bell—and then the snow arrived horizontally, erasing the courtyard, the candles, the mountains, everything. By midnight the pass was closed. By midnight, Petra understood, it was closed to everyone: to him, to the soldiers in their barracks, and to whoever else might be climbing this road behind him with a dropped button in a tin box and a warrant in his pocket.

Trapped, then. Sealed in a house of faith with fourteen witnesses to his borrowed grief, waiting on weather and a priest.

Near dawn, unable to bear the straw and the breathing and the weight of the letter against his ribs a moment longer, Petra rose, wrapped himself in Assunta's black scarf, and crossed the frozen courtyard to the chapel, where a light was already burning.

The old priest was there, of course. Priests keep the hours fishermen keep. He was kneeling before the altar in the blue half-dark, and he did not turn when the door groaned, only said, in a voice like gravel under snow:

"Come in, son, and shut the cold out. You've been standing outside my door for three days. I thought you'd never knock."
