## 14. A Face on Every Wall

The notices went up at dawn, four hundred of them, printed in a run that had taken the provincial press a single night. Captain Emilio Riva stood in the grey light outside the harbor office at Rapallo and watched a gendarme paste the first one to the customs house door, smoothing it flat with the flat of his hand as though he were laying a tile. The paste was thin and the paper was cheap; by the next rain half of them would peel. It did not matter. A notice did not need to survive the weather. It only needed to be seen once, by someone, somewhere, at the right hour.

The face looked back at him from a hundred doors along the coast: long, weathered, ordinary. Hollowed cheeks. Eyes that seemed to look slightly past the artist rather than at him, which was, Riva supposed, accurate. The engraver had worked from the harbor inspector's description and from a button.

The button sat now in Riva's breast pocket, wrapped in a scrap of linen. Horn, not bone. Salt-swollen thread still through two of its four holes. He had carried it for three days the way other men carried relics, turning it over in meetings, pressing his thumbnail into its worn edge while some prefect or shipping agent droned about schedules. Evidence, properly speaking, meant nothing. Anyone could lose a button. But a button lost at a checkpoint, matched to a coat described by an inspector who had been questioned for six patient hours — that was not evidence. That was a sentence with a verb in it.

*Marchetti*, said the notices. PETRA MARCHETTI, of the Punta Vecchia light. WANTED FOR QUESTIONING IN THE MATTER OF SEDITION AND THEFT OF CORRESPONDENCE. There was a reward figure beneath, generous enough to loosen tongues in every village between here and the mountains, modest enough that no one would suspect how much the matter was truly worth.

Riva lit a cigarette and considered the coastline, the way it folded into itself — cove behind cove, road behind road — like a liar's story.

He did not hate the man on the paper. That was a common mistake, made by loud men, by cruel men, by men who needed their work to feel like war. Hatred was a luxury. It spent attention you could not afford. Riva felt about Marchetti what a locksmith feels about a lock: professional interest, a certain respect for good workmanship, and total confidence in the outcome. The man had kept himself invisible for seven years on a rock in the sea. That took discipline. Discipline was interesting. Discipline could be predicted.

And disciplined men, Riva had learned across fifteen years of this trade, always moved along the same lines when frightened. They avoided the open road. They avoided the cities until forced into them. They trusted strangers reluctantly, and then — because loneliness is a debt that comes due — they trusted one stranger too completely.

There were exactly three ways inland from this stretch of coast in February. The harbor at Rapallo, watched since the night of the search, though the bird had already flown the cage. The pass above Sant'Ilario, closed by snow but passable on foot in company, because mountain priests never turned away a pilgrim even when the soldiers turned away everyone else. Or the long southern loop through Chiavari, adding days, and no frightened man added days.

So: the shrine. Our Lady of the Snows, or one of her sisters along the ridge line. Riva had already sent word ahead in triplicate — one copy to the gendarmerie post, one to the parish itself under a neutral seal, one to his own file, because files outlived everything, including careers, including men.

He dropped the cigarette, ground it under his heel, and went inside to write the morning report.

That was the thing no one understood about this work, watching from the outside, imagining violence, imagined drama. Ninety parts in a hundred of it was paperwork. Reports in quadruplicate. Requisitions for the paste. Chits for railway warrants. A ledger of informants, each entry coded by number so that if a ledger burned the names would burn with it. Riva kept his own ledgers in a hand so neat his superiors had twice complimented it, and both times he had smiled and thought: *you are admiring the instrument that will one day be used on you.* Not that he intended anything against his superiors. It was simply good practice to remember what the ledgers were for.

Power, in Riva's experience, was not passion. Passion was what the poor had instead of power. Power was habit. It was knowing which clerk to greet by name, which minister's aide drank at which café, which forms moved faster on blue paper than white. Ferrante had taught him that, years ago, without ever meaning to teach anything. Ferrante did not rage. Ferrante initialed.

* * *

In Genoa, in an office on the top floor of a palazzo whose marble had been quarried before the Republic fell, Aldo Ferrante read a telegram and set it down on the blotter precisely in the center of the desk.

"Lost at sea," he said. "One courier. One satchel." He looked up at the minister's aide, a soft young man named Bellini whose father Ferrante had owned since before Bellini could shave. "You understand what was in the satchel."

"I'm told it's being characterized as commercial correspondence," Bellini said.

"It was eleven years of my correspondence," Ferrante said, "kept by a dead woman on an island nobody can find on a map, carried by a widower nobody has heard of, addressed to a newspaperwoman who prints things." He let that sit a moment. Outside the tall windows the harbor lay under a lid of cloud, cranes motionless, the whole port looking like machinery switched off. His machinery. Every crane, every berth, every customs stamp that moved cargo through it — purchased, leased, or owed. Eleven years of buying, all of it reducible to paper, and all of that paper now walking toward Genoa in the coat lining of a lighthouse keeper.

"What do you need from the ministry?" Bellini asked.

"Three things." Ferrante counted them off against the blotter with one finger, unhurried, a man ordering courses of a meal. "First: nothing official may acknowledge any investigation of my companies. If a journalist telephones, the answer is *no comment pending review*. Those are the exact words. Second: the coastal gendarmerie reports directly to Captain Riva for the duration, bypassing the prefect. The prefect talks. Third—" and here he paused, because the third item was the only one that required anything resembling care, "—whatever happens, however it ends, the story must never reach print. Not because it will be proven false. Because it will not need to be proven true. Do you see the difference?"

Bellini nodded, and Ferrante saw with mild distaste that the young man did not see, that he had never once in his life needed to see. That was fine. Seeing was Riva's department.

"And if the keeper is caught," Bellini ventured, "and the papers recovered?"

"Then we have them, and there was never any story." Ferrante rose and went to the window. Below, a steamer was taking on freight in the rain, small figures moving crates along a gangway, each one doing exactly what he had been hired to do, none of them asking why. "And if the keeper reaches the journalist, and she prints—" he shrugged, a small economical movement, "—then we have a different problem, and I pay a different kind of money. Either way, Minister Vassalli signs nothing, says nothing, and remembers nothing. That is what I am buying from you today. Memory. It is the cheapest thing in Genoa."

He dismissed the aide and stood alone a while, watching the rain arrive off the water in grey sheets. He was not a cruel man either, whatever they said. Cruelty required the person in front of you to exist as more than a line in a column. Ferrante had spent thirty years learning to see columns. Columns balanced or they did not. A courier drowned at sea was a loss entered against expected profit. A keeper with a name on four hundred walls was a variance, and variances got resolved.

It occurred to him, briefly, standing there — the way certain thoughts arrive uninvited, dressed as memory — that Lucia's family had once moved paper through these same waters, and moved it well. He put the thought down. Sentiment was a cost center.

He rang for his secretary. "Confirm the printing contracts for the election districts," he said. "And tell Riva I want weekly summaries, not daily. Daily reports make a man feel busy instead of effective."

* * *

By Thursday the notices had reached the mountain villages, and Riva reached them a day behind, traveling by car as far as the road allowed and by mule the rest of the way, arriving wet and unannounced at the gendarmerie post above Sant'Ilario.

The sergeant in charge was a decent, dull man named Fassio, who gave him coffee that tasted of chicory and showed him his books with the pride of a housewife showing clean linen. Riva read the books the way he read everything: quickly, and for what was missing.

"You turned back travelers on the eighth," Riva said. "The day of the storm."

"Closed pass. Standard order."

"How many turned back?"

Fassio flipped pages. "Eleven. Two carters who waited and came back down. A family going to a funeral in the valley. Four pilgrims."

"Pilgrims." Riva set down the cup. "Going where?"

"The shrine, presumably. Our Lady of the Snows. Though the priest at the shrine wrote us—" Fassio found the letter, held it out — "that no pilgrims arrived on the eighth. Weather held them at the chapel below, he says. They stayed two nights. Then went on."

"When?"

"Two days ago."

"And how many stayed at the chapel?"

Fassio blinked. "I — the priest wouldn't have written that, Captain. We don't count pilgrims."

"No," Riva agreed mildly. "Nobody counts pilgrims. Pilgrims are the one class of traveler in Italy whom every authority agrees to ignore. Gendarmes don't question them, innkeepers don't remember them, and priests feed them and forget their faces. It is almost as if the roads themselves were designed for them." He stood, buttoning his coat. "Which, Sergeant, is because they were. A hundred years before your orders existed, someone was already moving things along these ridgelines that could not go by the roads below. Shrines make excellent relay stations. No paperwork, a guaranteed stranger every week, and God for a customs officer who never inspects."

Fassio looked faintly scandalized. "The priest is a holy man—"

"They usually are. That's what makes them useful, and it's also what makes them dangerous. Holy men keep confidences better than criminals do, and unlike criminals, they don't sell out." Riva pulled on his gloves. "Get me a description of the pilgrims from anyone who saw them on the road. And send a wire to the shrine parish — no, don't. Send nothing." He stopped, reconsidering, and something in his face made the sergeant sit very still. "If our keeper spoke to anyone on that mountain, I want to know it before he knows I know it. Wire me from the valley station, not this one. And Sergeant — the notice. Has anyone local come forward about the reward?"

"One man. Day before yesterday. A drover, says he saw a traveler near the chapel matching the likeness. Wanted the money in advance, though, against his testimony."

"In advance." Riva smiled without warmth. "Of course he did. Where is he now?"

"Said he'd wait at the inn at the crossroads till word came. He's still drinking there, far as I know."

Riva considered the drover for a moment: a man who had seen a face, priced it, and settled in comfortably to sell it twice. Useful, the way a leaking barrel is useful — you learn the shape of the leak. "Pay him nothing. Note his name. If he sold the sight once he'll sell the news of selling it, and men like that talk loudest to whoever buys last. Put a man within earshot of him and wait. Sooner or later he'll boast to someone about the traveler he found, and I want to hear what he boasts."

Outside, the snow had begun again, fat flakes falling straight down through windless air, erasing the road in slow strokes. Riva stood on the post's stone step and looked up at the ridge, invisible now behind the white, where a chapel and a shrine and whatever had passed between them sat under the same silence.

The keeper was three days ahead and slowing. Frightened men slowed in snow. And ahead of him lay only Genoa — one city, one gate, one journalist, and one captain who had already wired ahead to the gate guards, to the railway police, to the port authority, and to a certain drover's inn.

Back inside, Riva spread a map of the province on the duty table and drew, with a ruler, a single pencil line from the coast to the city. Then he opened his ledger to a fresh page and began, in his small immaculate hand, to write the day's summary for Ferrante: *Subject believed to have traveled the shrine route via Sant'Ilario. Net closing at the city gate. Estimated interception: seventy-two hours.*

He signed it, blotted it, and filed the duplicate.

Seventy-two hours, he wrote. He was, as it happened, wrong by exactly one night — though not in the direction anyone would have guessed.
