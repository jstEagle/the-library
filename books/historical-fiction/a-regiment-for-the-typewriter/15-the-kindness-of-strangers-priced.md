## 15. The Kindness of Strangers, Priced

The rain had followed him down from the shrine like a debt collector.

It came in long grey sheets across the valley, filling the ditches, turning the road to a river of mud the color of iron, and Petra Marchetti walked through it with his collar up and his head down and the letter's ghost pressed flat against his ribs where his coat lining held nothing now but the memory of paper. Three days since the priest had given him the address on Via Garibaldi and the warning that sat in his chest like a swallowed stone: *one of them sold out years ago.* Three days of roads, of hedgerows, of farmhouses with their shutters closed against the weather, of the particular loneliness of a man moving through a country that was learning his face.

He had seen the notice for the first time that morning.

It was pasted to the wall of an osteria in a village whose name he never learned—a single sheet of cheap paper gone soft at the edges from the damp, its ink bleeding slightly so that the printed face beneath the words seemed to weep. *WANTED FOR QUESTIONING. PETRA MARCHETTI. LIGHTHOUSE KEEPER. APPROACH WITH CAUTION. REWARD OFFERED.*

Someone had nailed it up crooked, so that his own likeness tilted toward the road like a man leaning out a window to watch him pass.

Petra stood in the rain and studied himself. It was a poor likeness—too heavy through the jaw, too young around the eyes—but it was unmistakably him all the same, the way a bad portrait of a saint is still the saint. Seven years he had built a life out of not being looked at, out of being the man who trimmed wicks and logged winds and spoke to no one but the sea, and here was the state of Genoa's gratitude: his face reproduced by some clerk's hand and distributed to every wall between the mountains and the coast, priced like a sack of flour.

*Reward offered.*

They hadn't even bothered to say what he was wanted *for*. Questioning. As if the word itself were a set of irons.

He pulled his hat lower and walked on, and behind him the wet paper flapped once against the wall, patient as a fisherman watching bait.

* * *

The road down from the pass joined the valley road at a place where a stone bridge crossed a flooded stream, and there Petra fell in with other travelers, because a man alone draws the eye and a man in a column does not. There were pilgrims still, stragglers from the shrine, and carters hauling wine casks under dripping tarpaulins, and two women with market baskets covered in oilcloth, and a thin man in a brown coat who walked with the rolling gait of a sailor ashore.

Petra kept to the edge of the column and did his best to be furniture. This was the old discipline—the lighthouse discipline—the art he had perfected over seven winters of being overlooked. Walk at the pace of the group, no faster, no slower. Look at the middle distance, never at faces. Let your hands hang empty and your mouth stay shut. A man who wants nothing is invisible; a man who wants to pass unnoticed must want it more than anything, and that wanting shows in the shoulders, in the quickness of the eyes, in the way the breath goes shallow. So he breathed deep. He let his gaze rest on the drowned fields and the grey water standing in the furrows, and he thought about nothing, or about the rhythm of waves against the tower rocks, which amounted to the same thing.

For two hours it worked.

Then, at a crossroads where a shrine to some minor saint stood black with rain, the column stopped to rest, and the thin man in the brown coat sat down on the shrine's low wall directly beside him.

"You keep good watch on the middle distance," the man said. His voice was quiet, pitched under the sound of the rain on the shrine's little roof. He spoke Italian with a coastal accent—Ligurian, maybe eastern—and he did not look at Petra when he said it. He looked at the road ahead, at the mud, at the women shaking rain from their baskets. "I watched you watching nothing for two hours. Nobody watches nothing that well unless they're practicing."

Petra's heart did not leap; it sank, slowly, like a stone lowered into deep water. He had known this moment was coming since the osteria wall. He had simply hoped, the way a drowning man hopes the current will carry him past the rocks.

"I don't know what you mean," he said.

"No," the man agreed pleasantly. "You wouldn't." He produced, from inside his coat, a twist of paper, unfolded it, and showed what was inside: a strip of dried fig, half gone. He ate a piece, chewed, offered one. Petra shook his head. The man shrugged and put the rest away. "Name's Teodoro," he said. "Used to run cargo out of Sestri. Small stuff. Wine, tobacco, a little silk when the season was right." He wiped his mouth with the back of his hand. "You'd be surprised how many men on this road used to run something, once. The coast breeds us. All those little harbors, all those dark nights. You get an eye for a man carrying something he shouldn't be."

"I'm a pilgrim," Petra said. "I came down from the shrine."

"You did," Teodoro agreed. "I saw you come down. Mourner's band on your arm, boots that have never knelt on stone in their lives, and hands—" he glanced sideways, quickly, professionally, "—hands with lamp-oil in the creases. That black stain doesn't wash out in a week, my friend. I know it. My father trimmed lamps on the mole at Sestri before they electrified it." He chewed another piece of fig. "And you've got the look of a man walking away from something rather than toward it. Pilgrims walk toward."

The rain drummed on the shrine roof. Ahead, the carters were arguing about the depth of the ford below the bridge; the women had opened umbrellas; nobody was looking at the two men on the wall. The whole world had politely turned its back, and in that small privacy Petra understood that the decision had already been made somewhere else, by someone else, and that he had only arrived to hear the terms.

"Say what you want," he said quietly.

Teodoro smiled without warmth. "That's better." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, still watching the road. "There's a notice in every village from here to the sea. I saw it yesterday, in the osteria at Ponte Nera, over the wine barrels. Face, name, reward. And I'll tell you honestly—I'm not a clever man, but I've got an eye for faces the way you've got an eye for weather. Twenty years of reading customers in harbor taverns." He paused. "It's you. It's you even with the beard grown wrong and the hat pulled down. It's the way you hold your head. Like a man listening for bells."

Petra said nothing. Denial was a door and Teodoro had already walked through the room behind it.

"Now here's the thing." Teodoro's voice dropped further, almost tender. "I could shout. There are two carabinieri drinking grappa at the tavern at the next village—we'll pass within fifty meters of the door, you'll see. I could raise them, collect whatever they're offering—it won't be much, rewards never are, I've seen the schedules—and then I'd walk on to Rapallo with nothing but a story." He turned and looked at Petra directly for the first time, and his eyes were pale grey and entirely empty of malice, which somehow made it worse. "Or I could keep my mouth shut, which costs me nothing, because I owe you nothing either way, and you could make it worth my while to remember nothing."

"How much?"

"Fifty lire."

The number landed like a blow. Fifty lire was half a year of a keeper's wage. Fifty lire was everything Petra had carried out of the lighthouse plus most of what Bruno had pressed into his hand at the ferry landing, wrapped in oilcloth and called *Lucia's interest*, refusing to take it back. He had counted it three times in the dark of a hay barn, alone, and each time the total had shrunk in his mind as though the money itself were bleeding.

"That's robbery," he said.

"It's pricing," Teodoro said, unoffended. "Everything's priced. The grappa at the tavern, the ferry across the river, the silence of strangers. You thought your face was free? Your face is the most expensive thing on this road today. I'm offering it to you cheaper than the carabinieri would sell it back." He stood, stretched, rolled his neck until it cracked. "Fifty. And I'll tell you something for free, keeper: I'm not the only one who's seen it. There was a boy at the shrine who stared at you through the whole service, and an old woman at Ponte Nera who crossed herself twice when you passed. Somebody else will do the arithmetic soon. Maybe they'll want less than me. Maybe they'll want more. Maybe they'll just shout, because shouting's easier than bargaining and some people can't be bothered with commerce." He shrugged. "The market moves."

Petra looked down at his hands, at the black lines of lamp-oil worked into the creases that no scrubbing would ever fully lift. His father-in-law's hands, Lucia used to call them, laughing. Hands that smelled honest.

"The notice says questioning," he said. "Not arrest. Whatever they think I've done—"

"They don't think anything," Teodoro said. "That's what you don't understand yet. The men who put that notice up don't need you guilty. They need you found. What happens after isn't written on the paper." He tapped his temple. "But I was at Sestri when the *Fiore di Mare* went down with all hands in '09, and I knew two of the drowned, and I've heard things since about why she sank that made my blood go cold. So." He spread his hands. "Maybe I like the idea of costing them fifty lire they'll miss. Consider it a discount on my conscience. Fifty, and I'm a stranger you shared a shrine wall with, and nothing more."

* * *

He paid it in the lee of the bridge, in the noise of the swollen stream, counting banknotes out of the oilcloth roll into Teodoro's palm while both of them watched the road and neither watched the money. Teodoro licked his thumb and thumbed through the notes with the brisk reverence of a man saying grace, then folded them away into four different pockets, which told Petra everything about how often this man had been robbed.

"Pleasure doing business," Teodoro said.

"You'll say nothing."

"I'll say nothing *here*." The emphasis was small and precise, like a pin pushed through cloth. "A man's silence is only good where he gives it. At the next village, I'm a new man with a new day, and a new day has its own prices." He saw Petra's face and raised his hands, mock-placating. "No, no—look. I'll be fair, since you paid fair. Here's my advice, free, and it's worth more than the fifty you gave me: don't sleep indoors tonight. Don't take the main road past Camogli. And shave the beard off—not trim it, *take it off*—because the notice shows you bearded, and a man's chin is the one thing a poor likeness always gets wrong. Change how you walk. You walk like a man going home to a tower. Walk like a man going nowhere." He settled his own collar against the rain. "And stop paying people. Paying tells a clever man you're worth more than fifty. The next one will ask a hundred, and the one after that will ask for the letter."

Petra went cold from the scalp down. "What letter?"

Teodoro's grin was brief and crooked, a flash of yellow teeth in the grey afternoon. "There's always a letter," he said. "There's always papers, or a package, or a name whispered to somebody. Men don't run from notices, friend. Men run from what they're carrying. If you weren't carrying something, you'd have gone to the carabinieri yourself and laughed it off." He stepped backward into the rain, into the column of travelers reassembling beyond the bridge, and became, with horrifying ease, just another thin man in a brown coat. "Good luck, keeper," he called, not turning around. "Pray for rain. Rain empties the roads. Rain is the cheapest friend you've got left."

Then he was gone into the grey, walking nowhere, walking perfectly.

* * *

Petra did not take the main road past Camogli.

He sat instead until dusk in the dripping doorway of a ruined chapel above the terraced olive groves, with his back against cold stone and the valley smoking below him, and he took stock of himself the way he would take stock of a lamp after a storm: what had cracked, what still burned, what could not be mended.

Fifty lire gone. Two days' food gone with it, effectively, because he would not risk a tavern, not tonight, not tomorrow, perhaps never again. And worse than the money—money was only oil and wick, replaceable—was the thing Teodoro had shown him without meaning to, the way you show a man his reflection in a knife blade.

He had believed, all these days, that he was moving through the country the way he moved through fog: present, unseen, a disturbance in the air that resolved into nothing when anyone looked directly at it. Invisibility as craft, invisibility as virtue, seven years of practice finally paying their dividend. But the truth was uglier and simpler. He had not been invisible. He had been *unclaimed*. No one had looked closely enough to bother, because no one had been paid to, and now that a price hung on his face, the whole countryside had become a shop with him displayed in the window. Every pair of eyes was a till waiting to ring. The boy at the shrine. The old woman at Ponte Nera. The carabinieri who didn't even need him guilty. Somewhere behind all of it, patient as tide, Captain Riva walking the coast with his soft voice and his inevitable conclusions, buying what couldn't be bought and taking what could.

His solitude—that fortress he had built stone by stone after Lucia died, that beautiful terrible architecture of one—had turned out to be not a fortress at all but a commodity, sitting in the open market, and the world had come shopping.

And yet.

Petra pulled his knees up and watched the first lights kindle down in the valley, one window, then another, small warm yellows in the blue rain-dark, and he thought about what Teodoro had said with such casual contempt: *there's always a letter.* Yes. There was a letter—or there had been, or its contents had traveled on ahead of him in the pocket of a young guard who owed a debt to a dead woman's kindness—and the letter was the whole reason, and the letter was worth more than fifty lire, more than a hundred, more than everything the countryside could squeeze out of him, because it could sink Aldo Ferrante the way Ferrante had sunk ships full of men who also had names.

Lucia had carried things her whole life. Through checkpoints, through harbors, through years of marriage in which he had seen only the surface of her and called the surface the sea entire. She had moved through a country priced against her and had never once, as far as he knew, paid a man to look away. She had *paid people to look closer*—paid in bread, in medicine, in saved sons, in the slow coin of trust deposited in strange hands against a day exactly like this one. Bruno's son. The guard's mother. A network of debts running the length of the coast, and he had spent three days believing he walked alone because he had never learned to read the ledger.

He was alone because he had chosen to be, and choice, the priest had told him, kneeling in the storm-dark of the shrine, was the only identity history could never confiscate.

Rain ticked on the chapel's broken vault. Below, a cart creaked along the valley road, and Petra heard—faintly, carried on the wet air—the driver whistling, some aimless tuneless thing, alive and unafraid, and he felt the old temptation rise in him like water in a well: to go down, to find a boat, a cove, a fisherman with loose morals and a tight mouth, to row north along the coast in the dark and put Genoa and Ferrante and the letter and Lucia's whole secret inheritance behind him, to find some other island, some other lamp, some other seven years of blessed, purchased silence.

He let the temptation rise and he watched it, and he did not drink.

Because Teodoro would sell him again tomorrow, and the day after, and the price would climb, and eventually some frightened man with a reward schedule in his pocket would shout rather than bargain—but that was only the weather. Weather passed. What remained was the address in his head, inked by an old priest's trembling pen: a print shop, a street named for a revolutionary, a journalist named Elena Sarti who was, at this hour, somewhere in that smoking city, deciding whether to spend her life on a dead woman's evidence. He was the last courier of a network made of kindnesses, and the kindnesses were running out, and every mile he bought with shrinking coin was a mile Lucia had already paid for years ago.

*Fifty lire,* he thought, *for the privilege of being sold twice.*

He got up in the dark, took the beard-shears from his kit—he had bought them in a mountain village for reasons he hadn't then understood—and knelt at the rain barrel beside the chapel wall, and shaved himself blind by feel, blade whispering, whiskers floating in black water, until the face that rose out of the reflection was thinner, stranger, younger, a man the notice had never met. Then he bound his few remaining notes flat against his body, pulled his hat down over his naked chin, and started walking—not toward Camogli, not toward any road Teodoro might guess, but along the high terraces through the olive trees, east and south in a wide silent arc, toward the coast road below Rapallo where, if Bruno's map was true and God was merciful, a night boat ran contraband passengers to the city.

Behind him, in the village below, a dog began to bark at nothing.

Petra walked faster, and did not look back, and tried very hard not to count the eyes he could feel opening in the dark along the road—every shutter, every window, every wall where a wet paper waited with his name on it—and failed, and kept walking anyway.

At the bottom of the valley, where the coast road bent toward the sea, a lantern swung once in the darkness ahead of him. Once, then again. Deliberate. Spaced. Not the swing of a drunk or a shepherd.

The old signal rhythm. The one from Lucia's receipts. *Two short, one long: safe passage, come.*

Petra stood in the rain with his heart hammering against his hollowed ribs, staring at that small swinging light, knowing it could be rescue and knowing it could be Riva, knowing that after today he no longer had the luxury of telling the difference—and stepped off the terrace into the dark toward it.
