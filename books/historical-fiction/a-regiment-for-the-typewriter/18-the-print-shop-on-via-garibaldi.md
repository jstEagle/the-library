## 18. The Print Shop on Via Garibaldi

Petra had imagined the city as a single beast—one mouth, one throat, one set of teeth—and he had spent three days learning instead that Genoa was a thousand small animals, each one watching for itself. The alleys of the old town were so narrow that the buildings leaned together overhead like conspirators, and the sky appeared only in strips, thin as ribbon, gray with the coming storm. Water ran in the gutters from a morning rain that had not decided whether to become something worse. He kept his collar up and his eyes down and walked the way he had learned to walk on his own island: like a man whose business was no one's business but his own.

The priest's address was written on the back of a shrine receipt in faded violet ink: *Via Garibaldi 41 — ask for Sarti at the door with the iron ring.* Via Garibaldi was not an alley. It was a street of palaces, facades the color of old honey and dried blood, balconies where the great families of the republic had once watched processions go by. Petra felt his borrowed coat—Bruno's coat, too big in the shoulders, smelling of tar and wool—like a costume he had forgotten to take off before walking onto a stage.

Number 41 stood between two restored palaces, its plaster stained dark where decades of rain had wept down from the cornice. The door was tall and green and flaking, and it bore an iron ring polished bright by hands, which meant hands still came. He lifted it and let it fall. The knock went through the whole door like a pulse.

He waited. Somewhere above him a shutter creaked; somewhere below, a cat moved through standing water with a sound like tearing paper. He counted to sixty the way he counted intervals between fog signals, out of habit, because counting was how he held himself together when the world refused to hold itself.

He knocked again.

Footsteps now—heavy, unhurried, descending stone stairs behind the door. A bolt drawn back with a sound like a bone breaking. The door opened the width of a hand and an eye found him through the gap: brown, level, unimpressed.

"Who sent you?"

"A priest," Petra said. "At the shrine of San—" He stopped. He would not say the name into an open street. "A priest who knew my wife."

The eye did not blink. "Your wife is dead seven years."

"I know how long she's been dead."

The silence stretched. Then, quietly, almost gently, the voice behind the door said: "I know how long she's been dead, too." The door opened all the way. "Come inside, custodian. You look like a man who has been rained on since December."

* * *

Elena Sarti was smaller than her voice and older than her reputation and she moved through the house the way water moves through rock—without asking permission. She led him up three flights of stairs past rooms that smelled of ink and cold coffee and lamp oil, past a window where a stack of galley proofs leaned against the wall like kindling, into a back room where a desk stood buried under newspapers in four languages and a photograph of a man with a magnificent mustache hung slightly crooked over the hearth.

She lit the lamp herself, turned the wick low, and studied him across the flame.

"You're thinner than the notice," she said.

"The notice flatters everyone."

"It flattered Ferrante for thirty years. That was the problem." She sat down behind the desk, and only then did he see that her left hand was bandaged at two fingers, wrapped in a strip of linen gone gray at the edges. She caught him looking. "Compositor's cramp. We've been setting type by hand since Tuesday. Sit down, Marchetti. Tell me what you carried and tell me fast."

"You have it already?"

"I have a letter." Her eyes were very steady. "Sealed, wax broken once and re-sealed with my own stamp, delivered yesterday morning by a boy of perhaps nineteen wearing a customs guard's greatcoat two sizes too large for him. He would not come inside. He stood on the step like a stork and handed it over and said, 'From the keeper of the light, who could not carry it himself.' Then he asked me—" and here something moved across her face, some weather too quick to name— "he asked me if it was true that a newspaper could frighten a rich man. I told him yes, if enough people read it on the same morning. And he nodded, very seriously, and went away down Via Garibaldi like a boy late for supper."

Petra closed his eyes. The room swayed gently, the way the tower swayed in a gale, though the floor was stone and still. "Tomasso," he said. "His mother was Lucia's friend. I never learned her name. Only that there was a debt."

"There are more debts attached to your wife than to half the banks in this city," Elena said, without cruelty. "That is why the letter reached me at all. Now." She drew the letter from beneath a folio of proofs and laid it on the desk between them, and Petra saw the wax, broken and re-stamped, and beside it a small mountain of papers—the evidence itself, copied, translated, cross-referenced in a hand that slanted forward like a runner leaning into wind. Manifests. Ledgers. Letters bearing Ferrante's own signature, authorizing the scuttling of the *Andrea Doria Vecchia* and the *Sant'Anna*, insured above their worth, crews written off as weather. Names of port officials with sums beside them. Dates going back eleven years.

Eleven years. Two years before Lucia married him.

"How much of this did you know?" he asked. It came out harder than he intended, and he did not take it back.

"More than most. Less than you think." She met his anger without flinching. "I have been writing about Aldo Ferrante for six years, Marchetti. Every time I got close, a source withdrew, a document vanished, an editor above me grew suddenly cautious or suddenly wealthy. Six years of building a case out of fog. Your wife's network spent twenty years moving this kind of paper out of Ferrante's offices and into safe hands, one link at a time, and they called it smuggling because smuggling was the only honest trade left." She tapped the ledger with her bandaged hand. "This is the first time the whole chain has ever sat on one desk. Because a drowned man strapped it to his chest, and a lighthouse keeper walked it through three checkpoints on a face the whole province is hunting."

"You know about the checkpoints."

"I know about Riva."

The name landed in the room like a dropped anchor. Petra heard himself say, "Then you know he'll follow the same thread I followed. The guard. Tomasso. If he found me at the gate, he can find the boy who let me go."

"He already has tried." Elena's voice stayed level, and that was worse than alarm. "There was a man at the corner of the street yesterday evening. Gray coat, gray hat, standing under the arcade opposite number 41 for four hours. He had the patience of a paid man, not a curious one. My typesetter made him first—I taught her to count faces the way sailors count waves. He left after dark. He will be back, or his colleagues will, because Riva does not send one dog where he can send a pack." She leaned forward. "Which brings us to the question I have been waiting all day to ask you. Are you ready to run? Because if you are, there is a fishing boat out of Nervi, a hold full of sardines, and a skipper who owes me, and you can be in France by Thursday."

Petra looked at her for a long moment.

"No," he said.

"Think carefully. You have been running since the island. No one would blame—"

"I said no." He heard his own voice and recognized it: it was the voice he used in storms, the one that came out flat and calm because everything underneath it was burning. "I spent seven years being invisible, signora. I did it well. I was so good at it that I missed an entire life happening across the breakfast table from me. I am finished with invisible. And besides—" he gestured at the papers, at the letter, at the crooked photograph of the mustached man— "that boy gave up his career, maybe his freedom, maybe his neck, to put this on your desk. If I run to France, then everything was for nothing. Him. Bruno. The priest. Lucia. All of it."

Something changed in Elena Sarti's face. Not softening exactly—it was more like a lock turning.

"Do you know what my editors say?" she asked. "My publisher says run it next week, when the political winds shift. The lawyers say soften the names. The printer says the presses are booked for a consignment of olive oil labels until Saturday." She smiled, and it was not a warm smile; it was the smile of a woman sharpening something. "And I say the presses roll tonight. Tonight, while Ferrante still believes his lost courier is sleeping at the bottom of the sea and his enforcer is chasing shadows toward France. Tomorrow he will know. Tonight he is only afraid, and fear makes powerful men slow."

"And if Riva comes tonight?"

"Then he comes to a print shop full of compositors, proofreaders, my sister, and forty reams of wet paper." She stood, wincing at her hand, and began pulling shutters closed against the streetlight. "But you're wrong about one thing, Marchetti. You said if you ran, it was all for nothing. That's not why you stay. You stay because this—" she touched the ledger again, lightly, the way one touches a sleeping child— "is not cargo anymore. You told the priest that, I'd wager. It took me twenty years to learn it. Paper is only paper until someone is willing to be seen holding it. After that, it's testimony."

Outside, the wind arrived. It came off the harbor in a long moaning rush, rattling the shutters she had just closed, driving rain against the glass in fistfuls. Somewhere below in the street, a lamplighter fought his ladder against the gusts and gave up, and Via Garibaldi went dark except for the one flame between them.

"That's early," Petra said quietly. "The storm. It's days ahead of the barometer. On the island we'd have called it—"

"Don't say it."

"—a bad omen," he finished anyway, and found, to his surprise, that he was smiling. "Lucia hated when I said it. Said omens were just weather that hadn't learned manners yet."

Elena paused with her hand on the lamp. "What else did she hate? What else did she love? I printed two paragraphs about her network in '09 and never knew the woman at all." She shook her head. "Later. There's no later unless we work. Can you set type?"

"No."

"Can you feed paper into a press without losing fingers?"

"I trim lamps for a living. I can keep my fingers."

"Then welcome to the regiment, custodian." She snuffed the desk lamp and the darkness folded around them, warm with ink and oil, and beyond the shutters the storm gathered itself over the city like a lung filling. "We have six hours until dawn, thirty-two pages, and one name that has waited eleven years for daylight. Let us see which runs out first—the night, or our luck."

They went downstairs together into the smell of molten lead, where the compositors looked up from their cases at the stranger, and one old woman with ink to her elbows looked longest, and then simply moved over and made room.

None of them saw, three streets away and closing, a carriage with no lamps moving slowly through the rain.
