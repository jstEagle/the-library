## 22. A Regiment of One

The lamp on Elena Sarti's desk was a cheap thing, brass gone green at the seams, bought from some junk stall near the port for three lire. It had no history. It had never guided anyone home. Petra trimmed its wick anyway.

He did it without thinking—the way a man who has spent seven years alone at night does everything: with his hands before his mind, his fingers finding the little brass wheel, turning it until the flame stood tall and clean-edged, the glass chimney wiped with his sleeve so the light would not be dimmed by his own breath's residue. Only when he finished did he realize what he'd done, and that Elena was watching him from across the desk with her ink-stained fingers laced together and an expression he had learned to read as carefully as weather.

"You do it like a priest," she said. "Like it matters."

"It does matter," Petra said. "That was the whole of my life for seven years. A light that answers no one."

"And now?"

He set the trimmer down. Outside the window, Genoa was doing what Genoa did in the first gray hour after dawn—unloading fish, arguing about prices, sweeping last night's papers off the cobblestones. But among those papers now were copies of *La Nuova Alba*, four days old and into its second printing, with Ferrante's face above the fold and the word *ARRESTATO* beneath it in letters the size of sins. A boy in the street below was selling them by voice, his cry rising up through the window glass like something alive.

"Now," Petra said, "I don't know yet."

* * *

The offer had come three days earlier, in this same office, though the room had changed since then. Riva's men had come through it with boots and bayonets during the raid; there was still a gouge in the floorboards where a rifle butt had slipped, and the door hung slightly crooked on its hinges, as if the building itself had flinched and never quite recovered. Elena refused to have either repaired. "Let them look at it," she said. "Let everyone look at it."

She had sat across from him then too, but the light had been different—lamplight then, the yellow light of siege—and now it was morning, honest morning, the kind that shows everything including dust.

"I need an archivist," she said. "And I need a courier I can trust, which in this city currently means a courier whose face half the province has memorized from a wanted notice. You are, at present, the most famous invisible man in Liguria." She smiled, but not all the way; she was a woman who rationed her smiles like a captain rations water. "It won't be comfortable work. The men who bought Ferrante are still buying. The ones more powerful than Ferrante—they weren't touched by any of this. They're waiting to see if we tire. Papers always tire. Editors get bought or buried or bored. I need someone who knows how to keep a small light burning through a long season, someone who understands that most of the work is repetition and nobody applauds."

"Seven years trimming wicks in fog," Petra said. "I know something about nobody applauding."

"I thought you might." She slid a paper across the desk—not a contract, just a page of notes in her quick slanted hand—but her hand stayed flat on it, holding it, and she looked at him with that directness that had frightened him the first week and steadied him ever since. "Before you answer, Marchetti. Understand what you'd be giving up. This isn't the island. There will be no solitude. Your name is going to be in print, attached to things, argued about. Some people will love you for the wrong reasons and hate you for worse ones. The quiet you built—you'll never get it back exactly as it was."

Petra looked out the window. Below, the boy was still crying the news, and a woman had stopped to buy a paper, and Petra watched her fold it under her arm and walk on into her ordinary day carrying an extraordinary thing, not even knowing its weight.

"The quiet I built," he said slowly, "was a wall. I told myself it was peace. It was a wall with one lamp behind it and one man." He turned back to her. "I'm done building walls. I'd rather keep a light other people can find."

Elena nodded once, and pushed the page across, and that was that. No ceremony. In the new world they were all trying to build, Petra suspected ceremony would be the first luxury to go.

* * *

His rooms were two streets from the office, over a cobbler's shop, and they were nothing like the lighthouse—which was precisely why he had chosen them. Stairs he shared with others. A landlady, Signora Baffico, who knocked on his door to complain about the weather and stayed to complain about her son-in-law, and expected answers, and got them. Neighbors who knew his step on the stairs. He had lived seven years being overlooked, perfecting it like a craft, and now he was learning the opposite discipline: to be known. To say good morning first. To be predictable in the way that lets people build their lives around you a little.

Some nights it exhausted him more than any storm had.

Tonight he climbed the stairs past the smell of leather and glue, lit the cheap green-brass lamp, and sat down at the table where Lucia's letters waited in their bundle, tied with the same twine he had untied and retied a dozen times since the island.

He had read them all now. That was the strange arithmetic of grief—that it took a drowned stranger and a burning lighthouse and a winter of running to bring him, finally, to the reading of letters his wife had written and never sent, hidden where only the second key could reach. Forty-one letters. To no one. Or to everyone. Or to the woman she had been before she was his wife, the girl who had run messages for a network of priests and fishermen and ferry-men who believed that truth, like contraband, moved best at night and in small boats.

*I married a man who keeps a light,* she wrote in the ninth letter, *and I have decided that this is not a contradiction of who I am but a completion of it. Aldo Ferrante moves ships full of lies and calls it commerce. My husband moves light across the water and asks nothing back. Someday, if I am brave, I will tell him what I know. Someday.*

Someday had never come. She had died in the spring, quickly, the fever taking her between one tide and the next, and Petra had mourned her as the woman he knew—and had not known, until this winter, that he was also mourning a woman he had never met at all. That discovery should have broken something in him. For a while, in the shrine in the mountains, with the storm sealing the passes and the old priest listening, he had thought it would. *Was our marriage itself a hiding place?* he had asked, and the question had tasted like rust.

But the letters answered it, in the end, in Lucia's own hand. She hadn't hidden from him. She had hidden *for* him—or believed she did—and the tragedy wasn't deception; it was the someday she kept postponing, the conversation two careful people kept deciding could wait until the weather cleared. And weather, on that coast, rarely clears for long.

He had forgiven her. It had happened not in one moment but the way ice goes off a roof—all at once, long after you'd stopped watching for it. And he had forgiven himself, which was harder, for the blindness he called respect, the silence he called privacy, all the years he had spoken aloud to her ghost about wind and wicks and never once asked her who she had been. Forgiveness, he was learning, was less a feeling than a maintenance task. You did it daily, like trimming a lamp, whether or not you felt like it, and the light stayed clean because you tended it.

* * *

In the morning he went down to the harbor, because Elena needed the Milan dispatches carried to the typesetter by noon and trusted no one else's route, and because—though he would not have admitted it—he liked walking through the city as himself.

That was new. For one whole winter he had been a man whose own face was a liability, printed on walls, hunted through checkpoints, betrayed by a traveler for thirty lire. He had learned invisibility so well it had nearly become him again, the old habit wearing new clothes. And then the story broke, and the notices came down, and something else went up in their place: not posters, but glances. Recognition. The fishwife at the gate who pressed a paper into his hand and said, *You're the keeper, aren't you, the one from the print shop.* The students who raised their caps to him outside *La Nuova Alba*'s offices as if he were a general. The old women who crossed themselves—not in fear anymore, he had decided, but in the particular way coastal people blessed anything that had survived the sea.

Fame, he discovered, was just another kind of visibility, and visibility was just another kind of weather. You didn't fight it. You dressed for it.

At the typesetter's, the apprentice—a boy of maybe sixteen with ink to his elbows—took the dispatches and then hesitated, working up to something.

"My father says you were a lighthouse keeper," the boy said. "On the island. Before."

"Twenty-two years," Petra said. "Give or take."

"Is it true you signaled across the rooftops with mirrors? During the raid? That the whole city saw?"

Petra considered lying, out of modesty, and found he had used up his ration of hiding. "It's true. Old smugglers' signals. My wife taught me the codes without telling me what they were for." He smiled. "She'd have liked that night very much."

The boy grinned and ran the dispatches inside, and Petra stood a moment in the street among the noise and the smell of ink and salt, and let himself be seen a while longer than was strictly necessary.

* * *

A letter came at the end of the month, forwarded through the newspaper, postmarked from the mainland. Bruno's hand, big and seamanlike, the pen pressing hard enough to emboss the page.

*The light is automated now,* Bruno wrote. *They put in one of those new machines, no keeper wanted. I rowed out last week to see it with my own eyes. It turns and turns and doesn't care if anyone watches, which I suppose is progress. The house stands empty. The government wants to sell it. Thought you'd want to know. Thought maybe you'd want to come see it once, before strangers do. A man ought to say goodbye to his own walls.*

Petra read it twice at his table, the green lamp burning beside him, and felt the old ache move through him—not grief exactly, and not regret. Recognition. Like hearing a song you danced to at your wedding played at a funeral.

He had lost the lighthouse. He had known that since the night Riva's men burned the records and the ministry, embarrassed by the scandal touching even its own coastal appointments, had quietly abolished the post. For months he had carried the loss like a stone, assuming it was permanent, part of the terrible cost the winter had charged him. But stones, he was learning, could be set down.

He would go. Not to stay. To stand in the tower one more time, and read Lucia's letters in the place they were written, and forgive the walls themselves for what they had sheltered and what they had concealed. To thank the light, in whatever language lights understand, for keeping him alive long enough to find out who he was.

And then he would come back down the mountain road, and take the ferry, and walk up the cobbler's stairs, and be home—because home, he understood now, was not a tower or an island or a woman's arms. Home was a thing you chose, daily, the way you trimmed a lamp: deliberately, attentively, whether or not anyone applauded.

* * *

The evening of the spring equinox, Elena worked late, and Petra stayed with her—not as staff, but as company, the distinction mattering more than any wage. The presses thundered below like a held breath released, and the office filled with the smell of hot metal and oil, and through the windows the city glittered its ten thousand lamps all the way down to the black water.

"There's a story coming," Elena said at midnight, not looking up from her proofs. "Milan. Bigger than Ferrante. The ones who bought him—they're exposed too, or will be, if we can get documents out of a ministry archive without anyone hanging for it." She finally looked up. "It means a courier. Someone careful. Someone the archives clerk will trust on sight, because his face has been in the papers for the right reasons."

"When?" Petra said.

"Three weeks. Maybe four."

Petra nodded. Then, out of habit older than the republic of his new life, he took up the trimmer and attended to the green brass lamp—turned the wheel, squared the flame, wiped the chimney with his sleeve until the light stood clean and tall on the desk between them.

Elena watched him do it and said nothing, but she was smiling when he finished.

He did not turn the lamp low for the night. He left it burning bright, the way you leave a light in a window for someone crossing dark water, and went down the crooked stairs into the city that knew his name, toward the harbor where the ferries waited, and the island beyond them where his old walls stood empty in the spring wind—carrying, folded against his heart, the address of a stranger in Milan who had promised to send him the first document, and the memory of a wife who had once written, in a letter never sent: *Someday, if I am brave.*

Someday had arrived. He walked toward it with his collar open and his face to the wind, a regiment of one, marching.
