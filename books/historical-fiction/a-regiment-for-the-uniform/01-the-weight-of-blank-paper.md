## 1. The Weight of Blank Paper

The candle had burned down to the last knuckle of its life when Hazel Eriksen finally set down her pen and admitted what her wrist had been telling her for an hour: it was time.

She flexed her fingers, one by one, and watched the ink dry on the chart before her — the Skarvholm approaches, corrected. Not *corrected*, she reminded herself. *Continued*. In her own mind she was careful with the words, because words had a way of escaping through cracks and becoming other people's property. Aldric Vane had begun this atlas. She was merely finishing it, in his hand, in his style, with his abbreviations and his particular way of drawing soundings like a row of patient little teeth. That was all. Anyone who said otherwise — anyone inside her own skull who said otherwise at three in the morning — could be ignored on grounds of exhaustion.

Outside the workshop windows, Bergen was doing what Bergen did in November: raining sideways, with opinions. The gutters ran loud enough to cover the scratch of a pen, which was convenient, because the scratch of a pen at three in the morning belonged to no one the guild needed to know about.

Hazel rose, stretched until something in her back went off like a small firework, and crossed to the shelf where the atlas lived by day. It was a magnificent lie of a shelf — dusty, disorganized, stacked with genuine clutter she'd collected for the purpose. The draft sat in the middle of it wearing a plain brown wrapper, labeled in her most boring handwriting: *VANE — COASTAL SURVEY, UNFINISHED. DO NOT REORDER.*

Boring was armor. She'd learned that early. The guild inspectorate could smell ambition at forty paces; they never once looked twice at a thing labeled DO NOT REORDER.

She slid the atlas back between two ledgers, blew out the candle, and stood a moment in the dark, listening to the rain and the sea arguing below the cliff road.

"Aldric," she said quietly, to nobody. "Your coastline is a menace and your handwriting is worse. I hope you appreciate this."

Nobody answered, which was correct. Dead men were very good listeners and terrible correspondents. It had been four months now since the boat went over at Fedje with him aboard, four months since the guild had held its solemn memorial service where Director Rask had spoken movingly about loss and diligence while standing slightly too close to Aldric's empty chair, as though measuring it for someone else. Four months since Hazel had gone home and opened Aldric's desk drawer — the one he'd always locked, the one whose key she'd found taped under the inkwell two weeks after the funeral, along with a note in his cramped hand: *If you're reading this, I'm either dead or extremely late. Either way, keep working.*

So she kept working. Every night. It was not courage. Courage sounded grander. This was closer to laundry — a thing you did because stopping would mean the pile won.

* * *

Morning arrived gray and punctual, as mornings in the workshop always did, heralded by Signe banging the stove door like it owed her money.

"Up," Signe said, to the room generally, because Hazel was already up and Bram was pretending otherwise from behind his partition of reference folios. "Kettle's on. Also we've got an inspector coming at ten."

Hazel's hand paused on the teapot. "We have a what?"

"Guild inspector. Voss." Signe read from the note in her hand with the flat delivery she reserved for bad news and bad poetry alike. "'Routine inspection of apprentice work and inventory.' Routine, apparently, is why it's signed personally and delivered by courier instead of posted."

"Maybe he likes us," offered Bram from behind his folios, in the tone of a man who knows he is wrong and is saying it anyway to hear how it sounds.

"Nobody from the directorate likes us," said Signe. "They *audit* us."

Hazel poured tea with the steadiness of long practice, which was fortunate, because her pulse was doing something unsuitable. Voss. Inspector Voss had visited twice since Aldric's death, both times with the same soft questions wrapped in the same polite wool. *How is the master's unfinished work being handled? Who has access? Has anything been continued in his absence?* And each time she'd shown him the brown-wrapped draft and said, truthfully, that it sat untouched, that grief had stalled everything, that the guild should feel free to send proper instructions whenever it pleased.

Lying, she had discovered, was mostly a matter of preparation. She'd been preparing for four months. She was getting quite good at it, and hated herself a little more each time it worked.

"Right," she said. "Signe, the apprentice sheets need to look apprentice-y. Take out anything you did after midnight, it'll only confuse him."

"My work doesn't look apprentice-y?"

"Your coastlines are better than mine," Hazel said. "That's the problem."

Signe flushed with pleasure and tried to hide it and failed completely, which was one of the reasons Hazel had recruited her — well, hadn't recruited her, exactly, but had let her stay close, which amounted to the same thing in a workshop of six people. Bram emerged from behind the folios, blinking, tall and rumpled as an unmade bed. "What do you want me to do?"

"Nothing suspicious."

"I'm excellent at nothing suspicious. Last month I spent an entire afternoon doing nothing suspicious and nobody noticed me once."

"That's the spirit," said Hazel, and went to get the inventory book, because if Voss wanted paper, paper was what he'd get.

* * *

Inspector Voss arrived at ten o'clock precisely, which told Hazel he was a man who took the ferry timetable more seriously than most men took scripture. He was narrow and neat, with a coat brushed within an inch of its life and eyes that moved around a room the way a customs officer's hands moved through luggage — thorough, courteous, and missing nothing.

"Miss Eriksen." A small bow. "You'll forgive the hour. The charter review keeps us all running."

"The guild works hard," Hazel said pleasantly. "Tea?"

"Thank you, no." He never accepted tea. She offered every time anyway; refusal was data, and so far the data said Voss was here to look, not to settle in.

He walked the workshop slowly. He looked at the drying racks, at the apprentice sheets, at Bram's carefully mediocre practice charts — Bram had outdone himself, producing a rendering of the Hjeltefjord so blandly competent it made Hazel's teeth ache — and at last, as she knew he would, at the shelf.

"And the master's great work?" Voss asked.

Hazel fetched the brown wrapper and laid it on the table between them, untying it just enough to show edges of paper, not content. "As it was when you saw it in September. We haven't the heart to touch it, honestly. It feels like—" She stopped, and let the pause do the work. Grief was the one material she never had to fake.

Voss studied her for a moment. "Of course. It must be difficult."

"It is what it is." She retied the string. "The guild will want it archived eventually, I expect."

"In time. These things take time." His gaze drifted across her face with mild, professional interest, and then past her shoulder, to the window, to the rain, to nothing at all. "You've kept the master's methods alive among the apprentices, I see. His notation. His style."

"They learned from him. It shows."

"Mm." He turned his hat in his hands once, twice. "It shows," he agreed, in a voice that agreed with absolutely nothing, and Hazel felt the first cold thread of it go down her spine: the sense — faint, absurd, deniable — that Voss wasn't inspecting the workshop at all. That he was checking it. The way you check a knot.

He thanked her. He bowed. At the door he paused and said, without turning around, "You work late, Miss Eriksen. The lamp in your corner burns past midnight some nights. The watchman mentioned it."

"Do I?" Hazel said, surprised and innocent, and was privately furious at how easily the surprise came. "I sleep badly, since— well. Since."

"Since," Voss said gently. "Of course. Grief keeps odd hours." He settled his hat. "Do try to rest. The guild values its people."

And then he was gone down the wet stairs, precise as clockwork, and Hazel stood in the doorway holding a smile she no longer needed, feeling it slide off her face like the rain off the eaves.

* * *

That evening she didn't light the lamp in her corner. She worked instead in the map archive at the back, by candlelight, with the shutters closed, and she thought hard about knots.

Because that was the thing about Voss's remark — it wasn't a warning, or not only a warning. It was a *measurement*. He'd told her he knew about the lamp to see what she did with the knowledge. If she stopped burning it, she confirmed it meant something. If she kept burning it, she confirmed she had nothing to hide. Either way, he'd be watching which way she jumped, and the trap was that there was no direction without weight.

Aldric would have laughed at her. *You're thinking like an honest woman, Hazy,* he'd have said, sharpening his pen knife on the strap he kept for no other purpose, because honest women were the only ones worth teaching to think crooked. *Stop asking whether he suspects. Ask what he wants. Nobody walks up a hill in November to admire the view.*

What does Voss want?

She pulled the atlas out and turned to the marginalia — Aldric's coded notes in the margins, tiny, dense, disguised as corrections and reminders. She'd been deciphering them for weeks. Most were survey notes, depth readings, tide tables in his private shorthand. But scattered through them, in a cipher keyed to his old field journal (which she also had, thank you, taped-under-the-inkwell man), were phrases that made her blood run cold and hot by turns. *Discrepancy at Skerries — chart says 14 fathoms, my line says 9. Whose line is true? Follow the insurance filings.* And once, in letters so small she'd needed the magnifier: *R. knows. If I go overboard, it wasn't the weather.*

She had read that line forty times and it had never once gotten easier.

Hazel sat back. The candle guttered. Somewhere below, the tide was turning against the harbor stones, and the rain had eased to that fine drifting mist that made the whole coast look like an unfinished sketch of itself.

The lamp question, she decided at last, had an answer she didn't like: she would burn it again tonight, and every night, same hours, same corner. Let Voss measure routine. Routine was the best disguise she owned — four months of it, worn smooth as sea glass. The atlas would stay on its dull shelf in its dull wrapper, and she would go on being the quiet apprentice with the dead mentor and the bad sleep, and Voss could stand in the rain taking notes on her lamplight until his ink froze.

It was a fine plan. It held together right up until she heard the knock.

Not the workshop door — that was bolted at dusk. This was softer, lower, coming from the tide gate at the bottom of the cliff stair, the little postern where fishermen came up from the water with their catch. Nobody legitimate knocked there at half past ten in November. Nobody illegitimate knocked at all, usually; they simply left things to be found.

Hazel put out the candle. She took up the poker, because sentimentality had limits, and went down the stair on feet she willed to be quiet, heart going like a signal drum.

Through the iron grate of the gate, by the light of the harbor lanterns below, she could see a figure on the landing — a man, soaked through, holding the rail with one hand like the ground had recently disappointed him. A ferryman's cloak, salt-stiff. A leather satchel strapped across his chest, held the way sailors hold things they've sworn to drown before surrendering.

Even at that distance, even through the mist, she could see the satchel was heavy with paper.

The man lifted his head. He had the look of someone who had been traveling a long time toward a specific door, and finding it, had suddenly forgotten every word he'd prepared.

"Miss Eriksen?" he called up, voice rough as a fouled hawser. "My name is Torvald. I've come from the south — from Fedje." A breath, and then, in a rush, as if the words might escape if he gave them time: "I have letters. From Master Aldric Vane. Written *after* his funeral."

The poker suddenly felt very small in her hand, and very necessary, and entirely beside the point.

The rain fell. The harbor lamps swung. And Hazel Eriksen, keeper of routines, stood at the tide gate with her whole careful life balanced on the head of a pin, and reached for the latch.
