## 2. A Stranger at the Tide Gate

The tide gate was the first thing Aldric Eriksen had ever taught me to draw, back when my lines trembled like a nervous bird's. "Everything in this town," he'd said, tapping his pen against the paper, "comes in and goes out through that one mouth. Learn to draw it honestly, girl, and you'll never have to lie about anything else."

I had thought of that lesson often since the funeral, usually at inconvenient moments. This morning it came back to me as I stood at the workshop window with a cup of tea gone cold in my hand, watching a stranger come up the harbor road through the rain.

He came the way the tide comes — steadily, without any apparent interest in whether anyone wanted him there.

Now, I should explain something about Skarvstrand in November. Nobody visits Skarvstrand in November. The herring boats have gone south, the summer lodgers have long since fled back to Bergen or Kristiania where the coffee is respectable and the wind is merely unpleasant rather than personal, and the ferry from the mainland runs twice a week on the principle that even a schedule should be allowed to grieve occasionally. So when a man stepped off the Wednesday boat — a big man, broad as a doorframe, wearing a coat that had lost its argument with the weather some hours ago — every eye on the harbor road turned to him, including mine, including Mrs. Holm's, and Mrs. Holm had not looked up from her knitting since the king's jubilee.

He was tall, which was unremarkable. He was wet, which was inevitable. But there was something in the way he walked that made me set down my tea. He walked like a man following directions he didn't trust, checking each turning against something written on his face. And when he reached the corner by the salt sheds, he stopped, took out a folded paper, consulted it, and then looked directly across the square at our workshop window.

At me.

I did what any sensible apprentice does when observed by a stranger: I stepped back from the glass so fast I knocked over the pen tray, swore in a manner Aldric would have charged me a copper for, and pretended to be extremely busy with an inkstone that required no attention whatsoever.

"Hazel." Signe's voice floated up from the binding table below. "What was that?"

"Nothing," I said. "A cat."

"It sounded like furniture."

"A large cat."

I heard her laugh, and then, because Signe was Signe, she appeared at the foot of the stairs wiping her hands on her apron, dark-eyed and skeptical, holding the look she kept reserved for my lies, which were famously poor. "There's a man at the gate," she said.

"There is no man at the gate."

"He's ringing the bell."

"I don't hear a bell."

The bell rang.

* * *

We had a system for visitors, Signe and I, established after the funeral when the guild began sending people around with questions shaped like sympathy. Inspector Voss himself had visited four times in three months, each time asking to see the atlas draft, each time accepting my explanation that it was unfinished, disordered, and frankly embarrassing, and each time lingering in the workroom a little longer than courtesy required, running his finger along the shelf edges as though measuring them for something.

So: visitors were met at the outer door, never invited past the drying racks. The atlas draft lived under the floorboards beneath the drafting table, wrapped in oilcloth, because grief makes you careful and suspicion makes you thorough. Aldric's other papers were sorted into three piles — innocuous, ambiguous, and burn-if-asked — and I knew the location of all three piles the way I knew my own teeth.

I opened the outer door six inches, which is the width of politeness without invitation.

"Yes?"

The man on the step was worse up close, if anything. Rain had gotten into everything about him — his hair plastered flat, his collar dripping, his boots leaving small brown lakes on our stone. He was perhaps forty, with a seamed face and the particular sunlessness of someone who spent his working life outdoors in Norwegian weather and had made peace with it the way one makes peace with a chronic injury. Over his shoulder he carried a canvas satchel, and his hand was resting on the flap of it in a way that suggested the contents mattered more to him than staying dry ever could.

"Miss Eriksen?" he said.

"No," I said, which was true, technically, because I was Miss *Hazel*, apprentice, and Eriksen had been the master's name, and the distinction had been the whole architecture of my life for eleven years. "This is the mapmaker's workshop. If you want charts, we're closed for the season."

"I'm not here for charts."

"Then you're lost. The church is behind you. The inn is left at the well. The ferry leaves Saturday."

"I've come from Aldric Eriksen," he said.

The name went through me the way cold water goes through a boot. I gripped the door edge. Somewhere inside me, the disciplined machinery of the last five months — rise, stoke the stove, grind the pigment, work until the light failed, do not think, do not feel, finish the book — gave a single grinding shudder, like a wagon hitting a rut.

"Aldric Eriksen," I repeated carefully, "is dead. He was buried in the spring. I attended. I dug part of the grave myself, actually, because the ground was soft and the sexton has a bad back. It is a thing one remembers."

"Yes," said the stranger. "I know. I carried the letters."

"You carried—" My mind snagged on the plural. "Letters."

He held out the satchel. "May I come in? I'd rather not show these on your doorstep. There's been a man asking questions at the ferry office, and I don't love being watched while I'm wet."

And there it was again — the prickle between my shoulder blades that had been visiting me for weeks now, the sense of eyes on the workshop windows, of footsteps pausing outside too long. I had told myself it was nothing. Grief does that; it teaches you to see watchers in every shadow, the way hunger teaches you to smell bread.

But this man had just confirmed, casually, as though remarking on the weather, that there *was* a watcher.

I looked at him for a long moment. Rain dripped off his nose with what I can only describe as patience.

"If you're lying to me," I said, "I should tell you that I know exactly where every heavy object in this workshop is located. Professionally."

"That's fair," he said. "I'm Torvald. Torvald Renn. I was your master's courier."

"We had couriers?"

"No," he said, and something moved at the corner of his mouth — not quite a smile, but a relative of one. "That was rather the point of me."

I let him in.

* * *

Signe took one look at him and put the kettle on, which is her answer to most crises and all strangers, and within minutes he was installed at the workbench nearest the stove with a towel and a cup of coffee so strong it could have tanned leather. Bram arrived from the store room, saw the stranger, raised both eyebrows at me in silent interrogation, and received my best attempt at a reassuring expression, which Signe later told me resembled indigestion.

Torvald Renn drank his coffee like a man who had learned not to trust hot things to last, and then he set the satchel on the bench between us with the care of a surgeon laying out instruments.

"I'll say it plainly," he said. "Plainly is all I've got. I was a courier for the guild for nine years. Special routes. Packages that didn't go through the ledger. Aldric Eriksen was one of maybe four people in the directorate who knew my face and trusted me, and the only one who ever asked what I thought about anything besides delivery times." He paused. "Eleven months ago he started sending letters through me that weren't guild business. Personal letters. To people on the coast. Widows, mostly. Then, in March, he sent me three letters with instructions: hold them, watch, and if certain things happened — if men came asking at his house, if the workshop was searched, if anyone official expressed sudden tender interest in his unfinished atlas — deliver them to Skarvstrand. To the mapmaker's shop. Not to the guild. Not to his family, because he hadn't any. To whoever was keeping the shop."

"To whoever was finishing the atlas," I said quietly.

His eyes came up to mine. They were grey, steady, and entirely without pity, which I found myself grateful for.

"To the one who finishes it," he said. "Those were his words. I asked him how I'd know who that was. He said, 'You'll know her because she'll lie to you at the door and make good coffee after.'" He glanced at Signe. "Or possibly that's the coffee."

"The coffee is Signe's," I admitted.

"Well, then half right. That's better than my usual record."

My heart was behaving badly. I pressed my hands flat on the bench to give it something else to consider. "Mr. Renn. Aldric Eriksen is dead. Whatever arrangement you had with him ended in April, when we buried him. If you've brought debts or guild business—"

"I've brought this."

He opened the satchel and drew out three letters, tied with the plain twine Aldric used for everything, and laid them on the bench in front of me.

I knew the handwriting before I knew anything else about them.

You have to understand what that meant to me. For eleven years I had sat close enough to that hand to hear the nib breathe. Aldric wrote the way he drew coastlines — economical, slightly forward-leaning, with a peculiar flat-topped *d* that he'd developed, he claimed, to save time and I suspected he'd developed because his old master had written round-topped ones and he'd hated him. I had forged that hand for practice exercises. I had corrected apprentices' labels against it. I had spent five months writing annotations in it, alone, at night, trying to keep the atlas's voice alive, telling myself it was for consistency's sake, knowing perfectly well it was because I couldn't bear for the book to sound like it had lost him.

I picked up the top letter. My own hands were steadier than I deserved.

The date read the 14th of May.

Aldric Eriksen died on the 3rd of April. I knew because I had helped carry him to the churchyard, and because the date was carved into the slate marker that I cleaned of lichen every Sunday, and because grief keeps accounts the way tide tables do — exact, indifferent, relentless.

"This date," I said. My voice came out very level, which frightened me more than shaking would have. "This is six weeks after the funeral."

"Yes," said Torvald.

"This is his handwriting."

"Yes."

"This is *impossible*."

"Read it," he said gently. "It's addressed to you. Well — it's addressed to 'the apprentice at the Skarvstrand workshop, who will be angry.'"

I opened it. I would like to report that I read it with composure. What actually happened is that I read the first line — *If you are reading this, Hazel, then either I am dead or I am about to wish I were* — and had to set it down and look out the window at the rain for a while, breathing the way Signe had taught me during the worst week in April, in-through-the-nose, count to four, the storm passes, the chart remains.

Signe put her hand briefly on my shoulder and went back to the kettle, giving me the privacy of ordinary noise. That is the kind of friend she is.

When I could, I read it properly.

*Hazel — By now you will have seen the dates and decided I am either a ghost or a fraud, and I take some comfort in knowing you'll be furious about it, because fury is the correct response and you were always the correct responder. Listen carefully. The death was arranged. Mine. I will not write here how, because letters are taken from couriers sometimes and this must survive. Ask Renn; he knows enough to be believed and not enough to be dangerous. What you must know is this: the coastal charts issued these last six years contain errors that were not errors. They were edits. Ships have broken on rocks the charts said were clear water, and men have been paid for the losses, and the paying men sit in the directorate and sign their names very legibly.*

*The atlas you are finishing — and you will finish it, because you are stubborn in a way I counted on for eleven years — contains in its margins everything needed to prove it. You have been reading my notes for months without knowing they were testimony. I'm sorry for that. I'm sorry for all of it. But the plates, Hazel. Find the plates. The atlas will show you where.*

*Do not trust Voss. Do not trust the archive. Trust Renn, whom I paid poorly and who never once complained, which in my experience is the truest measure of a man.*

*Finish it. Put your name on it. I mean that. You will argue with me about it and I cannot hear you, which will finally give me the advantage in an argument.*

*— A.E.*

The workroom was very quiet. Even the stove seemed to be listening.

"He staged his death," I said at last. It wasn't a question. Questions end upward; this ended downward, like a sounding lead touching bottom.

"He staged his death," Torvald agreed. "With my help and two other people's, and it cost him nearly everything, and he did it because the alternative was letting the directorate bury the evidence along with him. A dead man's protest gets filed. A living man's gets answered — with a rope, if they catch him. He chose to be useful rather than safe."

"And the letters dated after — those were written before. Held back."

"Written before, dated forward. He planned it like a survey. Every contingency plotted before the expedition set out." Torvald turned his cup slowly on the bench. "I delivered the eulogy flowers to the church myself, Miss Hazel. I stood at the back of that funeral and watched you and the other apprentices cry, and I knew what I was carrying in my coat, and I have not slept properly since April. I'm telling you that so you understand I'm not enjoying any of this."

Bram, who had been silent in the doorway this entire time, spoke for the first time. "Who else knows he's alive?"

"Three people living. Me, and two others who helped with the staging and believe he's simply gone abroad. The directorate believes he's dead — that was rather the achievement — but Voss doesn't act like a man whose suspicions are settled. And there's been someone asking at the ferries. Someone official-shaped. Which is why I came straight here instead of waiting for a dry day, and why I'd take it kindly if we could discuss what happens next quickly, and why—" he hesitated, and for the first time the steady grey of him flickered, "—why I'd ask you not to decide anything tonight except whether I sleep in your loft or your woodshed. Either suits me. I've slept in worse. I once slept in a beached pilot boat that was occupied by gulls, and they had opinions."

Despite everything — despite the letter still open on the bench, despite the cold walking up my spine — I heard myself laugh. One short, cracked note. Aldric would have said it sounded like a hinge.

Because underneath the fear, underneath the impossible arithmetic of a dead man's handwriting, one sentence from that letter had already taken root and begun to grow, the way his sentences always had:

*You have been reading my notes for months without knowing they were testimony.*

I thought of the marginalia. Hundreds of them. Little coded remarks I had catalogued as quirks — the numbers beside soundings that matched no survey I knew, the tiny symbols in the corners of the coastal sheets, the phrases repeated in what I'd assumed was sentimentality. *Note the keeper's light.* *Ask the widow at Helle.* *Count twice, publish once.* Five months I had read them as a dead man's habits.

They were instructions. They had always been instructions. The atlas was not just a book to be finished.

It was a map. To its own vindication — and to whatever Aldric had hidden where only someone who loved it enough to finish it would ever think to look.

I looked up and found Torvald watching me with that same expression he'd worn on the harbor road: a man following directions he didn't quite trust yet, checking my face against something written on his.

"You've thought of something," he said.

"Yes," I said. And then, because the fear had arrived fully now — cold, specific, wearing Voss's face and standing in my doorway with its finger on the shelf edges — I reached down, lifted the loose board beneath the drafting table, and brought the atlas draft out into the lamplight where, for the first time since April, another living soul could see it.

"Then we'd better start reading the margins," I said, "before the man from the ferry office finishes his tea."
