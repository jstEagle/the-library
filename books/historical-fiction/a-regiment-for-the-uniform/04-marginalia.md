## 4. Marginalia

The lamp had burned down to a stubborn thumb of oil by the time I admitted I was not reading the atlas at all.

I was staring at it.

Three nights now I had sat with Aldric's draft spread across the worktable, and three nights the margins had been doing something to me that I could only describe as *smirking*. Not literally — Aldric had been many things, but a margin-smirker was not among them; he believed white space was a moral obligation — but there was something in those little penciled marks that refused to be ordinary. A squiggle beside the Skerries light. A small hooked cross near the Meridian shoal. Tiny numbers tucked into the flourishes of compass roses like coins sewn into a hem.

For two years I had assumed they were working notes. Draftsman's shorthand. The sort of thing every apprentice learns to ignore because half of it is "fetch more gum arabic" and the other half is Aldric complaining about his knees.

But Aldric had not died complaining about his knees. He had died — allegedly, apparently, impossibly — falling from the north pier on a night so calm the harbor water had lain flat as poured glass. And Torvald had arrived carrying letters dated after the funeral. And somewhere between those two facts, the margins had stopped being shorthand and started being a cipher.

"You've turned the page four times," Torvald said from the corner, where he sat mending a coat button with the intense concentration of a man who would rather do anything than be idle. "The same page. Twice forward, twice back."

"I'm thinking."

"Out loud it sounds like breathing and sighing."

"That *is* how I think."

He set down the needle. In three days he had learned the workshop's rhythms with unsettling speed — where the spare wicks lived, which floorboard creaked, that the kettle must never be left on Bram's side of the stove unless one wanted an argument. Now he crossed to the table and looked over my shoulder at the chart of the outer sound, at the innocent little squiggle beside the Skerries light.

"What does that mark mean?"

"It means Aldric wanted me to check the light's elevation against the 1888 survey." I said it automatically, the way you recite a catechism, and then heard myself. "Except I did check it. Two years ago. It was correct. There was nothing to check."

"So why write it?"

"Because—" I stopped.

Because Aldric never wrote a note about a thing that needed no noting. That was practically his eleventh commandment: *Ink is expensive, Hazel, spend it like blood.* If he'd written a correction note for an error that didn't exist, then the note wasn't about the light at all. The note was about the note.

I pulled the lamp closer. My hands were suddenly very steady, the way they go steady when something important is happening, which has always struck me as the body's strangest courtesy.

"Torvald," I said, "how well did you know his hand?"

"Well enough to carry it across half the coast without opening it. Why?"

"Because I think he's been talking to us this whole time. And we've been politely ignoring him."

* * *

We worked until the oil gave out entirely and I lit the second lamp, the good one with the cracked chimney that whistled faintly on the left side, like a kettle with opinions.

Here is what we found, and I will set it down plainly because my hands are still not entirely convinced it happened:

Aldric's margin notes came in two kinds. The honest kind — corrections, reminders, the small domestic debris of a working draftsman. And a second kind that looked exactly like the first but wasn't. They were keyed to each other. The note by the Skerries light ("check elev., 1888 surv.") paired with a tiny number hidden in the compass rose of the same sheet: 17. The hooked cross by the Meridian shoal paired with another rose-number: 3. A notation about tide-table errata on the Hvalvik chart carried its partner in the decorative border — a fish, of all things, drawn slightly too carefully to be decoration, with a number worked into the pattern of its scales: 22.

Seventeen. Three. Twenty-two.

"He's counting something," Torvald said. He had gone very quiet, in the way of men who have spent years around secrets and recognize their smell. "Or numbering something."

"Numbering *what*? There are only eleven sheets in the draft."

"No." He laid one finger on the Skerries chart. "*You* have eleven sheets. He was writing for someone who might have fewer. Or more. Someone who'd have to find the rest." He looked up at me. "Who else has seen these pages?"

"The guild archive took copies of the first survey series. Before he— before." I frowned. "And there were the reference folios. His private ones. They're still in the back room, in his locked case."

"Have you ever gone through them?"

"They're his," I said, and heard how it sounded, and hated it a little. "Were his. It felt like reading someone's letters."

"Hazel." Torvald said my name gently, the way you'd set down something breakable. "He faked his own death and sent a stranger to find you. I don't think he was saving those folios for privacy."

That stung, mostly because it was true. Grief makes a strange kind of etiquette out of avoidance — you tell yourself you're being respectful when really you're being afraid. I fetched the key from the drawer where Aldric kept everything worth keeping (the same drawer as the licorice drops and the spare nibs, because the man organized his life like a poem rather than a ledger), and opened the case.

Nine reference folios. Leather-bound, salt-spotted, unnumbered on the spines.

But inside the front cover of the third one, in the top corner, in pencil so faint I nearly missed it: a small hooked cross.

"Folio three," I whispered.

Torvald exhaled slowly. "Try twenty-two."

Folio twenty-two did not exist — there were only nine — but folio *two*, the one on coastal soundings, bore the careful little fish in its corner. And folio seventeen, the thickest of the lot, full of light-station records, carried the squiggle.

They were numbered. All of them. Marked in a code dressed up as drafting habit, invisible to anyone who hadn't spent six years learning to read Aldric Eriksen's mind through his pen.

"Oh, you *difficult, wonderful man*," I said, to no one living.

* * *

By the time Signe knocked at dawn to say the stove wanted lighting and Bram had already broken a nib and blamed the nib, Torvald and I had the shape of it.

Each folio contained, buried among genuine working data, a sequence of false entries. Not errors — errors drift randomly, and these didn't. They clustered. A sounding here, a bearing there, always just plausible enough to pass review, always just slightly wrong in the same direction. And each cluster, decoded through the margin-key, resolved into numbers.

Coordinates.

"He hid them in bad data," I said, pacing — I pace when I think, and the workshop is narrow, so my thinking involves a great deal of turning. "No one falsifies *against themselves*. No auditor looks twice at a master correcting his own draft. The guild read these folios, Voss's people read these folios, and all they saw was an old man fussing over soundings."

"And you saw?"

"A map." I stopped turning. "Torvald, he made a map out of mistakes. Every wrong number is a right number wearing a disguise. Put them together and—" I spread the loose sheets across the table, the decoded figures in my own hand beneath each pair of marks, and drew the lines connecting them. "There. An island."

Torvald leaned over the table. The lamplight caught his face, and I watched him do the arithmetic of geography the way sailors do — by instinct first, proof after.

"Skarvholm," he said. "The old survey station. It's been shut since the reorganization. Guild property. Nobody goes out there except—" He paused.

"Except whoever holds the keys," I finished. "Which would be the directorate."

"Which would be Rask."

We stood looking at the little constellation of ink between us. Outside, the gulls were starting their morning argument with the harbor, and the kettle had begun its low whistle, and none of it seemed to belong to the same world as the thing on the table.

Because here is what I understood, standing there with cold feet and a hot heart: Aldric hadn't just hidden the plates. He'd hidden the *directions to the plates* inside the very atlas the directorate wanted destroyed. They couldn't burn the atlas without burning the only key to their own exposure. It was elegant. It was ruthless. It was so entirely Aldric that I laughed — one short, startled laugh that turned embarrassingly wet in the middle.

"What?" Torvald asked.

"He told me once that a map should be able to vouch for itself. That the best charts are the ones where the evidence and the territory are the same object. I thought he was being philosophical. He was being *literal*. The atlas isn't just the work, it's the treasure map to the proof of the work." I wiped my eyes with the heel of my hand, smearing graphite across my cheekbone, and found I didn't care. "He built the vindication into the manuscript. We don't need to argue with the directorate. We need to *read*."

"Skarvholm is a day's sail in decent weather," Torvald said slowly. "And it'll be watched. Anything the directorate owns and doesn't use, they watch."

"Then we go carefully."

"We? Hazel, the last time I followed this man's instructions, I ended up delivering letters to a grieving apprentice while pretending her master was dead. I've learned to be careful about the word 'we.'"

"You said he sent you to find 'the one who finishes it.'" I tapped the atlas draft, the eleven sheets, the margins full of his voice. "Well. Here she is. Finishing it. Which now includes whatever comes next, because you cannot finish a map whose ending is on an island you haven't visited." I straightened. "Besides, you can't row."

"I can *absolutely* row."

"You rowed into the tide gate backwards. Signe saw. She hasn't stopped mentioning it."

Torvald opened his mouth, considered, and closed it again with the dignity of a man conceding a point under protest. "We'll need weather. And an excuse to be on the water. And—" he glanced toward the front room, where the workshop door faced the lane, "—we should assume we're not the only ones who knew Aldric well enough to suspect him."

That sobered me. Because he was right, of course. Three days ago I had been a quiet apprentice with quiet griefs and a quiet life, and now there was a coded island, a staged death, and a stranger in the workshop who mended buttons like a man expecting to run.

And yet — I looked at the atlas. At the little fish swimming forever through the border of the Hvalvik chart, carrying its secret number like a pearl. Somewhere out past the Skerries, on a cold island the guild had abandoned, the true plates waited: every real sounding, every honest bearing, two decades of Aldric's actual eyes on the actual sea. Proof that the charts the guild sold to captains — the charts that broke ships on rocks that weren't where the paper promised — had been lies, and that one man had spent his last good years hiding the truth in plain sight, in margins, in bad data, in the patience of ink.

"Tomorrow," I said. "First light, if the weather holds. I'll tell Signe and Bram we're taking survey samples to the old station — which is even true, if you squint."

"You're getting better at lying."

"I hate it."

"I know." He almost smiled. "It's how I'll know you're still you."

I was blowing out the lamp — the whistling one, whose opinion of our plan I chose not to solicit — when I saw it. One more mark, on the final sheet of the draft, in the bottom margin where Aldric always signed off his sheets with a date and his initials. *A.E.* But beneath the initials, added later, in pencil, small enough to miss:

*A number follows the name. Find the plate that bears your own.*

I stared at it for a long moment. Torvald, watching my face, came back to the table.

"What is it?"

"I don't know yet." And that was true — but underneath the not-knowing, a colder current moved. Because the handwriting was Aldric's, yes. But the pencil was fresh. Sharper-edged than the faded notes around it. As though it had been written not two years ago, but recently. As though someone had been in this workshop, with this draft, adding messages meant for me alone.

As though someone wanted very much for me to find Skarvholm — and wanted me to know they were counting on it.

I closed the draft, tied it, and put it in the iron box where it had slept for two years. Then I went to light the stove, because the apprentices would arrive within the hour, and whatever was coming, the workshop still needed its fire, and so, I suspected, did we.
