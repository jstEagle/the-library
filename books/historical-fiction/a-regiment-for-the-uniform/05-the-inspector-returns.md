## 5. The Inspector Returns

The knock came at half past seven in the morning, which was suspicious in itself. Guild inspectors did not keep morning hours. Guild inspectors kept the hours of men who had never once had to be anywhere before their breakfast was laid for them, and so a knock before eight meant one of two things: either the building was on fire, or someone wanted Hazel off her guard.

She looked down at the atlas draft, open on the workbench to the sheet of the Lofoten approaches, Aldric's marginal code marching up the margin in tiny, patient letters that she alone could read. Three years of mornings she had spent like this one — ink, bread crusts, the smell of lamp oil — and now the door.

"One moment," she called, and her voice came out steady, which surprised her. Grief, she had learned, was excellent training for lying. You practiced a calm face every day for three years and eventually your face simply *was* calm, all the way down.

She slid the draft into the map press — the deep drawer beneath the flat files where Aldric had kept his unfinished continental plates — weighted it with two brass scale bars and a folded oilcloth, and pulled the false front of the drawer closed with a click that sounded, to her strung nerves, loud as a musket shot. Then she stacked three blank folios on top of the bench, sprinkled a little sand on the floor near the door as though someone had been careless with an inkwell, and opened the door with the expression of a woman mildly inconvenienced by life.

Inspector Voss stood on the step with rain beaded on his gray coat and a leather satchel under his arm, looking exactly like what he was: a man whose entire personality had been issued to him by an office. He had small, precise eyes and the habit of standing slightly too close to thresholds, as if doors were things to be audited rather than passed through.

"Frøken Eriksen." He inclined his head. "You're up early."

"The tide doesn't wait for inspectors," Hazel said, and immediately regretted it, because it sounded like something Aldric would have said, and Aldric's phrases coming out of her mouth were how she got caught, eventually. She softened it with a smile. "Forgive me. We've been short-handed since Master Eriksen died."

"I know," said Voss. "I signed the condolence letter."

He had. It had arrived eleven weeks after the funeral, printed from a guild form, with the wrong title above Aldric's name. Hazel had kept it, because she kept everything, and because someday — though she hadn't known it then — she would want evidence of exactly how much the guild had cared.

"Won't you come in," she said, because refusing would have been worse.

* * *

He walked the workshop the way he always did, hands clasped behind his back, reading the room like a chart. The long benches with their parallel rules. The racks of pens sorted by nib width. The wall where the finished charts hung in their oiled sleeves. And then, inevitably, his gaze settled on the flat files, and stayed there the way a cat's gaze settles on a bird that has made the mistake of holding still.

"You've continued production," he observed.

"We fulfill existing commissions," Hazel said carefully. "Nothing new. The master's outstanding orders."

"Mm." Voss set his satchel on the corner of the bench and opened it with the ceremony of a man unwrapping something unpleasant. "Then this will simplify matters. The Directorate has authorized a full archival survey. All unfinished works of deceased members are to be surrendered to the guild archive for cataloguing." He produced a document, heavy paper, wax seal, the works. "Master Aldric Eriksen's atlas falls under the provision."

Hazel looked at the seal. She looked at the signature. Rask's signature — she'd seen it enough times on commission letters to know the flourish, confident as a wave breaking. And underneath it all, the word *archiving*, doing its quiet, terrible work.

Archiving. As if the atlas were a dead man's boots.

"It isn't finished," she said.

"No," Voss agreed pleasantly. "That is precisely why we want it. An unfinished work in private hands deteriorates. Ink fades. Paper warps. Whereas the archive has climate-controlled cabinets, acid-free interleaving, trained conservators—" He smiled, and it was a good smile, professionally calibrated. "You would be honoring him, Frøken Eriksen."

*I would be burying him twice,* she thought. *And this time they'd throw away the key.*

Because she had spent three years inside Aldric's margins, and she knew what the directorate would find if they ever read them closely. Not errors — Aldric had been too good for errors — but corrections. Depth soundings that disagreed with the guild's published charts. Coastlines drawn a quarter-mile east of where the official plates put them. Little annotations in cipher that said, in effect: *this is not what they told you, and here is the proof.* If the atlas went into the guild archive, it would go into a cabinet, and the cabinet would go into a room, and within the month the sheets would quietly stop existing. She had seen it happen to other dead men's papers. Everyone had seen it happen. That was rather the point of it happening quietly.

"That will take some preparation," she said aloud.

Voss raised an eyebrow. "Preparation?"

"He worked in layers." She gestured toward the flat files with the weary authority of a woman who has explained this to idiots before. "Draft sheets over proof sheets over base surveys, sometimes three and four deep, pinned through registration holes. They can't simply be lifted out. Pull them apart carelessly and the registrations tear, and then you don't have an atlas, Inspector, you have a box of expensive confetti." She let a beat pass. "I assume the guild would prefer the atlas intact."

Something flickered behind those small precise eyes. Not doubt, exactly. Calculation.

"Naturally."

"Give me a fortnight. I'll separate the layers, index the sheets, wrap each one properly. It'll take less time than repairing damage, and I'll include a full inventory." She reached for the ledger on its shelf — the real one — and flipped it open to a page near the back as though consulting it. "In fact, I've begun already. See?"

Voss leaned over the ledger. Hazel felt her own heartbeat with unusual clarity, the way you feel the ground during an earthquake — not because it's moving yet, but because you know it could.

The page showed columns in her neatest hand. *Sheet 41, Vestfjorden approaches, draft state, wrapped. Sheet 42, Skrova soundings, draft state, wrapped.* All of it fiction. She had written it the night before last, unable to sleep, in a fit of foreboding she hadn't understood until this moment. There was a version of herself, apparently, who forged documents at two in the morning on instinct, and Hazel decided she would rather not think too hard about that woman just now.

"Hm," said Voss.

It was not a satisfied hm. It was the hm of a man adding figures and getting a number he didn't like. But he straightened, closed the ledger, and nodded once.

"A fortnight. I'll return on the first of the month with a cart." He gathered his document, tucked it away, and paused at the door — that threshold habit again, one foot in, one foot out, like a man deciding whether a room merited a second reading. "You've kept the workshop beautifully, Frøken Eriksen. Your master would have been proud."

"He'd have complained about my ink discipline," Hazel said, and this, at least, was true, and saying something true steadied her voice better than any lie.

Voss smiled his calibrated smile and stepped out into the rain.

* * *

She watched him from the window until he turned the corner by the chandlery. Then she counted to one hundred, which was how long it took him to reach the ferry landing if he walked briskly, which he did, being a man with an appointment somewhere more important than her.

Then she went to the map press, drew out the atlas draft, and sat down on the stool with it in her lap like a child holding a cat that might bolt.

"Well," she told it. "We're liars now."

The atlas said nothing, being paper, but the margins seemed to look at her. *A fortnight,* she thought. Fourteen days to do honestly what she'd claimed she'd already done — separate, index, wrap — except that wrapping wasn't really the plan, was it. The plan was copies. Or concealment. Or both. She turned to the flyleaf, where Aldric had written in his upright, unhurried hand: *A chart is a promise made to strangers. Keep it or admit you broke it.*

"You couldn't have left me something simple," she muttered. "A recipe. A stamp collection."

The bell over the workshop door jingled.

Hazel's heart performed a maneuver she felt in her teeth. She shoved the draft back into the press, stood up, arranged her face into *calm apprentice, nothing to see*, and turned —

— to find Torvald dripping in the doorway, holding a paper-wrapped parcel of coffee like an offering, looking between her and the map press with the particular alertness of a man who has arrived somewhere mid-crisis and knows better than to announce it.

"I heard voices," he said. "From the street."

"Inspector Voss."

Torvald set the parcel down very slowly. "What did he want?"

"The atlas. For archiving." She gave the word its full weight, and watched it land. "Signed by Rask himself. I bought us fourteen days with a forged inventory and a lecture about registration holes."

Torvald blinked. Then, slowly, the corner of his mouth moved. "Registration holes?"

"Layered drafting. Pinholes. It sounded very technical." She crossed her arms. "I learned it watching Aldric swear at apprentices who tore his proofs."

"And he believed you."

"He wants to believe me. Believing me means he doesn't have to report a fight to Rask before he's certain there's something worth fighting over." She sank onto the stool again, and the bravado drained out of her all at once, the way it does when the door finally closes. "Fourteen days, Torvald. And I lied to a guild inspector's face and he *thanked* me for it. Do you understand? Two years ago I couldn't lie about whether I'd swept the floor. Now I invent ledgers before breakfast." She laughed, briefly and without much humor. "I'm good at it. That's the part I hate. It comes easily."

Torvald crouched so they were eye level, and his voice dropped into something gentler than she'd yet heard from him. "Your master staged his own death to buy the truth time. You bought it fourteen days with a straight face and a cup of tea. I'd say the regiment's in good order."

"The regiment," Hazel repeated. "Is that what we are?"

"What else would you call it? Uniforms are optional. Loyalty isn't." He straightened, glancing once more at the map press, then past it — at the narrow door to the back rooms, the ones Aldric had kept locked even from his own apprentices, and which Torvald had been eyeing with increasing frankness all week. "Fourteen days," he said. "Which means we shouldn't waste daylight."

"There's nothing back there but dust and old vellum scraps," Hazel said. "I've been through it after the funeral."

"You were grieving," Torvald said. "Grieving people see dust. I'm a courier. I see hiding places." He was already moving toward the back door, and there was something in the set of his shoulders that Hazel recognized from the letters — the same patient stubbornness as the handwriting, dated after the funeral, insisting on itself.

"Torvald—"

"Fourteen days," he said again, and unlatched the door. "Let's find out what he trusted us with."

Hazel stood alone in the workshop with the forge-forged ledger open on the shelf, fourteen days of lies ticking down behind her eyes, and followed him in.
