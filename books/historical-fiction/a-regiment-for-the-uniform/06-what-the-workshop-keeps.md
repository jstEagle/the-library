## 6. What the Workshop Keeps

Torvald had been in the workshop of Aldric Eriksen exactly four days, and in that time he had learned three things about the place.

First: it smelled of ink and tallow and, inexplicably, of dried fish — a smell that clung to everything, including, he was beginning to suspect, himself. Second: Hazel Eriksen worked with the door closed and did not appreciate being interrupted, which he had learned by interrupting her twice on the first morning and receiving a look that could have been used to calibrate instruments. Third: the workshop kept secrets.

That last one he'd suspected from the start. A man who fakes his own drowning does not leave behind an ordinary room. And Aldric — Torvald had carried his letters for six years, had learned his handwriting the way one learns weather, by living under it — had never once done anything without leaving a second meaning folded inside the first. His maps were honest; his margins were not.

So while Hazel bent over the atlas draft at her desk, pen moving in that careful apprentice script that was slowly becoming something more than apprentice script, Torvald did what couriers do best when left alone in a strange building. He snooped.

He told himself it was for the cause. The directorate's falsified charts, the wrecked ships, the insurance money — all of it needed documenting, and Aldric had said as much in his letters: *the proof is not only in the plates, Torvald. It is in the paper trail. Guilds are made of paper, and paper remembers.* But honestly, there was also this: Torvald had spent six years ferrying sealed envelopes across fjords and harbors without ever being permitted to open one, and some part of him had begun to feel like a man who delivers bread he is never allowed to taste. Curiosity, in his experience, didn't kill anyone. It just made them insufferable until they got answers.

He began, sensibly, with the obvious rooms.

The map room was a cathedral of drawers — forty-two of them, each labeled in Aldric's small precise hand, each holding charts rolled tight as sleeping adders. He checked the labels against nothing in particular and found them orderly, dull, and unremarkable. Aldric would not have hidden anything where an inspector might look, and inspectors looked everywhere that was labeled.

The drying room held stretched skins of vellum and a rack of pens sorted by nib width. The wash room smelled of gum arabic. The storeroom held reams of paper stacked with a precision that bordered on the religious.

*You are looking for a man who hid from the most powerful guild on the coast,* Torvald reminded himself. *Think like him.*

Aldric thought like a cartographer. Which meant he thought like a man who understood that the interesting things were never in the middle of the map. They were in the edges. The margins. The places marked *here be nothing*, which every working surveyor knew meant *here be something I couldn't be bothered to name.*

Torvald turned in the center of the main workroom and looked at the walls the way Aldric might have looked at them. There was the great chart table, scarred and wax-polished. There were the shelves of reference atlases, spines cracked with use. There was the tall cabinet of drawing instruments. There was —

There was a door.

Not a remarkable door. A narrow one, painted the same gray-green as the wall beside it, with a latch so worn it sat flush and cast no shadow. In a workshop full of doors, it was the kind of door a person stopped seeing after their third day. Hazel must have walked past it ten thousand times. Perhaps she saw it. Perhaps, like everyone, she had agreed silently with the building that it led nowhere worth going.

It opened onto a staircase going down.

"Well," Torvald said to no one. "Hello."

The stairs were stone, cold through his boot soles, and they descended farther than the building's footprint seemed to allow, which meant they went back — back into the hillside the workshop was braced against. Old coastal construction. The kind of cellar that predated the house above it and would outlast it too. He counted twenty-two steps, and at the bottom found a landing, a lamp bracket, and a second door — this one unlocked, because Aldric had apparently decided that hiding a thing behind two ordinary precautions was better than one extraordinary one.

The room beyond was long and low, its ceiling ribbed with old beams. Shelves ran along both walls. And it was not empty.

Torvald stood in the doorway a moment, letting his eyes adjust, letting the shape of the place tell itself to him. Crates of survey instruments, wrapped in oilcloth. A chest of what sounded like glass when he tapped it — negatives, maybe, or plate glass for printing. Rolls of tracing linen. And along the near wall, a writing desk, a chair worn shiny at the arms, and above the desk, pinned in neat rows like a collection of moths, dozens upon dozens of letters.

Letters that had been answered.

He knew this because Aldric had taught him, over six years of correspondence, the difference between letters received and letters kept. Received letters got filed. Kept letters got annotated. These were annotated — the margins dense with Aldric's hand, small and fierce, the way his writing shrank when he was angry. Torvald had once watched him fill half a page of margin denouncing the shading conventions of the Danish hydrographic office. These margins were angrier than that.

He lifted the first letter down carefully, the way one lifts a bird.

*Dear Master Eriksen,* it began, in a hand that shook. *My son Jens served aboard the *Solveig*. I am told the chart showed deep water where there was rock. I am told this by men who were there and lived. The guild sent me a form. The form asks how many dependents remain. I do not know how to answer it. I had three children and now have two, but the form has no space for the manner of losing, only the number...*

Torvald read it twice. Then he set it down very gently on the desk, because his hands had developed an opinion about holding it, and that opinion was trembling.

He took down another. A widow from Hovden whose husband had gone down with the *Kvitebjørn* on a shoal that appeared on no official chart and appeared, Torvald now understood with a sick lurch, on *this* chart — the corrected one, the true one, the one sitting upstairs in a locked drawer under Hazel's elbow. Another from a mother in Ålesund. Another from a man who signed himself *not a widow, but close enough*, whose brother had drowned within sight of a lighthouse built to guard water the chart said wasn't there.

And beneath each letter, in Aldric's hand: the answer. Every single one answered. Not with forms. With pages. Sometimes two pages. Torvald scanned one — Aldric explaining, in careful layman's language, how depth soundings could be altered between survey and publication, how a line could be moved a fathom at a time until a reef became a channel, how the guild's directorate had learned that a wrecked ship insured for triple value was worth more dead than afloat. Explaining it plainly, without jargon, the way you'd explain it to a grieving woman at her kitchen table, because that was where these letters had evidently been written to arrive.

*I cannot give you back your son,* Aldric had written at the bottom of the Jens letter. *But I can promise you this: I am making a record that cannot be lost, and when it is finished, no one will be able to say they did not know. Grief deserves better than a form. It deserves the truth, in writing, witnessed. You will have it. I swear it on my name and my trade.*

Torvald sat down in the chair. It fit him badly — Aldric had been a taller man — but he sat anyway, because his legs had made the decision independently and he trusted them.

Six years. Six years of carrying this man's sealed letters, wondering vaguely why a master cartographer wrote so often to people in fishing villages, and never once — not once — had Torvald imagined this. He had assumed Aldric was eccentric. Cartographers generally were. He had assumed the letters concerned surveys, tide tables, the eternal petty war over place-name spellings that consumed the guild's lower ranks like a slow fever.

Instead, the man had been conducting a correspondence with grief. Dozens of households. Years of it. Answering every letter personally, promising every one of them the same thing, and then coming down here, alone, beneath his own floorboards, to build the thing he'd promised.

*The human cost list.* That was what Torvald had heard him call it, once, obliquely — a phrase dropped into a conversation about shipping manifests like a coin into deep water. *The manifest tells you what the ship carried, Torvald. The other list tells you what it cost.* He had thought it was rhetoric. Aldric was prone to rhetoric the way other men were prone to drink — socially, at intervals, with visible enjoyment.

It hadn't been rhetoric. It had been an inventory.

Torvald got up and began counting the letters, because counting was something he could do with his hands while the rest of him reorganized itself around what he'd found. Thirty-one. No — thirty-four; three were tucked behind the others, perhaps the ones still awaiting reply. Thirty-four families. And that was only the ones who had written. How many hadn't? How many widows had received the guild's form, filled it out in a shaking hand, and posted it into silence?

He thought about the *Meridian* — the wreck Signe's father had died on, though he didn't know that yet; he only knew the name from the ledgers, one entry among many, tonnage and insurance value and a date. Somewhere in this room or soon to be in this room, Torvald realized, there might be a letter from Signe's family. Or might have been, if they'd known where to write. That was the cruelty of it — the guild controlled the information, so the bereaved didn't even know whom to accuse. They blamed the sea. The sea took the blame the way the sea takes everything: without complaint and without consequence.

The sea, Torvald thought, was the perfect accomplice. It never testified.

He was still standing there, letter in hand, doing the dangerous arithmetic of thirty-four families multiplied by however many years Rask's falsifications had been running, when he heard footsteps on the stairs.

Twenty-two steps. He had counted. He had time to decide whether to look guilty, and decided against it — guilt was for people who intended to stop.

Hazel appeared in the doorway with a candle and an expression he was beginning to catalog. This one he filed under *suspicion, mild, with underlying exhaustion.*

"You've been down here an hour," she said.

"Have I?"

"I can hear you moving around through the floor. You walk like a horse with opinions." She came down the last steps and stopped, taking in the room, and Torvald watched her face do something complicated. She had known about the room — of course she had; she'd grown up under this roof. But knowing a room exists and seeing it lit by someone else's candle are different things. "I haven't been down here since the funeral," she said quietly. "I couldn't."

"I understand."

"Do you?" She set the candle on the desk, next to the letter in his hand, and read it upside down with the speed of someone raised among documents. Her jaw tightened. "That's Fru Holm. Jens Holm. He was seventeen." She reached past Torvald and straightened the letter in the row, aligning its corner with the others — a small, automatic gesture, the gesture of an apprentice restoring order to a master's work. Then her hand stayed on it a moment longer than order required.

"He wrote back to all of them," Torvald said. "Every letter. I've only read three and I already — " He stopped, recalibrated. Honesty had become policy between them, if only because lying to Hazel Eriksen seemed likely to end badly for the liar. "I came down here to snoop. I'll admit that freely. I expected survey records. Ledgers, maybe. Evidence for the case against the directorate."

"This is evidence."

"It's worse than evidence." He gestured at the wall of letters. "Evidence convinces tribunals. This convicts. Look at it — thirty-four families who can stand up and say *my husband, my son, my brother, and here is the chart that killed them, and here is the master cartographer who told me so before anyone asked.* Rask can argue with soundings. He can bribe surveyors. He cannot argue with Fru Holm."

Hazel was silent for a while. The candle flame leaned and righted itself.

"He used to come down here at night," she said finally. "When I was young, I thought the floorboards creaking past midnight were the house settling. Then I thought he was doing secret work for the guild — important work, work a child wouldn't understand. I used to be proud of it." Her mouth did something that was almost a smile and mostly wasn't. "I was proud of the wrong secret. The real one was better and worse than anything I imagined."

"They're why he staged the drowning," Torvald said. "Not the atlas. Well — the atlas too. But you don't spend years answering letters like these and then settle for publishing corrections. Corrections get printed, disputed, and buried. He wanted to burn the directorate down to its charter." He paused. "Metaphorically, I want to stress. Given recent events, I should be clear that I mean metaphorically."

Something in her face loosened — barely, but he caught it, and counted it a victory. Four days, and he'd made her almost smile twice now. At this rate, by midsummer she might laugh.

"He'd have liked you," she said, which startled him. "You're both dramatic about paperwork."

"I prefer *thorough with feeling*."

"That's what I said." She picked her way along the shelf, reading spines, touching things lightly, relearning a room she'd mourned instead of explored. Then she stopped at a crate near the far end — newer than the others, the wood still bright — and lifted the lid.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth, lay bundles of paper. She unwrapped one, and Torvald saw her go very still.

"What is it?"

"Names," she said. "Dates. Vessels. Coordinates of wrecks matched against published charts, with the discrepancies noted in margin code. He was building a master index. Every falsification. Every loss." She looked up at Torvald, and in the candlelight her eyes were wet and furious at once, which he was learning was her natural condition and probably always had been. "He never finished it. There are gaps after March. After — " She couldn't say it. After his death, real or otherwise.

"Then we finish it," Torvald said. "That's rather the family trade, isn't it?"

She huffed — half a laugh, surrendering to it despite herself — and wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand in a brisk, annoyed motion, the way people do when crying feels like an inefficiency. "We'll need help. Properly, this time. Not just errands. If we're going to match names to wrecks to charts, I need people I can trust with the whole of it." She named them: "Signe. Bram."

"The apprentices?"

"The accomplices," Hazel said. "They just don't know it yet."

She blew out the candle, and in the sudden dark, Torvald felt the room settle around them — not a tomb anymore, he thought, but an arsenal. Thirty-four letters waiting upstairs in the light, and a list of names below, and somewhere out in the rain-slicked town, a directorate that believed the only witness capable of tying it all together was at the bottom of the harbor.

Above them, faintly, the floorboards creaked. Someone walking past midnight over a secret.

"Come on," Hazel said. "Tomorrow we tell them everything."

* * *

Torvald climbed the twenty-two steps behind her and paused at the top, looking back down into the black.

*A regiment for the uniform,* Aldric had written once, in a letter Torvald had carried but never been permitted to open — though the courier's curse was a good memory, and the title had lodged in him like a splinter. He hadn't understood it then. Standing at the mouth of the hidden room, with the smell of ink and old paper rising around him and a widow's shaking handwriting still fresh behind his eyes, he thought he was beginning to.

Tomorrow, the apprentices.
