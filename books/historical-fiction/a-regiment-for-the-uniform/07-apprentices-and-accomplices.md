## 7. Apprentices and Accomplices

There is an art to asking someone for help without letting them know they are being asked.

I learned it from Aldric, though he never taught it deliberately. He would simply leave a task half-finished on my desk — a coast line that stopped at a headland, a table of soundings missing its last column — and say nothing about it, and by morning the gap would itch at me like a splinter until I filled it myself. He called this *apprenticeship*. I have since come to suspect it was also how he tested whether a person could be trusted to care about things left in their keeping.

Now I needed two people who cared. And I could not tell them why.

* * *

Signe arrived first, as she always did — before dawn, before the kettle, before the gulls had finished arguing over the harbor refuse. She came in through the workshop's side door with her hood crusted in sea-fog and went straight to the drying rack, because Signe did not believe in idle hands or, as far as I could tell, in any other kind.

"Your burnisher's dull," she said by way of greeting. "It's been dull for a week. You've been pretending not to notice."

"I've been grieving," I said mildly, "and grief excuses dull burnishers."

"Grief doesn't excuse anything. My mother used to say sorrow is no reason for sloppy work." She took the burnisher off its hook anyway and sat down at the stone to true its edge, which was exactly what I had hoped she would do, because it meant she would be sitting there when I needed her sitting there.

I watched her work for a while. The lamp threw her shadow long across the floorboards, up the wall where Aldric's charts still hung in their careful rows. Three weeks since the funeral. Three weeks of me finishing his atlas alone at night, hiding sheets inside folios marked *miscellaneous*, flinching whenever boots sounded on the cobbles outside. Torvald had been gone four days now, up the coast to see what he could learn about the guild's shipping routes, and the workshop felt enormous with him absent — a cave where one person's breathing echoes.

"Signe," I said.

"Hm."

"When you were small — your father, on the *Meridian*. Do you remember the chart he carried?"

Her hands stopped on the burnisher. Not dramatically; just stopped, the way water stops when a stone settles into it. "The harbor approach sheet? I have it still. In a box. Why?"

"Do you ever look at it?"

"I looked at it once." She resumed sharpening, slower now. "Once was enough. It's a good chart. He trusted it." She said *trusted* the way one says a word whose meaning has recently changed without announcement.

That was all I wanted, for the moment. A thread laid down, not pulled. Aldric had taught me that much: never haul on a line before the knot is set. I thanked her for the burnisher, and she snorted, and we worked in the comfortable silence of people who have shared a stove through three winters.

But I noticed she glanced twice at the shelf where I kept the atlas draft, and the second time, her eyes stayed a moment too long.

* * *

Bram required a different strategy entirely, chiefly because Bram required a strategy at all.

He was nineteen, all elbows and appetite, the sort of apprentice who could reproduce a compass rose from memory but could not be trusted to remember to eat lunch unless the lunch was placed physically between him and his work. He adored puzzles. This was both his gift and, as I was about to demonstrate to myself, his weakness.

I left the errand where he would find it: a slip of paper in the margins of a tide-table he'd been correcting, written in the margin-code Aldric and I had built together over years — a private shorthand of dots and flourishes that looked, to the untrained eye, like ink blots or the idle scratches of a bored clerk. To anyone who knew the key, it read:

*Third drawer, north wall. Bring what you find to the back room after close. Tell no one.*

Then I spent the entire day failing to concentrate.

He found it at half past ten — I knew because I heard the drawer roll open on its swollen runners — and then nothing happened for six hours. Nothing happened so loudly that I began composing apologies in my head, cataloguing every way the plan might have misfired. Perhaps he'd shown it to someone. Perhaps he'd decided it was nonsense. Perhaps he'd simply forgotten, having been distracted by a sandwich.

At closing time he drifted into the back room with elaborate casualness, the way a cat enters a room containing a birdcage. He held the slip pinched between two fingers as though it might go off.

"This is your hand," he said. "Under the blot-code. Master Aldric's code — well, yours and his, everyone knows you two had your own alphabet, Signe thinks it was a game, I think Signe is unobservant." He turned the paper over, found nothing, turned it back. "The third drawer has nothing in it but spare nibs and a dead spider."

"It has something else in it now."

His eyebrows performed a small, interested maneuver. I opened the drawer myself and lifted out the oilskin packet I'd hidden there at dawn: inside it, one loose sheet from the atlas draft — not the most incriminating sheet, but a telling one — showing the coastline above Skarvholm with Aldric's marginal note in cipher along the edge. And beneath it, folded smaller, a page torn from a guild shipping ledger, the copy Torvald had carried, listing cargo declared on a vessel that had sailed from those very waters and never arrived.

Bram read. Bram was quick; that was never in question. I watched the quickness move through his face — first puzzlement, then the specific pleasure of a man solving something, then the pleasure draining away as he understood what he'd solved.

"These dates," he said quietly. "This ship was posted lost in March. But the ledger shows her taking on cargo here in—" He looked up. "Hazel. What is this?"

"A question," I said. "One I can't answer alone."

"That's not an answer."

"No," I agreed. "It's a test. You've just passed it, incidentally."

"I haven't answered anything."

"You didn't run to Voss with it," I said. "You brought it here, alone, and you closed the door behind you." I hadn't actually seen him close the door, but he glanced at it guiltily, which told me everything. "Aldric believed maps were promises kept to strangers. Every line we draw, someone will stake their life on it in weather we can't imagine. Most people never think about that. You just did, in the space of one ledger page. So."

"So?" His voice cracked slightly on it, which under other circumstances would have been delightful.

"So I'm going to ask you to do some things. Small things first. Errands. And I'm going to ask you not to ask questions for a while, and I need you to understand that the not-asking isn't distrust. It's protection. If you don't know, you can't be made to tell."

Bram looked down at the ledger page again. His thumb moved along the ship's name, back and forth, like a man smoothing a crease.

"My uncle was a fisherman," he said, to the page rather than to me. "Not wrecked. Just — gone out one November and the sea kept him. Nobody falsified anything. It was nobody's fault. I used to think that was the worst way, that there was no one to blame." He folded the page along its original crease and handed it back to me with great precision. "I think I'd hate it more if there was someone to blame and everyone agreed to look elsewhere."

"Yes," I said.

"All right. Errands." He pushed his hair out of his eyes. "What am I carrying, where am I carrying it, and do I get dinner?"

"Bram."

"What? Loyalty is easier on a full stomach. Everyone knows this."

* * *

And that, more or less, was how it began.

For two weeks the workshop ran its ordinary face toward the street — apprentices bending over drafting tables, the smell of gum arabic and lamp-black, the ordinary complaints about damp paper and dull nibs — while underneath it, quietly, a second operation grew like roots beneath a lawn.

Bram ran messages. He was perfect for it: young enough to be invisible, cheerful enough to disarm suspicion, and possessed of a memory that held directions the way wax holds a seal. He carried Torvald's letters up the coast and brought back replies sewn into the lining of his coat. He delivered a sealed packet to a retired pilot in Fjellvik who read it, went pale, and pressed Bram's hand hard enough to make the boy wince — and Bram came home bursting with questions I still would not answer, and did not ask them, though I could see them queuing behind his teeth like patients outside a surgery.

Signe I gave finer work. She had the steadiest hand of any of us — steadier than mine, though I'd have chewed glass before saying so aloud — and a talent for copying that amounted to forgery's respectable cousin. I told her we were preparing duplicate plates against loss by fire or flood, which was even true, and she accepted it with the flat skepticism of a woman who has mended nets all her life and knows a patched hole when she sees one. But she copied, and she copied beautifully, and she asked only one question, on the fourth night, working late by the stove while rain needled the windows.

"The duplicates," she said, not looking up from her pen. "Duplicates of what, exactly?"

"Charts."

"Mm. Charts." Her nib paused. "Hazel. I copied the Meridian approach last Tuesday. From a draft. And the draft doesn't match the printed chart my father carried. The shoal marker sits a full cable's length east in ours." She finally raised her eyes. They were not angry. That was somehow worse. "I've known that shoal since I was six years old. I've swum over it. The draft is right. The printed chart is wrong."

The fire ticked. Somewhere in the walls, the house settled its old bones.

"Say what you're going to say," I managed, "and I'll decide how much truth you can carry."

"I already said it. Your draft is right and their chart is wrong, and wrong by enough to put a keel on rock in poor visibility." She capped her ink bottle with a small decisive click. "My father died on rocks in poor visibility. So either you tell me what we're doing, or I start doing it without you, and you won't like my methods, because I'm less patient than you are."

I laughed. I couldn't help it — a startled, exhausted laugh that fogged the cold air — and Signe regarded me with deep disapproval, which made me laugh harder, which nearly undid me entirely, because underneath the laughter was the thing I'd been carrying alone since Torvald walked soaked through my door: the weight of it, the sheer tonnage of knowing.

So I told her. Not everything — not Torvald's whole history, not yet — but the shape of it. The directorate. The altered charts. The insurance money. Aldric's staged death, and the atlas, and the plates waiting at Skarvholm, and the fact that Inspector Voss had already come sniffing twice with his soft voice and his soft hands and his inventory lists.

She listened without interrupting, which was its own kind of discipline. When I finished she was quiet for a long moment, looking at her own hands — ink in the knuckle creases, a pen callus on the middle finger, her father's hands exactly, though I'd never tell her that.

"All right," she said at last. "I'm in. But I want it understood: when this is done, when the truth is out wherever it needs to be out — I want the Meridian named. Her name, and her crew, in the record. Not 'lost with all hands.' Names. Every one."

"You'll write them yourself," I said. "In your own hand. That's the point of all this."

She nodded once, briskly, the way she did when something mattered too much to discuss, and went back to her copying, and that was that. No oaths, no hand-clasping, no drama. Signe considered oaths a form of untidiness.

* * *

By the end of the month, the workshop had become a strange, doubled thing. To the street: the same quiet establishment it had always been, three apprentices and their dead master's legacy, nothing to see. Within: a conspiracy conducted largely in whispers and margin-code, with a rotation system for watching the door, and a signal arrangement involving a red cloth hung from the upstairs window should Voss or any guild man appear unexpectedly — a cloth which, I must record, Bram hung out by accident one windy Tuesday while using it to dust the sill, causing me to spend twenty minutes flattened behind the paper press with my heart going like a drum in a parade.

We developed rituals without deciding to. Signe took charge of the stove and fed us relentlessly, on the theory that frightened people forget to eat. Bram invented a knock — two, pause, three — that he deployed with such enthusiasm he used it even when announcing himself at breakfast. We learned each other's silences: Signe's silence meant *I disagree but will comply*; Bram's meant *I have thought of something clever and am deciding whether to risk it*; and mine, I suspect, meant *Aldric would know what to do*, which was becoming less a thought than a chronic condition, like weather in the joints.

One evening, near the end of that fortnight, I looked up from the atlas draft and found all three of us lit by one lamp, bent over the same table — Signe inking a fair copy, Bram sorting coded notes into sequence, myself annotating the margins — and the sight struck me so squarely that I had to put down my pen.

Aldric had worked alone. All those years, all that danger, and he had worked alone, because trust, for him, was a currency he'd learned early to hoard. I understood it now from the inside. Every person added to a secret multiplies the ways it can leak. And yet — looking at the bent heads, the ink-stained fingers, the plate of Signe's seed bread disappearing crumb by crumb into Bram — I understood something else too. Alone, I had been a keeper of a dead man's secret. Together, we were becoming the keepers of a living one. There is a difference, and the difference is everything.

"You're staring," Signe said, without looking up.

"Am I."

"You get an expression. Like the map's done something surprising."

"The map has," I said. "It turns out it draws people as well as coastlines."

Bram looked up hopefully. "Is that the code for dinner?"

"That is the code for dinner, yes."

* * *

It was Torvald's return that turned our quiet conspiracy into an expedition.

He came in past midnight, sea-spray stiffened in his beard, and stood dripping in the doorway looking at the three of us around the lamp like a man checking his bearings against an unexpected landmark. Then his eye fell on the atlas draft — open, uncovered, annotated in four hands now instead of two — and something in his tired face eased.

"You told them," he said.

"They recruited themselves," I said. "I merely failed to stop it."

"Failing efficiently," he agreed, and dropped into the fourth chair, which someone — Signe, I suspected — had quietly placed there days ago in anticipation.

He spread his findings on the table: schedules, guard rotations, the ferry timbers to Skarvholm, the narrow weather window when the crossing was possible and the island's keeper would be ashore at market. Two plates retrievable, perhaps three if fortune cooperated. And at the bottom of the pile, circled in his blunt pencil, a date.

"The storm breaks Thursday," Torvald said. "After that, four clear days. After that, the guild sends its own survey launch to the island for the winter archive transfer — Rask's men, Voss's schedule." He tapped the circle once. "If we mean to reach those plates, we cross within the window, or not at all."

The lamp flame leaned in the draft from the door. Outside, far off, the sea was already beginning to grumble about the weather coming, the way it does a day ahead, like an old man complaining of a knee before rain.

Thursday. Three days.

I caught Signe's eye across the table. She was already reaching for a fresh sheet, squaring it against the board's edge, jaw set in the particular way it set when she was about to do something irreversible and considered it merely practical.

"Well then," she said. "Someone had better draw us a chart."
