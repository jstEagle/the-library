## 8. The Plate Room at Skarvholm

### Hazel

The storm sat on Skarvholm for three days like a hen that had decided the island was its egg, and we sat in Signe's uncle's boathouse on the mainland shore, waiting it out with dried cod, bad coffee, and Bram's inexhaustible supply of opinions.

"We could row across," Bram said for the eleventh time, on the third morning. "People have rowed across worse."

"People have *drowned* across worse," Signe said. She was mending a net with the brisk violence of a woman who found knots soothing. "That's not an argument, that's an obituary."

Torvald said nothing. He had taken up a position by the boathouse door — he seemed to take up positions by doors generally, as if furniture were a tactical resource — and spent the hours studying the island through the gap where the door didn't quite meet its frame. Skarvholm was a low black shoulder of rock two miles out, crowned with a squat stone building the guild called an "annex" the way a man calls a smuggler's cellar a wine shop.

I used the delay the way Aldric would have: working. The atlas draft lay open across three crates of herring salt, weighted at the corners with coffee cups, and I copied out the marginalia one final time into my own hand, so that if anything happened to Aldric's original we would still carry the key.

*The plates sleep where the cormorants argue.* That was the first note, decoded on our second night together with Torvald's candle and my patience. *North wall. The door that is not a door answers to the tide's arithmetic.*

"The tide's arithmetic," I murmured now, watching rain hammer the harbor. "He never could resist a riddle."

"He knew you'd be the one reading it," Torvald said quietly, without turning from the door. "Riddles are only puzzles to people who already know the answer. He wrote them so they'd open for you and stay shut for everyone else."

I didn't answer. There was a lump in my throat that had no business interrupting cartography, so I bent over the page and pretended to check a bearing.

Signe caught my eye over the net and smiled the small, fierce smile she'd been wearing since her father's name had gone into the evidence ledger. Bram caught both our eyes and asked, for the twelfth time, whether we could row across.

At noon the storm lifted its skirts and went off to bother the Lofoten fisherman, leaving behind a sea like hammered pewter and a sky scrubbed pale. Torvald turned from the door at last.

"Tonight," he said. "We cross at slack water. We're back before dawn or not at all."

"How comforting," said Signe.

"I'm told honesty builds trust in a crew."

"Then you've been misinformed about crews."

But she was already coiling rope, and Bram was already checking the lantern oil for the third time, and I understood, folding the atlas draft into its oilskin wrap and strapping it against my chest where it rode like a second heartbeat, that none of us had seriously considered staying behind. Not once in three days.

The boat was Signe's uncle's, borrowed with a wink and a promise, and it crossed the pewter water under oars with the four of us breathing steam into the cold. Skarvholm grew from a shadow into a fact: black basalt ledges, white screaming birds, and the annex squatting on its crown with windows like closed eyes.

"Door that is not a door," Bram whispered as we grounded the boat among the rocks. "I love this. I want you all to know I love this."

"You'll love it less when the tide's arithmetic comes due," Signe said, and even Torvald almost smiled.

### Torvald

Aldric Vestergaard had taught me three things in the years I carried his letters: how to read a man's walk at two hundred paces, how to lie with a straight face and a friendly nod, and that every institution keeps its true self somewhere other than its front rooms. The guild's annex on Skarvholm was the truest thing the directorate owned — a plate room dressed as a storage shed, guarded by nothing but distance, weather, and the assumption that honest men don't cross open water in November.

Honest men don't. We weren't applying.

The north wall of the annex met a ledge of basalt, and where they met there was a seam in the stonework that no mason had built and every mason would have disowned. I put my fingers into it while Hazel held the shuttered lantern low and read from her copy by the light that escaped the shutter's edge.

"'The door answers to the tide's arithmetic,'" she whispered. "'Count the ebb from full moon; the lock wants what the water lacks.'" Her finger stopped on the page. "It's a combination lock. A number set against the tide tables." And then, softly, with something breaking open in her voice that she'd deny later: "He hid the plates behind mathematics. Of course he did. He hid them behind the one subject the directorate was too lazy and too proud to learn properly."

She worked the numbers aloud — full moon past, ebb height, the difference — and her hands, small and ink-stained and steadier than mine have ever been, found three recessed wheels inside the seam and turned them. On the third wheel there was a click like a knuckle cracking, and a panel swung inward on greased hinges, exhaling air that smelled of stone and tallow and thirty years.

"Thirty years," Hazel breathed. "This hasn't been opened since before I was apprenticed."

Inside, shelves ran back into darkness, and on the shelves, wrapped in waxed cloth and stacked like loaves, were the survey plates. Copper. True copper, hand-engraved, each one bearing the honest soundings Aldric and his survey teams had taken before Rask's directorate began redrawing the coast for profit. I lifted one and felt its weight settle into my arms like something alive.

"There must be hundreds," Bram whispered, running his lamp along the shelf ends. Labels in Aldric's hand: *Vester Sound, corrected 1887*. *Skarvrenna channel — TRUE depths*. *Meridian approach — see discrepancy report, enclosed.*

The Meridian. Signe made a sound beside me, small and controlled, and I saw her find her father's wreck in a shelf label and stand very still with it.

"We take two," I said. "The Meridian approach and the Skarvrenna channel. They prove falsification beyond argument, and four people can't carry more without slowing down. We come back for the rest with witnesses and writs."

"That's the sensible plan," Hazel said. "Say it again slowly, so I can bear leaving the others."

"We take two," I repeated, "and we come back with the law at our backs."

She wrapped her chosen plate herself, refusing help, the way she refused help carrying Aldric's atlas — grief and duty braided too tight for lending. We rewrapped the shelf to look undisturbed, closed the hidden door until it clicked home, and were halfway across the upper rocks toward the boat path when Bram, who had insisted on walking backward to watch our trail like a hero in a ballad, went down flat with a squawk and came up holding a wire.

It was thin, dark, strung ankle-high between two boulders, and attached to it — we all saw it at once, in the moment that lasts a year — a little brass mechanism bolted to the rock, and above the annex, in a window that had been a closed eye all evening, a light bloomed yellow.

"Alarm," said Bram, with scholarly precision, "tripped."

### Hazel

Afterward, I could never remember the chase in order, only in pieces, the way you remember a shipwreck.

The light in the window. Shouting, muffled by stone, then not muffled. Torvald's hand closing on my arm — not dragging, just confirming, the way he did everything, economical even in panic — and then we were running, four shapes across black rock slick with spray, the wrapped plate strapped to my chest bouncing against my ribs like a panicked heart.

They came around the east side of the annex. Three men, lamplight swinging, and one of them carried something long-barreled that I chose not to look at directly. Guild enforcers wore no uniform, which was itself a kind of uniform; Voss's agents, Torvald had called them, and I remember thinking, absurdly, as my boots skated on wet basalt, that I would have to ask him sometime how he knew their walk at two hundred paces, because I was learning it now myself, close up, at speed.

The boat path was a scramble of ledges down to the landing cove, and Signe took it like the fisherman's niece she was, calling back "left foot here — *here*, Bram—" while Bram narrated his own descent in a high whisper ("fine, fine, entirely fine"). Behind us a shot cracked off the rocks and went somewhere over the sea, a sound like the sky clearing its throat, and everyone ran faster, including, I suspect, the man who fired it, because nobody shoots well at a dead sprint and he wanted us to think he might.

Torvald stopped. That was the piece I remembered most clearly afterward: in the middle of the chase, with three armed men coming and the tide coming and the whole night going wrong, Torvald Kollseth stopped, turned, and put himself between us and them on the narrowest part of the ledge, where one man could hold against three.

"Go," he said. "Get the plate to the boat. I'm right behind you."

"Liar," I gasped — because he wasn't, because that stance was a man planning to be a wall — but there was no time to argue it, and later I would understand that this was the moment the alliance sealed in Signe's boathouse became something else, something with blood in its future.

The first enforcer reached him. I heard it more than saw it — a grunt, a scuffle, the ugly percussion of bodies on rock — and then Torvald's voice, calm as a customs clerk: "*Last chance to walk away, lads, the tide doesn't care whose side you're on—*" and then a cry that wasn't Torvald's, and the crack of a body meeting basalt, and I was at the bottom of the path hauling the boat off the stones with Signe and Bram while the sea argued with the hull.

Torvald came down the last ledge at a limping run, one sleeve dark to the elbow, and threw himself aboard as Signe's oars bit. An enforcer appeared at the top of the path and raised the long barrel, and Bram — dear, ridiculous, magnificent Bram — stood up in the stern, spread his arms wide, and shouted "THE TIDE'S ARITHMETIC, GENTLEMEN," which confused the man for precisely the two seconds we needed to swing out of range.

The second shot hit water where we had been. The third we never heard, because the cove's black shoulder swallowed it, and then there was only the rowing: four of us pulling for the mainland with our breath tearing, the wrapped plate between my feet, and Torvald in the bow holding his arm and laughing — actually laughing, low and wheezing.

"What," Signe demanded, "is *funny*."

"He had a knife," Torvald said. "The big one. And he telegraphed it from his shoulder, same as a man signaling a turn. Aldric would have loved this. He always said the guild's enforcers trained each other, so their mistakes bred true."

"Your arm."

"It's a scratch."

"It's bleeding through your coat."

"It's an *enthusiastic* scratch."

I tore a strip from my petticoat — Bram offered his scarf, I declined, some sacrifices are too great — and bound it by lantern-light while the island shrank behind us, its yellow window burning like a single furious eye. Two plates. Two out of hundreds, and now the directorate knew exactly what we knew, and would move the rest, or destroy them, or dig in.

"They'll fortify Skarvholm now," I said. "Whatever window we had for the remaining plates —"

"— is closing," Torvald finished. He looked back at the island, and his laugh had gone somewhere colder. "Which means we stop playing at retrieval and start playing at exposure. The plates we have are enough to prove the lie. What we need now isn't copper. It's witnesses."

No one spoke for a few strokes. Then Signe, rowing with her jaw set and her eyes fixed on the far shore, said quietly:

"The Meridian plate is in this boat. My father's chart. His wreck report will match it line for line." She pulled. "Let them fortify their island. We're bringing the island to *them*."

And I put my hand flat on the wrapped plate between my boots — Aldric's engraving lines under waxed cloth, honest soundings sleeping in copper — and thought: *we are coming, old man. Both names, soon enough.*

Behind us, Skarvholm's light burned on, and ahead, faint on the mainland shore, another light answered it — someone standing at the harbor mouth, waiting, in a coat I recognized.

Voss had not needed an alarm to know where we'd gone.
