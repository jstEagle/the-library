## 9. Salt and Ashes

We smelled it before we saw it.

The ferry from Skarvholm had barely cleared the harbor mouth when Signe went quiet at the rail, her hand tightening on the bundle of survey plates wrapped in oilcloth against her chest. I thought at first she was simply cold — the crossing had been miserable, all spray and grey water and Bram retching politely over the leeward side — but then I caught it too, threading through the salt: smoke. Not hearth-smoke, not the sweet peat smell that rose from a dozen chimneys on any evening in Vesterhavn. Something heavier. Charred tar and wet cinder.

"That's coming from the north quarter," Bram said, wiping his mouth with his sleeve. "That's—" He didn't finish. He didn't have to. The workshop sat in the north quarter like a fat brown hen among her eggs of smaller buildings, and every one of us was thinking the same cowardly thought at once: *it could be anything. A bakehouse. A lampworks. Anyone's roof but ours.*

The wind, as if to settle the question, shifted and delivered the smell straight into our faces, unmistakable now, with an undertone beneath the smoke that made my stomach drop through the deck planks. Burning linseed oil. Burning sizing. Burning paper.

"Row faster," Signe said to the ferryman, in a voice I had never heard her use. The ferryman, a placid man who had spent forty years being hurried by people with no authority to hurry him, actually rowed faster.

From the harbor steps we could see it: not the whole workshop, thank God, not the whole — but the eastern wing, the long room where we kept the reference library, was gutted. The roof had burned through in two places and been hastily patched with tarpaulin, which flapped like wounded birds in the wind. Water had run out the doors and frozen in black ropes down the steps. A knot of neighbors stood about in the way neighbors do after a fire, wearing the particular expression of people who are sorry and also, privately, glad it wasn't their house.

I do not remember walking up the street. I remember Torvald catching my elbow at the door because my legs had apparently decided the whole business was beneath them.

"Steady," he said quietly.

"Don't tell me to be steady."

"I wasn't telling you. I was telling myself. One of us has to manage it and you're better at everything else."

It was such an absurd thing to say that I nearly laughed, and I think he knew it, and I hated him for it, and I was grateful, all in the same breathless moment.

* * *

Inside, the damage announced itself by smell before sight. The eastern wing was a cave of char — shelves reduced to blackened ribs, their contents fused into sad gray bricks of ash and half-burned vellum. Three years. Three years of Aldric's reference collection, gathered across thirty: harbor charts going back a century, tide tables annotated in four hands across four generations, the correspondence files, the survey journals, the tracings of every coastline from Lindesnes to the Russian border. Gone, or gone enough. Water had finished what fire started, soaking the western edge of the collection so that what hadn't burned had bloated and slumped like drowned men.

Signe waded in up to her ankles in ash-slurry and pulled open a drawer with the tenderness of someone touching a body. What came away came away in pieces.

"The 1878 fjord soundings," she said. Her voice was very level, which is how I knew. "Bram, the 1878 soundings were—"

"I know." Bram stood in the doorway holding his hat, which struck me as such a useless, human gesture that I wanted to sit down on the wet floor and never get up.

"They took me two winters to collate. Papa helped carry them up from the archive when I had the fever and couldn't—"

"I know," Bram said again, uselessly, kindly.

I should record, for honesty's sake, that my first coherent emotion was not grief. It was rage, pure and hot and entirely unbecoming, arriving in my chest like a struck match. Because this was not an accident. I stood in the reeking dark of our ruined library and I *knew*, the way one knows arithmetic, that no accident begins at the eastern wall where the reference collection lives and stops neatly short of the drafting room where the guild's own commissioned work sits untouched under its dust sheets. No chimney runs along that wall. No lamp hangs there; Aldric had forbidden open flame near the paper stores since before I was apprenticed, and we had kept his rule the way sailors keep superstition — absolutely, without ever discussing why.

An inspector from the town would come, of course. An inspector would poke about and find a cracked flue or a careless apprentice or some other tidy explanation, because inspectors always do, and the fire would enter the record as an accident, the way ships enter the record as weathered.

"Voss," I said.

Torvald, who had been circling the room with the careful tread of a man reading a scene the rest of us were drowning in, nodded slowly. "Or Voss's friends. Look here." He crouched by the base of the eastern shelving and lifted something from the ash with two fingers: a scrap of oilskin, the kind used to wrap parcels for sea transport. And beside it, half-melted but recognizable, a lantern of the shipboard kind — the enclosed pattern, brass, with a sliding shutter. Not a household lamp. A lamp designed to be carried somewhere it shouldn't be carried and shuttered quickly when footsteps approached.

"Somebody brought his own light," Torvald said, "in a room full of men who've kept no flame here in twenty years."

"He wanted the references," Signe said. She had stopped crying, or rather she had converted crying into something else; I watched it happen, watched grief pass through her face and come out the other side as something with edges. "Not the atlas. The atlas is in your bag, Hazel — they'd have taken it too, wouldn't they, if they'd known? They burned what proves the charts wrong. The old surveys. The comparison material." She looked up at me, ash-striped, ferocious. "They're not trying to scare us. They're trying to make us *unprovable*."

The plates from Skarvholm rustled in their oilcloth against my chest, where I'd carried them pressed under my coat the whole crossing like a woman smuggling contraband infants. Two true plates, recovered at the cost of blood and Bram's dignity and Torvald's shoulder, which had taken a pistol-ball graze on the rocks and been bound afterward with a strip of my petticoat, a detail I mention only because he has made me promise never to mention it.

But the atlas draft — I had left it at home. At my own lodgings, wrapped in waxed cloth inside my sea chest, because I had grown, these past weeks, unable to bear its being out of arm's reach even overnight. I had told myself it was devotion. Standing in the ashes, I understood it had been instinct. Some animal part of me had already known what my mind refused to conclude: that the workshop was no longer safe, that nothing in Vesterhavn bearing Aldric's hand would be permitted to survive intact.

Three years of reference work. And the only irreplaceable thing in the building had survived because I had disobeyed every rule of good practice and carried my work home like a lovesick schoolgirl.

I sat down on a burnt shelf-stump and laughed until Signe put her arms around me, at which point the laughing turned into the other thing for a while, and nobody minded, and the neighbors outside pretended very hard not to hear.

* * *

By lamplight that evening — candles, strictly, per Aldric's rule, though I confess I held mine rather closer to the paper than the rule allows — we took inventory in the surviving drafting room. The list was short and each line of it hurt in a different key.

The atlas draft: safe. My copy of Aldric's margin-code key: safe, sewn into my bodice, a fact that caused Bram to turn an interesting shade when I mentioned it. The Skarvholm plates: safe. Everything else: ash, slush, or ruin.

"We can rebuild the reference collection," Bram said. He had appointed himself cheerfulness officer for the evening, a role he performed the way he did everything, with immense effort and visible strain, like a man carrying a piano upstairs alone. "Charts exist in duplicate at the Bergen office. Tide tables we can recopy. It'll take years—"

"It'll take years," I agreed. "And we don't have years. We have until the charter review." Nobody asked how I knew the date; we all knew it now, the way a family comes to know a diagnosis. "Rask doesn't need to destroy us. He needs to slow us past the review. Burn the proofs, keep us scrambling, and when the charter's renewed the directorate is untouchable for another decade." I looked around the candlelit room at their faces — Signe's set jaw, Bram's strained cheer, Torvald's quiet watchfulness, his bound shoulder making him hold himself slightly crooked, like a book with a broken spine. "He thinks he's bought himself time."

"Hasn't he?" Bram asked.

"No," said Torvald.

"Yes," I said, at the same moment, and then, because they both looked at me: "Both. He's slowed the *proof*. But he's forgotten what Aldric always said — that a map isn't the territory, it's the argument. You can burn the evidence. You can't burn the coast." I spread the two salvaged plates on the table, and there they were: the true soundings of the Meridian approach and the Selsvik channel, in Aldric's clean sure hand, next to which the guild's published charts were not merely wrong but *criminally* wrong, wrong in the specific confident way of a lie drawn with a ruler. "The coast is still out there. Every rock Rask erased is still sitting exactly where God put it, waiting to testify. If the archives are gone, we make new ones. We survey it ourselves."

Silence. Then Bram, faintly: "We're apprentices. Half-trained. In winter."

"You surveyed Skarvholm channel three days ago in worse than winter," Signe said, "and got shot at, and your readings were good. I checked them against the plate." She said it matter-of-factly, but I saw Bram stand about an inch taller, and I loved her for it, and him.

"And the widows' letters?" I asked Torvald gently. Those had lived in the back room, in Aldric's strongbox — the correspondence with the families of wrecked crews, the human ledger behind the falsified numbers. We'd found the strongbox intact, pried open, emptied.

"Gone," Torvald said. His voice did something complicated. "But letters have two ends, Hazel. Every widow who wrote to Aldric knows what she wrote. And every widow who wrote to Aldric has a husband's shipmates, a harbor town, a story." He met my eyes. "You can't burn testimony that's walking around wearing shawls."

So it settled over us, not as a plan exactly, more as a change in the weather of the room: we would rebuild and we would survey and we would gather, all at once, all faster than sense allowed, because the alternative was letting a fire be the last word.

Signe, who throughout this had been turning one of the charred drawer-pulls over in her fingers like a relic, suddenly said: "The 1878 soundings. I can redo them." We all looked at her. "I *remember* them. Three winters collating — you don't forget numbers like that, they get into your dreams. Papa and I used to quiz each other at supper like it was a game." Her chin trembled once and steadied. "It won't be the originals. But it will be true, and it will be mine, and he'd have said that counts double."

Nobody trusted themselves to speak for a moment. Then Bram cleared his throat and observed that if we were to survey half the coast before the charter review we would need a boat, and boats cost money, and none of us had any, and thus the great plan of resistance was revealed to rest upon our combined savings of eleven kroner and a promise from a fishmonger.

"There's the reward money," Torvald said.

"What reward?"

"For the return of guild property," he said, with the first real smile I'd seen from him since the rocks. "Which we happen to possess. I'm told the directorate pays promptly."

It was wicked and it was perfect and Signe laughed for the first time all day, a real laugh, startling us all like a gull off a cliff.

* * *

Later — much later, when the others had gone home to sleep and I remained because I am constitutionally incapable of leaving a mess for morning-me, who is a stranger to me — I stood in the doorway of the burned wing one last time.

Snow had begun falling through the broken roof, settling softly over the ash, and in the candlelight it gave the ruin a strange gentleness, laying white over black the way fresh paper covers an old mistake. Somewhere in that drift lay thirty years of Aldric's collecting. Somewhere lay Signe's father's help, and Bram's careful indexes, and my own first-year practice sheets with their wobbly coastlines, which I had secretly kept though Aldric had told me to burn them.

"You'd have said," I told him, "that paper is only paper, and the work is the work, and I'm being sentimental." I could hear him perfectly: *Sentimental is just accurate, girl, with the volume turned up.* "Well. Perhaps. But I'm keeping the sentiment anyway, and the rage too. I find they balance nicely."

I banked the candle and walked home through the falling snow with the atlas draft under my coat, over my heart, where it had learned to live.

There was a letter waiting under my door.

I knew the hand before I picked it up — heavy, formal, a director's hand, the kind of handwriting that expects to become a signature on other people's fates. It bore the guild's seal and Director Halvard Rask's name, and inside, in words I would read four times before sleep came anywhere near me, was an invitation.

The director, it said, wished to offer Miss Eriksen a commission of considerable honor. And her late master's courier, should he present himself, need fear no prosecution — provided certain property was delivered, complete and *uncorrected*.

He knew. He knew about Torvald, about the atlas, about everything — and he was offering mercy the way a man offers mercy, with one hand counting what it costs him.

I put the letter in the fire and watched it curl, and felt briefly, enormously better, and understood that tomorrow the war would begin in earnest.
