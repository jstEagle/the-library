## 11. Signe's Testimony

My father taught me to read water before he taught me to read letters.

"Letters lie when people are frightened," he said, rowing me out past the breakwater one gray morning, my hands too small for the oars so I held them in his wake like a little rudder of my own. "But water never lies. Water only tells you what it is."

I was seven. I believed him the way children believe lighthouse keepers about the sea — completely, and without ever once suspecting that the keeper might himself be deceived.

He was the mate on the *Meridian*. She went down off Vardnes Point on the ninth of November, four years ago this winter, with eleven men aboard and a cargo of salted herring nobody ever claimed. The guild report called it pilot error. The guild report was very tidy. The guild report had been written by a man at a desk who had never, I would wager, held an oar in his life, and it concluded that Captain Eilertsen had misread the soundings and carried his ship inside the point where the water shoals without warning.

Everyone in our street repeated it for a season, the way you repeat anything sad enough that saying it makes it finished. *Pilot error. Terrible thing. Good man, though.* And then the herring came back in spring and people stopped saying it at all, because grief in a fishing town is like weather: acknowledged, endured, and then replaced by the next front coming in off the water.

Except in our house, where grief did not move out. It just rearranged itself into smaller rooms.

* * *

The workshop smelled of glue and cold coffee the evening Torvald laid the plates out on Bram's worktable, and I remember thinking — absurdly, traitorously — that I hoped Hazel would let me sweep up before we did whatever we were about to do, because shavings underfoot make me itch.

There were two plates. Two true survey plates, pried off Skarvholm through storm and blood and Bram's sprained wrist, which he described in terms usually reserved for war heroes and which Hazel treated with a poultice and no sympathy whatsoever.

"Right," Torvald said. He'd pushed his sleeves up. He has forearms like mooring posts and the habit of standing very still while other people panic, which I found irritating until I understood it as kindness. "You wanted to see them. Both of you. Before anyone else."

Hazel stood at the end of the table with her arms folded, looking like someone holding a door shut against weather. Bram hovered, wrist bound, radiating the specific pride of a man who has recently bled for a cause and wants credit. And I stood where I always stand, slightly behind everyone, doing sums.

Because here is what nobody in this conspiracy of ink-stained fools seemed to have noticed yet, in all their talk of directorates and ledgers and Rask: they were chasing the falsified charts the way you chase a rumor. Broad. General. *The guild lied about the coast.* But I am the daughter of a drowned man, and I do not deal in generalities. I deal in one ship, on one night, at one point of rock.

"Vardnes Point," I said.

Torvald looked up. "What?"

"The *Meridian*." My voice came out steadier than I expected, which surprised me, because inside I felt like the moment when you step off a dock expecting solid ground. "If these plates are what you say they are — if they show the real soundings — then show me Vardnes Point. Show me the ninth of November, four years ago."

Nobody moved. The lamp hissed. Somewhere upstairs Signe-the-cat — no relation, though she answers to my name out of spite — knocked something off a shelf, which is her opinion on most matters.

"Hazel," I said. "Show me."

* * *

She didn't make me ask twice. That is the thing about Hazel Eriksen that people miss, because she is quiet and careful and apologizes when she bumps furniture: underneath all that there is a spine of pure iron, and when someone she loves needs something from her, the iron shows its face and does not waste time on ceremony.

She took down the guild chart first — the published one, the one every pilot in the district bought and trusted and steered by. She weighted its corners with the ink pots and turned it so I could see.

"There," she said softly, and tapped the point. "Vardnes. Guild sounding: fourteen fathoms clear across the approach, shoaling to six inside the headland. Safe passage for any vessel under nine feet of draft."

"That's what Eilertsen had," I said. "That's what my father had. Fourteen fathoms." I could hear my own voice going somewhere flat and strange. "He wasn't careless. You don't get to be mate on the coastal run by being careless. He'd have taken her wide anyway, in November, with a following sea. He always took the wide line in winter. Always."

"I know," Hazel said.

"You don't know—"

"Signe." She looked at me across the table, lamplight in her eyes. "I know Aldric suspected. I've told you that. But I didn't understand until right now why it mattered to him so personally." She paused. "It wasn't personal to him. It was personal to somebody. Maybe lots of somebodys. We just haven't met them yet."

Then she lifted the guild chart away, and beneath it she set the true plate — copper, cold-looking even under lamplight, the engraved lines fine as spider silk — and I leaned over my father's grave.

Fourteen fathoms became nine. Nine became five. Five became three and a half, and the shoal ran not inside the headland but straight across the approach like a bar of teeth, unmarked, unsounded, unrecorded anywhere a living pilot could have read it.

The room got very quiet. Even Bram shut up about his wrist.

"He came in at nine fathoms," I heard myself say. "Draft of the *Meridian* loaded was just under eight. On the guild chart, that's a margin of six fathoms — comfortable, generous, the kind of margin a careful man takes on purpose. On the truth—" My throat closed. I made myself finish. "On the truth, he had one fathom. One. And a November swell running better than that."

Hazel's hand came over mine on the table edge. Her fingers were ink-stained and cold and I gripped them hard enough that I probably hurt her, and she let me.

"He wasn't a bad pilot," I said. It came out half-plea, half-prayer. "Tell me he wasn't a bad pilot."

"He was a good pilot murdered by a map," Torvald said quietly, from the shadows by the stove. "I'm sorry. I wish there were a gentler sentence for it."

* * *

Here is the part I have not told anyone, not even Hazel, and I am telling it now because a testimony is worth nothing unless it costs something:

After the wreck, I wrote to the guild.

I was seventeen and furious and I had spent a week composing the letter, because my mother taught me that anger should be dressed properly before it leaves the house. I asked them — politely, oh, so politely, I thanked them for their service to navigation — how the official report could state fourteen fathoms when the fishermen of our parish had pulled cod off a reef at Vardnes since before the guild had a charter. I asked whether anyone who signed the report had ever seen the point. I asked, with a seventeen-year-old's devastating instinct for the soft place, who exactly had surveyed those waters, and when, and with what vessel.

I received a reply within the fortnight. On heavy paper. From the office of the Director of Charts.

It was courteous. It expressed sympathy for my loss. It enclosed a copy of the survey record — dated, signed, stamped — showing the 1886 survey of the Vardnes approaches, conducted by the guild sloop *Fulmar*, soundings consistent with the published chart. It wished me well in my bereavement and closed with the directorate's motto, which is *Fidelitas ad Littus*, faithfulness to the shore, which I now find so obscene I could laugh until I cried.

I kept the letter. Of course I kept the letter. Grief keeps things; that is nearly all it knows how to do.

And tonight, after Torvald and Bram had gone up to bed and Hazel was banking the stove, I fetched it from the false bottom of my tool chest where I keep everything that hurts, and I put it on the table in front of her.

"What's this?" she said.

"Their answer. To my question." I sat down across from her. "Look at the date of the survey."

She read it. I watched her read it, watched her face do the thing it does when numbers rearrange themselves into a confession — the small stillness, the way her breath goes shallow and her eyes go bright and terrible.

"They surveyed it in June," she said slowly. "Of '86. The *Meridian* went down in November of '87."

"And look at the vessel."

Her finger traced the line. "*Fulmar*." A pause. "Signe. Torvald told us the *Fulmar* was sold out of the fleet in the spring of '86. It's in the correspondence we found in the back room — Aldric flagged it. There is no *Fulmar* to survey with. The ship on this certificate didn't exist anymore."

"No," I agreed. "Which means either they surveyed Vardnes Point with a ghost, or they didn't survey it at all, and both of those mean the same thing." I heard my voice crack on the last word and let it. I was tired of dressing it properly. "They didn't just print a wrong number, Hazel. They printed a number knowing it was wrong, or not caring, and then when a girl wrote to ask, they sent her a forged certificate on nice heavy paper because they thought she was nobody."

"You're not nobody."

"No," I said. "I'm evidence."

* * *

We worked until the lamp burned low. That is the honest description, and I will give the honest description, because everything else in this affair has been dressed up enough.

Hazel pulled the wreck report — she keeps copies of everything now, after the fire, after learning what fire takes — and we laid it beside the true plate and matched them line by line, the way you'd match a wound to the knife. Time of grounding: 21:40, darkness, moderate swell from the southwest. Position given by the surviving boat crew: east of the point, which on the guild chart is open safe water at fourteen fathoms, and on the true plate is three and a half fathoms over bare rock. Cargo manifest. Crew list. My father's name, third from the top, in a clerk's indifferent hand: *A. Signussen, mate.*

Eleven names. I read all eleven aloud, quietly, because Hazel had started to say something and stopped herself, and I think she understood that I needed to. Eleven names, and under each one the report's verdict, identical, mechanical: *loss attributed to navigational error.*

"Error," I said, when I finished. "Eleven errors, all in the same place, all on the same night. What are the odds?"

"Astronomical," Hazel said. "Unless the error was made years earlier, on paper, by men who never left their desks."

"Rask's desk."

"Rask's desk." She said it simply, without theater, and somehow that was worse and better than shouting. Then she did the thing I have come to love her for, which is that she turns grief into work the way a mill turns water into flour. "Signe. If we can match one wreck to one plate discrepancy with documentary proof of a forged survey — the *Fulmar* certificate, your letter, the sale record — that isn't suspicion anymore. That's a pattern with a name attached. How many wrecks in the guild waters since '86?"

"Off the top of my head? Six serious ones. Seven counting the *Kirsten Marie*, though they called that one cargo shift."

"Six." Hazel wrote it down. Her pen moved fast. "And the widows' correspondence Aldric kept — Torvald found it in the back room. Some of those families will still have their own letters from the guild. Their own certificates. Their own tidy reports."

"You want to collect them."

"I want to collect them," she said, "and I want you to be the one who asks. Not me. Not Torvald. They'll trust you. You're one of theirs — you lost someone to the same water."

I thought about that. I thought about knocking on doors in fishing parishes in winter, asking women with chapped hands to dig out the cruelest letter they'd ever received so we could hold it up to the light in front of the whole guild. I thought about my mother, who would have done it in a heartbeat and then fed me afterward.

"Yes," I said. "On one condition."

"Name it."

"When it's read out — at the review, whenever it happens, wherever it happens — I read the eleven names. All of them. Out loud. Nobody gets reduced to 'navigational error' again. They get their names back."

Hazel reached across the table and shook my hand formally, like sealing a contract, except her eyes were wet. "Witnessed," she said. "By me and by God and by whoever's listening through that ceiling, which is probably Bram, who has never once successfully eavesdropped quietly in his life."

"I can hear you," Bram's voice said, muffled, from above.

"THAT'S THE PROBLEM," Hazel and I said together, and for a moment we were laughing, both of us, in a room full of dead men's names — laughing the way you're allowed to laugh after the worst part has been said out loud, which I think may be the whole secret of how people survive things.

* * *

Later, alone, I stood at the workshop window and looked north, toward the water I couldn't see in the dark but could feel the way you feel a scar.

Papa, I thought. They wrote you down as an error. A careless man in a careless boat. For four years I half-believed it, in the shameful corner of the heart where doubt lives even in the faithful, and I am sorry for that corner, and I have burned it out tonight with a copper plate and a forged certificate and a pen.

You read the water truly. Water never lies. Men did.

I was still standing there when Hazel came and stood beside me, not touching, just present, the way she is. After a while she said, "There's a problem."

"Only one?"

"One large one." She turned the true plate's edge toward the window-light, and pointed to the engraved notation in the corner — the surveyor's mark, the plate's own signature of origin. "Every plate carries its commissioning authority. These two came from Skarvholm, the old archive series. Look at the mark."

I looked. The letters were small and precise: *C.R.* — Commissioned, Rask. Dated 1885. Three years before the wrecks. The director's own initials, sitting on the plates like a brand on stolen cattle.

"The third plate," Hazel said quietly. "The one covering the northern approaches — the *Kirsten Marie* water, and two more of our six wrecks. It's not in any archive. Torvald's certain. Which means Rask never let it out of his sight." She exhaled. "It's in his house, Signe. The proof we need most is sitting on the man's own shelf, behind his own lock, guarded by his own fear."

I looked at the initials a long moment. C.R. A man I had imagined for four years as a distant clerk, and who had become, tonight, the hand that wrote my father's death warrant in fourteen comfortable fathoms.

"Then we'd best plan carefully," I said. "Because I intend to knock on every widow's door on this coast — and when I do, I'd like to bring her more than an apology. I'd like to bring her the third plate."

Hazel smiled, and it was not entirely a gentle smile.

"Bram," she called up the stairs, "put your wrist away and come down. We need a librarian."
