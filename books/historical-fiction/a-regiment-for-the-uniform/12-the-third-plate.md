## 12. The Third Plate

The workshop smelled of scorched timber for eleven days after the fire. Even after Signe scrubbed the surviving benches with lye soap and Bram rehung the shutters that had warped in the heat, there was a faint char beneath everything, like a memory the room refused to let go of. Hazel had stopped noticing it by the twelfth day, which she supposed was its own kind of grief — the moment when ruin became furniture.

She noticed it again on the thirteenth day, because that was the night Bram laid out his plan.

They had gathered in the back room, the one Torvald had turned into a map of their own making: pinned notes, copied ledger lines, the two recovered plates wrapped in oilcloth and stacked under the floorboards where Aldric's strongbox used to sit. A single lamp burned low. Outside, the harbor wind worried at the new shutters.

Hazel stood over the table with her sleeves rolled and her hands flat on the wood, feeling very much like a general who had never held anything sharper than a ruling pen.

"Say it again," she said. "Slowly. I want to hear how impossible it sounds out loud."

Bram grinned. He was twenty-two, freckled as a trout's belly, and possessed of the particular calm of a man who had spent six years shelving other people's documents and had concluded, somewhere along the way, that documents were more dangerous than knives.

"The third plate," he said, "is in Director Rask's house."

"Which is not a house," Signe said from the corner, where she was mending a tear in the oilcloth with waxed thread. "It's a fortress with a garden."

"It's a house with a delivery gate," Bram said. "And that is the whole point."

Hazel pulled the chair out and sat, because if this was going to be madness, she intended to be seated for it. "Explain."

Bram spread a sheet of paper — one of the few survivors of the fire, rescued from a drawer that had been closed when the blaze started, and therefore worth more to Hazel now than most things made of gold. On it he began to draw. Not a map, exactly. A diagram, the kind an apprentice learns in the first year: flow of paper through an institution. He drew a box labeled GUILD HOUSE. Arrows out of it. One arrow to the PRINTERS. One to the ARCHIVE. One arrow, drawn twice over so it was thick and dark, pointing toward a smaller box labeled R., PRIVATE RESIDENCE.

"Every week," Bram said, "the guild sends a courier run to the director's home. Tuesdays, before the market bell. It's been running since before either of you were born, I'd wager. Ledgers for his private review, correspondence, the sealed reports nobody else is meant to see." He tapped the thick arrow. "The courier goes in through the service gate on the north side. The courier comes out the same way. And here is the beautiful part —" he looked up, and the lamplight caught the mischief in his face, "— the courier is never the same man twice. It rotates. Voss's office assigns it. Any junior clerk or runner can be put on the route with a day's notice."

Signe's needle stopped. "You're proposing we become the guild's postmen."

"I'm proposing we *are* the guild's postmen," Bram said. "Or rather, that someone becomes them for one Tuesday. We've done it before, haven't we? The errands. The little tests." He nodded at Hazel. "You sent me to fetch the Meridian wreck file with nothing but a forged chit, and I walked out of the archive with it under my arm while the archivist complimented my handwriting."

"You did have very good handwriting," Hazel admitted.

"And Signe carried the copied ledgers past two enforcers in a bread basket."

"The bread was real," Signe said. "That mattered."

"So we know the guild will believe almost anything delivered with confidence and the right paper," Bram said. "Rask's own household runs on the same principle. Servants see a guild courier with a sealed satchel and the guild seal, they open the gate. They don't count seals. They count *boredom*. Nobody looks hard at routine. Routine is invisible. That's why it's perfect."

Hazel turned the diagram toward herself and studied it. There was something almost obscene about how tidy it was. Two plates had cost them blood on the black rocks at Skarvholm; Torvald had gone into the strongroom so they could walk away, and every night since, she fell asleep doing arithmetic about interrogation cells and lamplit rooms and whether a man could keep his silence long enough. And here was Bram, drawing arrows, suggesting that the last plate might be taken the way one took a parcel.

"That's it?" she said. "We ring the bell?"

"We ring the bell wearing the right uniform," Bram said. "With the right paperwork. Which I can make. You taught me the seal impressions, remember? The archive chits? This is bigger, but it's the same animal. Forged authorization, genuine format, correct rotation schedule — which I can get, because the courier roster is posted in the clerks' hall and I am, officially, still employed there three mornings a week. Nobody questions a clerk reading the roster. Clerks read rosters the way other men read weather."

"And then what," Hazel said. "We carry out a copper survey plate in a mail satchel? Those plates weigh as much as a dog."

"A small dog," Bram said.

"Bram."

"All right. A medium dog." He crouched and pulled a second sheet from beneath the table — he had, she realized with a mixture of admiration and alarm, prepared all of this already, and the meeting was theater. He unrolled it flat. It was a drawing of the plate itself, done from memory and from the descriptions in Aldric's marginalia: the southern approaches chart, the one covering the Skerries Channel, the falsification that had killed the *Meridian*. "But here's the thing about survey plates. They're stored in frames. Wooden frames, felt-lined, because copper scratches and scratched copper is worthless. Every plate in the guild travels framed. So the plate leaves Rask's house framed, in a document case, strapped flat to a courier's back — the way oversized folios are always transported. I've carried folios that size. So has Signe. It's heavy, yes. But it's shaped like work, not like theft. A man bent under a case looks honest. A man carrying nothing looks suspicious."

Signe set down her needle and thread and came to stand over the drawings. She had been quiet in the way she went quiet before saying the thing that mattered, and Hazel had learned to wait for it.

"There's a flaw," Signe said.

"There are several," Hazel said. "I'm saving mine for after."

"Two couriers go on that route," Signe said. "Always two. Guild rule since the Koldby affair — one carries, one witnesses, so no courier can steal or be accused of stealing. If we send one person, the gate turns them away. If we send two, then who watches whom?"

Bram didn't falter. He'd expected this too; Hazel could see it in the way his finger was already resting on a blank part of the page, ready to draw.

"Then we send two," he said. "One of us carries. One of us wears Voss's colors."

The room went still. Even the lamp seemed to lean in.

"He flipped," Hazel said slowly. "He gave us the strongroom layout. He corroborated at the review — well, he'll corroborate, when the time comes. But walking into Rask's own household wearing his inspector's badge — that's not flipping anymore, Bram. That's treason with excellent posture."

"It's worse than that," Signe said. "If Rask suspects him — if there's one face in that courtyard that hesitates —"

"Then we don't use his badge," Bram said. "We use his *signature*." He pulled a folded paper from inside his coat and laid it beside the diagram. A letter, in a hand Hazel knew too well by now — Voss's narrow, downward-slanting script, acquired through three careful exchanges since the night Hazel had offered him the right side of history, documented. "Voss authorizes a special retrieval. Sealed. His seal, his phrasing, his signature — real, not forged, because I asked him for it directly and he gave it to me with the expression of a man swallowing a fishbone. The letter instructs the household staff to release one archival frame to the bearers, for 'inventory verification ahead of the charter review.' It even apologizes for the inconvenience. Voss is very good at apologizing for inconveniences he intends to cause."

Hazel stared at the letter. Then at Bram. Then at the ceiling beam above them, where the smoke stain from the fire still shadowed the wood like a bruise healing wrong.

"You asked him," she said. "You sat across from that man — the man who burned our workshop, or at minimum smiled while someone else lit the match — and you said *please, sir, may we have a note*."

"I said it very respectfully," Bram said. "I called him 'honored Inspector.' Twice."

Signe made a sound that was half laugh, half prayer.

Hazel got up and walked to the window. Below, the street lay blue with early dark, and somewhere down toward the tide gate a gull was arguing with the wind about something only gulls found important. She thought about Aldric. She thought about the marginalia, the little coded whispers in the margins of his life's work — *verify against original*, *this figure lies*, *ask the widow of the Meridian* — and how each one had been a small act of courage dressed up as bookkeeping. That was what they were doing tonight, she realized. Bookkeeping. Audacity with a librarian's precision. Aldric would have loved Bram. Aldric would have corrected his spelling first, and then loved him.

"The flaw I was saving," she said, turning back, "is Rask himself. He knows we have two plates. He knows we're hunting the third. He offered me a commission, Torvald amnesty, the whole velvet trap — which means he understands exactly what we want. Would a man like that leave the plate in a wooden frame behind a servant's door?"

"No," Bram agreed cheerfully. "He keeps it close. Which is why it's in the house at all — probably in his study, probably locked, possibly under his desk where he can look at it and feel powerful. But look at what that means." He counted on his fingers. "It means the plate isn't in the guild strongroom, where it would take Torvald's memorized walls and a miracle to reach. It isn't in the archive, where Voss could stall us forever. It's in a private residence guarded by cooks and a gardener and whatever enforcers Rask keeps for show. Private residences are built for comfort, not defense. The guild's paranoia stops at the guild's doors. Past those doors, Rask is just a rich man who believes his own legend."

"And if he's home?" Hazel asked.

"Tuesdays he dines at the Merchants' Hall. Every Tuesday. The steward posts it on the household slate because the cook needs to know how many places to set for leftovers." Bram shrugged. "I checked."

"You *checked*."

"Hazel, I have spent my entire adult life learning where institutions write down the truth they don't think matters. The leftover count is truth. The dinner slate is truth. The courier rotation is truth." He flattened both hands on the table, and for a moment the humor dropped away and Hazel saw underneath it the boy who had shelved shipwreck reports for six years and read every one. "Rask wrote down everything. He wrote down the false soundings, he wrote down the insurance settlements, he wrote down which charts went to which harbors. Men like him can't help it. They think paper is theirs. They think it obeys them. And then one day somebody reads it."

Signe blew out a breath. "So. Tuesday. Market bell. Two of us at the north gate with Voss's letter, a document case, and a forged inventory chit. We walk in, we present the letter, some poor housekeeper unlocks the study because the seal says she must, and we carry out a forty-pound copper plate strapped to a back, past a gardener, through streets full of market crowds, to the workshop. And if anyone asks, we're doing inventory verification ahead of the charter review." She paused. "Which, technically, we will be."

"We'll be verifying it straight out of his house," Bram said.

"That's the spirit," said Hazel, and heard to her own surprise that she meant it.

They planned until the candle drowned in its own wax. Bram drew the north gate from memory — he had delivered there once, years ago, and remembered that the hinge squealed and the porter kept a dog that was "all bark and arthritis." Signe worked out the weight: the plate, the frame, the straps, and she began quietly reinforcing an old document case with doubled leather at the shoulders, muttering about load distribution in the tone other people reserved for hymns. Hazel took the paperwork. Of course Hazel took the paperwork. She forged the inventory chit in the guild's house style, aged the ink with a warm iron, blotted it with sand, and creased it along lines a busy clerk would actually use. When she finished, she held it up to the lamp and felt the old familiar sickness and thrill braided together in her chest. Lying well and hating it. But hating it less than she hated the falsified soundings that had opened the *Meridian*'s hull like a tin of sardines.

Near midnight, Signe gathered her mending and paused at the door.

"My father carried freight," she said, not looking at either of them. "Forty pounds was nothing to him. He'd have laughed at this plan. He'd also have insisted on checking the straps twice." Her jaw tightened, and then eased. "Tuesday, then."

After she left, Bram lingered, rolling up his diagrams with the tenderness of a man handling scripture.

"You're quiet," he said. "Quieter than usual, which for you is practically silence."

"I'm counting," Hazel said.

"What?"

"What it costs. Skarvholm cost blood. The strongroom cost Torvald. Everything we've taken, we've paid for." She looked down at the forged chit in her hand, at the neat guild seal drying brown as old blood. "This plan costs nothing. That frightens me more than the rocks did."

Bram considered this with his head tipped, like a wren examining something shiny.

"Maybe," he said, "it's not free. Maybe the price is that after Tuesday, there's no version of any of us that Rask forgives. Right now we're apprentices who got lucky. After Tuesday we're thieves who walked into his home and robbed him with his own rules. That's not nothing, Hazel. That's a debt he'll try to collect." He tucked the diagrams under his arm and headed for the door, then stopped. "But we were always going to owe it. Better to borrow on purpose."

His footsteps faded down the stair. Hazel stood alone in the back room among the pinned notes and the smoke-stained beams, and after a while she knelt and lifted the floorboard and unwrapped the two recovered plates just to look at them, the way one visits graves.

The copper caught the lamplight and threw it back dull and true. Somewhere in Rask's fine house, the third of their company waited in its frame — the southern approaches, the Skerries Channel, the lie that had killed the *Meridian* and Signe's father and a hundred others whose names lived only in Aldric's hidden letters. Three days until Tuesday. Two days to finish the straps, rehearse the walk, memorize the household slate.

Hazel wrapped the plates back in their oilcloth, pressed the board down, and whispered to the empty room, in Aldric's cadence without quite meaning to:

"*Verify against original.*"

On Monday evening, Bram came back from the clerks' hall pale as skimmed milk, holding the courier roster in one hand and a scrap of paper in the other, and said, in a voice carefully sanded flat:

"Small problem. Rask changed the dinner. He's hosting the charter committee at home Tuesday night — the whole directorate, at his own table." He swallowed. "The plate will be in the house. And so will every man who wants us hanged."
