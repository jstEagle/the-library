## 13. Night Work

Bram Vik had spent eleven years learning that a library was a fortress, and the key to any fortress was paperwork.

He sat in the cold loft above the workshop with three ledgers open before him and a stub of candle burning down to nothing, and he explained this principle for the fourth time to Signe, who kept pulling faces at the ceiling beams.

"The guild's delivery routes," he said, tapping the ledger. "Everything Rask owns comes through them. Coal, wine, lamp oil, the man's snuff — all of it moves on guild wagons, with guild manifests, through guild gates. And every wagon is logged here." He turned the page. "Tuesday last week: one crate, marked *survey instruments*, delivered to Halvard Rask's residence at Vollen. Signed for by his housekeeper."

Signe leaned over his shoulder. Her braid had come loose during the day and hung against his arm like a rope someone had given up on halfway through knotting. "So we forge a manifest."

"So we *use* one," Bram corrected gently. "There's a delivery due Thursday night. Wine from the south coast, twelve bottles, one crate. We don't need to forge anything. We need only be the thirteenth bottle's worth of weight in a wagon that's already expected." He allowed himself a small smile, the kind he usually saved for finding a misfiled folio. "A librarian's theft, Signe. No lock broken that wasn't already scheduled to be opened."

"That's the most frightening sentence you've ever said."

"I've been practicing."

* * *

Hazel approved the plan the way she did everything now — quickly, quietly, with her eyes somewhere over Bram's left shoulder where Aldric's atlas draft lay wrapped in oilcloth like something sleeping.

"You're certain about the routes?" she asked.

"Certain as ink," Bram said. "I've been copying the delivery ledgers since autumn. Nobody watches an apprentice with a pen. That's rather the whole tragedy of my life."

Torvald laughed from the doorway, where he was checking the buckles on a satchel with more care than it deserved. He'd been doing that lately — fussing over small things, counting things twice. Bram understood why. Torvald had been Aldric's courier; he knew better than any of them what happened to people who carried things worth killing for. But understanding didn't make it comfortable to watch.

"Then Thursday," Hazel said. "We go in, we find the plate, we come out. Nobody speaks to anybody. Nobody improvises." She looked around the room, and her gaze settled on each of them in turn — Signe, Bram, Torvald — and lingered half a heartbeat too long on Torvald. "Nobody is a hero. The atlas doesn't need heroes. It needs its third plate and four living conspirators."

"A regiment for the uniform," Signe murmured, and they all smiled, because it had become their joke and their prayer both — the phrase Aldric had scrawled once in the margin of a chart, meaning, as far as anyone could tell, that honest work required ordinary soldiers doing ordinary duty, not glory.

Thursday came gray and salt-smelling, with a wind off the fjord that promised rain by midnight and delivered on the promise early.

* * *

The wine wagon smelled exactly as good as it sounds and exactly as uncomfortable.

Bram rode in the back among crates, wedged between a barrel of claret and a coil of rope, with Signe pressed beside him and Torvald opposite, his long legs folded up like a folding rule. The wagoner — a guild man named Oskar whom Torvald swore could be trusted because he owed Torvald money from a card debt in Bergen — drove them up the coast road through rain that fell sideways and thought nothing of it.

"Remind me," Signe whispered over the drumming, "why I'm the one in the housekeeper's dress?"

"Because you're the smallest," Bram whispered back, "and because you can copy a hand better than either of us, and because when you glare at servants they apologize. It's a gift."

"It's a curse," she said, but she was smiling under the hood.

The plan was simple enough to fit on a manifest, which was how Bram liked plans. The wine would be carried in through the tradesman's door at the back of Rask's house. Signe, dressed as a hired girl sent up from the harbor, would carry the final crate herself — the one with Bram inside it, curled among empty bottles. Once past the door, she'd set him down in the scullery, change out of the disguise, and take up her real post near the front stairs with a duster as camouflage. Torvald would remain outside with Oskar and the wagon, keeping watch on the lane, ready to receive them at the same door within the hour. Bram, once unfolded, would make his way to the study — the location of which he knew precisely, having memorized the household inventory Torvald had obtained months ago — and find the survey plate, which intelligence suggested Rask kept locked in the map cabinet behind his desk like a man keeping a snake in a hatbox.

In and out. One hour. No heroics.

The tradesman's door opened onto warmth and the smell of boiled mutton. Signe knocked twice, gave the password — a delivery for Director Rask, compliments of the southern lodge — and then there was the jolt of being lifted, the scrape of the crate across flagstones, the murmur of voices, a dog snuffling interestedly at the slats an inch from Bram's nose (he held his breath and prayed to whatever saint governs fools in boxes), and then stillness. Silence. The click of a latch.

Bram pushed the lid.

Signe crouched over him in the dark of the scullery, already untying her apron. "You owe me," she breathed, "a new spine."

"I'll have it bound in calfskin."

She slipped away toward the front of the house, and Bram climbed out of the crate, straightened by inches, and stood listening to the great house breathe. Rain against glass. A clock somewhere marking the quarter hour with the smugness of expensive furniture. Distant voices at supper — Rask entertaining guests, which was better than Rask prowling, though Bram noted with professional displeasure that the man's voice carried even through walls, smooth and unhurried, a voice used to being agreed with.

The corridor to the study was a lesson in what stolen money bought. Carpet deep enough to swallow footsteps. Lamps turned low but never out. Bram moved along the wall with his candle shuttered, counting doors by the inventory in his head — *billiard room, conservatory, gallery, study* — and felt the peculiar calm that always came over him in other people's archives. Books and papers were honest company. They told you exactly what they were.

The study door was unlocked. Rask, evidently, trusted his locks more than his guests.

Inside, the map cabinet stood against the far wall beneath a portrait of the director himself, painted at perhaps forty, wearing the expression of a man who has just been told he is magnificent and believes it. Bram knelt before the cabinet, took out his picks, and went to work with the tenderness of a man tuning an instrument.

Three locks. The first surrendered in forty seconds. The second fought him, then yielded to patience. The third was simply a matter of knowing that wealthy men buy locks from catalogs, and Bram had read the catalog.

The drawer slid open.

There it was. The third plate — copper, heavy, cold as sea-bottom — wrapped in flannel, with Rask's own inventory tag tied to it in a neat clerk's hand: *Skarvholm duplicate — Meridian approach — DO NOT CIRCULATE.*

*Do not circulate.* Bram almost laughed. The whole war, in three words. Somewhere in this very drawer's history, a surveyor had engraved the true soundings of the Meridian approach — the rocks that had killed the *Meridian* itself and Signe's father with it — and some directorate hand had looked at truth and written *do not circulate*, and men had died, and widows had waited, and the tide had kept its counsel.

Bram swapped the plate for the lead dummy they'd cast from wax impressions — it wouldn't survive close inspection, but it might survive a week, and a week was all they needed — closed the drawer, relocked all three locks, and stood.

That was when the study door opened.

* * *

Later — much later, in the weeks after — Bram would replay this moment a hundred times, and in every version he froze differently, ran differently, shouted a warning sooner. In none of the versions did he manage anything useful, because in fact what happened was this: the door opened, lamplight spilled across the carpet, and Bram dropped behind the wingback chair by the fireplace with the plate hugged to his chest like a stolen infant, heart hammering so hard he feared it would give him away by echo.

Voices. Two men. Rask, concluding an evening — "*—no, no, my friend, the charter review will proceed on schedule, I guarantee it*" — and another man whose laugh Bram didn't know. They crossed the study. A decanter clinked. And then, horribly, the chair beside which Bram crouched creaked under the weight of a sitting guest, close enough that Bram could smell brandy and wet wool and could see, if he dared tilt his head, the shine of polished boots.

He did not dare. He counted his own heartbeats and made a private vow to donate generously to libraries for the rest of his life.

Minutes passed. Five. Seven. The guest droned about herring quotas. Then — blessed, miraculous — Rask rang for his man, the guest rose, the door opened and closed, and the study belonged to Bram and the rain again.

He gave it another two minutes out of pure spite, then rose on legs gone wooden, retrieved the plate from where he'd tucked it beneath the chair skirt, and made for the window he'd chosen on the plans — the one over the conservatory, with a drainpipe and a drop into soft border earth.

The plate was heavier than he remembered carrying it in. Or he was lighter. Either way, the descent took an age, the drainpipe groaning like a ship's rigging, and when his feet finally met the flowerbed the impact shot up through his knees and he stood swaying in the rain, grinning like an idiot in the dark.

They had it. All three plates. The whole true coastline of the lie.

He circled toward the tradesman's lane, keeping to the shadows of the garden wall, and saw the wagon where it should be, lamps hooded, Oskar on the box, Torvald standing at the horses' heads pretending to adjust a harness that needed no adjusting.

And that was when Bram heard the second carriage coming down the lane.

It came fast, unhooded lamps swinging, and pulled up short of the wine wagon, and a lantern bloomed, and a voice called out — a voice Bram knew from the workshop, from the guild halls, from nightmares — Voss's junior man, Gjelsvik, one of the directorate's watchers, saying something cheerful and terrible about roads and weather and *hold there a moment.*

Bram flattened against the wall. The plan, at this point, had a single line left in it: nobody is seen, everybody walks away. Torvald needed only to stay by the horses, be boring, be a courier, be invisible.

Instead, Bram watched Torvald do arithmetic with his face — quick, grim, the calculation of a man who could see what Bram could not from his angle: that Gjelsvik's lantern light was falling across the wagon bed, across the tarpaulin, and that under the tarpaulin, besides twelve bottles of wine, there was a canvas satchel containing two survey plates and the corrected drafts, because they'd brought everything, because Hazel had insisted the plates never leave their sight, and Gjelsvik, curious and bored and drunk on his own authority, was walking toward the wagon with his hand already lifting the lantern.

Bram saw Torvald step forward. Saw him plant himself squarely in the lantern's path, hat tipped back, wearing the easy insolence of a Bergen card-player — and heard him say, loud enough to carry, "Evening, friend. Careful with that light, you'll spook the horses, and they're worth more than your pension."

Gjelsvik stopped. Squinted. The lantern rose higher.

"*Torvald?*" The name came out wondering, then sharp. "Torvald Ness? By all the saints — they said you were dead, or fled, or—" He turned his head and shouted toward the house, up at the lit windows, and his voice cracked with the joy of a man about to be promoted: "*Guards! To the gate! I've found him! I've found the courier!*"

What happened next happened fast and badly, the way real things do. The tradesman's door burst open. Boots on gravel. Torvald could have run — Bram, pressed into the shadow of the wall twenty feet away, screamed at him silently to *run* — but running meant leading them to the wagon, and the wagon meant the plates, and the plates meant everything: Signe's father, the drowned crews, Aldric's name, Hazel's three years of grief and ink.

So Torvald did not run. He swung instead — one wild, brave, hopeless blow that caught Gjelsvik across the jaw and put him down in the mud — and then two guards took him by the arms and a third took him around the neck, and Bram, moving at last because staying still had become the greater danger, slipped along the wall, over the low gate at the lane's end, and into the rain.

From the darkness beyond the hedge he heard Torvald's voice one last time, clear and infuriatingly unafraid:

"Gentlemen! Gentlemen — I'll come quietly, but I warn you, I'm *terrible* company!"

Then the gate of Rask's house closed on the sound of him, and the lane was only rain and the wagon's horses stamping, and Bram stood in the black garden of a stranger with a copper survey plate against his chest, victorious, complete, sick to his soul.

* * *

Oskar drove the wagon out slow and lawful, exactly as a wine wagon should, while Bram lay in the back under the tarpaulin with the plate and shook — not from cold, or not only from cold. Signe met them two streets from the harbor, breathless in her servant's dress, having slipped out the front door mid-crisis with the composure of a born liar and the eyes of someone who had heard shouting and known.

"Tell me," she said.

"They have him."

She closed her eyes. For a moment she looked very young, and very old. "The plates?"

"Safe. All three. We got everything." Bram heard his own voice go strange and flat. "We got everything, and they got Torvald. He stepped into the light on purpose, Signe. He *chose* it. Gjelsvik was going to look under the tarp and Torvald walked into the lantern like — like stepping in front of a cart."

Signe was quiet for the length of two streets. Then she said, in the voice she used for cataloguing wrecks, "Then we know what to do tomorrow."

"Yes."

"And tonight?"

Bram looked at the plate in his lap, the true soundings of the Meridian approach gleaming dull gold in the passing lamplight, and thought about the word *do not circulate*, and about Torvald's terrible joke ringing off the gravel as they dragged him inside, and about Hazel, waiting at the workshop with candles lit and supper kept warm, believing they'd all come home together.

"Tonight," he said, "we tell her. And then nobody sleeps."

* * *

Hazel took the news the way the sea takes a stone — without visible disturbance, and with the certainty of swallowing.

She stood in the workshop doorway with the lamp held high, listening to Bram's account given twice, once for her and once for Signe arriving soaked behind him, and her face did not crumple and her voice did not shake. She set the lamp on the workbench. She unwrapped the atlas draft from its oilcloth with steady hands. She looked at the third plate a long moment, running her thumb along the engraved soundings of the Meridian approach as if reading braille.

"He'll be held in the guild strongroom," she said at last. "Not the town jail — Rask will want him where the charter review can't reach him, where Voss controls the keys and the directorate controls Voss."

"You know that how?" Signe asked.

"Because it's what I'd do," Hazel said, "if I were a coward with something to hide." She began, calmly, terribly, to sharpen a pencil. "They'll question him. They'll try to make him give up the workshop, the plates, us. So we have days — maybe only one — before they break him or move him or worse."

"We could run," Bram said, and hated the words even as he offered them, because offering them was honest, and honesty was his one virtue. "Take the atlas, take the plates, get to a printer in Bergen or Christiania. Publish without him. He'd want—"

"He'd want us to finish it," Hazel said. "But we're not leaving him, Bram. We are a regiment, and a regiment does not abandon its courier." She laid the pencil down and looked at them both, and beneath the calm Bram saw the thing that had been burning in her since the workshop burned — grief and fury fused into something with a working edge.

"Voss holds those keys," she said. "And I had a conversation with Voss once, in this room, that he thinks ended with him winning." A thin smile, the first of the night. "Tomorrow I'm going to remind him how it actually ended. Because we're getting Torvald back — and while the directorate is busy interrogating one courier, we're going to copy every ledger in their strongroom and burn their lies down with their own fire."

Outside, the rain kept falling on the ruined workshop roof, and inside, the lamp burned over the finished atlas and the stolen plate, and Bram, who had planned this night to the minute and lost it anyway, understood at last what Aldric's marginal code had been trying to teach all along: that the map was never the country, the plan was never the night, and the only reliable chart through dark water was the people willing to row it with you.

Somewhere across the harbor, in a stone room with a single lamp, Torvald was beginning to count the doors.
