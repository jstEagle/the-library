## 14. Interrogation by Lamplight

The chair they gave Torvald was bolted to the floor.

He took this as a compliment. In his experience — and his experience of chairs was, by now, embarrassingly extensive — a man only got bolted down when someone important had decided he was worth the carpentry. Voss wanted him comfortable enough to talk and trapped enough to stay talking. Torvald resolved to disappoint both hopes with equal dedication.

The room was small, windowless, and lit by a single brass lamp on a table between the two chairs. Someone had thoughtfully provided a second chair for the interrogator, which struck Torvald as almost domestic. Two men, one lamp, a long night ahead. It might have been a fishing cabin if not for the iron ring in the wall beside him and the fact that one of his wrists was attached to it by a chain short enough that he couldn't reach the lamp but could, just barely, reach the water pitcher they'd left for him.

He drank some water. He made it last.

*Count everything,* Aldric had told him once, years ago, over cards in a smoky tavern in Bergen while Aldric cheated him at rummy with the serenity of a bishop. *A courier's first skill isn't running. It's noticing. Any fool can run. Not every fool knows which way.*

So Torvald counted.

The room: eight paces by six, though he'd have to pace it later to be sure, and he intended to pace it later. Stone walls, older than the guild, probably part of the original harbor fort before the cartographers moved in and put shelves everywhere. One door, oak, opening inward — he'd watched it open when they brought him in; hinges on the far side, so anyone entering would be briefly framed in the corridor light. A ventilation grate near the ceiling, north side, breathing cold salt air. The lamp burned whale oil, trimmed well, maybe six hours' fuel.

Six hours. He hoped Voss talked slowly.

* * *

Voss arrived at what felt like midnight but could have been any hour, because the guild strongroom kept no clocks and Torvald suspected this was deliberate. A man without time is a man who can't measure how long he's held out.

Voss carried a folder under his arm and two candles he didn't light, setting them on the table like props in a play he'd performed many times. He sat, folded his hands, and regarded Torvald with the expression of an accountant who has found a discrepancy he intends to enjoy finding.

"Torvald Lund," Voss said. "Or is that your name? We've been having difficulty confirming it."

"It's my name." This was even true. It simply wasn't his *only* name, and Torvald saw no reason to mention the others, which existed for exactly this kind of evening.

"Courier. For whom?"

"For whoever pays me." Torvald smiled and let it look tired rather than insolent. Tired was disarming. Insolent got you hit, and he'd already taken a knock across the ear during the arrest that still rang like a bell in a gale. "I'm afraid I'm not a very good witness, Inspector. I carry things. I don't read them. Reading them would be dishonest."

"Dishonest," Voss repeated, tasting the word.

"A man who reads other people's letters is a snoop. I have standards."

"You were captured coming out of Director Rask's residence at three in the morning with survey plates in your coat."

"Was I?" Torvald looked down at himself with mild curiosity. "They took my coat. I couldn't say what was in it. I'm often cold; I take coats. Perhaps it wasn't even mine. Have you checked the collar for a name?"

For a moment — a brief, bright moment — Torvald genuinely believed Voss might strike him. Instead the inspector did something more alarming: he smiled, opened the folder, and began laying papers on the table one at a time, each face-up, each positioned precisely where Torvald could read it upside down from four feet away.

This, too, was deliberate. Everything about Voss was deliberate. Torvald filed that away with everything else.

* * *

The papers came slowly, and Torvald understood quickly that they were not evidence. They were bait.

A sketch of Hazel Eriksen, done by someone competent, annotated in pencil: *apprentice, mapmaker's, workshop on Strandgaten.* A sketch of Signe, younger-looking, jaw set like her father's must have been. Bram, looking in the drawing exactly as startled as Bram always looked. And beneath those three, a fourth sheet that made something go cold and quiet behind Torvald's ribs: the workshop itself, drawn from the harbor side, with small crosses marking the doors and — circled twice — the back stair to Aldric's old workroom.

"They're not accused of anything," Voss said mildly, watching him. "Yet."

"Neither am I, I hope."

"You were found inside a private residence holding stolen property belonging to the directorate. That's not nothing, Lund. That's years. That's a cell in Trondhjem where the damp gets into your bones by thirty-five and stays." Voss leaned back. "But I'm not the directorate. I'm the guild's inspector. My job is accuracy." He tapped the folder. "And I've been noticing, lately, that accuracy and Director Rask are not on speaking terms."

Torvald kept his face pleasant and empty, the face of a man following a conversation slightly behind its pace. Inside, his mind was working fast and cold. *He suspects. He suspects and he's frightened of suspecting. He wants confirmation but he can't ask directly, because asking directly makes him complicit in the question.*

"He seems a vigorous administrator," Torvald offered.

"He falsified coastal charts." Voss said it flatly, testing it against the air between them. "You know that. You stole the proof of it. So here is my offer, and you may consider it generous or consider it a trap, whichever keeps you awake tonight: tell me where the rest of the plates are, tell me who else is involved, and your friends—" he touched the sketches, all four, lightly, "—stay sketches."

*There it is.* Not justice. Leverage. Voss didn't care about drowned sailors; he cared about being the man standing on the right side when the floor collapsed, and he wanted to choose his spot in advance.

"I don't know where the plates are," Torvald said truthfully, because half-truths worked best anchored to real things. "I was paid to retrieve documents. I don't ask my employers their reasons. If I asked questions I'd have been out of work years ago."

"Who paid you?"

"A woman in Bergen. Middle-aged. Paid in coin, wore a gray cloak. I never saw her face twice."

It was a lovely lie because it described roughly forty percent of Bergen. Voss wrote it down anyway, which told Torvald the inspector was collecting, not convicting — building a file thick enough to hide behind whichever way events broke. A survivor, this one. Torvald could respect a survivor. He was one himself.

"The Eriksen girl," Voss said. "She's finishing Aldric Vester's atlas."

"Is she? I wouldn't know. Is that unusual? Apprentices finish masters' work. That's what apprentices are for."

"After a master dies, yes. But Aldric Vester isn't dead, is he."

And there it was, dropped on the table like a fish knife. Torvald let three full seconds pass, then frowned with genuine puzzlement, because he had, in fact, attended the funeral, and grief was the safest ground available.

"He's dead," Torvald said quietly. "I carried letters for him for six years. Good man. Terrible handwriting. I buried him."

"You carried letters for him." Voss pulled another sheet from the folder. "Dated after the funeral. In his hand. Do you want to see them?"

"No," said Torvald, and let his voice crack slightly on it, and watched Voss watch the crack and mark it — but mark it wrong, or at least he prayed so. Because here was the trap's true teeth: if Torvald denied knowing Aldric lived, he was lying, and Voss might prove it. If he admitted it, he confirmed the conspiracy and damned everyone including Hazel.

But there was a third road, and he took it. "If someone showed me letters in Aldric's hand dated after his funeral," Torvald said, slowly, "I would think either he's alive, or someone forged them — and if someone forged them, Inspector, then somebody wanted very much for people to believe he was alive. Ask yourself why. A dead man can't be questioned. A living one can. Which one frightens the directorate more?"

Voss sat back. The lamp hissed. Somewhere above them, through the vent grate, came the faint clang of the harbor bell, and Torvald counted the strokes — seven — and fixed the hour in his memory. Seven bells. The grate breathed from the north. The door opened inward. Eight by six paces, stone, one lamp, one chain, one bolted chair.

*Good. Keep talking, Inspector. Every word you spend on me is a word you don't spend watching Strandgaten.*

* * *

They talked until the lamp burned low.

Or rather, Voss talked and Torvald wandered, giving him just enough to seem broken-open and never enough to point anywhere true. He confessed, wearily, to being a courier for "parties interested in chart accuracy" — vague, deniable, technically damning but legally weightless. He named no names except names already burned: Aldric, dead and beyond harm; the gray-cloaked woman of Bergen, who multiplied obligingly every time she was mentioned until Torvald privately worried she'd need her own census entry.

Twice Voss left the room, and both times Torvald used the minutes like a miser.

First absence: he tested the chain. Four feet, no give, ring set deep in mortar — but the mortar around it was old and crumbled at one edge. He pressed a thumbnail into the gap. Soft. A man with a knife, a nail, an hour of patience, could worry that ring loose. He marked the mortar's color, pale gray with rust-bleed at the base, so he'd know it again in the dark.

Second absence: he stood, dragging his chair with him — the bolt allowed perhaps a foot of arc — and mapped the room properly. Door: oak, inward, iron lock, old pattern, the kind that surrendered to a bent nail if the nail knew locks, and Torvald knew locks the way sailors know weather. Vent grate: screws, painted over, but painted over *once*, recently, meaning someone used that passage or feared it. Floor stones: one near the table sat a finger's-width proud of its neighbors, worn proud by feet pacing this same circle before him. Others had sat in this chair. Others had counted these stones. The thought was strangely companionable.

When Voss returned, Torvald was sitting again, looking defeated, and the room was written on the inside of his eyelids.

"Last question," Voss said, gathering his papers with the air of a man concluding a transaction he wasn't sure he'd won. "Where will the atlas surface? There's a charter review in nine days. If corrected charts appear before the assembly—"

"Then someone will have committed fraud against the guild," Torvald said, "and I'd advise them to run somewhere with good harbors, since the charts will be honest ones."

Something flickered across Voss's face — annoyance, certainly, but underneath it, unmistakably, something that might have been the ghost of respect. Or appetite. With inspectors the two were hard to tell apart.

"You'll stay here," Voss said, rising. "Consider it protective custody. Consider it whatever helps you sleep." At the door he paused, hand on the frame, and did not turn around. "The Meridian," he said. "My brother was bosun on the Meridian."

The door closed before Torvald could answer, which was probably the point. Some confessions are only safe when no one hears them.

Torvald sat in the dark a long while after the footsteps faded, the chain slack against his wrist, the last of the lamp oil guttering.

Nine days. Nine days of stone and salt air and one proud floor-stone, while Hazel finished the atlas alone and Bram and Signe ran messages through a city crawling with Voss's agents. He should feel afraid. He supposed, distantly, that he did — fear was a sensible thing to feel, chained in the belly of the enemy's house.

But mostly he felt the room. Eight by six. North vent, inward door, soft mortar, proud stone. He closed his eyes and walked it again, corner to corner, until he could have drawn it blindfolded, which was, in fact, the plan.

Then, from beyond the door, muffled by oak and distance, he heard boots pass in the corridor — two pairs, unhurried, speaking low — and caught a single fragment:

"—the girl went home. She's finishing it *there*, the fool."

Torvald smiled into the dark, and began counting the hours until dawn.
