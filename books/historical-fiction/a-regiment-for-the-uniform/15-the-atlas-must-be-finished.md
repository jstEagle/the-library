## 15. The Atlas Must Be Finished

The workshop smelled of scorched pine and cold ash, even three days after the fire, and no amount of scrubbing seemed to change it. The smell had gotten into everything — the floorboards, the drapes, the wool of my sleeves. I had stopped trying to wash it out. It was, I decided, a fair trade: the workshop had kept me for eleven years, and now I would keep its grief a while longer.

I had moved what remained of the atlas to the kitchen table of my small rooms above the chandlery, because the table was wide, the light from the window was honest, and because Signe had declared that I was not to work alone in the burned workshop after dark. "You'll set yourself alight and then where will we be?" she'd said, hands on hips, looking for all the world like her mother must have looked when scolding herring boats back to harbor.

"I'll be careful," I'd said.

"You'll be *careful*," Bram had echoed, in a voice pitched exactly like mine, which I felt was insubordinate but also accurate.

So now it was four nights before the charter review, and I sat at my kitchen table at an hour when sensible mapmakers were asleep, with forty-one finished sheets stacked to my left and nine unfinished ones waiting to my right like patients in a ward.

Nine sheets. Four days. One apprentice who had never once, in eleven years, drawn a coastline that anyone outside this room would ever see.

I flexed my right hand. It ached in the particular way that hands ache when they have been holding a pen for fourteen hours a day — not pain, exactly, more a low complaint, like a dog that wants to be let out. I ignored it. There would be time for complaints later. Possibly much later. Possibly never.

* * *

Aldric's voice lived in the margins.

That is not poetry. Well — it is partly poetry, but it is also literal fact, which is the best kind of poetry. For years he had written his working notes in the margins of his drafts, and over those same years I had absorbed them the way one absorbs a language: without noticing, until one day you catch yourself thinking in it.

His notes were never dry. A man might expect a master cartographer's margin to read *verify soundings*, or *recheck bearing against 1887 survey*. Aldric's margins said things like:

*The skerry at 61°12′ has eaten two buoys and shows no remorse. Chart it wider than the instruments say. The sea lies; we do not.*

And:

*A fisherman named Ove swears there is deep water here. Ove has fished this coast for fifty years and buried a brother at Ternskerry. Believe Ove. Instruments are opinions. Ove is testimony.*

I had spent the first weeks of finishing the atlas merely copying his style, the way an apprentice copies letterforms — faithfully, hollowly. Somewhere around sheet thirty, something shifted. I stopped imitating him and started *answering* him. His margin on sheet thirty-four asked, in effect, whether the currents past Gråholm had been re-sounded since the storm year. And on the night I sat down with sheet thirty-four, I found myself writing in the margin:

*Sounded twice, October last, by your apprentice, who fell in once and can confirm the current's temper personally. Depths corrected accordingly.*

I stared at what I'd written for a long moment. Then I did not cross it out.

By sheet thirty-eight I was arguing with him. He had charted a stretch of coast near Vardnes with a confidence I now knew — thanks to the true survey plates, thanks to Torvald's smuggled knowledge — was built on guild-falsified soundings. Aldric had suspected it; his margin practically vibrated with suspicion (*These figures arrive suspiciously round. The sea does not count in tens.*). But he had been forced, by the directorate's review process, to include them anyway.

I corrected them. Every one. And beside the correction I wrote, in the small precise hand that was becoming mine:

*You were right, Master. They were lying. We have the plates to prove it. Forgive the liberty — you taught me that the map serves the sailor, and the sailor is owed the truth even when the guild would rather he drown quietly.*

My eyes blurred. I let them. Grief, I have learned, is like ink: it smears if you touch it too soon. Give it a moment and it sets.

* * *

Signe came up near midnight with tea and the expression of a woman delivering tea under protest.

"You should be asleep," she said, setting the cup down with a click that meant business.

"So should you."

"I'm not the one performing miracles on a deadline." She peered at the sheets, and her face did the thing it always did when she looked at the atlas — went soft and fierce at once, like a mother watching her child argue with an adult and win. "Which one's this?"

"Vardnes. The falsified stretch."

"Then make it sing," she said simply, and left me to it, taking the empty cups and, I noticed, the stub of candle I'd been saving, replacing it with a fresh one from some mysterious household reserve. Found families, I have concluded, are chiefly a system for making sure someone else remembers to buy candles.

I worked until the window went grey, then blue-grey, then the particular bruised gold that means the fishing fleet is already out and you are officially ridiculous. Sheet thirty-eight done. Eight remained.

* * *

Bram arrived mid-morning with bread, cheese, and news delivered in his usual manner, which was to say sideways, while pretending great interest in the window latch.

"Torvald says the guild's sent notices. Charter review's confirmed for Friday, full assembly, Rask presiding." He tore the bread with unnecessary violence. "Also Voss came by the workshop again. Asked Signe where you were."

"And Signe said?"

"Signe said you were 'attending to family matters.' Which is technically true, as we are now your family and this atlas is a matter." He grinned, then sobered. "He also asked about the plates. Whether anything had come back from Skarvholm."

"What did she say to that?"

"That the weather out there eats boats and asks no questions." Bram shrugged. "She's better at lying than either of us. Terrifying, really. I've started taking notes."

After he left, I turned to sheet thirty-nine, and the day folded itself away behind me.

* * *

There is a strange country between exhaustion and clarity, and I lived there for the next three days. Sleep came in shifts of two hours, timed by candle-length rather than clock. Signe enforced meals with the iron consistency of a tide table. Torvald appeared each evening to report on preparations — Voss's wavering loyalties, the ledgers we'd need, the sailors being gathered to speak — and each evening he would stand at my elbow for a few minutes, reading the day's finished sheets, saying nothing.

It unnerved me at first, that silence. Then, on the second night, he tapped one margin with a blunt finger — the one where I'd answered Aldric about the Gråholm currents — and said only, "He'd have laughed at the falling-in part. Loudly. In front of people."

"He'd have made me repeat the story at every guild dinner for a decade."

"Yes," Torvald said, with such warmth that I understood, finally, why Aldric had trusted this man with the most dangerous errand of his life. "Exactly that."

On the third night, seven sheets remained. On the fourth morning — Thursday, one day before the review — five.

* * *

The fifth-to-last sheet nearly broke me.

It was the Meridian channel. Signe's father's ship had broken there, on a chart that showed safe water over a reef the guild had quietly erased. I knew the numbers by heart now; Signe had matched them to the wreck report until they were branded into all of us. But knowing numbers and drawing them are different acts. My hand shook so badly on the first attempt that the line wavered like a thing alive, and I had to scrape the ink back off the vellum and begin again, breathing, the way Aldric had taught me to breathe before fine work: in for the length of a wave, out for the length of a wave, until the sea inside you settles.

When my hand steadied, I drew the reef exactly where it was. Not where the guild wished it. Where it was. And in the margin, where Aldric would have written something wry and cutting, I found my own words arriving before I'd decided on them:

*Here the Meridian broke, 14 March 1889. Nine drowned. The chart in circulation omitted this reef by directorate order. It is restored here, with their names: Halversen, Koll, Koll, Brandt, Iversen, Iversen, Skau, Skau, and the boy Nilsen, fourteen, who had wanted to see the world.*

*I did not know any of them. I know them now. That is what maps are for.*

I put down the pen. I walked to the window. I watched the harbor for a while, the little boats going out with their faith placed in paper like this, and I let myself feel all of it — the fury, the sorrow, the terrible honor of the work — and then I went back and kept drawing, because feeling is not the work, but it is the fuel, and there was still vellum waiting.

* * *

Friday dawned clear and hard, the kind of light that makes everything look sharper than it is, which seemed appropriate.

Two sheets remained. I had planned, insofar as planning survived contact with reality, to finish them Thursday night. Instead I slept four hours at Signe's insistence — "Dead witnesses are less persuasive than living ones, and you look like a drowned thing" — and woke before the gulls to find Torvald already downstairs, brewing coffee in my kitchen with the ease of a man who had been doing it for years instead of days.

"The assembly convenes at ten," he said. "Rask will open with ceremony. Voss will sit where I can see him. The sailors arrive at half past — Signe is bringing them the back way." He handed me coffee. "How close?"

I looked at the two remaining sheets. The final one was the master index — the sheet that bound all others together, that declared, in effect, *this is the truth, whole and entire, and here is how to read it.*

"One hour," I said. "Perhaps ninety minutes. If nothing goes wrong."

"Something always goes wrong."

"Thank you, Torvald. Your optimism is a lighthouse."

"It's kept ships honest for years." He almost smiled, then didn't. "Hazel. When you walk in there today—"

"Don't." I held up my hand. "If you say something stirring, I'll cry, and I've used all my crying on sheet forty-nine, and I haven't the reserves."

He nodded, and drank his coffee, and let the silence do the stirring instead, which was more effective anyway.

* * *

The last sheet took ninety-four minutes. I know because Bram counted aloud until Signe threatened to count *him* into the fjord.

The index demanded everything: every sheet referenced, every correction logged, every discrepancy between the guild's published charts and the true survey noted in Aldric's code, keyed to the recovered plates. It was the atlas's spine, its argument, its confession and accusation in one. As I wrote the final entries, I heard his voice so clearly that twice I glanced up, certain he was standing behind me — not a ghost, nothing so dramatic, just the residue of eleven years of a man talking while he worked, narrating his craft the way some men hum.

*Index honestly, girl,* the ghost-voice said. *An index is a promise that everything in the book can be found. Everything. Even the parts that shame us. Especially those.*

*Yes, Master,* I wrote, in the margin, in the space he had left me without ever having met me.

And then, at the bottom of the final sheet, beneath the title block, there came the question I had been circling for weeks the way a moth circles a lamp: whose name goes on this?

Aldric Eriksen, dead and staged-dead and alive somewhere beyond the sea. Or Hazel Eriksen, apprentice, invisible, safe.

My pen hovered. Outside, the gulls argued. Downstairs, my strange new family waited — a courier, a widow's daughter, a boy who lied beautifully, and a workshop that smelled of ash and refused to die.

I thought about what Aldric had taught me about margins. That they were not empty space. That they were where the map told the truth about itself.

I wrote both names.

I added, beneath them, in letters small enough to require attention but large enough to be found by anyone who bothered to look — because that, too, was his lesson:

*Finished by the apprentice, who answers the master's questions still.*

Then I capped the ink, squared the sheets, wrapped the atlas in oilcloth, and held it against my chest, where I could feel my own heartbeat through it, quick and stubborn and alive.

From the street below came the sound I had been dreading and awaiting in equal measure: boots on cobbles, several pairs, unhurried, official. Bram appeared in the doorway, pale.

"That's guild escort," he whispered. "They're at the door. And Hazel—" he swallowed, "—they're asking for you. By name. Voss is with them."

I looked down at the atlas in my arms. Ninety minutes ago it had been unfinished. Now it was the most dangerous object on this coast.

"Well," I said, and was surprised to hear my voice come out steady, "let's not keep the guild waiting."

I started down the stairs.
