## 16. Voss's Bargain

He came to the workshop at dusk, alone.

That was the first strange thing. Guild inspectors did not travel alone; they traveled in pairs like a matched set of calipers, one to measure and one to witness. But Inspector Voss stood at my door in the blue half-light with his hat in his hands — actually *in his hands*, as if he had forgotten he was holding it — and no second man lurking by the gate, no clerk shivering in a doorway pretending not to take notes.

"The ferry leaves tomorrow," I said, which was true, and which was also the only sentence I could find that wasn't either an accusation or a scream.

"I know the timetable," he said. "I've ridden it eleven times."

I stepped aside and let him in, because Bram was three streets away watching Rask's residence and Signe was copying ledgers under Torvald's instruction, and because a man who comes alone is a man who has already decided something, and I wanted very much to know what.

* * *

I lit two lamps instead of one. It felt important, somehow — a negotiation conducted in half-darkness would have felt like conspiracy, and what I intended was not conspiracy. It was, I told myself, *bookkeeping*. The guild had kept false books for twenty years. Someone needed to keep honest ones, and if that someone happened to be me, well. I had been keeping Aldric's books since I was fourteen, and before that I had kept my father's, and before that I had kept tally of everything my brother owed me for minding his fishing lines, in a notebook with a small drawing of him drowning on every page where the debt grew.

Voss sat on the workbench stool like a man sitting on a church pew during a sermon he disagreed with. His hands were folded. His hat lay on the table between us like a surrendered weapon.

"You forged your inventory pages," he said.

"Yes."

"Badly, at first. You corrected yourself by the third page. The ink was too fresh on the first attempt — you wrote over wet sizing." He turned the hat brim a quarter-turn, the way a man turns a ring when he is nervous. "I noticed immediately. I said nothing."

"Why?"

"Because a liar who improves mid-forgery is worth more than an honest fool." He looked up at me then, and I saw for the first time how tired he was. Not the tiredness of late hours. The tiredness of a man who has been carrying a ledger of his own for years and has never once been able to balance it. "You want to know why I'm here, Eriksen. I'll tell you. But you will let me tell it in order, because I have practiced it, and if you interrupt me I will lose my nerve, and then we shall both go back to being enemies, which is exhausting."

I poured him coffee. He took it without asking whether it was good. It was terrible — Signe had made it, and Signe believed coffee should be boiled until it confessed — and he drank it anyway, which told me more about his state of mind than anything he'd yet said.

"In order," I agreed.

* * *

"My father was a chart-man," Voss began. "Before the directorate. Before the charter reform of '74 unified the surveys. He drew the Lofoten approaches from an open boat, in weather that would frighten you, and he was proud of it in the way craftsmen are proud — quietly, and only when drunk." A thin smile. "He taught me that a chart is a promise. That every line on it says: *a man stood here, and measured honestly, and you may trust this.*"

"And then?"

"And then I joined the guild, rose through the archive division, and learned what happens to promises when they pass through Director Halvard Rask's office." He set down the cup. "You know the shape of it. I know you know, because you've spent three months behaving like people who know. The plates are altered. The alterations are logged nowhere. The insurance settlements are routed through three shipping houses that all share a notary, who shares a card table with Rask every Thursday. Ships break. Men drown. The directorate collects."

"You've known this how long?"

"Nine years." He said it flatly, without drama, and the flatness was worse than any confession shouted could have been. "Nine years I've filed reports that were accurate up to the point where accuracy became dangerous, and then filed them slightly bent, just enough to keep my post, just enough to stay inside where I might someday see the whole machine. That is what I told myself. *Stay inside. Wait. Watch.* Do you know what staying inside accomplishes after nine years, Eriksen?"

I didn't answer.

"Nothing," he said. "It accomplishes nothing. It accomplishes a salary and a pension and a drawer full of reports so carefully worded they could be read aloud in church. And meanwhile there is a woman in Bodø who writes to the guild every autumn asking why her husband's wreck report places the shoal four hundred meters east of where any fisherman in the parish knows it lies, and every autumn she receives a letter signed by me explaining that the discrepancy falls within acceptable survey tolerance." He laughed, briefly, horribly. "Four hundred meters. Acceptable tolerance. Her husband is at the bottom of the sea within our tolerance."

The lamp flame leaned in some draft I couldn't feel. Neither of us moved to straighten it.

"So why now?" I asked. "Why come here, tonight, to the workshop you've been circling for months? Why not go to the magistrate?"

"The magistrate is Rask's cousin by marriage."

"The press, then."

"Rask holds paper on two of the three presses that would dare print it, and the third is run by a man whose son captains a coastal steamer whose charts the guild supplies." He spread his hands. "You see my problem. Nine years of watching taught me exactly why nobody has ever watched back. The machine is built so that every door out leads into another room of the machine."

"Then why here?"

He was quiet a long moment. Outside, the tide was turning; I could hear it working at the pilings beneath the floor, patient as everything else the sea does.

"Because of the atlas," he said finally. "Because three months ago I came to inspect this workshop intending to find grounds to seize it, and instead I found margin notes in Aldric Sørensen's hand that were dated *after* his funeral, and instead of reporting it I sat in the ferry cabin on the way home and thought: *someone is finishing it.* Someone is finishing the atlas he died — or pretended to die — for. And I thought: that person is doing in secret, at night, unpaid, what I have failed to do in nine years of daylight and wages." He met my eyes. "I am here because I cannot bear to be outdone by an apprentice any longer. My pride has finally overruled my cowardice. I am not proud of the mechanism, but I am not going to argue with it."

* * *

I should say now what I felt, because I have tried, in this account, never to pretend my feelings were tidier than they were.

What I felt was rage. A clean, cold, entirely reasonable rage — the rage of a woman who has burned her inventory pages twice and hidden the atlas behind a false wall three times and slept with it under her bed like a child with a doll, and who now learns that the man who forced all of that hiding, the inspector whose visits made Signe's hands shake, has known the truth about Rask for *nine years* and filed politely worded memos about it.

But underneath the rage — I am ashamed to record this, and I record it anyway, because a map that omits its reefs is worse than useless — underneath the rage was recognition. Because I too had stayed quiet. I too had finished sheets and said nothing, filed nothing, published nothing, for three years. My reasons were better than his. I believe that sincerely. But the shape of the silence was the same shape, and I knew what it cost to break it, and some traitorous part of me respected that he had walked through my door at all.

"You're afraid of ruin," I said.

"I *am* ruin," he said. "I have been ruin since 1885, walking around in a decent coat. What I fear is that it will have been for nothing. That I bend for nine years and then break anyway, and the breaking buys nothing, changes nothing, and the woman in Bodø gets another letter with my signature on it." He picked up the hat, turned it, set it down again. "So. You have evidence. Plates, discrepancies, ledgers — I assume you have ledgers by now, you have Torvald Reine, and that man could steal the fillings from a sleeping bishop. And in nine days the charter review convenes, and Rask will stand before the assembled guild and be confirmed for another seven-year term unless something lands on the table between him and the votes."

"There is a plan," I said carefully.

"There is always a plan. Plans are cheap. What plans require is access." He leaned forward. "The review convenes in the guildhall. The directorate's chambers adjoin it. During the session, the strongroom below holds the current term's full correspondence — including, I happen to know, the insurance routing letters Rask has been too arrogant to destroy, because destroying them would require trusting someone enough to hand them over, and Rask trusts no one, which is his single virtue. The strongroom opens for the duration of the review. Twice daily, for the document clerks. Guarded by two men. Both of whom answer to me for scheduling."

My heart was doing something unprofessional. I made myself sit still.

"You're offering to open the door."

"I'm offering to arrange that the door is *unattended* for eleven minutes, and to have written, dated, sealed testimony of everything I have just told you — everything I know, names, routes, the notary, the Thursday card games — deposited somewhere that surfaces whether or not I do. I am offering, in short, to hang myself alongside Rask, and I want it done properly. Witnessed. Documented. On the record." He smiled without humor. "If I am to be ruined, Eriksen, I intend to be ruined *accurately*. By the correct account. In a fair hand. With the dates right."

And there it was. Not conscience, or not only conscience. Ambition — the twisted, buried, starving ambition of a man who had spent nine years being accurate in service of a lie and wanted, more than safety, more than pardon, to be accurate in service of the truth *once*, publicly, where it counted. He didn't want forgiveness. He wanted attribution.

I understood that completely. God help me, I understood it completely.

* * *

"Here is what I can give you," I said.

I took a sheet of good rag paper — the heavy stock we saved for title pages — and dipped my pen, and wrote while I talked, because talking across a bare table is negotiation but writing while talking is a contract.

"A page in the atlas," I said. "The appendix. Where the sources are named. Every survey cited, every plate referenced, every testimony credited — there will be a line reading: *corroborated by Inspector Anders Voss, Guild Archive Division, who provided access, testimony, and schedule of the strongroom, at personal risk, on the sixteenth of October.* Signed by me. Printed. Bound into every copy that ever leaves the press." I looked up. "You will not be a footnote, Voss. You will be part of the apparatus. Anyone who ever consults this atlas — every navigator, every harbor master, every apprentice for the next fifty years — will read your name in the same breath as the correction of the charts themselves. That is the thing you want. That is the only version of 'the right side of history' that lasts longer than the men standing on it. Not a pardon. A *citation*."

His face did something complicated. For a moment I thought he would refuse — I watched the old cowardice come up in him like a tide, the nine years of bent reports rising to defend their author — and then it went out again, and what remained was naked and almost young.

"No one will believe it," he said hoarsely. "When it's done. They'll say I was flipped, bought, coerced—"

"You were flipped," I agreed. "Bought with a paragraph and coerced by shame. It will all be in the citation. I'm a mapmaker, Inspector. I don't leave things out because they're unflattering. I leave things out because they're *irrelevant*, and your motives stopped being irrelevant about ten minutes ago."

A sound escaped him that might have been a laugh. He pressed his knuckles to his mouth.

"The eleven minutes," he said, recovering. "Second day of the review, mid-session, while Rask is on the floor answering questions about the northern survey budget. He'll be occupied. He's never once missed the chance to hear himself discuss money. The clerks' rotation is mine to write; I'll write myself onto another assignment and leave the roster showing two men who won't question a gap, because the gap will be initialed." He paused. "There is a condition. Two conditions."

"Name them."

"First: the woman in Bodø. When this is done, someone goes to Bodø and tells her the truth with their own mouth. Not a letter. Not a settlement. A person, standing in her kitchen. I'll do it myself if you'll allow it, though I warn you I am not good at kitchens." His voice caught on the last word and he pretended it hadn't. "Second: Torvald Reine. Whatever else happens — whatever he stole, whoever he answers to — he walks free at the end of it. He carried letters for Aldric Sørensen for six years, and I intercepted three of them in '88 and read them and sent them on anyway, because they were beautiful and I was already damned, and I have wondered ever since whether sending them on was the last honest act of my career or merely the most comfortable crime of it." He stood, and put on his hat, and became, by visible effort, an inspector again. "I'd like the chance to find out."

"He'll walk free," I said. "He'll probably complain the whole time. He complains when things go well. It's how he prays."

Something warmed in Voss's face at that — the first unfrozen expression I'd seen on him — and he took the contract page I'd written, read it twice, and signed beneath my words with a hand that shook only slightly, and only at the end.

At the door he stopped, hand on the latch, back still to me.

"Eriksen. The forgery. The third page, where you corrected yourself." He didn't turn around. "It was good work. If this ends badly for all of us, I want you to know that I noticed, and that it was good work, and that in nine years of reading other men's records it was the first thing I'd seen in the guild's files that I was glad to be lied to about."

Then he was gone, down the steps and into the dark, and I stood in the lamplight holding a signed confession worth more than every plate in Skarvholm, and I did the only sensible thing.

I ran to find Signe, and I laughed, and possibly I cried a little, and I definitely began drafting the appendix page in three different hands to choose the fairest one, because some promises you make to strangers, and some you make to tired men in hats, and both kinds go on the map.

Two days, then. Eleven minutes. One door.

Time to go and steal a guild.
