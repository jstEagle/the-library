## 17. Breaking the Strongroom

Hazel had always assumed that the hardest part of any great undertaking would be the danger — the locked doors, the lantern light, the held breath. She was wrong. The hardest part was standing in a corridor of the guildhall, holding a tray of tea things she had no business carrying, while Inspector Voss adjusted his cuffs and pretended not to know her.

"You're late," he said without looking at her.

"The ferry was delayed."

"The ferry is always delayed. That's why one leaves early." He took the cup from her tray, drank, and grimaced. "You've over-sweetened this."

"You've under-appreciated it," Hazel said, and saw — just barely — the corner of his mouth move.

It had taken three meetings to bring him fully around, and even now she did not entirely trust him, and she suspected the feeling was mutual, and that both of them found this arrangement more comfortable than trust would have been. What Voss wanted was simple: to be standing on the right side of the room when history read itself aloud. What Hazel had offered him was simpler still: his name in the record, written fairly, as a man who chose correctly when choosing cost something.

"Rask arrives at nine," Voss murmured now, eyes forward. "The charter review convenes at half past. The directorate will be occupied for two hours at minimum — Rask loves the sound of his own procedural language, and there is a great deal of it. You'll have your window."

"And Voss's key?"

"My key," he corrected sharply, "is on my belt, where it has been for eleven years, and where it will remain until precisely ten o'clock, when I am required to fetch the annex files for the second session. It will be off my belt for no more than twenty minutes. If you are not through by then—"

"We'll be through by then." She paused. "Thank you, Inspector."

"Don't thank me." He drained the terrible tea and set the cup back on the tray. "If this goes wrong, I was never sweetened. Never late. Never anything at all." And he walked toward the assembly hall with the straight-backed stride of a man going to betray someone very carefully.

* * *

Torvald had spent four days in the strongroom learning it with his skin instead of his eyes.

That was the trick of endurance, Aldric had told him once, years ago, on a fog-bound dock in Bergen: *when they take everything from you, take inventory of what they cannot.* They had taken Torvald's boots, his coat, his knife, his name — the register called him Prisoner 44, which he felt lacked music. But they could not take the hours he'd spent memorizing. Twelve paces from the cell door to the turning stair. The stair turned left, then left again — the masons had built the strongroom inside the old salt cellar, and salt cellars were never built with efficiency in mind. A drain grate in the third corridor, loose at its north corner; he'd tested it with his thumbnail through the bars of the food hatch when the guard dozed. Two sentries on the outer door, changing watch at the quarter hour — but the changeover took ninety seconds, because Guard Halsten liked to finish his pipe first, and Guard Eik disliked Halsten enough not to hurry him.

And one more thing, learned tonight through the wall, in a whisper pitched so low it was nearly breath:

"Hazel?"

"I'm here." Her voice came through the stone like water through cloth. "Are you intact?"

"Bruised, bored, and insulted by the food. In that order."

"Good. Signe says you're to stop being charming to the guards; Bram says you're to keep being charming to the guards; I say we don't have time to argue about it. Ten o'clock. Voss's key opens the corridor gate. Signe will make noise in the east records — a small fire scare, nothing that burns, just smoke and shouting. When the guards go to look, you come out. Bram will meet you at the drain grate with tools and a very bad joke."

"What's the joke?"

"He hasn't written it yet. He's been workshopping it all week. Torvald —" a pause, and he could hear her pressing her forehead to the stone, the way she did when she was choosing words like she chose ink, carefully and forever. "We get the ledgers too. While we're in. The copying set is ready. We're not leaving empty-handed and we're not leaving anyone behind."

"Aldric would have said something wise here."

"Aldric would have said something cryptic and made me figure it out myself for six months."

"That too." He smiled at the wall, knowing she couldn't see it, knowing somehow that she felt it anyway. "Ten o'clock. Go be magnificent somewhere else."

* * *

The east records fire-scare, as it happened, was Signe's masterpiece.

She had spent two days cultivating a relationship with the hall's aging stove, feeding it slightly damp birchwood until it sulked and smoked exactly as much as a stove ought to smoke if it were having a bad day and no more — enough to sting eyes, not enough to char paper. At five minutes to ten she burst into the corridor outside the assembly hall coughing theatrically, crying "The records! The east records are smoking!" with such conviction that two clerks fainted in sympathy, and the entire east wing emptied in ninety seconds flat.

Bram, meanwhile, stood at the drain grate with a crowbar, a lantern shuttered to a coin of light, and the joke he had finally settled on, which he delivered to Torvald as the man slithered out of the gap smelling of salt and prison straw:

"What does a cartographer call a narrow escape?"

"I don't know," said Torvald, taking the crowbar, "what?"

"A margin." Bram grinned. "He got out by a margin. Signe said it first. I'm just delivering."

"It's terrible."

"She worked on it for three days."

"Then it's terrible and true, which is the best kind." Torvald flexed his fingers, rolled his shoulders, and looked up the dark corridor toward the directorate archive. Somewhere above them, Director Halvard Rask was holding forth on the sanctity of the guild charter, using the word *fiduciary* as though it were a hymn. "Ledgers first, or ledgers after?"

"After. You first, because you know the way, and me second, because if anyone catches us I'm the one who looks harmless."

"No one who has met you thinks you look harmless."

"Then we're agreed on the plan."

They went up together, quick and quiet, two shadows moving through a building full of men arguing about themselves.

* * *

Voss's key worked the way good keys do — without drama. The corridor gate swung open on hinges that needed oil, and Torvald noted that too, filed it away, an old habit from a life of couriers and secrets. The directorate's archive room smelled of dust and lamp oil and money; the ledgers sat in their cabinet like fat, well-fed hens, each spine stamped with the guild seal and a year.

Bram laid out the copying kit Hazel had prepared: thin paper, soft pencils, two sets of hands working opposite ends of the same page. They were not stealing the ledgers. Stealing them would alert Rask by morning, and morning was too soon. Instead they copied — dates, disbursements, the payments marked only as "salvage compensation" that flowed, year after year, to the same seven names, in the same months that ships broke on coasts that appeared safely distant on the charts the guild sold to every captain in Norway.

"Look at this," Bram whispered, pencil flying. "'Salvage compensation, Meridian, March '88.' My father's ship." His voice didn't waver, but his hand did, once. "They paid themselves for wrecking us."

"They paid everyone but the families," Torvald said quietly, and kept copying.

Above them, faintly, came the drone of Rask's voice through the floorboards — *the charter, gentlemen, is the bedrock of our civilization* — and the absurdity of it nearly made Torvald laugh, which would have been fatal, so he converted the laugh into speed.

Twenty minutes. Twenty-five. Bram's stack of copied pages grew. Thirty.

Then boots in the corridor.

Torvald killed the lantern with his palm. They pressed into the shadow behind the cabinet as the door opened and lamplight spilled in — one guard, young, yawning, come to check on the disturbance below. He glanced around the archive, saw nothing but furniture and dust, muttered something about stoves and clerks, and left. The door closed. Neither of them breathed for a full minute afterward.

"Forty minutes," Bram whispered. "Voss's window closes soon."

"Five more pages."

"You said thirty-five was enough."

"Aldric taught me that maps should always have a little more coast than you strictly need." Torvald bent back over the ledger. "You never know when someone will need the extra."

* * *

Getting Torvald out of the building was the part Hazel would remember longest, though at the time it passed in a blur of borrowed livery, a laundry trolley, and Bram's unshakeable insistence that the fastest way past suspicion was to look annoyed about laundry.

She waited at the tide gate with the boat, hands white-knuckled on the tiller, watching the guildhall windows glow against the dark. When the gate finally opened and two figures emerged — one large and limping, one carrying a satchel of papers worth more than the whole harbor — she let out a breath she felt she'd been holding for four days.

"Well?" she demanded, as they piled in.

Torvald held up the satchel. "Seven names. Eleven years. Every wreck accounted for in their own handwriting." He was thinner than when they'd taken him, and there was a fresh bruise along his jaw, and his smile was the same. "And I brought you something else."

"What?"

"Rask's private appointment book. He keeps it in the strongroom safe — combination written on a card in the ledger drawer, because the man lectures others on security." He handed it over. "Tomorrow afternoon. An entry. Read it."

Hazel held the lantern close. The entry was short, in Rask's tidy, self-satisfied script:

*3 o'clock — H. Eriksen. Final terms re: atlas delivery.*

She looked up. Torvald's eyes gleamed in the lantern light, tired and fierce.

"He doesn't know you're here," Torvald said. "He still thinks you're coming to negotiate. Which means tomorrow at three o'clock, Director Halvard Rask is expecting a frightened apprentice with an atlas to trade—" the boat bumped free of the gate, and the current caught them, carrying them out toward open water and the dawn coming up gray over the islands "—and instead he's going to get you."

Hazel turned the boat toward home, toward the workshop, toward Signe and Bram and the finished atlas wrapped in oilcloth beneath her floorboards, every sheet corrected, every annotation true. Tomorrow the assembled guild would hear the reading of the charts. Tonight there was ink to check and testimony to rehearse and perhaps, if there was time, sleep.

"There's just one thing," Bram said, from the bow, in the tone of a man delivering news he'd been sitting on for an hour. "When we got out — I heard the guards talking. Voss has been summoned to appear before the review board. Tomorrow. As a witness. Rask's calling him to account for the prisoner's 'irregular treatment.'"

Silence on the water. Then Signe's voice, arriving with the last of the night: "So Voss either lies for Rask tomorrow—"

"—or stands up in front of the whole guild and tells the truth," Hazel finished softly. She thought of over-sweetened tea, of a man who wanted desperately to be on the right side of history, and who was now going to find out, in front of everyone, whether he meant it. "Then we'd better make certain, by three o'clock, that telling the truth is the easier thing to do."

She put her shoulder to the tiller, and the boat ran on into the graying dawn.
