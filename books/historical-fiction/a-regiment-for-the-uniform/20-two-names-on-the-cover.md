## 20. Two Names on the Cover

The printing house of Monsen & Daughters smelled of hot lead, lamp oil, and the particular sweetness of paper waiting to become something. Hazel had visited twice before — once to negotiate terms with old Monsen himself, once to smuggle Signe in through the alley door so she could see where her father's name would appear beside a wreck report that finally told the truth. But today was different. Today the atlas was not a draft, not a fugitive, not a bundle of sheets wrapped in oilcloth under Torvald's coat. Today it lay open on Monsen's largest table, its pages squared and true, ready for the press.

"Remarkable," Monsen said for perhaps the eleventh time. He was a broad man gone soft at the edges, with ink permanently shadowed into the creases of his knuckles, and he handled each sheet as though it were a sleeping child he might wake by breathing wrong. "The coastal survey alone — do you know what I thought when your man first brought me samples? I thought, this is guild work. This is official work. And then I looked closer."

"And?" Hazel asked, though she knew.

"And the corrections are *better*." He said it the way other men said *heretical*, with a kind of delighted horror. "The Skarvholm shoal marked half a mile east of where the guild chart puts it. The Meridian approach depths — corrected. The whole northern archipelago re-sounded." He shook his head. "Forty years I've printed charts, fru Eriksen. Forty years of printing lies because the guild paid well and asked no questions. My daughters will print something else."

Hazel's chest did the thing it had been doing lately whenever she thought about the future — a strange loosening, like a fist unclenching so gradually she hadn't noticed it had been closed. "Your daughters have good instincts."

"The middle one has *suspicious* instincts," Monsen said. "Best kind." He tapped the table beside the atlas, where a single sheet of heavy cream paper waited — the title page, still blank except for faint guide lines. "Now. The difficult part."

She had been dreading it for three days, which was absurd, because she had faced down Inspector Voss across a table while lying fluently about inventory pages. She had stood before the assembled guild with her hands steady while Director Rask's face went through expressions usually reserved for men watching their ships go down. She had slept — eventually — after the night they pulled Torvald from the strongroom, bruised and furious and so relieved to see them that he'd nearly forgotten to be gruff about it.

But a blank title page was different from all of those. A blank title page asked a question she had spent four years avoiding.

*Whose work is this?*

"I can leave you to it," Monsen said, reading her correctly, the way printers probably learned to read everyone. "Take whatever time you need. The press won't mind. It's very patient — it's been waiting on honest work for decades." He gathered his apron and withdrew toward the back rooms, where his daughters' voices argued amiably about kerning.

Hazel sat down alone at the great table.

The atlas breathed under the lamplight. That was how she thought of it now — not as an object but as a living thing she had carried through fire, literally, its edges smoke-scuffed where the workshop's burning had reached her satchel before Torvald dragged her clear. Three years of reference work had gone up in that fire: Aldric's tide tables, her sounding logs, the letters, everything. Only the atlas itself survived, because she had carried it home every night since the inspector's second visit, a habit that had seemed paranoid at the time and had turned out to be simply accurate.

She turned to the last completed sheet. In the margin, in Aldric's hand — or in her hand doing Aldric's hand so well that sometimes she dreamed in it — ran the final coded annotation she had deciphered on that long-ago night when the atlas first revealed itself as a map to its own vindication: *They will ask who drew the truth. Answer them.*

She picked up the pen.

Her hand hesitated above the cream paper, and in the pause she heard the workshop again: the scratch of Aldric's nib two desks away, the way he'd hum off-key when a coastline pleased him, his voice saying — not once but a hundred times, patient as tide — *"A map is a promise kept to strangers, Hazel. Sign nothing you wouldn't stake your life on."*

She had signed nothing for four years. Guild apprentices didn't sign; their work flowed into the master's name like tributaries into a river, invisible and uncomplaining. And after Aldric died — after the funeral with its wrong-smelling coffin and the directorate's smooth condolences — signing anything would have meant announcing herself to men who burned workshops and wrecked ships for insurance money. So she had worked in the dark, in the margins, in the space between one name and no name at all.

But Rask was gone. Taken at the harbor three days ago, trying to board a tramp freighter with two ledgers and a false passport, according to Bram, who had sources everywhere and enjoyed them shamelessly. The directorate was dissolved pending the charter review's findings, which were no longer really in doubt. Voss had testified — Voss, of all people, standing before the assembled guild with the gray look of a man amputating his own ambitions and finding, to his surprise, that he could still stand — and the sailors had testified, and Signe had stood up with the Meridian's wreck report in both hands and read the falsified soundings aloud in a voice that did not shake until the names of the dead, and then shook, and continued anyway.

There was no one left to hide from.

Except, Hazel thought, setting the pen down again, herself.

* *

"You're stalling."

Torvald leaned in the doorway of the printing house, arms crossed, still favoring his left shoulder where the strongroom door had caught him. He'd refused the doctor's salve for two days out of pure stubbornness and had finally submitted that morning when Signe threatened to apply it *for* him, at speed.

"I am contemplating," Hazel said.

"For three days?"

"Monsen only finished setting the plates yesterday."

"Mm." Torvald came into the room, moving around the great table with the careful tread of a man who had learned to respect other people's instruments. He stopped opposite her and looked down at the blank title page for a long moment. Then: "I've seen you chart a reef at midnight in a gale. I've seen you lie to Voss's face with better craftsmanship than anything in this building. And this—" he gestured at the empty cream rectangle "—is what defeats you."

"It's not the writing." Hazel turned the pen in her fingers. "It's deciding what goes on the page. One name or two."

Torvald was quiet. Outside, the harbor sounds filtered in — gulls, rigging, someone swearing cheerfully at a crate. When he spoke again, his voice had lost its teasing edge. "Aldric sent me to find 'the one who finishes it.' Those were his words. Not 'my apprentice.' Not 'the girl in my workshop.' The one who *finishes* it." He met her eyes. "He knew, Hazel. Before the end, he knew whose atlas this was going to be. He just didn't live long enough to watch you believe it."

"He staged his own death," Hazel said. "He wasn't always right about things."

"No," Torvald agreed. "But he was right about you."

She looked down at the title page. The guide lines waited, patient as Monsen's press.

"When I started," she said, "the plan was simple. Finish it quietly, put his name on it, and let the truth do its work. His name carries weight. His name opens doors. Mine opens—" she gestured vaguely at herself— "apprenticeships."

"And now?"

"Now I keep hearing him." She hadn't meant to say it aloud, and saying it made her throat tight, so she pressed on before the tightness could win. "Every margin note, every correction — his voice. For four years I've written in his hand because his hand was safe, because his hand was *dead*, and dead men don't get their apprentices arrested. And some part of me thinks the decent thing, the humble thing, is to let the cover carry only him. Let him have the whole credit. He paid for it."

Torvald pulled out a stool and sat, heavily, wincing at his shoulder. "May I tell you something about staging a death?"

"That seems on brand, yes."

"It's very easy to arrange the dying." His mouth twisted. "What's hard is everything after. You spend months being nobody. Watching your own funeral from the back of a church — I attended, incidentally, in case you wondered. Aldric insisted. Said if we were going to commit fraud against the directorate we should commit it *thoroughly*. There were lilies. Terrible ones."

Despite everything, Hazel laughed — a short, wet-edged sound. "He hated lilies."

"He said they smelled like funerals pretending to be gardens." Torvald's smile faded into something older and more careful. "Here is what I learned, being nobody for eight months. A name isn't credit. Credit is a ledger thing — debits and honors, who deserves what. A name is a *promise*. It's what strangers trust when they trust a map with their ship. Aldric gave forty years of his name to keeping promises. And when he couldn't anymore, when they'd have taken his name and used it to sign their lies — he handed it to you. Not to bury it in. To *keep* it working."

Hazel looked at the atlas between them.

"So put both names," Torvald said simply. "Not because you earned half. Because the promise needs two keepers now — the one who drew it, and the one who carried it through the fire. He'd say exactly that, if he were here."

"He'd also complain about the cost of engraving."

"*Immediately.*"

* *

Monsen returned an hour later to find Hazel still seated at the table, but the pen was moving now, and he had the good grace — or the good training — to stand back and simply watch.

She wrote Aldric Eriksen first. Her own hand, not his — that had been her decision, made somewhere in the last hour without quite noticing it. His name deserved to rest in letterforms that weren't a disguise. She wrote it large and level, the way it would appear on a spine, the way sailors would run their thumbs over it in chandlers' shops for the next fifty years.

Then, beneath it, smaller but not small — not apologetic, not tucked away, but set with the same deliberate pressure as the first:

*completed by Hazel Eriksen.*

No *assistant*. No *under the direction of*. No ellipsis of humility trailing off the edge of the page. Just the fact of it, stated plainly, in her own hand, which was — she realized as she watched the ink dry — not so different from his after all. Four years of imitation had left its sediment. She had built herself out of his teaching, and there was no dishonesty in that; rivers don't apologize for their beds.

"There," she said, and her voice cracked slightly on the single syllable, and she let it.

Monsen leaned over, read it, and nodded once, slowly, like a man confirming a sounding. "Both names."

"Both names."

"My daughters will want to know why you're crying."

"Tell them it's the lamp oil."

"Ah." He straightened gravely. "Yes. It gets in everything." But his eyes, above the beard, were kind, and when he lifted the title page with both hands to carry it to the composing room, he carried it the way Torvald had once carried the atlas out of a burning building — like something that mattered more than its weight.

Hazel stayed a moment longer, alone with the open atlas. She found the final sheet and, in the bottom margin, beneath Aldric's coded instruction — *They will ask who drew the truth. Answer them* — she added one last line, uncoded, in plain Norwegian any stranger could read:

*Answered.*

Then she closed the cover, both names facing outward, and went to find her family. Signe and Bram would be arguing about survey routes in the tavern across the street — they'd been arguing productively for three days, which Signe claimed was how all good expeditions began — and Torvald was already halfway to the door, and spring was coming, and there were honest charts to make.

On the table behind her, the press waited, patient at last, for work worth printing.
