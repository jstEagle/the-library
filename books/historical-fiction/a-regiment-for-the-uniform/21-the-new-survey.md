## 21. The New Survey

The letter arrived on a Tuesday, which Signe considered fitting, because Tuesdays were the day her father had always said nothing good ever happened. He'd said it every week of his life, cheerfully, the way other men complained about weather, and then he'd died on a Wednesday aboard the *Meridian*, proving that even his pessimism had been optimistic.

So when Bram came pounding up the workshop stairs with an envelope bearing the reformed guild's seal — the new seal, the one with the lighthouse instead of the compass rose, because the directorate's old rose had come to mean something else entirely to anyone who'd lost someone at sea — Signe braced herself.

"It's from the guild," Bram said, breathless. He was holding it the way he held everything important: too tightly, with ink-stained fingers.

"I can see that."

"Aren't you going to open it?"

"I'm going to finish my tea first," Signe said, "because whatever it says, I want to be sitting down and caffeinated for it."

She was, in truth, more frightened than she let on. The charter review had been four months ago. Rask had fled and been taken at the harbor trying to board a ship under a name so obviously false — *Herr Strand* — that the harbormaster later admitted he'd have arrested him on principle alone. The directorate had been dissolved in a session that ran eleven hours and ended with three former directors escorted out past a crowd of widows who did not throw anything but whose silence, Torvald said afterward, was worse than stones.

Since then: quiet. Too much quiet. The provisional council had thanked them, commended them, promised reforms, and gone silent as a flooded hold. Signe knew how institutions worked. She'd grown up around a father who trusted paperwork the way sailors trust weather glass — useful right up until it lies.

She opened the letter.

Read it once. Read it twice. Set it down on the table between the teacups and the drying charts, where the morning light from the rebuilt east window fell across it.

"Well?" Bram demanded.

"They're commissioning us," Signe said. Her voice came out strange, so she tried again. "Us. The workshop. A full coastal survey — Skarvholm to the southern lights, every chart redrawn from new soundings. Ours."

Bram made a sound like a kettle achieving sainthood.

* * *

By noon, everyone had gathered in the main room, which still smelled faintly of new timber because half of it *was* new timber. The fire damage had been worse than Hazel had pretended at first; she'd stood in the ruins for a full hour before saying a single word, and what she'd finally said was, "We'll need more shelving," which was how Signe had learned that grief and practicality could live in the same sentence.

Now the shelves were full again, mostly. There was one empty case by the window that no one used, and no one discussed, though Signe had noticed that Hazel dusted it every morning with the particular care one gives a grave.

Hazel read the letter aloud, standing, because she said commissions should be read standing, like verdicts. Torvald sat by the stove with his bad leg stretched out — it had never quite recovered from the strongroom, and probably never would, and he'd stopped apologizing for it around midwinter, which everyone agreed was progress.

"'...in recognition of the corrected atlas and the testimony rendered before the assembled guild,'" Hazel read, "'the provisional council hereby engages Eriksen & Kavanagh—'"

She stopped.

"That's not our name," Bram said. Then he blinked. "Is that our name?"

"It would appear," Hazel said carefully, "that they've registered the workshop under Aldric's surname and mine." Her voice did something very small on the second word.

Torvald smiled into his pipe smoke. "I told you they would. Institutions love nothing so much as a tidy ledger. Two names, one firm — it balances beautifully."

"You could have told me *before* I read my own mail aloud," Hazel said, but there was no edge in it. She cleared her throat and continued. "'The survey is to begin with the spring thaw, full provision granted, and the resulting charts to bear the surveyors' names — all surveyors' names — on every plate.'"

She lowered the letter. For a moment nobody spoke. Outside, the harbor ice was breaking up in long groaning sheets, the way it did every year, the sea shrugging off winter like a man removing a coat he never liked.

"Every plate," Signe repeated. Her father had drawn charts for thirty years, and his name appeared on exactly none of them. The guild took the work and gave the credit upward, the way a tide takes sand. She hadn't known to be angry about it until recently; now the anger had somewhere to go, and this — this letter, this absurd, bureaucratic, magnificent letter — was where.

"All right," Hazel said, rolling up her sleeves, which was how everyone knew the meeting had started regardless of what anyone had said. "Provisioning lists. Bram, ink and paper, double what we think we need, because you'll spill half of it. Torvald—"

"Boats," Torvald said. "I know. And a crew that can keep their mouths shut and their stomachs settled. Leave the harbor to me."

"Signe—" Hazel began.

"Soundings tables, wreck reports, and the discrepancy log," Signe said. "Already started. I started last night, actually. Possibly the night before that."

Hazel looked at her for a moment. "You know," she said, "for most of my life I thought I was the only person who worked like that. It's been deeply educational having all of you."

* * *

They provisioned through March, and if you had asked Signe — and Bram did ask her, repeatedly, at increasingly inconvenient hours — she would have said the strangest part wasn't the work. It was the ease.

The old guild had moved like a body guarding an injury: slow, suspicious, everything routed through three locks and two signatures and Director Rask's personal seal. The provisional council, chastened and terrified of its own history, approved things in days. When Bram requested forty sheets of the good rag paper, the requisition came back signed within a week with a note reading *Please take sixty, Mr. Vik, we would rather overstock than apologize again.*

"We would rather overstock than apologize again," Bram read out loud, delighted. "Signe. Signe. An institution apologized to me. In writing. Frame it."

"I'm not framing your mail."

"I'm framing my mail."

He framed it. It hung above his desk between a tide table and a drawing Signe had done of him asleep face-down in a proof sheet, captioned *Accurate Chart of the Surveyor at Rest*. He claimed not to mind. He minded slightly. This was also, Signe reflected, what family was: people whose walls you were allowed to deface.

Torvald found them a boat — a broad-beamed ketch called the *Søster*, which the fisherman selling her swore was named after his sister and which Torvald privately suspected was named after a curse word in some older dialect, given the vessel's temperament. She leaked a little at the garboard seam and handled like a dignitary being asked to hurry. Torvald loved her instantly.

"She reminds me of someone," he said, patting her rail.

"Who?" Bram asked.

"Me, thirty years ago."

Hazel spent the winter evenings at the drafting table doing something she refused to explain, and Signe, who shared the late shift with her more often than not, eventually worked up the courage to look. What she saw made her stop in the doorway.

Hazel was redrawing the marginalia. Aldric's code — the little constellation of marks and abbreviations they'd spent weeks deciphering, the code that had led them to Skarvholm and the plates and everything after — translated into plain notation, set down clean, with a key.

"You're teaching it," Signe said.

"I'm standardizing it," Hazel corrected, without looking up. "Teaching implies students. Students imply risk." But her pen didn't pause, and the pages kept accumulating, and by the time the ice went out of the harbor there was a slim bound manual on the shelf — unmarked, unindexed, filed under nothing — that any apprentice in the rebuilt workshop could learn from, if Hazel judged them ready.

Signe never asked if she was in it. Some invitations you wait to receive in handwriting.

* * *

The *Søster* put out on the first clear morning of April, with a wind from the southwest and the whole coast lying ahead of them like a question that had waited too long for its answer.

Their first station was the shoal off Vardnes Point — the one that wasn't on any official chart, the one the falsified plates had shown as safe water, the lie that had broken the hulls of two ships Signe could name and, the records suggested, several she couldn't. They anchored, dropped the lead line, and waited for the reading.

Signe swung the lead herself. She wanted to feel the weight go down and the line pay out and stop — *stop* — at fourteen fathoms where the chart promised twenty-two. She wanted to feel it in her hands, the lie made physical, the way you can only feel a thing when you're holding the rope yourself.

"Fourteen," she called. Her voice cracked on it, and she let it.

Nobody made it gentle. That was another thing about this crew. Bram wrote the number down without ceremony. Torvald marked the position. Hazel began the correction on the working draft immediately, her pen steady, and when she finished the sounding mark she added — small, in the margin, in the old code — a single character that Signe had learned to read back in the days of candlelight and decipherment.

*Here. Truthfully.*

They sounded it twice more to confirm. Fourteen fathoms, both times, the sea keeping honest books now that someone had finally asked it properly.

By evening they had three stations done, and Bram had spilled ink on exactly one chart, and Torvald had cooked a fish stew aboard the *Søster* that Hazel pronounced structurally unsound but ate two bowls of anyway. They moored in the lee of Vardnes Point, and Signe took the first watch because she liked the night watch best — the water black and patient, the stars doing their ancient, indifferent bookkeeping overhead.

Around midnight, footsteps on the deck. Hazel, wrapped in a coat clearly borrowed from Torvald, since it reached her knees and smelled of pipe smoke. She settled against the rail beside Signe and handed her a tin cup of coffee that was terrible in the specific way shipboard coffee achieves only through genuine effort.

"My father sailed these waters," Signe said, after a while. Not because it needed saying, but because it was true, and true things deserve saying sometimes just for the pleasure of it.

"He drew the old charts of them too, didn't he? Before the directorate." Hazel said it quietly. "I found his initials in three of the reference sheets we salvaged. Neat hand. Very neat."

"He was proud of that hand. Said a chart was a promise you make to strangers." Signe turned the phrase over, tasting it. "You say that. In the workshop. To the apprentices."

"I stole it," Hazel admitted. "From a dead mapmaker's daughter, effectively. Everything worth keeping gets stolen from somebody."

The tide shifted below them, unhurried. Somewhere out in the dark, the new lighthouse at Skarvholm swept its beam across the water — the light they'd argued the council into building, on the rock where the alarm had tripped and blood had been spilled, on the island where the true plates had waited all those years for someone to come collect the truth.

"Fourteen fathoms," Signe said. "It'll be in print by autumn. Every pilot will have it. Every captain's daughter will grow up with a chart that doesn't lie to her."

"Yes," Hazel said.

"And Dad's initials are in the salvaged sheets, so some of his lines survive in the corrections."

"Some do."

Signe nodded, and drank the terrible coffee, and watched the light sweep and return, sweep and return. It occurred to her that hope, the way she'd understood it growing up, had always seemed like a wish — something you folded into a letter and sent toward the horizon and tried not to expect back. But this was different. This was a plan with provisions. This was a lead line that stopped where it stopped. Hope as a working document, subject to revision, initialled by everyone who'd earned a place on the plate.

* * *

They were three weeks into the survey when the *Søster* came about off Hjelmenesset and the lookout — Bram, perched in the shrouds because Torvald refused to climb rigging anymore on principle — shouted down that there was a boat ahead, anchored where no boat should anchor, right on the line of their next station.

They closed the distance under shortened sail. The stranger was a small, well-kept sloop, riding easy, flying no colors at all, which on this coast meant either smugglers or officials, and the distinction had narrowed considerably in recent years.

A figure stood at her rail. As they drew near, Signe saw it was a woman, gray-haired, weathered, with the stillness of someone who has spent a great deal of time waiting for things to arrive. She raised one hand — not waving, exactly. More like hailing a witness.

On her stern, half worn away by salt and sun, Signe made out a name: *Meridian*.

Her hands went cold on the tiller.

"Hazel," she said, and her voice carried strangely across the water, flat as a sounding that stops too soon. "That's the wreck report number. That's — that's impossible. The *Meridian* broke on the rocks off Skarvholm eight years ago. I've read the report. I've *matched the plates against it.*"

"I know," Hazel said quietly, already reaching for the spyglass.

Through the lens, Signe watched the gray-haired woman watch them, patient as stone, waiting for them to come alongside. Waiting, Signe realized, the way a person waits when she has been carrying something for eight years and has finally decided today is the day she sets it down.

And stitched on the breast of the woman's coat, in faded thread, was a guild crest — the old one, the compass rose — torn cleanly through the center, as if by hand.

"Come about gently," Torvald murmured, taking the wheel. "Whatever she is, she's been waiting for us longer than we've been looking for her."
