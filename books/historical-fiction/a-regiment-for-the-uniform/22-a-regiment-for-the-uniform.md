## 22. A Regiment for the Uniform

The morning lesson began, as all good lessons did, with a lie.

"Maps are honest things," Hazel told the four apprentices arranged around the long table, their pencils sharpened to needle points, their faces wearing that particular expression of young people who have been told something is important and are trying very hard not to look bored. "A map is the most honest document a person can make. Every line on it is a promise: *here* is where the rock waits beneath the water. *Here* is where the channel deepens. *Here*, if you trust me, you will live."

She let the silence sit for exactly as long as Aldric used to let it sit — three heartbeats, no more — and then she spoiled it, because she was not Aldric and had never pretended to be.

"And every one of you will be tempted to break those promises at least once, because breaking them is easier, faster, and someone will pay you handsomely for it. Signe's father's ship broke on a chart like that. So when I say honest, I mean it in the way I mean most things: as an instruction, not a compliment."

Young Petter, who was fourteen and had the unshakable conviction of boys his age that rules existed for other people, raised his hand halfway. "Master Eriksen, what happens if you get it wrong by accident?"

"Aha." Hazel pulled the atlas from its shelf.

It sat at the end of the reference row now, between the tide tables and the old harbor ledgers, and anyone unfamiliar with the workshop might take it for just another worn volume — which was, in a way, the finest thing that could be said about it. Its spine had gone soft from use. The corners were rounded like sea stones. The gilt lettering had faded until only *A REGIMENT FOR THE UNIFORM* remained legible, and even that required a certain charitable squinting. Two names were stamped beneath it, small and plain, because Hazel had insisted on plainness: **ALDRIC VOLLAN** and, beside it, in letters no larger, **HAZEL ERIKSEN**.

She set it on the table before Petter with the reverence of a priest handling relics and the practicality of a woman who had spilled tea on it twice.

"Open to sheet forty," she said. "The Skarvholm approach."

Petter opened it. The others leaned in. On the page, the island archive rose out of carefully stippled water, and along the margin ran a thin thread of notation that looked, to an untrained eye, like idle decoration — little loops and dashes, a bird here, a wave there, drawn as though Aldric had been doodling while he thought.

"Now," Hazel said. "Tell me what the margins say."

"They say nothing," said Petter. "They're just — drawings. Pretty ones."

"Signe taught me this code two years ago and I still think they're pretty," admitted the second apprentice, a serious girl named Maren whose father fished the outer banks. "But they're not *just* anything. Look at the third loop from the left. It crosses the border line. Anything crossing the border line is a measurement correction."

"And the little wave with the notch in it?" Hazel prompted.

"Depth discrepancy," Maren recited. "Notch pointing seaward means the published chart is wrong by more than a fathom. Notch pointing landward means less."

"Very good. And the bird?"

Nobody answered. Hazel waited. This was tradition now; the bird was always last, and the answer was always the same, and she never tired of hearing it, because the answer had been given to her once by a dying man's own hand and she had spent a decade making sure it would outlive them all.

"The bird means," said Bram's daughter Tove, who was twelve and frighteningly quick, "*someone died here.*"

"*Someone died here,*" Hazel confirmed quietly. "And it means more than that. It means: *we checked. We measured again. We made sure no one else dies here. And we wrote it down so that strangers we will never meet can pass safely in the dark.*" She tapped the little bird with one ink-stained finger. "Your master Aldric invented this code under threat of his life. Your masters Torvald and Signe carried it across black rocks in a storm while men shot at them. I finished a book with it while my whole world burned down around my ears. So when you draw your margins, you are not decorating. You are keeping a promise to people you will never meet. That is the regiment. That is the uniform. We wear it on paper."

Petter looked at the atlas with new eyes, which was the entire point of mornings like this.

"Did it really burn?" he asked. "The workshop? The first one?"

"It really burned," said Torvald's voice from the doorway.

He stood there with a tray of coffee cups balanced on one arm and a loaf of bread under the other, silver at his temples now and a limp that appeared only in wet weather, looking — as he had looked for twenty-odd years — like a man perpetually arriving off the ferry with something urgent in his pockets.

"The guild called it an accident," he went on, setting the tray down amid the charts. "Rask called it regrettable. The insurance assessor called it suspicious, and then mysteriously lost his notes. I call it the reason we rebuilt this place with stone walls and iron shutters and a lock Signe designed herself." He poured coffee with the ease of a man who had done ten thousand small kindnesses in this room. "Also the reason we keep duplicates of everything in three locations, which you lot complain about constantly and will thank us for someday."

"We thank you for it now," Maren said loyally.

"You thank me for the bread," Torvald said. "Eat."

Hazel watched them fall upon the loaf with the appetite of the young and felt the particular warmth that had, over the years, replaced the colder thing she'd carried in her chest after Aldric's funeral. Grief didn't leave, exactly. It settled. It became load-bearing, the way ballast keeps a ship upright — heavy, but useful, and hers.

***

At midday the ferry came in, and with it, mail.

Hazel walked down to the tide gate herself, as she did whenever the weather allowed, because the walk was good for her knees and better for her thinking, and because the harbor still gave her a small quiet satisfaction every time she saw the new beacon chart nailed up inside the pilot house — *their* chart, corrected, honest, printed in the workshop's own press.

The mail held the usual: a supply invoice, a letter from the southern guild requesting permission to adopt the margin-code officially (her third such request that year; the code had spread the way good ideas do, quietly and by theft), and a fat envelope postmarked from a fishing village she didn't recognize, containing a child's drawing of a coastline and a note in a schoolteacher's careful hand:

*Dear Master Eriksen — My students have been studying your atlas. Young Halvard (no relation, he insists) has drawn our harbor as he believes a mapmaker would see it. Would you tell him what is right and what is wrong? He asks whether the lady who wrote the book ever makes mistakes.*

Hazel laughed aloud on the dock, startling a gull.

She folded the drawing into her coat to bring home. There was a mistake in it, of course — the boy had put the lighthouse on the wrong headland — but there was also a sound instinct for where the shoal lay, an eye for the way the coast curved, and she intended to write back and tell him so, and to tell him about the lighthouse too, gently, because corrections given kindly were how you built a cartographer instead of merely training one.

On the walk back she passed the spot where the old workshop had stood. The charred foundation had long since become a low garden wall; Signe grew beans against it every summer and claimed, with a straight face, that they tasted of vindication.

***

The afternoon brought Signe back from the survey boat, salt-crusted and triumphant, waving a sheaf of field measurements.

"The Sørhavn channel," she announced, dropping into a chair across from Hazel's drafting desk. "Re-sounded it ourselves, all six miles. The new plate matches the atlas within inches. Inches, Hazel. Twenty years of tides and silting and it hasn't shifted enough to matter."

"Of course it hasn't. We measured it properly the first time."

"I know. I wanted to hear myself say it." Signe grinned, and for a moment she was nineteen again, running errands through a burning city with contraband plates sewn into her coat lining. Then the grin softened into something older. "I found another bird today. In the old wreck report file. The *Meridian*. I hadn't noticed Aldric marked it in his working notes. He knew. Before any of us, before Torvald, he knew what Rask had done to my father's ship, and he marked it, and he kept going anyway."

Hazel set down her pen.

"He kept going because of people like your father," she said. "That was the whole engine of the man. You knew that."

"I know it." Signe looked out the window toward the water. "I just like saying it out loud sometimes. That he knew. That it mattered to somebody before it mattered to a court."

"It mattered to you. You made it matter to everyone else." Hazel nudged the bread plate toward her. "Eat. You've been sounding channels since dawn; you're no good to me hollow."

This was how the evenings went, mostly, and had gone for years: the four of them orbiting the same rooms, arguing about bearings and bread, teaching children who would one day teach children of their own. Bram came by Thursdays with ledger copies from the reformed guild's archives, where he had risen, to everyone's amusement including his own, to the position of Chief Archivist — the man who had once proposed robbing the directorate's private residence now legally controlled its paperwork, and enjoyed every minute of it with the serene menace of a librarian holding overdue notices over former enemies.

Torvald had been offered reinstatement to the courier service three times. He had declined three times, each with a different polite excuse, though they all knew the true one: he liked being home. He had spent enough of his life carrying other people's secrets between towns. These days his secrets stayed in one kitchen, where they could be fed.

As for Hazel — she had been offered honors, titles, a chair at the southern academy, a street named after her in a town she'd visited once. She had accepted none of them. But every spring, when the new apprentices arrived and stood awkwardly around the long table waiting to see what sort of teacher the famous Hazel Eriksen would be, she caught sight of her reflection in the darkened window — a gray-haired woman with ink permanently under her nails — and thought: *you turned out to be someone after all. Not the someone you planned. Better.*

***

That evening, late, when the apprentices had gone home and Signe and Torvald had retired, Hazel took the atlas down from its shelf one last time before bed.

She did this perhaps once a month. It was not sentiment, she told herself; it was quality control. A living document deserved regular inspection.

She turned to the final sheet — the one she had completed alone, in the days after Torvald's capture, with Voss's borrowed lantern oil and her own shaking hands. Aldric's voice lived in every annotation there, because she had written it *in* his voice, deliberately, borrowing his phrasings the way you borrow a dead friend's coat. But near the bottom margin, in the smallest possible script, there was one line that was entirely hers:

*A regiment needs no uniforms. Only a standard, and people who refuse to lower it.*

Below it, both names. His first, always his first. Hers beside it, equal in size, unequal in nothing that mattered.

She traced the worn spine once with her thumb — the groove her own hands had made over twenty years of taking it down and putting it back — and shelved it gently between the tide tables and the ledgers, where tomorrow morning four young faces would gather around it, and one of them, probably Petter, would ask a question with the wrong assumption buried in it, and she would get to correct him, kindly, and begin again.

Outside, the harbor light swept its slow white arm across the water, steady on schedule, faithful to its chart.

Hazel banked the stove, trimmed tomorrow's pencils, and left the workshop door unlocked — as it always was, as it would always be — because a promise kept to strangers begins with leaving the door open.

In the morning: new charts, new questions, new hands to stain with ink.

The regiment mustered at dawn.
