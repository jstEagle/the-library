## 1. The Weight of Lead

The type told her everything she needed to know about her hands.

Isandro held the punch up to the window light and turned it slowly, the way her father had taught her before her voice changed, before Tomás drowned and the world contracted to a single necessary lie. The steel caught the morning gray and threw it back in a thin bright line. Clean shoulders. A crisp bevel at the counter. No burr where the graver had skipped.

Good. Her hands still remembered how to be a man's hands, even if the rest of her had to be reminded every hour of every day.

She set the punch down on the cutting bench and flexed her fingers, feeling the ache that lived now permanently in the joints — a printer's ache, a carver's ache, an ache she had chosen over the other ache, the one that came with skirts and curfews and the particular way soldiers looked through women as though they were furniture with opinions. Across the shop, in the gloom behind the composing stone, her father coughed twice, low and controlled, the cough of a man managing his body like a difficult customer.

"Papa," she called, using the flat register she'd built for herself out of Tomás's old voice, pitched just below where hers wanted to sit. "The twelve-point Caslon is done. Where do you want it?"

"Case four," he said. "And then the Delacroix order. They want five hundred handbills by Thursday, and Thursday is not going to wait for us to be sentimental about steel."

Nothing waited for them to be sentimental. That was the first law of the shop, older than any of its laws about ink or measurement: the work does not care what you are carrying. She opened case four and began sorting the new sorts into their compartments, each letter going home with the small iron click that was, after six years, as familiar to her as her own heartbeat. E's in their hundreds. T's worn nearly round from use. An asterisk nobody in this town had ever needed until the war made asterisks of everyone.

Outside, the street was doing what the street always did at this hour — the baker's boy dragging his cart, two women arguing cheerfully about the price of lard, the clock above the mairie holding its hands at ten past seven because the man who wound it had been taken for road labor in October and nobody else could reach the mechanism. But underneath the ordinary sounds there was the other sound, the one that had been growing all winter like damp under wallpaper: boots. Not marching yet. Just more of them than there used to be, passing through on the east road, and carts with canvas hoods, and once, three weeks ago, a staff car that slowed as it went by the print shop's windows, so slowly that Isandro had stopped breathing entirely until it was gone.

Her father said the armies would pass around them, the way water passes around a stone. He said the town was too small to matter and too far from anything worth taking.

He also said, in the same breath, that paper remembers what men prefer to forget, and that a printer who believed his own reassurances deserved the poverty he got. So Isandro did not believe him about the stone and the water. She believed him about the paper.

* * *

At noon she locked the shop, walked the eleven minutes to the cemetery, and stood at her brother's grave the way she did every day, hat brim low, collar high, hands clasped behind her back like a young man bored with waiting.

Tomás Underhill. Beloved son. 1899–1913.

Fourteen years old when the river took him. Fourteen years old forever, while his sister grew into his clothes, then past them, then had to let out the seams herself by candlelight with a borrowed awl, learning tailoring the way she'd learned everything since — alone, badly at first, adequately by necessity.

"I'm keeping the letters straight," she told the headstone. It was what she always said. Some days it felt like prayer. Some days it felt like the only true sentence in her mouth.

A woman from the tannery passed along the path and gave her a nod — *Tomás, good lad, steady worker, his poor father* — and Isandro nodded back with Tomás's small closed-mouth smile, which she had practiced against a mirror until it no longer frightened her. The tannery woman's son had been requisitioned in November. Everyone's son had been requisitioned in November, or December, or would be in January; the army had discovered that the border towns were full of boys who were only boys on paper, and paper was something the Underhill family understood better than anyone.

Which was why, walking home with her collar up and her heart doing its quiet criminal work, she took the long route. Past the mill. Past the wall where the new notice had gone up two days ago, printed by the garrison press in a typeface so crude it offended her professionally: **ALL PERSONS TRAVELING EAST OF THE RIVER MUST PRESENT VALID PERMITS AT CHECKPOINTS. PERMITS ISSUED ONLY BY MILITARY AUTHORITY.**

She had read it forty times. She had read it the way a locksmith reads a lock — looking for the flaw, the tolerance, the place where the mechanism forgives you.

The permits were stamped in violet ink. The stamps were cut by a man named Ostermann in the garrison office, and Ostermann was lazy about his serifs, and lazy men are gifts to the desperate. Isandro had already begun, in the hours after midnight when her father slept, cutting a facsimile of the seal into a blank sort of soft lead. Three nights' work. Two nights remained before she would trust it against a real permit, if she could ever get close enough to a real permit to compare them.

She hadn't told her father. There were things a father should not have to know, and one of them was that his surviving child spent her nights committing crimes with his own gravers, at his own bench, under his own roof.

* * *

The afternoon light came into the shop at an angle that turned the dust gold, and for an hour the work was only work, and the dread receded to its usual depth beneath the floorboards of her attention. She composed the Delacroix handbills — *HORSES BOUGHT AND SOLD, FAIR PRICES, DELACROIX & SON, BY THE MILL BRIDGE* — and locked the chase, and inked the proof, and pulled it, and the smell of the ink rose up around her like a language she was fluent in, and for sixty seconds she was simply a printer, whole and unremarkable, and those seconds were the closest thing to sanctuary the world offered.

"You're pulling too hard," her father said from the stone. "Let the press do the pressing. You fight it like it insulted you."

"The press likes me."

"The press tolerates you. Like everyone." But there was warmth under it, the dry warmth he rationed like sugar. He came to stand beside her, and she saw — the way she had learned to see without being seen to look — that his knuckles were swollen, and that he leaned on the stone a half-second longer than steadiness required. "Delacroix will pay late. He always pays late. Record it anyway."

"I always record it."

"That," he said, "is the only reason we are not beggars."

It was almost true, and they both knew the shape of the truth underneath it, and neither of them touched it, because touching it would mean discussing the ledger, and discussing the ledger was not yet possible. The ledger sat on its shelf behind the counter — a thick book, bound in green calf, ruled in columns her grandfather had drawn himself — and in it, in her father's upright hand and lately in her own careful imitation of his hand, was recorded everything the shop had ever done. Every job. Every payment. Every favor traded, every debt carried, every name.

Every name.

She had thought about that ledger more than she let herself think about it, the way you think about a tooth that aches only when you bite down. Because the favors column had grown strange these last months. *Permit consultation, no charge.* *Document repair, trade against flour.* *Night work, settled privately.* Entries written to look like nothing, entries that were in fact a complete confession if anyone ever read them with the right eyes — a record of every neighbor she had helped move east of the river, every forged signature, every violet-inked lie, all of it itemized in her dead brother's name in her father's sacred book of account.

Paper remembers what men prefer to forget. Her father's own law, coming home to roost.

"Tomás." Her father's voice pulled her back. He was looking at her with that expression she'd catalogued years ago — the one that meant he was seeing her and refusing to say so, the arrangement they maintained like a load-bearing wall between them. "You've been staring at the ledger shelf for a full minute."

"Counting what we're owed."

"We're owed nothing anyone will pay." He turned back to the press, and then, quietly, without turning around: "There was a boy at the door this morning. While you were at the cemetery. Dark-haired. Stood there counting the soldiers on the east road like he was tallying a harvest."

"Ferren," she said.

"If he comes inside again, give him bread. Not information."

"He doesn't want information. He wants to watch."

"Then let him watch from the doorway." Her father picked up his apron, and paused, and she heard the next sentence arrive in him before he spoke it, the way she heard weather arrive. "Mateo will come by tonight. He wants extra hours. I told him yes."

"Mateo always wants extra hours."

"Yes," her father said mildly, tying the apron. "Well. So do you."

He said it without inflection, and walked away, and left her standing at the stone with heat climbing her neck like a fever she couldn't treat. Mateo. Who had apprenticed here two years and never once asked why Tomás's voice cracked downward instead of up. Who had hands stained permanently black to the second knuckle and a way of standing at the composing stone that made the room feel differently occupied. Whom she loved in the specific, unspeakable way of someone who cannot afford to love anything, because everything loved becomes evidence.

* * *

Evening came down over the town like a shutter closing, and with it came the boots again, more of them, a whole column this time on the east road, and the sound of them carried into the shop through the propped door and made the type in the cases shiver faintly in their compartments — or she imagined it did; she could not prove it did; but she pressed her palm flat against case nine and felt the letters lying there in their ranks like sleeping conscripts, and thought: *not you. Not yet.*

Ferren appeared in the doorway at dusk, exactly as predicted, a thin shadow with a thin face and eyes too old for the rest of him. He didn't ask for bread. He stood with his hands in his pockets and watched the east road through the glass, counting under his breath, and when the column had passed he said, in the toneless voice of a child reporting facts:

"Ninety-two today. Yesterday was sixty. The day before, forty."

"Someone should tell you to stop counting."

"Someone should listen." He looked at her then, directly, with that flat assessing stare, and something moved behind it — curiosity, or appetite, or the beginning of knowledge. "Your father says give me bread. I don't want bread."

"What do you want?"

He shrugged, and turned back to the road, and stayed another ten minutes in silence while she worked, and the silence between them had the quality of two people guarding opposite sides of the same wall.

After he left she banked the stove, checked the bolt on the door twice, and climbed to the loft where the type cases slept in their long shelves, and took down the soft lead blank she had been cutting for three nights, and set it beside the real permit she had finally obtained — bought from a deserter's widow for two days' flour — and held the two of them together under the candle.

Violet ink. Ostermann's lazy serifs. Her copy, side by side with the original.

They matched. Almost perfectly. Almost well enough.

She sat back on her heels in the dark of the loft, listening to her father's controlled coughing below, and to the distant rhythm of the east road, and understood with complete clarity that she had crossed some line whose location she had never actually seen. Tomorrow she would test the seal properly. Tomorrow the shop would take on its third life — print shop by day, forge by night, sanctuary and crime scene occupying the same square meters of floor.

Below, the front bell chimed. Footsteps on the stairs. A knock, soft and familiar.

"Tomás?" Mateo's voice, muffled through the boards. "Your father said you'd still be up. Can I show you something?"

Isandro blew out the candle, slid the false seal into the hollow space behind case seventeen, and went down the ladder toward the voice of the one person alive whom she could not forge her way past.

The permit lay warm where she'd hidden it. Outside, the boots kept coming.
