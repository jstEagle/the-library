## 2. Names in the Ledger

The ledger lived on the shelf beneath the cash box, and it was the most dangerous object in the town.

Isandro had understood this for some time, in the way you understand a load-bearing wall — abstractly, without looking at it too hard. Her father had kept accounts the way other men kept religion: daily, scrupulously, with a kind of trembling devotion. *Every job recorded,* he said, and said it again whenever she tried to skip an entry. *Names, payments, favors. A print shop that doesn't know what it printed is a print shop that will print anything.* He meant it as ethics. He had never once considered it as evidence.

She considered it now.

The shop was closed. Evening came down through the front window in long amber bars, and Isandro stood at her father's desk with the ledger open on her knees, reading backward through three months of her own secret life written in her dead brother's hand — because everything in this town was now written in Tomás's hand, including, God forgive her, her father's signatures when the shaking got bad.

*14 March. Permits, travel, four persons. Paid: nothing. Favor.*

*22 March. Certificates of passage, six. Paid: two hens, one sack of flour. Favor.*

*9 April. Blank forms, army issue (reproduced). Paid: silence. Favor.*

She had written *favor* because her father wrote *favor*, because there was no honest column heading for what people gave her and no honest way to describe what she gave them back. Fourteen names in the last column since winter began. Fourteen families walked out of a town the requisitioning army had begun to treat as a larder. And every one of them listed here in copperplate, dated, cross-referenced by payment, as neat and damning as a confession signed twice.

If the soldiers ever opened this book, they would not find a printer's accounts. They would find a roster.

She turned pages with fingers that smelled of lead and lamp oil. Outside, the street was doing its evening pantomime of normalcy — the baker's shutters coming down, someone's goat complaining about something, the far-off clank of the well chain. But under it all ran the new sound, the one that had arrived with the spring and refused to leave: boots. Not garrison boots, which she knew by rhythm, but marching boots, company-weight, somewhere south of the river and getting less imaginary every day.

Her father coughed in the back room. The long, wet, deliberate cough of a man who had decided not to make a fuss about dying.

"Tomás," he called, when it released him. "Bring the light."

She carried the lamp back. He was propped in his chair by the stove, blanket over his knees, the day's proofs face-down on his chest like a man shielding his heart from bad news. Up close, in lamplight, her father's face had gone the color of the paper they used for cheap handbills — thin stock, high rag content, made to be read once and thrown away.

"You're reading the ledger," he said.

It wasn't a question. He'd heard the shelf.

"Checking entries."

"Find any errors?"

"One or two." She set the lamp on his worktable. "You charged Widow Serra nothing for the funeral notices and then recorded it as paid in kind. One candle. There was no candle."

"There was a candle." He almost smiled. "The one she lit while I told her the price was nothing. That's worth entering. A man should pay for what he receives, even when the price is a kindness. Especially then."

"Papa."

"Every job, Isandro." He used her true name only in the house, behind the closed shutter, in the hour when the shop was dark and the street could keep its fictions to itself. Everywhere else she was Tomás — flat-chested under the binder's apron, jaw shadowed with a walnut-stain beard applied each morning before dawn, voice held low and level in a register she'd practiced until it stopped feeling like a costume. Tomás Underhill, who had died at the frontier two years ago in a uniform that fit him badly, and who now cut type, set chases, pulled proofs, and did not exist. "Every job. Especially the ones we're ashamed of. Especially the ones we're proud of. The book is the shop's soul. You can burn a shop down and rebuild it if the book survives. Lose the book—"

"—and the shop is just furniture," she finished. She'd been finishing his sentences since she was nine. "I know."

He looked at her for a moment longer than the sentence required. His eyes were still sharp; it was everything downstream of the eyes that was failing.

"Something's troubling you," he said. "Besides my health, which troubles both of us, and besides the war, which troubles everyone equally and therefore no one."

She weighed it. Lying to her father had a specific texture — a resistance, like setting type against the grain. She usually didn't bother. But the ledger sat three rooms away, full of fourteen names, and some instincts were older than honesty.

"The army's moving north along the river," she said, which was true, and adjacent to the trouble. "Ferren says two columns past the mill since Sunday."

"Ferren says a great many things. Half of them are even true." He shifted, winced, settled. "Then we'll be busy. Armies want printing. Proclamations, requisition orders, death lists dressed up as casualty reports. They always come to the local shop first because hauling a press is beneath them." He tapped the arm of his chair. "Which means our book gets fuller. Which means, whatever else happens, the record holds. Whatever they do here — whoever they take, whatever they burn — there will be a page that says it happened, in whose hand it happened, and who paid."

She felt the floor tilt slightly, the way it does when a familiar room reveals a second floor you never suspected. He meant it as comfort. It landed as prophecy.

"Yes, Papa," she said, and took the lamp back into the front room, where the ledger waited on its shelf like a witness nobody had thought to swear in.

* * *

She worked late. This was normal; Tomás worked late; the whole street knew Tomás Underhill kept odd hours and a sour temper about being interrupted after dark. So when the knock came at half past ten — three knocks, pause, one knock, their old signal from the days when Mateo had merely been the apprentice fetching type — she opened the door on the chain, looked out, and let him in fast.

Mateo Ferrán came in smelling of night air and the tannery where he lodged, and stood just inside the door the way he always did, hands at his sides, as if the shop were a church he hadn't been confirmed into. He was twenty-three, two years younger than she was, with forearms scarred white where hot metal had kissed them during his apprenticeship, and a way of looking at her — at Tomás — that she had never fully decoded and had privately stopped trying to.

"You shouldn't knock like that anymore," she said, sliding the bolt. "Three-and-one. If anyone's watching, it reads as a signal."

"It is a signal."

"It used to be. Now it's a habit, and habits get people arrested." She lit the desk lamp, keeping the wick low. "What do you need? Type? We're short on 12-point capitals since the garrison bought out our Q's."

"I don't need type." He took off his cap and turned it in his hands. Under the lamp his face had the set look she'd learned to dread — the look of a man rehearsing. "I need a document."

"What document?"

"A travel permit."

"For whom?"

"For me."

The word hung there. Isandro found herself doing the arithmetic instantly, involuntarily — ink, paper stock, the garrison stamp she'd carved in birchwood last January and kept inside the hollow sort in case H — and hated herself for how quickly the craft answered before the person did.

"Where," she said carefully, "are you going?"

"Nowhere. Yet." He set the cap on the counter. "I'm not asking you to forge anything tonight. I'm asking you to have one ready. Made out to me. Dated blank. In case."

"In case of what?"

"In case of the day the columns stop being south of the river." He looked at her straight on, and there it was, the thing under the thing. "Tomás. Isandro." Just the name, bare, in the empty shop, the first time he had ever said it aloud indoors with the shutters closed and no one dead in either family to justify it. "I know what you do at night. I've known since February. I set the forme beside you; I know how much ink goes missing. I know the press runs warm at hours when no legitimate job needs warmth. I'm not telling you this to frighten you. I'm telling you because I want one of those papers to have my name on it, and I want you to make it knowing that I know, and that I would die before I put your name in a soldier's mouth."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere down the street a dog started up and thought better of it.

"You've known since February," she repeated. Her voice came out in Tomás's register, low and even, because under stress the costume held better than her own voice would have. "Eight months. You've set type next to me eight months and said nothing."

"Saying something would have changed nothing except making us both careful. We were already careful." He hesitated. "Mostly."

"Mostly."

"There's a ledger," he said quietly.

The room went very still, the way a room does when a load-bearing wall makes its first small sound.

"What about the ledger?"

"That it exists. That your father records everything. That if the army takes this town the way Ferren says they will, the first thing a requisition officer does is seize records." He spread his hands, scarred palms up. "I grew up in a print shop too, remember. I know what a shop's books are worth to men who count things for a living. Every permit you've forged, every name you've saved — it's all in there, isn't it? Dates. Payments. Favors."

She didn't answer. She didn't have to. He watched her not answering and nodded slowly, as though she'd delivered a lecture.

"So I'm asking for a blank-dated permit with my name on it," he said, "and I'm asking you to think about what happens to that book. Not tonight. Tonight I'll help you set the morning's job and walk home the long way so nobody counts our shadows together. But think about it. Because a record that can save fourteen families can hang them."

He picked up his cap. At the door he paused, and for a moment something crossed his face that was neither the apprentice nor the conspirator — something younger, unguarded, gone before she could name it.

"The Q's," he said. "When you cast new ones — cast a few extra. For me. I liked your father's Q better than ours. The tail curls like it's hiding something."

Then he was gone into the dark, and Isandro stood alone in the lamplight with the ledger on its shelf, hearing the sentence over and over the way the press repeats a forme: *a record that can save fourteen families can hang them.*

Outside, far south of the river, faint as a pulse, the boots went on counting themselves toward the town.
