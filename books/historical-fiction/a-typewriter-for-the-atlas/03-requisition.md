## 3. Requisition

The notices went up on a Tuesday, pasted to the bakery door and the customs house and the stone post beside the bridge, four corners tacked down with nails driven by soldiers who worked in silence, like men hanging funeral wreaths. Isandro saw the first one on her way back from the well with two full pails she would later spill half of without noticing.

The paper was good stock — better than anything the requisitioning army had shown so far — and the printing was competent, machine-set, the kind of work that came out of a garrison press with steel rollers and no soul in it. She stood in the cold reading it twice while the pails swung and her shoulders burned.

*By order of the Provisional Command: the town of Veyra is hereby commandeered for the duration of operations. All inhabitants will surrender to the designated office all records, registers, ledgers, rolls, and lists of names within three days' time. Failure to comply will be treated as conspiracy against the occupying force.*

Three days.

She walked home slowly, setting the pails down twice to reread the thing, as if the words might rearrange themselves into something survivable. They did not. *All records. All ledgers. All lists of names.*

Her father's ledger sat on its lectern behind the shop counter like a confession waiting to be read.

* * *

By noon the whole town had read the notice, and the whole town had arrived at the same arithmetic at the same hour, which was how Isandro found herself standing behind the counter with ink still on her fingers from the morning's job — fifty handbills for Madame Corvez's millinery sale, God help them all, as if hats would matter now — while a line formed outside the door that had nothing to do with type.

They didn't say it aloud. Nobody said anything aloud anymore; the walls had ears the way churches had saints, everywhere and watching. But they came in one at a time with small errands they didn't need done, and each of them, in the pause before leaving, looked at the ledger on its lectern the way you look at a wound you already know is fatal.

Old Bram Senecal, who sold charcoal and hadn't bought a printed thing in eleven years, asked whether Underhill & Son could run him off a price list. His eyes went to the ledger. Back to her face. Away again.

"Of course," Isandro said, in Tomás's voice, low and even, pitched just above the press's rumble. "Come Thursday."

Thursday was one day past the deadline. Bram understood this perfectly. He nodded as though Thursday were an excellent day, touched his cap, and left.

The errands kept coming all afternoon, none of them real, all of them the same message: *you know what's in that book, and we know you know.* The widow Aunor Fex needed a notice printed for a lost goat. The goat had been lost since spring. The ferryman Pell wanted calling cards, though he had called on no one in his life. Each of them glanced at the ledger. Each of them left a little lighter or a little heavier depending on what they'd seen in Isandro's face, and she made sure they saw nothing at all, because Tomás Underhill's face was a composing stick — it held whatever was set in it and gave back nothing loose.

Inside her, underneath the composed face, something was screaming in a register only she could hear.

Because here was the truth of it, laid out flat as fresh stock: every name in that ledger was a name the army could hang. Page after page in her father's careful upright hand — *Paid in kind, two hens, Mrs. Ondine Ravel, permit work, no charge* — *permit work, no charge* — over and over, forty-one times, forty-one permits she had cut and forged and pressed in the small hours while her father slept, forty-one families walked out of Veyra ahead of the requisition carts because of entries written in her father's own honest hand. He had insisted on it. *A shop that doesn't record its work isn't a shop,* he liked to say, *it's a rumor.* Ten years of bookkeeping virtue, and now the book itself was the gallows ledger.

And worse — worse, because it cut closer than fear — the ledger held names that were not yet out. Names of people who had come to her asking, who were waiting, whose permits existed only as entries in that damning column. If the ledger went to the commission's office, those people wouldn't flee. They'd be collected.

She closed the shop early, turned the sign, shot the bolt, and stood in the dimness listening to her own heart try to climb out through her throat.

* * *

Her father was at the worktable in the back, sorting sorts by feel with his eyes shut — a game he played with himself, an old compositor's meditation he claimed kept the fingers honest. He opened his eyes when she came in and read her face the way he read galley proofs, top to bottom, catching every error.

"You've seen the notice," he said.

"Everyone's seen the notice." She sat across from him, and for once didn't bother adjusting her voice, didn't bother being Tomás, because there was nobody there but him and he knew, he had always known, from the first week when grief for Tomás had nearly swallowed her and dressing in his clothes had been the only rope she could reach. He had never once said her name aloud since. Saying it would have made her real again, and real girls couldn't keep print shops alive in towns about to be eaten by armies.

"They'll take the ledger," she said. "It has everything. Every permit. Every name."

"I know what's in it. I wrote most of it."

"Then help me think."

He considered her for a long moment. In the lamplight his hands looked enormous and gentle among the cases of type, hands that had set ten thousand pages and never once dropped a letter. Then he coughed — short, dry, turned away from her toward the wall — and came back, and said:

"Think like a printer, not like a fugitive. What do we do with a document we can't let be read?"

"We destroy it."

"And lose ten years of the town's memory? No." He shook his head. "We do what printers have always done with dangerous text. We don't destroy it. We distribute it. You can't confiscate what's everywhere."

"That's madness. I can't copy the whole ledger—"

"Not the whole ledger." He tapped the table. "The names. Just the names, and just the ones that matter. The ones tied to the permits. Copy them small, copy them onto thin stock, and put them where no clerk will ever think to look."

Isandro stared at him. Then she turned and looked at the type cases along the back wall — dozens of shallow wooden drawers, each divided into a hundred little compartments, each compartment filled with slugs of lead, and between the lead, in every case, gaps where the letters had been distributed out for the day's work. Gaps the size of folded rice paper.

"The cases," she breathed.

"The cases." Her father permitted himself the ghost of a smile. "An army officer will search a mattress, a floorboard, a Bible. He will not search a printer's type case, because to search it properly he'd need to know what he was looking at, and no officer in that army knows a quoin from a quoit." He paused, and the smile faded into something older and sadder. "Your brother used to hide sweets in the lower cases when he was six. I never had the heart to tell him I knew. Some hiding places are inherited, like eye color."

* * *

That night she did not sleep.

She waited until her father's lamp went dark, until his cough settled into the long ragged rhythm that meant sleep if not rest, then took a candle down into the shop and got to work.

First the paper. Rice paper, tissue-thin, the stock they kept for carbon copies and interleaving fine plates — a full sheet weighed less than a moth. She cut it with the guillotine into strips barely wider than her thumb, folded each strip three times into a hard little packet the size of a grain sack in miniature, and tested one in an empty compartment of the upper case, laying lead sorts around it until it vanished completely. She lifted the case, tilted it, shook it gently. Nothing moved. Nothing showed.

Then she opened the ledger to the first page of permit entries and began to copy.

This was the part that frightened her more than the soldiers did, and she was honest enough with herself to know why. It wasn't the risk of being caught with the pen moving at midnight. It was what the copying meant. Every name she wrote down was a name she was admitting, somewhere inside herself, might otherwise be lost — might be taken, questioned, shot, buried unregistered, erased the way the army erased things, which was to say thoroughly and without paperwork, except hers. The act of writing them felt like pre-registering the dead. Her pen shook on the third name and she had to stop, press her wrist flat against the desk, breathe until her hand remembered it was a compositor's hand and compositors did not tremble.

*Mrs. Ondine Ravel. Paid in kind, two hens.*

Ondine had crossed the mountains in March with her sister's family. Isandro had watched their cart go from the shop window and had felt, that day, like a locksmith opening doors for people fleeing a fire. Now she wrote the woman's name in letters two millimeters high and slid it into a case, and it felt like burying something precious in a field the enemy was going to plow.

She worked through the night. Forty-one permits, forty-one entries, but some entries held several names — families, cousins, witnesses — and by the time the window went gray she had sixty-three names on thirty-one packets of rice paper, distributed through seven type cases according to a system only she would ever know: the R's in the case that held the roman capitals, the S's among the small italics, and so on, a private geography of survival laid over the public geography of the alphabet.

Her fingers were gray with lead dust. Her eyes burned. In the east the sky did that thing it did before bad days, going the color of dishwater.

She locked the ledger back on its lectern, straightened the shop, and stood a moment in the middle of the room with her arms wrapped around herself, listening to the building settle.

Three days. She had copied what was written. But the ledger kept living — tomorrow there would be new jobs, new requests, maybe new names, and the deadline would arrive whether she was ready or not. And underneath all of it, coiled and patient, was the thought she couldn't make lie still: the ledger wasn't the only record. There was her father's memory, which was failing. There was Mateo's quick mind, which held everything he'd ever set. There was the boy Ferren, who loitered outside the shop counting soldiers the way other children counted crows — a boy who saw too much and owed them nothing.

Sixty-three names hidden in lead. And the knock, when it came, would come for the book.

She blew out the candle.

Two days later — one day left, the shop shuttered, her father sleeping badly upstairs — someone knocked anyway. Not the heavy double-knock of soldiers. A quick, light triple, the knock of someone who'd learned the rhythm the apprentices used.

Through the shutter crack she saw Mateo's face, pale and set, and behind him, half-hidden in the alley shadow, the small sharp figure of Ferren, who never came to the door, who had never once in his life knocked on any door at all.

Mateo leaned close to the shutter. When he spoke, his voice was low and fast, and it emptied the street of every other sound in Isandro's head:

"Tomás — they've posted the berth lists at the garrison. There's a train coming. And the boy says the officers were asking at the barracks tonight — asking specifically for the Underhill ledger."
