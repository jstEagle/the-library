## 4. What Can Be Carried

The knock came at dawn, soft and wrong. Neighbors knocked loud in this town; they had always been a people who announced themselves. This knock was three knuckles, twice, then silence — the knock of someone who did not want to be heard.

Isandro opened the shop door with her brother's name already settled on her face.

Marta Ceylan stood in the gray light with a bundle wrapped in a flour sack, held against her chest the way you hold a thing that is either very valuable or very dangerous. Behind her, at the corner, old Piotr Voss waited with his cap in his hands like a man outside a church.

"Tomás," Marta said, and stepped inside without being asked, which told Isandro everything before the sack was even opened. "You'll take it."

Not a question. Isandro closed the door and turned the bolt, then the second bolt her father had installed after the last war, back when bolts had seemed like enough.

Inside the flour sack: two candlesticks, tarnished silver, and beneath them a smaller bundle — a christening gown, folded to the size of a fist, yellowed to the color of weak tea. Marta's daughter's gown. The daughter who was buried behind the chapel in '13, before anyone had thought to measure a life by what could be carried out of it.

"The soldiers will take the house," Marta said. Her voice was flat as a composing stone. "They take what's in houses. But they don't search print shops. Nobody loots a church of paper."

"We're not a church."

"You're the closest thing left." She set the bundle on the counter, next to the galley where Isandro had been setting type since before light. "Bury them in the type cases if you have to. I don't care where. I care that when this is over — if I'm over — there's something of hers that wasn't fed to their fires."

Isandro looked at the candlesticks. Silver melts down into bullets; everyone knew that now. And a christening gown would burn in one breath, a whole childhood gone in less time than it took to strike the match.

"I can't promise they'll survive," she said. "I can promise they won't be found in *your* house."

"That's all hiding ever was." Marta touched the counter once, lightly, the way you touch a coffin lid, and went out into the morning.

Piotr came in after her, then the widow Havel with her husband's medals wrapped in oilcloth, then the Ferreiras from the tannery with a tin box that clinked, then a boy Isandro didn't know carrying a violin case that held no violin. By noon the back room looked like a pawnshop run by ghosts: jewelry, documents, photographs, one entire set of false teeth in a matchbox that made Mateo go pale and say nothing for an hour.

* *

Her father watched from his chair by the press, wrapped in two blankets though the stove was lit. He had not cut type in four days. His hands, which had set every page this town had printed for forty years, lay in his lap like tools hung up for winter.

"You're building us a museum," he said.

"I'm building us nothing. I'm keeping other people's things safe."

"A museum of other people's things." He coughed — dry, then wet, then the long terrible pause between coughs that she had learned to dread more than the coughing itself. When it passed, he said, "Your mother used to do this. During the first war. She hid a rabbi's books in the ink cellar. Six hundred volumes. She never told me until afterward."

"You never told me that either."

"There's a great deal neither of us told you." He smiled, and the smile cost him something; she could see it in the way his shoulders dropped after, spent. "She said a printer's shop is the only honest hiding place in the world. Because everything in it looks like everything else. A case full of lead sorts, and among them a case full of secrets, and God himself couldn't tell the difference unless He knew how to read."

Isandro kept sorting the widow Havel's medals into a drawer of twelve-point em quads, wrapping each in cotton waste. "And could Mother read?"

"She learned." He looked at his hands. "That was the year I understood I'd married above my station."

The stove ticked. Outside, a column of carts went past on the road — too many wheels, going too slowly, the sound of an army that had decided to stay.

"Papa." She said it low, so the neighbors in the front room wouldn't hear through the wall. "If they come here—"

"They won't come today."

"If they come—"

"Then I will be an old printer with a bad chest and a son who cuts type too slow," he said. "I've played simpler roles with worse scripts."

He thought she didn't see him fold the handkerchief away after. But she did. She saw it every time now — the turn of his shoulders, the quick economy of a man concealing evidence, and the small red signature he pressed into the cloth before folding it small enough to disappear. Three times yesterday. Twice this morning. He was a compositor; even his dying was typeset neatly, justified flush to the margin, so no one would have to see the ragged edge.

* *

In the afternoon she took inventory, because inventory was the only prayer she knew.

The back room held, as of four o'clock: eleven bundles belonging to nine families. One strongbox with a broken lock. Fourteen letters that were certainly treasonous and probably worthless. Two wedding rings that had come separately and belonged, Isandro suspected, to the same marriage now quietly ending. And underneath the type cases, wrapped in rice paper in a drawer marked SPACERS, the list of names — thirty-one families smuggled east on papers she had forged, each entry copied in her own hand because her father's rule had never wavered:

*Every job recorded.*

He'd meant it as bookkeeping. It had become testimony. The ledger downstairs held every favor, every payment, every forged permit — and the requisition notice nailed to the chapel door gave them three days to surrender all records or be charged with obstruction. Three days, and tomorrow was half gone.

She sat on the floor amid other people's treasures and did the arithmetic that mattered, the arithmetic nobody taught you: what weighs, and what waits.

The candlesticks weighed. So did the strongbox, the medals, the teeth in their matchbox. All of it was weight — the dead gravity of a life lived in one place, in one house, under one roof that had seemed permanent right up until the notices went up. None of it could be carried. That was the lesson the century kept teaching and nobody learned: you could carry bread, a knife, a child, a document. You could carry perhaps ten pounds of your life across a border, and the rest stayed behind to be inventoried by strangers.

Except paper. Paper weighed nothing.

A christening gown weighed almost nothing either, she supposed. But Marta hadn't brought it because it was light. She'd brought it because it was irreplaceable, and irreplaceable things had a different physics entirely — they got heavier the farther you carried them, until your arms gave out, and so you handed them to someone else to hold.

Isandro wrapped the gown in fresh rice paper and slid it into the case reserved for eight-point Bodoni, between the lowercase e's and f's, where no soldier in history had ever looked.

Then she went downstairs and unlocked the ledger.

* *

Mateo arrived at dusk with ink on his cuffs and news in his mouth.

"The bakery's making hard bread," he said. "Army bread. They've contracted the whole output. Frau Lindt says the ovens haven't stopped since Tuesday." He hung his coat on the peg that still, out of forty years' habit, he reached for without thinking — the apprentice's peg, lowest on the rack. "Hard bread keeps. Armies buy what keeps."

"Then we eat soft bread while we can." Isandro ladled barley soup into three bowls. Her father's portion she cut the bread into for him, small pieces, the way you feed a bird or a convalescent, pretending all the while this was ordinary.

They ate standing, the way printers ate, in the narrow aisle between the press and the stone sink. Her father sat and watched them with an expression she couldn't read — some mixture of pride and grief that he wore like an apron these days, always on, always stained.

"Mateo," he said suddenly. "Show me your hands."

Mateo put his hands out flat, palms up, over the table. Ink in every crease. The callus on his right middle finger where the composing stick pressed. A healing split along his thumb from Sunday's eighteen-hour setting of funeral announcements — the town's other growth industry.

Her father turned the hands over gently, like a man appraising type. "Clean pulls," he said. "Even pressure. You throw the lever like you mean it but you don't bully her. Good." He released the hands. "Better than Tomás, if we're honest."

"Papa." Heat crawled up her neck.

"It's true. You set beautiful pages but you pull timid. Your brother—" he caught himself, smoothly, a man stepping over a crack in the pavement, "—you're improving. That's all I meant. You're improving."

But Mateo had seen the crack too. Isandro watched him see it — watched the question form behind his eyes and, remarkably, get set aside, locked in a case of its own, to be sorted later. He had been doing that for weeks now. Collecting the inconsistencies of Tomás Underhill and storing them without comment, like a compositor gathering loose sorts off the floor, waiting to see what sentence they spelled.

After supper he stayed to wash the bowls, which he never did, and she stood beside him drying, and the shop was quiet except for water and crockery and her father's breathing from the chair, which she counted without meaning to, the way Ferren counted soldiers.

"The Voss girl came by the market asking for you," Mateo said. "Asking for Tomás, rather. About permits."

"How many?"

"She said six. She said there are six families on the river lane who want to leave before the requisition finishes." He set the last bowl in the rack and did not look at her. "Six permits. On top of the ones you owe the Ferreira cousins. On top of whatever's in the ledger that has to vanish in two days."

"You know about the ledger."

"I set half those entries myself." Now he looked at her. In the lamplight his face was young and utterly steady. "Whatever happens to that book, it doesn't happen alone. That's all I'm saying. Whatever happens."

Above the sink, her father coughed — the long kind, the kind with a pause after it like a held breath. Isandro dried her hands and went to him and did not let herself watch the handkerchief. Some things you carried by refusing to weigh them.

* *

She worked past midnight, copying names onto rice paper while the shop slept around her. Thirty-one families. One hundred and fourteen souls. Each name set down twice — once in the hidden copy, once in memory, which was the only vault whose lock could not be picked.

At some point she realized her father had not coughed in over an hour, and went up to check, and found him awake in the dark, sitting upright, looking at the press.

"I've been thinking about what can be carried," he said, as if the conversation had never paused. "All day, I've been thinking. Everyone brings me — brings you — the heavy things. Silver, rings, documents worth money. And I keep thinking: none of that survives. Melted, divided, scattered. Money gets stolen, silver gets melted, even names on documents get crossed out." He gestured at the press in the dark, a black mass that might have been furniture or an altar. "But lead remembers. You cast a letter and it holds its shape for five hundred years. You can melt it down and recast it and it's still the same letter. Nothing else in the world forgives you like that."

"What are you saying, Papa?"

"I'm saying that when they come for the records — and they will come — the paper isn't the record." His voice was thin but level, a man reading aloud a truth he had set in advance. "The paper is just the impression. The record is whoever read it. Whoever remembers. You've been memorizing that list for two days, little one. Don't think I haven't noticed."

The endearment slipped out and hung in the dark between them. He had not called her that since before she began wearing Tomás's shirts. Neither of them remarked on it. Some errors, once set, you simply ran the edition.

"Go to sleep," she said. "We have two days."

"We have tonight," he said. "Two days is a rumor. Tonight is real." He lay back down, obedient at last to his own body, and within minutes his breathing slowed into the rhythm she could count without fear.

Isandro stood a while longer in the doorway, listening to the shop tick and settle around its cargo of other people's lives. Eleven bundles upstairs. Thirty-one families in her head. One handkerchief folded somewhere in the bedding, holding its red proof.

Outside, far off toward the river, boots on cobblestone — a patrol, unhurried, counting the town the way she counted names.

Three days to surrender the ledger, and the ledger held everything: every permit, every favor, every family she had moved east now written down in her father's careful hand, waiting in the archive of the enemy like a loaded gun laid on a table.

Two days, now.

She blew out the lamp and went to find more rice paper.
