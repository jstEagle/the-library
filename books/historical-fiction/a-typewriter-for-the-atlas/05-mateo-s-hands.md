## 5. Mateo's Hands

Mateo Vidal had worked for Underhill & Son for three years, and in all that time he had never once been alone in the shop after dark. That was the arrangement, unspoken but iron: Tomás Underhill locked the door at nine, took the key upstairs to the rooms above, and Mateo went home to his aunt's house on the Calle de los Tilos with ink to the wrists and a walk of twelve minutes to think about nothing at all.

Tonight the lock had not come. Tonight Tomás had pressed a coin into his hand at closing — *for the trouble* — and asked him to stay and wash the forme for the funeral notices, because there was a job that couldn't wait until morning.

So Mateo stayed. He washed the forme. He swept. He oiled the press spindle with the little can of whale oil that lived under the stone sink, and he listened to the rain come off the mountains and drum the shop's tin awning like fingers on a coffin lid.

And then, at half past eleven, when he had every reason in the world to be gone, he heard the key turn in the front door.

He did not move. He stood behind the composing stone with the oil can still in his fist and watched, through the gap between two standing cases, as Tomás Underhill came into the dark shop without lighting a lamp — came in the way a man comes into a room where he has done something wrong a hundred times and expects to do it a hundred and one.

Except that Tomás was not a man. Mateo had known that for a year.

---

He had known it the way you know a thing you have no permission to know: by accumulation, by small evidence stacked like type in a stick. The way Tomás bound his chest flat under the shirt and moved his shoulders wrong to compensate, too careful, too deliberate, a man performing a man the way an apprentice performs mastery — correctly and without ease. The way he never undressed in the shop even on the hottest days of August, sweating through wool while Mateo rolled his own sleeves to the elbow. The way he washed his hair at the sink before dawn on Sundays, when no one could see him, and once — only once, and Mateo had looked away so fast he'd nearly knocked over the chase — the way his voice slipped its mooring and came out soft and low and unmistakably a woman's, when he thought the shop was empty and sang three bars of something his mother must have sung him, before catching himself in the silence like a man grabbing a ledge.

Isandro. He'd found the name the way he found everything, in the type drawers, in the old forms from years back — a memorial card, five years old now, for Isandro Underhill, beloved daughter, aged fourteen. And beside it in the same drawer, another card: Tomás Underhill, beloved son, aged seventeen. Two children dead within a year, if you believed the cards. Mateo had set enough memorials to know that towns sometimes buried people who were merely inconvenient. He had never asked. Asking would have made it his, and it wasn't his; it belonged to her, whoever she was, whatever name she carried like contraband against her ribs.

But knowing a thing is not the same as being permitted to stand six feet away from it in the dark and watch it happen.

Tomás went first to the far case, the case of long sorts that nobody used for ordinary work — the ornamentals, the borders, the blank quads — and slid the whole drawer out onto the stone. From inside his coat he brought a folded paper. Mateo heard, more than saw, the small sounds that followed: the whisper of rice paper, the click of a pen nib uncapped, the scratch-scratch of writing done fast and small, the way you write when you are copying rather than composing.

Then the lamp above the stone desk clicked on, and Mateo stopped being able to pretend he hadn't seen.

The paper was a travel permit. He knew the form — everyone in town knew the form now, since the requisition notices went up, since the word *Ausweis* entered the language of the market like a stone entering water. This one was filled out in a hand that was not Tomás's printing hand. It was better than that. It was the hand of a tired clerk who had written ten thousand of these and hated every one: cramped, slanted, bored. There was a stamp beside the signature, red-brown in the lamplight, and the stamp was not theirs. They owned no such stamp.

Tomás lifted the finished permit, held it toward the lamp, tilted it — checking weight, checking grain, checking everything — and that was when he saw Mateo's shadow on the floor.

---

What happened next happened in about four seconds, and Mateo would spend the rest of the night taking it apart.

Tomás went still. Not frozen — still, the way water goes still, which is not peace but depth. His eyes went to the oil can in Mateo's fist, to the distance between them, to the door behind Mateo and whether it was bolted. Mateo watched him do this arithmetic and understood, with a lurch, that Tomás Underhill was calculating whether he would have to hurt him.

"I'm not going to say anything," Mateo said.

"Everyone says that."

"No. I mean I'm not going to say anything ever. To anyone. About any of this." He set the oil can down on the stone, slowly, the way you set down a weapon. "I've known for a year. Longer. Since the memorial cards. It doesn't matter to me what your name is. It matters what you're doing, and I can see what you're doing."

Tomás — Isandro, he should think of her as Isandro, though the name stuck in his throat like a fishbone — didn't relax. "You see a crime."

"I see Mrs. Ferrán's son crossing into the hills next week because if he stays they'll put him in a column and march him east. I see the Ondiz family gone since Tuesday and nobody asking where." He swallowed. "I see my master's daughter doing the work that keeps this town breathing, and doing it alone, and badly lit, and exhausted, and I'm asking—" and here his voice did something embarrassing, cracked in the middle like a boy's, "—I'm asking why you never let me help."

For a long moment the rain filled the silence, drumming the awning, running in the gutters.

"You knew," she said at last. Not a question. She sat down on the stool by the stone desk, all at once, like someone whose legs had been holding up more than her body. In the lamplight, without the performed shoulders, without the flat public voice, she looked younger and older at the same time. "A year. You let me believe—"

"What was I supposed to say? *Good evening, señorita, I notice you are committing treason?*" He almost smiled. "Besides. It's yours. Your secret. I don't get a vote on it just because I sweep the floors."

She laughed then — short, startled, real — and pressed the heel of her hand against her eye. "God. Three years I've done this and I'm undone by the floor sweeper."

"Apprentice," Mateo said. "I set a clean page. Ask anyone."

"Your kerning is criminal."

"My kerning is *generous*."

And that broke it open. That broke whatever it was — the terror, the four seconds of arithmetic, the year of parallel silences — and left two people laughing in a lamplit print shop at midnight, quietly, helplessly, because the alternative to laughter was weeping and neither of them could afford it.

---

She showed him the work because he asked, and because — he understood this later, turning it over in the dark of his aunt's kitchen — because she was so tired of carrying it alone that the relief of setting it down outweighed the danger.

"The permits are the easy part," she said. "Form, hand, stamp. The stamp I cut myself, from a boot heel and a piece of brass curtain rod. Look." She turned it over: a crude but convincing eagle, or what passed for an eagle at arm's length in bad light, which was all a stamp needed to be. "The hard part isn't making them. It's knowing which ones to make. A permit for a farmhand reads differently than a permit for a merchant. Wrong class of paper, wrong class of ink, and the checkpoint man starts wondering why a laborer writes like a schoolmaster." She tapped the ledger, closed, sitting heavy on the corner of the desk. "Everything's in there. Who needs what. Who's already gone. Who can't be helped yet."

"That book will hang you someday."

"It'll hang us all someday," she said, without drama, the way you state the weather. "Right now it's the only memory this town has. When the army takes a family, the family stops existing — no record, no inquiry, nobody to ask after them. As long as their names are written somewhere, they exist. My father taught me that. Ink outlives the hand that sets it." She said it like scripture, and Mateo understood she had been raised on it the way other children were raised on psalms.

"Teach me the hand," he said.

She looked up sharply. "No."

"Not to make permits. I'm not asking to join your conspiracy." Though he was, and they both knew it. "I'm asking you to teach me the clerk's hand so I can set the funeral notices and the invoices and leave you the nights. You haven't slept in a month. You think I haven't noticed? You fall asleep standing at the stone like a horse." He kept his voice level, kept it in the register of craft, because the other register — the one underneath, the one that wanted to say *let me carry some of this because I cannot watch you break* — that register would frighten her off like a bird. "You need a second pair of hands. Every printer does. Even you."

"And if I teach you and you're caught—"

"Then I'm caught holding a funeral notice."

"You'd be caught holding me," she said, and then went white, hearing it.

The rain drummed. Neither of them moved.

"Yes," Mateo said finally, very carefully, the way you set a fragile sort into a tight line. "That's generally what being caught means."

---

They worked until three.

She taught him the clerk's hand the way she'd been taught everything, he suspected — by demonstration, without mercy, with corrections delivered in a voice stripped of all softness because the softness was the dangerous part and had to be kept caged. *Slant it more. No, more. A clerk is tired, not artistic.* She made him write the same sentence forty times: *Permission is hereby granted to pass through the district of—* until his wrist burned and the letters stopped being letters and became a rhythm, a gait, the tired shuffle of an imaginary bureaucrat.

"Better," she said on the forty-first. Then, grudgingly, the way you praise: "Much better. Your *g* still shows off."

"My *g* has always shown off."

"Kill it. Kill everything in yourself that wants to be admired. A forgery that admires itself gets people shot." And then, quieter, watching him blot the practice sheet: "That's the whole art, really. Disappearing. Good printing disappears — you read the words, not the type. A good forgery is just printing that lies. Same discipline."

"Same hands," Mateo said.

She looked at his hands then — really looked, for the first time, he was almost sure — ink to the knuckles, split at two cuticles from the winter alkali, steady on the pen. Something crossed her face that she did not permit to become an expression. She turned back to the drawer of hidden papers, and her voice, when it came, was strictly professional.

"Thursday. The Ferrán boy's permit. I'll do the hand, you do the layout — you're faster at fitting a line than I am, always have been. We'll finish in half the time if you take the forme and I take the writing."

"We?"

"You asked," she said. "Don't make me regret the grammar."

It was not a declaration. Nobody declared anything. But when they finally doused the lamp and she walked him the length of the dark shop to the door — walking, he noticed, with her own true stride now, unperformed, easy, because there was no one left in the world to perform for except him — she paused with her hand on the bolt and said, to the door rather than to him:

"Mateo. What you saw tonight. If they ever take me, you were here washing a forme and you went home at nine. You swear it on the press."

"On the press," he said. It was the only oath either of them believed in.

She opened the door onto the rain and the smell of wet stone, and he stepped out into it, and she said his name once more, softly, behind him — not *goodnight*, not *thank you*, just his name, testing the weight of it in her mouth like a compositor testing a new sort — and he walked home through the empty streets with his heart going like a platen, twelve minutes that felt like the longest walk of his life.

---

His aunt was asleep. He did not light a lamp. He sat in the kitchen in the dark with his ink-stained hands open on the table, looking at them as if they belonged to someone else — someone new, someone conscripted, someone who had enlisted tonight without signing anything.

He thought: *she sings when she thinks no one is listening.*

He thought: *the Ferrán boy, Thursday. Fitting the lines.*

He thought: *I would learn any hand in the world if it meant standing next to her in that light.*

Outside, far off toward the river, a train whistle blew — long, freight-heavy, wrong for this hour, wrong for this town — and did not stop blowing for a long time. Somewhere down the valley, things were moving that had nothing to do with ink or permits or the small warm conspiracy of a lamplit shop. Mateo listened until the whistle faded, and then he went to bed, and slept badly, and dreamed of type cases full of names, every compartment labeled, every label in her handwriting.

In the morning there would be requisition notices to read again, closer this time. In the morning, the town would learn that all records were to be surrendered within three days — and Mateo, sweeping the doorstep at dawn, would see the posted order flapping on the church door, and would understand, with cold clarity, exactly which record they meant, and whose handwriting was all over it.
