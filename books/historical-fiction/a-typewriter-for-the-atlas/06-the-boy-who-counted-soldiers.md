## 6. The Boy Who Counted Soldiers

Ferren counted things because counting was the only thing nobody could take from him.

He had counted the steps from the bakery to the fountain (one hundred twelve, or one hundred fifteen if he took the cobbles around the puddle that never dried). He had counted the bells of the church of Saint Aurel, which rang eleven at noon and never explained themselves. He had counted the days since his mother stopped coming back for him — four hundred and six, though he'd lost a week somewhere in the spring when the fever took him and he woke in the charity ward with no memory of it, so it might be four hundred and thirteen.

And he counted soldiers.

This was not sentiment. This was trade.

The boys who ran messages for the fishmonger earned two sous a trip. The boys who swept the requisition office steps got bread crusts and a kick. But the boy who could tell you how many supply wagons had come over the north bridge since dawn, how many wore the grey of the western column versus the green of the river garrison, which officers had new boots — that boy could sell what he knew, and what he knew was worth more than crusts.

Ferren sold to anyone with coin: the barber, the smuggler at the tannery, occasionally a man he suspected worked for the other side, whoever that was this month. He did not sell to the occupying command itself, on principle, though the principle had less to do with loyalty than with arithmetic. Armies that bought information about their own movements were armies that shot the sellers once they'd finished buying. He'd watched it happen in Vellacourt to a boy named Anselme, who had been very good at counting right up until the moment it stopped mattering.

So Ferren kept his ledgers in his head, and his head kept him alive, and the town of Saint-Brise-sur-Lomme went on bleeding quietly around him while he stood in doorways and tallied boots.

The print shop on the rue des Casses was the best place in town to count from. Its window sat at the crook where the street bent toward the bridge, and its light burned later than any other light, and through the diamond panes a person could watch the whole evening traffic of the occupation pass by: wagons, patrols, officers' cars, the water cart, the priest on his bicycle with his collar tucked into his coat like a man ashamed of God.

Ferren had been loitering outside Underhill's for three weeks before he understood that something in the shop did not add up.

* * *

It started as an error in his columns.

The printer's son — Tomás Underhill, the name everyone used — came and went at hours that made no sense. Out at dusk when the patrol changed. Back after curfew, moving along the wall side of the street where the lamplight failed. A boy running errands would cut through the market square, because the square was open ground and faster; Tomás never did. A boy carrying type deliveries walked heavy, shoulders square; Tomás moved like someone perpetually stepping over a wire.

Then there was the night of the rain, the second Tuesday of October, when the patrol on the bridge turned back early because the water was up and the sergeant had no taste for drowning. Ferren was under the awning of the shuttered chandlery across the way, dry as a cat in a cupboard, watching. The shop door opened. Tomás stepped out into the rain without a hat, and the lamp above the door caught the face full-on for perhaps three seconds.

Three seconds was plenty. Ferren had spent two years learning to read faces at distance — you had to, if you wanted to know whether the man walking toward your corner was going to pay you or arrest you.

Tomás Underhill's face was the wrong shape for the shoulders above it. Too fine at the jaw. And the way the figure pulled its cap down, the angle of the hands — he'd seen girls do exactly that in Vellacourt, the mill girls walking home past the barracks, every one of them performing the same small violence against their own outlines.

He stood under the awning a long time after the door closed, rain drumming the canvas, and re-counted everything.

Tomás's hands. He'd seen them once at close range, taking a bundle of handbills off him — ink in every crease, but narrow hands, unbroken nails. Boys who set type all day had nicked thumbs and callus ridges. These hands had callus in the wrong places, from something else. From a knife, maybe. From cutting.

The ledger. Everyone knew old Underhill recorded everything — payments, favors, sins, probably the weather. And everyone knew the occupying command had begun collecting paper the way other men collected watches. If half the town owed its escape to papers printed at Underhill's, then that ledger wasn't a ledger. It was a gallows with entries.

And now, tonight, the third thing. Tonight the shop light had gone out early, and Tomás had left through the alley door instead of the front, and Ferren had followed at forty paces, soft-shoed over wet cobbles, all the way to the chancery wall where the figure had pressed flat into shadow while a patrol passed, and Ferren had watched her — *her*, the word arrived in his head without ceremony, the way true words do — watch *her* slide a folded paper into the gap between two stones and stand there breathing until the boots faded.

A boy running errands did not hide papers in walls.

Ferren leaned against the chancery wall long after she'd gone, and did what he always did with a fact too large to hold: he broke it into counts. One girl. One dead brother whose clothes she wore — he remembered now, the whole town remembered, the Underhill boy who'd died two winters back, fever or shrapnel, depending who told it. One father coughing himself hollow behind that lit window. One ledger sitting in the shop like a bomb with handwriting in it.

One piece of knowledge worth more than anything Ferren had ever carried.

He was eleven years old and had been alone for four hundred and thirteen days, give or take a week, and he stood in the dark doing the sums of his own life and found that the only column that ever balanced was the one marked *leverage*.

* * *

He waited two days, because a fact spent carelessly is a fact spent once.

On the second day he learned why waiting mattered. At noon the requisition notices went up on the church doors and the mairie: ALL RECORDS, REGISTERS, LEDGERS AND LISTS TO BE SURRENDERED WITHIN THREE DAYS, ON PAIN OF ARREST. He read it three times through the crowd, lips moving, and felt the arithmetic shift under him like ice on a pond. Three days. Whatever lived in that ledger had three days left in the world — and whatever lived in the girl's head had three days to become worth more than silence.

That evening he took up his post across from the print shop and did not leave it.

The light burned late. Past ten, past eleven. Through the panes he could see two figures bending over the composing stone — the old man swaying like a mast in wind, and the girl, sleeves rolled, hair shoved under her brother's cap, feeding letters into a stick one by one with a speed no apprentice alive could match. They weren't printing. Printing was loud. They were *setting* — building pages that would print later, somewhere else, or maybe never, pages that existed first in lead because lead couldn't be searched.

At half past midnight the front door opened and Mateo Ruiz came out — the apprentice, the one with the forearms and the quiet eyes, who carried flour sacks for the widow Cordova without being asked and never told anyone about it either. Ferren filed that too. He filed everything. It was the family trait he'd invented for himself, since he'd been given no actual family to inherit traits from.

Mateo turned toward the lodging house. The door closed behind him. The lamp inside the shop guttered, steadied, went low.

Ferren crossed the street.

* * *

He didn't knock. Knocking was for people whose business could survive daylight. He pressed his palm flat against the wood and rapped twice, low, with the heel of his hand — the sound a shutter makes in wind, nothing more.

Inside, silence. Then, faintly, the scrape of a chair. Footsteps, careful, weight rolled to the outside of the feet. She opened the door on the chain — he could see the chain glinting, see her eye in the four-inch gap, and up close, at last, there was no mistaking anything: the lashes, the line of the throat, the cap sitting on hair pinned hard and hidden.

"Shop's shut," she said, and her voice was a masterpiece, pitched low and even, a boy's voice worn like a borrowed coat. "Come back tomorrow."

"I don't want type," Ferren said. "I want to talk about the wall by the chancery."

The eye in the gap did not move. That was the first thing that frightened him — not anger, not fear, but the absolute stillness of someone recalculating their entire life in the space of a breath. He'd seen soldiers do it. He'd seen horses do it.

"I saw you put something between the stones," he went on, keeping his voice flat and businesslike, a tradesman's voice. "Two nights ago. Patrol came from the bridge side and you waited till they passed. I know how many men were on it. I know their sergeant's boots are new. I know you've been printing permits, because I've delivered three of them myself without knowing what they were, and now I do." He paused, and made himself say the rest, because a bargain half-spoken was a bargain wasted. "And I know Tomás Underhill died two years ago, and the person wearing his clothes is a girl."

The chain did not come off. Behind her, deeper in the shop, a floorboard creaked — the old man, awake, listening. Ferren kept his eyes on hers.

"You can shut the door," he said. "You can go to the commandant tomorrow and tell them a street kid is telling lies, and maybe they believe you and maybe they don't, but they'll search your shop anyway, because that's what they're already doing to everyone, and you have—" he glanced up at the moon, did the count, "—two days left before the notices come due. Or you can hear what I want. It isn't much. It's less than what I'm not asking for."

"What are you not asking for," she said. Still that flat, level, borrowed voice. But underneath it he heard the real one, just barely, the way you hear the sea under wind.

"Money," Ferren said. "Passage. A berth on whatever leaves this town when the shooting stops — the train, the barge, a cart, I don't care. Somewhere south, somewhere the requisitioning doesn't reach. You're putting people on lists. I want to be on one."

Silence. The lamp behind her made her shadow stretch out across the threshold and touch his shoes.

"And in exchange," she said slowly, "you'll keep quiet."

"I'll keep counting," Ferren said. "Quiet's free. Counting's what costs."

* * *

She let him in.

Not far in — three steps, into the smell of ink and oil and cold metal, the composing stone a pale rectangle in the gloom, cases ranged along the walls like drawers in some enormous cabinet of everything people had ever said. The old man sat propped in a chair by the stove with a blanket over his knees and eyes that were terribly clear, clearer than any dying man's had a right to be, and Ferren understood at once that this one had known he was coming, or something like him, for longer than the girl had.

"So," the old man said. "The boy who counts."

"You know me?"

"I know the whole street knows you. I know you sold the tannery man the wagon count that kept his carts moving through the checkpoint in September. I know you didn't sell the same count to the garrison." The old man coughed, a deep wet sound he seemed barely interested in. "A boy who chooses his customers is not a boy who betrays them. Sit down, Ferren."

He hadn't told them his name.

He sat anyway, on a stool by the stone, because his legs had decided the matter without consulting him. The girl — Tomás, Isandro, whoever she was tonight — stood with her back against the type cases, arms crossed, watching him the way you'd watch weather.

"Here's my count," Ferren said. "Since August: eleven families gone out of this quarter, nine with papers nobody asks where they came from. Two taken instead, because their papers were slow. The commandant's office has a list of everyone who's left without authorization — I've seen the clerk writing it, I sweep near his window. Every name on that list goes in a book. And your ledger has the same names in it, plus the ones still here." He looked at the girl. "When they take your ledger in two days, they won't get names. They'll get a map. Of everyone you helped, everyone who owes you, everyone who might help you again."

"That's our problem," she said.

"It's mine too if I'm on it," Ferren said. "I've run your papers. I've watched your window forty nights. The neighbor women talk about me in front of me like I'm furniture — furniture hears everything. When they come for you, and they will, because the baker's wife already told a corporal that Underhill prints 'anything for anybody,' they'll ask who else comes around." He spread his hands. "I'd rather they ask me as a friend than as a witness."

The word hung there. *Friend.* He hadn't meant to say it. He'd meant to say *customer*. He watched her hear it, watched something flicker behind her eyes that wasn't calculation, and hated himself a little for the flicker, because flickers were how you ended up dead in Vellacourt like Anselme, trusting the wrong column to balance.

"A berth," she said. "On the evacuation train, if there is one. There may not be. There may be no train at all. I can't promise what doesn't exist."

"You forge permits for people to leave a town that doesn't exist anymore," Ferren said. "Promising things that don't exist is the whole trade. I'll take the promise."

The old man laughed — a short, cracked sound that turned into coughing, and the girl was at his side at once, one hand on his shoulder, and Ferren watched the way her whole body changed when she touched him, the borrowed boy falling away for a second like a coat slipping off a hook.

"Father," she said. "You should be asleep."

"I should be many things." The old man waved her off and fixed his clear, ruinous eyes on Ferren. "Boy. Do you understand what you're selling? Not the berth. The silence. Once you sell it, you own a share of every risk in this room. If they take her, they take you, because you knew. If they take you, they find her, because you'll talk — not because you're weak, but because everyone talks eventually, it's the one thing every army in history has proven." He laid one ink-stained finger on the composing stone. "So think past the money. Think about whether you want your name in this shop's books. Because once it's in, it's in lead. Lead doesn't erase."

Ferren looked at the stone. At the cases of type, thousands upon thousands of letters, each one a small metal decision that had survived being melted down and recast and survived again. At the girl standing between him and her father with her arms still crossed, but differently now — not guarding against him, he thought, just holding herself together.

Nobody had ever offered to write his name anywhere.

"My name's Ferren," he said, uselessly, and then, because the old man was waiting for an answer and because four hundred and thirteen days of arithmetic deserved at least one honest sum: "Everyone talks. Fine. So don't let them take me. Put me on the list, teach me what to carry, what to say at checkpoints — Anselme died because he only knew how to count, not how to lie. Teach me the lying part." He stood up. "Then I'm not a witness. I'm staff."

The girl stared at him. For the first time since the door had opened, the performance dropped entirely — and underneath it she was just tired, and young, and carrying more than any cart in the occupation.

"You're eleven," she said.

"I'm eleven and I count better than the garrison clerk," Ferren said. "Age is the one thing about me nobody's ever paid for."

The old man smiled, and it cost him, and he paid it gladly. "Ink," he said to his daughter. "Get the boy's name down properly. If we're keeping a ledger of the damned, we may as well keep it accurate."

* * *

She wrote it herself, in the big book, in her dead brother's slanted hand: *F. — runner. Owed: one passage. Paid: discretion.*

"Discretion," Ferren read upside down. "Is that what we're calling it?"

"That's what the book calls it," she said. "What we call it at checkpoints is 'nothing to declare.'"

She blew out the lamp, and in the dark the shop smelled of metal and oil, and Ferren walked home through streets he suddenly counted differently — not counting exits anymore, or patrols, but counting names: the ones in the ledger, the ones on the commandant's list, the ones she held in her head behind those steady frightened eyes, and his own name, newly set among them, one letter at a time.

Two days later the soldiers came up the rue des Casses with a requisition order and a canvas sack for paper, and Ferren, watching from the chandlery awning, began — for the first time in his life — counting down.
