## 7. Ink Under the Fingernails

The knock came at half past seven, before the shutters were even down.

Isandro heard it through the floor, where she knelt among the type cases with rice paper drying across her knees. Eleven names so far, written in a hand she'd practiced until it looked like nobody's hand at all — the clerk's script, bland and official, the kind of writing that belonged to no one because it belonged to everyone. She slid the sheets beneath the bottom tray of the job case, pressed the lid down over them, and stood. Her knees cracked. The ink was still wet on her right thumb.

Upstairs, her father had already opened the door.

She came up the stairs fast but not too fast — Tomás never hurried for soldiers, Tomás found them tedious — and arrived in the shop just as three of them entered: a lieutenant she didn't know, two privates with rifles slung loose, and behind all of them, holding a clipboard against his chest like a hymnal, a corporal with a face like a closed ledger.

"Print shop," the lieutenant said, as if confirming it to himself. He walked the room the way a man walks a room he intends to own. He ran one gloved finger along the composing stone and examined it in the light. "Underhill."

"Tomás Underhill." Her father's voice was steady. He sat in his chair by the stove with a blanket over his knees that hadn't been there an hour ago — he'd put it on deliberately, Isandro realized, dressing himself in frailty the way another man might dress in medals. "You'll want to see the notice posted outside. I've read it. All records surrendered within three days. I keep a shop ledger, Lieutenant, as every business does. It will be handed over."

"The ledger," the lieutenant said. "And your stock."

"My stock is lead and ink. Heavy and worthless. You're welcome to both."

The lieutenant smiled without warmth. "We'll see what it's worth." He turned. "Search it. Shelves, drawers, the press cabinet. Paper especially. Blank forms, permits, anything with an official seal."

Isandro kept her hands folded and her face bored. *Bored.* That was the whole performance, and she'd learned it well these past months: a young man who found soldiers tiresome, whose shoulders slouched with the particular insolence of someone who had never once been afraid. Inside, under the boredom, her heart was going like a press run gone wrong — sheet after sheet after sheet, none of them straight.

The rice paper was downstairs. Eleven names. Twelve, if you counted old Wrenna Halvard, whom she'd added last night because Wrenna's son had come at midnight smelling of lamp oil and fear. Beneath the bottom tray of the job case. Three steps behind her, past the composing stone, down the narrow stair.

They would not find them. They could not.

A private pulled open the drawer of the standing case near the door. Another went along the shelf where the blank permit stock lived — she'd hidden those weeks ago inside hollow sorts, brass cylinders bored out and filled with rolled papers, sitting in plain sight in their compartment like any other type. But the rice paper, the names —

The lieutenant drifted toward the stairs.

"They're steep," her father said. "I'd offer to guide you down, Lieutenant, but my legs aren't what—"

"I can manage stairs."

"He fell once," Isandro said. Her own voice surprised her — flat, unbothered, Tomás to the bone. "Last winter. Cracked two ribs. There's a low beam halfway down. We keep meaning to plane it."

The lieutenant glanced at her, and something in her chest went very cold and very still, because he was looking at her the way men sometimes looked at Tomás — a half-second too long, cataloguing the jaw, the hands, the throat. Then the moment passed, or seemed to, and he went down the stairs anyway, ducking under the beam without looking for it, which meant he hadn't believed her, which meant nothing, which meant everything.

She counted his footsteps below. Seven. He stopped. Silence — the silence of a man reading shelves.

Her father's hand found hers where they stood by the stove. His grip was still strong; that was the terrible thing about him now, how much strength the illness left him on some days, enough to hold on with, enough to make you believe — and his thumb moved once across her knuckles. *Steady,* the gesture said. *Whatever happens, steady.*

Below, a drawer scraped. Metal clinked — type cases being shifted. The rice paper lay under the bottom tray of the job case, and the job case was the third along the wall, and if he lifted the trays one by one, top to bottom, he would reach it in under a minute.

Isandro began, very quietly, to plan how she would kill him.

Not with violence — she'd never held anything heavier than a quoin key — but she thought, in a cold clear thread, about the lamp oil, the press, the fire that would take the shop and every name in it rather than let the names be used as a list of the hunted. She thought it completely, calmly, the way her father must have thought about things when he decided, nineteen years ago, to bury his daughter's name and raise his son's. Some arithmetic you do once and carry forever.

The footsteps stopped again. A long pause. Then, from below, the lieutenant's voice, muffled: "There are papers here."

Her father did not tighten his grip. That was the masterstroke of it — he let go. Turned toward the privates with the slow irritation of an honest man interrupted. "Those are proofs," he called down. "Galley proofs. Job work. Wedding invitations, mostly. Half this town is getting married off the back of my press, God help them. Bring up a stack if you want to read love letters, Private, but mind the ink, it stains."

The private looked at the lieutenant's empty post at the foot of the stairs, then at Isandro, uncertain.

"Leave them," Isandro said. "He'll only count them."

It was the right note — the bored heir, embarrassed by his father's fussing — and it bought four seconds, five, while the private hesitated and the lieutenant below stood with the job case open and the truth under his glove.

Then her father coughed.

It started small, the way it always did now, a dry tick in the chest — but she saw it catch, saw it deepen, saw the color leave his face in the space between one breath and the next, and then he was coughing in earnest, doubled over the blanket, and when he brought the handkerchief away from his mouth there was blood on it, fresh and bright, more than yesterday, and he made no attempt at all to hide it. He held it there, open-handed, bloody linen displayed like a document.

"Gentlemen," he said, when he could speak. His voice was wrecked and papery. "If one of you would be kind enough to fetch me a glass of water, I'll thank you. If not, kindly finish your search and let a dying man get back to his chair."

Nobody fetched water. Nobody finished the search either. The lieutenant came up the stairs fast, and whatever he'd seen below he carried on his face unreadable, and he looked first at the handkerchief, then at her father's gray lips, then at Isandro, and something passed over his expression that she would spend the rest of her life failing to name — not pity exactly, not shame, but the look of a man who has been shown something he didn't ask to see and cannot unknow.

"We're done here," he said. "The ledger, within three days."

"You'll have it tomorrow," her father said. "Tonight, if it speeds you. I've nothing left to hide in this world, Lieutenant. Ask anyone."

They went. The door closed. The boots faded down the cobbles toward the square.

Isandro got her father into the chair and knelt in front of him and took the handkerchief from his hand, and for a moment neither of them said anything, because the shop was very quiet and the blood on the linen was very red.

"You knew," she said finally. "About the beam. You never fell. There is no low beam."

"There is a low beam," her father said. A ghost of a smile. "Your grandfather cracked his head on it twice." He closed his eyes. Opened them. "Did he find them?"

"No."

"Then we're even, you and I. I lied for you today. You've lied for me since March." He touched her cheek — her real cheek, the one that belonged to no brother anywhere — with fingers stained faintly black at the cuticle, ink under the fingernails, forty years of it, ground in past washing. "That's what this family does. We lie in ink. It's the only honest lying there is."

* * *

Dr. Vasseur came at noon, climbing the stairs two at a time out of habit and then pausing on the landing to catch his breath out of age, which was its own kind of arithmetic.

He listened to her father's chest for a long time. He asked questions in a low voice, some of which Isandro answered and some of which her father answered for himself, waving the doctor's concern aside like a fly. At the end Vasseur came into the kitchen and sat down heavily and accepted tea he didn't drink, and Isandro stood at the window with her arms crossed, waiting, wearing Tomás's impatience like a coat over the thing underneath.

"How long," she said. Not a question. Tomás would have asked it that way — blunt, unsentimental, a man who wanted facts. The voice helped. The voice was load-bearing.

Vasseur turned the cup in his hands. "Weeks," he said. "Not months. I won't insult you with a range. The lung is compromised and the coughing brings the bleeding, and each time it does less comes back. He knows. He's known longer than I have, I think. Men who work with numbers always know the sum before they admit the column."

"He covered for me today." The words came out before she could stop them, and once out they kept coming. "There were papers downstairs. My fault entirely, mine alone, and he stood up and bled at them on purpose, Vasseur. He spent blood like money to buy thirty seconds, and it worked, and I don't—" Her voice cracked, one hairline fracture, and she hated it and pressed on. "I don't know what to do with that. There isn't anywhere to put it."

Vasseur looked at her for a long moment, and she understood that he saw through Tomás entirely, and always had, and had never once let it show, and that this was its own kind of covering-for, its own quiet forgery, and that the town was full of people committing it on her behalf.

"Put it here," he said, tapping his own chest. "Where you keep everything else you can't spend. And then do what he'd want, which is the work. He told me last month — made me swear discretion — that whatever you're doing at night, he's proud of it, and that if it kills him to help, it's the best trade he ever made." He rose, joints crackling. "Rest him. Feed him. Don't argue with him about dying; it wastes the weeks."

At the door he paused. "One more thing. The commission billets at the Hôtel de la Gare starting Thursday. Negotiators, clerks, the whole apparatus. There's a clerk among them — Delacroix, gray beard, spectacles — who ordered type from your father years ago, for the prefecture. Your father set his wedding announcements, if you can believe it." Vasseur almost smiled. "Small world. Small towns. Keep your head down anyway."

Then he was gone, and the house was quiet, and Isandro stood in the kitchen with her father's death folded up inside her like wet paper.

* * *

She went downstairs that night and took out the rice paper and read the twelve names by candlelight, and then she added a thirteenth at the bottom, though it wasn't a name yet, only a space, ruled and waiting:

**UNDERHILL, T.**

Because if the lists were coming — if the town was becoming paperwork, if people were turning into entries that could be seized and carried away — then her father would go on one of them someday soon, and she would not have him vanish unwritten. Whatever else happened. Whatever it cost.

She blotted the ink. Upstairs, she could hear him coughing, stopping, coughing again, and between the fits a sound she knew was worse than any of it: the small rhythmic tap of a composing stick against a case, working, always working, setting type he would likely never live to see printed.

She blew out the candle and sat a while in the dark with her thumb pressed against her own fingernail, checking, the way she did now without thinking, for ink.

Three days, the lieutenant had said. The ledger was due tomorrow. And tonight, for the first time, she let herself think the thought whole: that the ledger wasn't evidence of her crimes at all.

It was a list of everyone she had saved — and tomorrow she would have to hand it to the men hunting them.
