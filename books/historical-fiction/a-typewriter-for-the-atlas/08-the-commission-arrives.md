## 8. The Commission Arrives

They came on a Tuesday, in three grey automobiles that moved through the town gate at the pace of a funeral, which was appropriate enough, since half the town had already held funerals for people who were not dead and might never be.

Isandro heard them before she saw them — the wrong kind of engine note, too smooth for an army lorry — and set down her composing stick with a care that cost her something. In the window of Underhill & Son, Printers, the morning light lay across the type cases like a hand laid flat to check for fever.

"They're here," Mateo said from the back room, where he'd been washing ink off his hands for the better part of ten minutes because he did not want to come out.

"I know."

"You can feel it. The whole street changed."

The whole street *had* changed. It had gone still in that particular way towns go still when something official enters them — shutters angled a degree wider, faces arranged into expressions of dull compliance, every man suddenly very interested in his own boots. Isandro had watched it happen when the requisition officers came. She watched it again now, and noted the difference: this time the fear was quieter, more ashamed. People were not afraid of being robbed today. They were afraid of being *noticed*.

The automobiles stopped outside the requisition office, the one that now held the town's ledger in its archive room, locked behind a door Isandro had spent four sleepless nights imagining. Officers stepped out first — she counted them out of habit, out of Ferren's habit, really, because the boy had taught her to count without meaning to — and then men in civilian clothes, soft-hatted, soft-voiced, carrying document cases the way priests carry reliquaries.

The commission. The actual commission. The men who would sign the paper that ended the war or didn't, who would draw lines across the border country with rulers borrowed from schoolchildren, and whose signature would decide whether the evacuation train ran at all.

"They'll billet here," Mateo said. He had come to stand beside her at the window, close enough that she could smell the solvent on him, and she did not move away because moving away had become impossible sometime around chapter five of their acquaintance, though neither of them had said so. "Officers always take the good houses. The clerks will take the rest."

"Then we'll have clerks sleeping above the bakery and across from the church," Isandro said. "And every one of them walking past this window twice a day."

"Is that a problem or an opportunity?"

She looked at him. He had learned to ask questions like that — questions that sounded innocent and weren't, questions that showed he understood exactly what she did after dark. It was a dangerous education she'd given him. She kept giving it anyway.

"It depends," she said, "on whether any of them can read backwards."

* * *

Father heard the engines from his bed upstairs and insisted on being told everything twice, once as fact and once as feeling, because his memory now filed things in two different drawers and only sometimes opened both.

"Commission," he repeated, propped against pillows gone yellow with age and illness. His hands lay on the blanket like two pieces of type someone had sorted badly — still recognizable, no longer useful. "Underhill type has been in commission offices before, you know. Nineteen-eleven. A delegation came through buying specimen sheets. One of them — a young fellow, glasses, wrote his name in my ledger in a hand like a spider had drowned in the inkwell —"

He stopped. The drawers opened.

"Vasseur," Father said. "Aubin Vasseur. He bought a fount of Baskerville and paid too much for it because I told him it was cast in Lyon and he wanted it to be true." A cough took him, long and wet, and Isandro sat very still until it passed, holding the cup of water he would not drink yet because drinking meant the coughing had won. "He said print was the only honest profession left in Europe. I liked him. I shouldn't have, but I liked him."

"D'you think he's come back?"

"He's come back or he hasn't. But if he has—" Father's eyes found hers, and there was a lucidity in them that frightened her more than the confusion did, because lucid Father saw things. "If he has, then there is a man inside that office who knows what our type looks like. Who knows what a genuine Underhill job looks like next to a forged one."

Isandro kept her face level. She had gotten good at that. Two years of wearing her brother's name had made her face into a well-set line: nothing loose, nothing bleeding at the edges.

"The permits are good work," she said. "You taught me."

"The permits are *your* work," Father said, "which is better than mine ever was, which is precisely the problem. A man who remembers my type will look at yours and see me looking back at him."

* * *

The billeting notices went up by noon. Isandro read them in the crowd, standing on the balls of her feet like a boy craning at a hanging, and copied them into memory the way she copied everything now — fast, exact, permanent.

Two delegates lodged at the Hôtel des Voyageurs. Three staff officers in the mayor's house. And the support staff — clerks, stenographers, a cartographer, two archivists — distributed among private houses along the rue Basse, including number eleven, directly across from the print shop.

Number eleven belonged to Madame Roux, who made candles and asked no questions of anyone, which was why Isandro had sold her three forged departure passes at prices that embarrassed them both. Now a commission clerk would eat Madame Roux's candle-scented stew and sleep under her roof and walk past the window where Isandro stood every morning cutting type in her dead brother's shirts.

She went home and told Mateo and Ferren, all three of them crowded into the shop's back room where the light wouldn't reach the window.

"A clerk named Vasseur," she said. "Possibly. My father sold him type eight years ago. If he remembers the shop—"

"If he remembers the shop fondly, he's an asset," Mateo said. "If he remembers it *accurately*, we're finished."

Ferren sat on the flour sack by the stove, knees up, watching them the way he watched everything: with the total, unnerving attention of a child who had learned that information was food. He was twelve now, or thirteen; nobody knew, including Ferren. He hadn't spoken much since the executions in the square last spring, but he spoke plenty otherwise, and what he said now was:

"Clerks count things. That's all they do. I watch them from the wall. They count wagons and write numbers and the numbers go in boxes." He drew a box in the air with one finger. "If you want a clerk to help you, you don't ask him to break a rule. You ask him to fix a mistake. Clerks hate mistakes worse than they love rules."

Isandro looked at the boy and felt the old cold move through her, the cold that was also admiration. Someday, if the world allowed it, this child would run something — a shop, a syndicate, a small nation — and God help everyone involved.

"And how," she asked carefully, "would I know whether Monsieur Vasseur thinks we're a mistake?"

Ferren shrugged. "You make one and see if he comes to correct it."

* * *

So she made one.

It took three days of nerve. On the fourth day, a printed notice came down from the requisition office requiring all households within the commission's billeting quarter to display updated registration cards — new forms, new format, issued by the commission itself — and Isandro saw her opening lying in the street like a dropped purse.

Registration cards required a printer. The occupying command had its own press, but the commission, in its civilian finery, had contracted local services for the card stock — and the order, delivered by a bored corporal who clearly resented the errand, specified *Underhill & Son, rue Basse, per prior arrangement.*

Prior arrangement. Eight years old and still on file somewhere in some archive, in a drowned-spider hand: *type supplied to Underhill & Son, satisfactory.*

She printed the cards herself. That was the trap and the gift of it — the commission wanted her hands on their paper, her type in their registry, and every instinct she owned screamed both *yes* and *no* at volumes that made her ears ring. She printed them beautifully. She printed them so beautifully that any clerk with a loupe would see the work of a master and know immediately that a master lived at this address, which was fine, which was intended, because Tomás Underhill was supposed to be a master. What she could not risk was a closer reading. What she could not survive was Aubin Vasseur setting his remembered specimen sheet beside her card stock and noticing the small differences — the slightly deeper impression, the kerning decisions that were hers and not her father's — and doing arithmetic with them.

She delivered the cards personally. Of course she did. She put on Tomás's best coat, the one with the padded shoulders, bound her chest flat beneath it, rubbed a little burnt cork along her jaw to suggest the shadow of a shave, and walked into the requisition office carrying a portfolio of finished work like an offering.

The corridor smelled of pencil shavings and cold tea. Soldiers stood at intervals, rifles slung, eyes forward. At the end of it, a door stood open on a room stacked with document cases, and at a table before the window sat a thin man of fifty-odd with spectacles and a pen behind each ear, sorting papers into piles with the tenderness other men reserved for children.

"Monsieur Vasseur?" Isandro said, and her voice held at Tomás's pitch, low and even, the voice she'd built over two years out of practice and grief.

He looked up. Behind the spectacles, his eyes were pale and quick and entirely without cruelty, which in her experience was either the best thing or the worst thing a bureaucrat's eyes could be.

"Yes?"

"Tomás Underhill. Your card stock."

He took the portfolio. He examined the top card for perhaps four seconds — she counted them, counting was Ferren's gift to her too — and she watched his face do nothing at all, which terrified her more than any expression could have.

"This impression," he said. "It's deeper than your father's work."

Her blood went to ice water. "I cut the type myself these days. My father's hands—" She let the sentence die, which was truthful, and truth read as innocence.

"Ah." Vasseur set the card down and removed his spectacles and polished them on his sleeve, and while he did it he said, conversationally, to the middle distance: "Nineteen-eleven. Lyon. Your father told me the Baskerville was cast there and I believed him because I wanted to. I found out later it was his own casting, his own alloy. I have often thought about that. About how a lie can be the most honest thing in a transaction, if both parties agree to need it."

Isandro said nothing. Her heart was performing percussion.

"He's ill, your father?"

"He is dying," Isandro said. Flatly. Because the flatness was armor and because it was true.

Vasseur put his spectacles back on. He signed the delivery chit without checking the count — *without checking the count*, and she would remember that for the rest of her life — and slid it across the table.

"Satisfactory," he said, and the word landed strangely, like an echo of a ledger entry, like a password exchanged between people who had agreed to need the same lie. Then, as she turned to go: "Underhill. The registration lists. There was a town ledger surrendered in October. Names, payments, debts, favors. You'll recall your father kept meticulous books."

Every joint in her body unlocked and refroze in the same instant.

"I recall," she managed.

"When the commission reviews requisitioned property — and we review everything, that is the entire purpose of us — ledgers of that quality are treated as evidence. Not against the town." He was looking at her again, those quick pale eyes. "Against the army that took it. Property seized becomes property accountable. Persons recorded become persons owed. Do you understand me?"

"No," said Isandro, who understood perfectly.

"Good," said Aubin Vasseur, and returned to his papers.

* * *

She crossed the street on legs that belonged to somebody else. Inside the shop, Mateo took one look at her and put the kettle on without asking, which was its own kind of language, and Ferren appeared from the back room with the silent suddenness he'd perfected.

"Well?" Mateo said.

"He knows the ledger exists. He called it evidence against the army. He said persons recorded become persons owed." She sat down hard on the composing stone, which no compositor should ever do, and heard her father's ghost object. "Mateo, he checked nothing. He signed for two hundred cards without counting them. Either he's careless—"

"He's not careless. Clerks aren't careless. Ferren says."

"Ferren says clerks hate mistakes," Isandro said. "So maybe I'm the mistake he's decided not to correct. Maybe he's known since 1911 that Underhills lie kindly about their type, and maybe he's just decided to keep needing the lie." She pressed the heels of her hands into her eyes. "Or maybe he's building a case and wants me comfortable."

The kettle began its climb toward whistling. Ferren climbed onto the stool opposite her, folded his arms, and regarded her with the gravity of a magistrate.

"He gave you a message," Ferren said.

"What message?"

"'Persons recorded become persons owed.' That's not talk. That's a promise with the shape of paperwork." The boy tapped the composing stone with one grimy finger. "He's telling you the ledger isn't lost. It's *filed*. And filed means findable, if you get to him before the filing gets to the archive."

Outside, across the rue Basse, a light came on in the upstairs window of Madame Roux's house at number eleven, and a thin silhouette moved past it, arranging papers by lamplight, a man sorting the world into piles.

Isandro watched him through the glass and felt the thing she had been warned her whole life to distrust: hope. Dangerous, specific, addressable hope, with a name and a window and a pair of spectacles — hope that could be killed with one denunciation, one search warrant, one honest clerk doing his duty.

But the train list was being drawn. The armistice would be signed within weeks. And somewhere in that lamplit room sat the only thread that ran from her father's dying hands to the names in the stolen book.

She had spent two years forging documents that opened doors.

Tomorrow, she decided, she would forge one that opened a window — a small, polite, printed request for clarification of the registration requirements, delivered by hand to number eleven, addressed in a hand like a spider drowned in the inkwell, because if Aubin Vasseur remembered her father's ledger, then let him remember it one entry further: *Underhill & Son, correspondence received, satisfactory.*
