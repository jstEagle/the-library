## 9. Berths on the Last Train

The rumor arrived the way most true things did in San Vero: carried in a bread loaf.

Mateo was pulling the morning's proofs when Ferren slipped through the back door with a loaf under his arm and a look on his face that Mateo had learned to read the way he read a galley of type — quickly, and with a certain dread. The boy set the loaf on the composing stone, tore it open, and extracted from its middle a scrap of paper no bigger than a playing card.

"Bakery man says it came up the river," Ferren said. "From the commission's own boat."

Mateo took the paper. The handwriting was hurried but not frightened — that distinction mattered now; they had all become scholars of handwriting — and it said: *One train. Before signing. Berths by command list only. No names added after Thursday.*

He read it twice. Then he went to the doorway where Isandro stood sorting leads, her brother's cap pulled low, and held the paper out without a word.

She read it the way she read everything: once fast, then again slow, her lips moving slightly on the second pass as if she were setting each word in type before she allowed it to mean anything.

"One train," she said.

"That's what it says."

"Before signing." She set the paper down on the stone, weighted its corner with a quooin, as though even a rumor ought to be kept flat and orderly. "How many berths?"

Ferren shrugged. "Bakery man didn't say. He said lists."

"Lists drawn by whom?"

"The command," Ferren said. "Occupying command. He said names get posted on the customs house door Thursday morning, and the train leaves Friday at first bell."

Mateo watched Isandro do what she always did when the world handed her arithmetic instead of answers: she reached for the abacus of her own family. He could almost see the beads moving behind her eyes. Father. Herself. Himself — and here her gaze flicked to him and away again, quick as a compositor's hand slipping a sort into place. And Ferren, standing there with crumbs on his shirt and no name on any register in the town, because orphans in San Vero were like apostrophes — present, necessary, and officially unrecorded.

"Thursday," she said. "That gives us two days."

"Two days for what?" Ferren asked.

Neither of them answered him. It was becoming a habit, Mateo thought, this silence around the boy — protective and cruel in equal measure, like a blanket pulled too tight.

* * *

By noon the rumor had hardened into fact, which in San Vero happened not through official announcement but through repetition. The baker said it. The ferryman confirmed it. Old Marta, who had been wrong about every event since the war began, was right about this one, and being right made her insufferable and terrified in alternating sentences.

A notice went up on the customs house door at three o'clock, pasted by a soldier who worked with the brisk indifference of a man hanging a poster for a fair. Mateo joined the crowd gathered beneath it and copied the text into his memory the way Isandro had taught him — not word by word but line by line, each line as a block, so the whole could be recomposed later.

*BY ORDER OF THE OCCUPYING COMMAND: ONE EVACUATION TRAIN WILL DEPART SAN VERO PRIOR TO THE SIGNING OF THE ARMISTICE. BERTHS ARE LIMITED. PASSENGERS SHALL BE DESIGNATED BY LIST. NO APPLICATIONS WILL BE RECEIVED. THE LIST IS FINAL.*

No applications will be received. Mateo turned the phrase over as he walked back down the hill. It had the clean, closed quality of all the occupation's language — a door shut so smoothly you couldn't even find the seam to put your knife in. You could forge a permit. You could forge a travel pass, a requisition exemption, a burial certificate. But you could not forge your way onto a train whose manifest already existed in some clerk's strongbox, typed and sealed, its ink drier than anything Mateo would ever set himself.

Unless.

He stopped halfway down the hill, struck by the thought the way you're struck by a misprint in a page you've proofread four times — suddenly obvious, suddenly terrible.

You couldn't forge your way onto the list. But if the list was printed anywhere — if the commission's clerks ran off copies, if the command needed manifests duplicated for the conductors, the stationmaster, the archive —

Then somebody would have to print it.

And in San Vero, there was one press.

* * *

He found Isandro in the shop, standing before the case drawer where she'd hidden the rice-paper copies of the ledger, her hand resting on the wood the way you'd rest a hand on a sleeping animal you weren't sure you wanted to wake.

"I've been thinking," Mateo said.

"Don't." She didn't turn around. "I can hear you thinking from across the room, and I don't like the sound of it."

"They'll need copies of the berth list. Manifests. Maybe a dozen, maybe fifty — the conductor gets one, the station gets one, the commission keeps three for the archive. Clerks don't write things out by hand anymore, Isandro, not when there's a press sitting idle in a town they've already requisitioned. They'll bring their own printer, maybe. Or—"

"Or they'll come here." She turned. In the afternoon light her face looked the way the type metal looked just before it was poured — bright, liquid, and about to be shaped by forces she hadn't chosen. "They'll walk through that door and ask Tomás Underhill to set the list of people leaving San Vero. And Tomás Underhill will see every name before anyone else does."

"And know who isn't on it."

"And know who isn't on it." She sat down heavily on the composing stool, and for a moment she wasn't the forger, wasn't Tomás, wasn't the steady center around which the whole fragile network turned — she was just a tired woman in her dead brother's clothes, counting. "Father can't travel. You've seen him. The doctor says weeks, and a train is not weeks' worth of mercy — the jolting alone. If his name is even considered, and it won't be, because the command doesn't list printers they've already interrogated—" She stopped herself. "Me. They might list me. Tomás Underhill, master printer, useful tradesman, cooperative subject. There's a version of the arithmetic where my name goes on that list and I'm expected to be grateful."

"There is," Mateo agreed carefully.

"But yours doesn't. Apprentices aren't heads of household. And Ferren's doesn't exist anywhere except in the ledger we lost." She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "Three of us who are a family in every way that matters, and the number comes out to one. One, Mateo. Do you understand? Whatever happens Thursday, the mathematics has already decided, and the mathematics says one."

Mateo crouched in front of her so their faces were level. Her hands smelled of ink and lamp oil and, faintly, of the rice paper she'd been handling for weeks — a smell he'd come to associate with fear, and with her.

"Then we change the mathematics," he said.

"How?"

"I don't know yet. But you taught me that a locked form is just a form nobody's taken apart. Every lock has a furniture joint. Every list has a margin."

She lowered her hands and looked at him, and something passed between them that neither of them named, because naming it would have required a vocabulary larger than type provided, and they were both, in their different ways, people who trusted objects more than words.

* * *

That evening Father asked to be carried to the shop.

It was the first time he'd left his bed in nine days, and the request alarmed them all until they understood its shape: he didn't want to die in the bedroom upstairs, with its water-stained ceiling and its view of nothing. He wanted to die among the cases, within reach of the smell of ink, in the room where he'd spent forty years turning other people's grief and joy and commerce into something permanent.

Isandro and Mateo got him down the stairs between them, his arms over their shoulders, his feet finding the treads with the stubborn precision of a man who had climbed these stairs drunk, exhausted, and grieving for thirty years and did not intend to fail at it now. They settled him in the old armchair beside the press, the one with the springs gone that everyone pretended still worked, and draped a blanket over his knees, and for a while nobody spoke. The shop at dusk was its own kind of church. The type cases glowed dull silver in the last light. Somewhere outside, a patrol's boots marked time on the cobbles, four beats and a turn.

"You've heard about the train," Father said at last. It wasn't a question. Nothing in San Vero stayed unheard longer than half a day.

"We've heard," Isandro said.

"And the list."

"Yes."

Father nodded slowly, working it through, and Mateo realized with a small shock that the old man was doing exactly what his daughter did — running the beads of the same abacus, arriving at the same merciless sum.

"One berth," Father said.

"It may be more than one. We don't know the size of the list."

"We know." He coughed, softly, into the handkerchief, and folded it away with the practiced discretion of a man managing his own death like an account to be balanced. "Lists drawn by occupying commands are never generous. One berth. Perhaps two, if the commission wants to look humane in the record." He looked from Isandro to Mateo and back again, and his eyes, clouded as they were, still held the exacting clarity of a proofreader. "So. Who goes?"

"Father—"

"No." His voice cracked like a rule being laid down, the voice he'd used in the shop for forty years, the voice that had corrected Mateo's spacing and Isandro's kerning and both their characters, letter by letter. "We will do this properly. In this house we do not lie about numbers. Numbers are the one honest thing left in this town. So: who goes?"

Silence. The boots outside turned their corner and faded.

"Tomás goes," Father said finally. "If the name is on the list, Tomás goes. That is not a discussion."

"And if it isn't?" Isandro asked quietly.

"Then we make sure it is. You have friends in strange places, son. Use them." He said *son* without a flicker, the way he always had, the way he had since the day — Mateo had never learned exactly which day — when the truth had entered the house and been absorbed into its foundations like damp into stone. "And the rest of us—" He paused, and Mateo saw him choose, deliberately, to be brief rather than true. "The rest of us will manage. We have managed worse."

They had not, in fact, managed worse. But Mateo understood that this was the currency fathers paid in: confident falsehoods, minted in the shop, spendable nowhere else.

Father slept in the armchair that night. They covered him with the good blanket and let him stay, and Isandro dimmed the lamp, and in the dark Mateo heard her whisper, barely aloud, the way you'd speak to a form of type you were checking against the galley:

"One berth. God help me. One."

* * *

Wednesday passed in the particular agony of waiting rooms and border towns: everyone busy, no one productive. The command posted a supplementary notice clarifying that the list would include "essential civilian personnel" as determined by the office of the chief quartermaster, which sent a ripple of desperate speculation through the town — was a baker essential? A doctor, certainly. A midwife, probably. A printer?

Mateo spent the day doing ordinary jobs badly. He washed the press rollers and let them dry too long. He distributed type and put it back in the wrong cases. Twice Ferren caught him staring at nothing and asked him what was wrong, and twice he said *nothing*, which was the largest lie he'd told all year, and Ferren knew it, and let it go, because the boy had learned when questions cost more than silence.

In the late afternoon, Ferren came back from the customs house with intelligence.

"The clerk," he said, sliding onto the workbench, legs swinging. "The one who bought type before. He's been sent to the station. He's making up the manifest boards himself — big ones, for the platform, showing who boards and in what car. He requisitioned paint and board from the carpenter. But the actual lists, the paper ones—" Ferren grinned, and it was a terrible grin, too knowing for a face that young. "The paper ones get run tonight. On the commission's field press. They brought a little platen job in a crate, army issue, prints a thousand an hour."

"A field press," Mateo repeated.

"So you can't volunteer," Ferren said. "I figured that's what you were chewing on yesterday. Thought you should stop chewing."

Mateo looked at the boy — this ragged, unregistered child who had blackmailed his way into their lives and then, without ceremony, become indispensable — and felt the full weight of the arithmetic settle onto his chest. Because here was the truth he'd been circling for two days: Ferren's name was on no list, existed on no paper, and the only document in the world that could give him a claim was the ledger the soldiers had taken. The boy who had counted soldiers was himself uncounted.

"Ferren," Mateo said. "When the list posts tomorrow. If you don't see your name—"

"I won't see my name." Flat. No self-pity in it at all, which was worse. "Orphans don't evacuate. Orphans get left. Everybody knows that. Even the orphans know it." He hopped off the bench. "I just wanted to tell you about the field press so you'd stop looking like that. Looking like that doesn't help anybody."

He went out through the back, and Mateo stood alone in the shop with the presses and the cases and the dying man asleep in the corner, and understood that the question was no longer whether Isandro would sacrifice something. The question was how many ways she'd find to do it, and whether any of them would leave enough of her behind to recognize.

* * *

Thursday morning came gray and windless. By seven, a crowd had formed at the customs house before the notice was even pasted — the whole town drawn by the gravity of a single sheet of paper, the way iron filings answer a magnet.

Mateo went with Isandro. She wore Tomás's coat, Tomás's cap, Tomás's deliberate stride, and Mateo walked beside her carrying a delivery satchel as cover, because a printer's apprentice hauling proofs was invisible; the town had long ago learned not to see either of them clearly.

The clerk from the commission — Herr Vossel, the one who remembered buying type from Underhill, the one who had watched Isandro's interrogation in silence — posted the list himself. He worked slowly, smoothing each sheet with the flat of his hand, a craftsman's gesture, and Mateo hated him a little for it. Carelessness you could forgive. Precision, in the service of this, was obscene.

The crowd pressed forward. Names were read aloud in a rising, ragged chorus — someone reading for someone else, someone reading for a neighbor housebound with fever, voices crossing and tangling until the square sounded like a market of the doomed.

Mateo read down the list twice, fast, then slow, Isandro's method, and found:

*T. UNDERHILL, PRINTER — CAR 3, BERTH 11.*

One name. Not two. Not three. Father's name absent — the interrogated printer, the unreliable subject, crossed off whatever internal list had considered him. Mateo's name absent, as predicted, as inevitable. Ferren's name absent from the universe itself.

Beside him, Isandro stood very still. Too still. He had seen her calm during the checkpoint inspection, calm under the interrogation lamp, calm through nights of forging that would have hanged them all. This stillness was different. This was the stillness of someone watching a sum come out wrong and understanding, finally, that the error was hers to correct.

"Berth eleven," she said quietly. Just to him. Testing the weight of it. "Car three."

"Yes," Mateo said. "You take it."

She didn't answer. She was looking at the list, and then past it, at the rail line running east toward wherever the train would go — toward safety, toward the world after the war, toward all the places where a person could start over under a name that had never been forged. And Mateo watched her face and saw her doing the thing he dreaded most, the thing he'd seen her do with every permit and every name in the ledger:

She was deciding who deserved to live more than she did.

"No," he said, before she could speak. "Whatever you're composing in there — no. Don't set it."

"Mateo—"

"Promise me nothing," he said. "But don't decide tonight. Lists change. Margins have joints. You said so yourself."

She turned and looked at him then, really looked, and for one unguarded second Tomás fell away entirely and it was Isandro's eyes, Isandro's fear, Isandro's terrible tenderness meeting his own.

"The list is final," she said. "The notice says so."

"Notices," Mateo said, "are just type."

That night, after Father was asleep and the shop lamps were out, Mateo lay in his cot above the storehouse listening to the wind worry the shutters, and he heard the floorboards below shift — soft, deliberate, once, twice — the sound of someone moving through the dark toward the type cabinets, toward the rice paper, toward whatever forgery the arithmetic of one berth was about to demand.

He got up. He lit no lamp. And he went down the stairs after her, because whatever she meant to set in secret, she was not going to set it alone.
