## 10. The Stolen Ledger

The requisition office had been the town's granary once, back when grain was what armies wanted. Now it held paper instead, and Isandro thought there was no difference worth arguing about. Grain fed men. Paper fed them too — it told them who they were, who owned what, who could be taken. She had learned that at her father's composing stone before she was tall enough to reach it: *ink is a lever, mija. Mind which end you stand on.*

The occupying command stood on the wrong end, and they didn't know it yet.

She watched the building for three nights from the loft above Herr Wenzel's cooperage, wrapped in Tomás's coat with her father's cough still ringing in her ears from the evening's dose of laudanum. The first night she counted sentries. Two on the door, one making a circuit of the yard every eleven minutes by the church bell's habit of running slow. The second night it rained and the circuit stretched to fourteen minutes, because soldiers are only men, and men do not love standing in rain. The third night she went.

Ferren had drawn her a map in charcoal on the back of a flour sack — the boy counted everything, doors and hinges and the exact number of steps between the yard gate and the loading ramp, and his map was better than any surveyor's because Ferren drew only what mattered. A loose board behind the coal chute. A cellar stair that smelled of rot and was therefore unguarded. A window into the records room, painted over except where a pane had cracked and someone had papered it, and the paper had curled at one corner like a leaf in November.

"You come back," Ferren had said when he handed it over, gripping her sleeve with those small hard fingers. "Or I tell everyone Tomás Underhill is a liar."

"That's your blackmail?"

"I'm saving the good threats." His face in the lamplight was old and young at once, the way children's faces go after they've seen something they shouldn't have. "Come back."

She came back to that sentence more than once as she crouched in the coal dust, waiting out the sentry's fourteenth minute.

* * *

The cellar stair gave onto a corridor that had been the granary's weighing floor. Someone had strung electric wire down its length — the commission traveled with its own light, its own clocks, its own certainty — and a single bulb burned at the far end, humming. Isandro moved along the wall opposite, into the bulb's blind spot, and listened.

Above her, boots crossed a floor. Voices, low, unhurried. The language of clerks everywhere: filing, cross-referencing, the soft administrative music of an army that had learned to eat a country with forms.

The window with the cracked pane opened into the records room without a sound. She'd oiled the frame with tallow from the kitchen two days earlier, working it into the groove with a fingertip while Mateo kept watch below and pretended not to understand what she was doing. He understood. He always understood. That was the terror of him and the comfort of him both.

Inside, the smell hit her first: dust and glue and the particular sourness of cheap official ink. Then the scale of it. Filing cabinets in ranks, dozens of them, each drawer labeled in a hand so neat it looked printed. *Requisitions. Billetings. Transport. Personnel. Confiscations.* An entire town reduced to drawers, an entire war reduced to labels.

And on a long table under the one working lamp, the ledger.

Her father's ledger. She knew it the way you know your own heartbeat — the water-stained cover, the corner gnawed by the shop cat years ago, the spine cracked at the page where the year turned from 1916 to 1917. It lay open under the lamp as if it were being read right now, and maybe it had been, all day, all week, by some junior officer running his finger down columns of names and payments and favors, learning the town faster than the town knew itself.

Every name she had saved was in there.

Not marked as saved — nothing so kind. But the pattern was legible to anyone patient enough to read it backward. The families she'd forged permits for had paid the shop for ordinary jobs the same weeks they vanished: mourning cards, business circulars, a wedding announcement for a couple who had married in a hurry and left the same night. A clerk with a ruler and a suspicion could draw lines between those entries and the empty houses on the east road. Her father's insistence on recording everything — *a shop that keeps no books is already dead,* he liked to say — had become a map of everything she'd done.

She stood very still and let the cold clarity settle over her, because panic was a luxury and clarity was a tool.

It wasn't the soldiers she should fear. Soldiers were weather; they passed through and left wreckage and moved on. It was this room. These cabinets. The man with the ruler. An army could burn a village and the village would be ash and grief and eventually grass, but a list — a list could follow you across borders. A list could wait years. Somewhere in these drawers there might already be a folder with her forgeries pinned inside it, descriptions of Tomás Underhill, the print shop flagged in red or whatever color this commission used for trouble.

She crossed to the table and looked down at the open page.

There it was, three entries up. *Underhill, T. — 200 broadsheets, paid in kind, flour and salt.* The permit blanks she'd printed disguised as funeral notices. And beside it, in a different hand, a small annotation she couldn't read from standing height. She leaned closer.

*Cross-ref: Transport Manifest 44-C.*

Manifest 44-C. The evacuation train. They were already cross-referencing the shop against the berths.

She memorized the notation — the shape of the hand, the initials beneath it, *V.M.* — and then she did the thing she had come to do, the thing that had kept her awake through two nights of counting sentries: she looked for the archive of the taken. Not the permits. Not the manifests. The master list. Every name the occupying command had recorded as requisitioned labor, seized hostages, arrested persons. The names of the disappeared.

It took her twenty minutes in the cabinets, working by the spill of lamplight, gloves made of stocking-feet pulled over her hands so she'd leave no prints. *Confiscations* was a dead drawer — household goods, livestock, a piano from the schoolhouse. *Personnel* was closer, thick with service rosters. And then, in a cabinet set slightly apart, newer than the rest, a drawer labeled simply *Verzeichnis.* Index.

Inside, folders. Hundreds of them. Each one thin — a name, an origin, a date of seizure, a disposition column mostly blank. Blank, she realized, because most of these people had not been disposed of at all. They were somewhere in camps and compounds and labor battalions, alive or not, accounted for nowhere. The blank column was the whole horror of it. The commission had written these people down and then stopped writing.

She found the Aldás family. The Kesslers. Old Marta Vojtech, who was seventy if she was a day and had been taken for shouting at a supply sergeant. Page by page, name by name, the town's missing assembled themselves in her memory — and here was the thing, here was the terrible useful thing: she did not need to steal anything. Stealing would alert them, bring dogs and searches down on the shop, on her father, on Mateo. No. She had spent her life setting other people's words in metal. She could carry these names the only way that couldn't be searched at a checkpoint.

In her head.

She read each folder twice. First time for the name. Second time for everything else — dates, dispositions, the few annotations. Some of the handwriting was bad; she forced herself through it anyway, because a name misremembered was a person lost twice, and she had promised herself — standing in the loft, standing at her father's bedside, standing everywhere she ever made a promise anymore — that no one from this town would vanish twice. Once by the army. Never again by omission.

The lamp hummed. Boots crossed the floor overhead. Somewhere a door closed and stayed closed.

When she reached the last folder she closed the drawer exactly as she'd found it, aligned to the worn scratch mark on the cabinet rim, and returned to the table. One last look at the ledger. Her father's careful columns. The annotation. *V.M.*

Then, because she was her father's daughter and a printer to the bone, she did one thing more. From the shelf of office supplies by the door — the commission traveled well-stocked; paperwork was their ammunition — she took a single sheet of the commission's own letterhead, folded it small, and put it in her coat. Watermarked. Dated stock. Proof, if she ever needed it, that she had stood inside this room. Or material for something better. She hadn't decided which. She suspected she wouldn't decide until the moment demanded it, and the moment was coming; moments like this always came.

* * *

The coal chute delivered her back into the rain and the dark, and Ferren was waiting at the cooperage loft with bread and a face like a storm deciding.

"Forty-one minutes," he said. "You said thirty."

"The reading took longer than the walking."

"Reading what?" He tore the loaf and handed her half, watching her with those counting eyes. He'd eaten; she could tell by the crumbs, but he'd saved hers anyway, and something in her chest pulled tight and painful. "You can't say. Fine. I know it's the ledger. Everyone knows the ledger's there. What I don't know is why you went to look at it instead of taking it back."

"Because a stolen ledger is just evidence against us. A read ledger—" She stopped. Chewed. Chose. Ferren had earned truth the way he earned everything, by paying attention. "I memorized the index of the taken, Ferren. Every name. Every file. In my head, where they can't confiscate it."

He stared at her. Rain ticked on the loft boards. Down in the town, the last lit windows were going dark one by one, the way they did now, the town putting itself to bed like a sick animal.

"All of them?" he said finally. Quietly. All the swagger gone.

"All of them."

"Including—" He didn't finish. He didn't have to. His own family had gone into the first seizures, two winters ago, and no one had ever told him anything except *later, boy, later.*

"Including yours," Isandro said. "Kovacs, Ferenc. Seized March 1917, transport nine. There's a file with his name in it, Ferren. Your father is a line in a drawer in that building, and tomorrow morning he'll be a line in my head, and after the armistice he'll be a line in a book that the commission has to answer for. Nobody disappears twice. Not him. Not anyone."

Ferren looked at the bread in his hands for a long moment. When he looked up his eyes were wet and furious and thirteen years old and older than the war.

"Good," he said. Just that. *Good.* And then, because he was still Ferren: "You owe me forty-one minutes. I keep accounts."

"So does my father," she said, and almost smiled. Almost.

* * *

The shop was dark when she let herself in the back, and for one lurching second she thought — but no, the shape by the stove was Mateo, sitting up waiting, sleeves rolled, ink to the second knuckle as always. He rose when he saw her and didn't ask where she'd been, because he'd kept watch below the window himself and knew exactly where she'd been.

"Well?" he whispered.

"It's there. All of it. Father's ledger is on their table like a hymnal, and there's an annotation linking us to the train manifest. Initials V.M." She peeled off the stocking-gloves, flexing her stiff fingers. "Mateo, they're not hunting people. They're *filing* them. Do you understand? The men with rifles are just the hands. This is a machine made of paper, and we've been fighting the hands."

"Then we fight the paper." He said it simply, the way he said everything about the work — the way he'd said *teach me* that night at the press with his hands steady on the forme. "You got the names out?"

"In here." She touched her temple. "Two hundred and six. Plus the files' particulars. Dates, transports, dispositions — or the lack of them. That's the crime, Mateo. The blank column. They wrote people down and then stopped writing."

Mateo was quiet a moment. In the dark his face was mostly eyes. "Two hundred and six," he repeated. "Can you set that much type before the signing?"

The signing. Three days away, maybe four — the rumor hardened hourly, delegates arriving across the river, bells rehearsed for the hour. And the berth list finalized tomorrow. One seat. She had spent the walk home not letting herself think about it, and now Mateo had said *before the signing* and the arithmetic she'd been refusing came due all at once: her father, who could not survive the journey alone; Mateo, who would not leave; Ferren, who had no claim at all; herself, whose name was on the manifest under a dead boy's.

"I don't know," she said honestly. "But I'm going to find out. Wake me at five. We start composing at first light."

"There's something else," Mateo said. He hesitated — Mateo never hesitated — and then held out a scrap of paper. "Came under the door an hour ago. No signature."

She struck a match and read it. Three words in a clerk's precise hand:

*Burn the annotation.*

*V.M.*

Isandro stood with the match burning down toward her fingers and felt the ground shift under the whole shape of the war. Someone inside that archive knew she'd been in the room. Someone inside that archive was offering to help.

Or offering bait.

The match died. In the dark, she heard Mateo breathing, heard the rain, heard the far-off sound of the river where men with clean pens were preparing to sign her town away.

"Get me the good magnifier," she said. "We're going to look at this handwriting very closely."

Because if the enemy was paperwork, then whoever wrote this note had just picked a side — and Isandro Underhill intended to know, before the sun rose, whether she'd just gained an ally in the archive or a hook with her name already set in it.
