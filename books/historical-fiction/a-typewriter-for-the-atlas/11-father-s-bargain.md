## 11. Father's Bargain

Emeric Underhill had spent sixty years teaching himself to read the world the way he read type: upside down and backward, everything arriving mirrored, everything truest when reversed. It was how he knew, lying in his bed above the shop with the winter light going gray at the shutters, that the war was ending — not because anyone told him, but because the sounds had changed. For four years the road past the house had carried boots in unison, wagons groaning under requisition weight, the particular rhythm of men who believed they would be passing through forever. Now the boots came ragged. The wagons went out lighter than they came in. Men who meant to stay forever begin, near the end, to count what they can carry.

He heard it all from upstairs, the way he'd once heard the press running below him as a boy, his own father's hands moving over the forme, ink and oil and the soft iron breathing of the machine. A shop teaches you its language. You never lose it.

What he could not hear clearly anymore was Isandro.

That frightened him more than the army did.

* * *

The lucidity came back to him in the evenings now, the way light comes back after a long cloud, sudden and almost apologetic. Mornings he drifted. He woke some days unsure whether Tomás or Isandro was standing at his bedside, unsure whether the war was this one or the one his father had limped home from, unsure of anything except the weight of lead in a case and the smell of the good ink, the Swedish stuff they saved for fine work. The doctor — young Vaszary, who charged nothing and apologized for it — called it the lung giving way by degrees, and gave him tincture for the cough and a look over Isandro's shoulder that Emeric had seen before on other doctors' faces, in other years, over other bedsides. Weeks, not months. He'd done the arithmetic himself before the doctor said it aloud. He was a printer. He understood impressions wearing thin.

But tonight — tonight the fog had lifted clean away, and he lay listening to the house with the whole of his mind available to him, and he made a decision he had been circling for days like a compositor circling a stubborn line of type.

He would have them all in the room at once. Tonight. Because there were things a man should say while he still owned the words, and because he had watched his daughter carry this family on her shoulders for so long that she had stopped noticing she was carrying it, and carrying things you don't notice is how backs break quietly.

"Tomás," he called, and then, deliberately, "Isandro."

Below him, the press went silent mid-pull. That was another thing the fog had taught him: she worked at night now, hours no legitimate job required. He was not a fool. He had been a smuggler of ideas his whole life — every printer is; it's the trade's oldest sin and oldest honor — and he knew what a press sounded like when it was running something hurried, something run off quick and wiped down fast. He had known since autumn. He said nothing, because saying nothing was also a kind of setting type: you placed the silence exactly where it needed to go and let it hold the line together.

Footsteps on the stairs, two sets. Then a third, lighter, hesitant — the boy, Ferren, who had attached himself to the household like a burr and whom Emeric had privately begun to think of as an apprentice nobody had hired. And behind them, Mateo, whose hands smelled always of solvent and whose eyes went to Isandro first and last and in between, in the manner of a man reading the same line over and over because he cannot stop.

They filled the doorway the way families do: too many people for one frame, none willing to enter first.

"I'm lucid," Emeric announced, "so whatever you've been not-telling me, you may as well tell me now, while I'll remember it."

* * *

They told him about the train.

He listened without moving, which was an old habit from negotiation days — suppliers, censors, tax men; a printer's life was one long negotiation conducted across a counter with the censor's stamp hovering. One evacuation train. Before the signing. Berths drawn up by the occupying command, three names submitted from each registered household, one seat guaranteed per list if the lists held. Rumor hardening into fact the way rumor did in border towns: softly, then all at once, like ice taking a pond.

Three names. One seat.

He looked at his daughter standing at the foot of his bed with her brother's jacket squared on her shoulders and her hands folded like a man waiting for a verdict, and he did the arithmetic she had already done, and he saw her arrive at the answer she thought she'd hidden.

She thinks the seat is hers to give away. That was the first thing he understood, clear as ink on damp paper. She has already decided it isn't hers to keep.

The second thing he understood was that she was wrong — not about the sacrifice, but about the geometry of it. She thought the choice lay between herself and the others. She hadn't yet seen that there was a fourth name on the list, the one written smallest, the one everyone was being very careful not to say aloud because saying it would make it real.

His own.

"So," he said, when Mateo finished. "Three names, one berth." He pushed himself up against the headboard, and the cough came, and he let it spend itself rather than fight it — fighting it only made the blood come sooner — and when it passed he wiped his mouth with the handkerchief and folded it small, the blood-side in, an old courtesy, and set it beneath the pillow where they all pretended not to know it lived.

"Papa," Isandro began.

"Don't." He raised one finger. "You've been composing your argument since you walked in. I can see it set up in your face, all locked and ready to print. Let me put mine down first. I'm your father and I'm dying and those are the two only advantages I have left, and I intend to spend both tonight."

Mateo shifted his weight. Ferren, at the edge of the lamplight, had gone very still, the way children go still around dying things — not fear exactly, but the careful attention of someone learning something they'll need later.

"The berth," Emeric said, "is mine."

"No—" Isandro started.

"It's mine," he repeated, "because I say it is, and because I'm the one who taught you that a document means what its maker intends, not what its reader hopes. Listen to me. If I take that train, I die in a hospital car between here and wherever the rails end, coughing into a handkerchief in front of strangers, and I am buried under somebody else's number in a field I've never seen. That is not survival. That is relocation of a corpse. I decline."

"You don't know that—"

"I know paper," he said. "I know what lasts and what doesn't. My lungs are spoiled stock. Nobody reprints from spoiled stock." He held her gaze until hers dropped, and he hated himself a little for using the old authority, the counter authority, the voice that had once made censors blink. But love, he had learned, sometimes required forgery — you signed yourself steady when you were nothing of the kind, because the people watching you needed the signature more than the truth.

"So my name goes on that list," he said, "and when the berth is assigned, whoever needs it most takes it. Not whoever deserves it — need, not desert. We are not martyrs, any of us; martyrs are just people who ran out of patience. Whoever needs it most. That's the whole clause. No amendments."

"And who decides who needs it most?" Isandro asked quietly.

"I do," said Emeric Underhill. "I'm the dying man. Dying men get to decide one thing. It's traditional."

Nobody laughed. He hadn't expected them to. But Ferren's chin came up a fraction, and Mateo looked at Isandro, and Isandro looked at the floor, and Emeric understood that the floor was where she kept her arguments when she lost.

* * *

Later — after Mateo had taken Ferren down to the kitchen and the sound of bread being cut reached them through the boards, the ordinary sound of it, almost unbearable — Isandro sat in the chair beside the bed and did not speak, and Emeric let the silence run because he wanted one thing more from the evening and he needed the right setting for it.

The lamp threw their shadows tall on the plaster. Below, faintly, the tick of the shop cooling. Somewhere across the river, muffled by distance and water, the commission's generators or typewriters or whatever machinery peace ran on, humming its small indifferent hum.

"When your grandfather came here," Emeric said at last, "he carried two suitcases. In one were his clothes. In the other were his sorts." He turned his head on the pillow to look at her. "Clothes wore out inside ten years. I'm told the suitcase itself rotted in a cellar in the forties. But the type —" he tapped the blanket with one knuckle, twice, a compositor's gesture, tapping a line into place — "the type outlived him, me, your mother, and everything either of us ever printed that we're ashamed of. There are letters in that case older than this country's current borders. Older than the borders before that. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

"That the type matters," Isandro said.

"No." He shook his head slowly. "You're reversing it wrong. That's the mistake everyone makes with mirror-reading — they think the trick is the reversal. The trick is that it reads the same both ways." He gathered breath; the lung complained; he overrode it. "The type doesn't matter. What matters is that someone keeps setting it. Type in a case is just metal. Metal is nothing. Lead is the humblest metal we have — it doesn't ring, it doesn't shine, it melts in a kitchen fire. Its whole value is that a hand picks it up and puts it in a stick and makes it mean something. Your grandfather's type survived because hands kept meaning things with it. That's all inheritance is. Not the property. The duty."

Isandro's jaw tightened. He watched her decide whether to argue and watched the argument lose.

"You want me to promise," she said.

"I want you to promise the type will outlive us both. Not the shop — the shop is walls and a leaky roof and I'll thank the army to burn it, honestly, it would save the surveyor's fee. The type. The practice. Somebody, somewhere, in fifty years, setting a name in lead because it deserved to be set." His voice roughened and he let it. "Because listen to me, girl, and hear me all the way through: they took the ledger. Whatever else happens — the train, the armistice, all of it — they walked into this town and carried off the record of who lived here. Names are how people refuse to disappear. Take a man's name and you haven't killed him, but you've made him killable. You've made him optional." He fixed her with the look he'd used on apprentices for forty years, the one that said the next thing is the thing the whole page turns on. "Someone in this town has been writing names down faster than they can be taken. I don't ask how. I don't want to know how — plausible deniability is a father's privilege. But if the ledger is gone, then memory is the only forme left, and memory needs a press, and the press needs hands, and I am looking at the only pair in this house that hasn't failed me yet."

"Papa—"

"Promise me the type outlives us. That's all. Not that you survive — though God knows I want it, I have never wanted anything the way I want you on the far side of this war, Isandro, Isandro and not Tomás, do you hear me, I have known since you were nine years old and I have waited sixty years' worth of nights for you to print your own name again —" his breath caught, and he pressed on through it, ruthless, because he might not get another lucid evening and the sentence had waited decades already — "not that you live, not that you win, just that the type goes on. Promise me the duty, not the property. Property burns. Duty walks out of fires carrying suitcases."

The lamp hissed. Downstairs, Ferren laughed at something Mateo said — a small, rusty sound, the first Emeric had heard from the boy, and it went through him like a quoin tightening.

Isandro took his hand. Her fingers were stained at the cuticles, ink worked deep into the whorls, the permanent tattoo of the trade, and he closed his eyes briefly at the sight of it because it was his own hand forty years younger and it meant she had already promised, had probably promised years ago, in the only language he'd ever taught her properly.

"I promise," she said.

"Say the rest."

Her mouth pressed flat. But she was her father's daughter, and she knew a locked forme when she felt one. "The type will outlive us both," she said. "Whoever holds it keeps setting it. Names in lead. However long it takes."

"However long it takes," he echoed, and released her hand, and felt something in his chest settle that had nothing to do with the lung — an impression pulled clean, a sheet lifted perfect off the stone, the rare flawless pull a printer gets maybe once a year and remembers forever.

"Now get out," he said gruffly. "Dying men need their sleep, and you have work. Don't insult me by pretending you don't. I've heard that press running at hours the bishop wouldn't approve of, and whatever you're printing down there, print more of it. There's going to be demand."

* * *

He slept, and woke once in the black hours, lucidity fading at the edges but the decision holding firm, set and locked. Through the floorboards he could feel it rather than hear it: the press below, running again, that patient iron heartbeat.

And beneath it, fainter, another sound he took a moment to place — footsteps in the street outside, quick ones, several sets, stopping at the door. A knock, soft, then softer, the knock of people who have learned that loud knocking costs lives.

Then voices, low and urgent, and Mateo's feet on the stairs, taking them two at a time.

Emeric lay in the dark and smiled into his pillow, an old man's crooked smile, and thought: *There it is. Demand.*

He had told the truth downstairs without knowing quite how true it was. Word traveled the way ink does — along the fibers, finding every channel. They had forged papers for neighbors, for strangers, for anyone with a name worth saving, and now, on the eve of the last train, with the town emptying itself toward that single berth like water toward a drain, the desperate were coming out of the woodwork to the only door in town that could open for them.

He heard Isandro's door open. Heard her pause — one breath, listening, the compositor checking the lock before the pull — and then her feet went down the stairs fast and sure, and the bolt slid back, and the night's work began.

Emeric Underhill closed his eyes. Above the shop, in the dark, the dying man kept the only vigil left to him: listening to his daughter's press run, counting pulls, keeping the count, so that someday — however long it took — someone could set even this in lead.

*How many names tonight,* he wondered, drifting. *How many names before morning.*
