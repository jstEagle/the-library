## 12. Permits for Strangers

The first knock came at half past four in the morning, and Mateo knew before he opened the door that it was trouble, because nobody knocked like that unless they had been running.

It was the widow Sarrate from the tannery road, her shawl pinned crooked, her breath steaming in the cold. She didn't come in so much as fall in, catching the doorframe with one hand and pressing a folded scrap of paper into his palm with the other. On it, in a hand that shook: her son's name, his age, and the name of a village forty kilometers south where an aunt might still be living.

"They came for the miller's boys yesterday," she said. "They took them off the street. Not conscription — requisition. Like sacks of flour." Her eyes went to the stairs behind him, to the dim shape of the shop below the apartment, the smell of ink that lived in everything they owned. "I was told — people said — if anyone could make a paper that would hold—"

"Who told you?"

"It doesn't matter."

It did matter, enormously, but there was no time to explain why, and besides, the answer was already written on her face: everyone told everyone, now. The secret had stopped being a secret somewhere around the third requisition notice and had become something else instead — a kind of weather, a thing the whole town knew and moved through without naming. *Go to the printer's. Ask for Tomás.*

Mateo took the paper. "Come back Thursday," he said quietly. "After dark. Knock twice."

She pressed both hands over her mouth — not to hide words but to keep something else in — nodded, and was gone into the blue-dark of the street.

He stood holding the scrap for a long moment. Upstairs, he could hear Isandro already moving, already awake; she slept the way a cat sleeps, surfacing at any change in the house's breathing. He heard the floorboard above the stairwell, then the creak of the third step, and then she was beside him in her brother's shirt, hair tied back, reading the widow's paper upside down in his hand.

"Sarrate," she said. "The son is what, sixteen?"

"Fifteen, I think."

"They'll take him either way." She held out her hand for the paper. He gave it to her. It was strange, he thought — not for the first time — how naturally their hands had learned this exchange, how the passing of contraband between them had become as ordinary as passing salt. "We'll need the seal from the garrison file. The third one, not the second. They've started checking the second against their registry."

"You memorized which seals they check?"

"I memorized which ones they check *this week*." Something flickered across her face that was almost a smile. "They rotate. Ferren found out. He counts everything now, not just soldiers."

Mateo looked down at the widow's trembling handwriting, at the boy's name on it — *Anselmo Sarrate, b. 1904* — and felt the familiar tightening behind his sternum that had become, over these weeks, indistinguishable from purpose. When he had first caught Isandro forging permits — that night at the press, her hands sure on the type, her lie dissolving under his eyes — he had thought he was choosing between loyalty and law. It had turned out to be no choice at all, and also not the real choice. The real choice was this one, repeated nightly: how much of himself to spend, and on whom.

"Thursday," he said. "Tell her Thursday."

Isandro was already halfway down the stairs.

* * *

By Friday the shop had stopped being a shop.

Not officially — officially it still printed funeral notices, price lists for the cooper, the parish bulletin with its careful, harmless columns. But the back room, the one with the broken casement that let in river damp, had become something else. Mateo thought of it sometimes as a second ledger: not names and payments, but names and distances. Where each person needed to go. What each paper needed to say. Which checkpoints were staffed by men who read closely and which by men who wanted only to be warm.

Demand had tripled in nine days. He kept count because counting was the only way to keep from drowning in it. Eleven papers in the first week after the miller's sons were taken. Nineteen the next. This week, thirty-one requests, and they had finished twenty-six, and the five unfinished sat in his chest like stones.

The work itself had developed its own liturgy. Isandro cut the type — she was faster than anyone he'd ever seen, faster than her father had been even before his hands began to fail, and there was something almost unbearable in watching her do it, this girl wearing her dead brother's name like a coat two sizes too small, cutting letterforms by lamplight with the concentration of a surgeon. Mateo set the forms and worked the press. Between them passed the blanks, the seals, the rice-paper copies of the stolen ledger that Isandro was still adding to, name by name, from memory, in the margins of everything.

And Ferren ran.

The boy had appointed himself to the role without asking anyone, the way he did most things. He was twelve and made of wire and silence, and he knew the town's every alley, every gap in a fence, every soldier's shift change, because he had spent months counting all of it when counting was just a game that kept the loneliness off. Now the game was infrastructure. He carried messages folded into the lining of his cap, into the hollow heel of a boot he'd found, once inside a loaf of bread whose baker never asked questions. He could cross the occupied quarter in eleven minutes. Mateo timed him.

"Twelve tonight," Ferren reported on Saturday, dropping onto a crate in the back room and pulling off the boot. "There's a new sentry at the bridge. Young. Nervy. He grabbed my collar."

"And?"

"I cried." Ferren said it flatly, without shame. "About my papa being taken. He gave me back and looked away." He dug a crumb of bread out of the boot's hollow and ate it. "Crying works better than running. Running makes them curious."

Isandro, at the composing stone, didn't look up, but Mateo saw her hand pause over the case, half a second, the way it always paused when Ferren talked about his father. Nobody in the room said anything about the execution at the bakery wall. Nobody ever would, Mateo suspected, until the boy himself decided otherwise, and he showed no sign of deciding soon. He had gone quiet after that day in a way that wasn't the old quiet. The old quiet had been a wall. This one was a scar.

But he worked. God, how he worked. And somewhere in the last three weeks — Mateo couldn't have named the day it happened — the boy had stopped calling Isandro *Tomás* when they were alone. He just called her nothing, or *you*, or, once, in exhaustion, something soft and unguarded that Mateo pretended not to hear and treasured anyway.

* * *

On Sunday night the fingers split.

It was Mateo's right index finger first — the skin cracking along a crease from washing the press rollers in cold water, the water gone harsher lately with whatever the army put in the wells. Then a crack opened along his thumb. Isandro's hands were worse; hers had always been worse, since she'd been cutting type since childhood, and by Monday she wore strips of linen wound around three fingers of her right hand and still worked, wincing, adjusting her grip, refusing to stop.

"Two days' rest," Mateo said. "That's all I'm asking."

"If I rest two days, Anselmo Sarrate doesn't travel. If Anselmo doesn't travel, he goes on a requisition list. If he goes on a list—" She flexed the bandaged fingers, testing them. "I know how the sentence ends, Mateo. We both do."

"That's exactly why you should sleep."

"No." She said it without heat, which was somehow worse. "Sleep is for people who can afford to lose a day."

He wanted to argue. He wanted to take her hands — carefully, the way you'd handle standing type, loose enough to spill — and wrap them in something warmer than linen and tell her the world could wait. Instead he heated water, added the glycerin the doctor had left for her father's cracked lips, and set the basin in front of her without a word. She looked at it, looked at him, and soaked her hands, and neither of them spoke while the steam rose, and it was, absurdly, one of the most intimate hours of his life: two people saying nothing over a basin, their whole argument conducted in the lowering of her shoulders, the slow unclenching of her jaw.

The network fed them. That was the other thing Mateo hadn't predicted — that the work would feed them back. The baker who never asked questions sent bread that appeared on their step. The cooper brought firewood "for the press room, wood keeps the ink at temperature," which was nonsense and understood by both of them to be nonsense. Old women pressed eggs on Ferren. A man whose permit had gotten his family onto a farm cart outside Vellacorte returned a month later with a wheel of cheese and stood in the doorway unable to speak, and finally just touched the doorframe, twice, like a man touching an altar.

"We're not a print shop anymore," Isandro said one night, watching the cooper's cart rattle away. "We're a—" She searched for the word and didn't find it, and Mateo didn't offer one, because he suspected the word was *family*, and he suspected she wasn't ready for the word, and he suspected she knew he suspected it, and all of that was fine. Some things could be true without being said. That was practically the shop's founding principle.

Her father knew, though. Father Underhill knew everything — had known, Mateo was nearly certain, since the checkpoint inspection, since the old man had lied to a sergeant with a steadiness that must have cost him a week of life. Now he sat propped in the back room most evenings in a chair they'd moved down for him, wrapped in blankets, doing the one job left to him: he checked the finished papers. His eyes were still the best eyes in the building. He would hold a permit at arm's length, tilt it toward the lamp, and find things no one else saw — a date that implied a Sunday issuance, a village name spelled the civilian way instead of the army's way, a seal stamped a degree too far left.

"The left," he murmured on Tuesday night, tapping a finished permit. "Their clerk stamps left-handed. You can see it in the smudge direction. Move it."

Mateo moved it. The old man smiled — a small, dry smile, rationed like everything else about him now — and said, "Good. Again."

Three generations of craft in one room, Mateo thought, except the generations weren't blood-kin, or weren't all blood-kin, and the craft was forgery, and none of them had chosen any of it, and yet when the lamp guttered and Isandro trimmed the wick and Ferren fell asleep sitting up mid-sentence about sentry rotations, the room had a warmth in it that Mateo had never once felt in his own childhood home. He was twenty-three years old and committing crimes nightly, and he was happier than he had ever been, and the two facts were the same fact, and he understood that too.

* * *

The cost arrived on a Wednesday, the way costs do — without announcement, in ordinary clothes.

Ferren came in at dusk, faster than usual, and didn't do his usual sweep of the room before speaking, which meant it was bad. "They've changed the manifests," he said. "At the freight office. I heard the clerks. There's going to be an evacuation train — one, before the signing — and the berth lists are being drawn up by the commandant's office. Not the commission. The commandant." He swallowed. "Tomás — you're on a draft list. I copied it." He unfolded a scrap covered in his tight, count-everything hand. "'T. Underhill, printer, and dependents.'"

The word *dependents* hung in the air. Mateo watched Isandro read it, watched her do the arithmetic he could see happening behind her eyes — herself, her father, Mateo, Ferren, and how many seats, and who decided, and what *drafted* meant versus what *evacuated* meant, and whether the difference was a kindness or a trap.

"How many berths?" she asked quietly.

"I don't know yet. I'll find out." Ferren was already retying the boot.

"Ferren." She caught his sleeve — gently, Mateo noticed; she'd learned gentleness for this boy the way she'd learned everything, by exact application. "Eat something first."

"There's no time—"

"There's always bread." She pushed the loaf at him, and the boy, scowling, tore off a piece, and for a moment the room was just a room: lamplight, flour dust, a dying man asleep in his chair, three people bound by paper and secrets, eating in silence because there was nothing to say that wouldn't frighten someone.

Mateo looked at the scrap in Isandro's hand. *T. Underhill, printer, and dependents.* Somewhere in the commandant's office, a clerk had written those words, and somewhere downstream a train would be filled or not filled according to them, and every name on every list — the saved, the hunted, the disappeared — was converging now toward a single number of berths that Mateo did not yet know and already dreaded.

He reached over and took Isandro's bandaged hand, and she let him, and outside the window the church bell rang the hour, and far off across the river, faint as a scratch on glass, came the sound of hammers — the commission building its platform for the signing.

"Whatever the number is," he said, "we figure it out together."

Isandro looked at the draft list a moment longer. Then she folded it away into the type case, into the hollow beneath the sorts where the last of the blanks lay hidden, and said:

"First we find out how many."
