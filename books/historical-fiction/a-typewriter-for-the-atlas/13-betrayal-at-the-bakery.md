## 13. Betrayal at the Bakery

The bread ran out of Vasseur's bakery on a Tuesday, and by Wednesday evening everyone knew why.

Ferren brought the news, sliding through the back door with the particular stillness he'd learned since the execution—the boy who had once counted soldiers aloud now moved like smoke, like something that didn't want to be seen counting anything at all.

"Madame Vasseur," he said. "They took her this morning. At the checkpoint on the mill road."

Isandro kept her hands moving across the case, distributing sorts, because her hands always had to be moving when the world came apart. "Took her where?"

"The requisition office." He stood just inside the doorway, cap in his fists. "She was carrying a permit. One of ours."

Mateo looked up from the press, and the room went quiet enough that Isandro could hear the ink fountain dripping its slow count against the night.

One of ours. Three words that meant everything they'd built since autumn: the permits for strangers, the rice-paper copies sleeping in the type cases, the network Ferren threaded together with messages carried in his boot and his grin. All of it resting on a single assumption—that no one would be caught, or if caught, would hold their tongue.

"Which permit?" Isandro asked. Her voice came out level, which surprised her. "Do you know?"

"Agricultural pass. The kind for market days." Ferren's eyes dropped. "I cut it myself. I mean—I set it. You pulled the copies."

"You did it well."

"It doesn't matter how well I did it." He was fourteen years old and had stopped sounding like a child somewhere between the execution and now. "She's Madame Vasseur. She's frightened of everything. She once cried when a soldier touched her flour sacks."

* * *

They worked until two in the morning anyway, because stopping would have been an admission. Isandro burned what could be burned—drafts, misprints, the practice sheets Mateo had used learning the forgery hand—and buried the ash in the coal scuttle beneath fresh coal. The blanks were harder. Forty-two remaining permit forms, printed on good stock, each one a small door out of the town, each one now evidence.

"We could put them in the stove," Mateo said.

"And if they search the ashes? They'll find the watermark fragments. They'll know exactly what we made." She turned a blank over in her fingers. Good paper. Paper bought honestly from a supplier in Virelles who had no idea what it became. "Paper remembers, Mateo. That's the whole point of paper. That's also the problem."

"Then where?"

She looked at the type cases along the wall. Her father's cases, some older than she was, oak frames holding lead the way the town held its breath. And then she remembered something her father had said once, drunk on cheap wine and pride, showing her the cabinet where he kept the display faces: *A printer's first duty is to make space where there appears to be none.*

"Hollow sorts," she said.

Mateo frowned. "There's no such thing."

"There will be by Friday."

* * *

Her father heard them working past midnight and came down in his dressing gown, one hand pressed to his chest as though holding himself upright by main force. He had lost weight all winter; the bones of his face had begun to organize themselves into a skull. But his eyes were clear, and they went straight to the workbench, to the drill, to the forty-two blanks laid out like a deck of losing cards.

"No," he said quietly. "Not the display sorts."

"Papa—"

"Do you know what those are?" He crossed to the cabinet with the shuffling gait of a man negotiating with his own body. His hand rested on the drawer of ninety-six-point wood type, the poster letters he'd used for fifty years to print fair announcements and funeral notices and, once, a broadside celebrating a visit by a bishop whose name everyone had since agreed to forget. "I bought these in 1871. From a foundry in Lyon that closed before your mother was born. There are maybe four cabinets of this face left in the country."

"I know."

"They're not hollow. They're solid pine. You can't hollow—" He stopped. Looked at her. Looked at the blanks. And Isandro watched him do the arithmetic she'd already done: forty-two doors out of the town, weighed against a drawer of irreplaceable wood type, and the answer arriving in him like a diagnosis.

He opened the drawer himself.

"The E's are thickest," he said. "Cut the counters out of the E's. Nobody misses an E until they need eleven of them, and nobody needs eleven." His voice caught on something that might have been laughter or grief. "Tomás. You understand you're drilling holes in your inheritance."

"I'm putting something inside my inheritance," Isandro said. "That's different."

He held her gaze a long moment—her father, who had known what Tomás really was for three years now and had never once said it aloud, protecting the fiction even alone in his own kitchen, even dying, as though the name itself were a sort that must not be dropped or mixed. Then he took the finest auger bit from the tool roll and set it beside the drill and said, "Show me your hands. If you're going to do this, you'll do it cleanly. A botched counter is visible from across a room."

* * *

They drilled through Thursday night and most of Friday. It was delicate, maddening work: boring out the center of each wooden letter, leaving walls thin enough to hide a folded blank but thick enough to survive a casual knock against the case. Isandro's father supervised from a chair dragged to the bench, correcting her angle, her pressure, the depth of each bore, until Mateo quietly took over the drilling and let the old man direct instead of labor.

Forty-two blanks, folded to the size of a communion wafer. Forty-one went into the letters—one per sort, wrapped in waxed paper against damp, seated in the hollowed counters, capped with a plug of matching pine shaved down and glued so fine that Isandro herself couldn't find the seams without knowing where to look.

The forty-second blank she did not hide.

"That one stays loose," she said. "In my coat pocket."

"Why?" Mateo asked.

"Because if they come, and they take me, and they search me—they should find one. Just one. Enough to explain itself." She met his eyes. "A man caught with nothing looks like a man hiding everything. A man caught with one blank looks like a fool who got careless once. Fools get beaten. Conspirators get shot."

"That's a terrible plan."

"It's the only plan where the numbers work."

Her father said nothing, but he nodded slowly, the way he nodded over a page proof that would never be perfect but would have to do.

The last letter they filled was an E. Mateo glued the cap, clamped it gently in the press's stone to set overnight, and then sat down heavily on the composing stool, his hands dark to the wrist with ink and sawdust.

"If they seal the shop," he said, "we can't pull another copy of anything. No permits. No lists. Nothing."

"I know."

"And the names. Your list. The copies in the cases—"

"Are staying exactly where they are." She had thought about this too, in the hours between drills. Rice paper, tissue-thin, tucked behind the brass reglets in six different cases, distributed so no single search would find them all. "If they seal the building, the names are sealed with it, and sealed isn't destroyed. Sealed is waiting."

"For what?"

"For someone to open the door again." She wiped her hands on the rag that lived by the stone, black as ever no matter how often it was washed. "Print waits, Mateo. That's what Papa taught me. Lead doesn't hurry and it doesn't rot. You just have to outlast whatever's standing on top of it."

* * *

The soldiers came Saturday morning.

Isandro knew before she saw them—not from any sound, but from the street. Sound carried differently when men moved with purpose; footsteps organized themselves; and there was a quality to silence in a small town that spoke louder than bells. She was at the window with her tea when she saw four of them coming up the lane in step, rifles slung, and behind them the sergeant from the checkpoint inspections, the one with the careful eyes, and behind *him*, walking free and unbound and looking nowhere at all—

Madame Vasseur was not with them. Isandro understood what that meant, or feared she did, and set down her cup with a hand that only shook a little.

By the time the knock came she had buttoned her collar, squared her shoulders, and become Tomás Underhill as completely as she ever managed: the slight forward set of the jaw, the hands kept large and loose, the voice pitched low.

She opened the door to the sergeant and three rifles.

"Underhill." The sergeant consulted a paper he clearly didn't need to consult. "By order of the occupying command, these premises are sealed pending investigation. No entry, no removal of goods, no printing activity. Effective immediately."

"On what charge?" Isandro asked. *Tomás* asked. "We're a print shop. We print what people pay us to print."

"Madame Vasseur was found in possession of a falsified travel document." The sergeant's gaze moved past her shoulder into the shop, cataloguing the press, the cases, the stone. "The document bears characteristics consistent with work done on a hand press. This is the only hand press within twelve kilometers."

"Bears characteristics," Isandro repeated. "Half the shops in the province run hand presses."

"Half the shops in the province aren't named by Madame Vasseur."

The sentence landed in the courtyard of Isandro's chest and lay there, heavy as a form of type. Not suspicion. Not inference. *Named.* Somewhere between the mill road and the requisition office, a frightened woman with flour on her apron had given them up—or been made to give them up, which amounted to the same thing and hurt considerably more, because Isandro could not even hate her cleanly.

"She's old," Isandro said. "She's confused. She buys bread—"

"She bought bread." The sergeant almost smiled. "Seal it."

Two soldiers entered and began the work of sealing: paper strips across the press, wax seals stamped with the command's insignia, a chalk mark on the doorframe. They were not gentle but they were not vandals either; this was procedure, and procedure has a terrible impersonal dignity. One soldier walked the type cases with a pencil, tapping drawers open, running his finger along the rows of sorts. At the display cabinet he paused.

"Poster letters," Isandro said, before she decided whether to say anything. "Ninety-six point. Worthless to you, worth a fortune to collectors, and every one accounted for in my ledger, which I assume you've already taken."

The soldier glanced at her, tapped the cabinet once—a hollow knock that Isandro felt in her teeth—and moved on.

The sergeant lingered at the door. "You'll report daily to the requisition office, Underhill. Both of you." A beat. "The boy too."

"The apprentice has nothing—"

"Daily," the sergeant said, "means daily. For everyone under this roof." And then, quieter, with something in his face that wasn't quite kindness and wasn't quite threat: "Cooperation shortens interviews. I've seen it go both ways."

They left. The wax seals cooled on the press. The chalk mark dried on the frame. And the shop that had been sanctuary, forge, and fortress stood suddenly transformed into a tomb with its occupants still breathing inside it—everything hidden and nothing reachable, forty-one doors locked inside letters of the alphabet, the names sleeping behind brass reglets, the whole secret architecture of Isandro's double life entombed under army wax.

* * *

That evening Ferren came over the back wall rather than use the door, dropping into the yard with the ease of a cat that had mapped every route years ago. He crouched by the rain barrel where Isandro waited, and for a while neither of them spoke.

"They executed her," Ferren said finally. No preamble left in him anymore; the boy who had once chattered about troop movements had spent all his words on silence and had none to waste now. "At dawn. In front of the bakery. Her own bakery."

Isandro closed her eyes. "Did you see it?"

"No. I heard it." Which was worse, she thought, or better—she couldn't decide. "People say she gave your name. The shop's name."

"People say a lot."

"Ferren says a lot," he corrected quietly. "And Ferren says: they're going to come for you. Not search—*come*. The sergeant was here today to look at your faces. Tomorrow or the day after, they'll want the rest." He picked a leaf off the surface of the barrel water, examined it, let it go. "What happens to me if they take you?"

The question hit her sideways. She had counted berths and counted risks and counted names, but she had not yet allowed herself to count Ferren among the things the arrest would cost, because counting it made it real, and real things had to be answered.

"You keep away from the shop," she said. "Completely away. You don't loiter, you don't count soldiers, you don't carry anything for anyone. You go to the widow Aubry's and you're a hungry orphan who does chores for soup. Nothing else."

"I'm not going to just—"

"*Nothing else.*" She gripped his shoulder, felt the sharp architecture of him under the coat. "Listen to me. Everything I built, everything we built—if it survives what's coming, it survives because someone outside these walls knows where the names are hidden and lives to tell it. Do you know where they are?"

"In the type cases. Behind the reglets."

"Six cases. Can you name them?"

"Jobbing, two-line brevier, the long primer, both the pica cabinets, and the case with the broken lock that you pretend is empty."

"Good." She released his shoulder. "Then you're not just a boy anymore. You're a backup copy. And backups stay safe. That's the whole discipline of it."

He looked at her for a long moment with those too-old eyes. "You sound like you're saying goodbye."

"I sound like a printer," Isandro said. "Everything I do, I do in duplicate."

But walking back through the kitchen, past her father asleep in his chair with the auger bit still on the table beside him like a relic, she let the truth stand up in her chest and be counted: tomorrow, or the day after, a summons would come. Daily meant daily; the sergeant had told her plainly. And somewhere in the requisition office a lamp was being positioned over a chair, and a clerk was sharpening a pencil, and Madame Vasseur's name was written at the top of a page, and below it, waiting, was hers.

*Tomás Underhill,* it would say.

And she would have to walk in there alone and make the whole world believe in a dead boy.
