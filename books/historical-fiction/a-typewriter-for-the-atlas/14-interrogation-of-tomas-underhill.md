## 14. Interrogation of Tomás Underhill

They came for her at the hour when the light goes gray and honest, and Isandro understood immediately that the hour was chosen on purpose. No shadows to hide in. No lamplight to flatter a lie. Just the flat, unforgiving dusk of a town under occupation, pressing against the shop windows while two soldiers stood in the doorway of Underhill & Son and read out her name — her brother's name, which was also hers now, worn so long it had grown into her like a splinter the body decides to keep.

"Tomás Underhill," the taller one said. "You'll come with us."

She wiped her hands on her apron first. It was an instinct, a printer's instinct — never touch anything with dirty hands — and she let it carry her through the first terrible seconds. Behind her, Mateo rose from the composing stone with a look she refused to meet. If she looked at him now, whatever he saw in her face would undo them both.

"My father is ill," she said. Her voice sat low in her chest where Tomás lived. "He can't be moved."

"No one said anything about moving him."

The words were nothing. The tone was everything. She untied the apron, folded it once, set it on the stone beside the type case as if she were merely stepping out for bread, and walked between the soldiers into the street.

* * *

The requisition office had been a schoolhouse before the war. She knew this because Ferren had told her, weeks ago, in his flat counting voice: *the office used to be where I learned my letters, before they took the desks out.* Now the desks were gone and the classroom held instead a single table, a single chair, and a lamp on a stand whose shade had been adjusted downward like a man leaning close to hear a secret.

They stood her against the wall first. That was part of it, she understood — the waiting, the letting the room do its work. The plaster near her shoulder was cold and slightly damp. Somewhere below, in what had been the coatroom, boots moved back and forth across tile. She counted the footsteps the way Ferren counted soldiers: one turn, two turns, three. A sentry who paced in threes was bored. A bored sentry was a sentry who had done this many times without trouble.

The door opened and the clerk from the commission came in.

Isandro's stomach dropped through the floorboards.

She recognized him instantly — Herr Vogel, though she'd never been told his name; the round-shouldered man with ink-stained fingers and spectacles that caught the lamp and threw it back doubled, so his eyes seemed always to be looking from two places at once. He had come into the shop three years ago, before the war reached this valley, before Tomás died and Isandro buried him twice, once in the ground and once in her own body. He had bought type then. Old Caslon figures, worn soft at the edges. He had run his thumb over the sorts the way a priest touches relics and said, *Your father still keeps a clean foundry, young man.* And he had paid full price and tipped Mateo and left with a parcel tied in brown paper and string.

Now he stood in the doorway holding a folder, and behind the doubled gleam of his spectacles there was nothing readable at all.

"You know this man?" the tall soldier asked him.

Vogel looked at her for a long moment. The lamp hissed. In that moment Isandro felt every false thing about herself rise up and stand in plain view: the bound chest beneath the shirt, the jaw she did not shave because she'd learned to say her beard came in patchy, the way she stood with her weight too far forward because Tomás had stood that way and she had practiced it in front of the mirror until her own posture forgot itself. Three years of careful architecture, and all of it balanced now on whether a clerk remembered a boy who sold him type.

"I've seen him before," Vogel said. "At the print shop. A transaction." He opened the folder. "Nothing more."

*Nothing more.* Two words, and each one landed like a coin dropped into a beggar's cup — small, precise, keeping her alive by increments.

They put her in the chair.

* * *

The questioning began the way weather begins: not all at once but in a slow darkening.

"State your name."

"Tomás Underhill."

"Age?"

"Twenty-six." Tomás had died at twenty-three; she had added the years he didn't live, the way you add interest to a debt.

"Your father's condition?"

"He's ill. As I said."

"Said to whom? You weren't questioned yet." The soldier — his name, she learned from his colleague's muttering, was Brandt — leaned against the table with his arms crossed. "Who have you spoken to about this interrogation?"

"No one. I assumed."

"You assumed." Brandt smiled without warmth. "Printers assume a great deal. They assume their customers can pay. They assume their ink won't run. They assume no one reads the small things they print." He straightened. "We found a permit yesterday, Underhill. Movement pass, district seal, signature of Captain Reiner. Do you know what's interesting about it?"

She kept her face still. Below, the sentry turned: four, five, six.

"It's very good," Brandt said. "Too good. Reiner's own adjutant couldn't tell it was false until we compared it against three others. And those others came from the same hand. Same paper stock. Same pressure on the seal." He picked up something from the table — she heard paper, the dry whisper of it. "Same spacing in the lettering. Whoever made these has typesetting hands."

"Many people have typesetting hands."

"In this town?" Brandt laughed shortly. "In this town there is one press. One foundry. One family of printers who have served every household within twenty kilometers for sixty years." He came around the table slowly, and she watched his boots enter the lamp's circle before the rest of him. "So here is what puzzles me, Tomás Underhill. The permits name men. Every permit we've recovered authorizes travel for a man. Yet the neighbors we've caught carrying them—" he paused, savoring it, "—include women. Wives. Daughters. Girls dressed as boys, even, in two cases. Someone in this town has been making papers that describe people who don't match themselves. Does that strike you as strange?"

"It strikes me as desperate," Isandro said. "People run from requisitioning. They'll wear anything that lets them through a checkpoint."

"Mm." Brandt crouched down level with her, and up close his face had the pores and stubble and small cruelties of any face. "Desperate people make sloppy forgeries. These are not sloppy. These are made by someone who loves the work." He tilted his head. "You love your work, printer?"

"I set type," Isandro said. "I don't love it. It pays for my father's medicine."

"Where is your father's medicine?"

"At home."

"We'll want to see it."

Something in her went very quiet, the way the shop went quiet before the press engaged — that held breath, that gathered force. *See it,* he meant. Search the house. Find the rice-paper copies folded into the type cases. Find the hollow sorts with blanks inside. Find everything.

"The medicine is quinine and cod-liver oil," she said. "Search whatever you like."

Brandt studied her. Then he smiled again, worse than before, and stood. "We will. But first—" he tapped the table, "—you'll write for me."

* * *

This was the trap she hadn't seen coming, and seeing it now, too late, she nearly laughed at its elegance.

They gave her pen and paper and dictated a sentence: *The bearer of this document has fulfilled all obligations to the occupying command and may pass freely.* Ordinary words. Words she had forged dozens of times, dressed in other names, other seals.

Write them plainly, Brandt said. No flourishes.

She understood. Somewhere in the folder on the table lay a confiscated permit, and somewhere in the commission's archive — or already in Brandt's pocket — waited a specimen of handwriting to compare against hers. Every hand betrays its owner the way a gait does, the way a voice does. Slant, pressure, the way an f crosses or doesn't, the little cowardly hesitations where a forger slows down.

Her own handwriting could hang her.

But Tomás Underhill had handwriting too. She had built it deliberately in the first months, after the burial, when she decided the shop would stay open if it killed her: a heavier slant, letters pressed hard, the t crossed with a blunt stroke instead of a flick. For three years every invoice, every receipt, every entry in her father's ledger had been written in the dead boy's hand. The ledger — God, the ledger, sitting in their archive with three years of Tomás in it — the ledger would corroborate her, if they went to compare. Her forgery and her life had the same author.

She wrote the sentence slowly. Not too slowly. A man being questioned writes fast and ugly or slow and careful, and she chose careful, because Tomás had been careful, because she had made him so.

Brandt took the page and held it to the lamp, and for a long time said nothing, and the silence stretched until she could hear her own pulse in her ears, thick as a press roller.

Then, without turning his head: "Vogel. You deal in documents. Look at this."

The clerk stepped forward. His shadow fell over her across the floor, thin and bent, the shadow of a man made mostly of paperwork. He took the sheet. He adjusted his spectacles. He held it beside — she couldn't see what; a folder page, angled away — and the room held its breath around the two of them.

Isandro looked at the wall. The damp plaster. The place where a schoolroom map had once hung; you could see the paler rectangle of it, some country that no longer existed in those borders. She thought absurdly of her father's hands, how they had taught hers to feel the depth of a letter's counter by touch alone, in the dark, eyes closed, so the fingers would know truth independent of sight. *Feel it,* he used to say. *A letter either has its proper body or it doesn't.*

Vogel set the paper down.

"The hand is consistent," he said. "With commercial work. Invoices, receipts. Nothing irregular." A pause, precise as a period. "It is not the hand on the permits."

Brandt frowned. "You're certain?"

"I am a clerk," Vogel said, with the faintest dryness, the first human thing he'd shown. "Certainty is my trade. This is a tradesman's hand. The permits are written by someone trained in secrecy — note the uniform pressure, no hesitation strokes. Different education entirely." He laid the sheet down between them like a man laying down a card he did not wish to win with. "If you arrest every printer in the province for writing well, you will arrest half the civil service, including, I should mention, myself."

The silence afterward had a different texture. Brandt looked from the clerk to Isandro and back, and Isandro kept her eyes on the pale rectangle on the wall where the old map had been, and inside her chest something hammered and hammered, because she could not tell — could not *begin* to tell — whether Vogel was protecting her or simply filing her away for later use, whether mercy and strategy wore the same face in a man like that, and which face she'd just been spared by.

"Fine," Brandt said at last, and the word came out like something pried loose with tools. "The hand matches nothing. But the shop stays sealed. Underhill & Son is closed by order pending further inquiry. The father will be questioned separately when his health permits." He gathered his folder. "You're free to go, Tomás Underhill. Don't leave town. Don't speak to anyone about tonight. We'll know if you do — this town tells us everything eventually." At the door he stopped, and half-turned, and the lamp caught the edge of his smile one last time. "One thing more. The neighbor woman, Marta Kessler — she named this shop before she was dealt with. Named it clearly. So understand me: I believe you're guilty of something, printer. I simply lack the proof. Men in my position learn to be patient."

He left. The boots below turned: seven, eight.

* * *

Vogel lingered.

He stood by the table adjusting papers that needed no adjustment, and Isandro remained in the chair because standing suddenly seemed like a confession of legs gone weak. The lamp hissed between them. When he finally spoke, his voice was pitched low, almost gentle, and aimed at the wall rather than at her.

"When I bought the Caslon figures," he said, "your father threw in a bonus sort. A fleuron — a little leaf ornament, very old cut. He said a document without ornament is a body without a soul." Vogel closed his folder. "I still have it. I've used it on perhaps six documents in three years. Private ones. Ones that mattered."

Isandro said nothing. Her throat had closed around every possible reply.

"He was right, your father." The clerk settled his spectacles. "About the soul." And then, moving toward the door, without turning, without inflection, in the voice of a man reading a shipping manifest: "The commission's archive is catalogued by district and date. Ledger entries older than the occupation are scheduled for destruction as pre-war material. Next week, most likely. If anyone wanted to consult those records before then, they should hurry."

The door closed behind him.

Isandro sat alone in the schoolroom under the hissing lamp, in the chair where her brother's name had been tried and — tonight, narrowly, inexplicably — found consistent. Her hands, she noticed distantly, were shaking, and she pressed them flat against her thighs and thought of type cases, of the discipline of the compartments, every letter in its cell, chaos defeated by order. She would need that discipline now. Because the archive — the ledger with three years of smuggled names in it, the list of everyone she had saved, sitting in a cabinet scheduled to become ash —

Because if it burned, the disappeared would vanish twice, and no one, ever, would be able to prove they had existed at all.

She stood. Outside, the dusk had gone fully dark, and the church clock struck the hour, and somewhere between one bell and the next she understood exactly what she would have to do, and how little time there was to do it.

The shop was sealed — but seals were only wax, and wax was only a material, and materials, in her experience, could be worked.
