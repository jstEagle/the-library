## 15. What Ferren Saw

The bakery smelled of ash and sugar for three days after.

Ferren knew because he went there every morning, the way he went everywhere every morning — on a circuit, counting. Counting was his work now, though nobody paid him for it and nobody had asked him to do it. He counted soldiers at the checkpoint by the mill. He counted wagons in the requisition yard. He counted the windows of the commission house that had been lit all night, three nights running, which meant the signing was close, which meant the train was close, which meant everything was close.

He counted because when he was counting he could not think about the bakery.

On the fourth morning the smell had thinned to almost nothing, just a ghost of burnt flour under the cold, and Ferren stood across the street from it with his hands in his pockets and made himself look. The door hung off one hinge. The window was starred white where a rifle butt had gone through it. Inside, past the glass, the ovens stood open like mouths, and someone had knocked every tray onto the floor, and the flour had been swept — no, not swept, *scattered* — into gray drifts against the wall, as if snow had come indoors and died there.

He had watched from behind the woodpile when they took Herr Brandt out. That was the thing nobody said aloud in the town, but everybody carried anyway: somebody always watched, and watching did nothing, and you watched anyway.

Ferren had counted eleven soldiers that morning. Two at the bakery door. Two holding back the small crowd that gathered the way crowds gather everywhere, quickly and without being called. Seven in a line against the far wall with their rifles down, which Ferren had thought, for one bright stupid second, meant nothing would happen. Rifles down. Rifles down meant standing down. Rifles down meant over.

Then an officer said something Ferren didn't hear, or half-heard, a word like *paperwork*, and the rifles came up, and they were not down anymore.

Herr Brandt had turned toward the crowd at the last moment. Not toward the soldiers. Toward the people. As if the people were the thing he wanted to look at last. His lips moved. Ferren was too far away to read them. He had spent weeks teaching himself to read mouths across distances — a useful skill for a boy who counted troop movements — and this once, the one time it mattered, the distance defeated him. Whatever Herr Brandt said to the town before they shot him stayed shut inside his mouth forever, and Ferren would carry that too, the not-knowing, stacked on top of all the other things he carried.

Afterward the soldiers nailed a paper to the post beside the bakery door. Ferren crossed the street when they were gone and read it twice. It said the man had been executed for possession of falsified documents. It said the documents were traced to their source. It said further acts of forgery would be met with equivalent measures.

*Equivalent.* Ferren turned the word over. It was a printer's word, or a clerk's word, clean and balanced, both sides weighing the same. A dead man weighed the same as a piece of paper. That was what equivalent meant.

He ran then. He didn't decide to run; his legs decided, and he let them, all the way through the tannery lane and the goose market and up the hill, past the church with its bells that hadn't rung since the occupation began, until he reached the print shop and hammered on the side door with both fists.

* * *

Tomás Underhill opened the door before the third knock, which meant she'd seen him coming, which meant she saw everything, which Ferren had known since the first week and had never once said out loud until the night he'd told her he knew her secret, and she'd gone still as set type, and then she'd let him in.

Now she looked at him and whatever she saw made her step back from the doorway without a word and leave it open behind her.

He got as far as the composing stone. The shop was dim, smelling of ink and oil and the particular metallic cold of lead type, a smell that had become, without his noticing, the smell of safety. There was a galley half-set on the stone, a job ticket clipped to its frame. He stared at it. The letters swam. He had counted thousands of things in his life and here was a thing he could not count: lines of type, rows and rows, and his eyes slid off them like rain off glass.

"Ferren." Tomás's voice — low, pitched down, the brother-voice, though they were alone. "What is it? What happened?"

He opened his mouth.

Nothing came out.

He tried again. His throat worked. His chest worked. Somewhere inside him there were words — he could feel them, whole sentences, whole hours of what he'd seen, lined up like type in a case waiting for the compositor's hand — and none of them would come. His mouth opened and closed. He heard himself make a sound, a small dry click, like a stick of type dropped on stone.

Tomás came around the composing stone fast. She took his face in both hands. Her fingers smelled of solvent and lead.

"Breathe," she said. "Just breathe. In through your nose."

He breathed. It didn't help. Nothing was wrong with his breathing. Something was wrong with everything else.

"Is it the soldiers? Did they see you at the shop?"

He shook his head.

"Is someone hurt?"

He started to shake his head again and stopped, because the answer was yes, actually, yes, someone was hurt, someone was hurt worse than hurt, and the shape of it rose in him like floodwater and he doubled over and Tomás caught him under the arms and steered him into the chair by the stove, the good chair, the one Father Underhill sat in when his lungs were bad, and she crouched in front of him and held his hands between hers and did not ask anything else for a long time.

The stove ticked. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard shifted — the father, moving, or trying to move. Ferren counted the ticks. Eleven. Twelve. Thirteen. Thirteen soldiers. He stopped counting.

When Tomás spoke again her voice had changed, dropped its guard, gone soft and low in a way he'd only heard once before, the night she'd shown him the rice-paper names hidden in the type cases.

"It was the bakery, wasn't it."

Not a question. She'd already heard. Of course she'd already heard — word traveled through this town faster than the army's own dispatches, and the print shop was where all words eventually pooled.

"They shot him." The words tore coming out, ragged, like paper ripped instead of cut. "They put him against the wall and they shot him and I watched and I couldn't — I couldn't do anything, I just watched, I counted them, I *counted* them, why do I always count—"

"Because counting keeps you alive," Tomás said. "It kept me alive too, once. Different counting. Same reason." Her thumb pressed hard against the back of his hand. "Ferren. Listen to me. You are twelve years old and you watched something no one should watch and you walked here afterward instead of lying down in the road, and I need you to know that walking here was the bravest part. Do you hear me?"

He heard her. He nodded. And then the rest of it came up out of him in pieces, in fragments he couldn't order: the officer saying paperwork, the rifles going up, Herr Brandt turning toward the crowd, the mouth moving, the mouth moving and Ferren too far away, too far, always too far, and then the sound — and here he stopped, because the sound was the thing, the sound was the thing that had broken something in him, and he found that even now, safe, held, warm, he could not describe it, could not get his mouth around it, and so he said nothing about the sound and Tomás, who understood everything, did not ask.

She wrapped him in the blanket off the press cot. She heated water and made him drink it with honey stirred in, though the honey was nearly gone and he knew what honey cost now, he knew what everything cost. She sat with him while afternoon came down gray through the front windows, the windows with the names of the business painted in gold leaf — UNDERHILL & SON — and neither of them looked at the SON.

Upstairs, coughing. Then quiet. Then Mateo's footsteps on the stair, and Mateo came down into the shop with his sleeves rolled and ink to the second knuckle, took one look at Ferren bundled in the blanket, one longer look at Tomás, and said nothing except to put another log on the fire and sit down on the crate opposite and stay.

That was the thing about these two, Ferren thought, from inside his silence. They had built a language out of not-saying. He had lived among adults who said everything and meant nothing, soldiers who announced their orders in a loud clear voice and shot a baker over a piece of paper. These two said almost nothing and meant everything. He envied them the way you envy warmth through a window.

* * *

He didn't speak for six days.

They let him stay. Nobody discussed it; there was simply a blanket folded on the press cot each evening and a bowl at supper, and Ferren washed dishes without being asked and swept the shop and learned, without being taught, to keep his eyes off the hollow sorts in case 14 where the blank permits slept. He was part of the machine now. He understood that. Machines don't have to talk.

But he talked to himself, silently, constantly, a running ledger of the days. Day two: soldiers repainted the notice on the bakery post, fresh white paper over weathered white paper. Day three: the commission's clerks carried boxes into the archive office until midnight; he counted nine boxes. Day four: Tomás burned something in the stove that wasn't wood — he smelled the scorch of paper and pretended he hadn't. Day five: Mateo came back from the market with a split lip and wouldn't say where it came from, and Tomás cleaned it for him with a tenderness that Ferren looked away from because some things belong to other people. Day six: the bells.

The bells rang at noon. All of them. Every church in the town, ringing wild and unscheduled, and Ferren ran to the window with everyone else in the street below, and the news moved up the hill like water finding its level: the armistice terms were agreed, the delegates had signed the protocol across the river, the signing of the full instrument would follow within days, and the evacuation train — the last one, the only one — would depart the morning after.

One train. Ferren stood at the window and felt the sentence land in him like a stone dropped into deep water. One train, limited berths, lists drawn by the occupying command. He had no papers. He had no name on any list. He was an orphan of nowhere in particular; the war had eaten his village before it ate his parents, and there was no authority anywhere on earth that would vouch for him. He had survived by being uncounted. Now being uncounted meant being left.

He went back inside. Tomás was standing in the middle of the shop with the news still on her face, doing arithmetic with her eyes — Ferren had learned to read that expression, the family-counting look, three souls, one seat — and when she saw him she smoothed it away too late.

That night he lay on the press cot listening to the house settle, and the sound came back to him. The sound he hadn't been able to describe. It filled the dark above him, and he pressed his palms against his ears and it didn't help, sounds like that don't live in ears, they live deeper, and he understood suddenly, lying there, why he hadn't spoken.

Words were how the soldiers did it. Words on paper. Possession of falsified documents. Equivalent measures. Every horror in this town arrived as a document first — a requisition notice, a permit, a ledger, a list — and Herr Brandt was dead because of paper, and the whole war was dead men because of paper, and if Ferren opened his mouth and let words out, he was afraid of what kind of word would come out. What if his words were the same kind. What if everything he ever said from now on sounded, to him, like a death warrant being read aloud.

So he kept the case locked. Six days. Seven.

* * *

On the seventh night, Tomás found him at the type cases.

It was near midnight. The shop lamp was turned low. She thought he was asleep; he'd learned that she checked on him the way she checked the stove damper, quietly, twice a night. But tonight he was standing at case 9, the small capitals, with a composing stick in his hand he didn't remember picking up.

He didn't know type. He'd never been taught. But he'd watched Mateo for months, and watching was what he did, and his fingers seemed to know things his mind had declined to learn. He tilted a sort into the light. An F. He set it in the stick. Then he hunted, slowly, clumsily, for the next letter.

Tomás didn't startle him. She came and stood beside him and waited, the way she waited for ink to dry, until he glanced up.

"What are we spelling?" she asked softly.

His throat closed. Opened. He looked down at the single F in the stick, alone, and something about it — one letter, standing upright, asking nothing — unlocked the door.

"Ferren," he said.

His voice came out rusted, cracked down the middle, barely his own. But it came out.

Tomás went very still. He could feel her not-making-a-thing-of-it, feel the enormous effort of her staying ordinary, and he loved her for it with a fierce sudden completeness that frightened him a little.

"That's a fine name," she said. "Short. Sets well. Won't break a line."

"My name." He swallowed. "Will it be in the book?"

She frowned gently. "What book?"

"The book you're making." He'd seen her at night, candle burning past two, setting line after line from memory, the broadside growing galley by galley under the cloth on the flatbed press. He'd never asked. Asking was talking. "The one with all the names. The ones they took. From the ledger."

"Yes," she said. "Every one. I carry the ledger in my head now, and I'm setting it all — every name, every family. When the commission signs, it goes up on their own door, and after that it goes out into the world in ink, and ink doesn't die the way paper does. Ink gets reprinted. Ink is forever if someone keeps setting it."

"Ink is forever," Ferren repeated. He looked at the F in his stick.

"So will mine be in it?" His voice was steadier now, steadier than it had any right to be, and he realized that this was what he had been unable to say for seven days, this one thing, this small enormous thing. "I'm not in any ledger. I'm not in the town book, or the parish book, or their lists. I'm not on the berth manifest. I'm not anywhere. If I die here tomorrow, there'll be no paper that says I was ever—" His breath snagged. "Everyone who disappeared, they're disappearing twice, you said. Once when they take them and once when nobody writes them down. I'm already halfway to the second one. I was born halfway there."

The lamp hissed. Upstairs, the faint rattle of the father's breathing, there and then not-there.

Tomás took the composing stick gently out of his hands. She set it on the stone. Then she took both his shoulders and turned him to face her fully, and in the lamplight her face was bare — no brother-mask, no printer's calm — just Isandro Underhill, looking at him straight.

"You listen to me, Ferren-of-nowhere-in-particular," she said. "You are in my book. You've been in my book since the night you stood in this shop and told me you knew my secret and asked for passage out, and I decided right then, while you were still talking, that if I ever set a list of the saved alongside my list of the taken, yours would be the first name on it. Not because you blackmailed me." The corner of her mouth moved. "Because you counted soldiers at ten years old to keep strangers alive, and because you walked here from the bakery when walking was the bravest thing anyone did that day. Names aren't given by ledgers. Ledgers only steal them. Names are given by whoever loves you enough to set them in lead. And I am telling you, on my father's type, on my brother's grave-name, on everything I have left: yours is set. It's done. It cannot be taken back."

Ferren's eyes were burning. He let them.

"And when the book is printed," he managed, "I want to be there. When the ink goes on. I want to pull the lever myself."

"You will."

"And I want it to say more than Ferren. I want—" He fumbled, twelve years old and reaching for a word bigger than he was. "I want it to say I was accounted for. Like the notice says. Equivalent measures. Well, I want my equivalent. Somebody has to account for me."

"Then we'll account for you," Isandro said. "Line for line."

He slept that night without the sound coming back. Or almost without it.

* * *

In the morning the house woke to knocking at the street door — the official knock, three measured blows, the kind that came with paper behind it.

Mateo reached the window first. "Two of them," he said, low. "Commission coats. And—" He paused, and Ferren saw him go careful. "And the clerk. The one who knows the family."

Isandro was already pulling her collar straight, already becoming Tomás, jaw squaring, voice dropping half a register somewhere behind her teeth. On the table the berth list lay where she'd left it, and Ferren watched her fold it away into her jacket with three quick movements, and he saw her face as she did it — the arithmetic still running underneath, three names, one seat, the geometry tightening by the hour —

—and he stepped forward to stand beside her, shoulder to shoulder, the way family stands, and said, "Open it. I'll do the counting."

She looked at him then, quick and startled, and something passed between them that needed no words at all.

She opened the door.
