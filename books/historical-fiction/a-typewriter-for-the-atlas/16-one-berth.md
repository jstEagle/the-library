## 16. One Berth

The list came on a Thursday morning, nailed to the requisition office door with four brass tacks and the casual cruelty of official paper.

ISANDRO saw it before she understood it — saw only that a crowd had gathered in the square in the gray light, that men stood with their hats in their hands the way they did at funerals, that no one was speaking above a murmur. Then she was through the crowd, Mateo's hand at her elbow as if she might faint, though it was not faintness she feared.

EVACUATION TRAIN — DEPARTURE SATURDAY, 06:00 HOURS
ONE BERTH PER FAMILY, BY ALLOCATION OF THE OCCUPYING COMMAND
FOLLOWING FAMILIES ARE ENTITLED TO SUBMIT ONE NAME:

And beneath the heading, in the neat, indifferent hand of some clerk who had never set foot in this town until a month ago, a list of eleven families. Eleven names for a town of two hundred souls still standing.

Underhill was third from the top.

She read it three times. The letters didn't change. One berth. One name per family, submitted to the commandant's office by Friday noon, after which the list would be closed and sealed and no appeal entertained. She knew the vocabulary; she had forged enough permits to know how these documents breathed. *No appeal entertained* meant no appeal would be heard. It did not mean no appeal existed. It meant only that the people who wrote it had decided, in advance, that hearing one would be inconvenient.

"Third from the top," Mateo said quietly beside her. "That's good. That's almost good."

"It's arithmetic," she said. "It's not good."

Around them the crowd began to move, breaking apart into family clusters, and she could see the same calculation happening in every cluster — the silent counting of faces against the single word *one*. The Widow Sarrail with her two daughters, fourteen and nine, standing very straight between them as though her spine could be divided. Old Pujol, whose wife had died in the first winter, touching his cap brim over and over as if the answer were hidden under it. The Ferran boy, seventeen, looking at his mother, and his mother looking at the ground, both of them already knowing which of them the other would say.

Isandro counted too. She couldn't stop herself.

Father. Who could not walk to the corner without resting twice now, whose cough had begun to sound like something tearing loose deep inside him, who weighed less than the case of ten-point Caslon he'd carried up the shop stairs thirty years ago. Father could not survive the journey alone — that was not sentiment, it was diagnosis. Dr. Amiel had said it plainly, in the low voice he saved for truths: *if he goes, someone must go with him, or he dies somewhere between here and the river.* And even with someone — even with her — the doctor had given it perhaps even odds. A week of travel, in November, in a cattle car or worse, with no medicine and no heat. But *perhaps*. Perhaps was more than nothing.

Mateo. Who had said, the night the rumors hardened into the timetable posted at the station, with the flat calm he used for setting a difficult line of type: *I'm not leaving. Don't ask me again.* He was twenty-three years old and healthy and the occupying command wanted printers — they had said so, they had posted *that* notice too, skilled tradesmen may register for labor service, and Mateo had walked past the notice every day for a week without slowing his step, which was its own kind of answer. He would not go. He would not be persuaded. She had known him since he was fifteen and she was thirteen and he was sweeping type dust off the shop floor with a paintbrush because Father said a broom scattered the sorts; she knew the geography of his stubbornness better than her own.

Ferren. Who had no name on any list at all. No family, no allocation, no entitlement, no claim. An orphan the town had fed out of habit and the war had made invisible. Eleven years old. He had stood at the edge of the crowd that morning — Isandro had seen him, small and still among the legs of adults, reading the notice with the careful, moving-lips concentration of a boy who'd taught himself letters from broadsides — and then he had looked up and found her across the square, and his face had done something terrible and brief, a whole argument collapsing behind his eyes, before he turned and walked away with his hands in his pockets like it didn't matter, like he'd never hoped, like hope was a thing that happened to other children.

One berth. Three names. Four people who loved each other badly and completely, and one seat.

* * *

Father heard the news from her, sitting up in his bed under the sloped ceiling of the back room, where they'd moved him when the stairs became a mountain. She told him plainly — he had earned plain speech, and lies tired him faster than truth.

"One berth," he repeated. His voice was a whisper with edges on it. "Per family."

"Yes."

"And they count us as a family. You and me." He almost smiled. "After all these years of the town calling you Tomás. The army has finally made an honest household of us."

"Don't joke about it."

"Somebody has to." He shifted against the pillows and the effort cost him; she watched him pay it. "Listen to me. This is not a difficult question."

"It is entirely a difficult question."

"No." He raised one hand — it shook, and he looked at it with distaste, as though it were an apprentice failing at a simple task — and pressed it flat against the blanket. "The berth is yours. You take it. You go Saturday. You carry nothing but yourself and whatever you can memorize, and you get to the commission, and you tell them what happened here. That is the job. I am assigning it to you the way I assigned you your first job in this shop, which was also too big for you, and which you did anyway."

"I'm not going anywhere without you."

"You are going without me precisely because I cannot go. Do you think I want my daughter—" He stopped. Coughed. The cough went long and wet and ended in a silence that frightened her more than any sound. When he could speak again it was barely above breath. "Do you think I want you to stay in this town when the armistice is signed and the armies move on and nobody remembers what happened here except the people who did it? They took the ledger, Isandro. They have our names. Every family we helped, every permit we forged, written down in my own careful hand because I insisted on recording everything, God forgive me, because I thought a ledger was honesty. If you leave and live, you are a witness. If you leave and die, you are still a witness — there will be papers, there will be questions. If you stay—" His eyes found hers and held them, and they were clear, clearer than they had been in weeks, lucid in the way the dying sometimes were, spending their last clarity like coins. "If you stay, you vanish. Like the others. Unrecorded. Unwitnessed. That is the one thing I cannot allow. I have let you risk everything else. Not that."

She sat with her hand over his shaking one and said nothing, because anything she said would either be a promise she wasn't sure she could keep or a refusal that would spend strength he didn't have.

He seemed to understand. He closed his eyes. "Think about it like composition," he murmured, drifting toward sleep. "You don't set the line you wish the sentence said. You set the line the sentence needs. Go home to the shop. Look at the case. The answer's in the type."

* * *

The answer was not in the type. The answer was in Ferren, who appeared at the shop door that evening just before closing, when Mateo was locking down the press and Isandro was banking the stove, and stood there with snow melting in his hair and his jaw set in a way she recognized — the way a person looks when they've rehearsed a speech so many times the rehearsal has worn all the feeling off it.

"They won't take me," he said. "On the train."

"No," Isandro said. Because lying to him had become impossible sometime around the execution, around the night he stopped speaking and started watching her with those old, unchildlike eyes. "They won't."

"I checked the rules. I read the notice four times. One berth per family listed. Orphans aren't families. Orphans aren't anything. There's no box for us." He said *us* and Isandro felt it land somewhere under her ribs. How many orphans were left in this town? Six? Eight? The number kept changing, the way it did, children absorbed into distant aunts or simply gone quiet, gone uncounted. "So I did the sums," Ferren went on. "Eleven families. Eleven berths. But the Sarrails — Madame Sarrail can't walk to the station. She'll send one daughter and keep the other, or she'll send the little one and the big one stays, that's what she'll do, I've watched them. And Pujol — Pujol told the baker he's not going, he says at his age the train and the town are the same thing, a box that moves. So really it's nine. Maybe eight. Out of two hundred people." His voice stayed flat, recitation-flat, but his hands had come out of his pockets and were working at each other. "I'm good at sums. I was always good at counting things. Soldiers. Trains. Berths."

"Ferren—"

"I don't want yours." He said it fast, like pulling a splinter. "I know what you're thinking. I've seen you thinking it since the notice went up. I don't want yours. Your father needs it. Or Mateo — no, Mateo won't go, everyone knows Mateo won't go — so your father needs it, and you need to go with your father, that's just obvious, that's just the sums. I don't want yours." He swallowed. The snow was dripping off his hair onto the floorboards and he didn't seem to know it. "I wanted to tell you the sums. In case you thought — in case you were going to do something stupid. Because of me. I wanted you to know I'd rather stay here forever than be the reason you did something stupid."

Mateo had come to stand beside her at the door. None of them moved. The stove ticked as it cooled.

"What will you do?" Mateo asked gently. "After Saturday. When the train's gone."

Ferren shrugged, and for a moment he was eleven years old again, and the shrug was the shrug of a boy asked what he wanted for his birthday. "Stay alive. I'm good at that too. Better than anybody I know, actually." Then, quieter, looking at the floor: "I just thought somebody should say the sums out loud. So nobody does the wrong ones."

He left. Isandro watched him go up the street, small against the falling dark, hands back in his pockets, and felt the geometry of the problem close another degree. He didn't want her berth. That was somehow worse. A claim she could refuse was a burden she could argue with; a renunciation freely offered was just weight, added to weight, added to weight.

* * *

She worked late. She always worked late now; sleep had become a country she visited rather than lived in. Mateo stayed, though she told him twice to go home, and sat at the composing stone doing the small maintenance tasks that calmed his hands — cleaning the quoins, distributing a forme of dead type back into the cases, each letter finding its compartment by touch while his eyes stayed somewhere far away.

"Say it," she said finally, without looking up from the galley she was correcting. "You've been circling it all evening."

"Your father's right."

"My father's right and it changes nothing. If I go, I go alone, because he can't travel and Mateo won't leave and Ferren isn't allowed, and I will spend the rest of my life being the one Underhill who got out. I'll be a witness, yes. I'll stand in front of the commission and recite names. And every night I'll hear this press in my head, running, with nobody to run it, and my father dying forty miles behind me in a room that smells of ink, and a boy on the street with no box on any form for him. That's the witness they'd get. Half a person reading a list."

"And if you stay?"

"If I stay, I'm nobody's witness. I'm a name in a ledger in a commission archive, waiting to be mislaid." She set down the bodkin. Her fingers ached; they had been aching for weeks, split at the cuticles from the forging, and she looked at them now with something like detachment, as tools belonging to someone else. "Unless."

"Unless what?" Mateo had gone still. She knew that stillness. It was the stillness of a man holding a line of type perfectly level, knowing that one tremor spoils the page.

"Unless I make myself a witness that can't be mislaid." She heard the words come out of her and knew they had been forming for days, maybe weeks, in the place below decision where decisions are actually made. "The ledger's gone. The commission has it. But I copied names onto rice paper, and more than that — I remember them. All of them. Every family, every permit, every name Father recorded and every name we never dared record. I could set them. Tonight, tomorrow, by Friday. A broadside. Every name taken, every name smuggled, every name this town lost — set in Father's own type and printed on his own press and pasted on the commission's door the hour they sign. You can't lose a thousand copies the way you lose one ledger. Paper multiplies. Paper escapes. Paper gets carried away in pockets and read aloud in cafés in cities that have never heard of this town."

Mateo was quiet a long moment. "That's not a plan for survival," he said. "That's a plan for a monument. With you inside it."

"Maybe."

"*Maybe?*" He stood, and the composing stick in his hand caught the lamplight. "Isandro. Take the berth. Take your father if he can be moved, take him and damn the odds — Amiel said even, didn't he, he said even — and let me stay and print your broadside. I can set it. You taught me; I'm fast now, I'm nearly as fast as you. Paste it on their door, mail copies downriver, do everything you'd do. And you're alive, and he's alive, and the names are out in the world anyway. Same monument. Nobody buried under it."

"And when they come asking who printed it? When they seal the shop again like they did after the bakery? You'd hang alone."

"Hanging alone is a trade I'm offering you. Take it."

"No."

"Why not? Give me a reason that isn't your father's face."

She looked at him across the composing stone, across thirty years of lead dust ground into the grain of the wood, and loved him with a violence that made the truth easy and unbearable at once. "Because you'd do it beautifully," she said. "And I'd never forgive myself for letting you. Because it's my father's ledger they stole and my forgeries they're hunting and my name — my real name, the one nobody in this town knows — that belongs on that page. Because I have spent six years being Tomás Underhill, a man who doesn't exist, signing documents for a man who doesn't exist, and I am not going to let the war end with me as a footnote in someone else's courage." She picked up the bodkin again. Her voice dropped. "And because someone has to decide, Mateo, and I'm the one who keeps the case. That's how Father put it once. The one who keeps the case decides the sentence."

Mateo sat back down slowly. For a while neither of them spoke, and the shop settled around them, ticking and cooling, full of ten thousand letters in their compartments, each one patient, each one waiting to be chosen.

"There's one flaw in your geometry," he said at last, quietly. "You've counted the berth three ways — you, your father, Ferren. You've forgotten to count the fourth option."

"There is no fourth option."

"There is. It's the one where nobody takes it. Where the train leaves Saturday with an empty seat and the commandant's clerk writes *declined* next to Underhill, and the town talks about it for fifty years." He met her eyes. "I'm just saying it exists. I'm not saying you'll choose it. But you should know it's on the stone."

She looked at the type case on the wall — Father's case, his father's before him, every compartment worn smooth by a century of thumbs — and thought: *you don't set the line you wish the sentence said.*

Outside, the snow thickened. Two days until Friday noon, when the lists closed. Three until the train. Somewhere across the river the delegates were arriving, hotels filling with men whose signatures would decide whether her broadside was history or treason, and Isandro Underhill — Tomás to the town, daughter to one dying man, ghost to herself — reached for the candle and pulled the first case off the wall.

There were names to set.
