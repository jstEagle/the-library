## 17. The Night Before Signing

The shop smelled of tallow and cold metal when Mateo came down the stairs past midnight. He had not meant to sleep. He had lain on the cot above the composing room listening to the sound of Isandro working — the small brass click of the type stick, the whisper of her hand across the case — and the sound had done what sleep could not: it had made him certain of what she intended.

She sat at the stone with a single candle burning low, its flame bent by the draft under the door. She was in her brother's shirt again, sleeves rolled to the elbow, her hair bound back so tightly it changed the shape of her face into Tomás's face. Before her lay a chase half-filled with lead — column after column of names, set in the small pica they used for funeral notices.

"You should be resting," she said without turning around. "Tomorrow will be long."

"So will tonight, apparently." He crossed to the case and stood beside her. The candle threw their shadows up the wall, enormous and wavering, two giants bent over a village of letters. "How many?"

"Two hundred and eleven." She said it evenly, but he saw her thumb press flat against the stick as if to hold something down. "I've done sixty-eight since dark."

Mateo looked at the columns. Each name was set letter by letter, chosen from the case by hand, locked against its neighbors so tightly that together they could not fall. That was the whole craft, his father had said once, before the war took his father: you make each piece small enough to be nothing alone, and then you refuse to let them separate.

"Read me some," he said, and pulled a second stool from under the stone.

She glanced at him — suspicion first, then something softer — and read. "Alda Ferreira and child. The Vás brothers, Joaquín and Emil. Old Marta, no surname ever given, who mended sails. Piotr Lenz. The three Ondarza girls." Her voice did not break. It went quieter instead, like a river going underground. "Sixty-nine. Serafín Cuello, who drove the milk cart. Seventy—"

"I'll take the next ones." He reached for a composing stick from the rack. "You compose. I'll justify. Your spacing's gone sloppy anyway; grief makes wide spaces."

"It does not." But she slid the galley toward him, and for a while there was only the work: the tick of type against type, the candle guttering, the wind worrying at the shutters the way it had all winter, like something large pacing outside and unable to get in.

They had filled four pages of the broadside before Mateo set his stick down and said what he had come downstairs to say.

"The berth is yours, Isandro."

Her hands stopped over the case. Not a flinch — she was too practiced for flinching — but a stillness, the stillness of an animal deciding whether it has been seen.

"The manifest says Underhill," he went on. "One name. One seat. Father signed it over this afternoon; I watched him do it. It's yours, and at six o'clock you'll walk onto that train, and I'll carry your bag, and that's the end of the argument because there isn't one."

"There isn't," she agreed. "Because I'm not taking it."

"You are."

"Mateo."

"You *are*." His voice came out harder than he meant, and the candle flame shivered. He made himself lower it. "Listen to me. I've listened to everyone else for months. Father, the neighbors, Ferren, the whole town telling me what has to be done, who has to be saved, whose sacrifice is necessary. So you'll listen to me now, once, all the way through, and then you can go back to setting type."

She folded her hands in her lap. It was the posture she used with difficult customers, patient and immovable, and he hated how well he knew it.

"Your father is dying," Mateo said. "You know it, I know it, he knows it better than any of us. If you stay, you watch him die in a town full of soldiers, and then what? You think the commission will thank you for your list? You think they'll pin a medal on the printer's daughter and open the shop back up? They'll burn the broadside off their own door and call it sedition, and they'll be right, because that's exactly what it is. It's beautiful and it's true and it's treason, and treason gets people shot, Isandro. I've watched it happen from a window. You haven't, and I would keep it that way if it cost me everything."

"And if I leave?" she said. "If I take the berth and ride out tomorrow morning — what do I become? A name that got away. One more person the war swallowed and spat out somewhere safe, who tells the story afterward at other people's tables. 'I knew a girl who stayed.' Past tense. Secondhand." She shook her head. "The names in this chase don't have anyone left to speak them, Mateo. Their brothers are dead or taken or scattered across three provinces. If I don't set them, nobody sets them. If I don't stay to paste them up, they stay in my head until my head stops, and then those people never existed at all. Twice vanished. I told you that the night we hid the rice paper in the cases. I meant it then."

"That was before the list was final. That was before—" He stopped, because the rest was before too much. Before the interrogation lamp. Before Ferren stopped speaking for eleven days. Before her father's handkerchief began to need washing twice a day.

"Before what?" she asked gently, which was worse than if she'd argued.

"Before I understood what staying costs." He got up and walked to the window, because it was easier to say to the glass. Through the shutter slats he could see the railyard lights, and beyond them the black shoulder of the bridge where the delegates would sign at noon. "I've spent three years learning this trade from your family. Three years of your father teaching me to space a line, of you teaching me everything else — the permits, the paper, how to lie with a straight face and a steady hand. Do you know what I thought I was learning? A trade. And then somewhere along the way I looked up and I wasn't learning a trade anymore, I was living inside a life, and the life was yours, and I never said so because there was never a safe hour to say it in. There's never a safe hour in this town. There's just hours, and they run out." He turned around. "So I'm saying it now, in the unsafe hour, with the candle burning down. I love you. Not sideways, not through the work. Plainly. And I am asking you to get on that train, and I'm telling you that if you stay, I stay, and if we're both here when they come for the printer of that broadside, then your father's last act on earth was saving one person and we've wasted it between the two of us."

The silence after was long. The candle burned with a small steady sound, like breathing.

Isandro rose and came to him. She put her hand flat against his chest, over his heart, the way you check whether a press is running true.

"Do you know why I can't take it?" she said quietly. "It's not only the names. It's that every good thing I've ever done, I've done standing in this room. When I cut my first letter, Father held my hands inside his so I'd learn the angle. When I forged my first permit, it was for Señora Ibáñez, and she kissed my cheek through the disguise and called me Tomás, and I let her, because the lie was the door and behind the door was someone getting out alive. This shop is the only place I have ever been entirely myself — both of myself, the girl and the brother, the printer and the forger. If I leave it, I survive, yes. But I survive as someone I invented. And the people in this chase —" she touched the galley of names, lightly, the way you'd touch a sleeping child — "they didn't get to invent anyone. They were taken as themselves. The least I owe them is to stay as myself and write them down."

"And yourself?" Mateo asked. "Who writes you down?"

"You will." She almost smiled. "If it comes to it. Set it in the same type. *Isandro Underhill, who kept the books.* Put my real name in. That's all the monument I want."

"That's not a bargain, that's an epitaph. I'm refusing the epitaph. I want the wife, not the memorial."

"Mateo—"

"No. Hear the arithmetic, since you love arithmetic." He stepped back and counted it off on his fingers, the way Ferren counted soldiers. "Three names on the berth list. One seat. Father can't travel — Dr. Ruiz said it in front of both of us, and you nodded like you were agreeing about the weather. Ferren has no papers, no claim, no family to vouch for him; without a forged manifest entry he doesn't board at all. Which leaves you. You. The one person the occupying command already suspects, the one person who broke into their requisition office, the one person whose face the clerk from the commission studied for two hours under a lamp three weeks ago. You are the *last* person they should let on that train and the *first* person who needs to be on it, and you're standing here setting a treason broadside by candlelight because—" he gestured at the case, the candle, the whole stubborn architecture of her— "because duty. Duty is a fine word for it, Isandro, but I notice duty always asks you specifically, and never asks me, and never once has asked whether the people who love you get a vote."

"They get a vote," she said. "I'm overruling it."

"That's not how votes work!"

"It's exactly how votes work in an occupation." For a moment the fire came up in her, the thing he had seen in her since the first week he arrived at the shop, the iron under the ink. Then it banked again, and she was tired, and young, and her hands had ink in every crease. "Come here," she said. "Help me finish page five. We'll argue while we work. We argue best while we work."

He should have refused. Some part of him understood, distantly, that every line he set tonight was a line closer to the thing he feared — that the broadside was not a document but a fuse, and they were both holding the match. But his hands went to the case the way they always did, because three years of habit is stronger than one hour of sense, and because loving her had never once been a decision he made. It was a fact of the shop, like the weight of the press, like the smell of the ink. You didn't vote on it. You worked under it.

So they worked. Page five: the men taken from the quarry road in October. Page six: the families from across the river whose names Isandro had learned from Ferren's whispered counts — the boy had kept tallies scratched into a slate, forty-one entries in a child's uneven hand, and Isandro had copied every one into memory and burned the slate herself rather than let it be found. Page seven was short, and ended with the whole town's dead of the winter, set smaller, in the type reserved for prices and dates.

Near three in the morning the candle drowned in its own wax, and Isandro lit another from the stove coals without stopping her reading aloud. Mateo justified lines and heard her voice go through the alphabet of the lost, and understood that this was her prayer, that she had turned typesetting into a liturgy, and that no argument he owned could reach a woman mid-liturgy.

But he tried once more, near dawn, when the last line was locked in the chase and the broadside lay complete on the stone — eight columns of names, two hundred and eleven souls, ready for the roller and the paper.

"If I carried you to that platform," he said, "and put you on that train bodily — you'd hate me."

"Yes."

"For years."

"Yes." She looked at him across the finished form. "And you'd be right to do it anyway. That's what makes it unbearable, Mateo. Both things are right. Getting on that train is right. Staying is right. The war made a world where the right things kill each other, and we're just the ones left holding them." She pressed her palms flat on the stone, on either side of the chase, framing the names like a doorframe. "So I choose the one I can live inside. Staying, I can live inside. Leaving, I'd just be walking around in a shape that used to be me, waiting for it to wear through."

"And me? Where do I fit in the shape?"

"Beside it," she said. "Or nowhere. I won't order you to stay. I've ordered enough people's lives with my lies."

"That's the problem," Mateo said. "You never ordered mine. You just assumed I'd follow, and I resent it completely, and I'm going to do it." He picked up the ink knife and began to work it across the plate, blackening the faces of two hundred and eleven letters, his hands steady, his heart anything but. "At six o'clock I'll carry your bag to the platform. You'll give the berth to whoever needs it — I already know you, so don't pretend you haven't decided that either. And then the bells ring, and they sign, and we paste this to the commission's door in daylight, in front of God and the delegates and every rifle in the garrison."

"You've thought this through."

"I've thought about nothing else for a week."

Outside, the sky was going the color of zinc. Somewhere across the river, in the hotel where the delegates slept behind sandbags, a clock struck the quarter hour, and the sound came flat over the water like a word spoken through a wall.

Isandro blew out the second candle, though the room was already grey. In the dark she found his hand among the tools and held it, ink to ink.

"One condition," she said.

"Name it."

"When it's done — whatever happens when it's done — you take Ferren and you go north. Promise me the boy sees spring somewhere that isn't here."

Mateo looked at her silhouette against the window, at the small straight figure that had held a town together out of lead and nerve, and understood that he could promise anything in that moment and she would believe him, because hope was the one forgery she wanted to be true.

"All right," he said, which was not the word *yes*, and they both pretended not to notice.

Then the bell in the church tower began to ring — early, ragged, wrong — and boots sounded in the street below, many boots, moving fast toward the square, and Isandro's grip tightened on his hand as the first shouts came through the shutters:

*"The train — they've moved the train — it boards now!"*
