## 18. The Last Train

The morning of the train came gray and thin, like paper held too long against a window. Isandro woke in the loft above the shop with the broadside finished beneath her floorboards and her berth papers squared on the table beside a cold lamp, and for one full minute she did not move, because moving would begin the day, and the day was the thing she had been dreading and rehearsing for a week.

She dressed as Tomás. It took longer than it used to. The bindings had to be right — she'd be standing on a public platform under the eyes of occupying clerks — and the jacket sat differently now that she'd lost weight over the winter. In the glass above the washstand, Tomás Underhill looked back at her: hollow-cheeked, ink-stained, twenty-six years old forever. She touched the glass where her own face would have been.

"Last job," she told him.

Downstairs, the shop smelled of oil and cold metal. Mateo was already there, which did not surprise her; he had slept three hours at most and looked like it, his collar dark with sweat, his hands wrapped where the splits had opened again overnight. He'd set two galleys of the broadside himself after she collapsed into bed, checking each line against her memory-copy twice without being asked. He didn't say good morning. He said, "The manifest clerk changed. The fat one from Tuesday is gone. There's a new man at the freight desk and nobody knows how careful he is."

"Then we find out how careful he is," Isandro said, "the way we found out everything else."

Ferren was not there yet. That was the problem she'd been turning all night like type in a composing stick: Ferren was not there yet, and the boy had no claim on anything — no name on any list, no family the commission recognized, no papers but the forged birth certificate she'd made him in January, which named him Ferren Alday, born in this parish, father a wheelwright dead of fever. A perfect document. Useless without a berth.

She had decided. She had decided days ago, really, since the night she set the last line of the broadside and felt something in her chest go quiet and final, like a form locked in the chase. But deciding and saying were different trades, and saying it out loud to Mateo while they waited for the boy felt like pulling the press bar down on her own hand.

"Mateo." She kept her voice level, workmanlike. "I want you to take the berth."

He looked up from the galley he was cleaning. "No."

"You haven't heard—"

"I don't need to hear it. No." He set down the brush with exaggerated care, the way he did when he was angry enough to break something and wouldn't let himself. "Isandro. Tomás. Whoever you are this morning. I've watched you give away everything that wasn't nailed down for eight months. Your sleep. Your safety. Your name, twice over. I'm not going to stand on a platform and watch you hand away the last thing, too."

"It isn't mine to keep." She heard her father's cadence come out of her mouth and hated it a little, and needed it more. "It's paper, Mateo. Paper is only worth what's printed on it, and what's printed on it is a boy who'll be shot or starved or worked to death if he stays in this town after the signing. You heard what the sergeant said about unregistered minors. You heard it same as me."

"And you? What happens to registered printers who forge manifests?"

"The commission needs its lists legible," she said. "They won't shoot the handwriting."

"That's not a plan. That's a hope wearing a plan's coat."

Outside, the bells began — not the hour yet, just the half, muffled and slow, as if even the bronze knew to lower its voice today. Somewhere toward the station a whistle sounded, long and low: the engine coming up the valley, early, hungry for its cargo of the saved.

Mateo came around the composing stone and stood close enough that she could smell the ink on him, the lye, the smoke of the night's work. "Come with me," he said quietly. "That's all I'm asking. One berth, one name on the manifest — we put your brother's name there, we've forged harder things before breakfast. We get out. We set up somewhere with a press and no occupation. You don't owe this town your life."

"I owe it my names," she said. "Every one of them."

"They're printed. They're safe. Two hundred and six names under your floorboards waiting to go up on a wall."

"Printed and hidden is not the same as printed and seen." She stepped back, because if she stayed that close she would say yes, she knew she would; her whole body wanted to say yes with an ache like a tooth. "The broadside goes up when they sign. If I'm gone, who pastes it? Who stands in front of the door and makes the clerk look? You can't do it alone, Mateo — they know your face less than mine, but you can't argue with a provost and paste paste at the same time."

"So we both stay. Fine. We both stay, and the berth goes empty, and—"

"The berth doesn't go empty." The words were out before she could soften them. She hadn't meant to say it yet. She'd meant to wait for Ferren, to do it kindly, with the boy's hand in hers so he couldn't refuse. But the bell was counting down and the whistle had sounded again and the truth came out the way truth does when time runs short: fast, ugly, whole. "The berth goes to Ferren. His name goes on the manifest in place of Tomás Underhill. I wrote the forgery last night. It's clean."

Mateo stared at her. And then, terribly, he laughed — one short, cracked note with nothing in it of humor. "Of course it's clean. Of course you did it already." He pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "You decided before you asked me to leave. You asked knowing I'd say no, so you could stand there looking reasonable while I argued with a decision that was made on Tuesday."

"Yes," she said. Because lying to him, after everything, would have been the one unforgivable thing left.

* * *

Ferren arrived at nine, scrubbed raw, wearing a coat two sizes too big that Mateo must have fetched from somewhere, the sleeves rolled four times. He stood in the doorway of the shop and looked at the three of them — Isandro, Mateo, the packed cases, the press under its dust sheet like a body laid out — and he didn't come inside. He'd learned that much about thresholds in his short hard life.

"Train comes at eleven," he said. "I counted the soldiers at the platform this morning. Sixty-two, plus clerks. The new manifest man wears spectacles and checks papers twice."

"You counted the clerks' spectacles," Mateo said.

"Somebody has to count things," Ferren said. "Since everybody else stopped."

Isandro crossed the shop and knelt — knelt, so that they were eye to eye, the way she had done once in the alley behind the bakery when he'd first told her his secret and she'd told him hers, the two of them bound together by the strange arithmetic of what children see. Up close she could see the execution still living somewhere behind his face, the thing that had taken his words for three weeks in February, that had returned them only in the form of questions.

"Ferren Alday," she said. "Born in this parish. Father a wheelwright. Do you remember your papers?"

"Dead of fever," he recited, flat as setting type. "Mother gone before the war. No siblings. Left-handed." He frowned. "Why am I saying them?"

"Because you're saying them at eleven o'clock to a man with spectacles." She took the folded papers from her jacket and held them out. On top, the berth ticket, third class, seat forty-one, car six. Beneath it, the manifest copy with TOMÁS UNDERHILL struck through in careful imitation of a clerk's pen and FERREN ALDAY entered in the margin, initialed, dated. She had practiced the initial for two hours. It was perfect. It was a lie built like a bridge, load-bearing, and it would hold. "This is yours. This is the train. You get on it, and you don't get off until it stops in a city with a harbor."

Ferren looked at the papers and did not take them. He looked at her instead, and she watched him understand — watched the counting mind behind his eyes run the numbers, the way he counted soldiers, the way he counted everything, because counting was how he kept the world from swallowing him.

"That was your name," he said. "Tomás Underhill. That was your seat."

"There are other seats in the world."

"Not for you. Not off this list." His voice climbed, cracked, dropped. "You gave the boy the bread every week. You gave the permits. You gave—" He stopped. When he spoke again it was very quiet, the question underneath finally surfacing after months underwater. "Will my name be in the book?"

And there it was. The question she'd answered for him once before, kneeling in the alley, and here it was again, larger now, because the book in question had grown: the ledger, the broadside, the great terrible account the commission was about to sign and seal and pretend was settled. She took his small chapped hand and closed it over the papers.

"In the book," she said, "and on the wall, and in lead, boy. First page. I'm setting your name myself, tonight, in twelve-point Caslon, and no army on earth can melt it down faster than I can cast it again. Now get on the train."

* * *

The platform was worse than she had imagined, and she had imagined it thoroughly. Sixty-two soldiers by Ferren's count, and it looked like more, bayonets catching the flat light. Clerks at trestle tables with their manifests weighted down with stones. Families pressed against the barrier in a silence worse than weeping, because weeping got you moved along and silence got you ignored. Down the platform, a woman held a birdcage with a cloth over it, and the cloth moved sometimes, faintly, with something alive.

Isandro walked Ferren down the platform herself, Mateo a pace behind, all three of them playing the parts they'd rehearsed: the printer seeing his apprentice off, nothing more, nothing to look at. At the freight desk the man with spectacles took Ferren's papers, read them, read them again, turned to the master manifest, ran one finger down the column of names.

Isandro stood with her hands loose at her sides and her heart going like a platen, and thought: *this is the moment, this is always the moment, the whole craft is this moment* — the second between the reader's eyes and the lie, the instant where ink either holds or breaks. She had lived inside that instant for a year. She would live in it now, whatever happened next.

The finger stopped. The clerk looked up over his spectacles — first at Ferren, small and scrubbed and terrifyingly calm; then at Isandro, at Tomás, the registered printer, the known quantity; then at the manifest, where the marginal entry sat in its perfect initialed confidence.

"Underhill," the clerk said. "Your name was on this line."

"My apprentice travels in my place," Isandro said. Her voice did not shake. Eighteen years of being someone else had taught her voice everything it knew. "My father is ill. Someone must keep the shop. The commission's records will require printing services through the transition — I'm sure you'd rather have a printer at his frame than on a train."

A pause. The pen hovered. Behind her, Mateo had gone very still, and somewhere down the platform the woman with the birdcage began, softly, to hum.

The clerk wrote. He drew the line through the correction, initialed the initial, stamped the ticket, and handed the papers back to Ferren with the brisk indifference of a man processing his four-hundredth document of the morning. "Car six. Seat forty-one. Move along."

Ferren took the papers. Then he turned — quick, before the guards could decide whether to allow it — and threw his arms around Isandro's waist, his face pressed into the rough wool of Tomás's jacket, and said, into the fabric, so quietly that only she could hear: "Twelve-point Caslon. Don't forget. Don't forget me."

"First page," she said. "Go."

She watched him all the way to the carriage steps — small figure in the huge coat, papers gripped in one fist like a talisman — watched the guard check his ticket, watch him turn once at the top step, once more in the window, his palm flat against the glass. She raised her hand. Held it there. Kept it there as the couplings slammed and the engine gathered itself and the train began, at last, unbearably, to move.

* * *

The church bells started as the train cleared the water tower.

Not the half hour. The hour. Eleven o'clock exactly, all the bells at once, the town's whole bronze throat opening, because across the river, in the commission hall with its flags and its telegraph wires, the delegates were putting their names to the armistice at that very moment — the pens moving, the wax dripping, the war ending in ink the way wars always ended, on paper, in a room, far from anyone who'd bled for it.

Mateo stood beside her at the end of the emptying platform. He had not tried to board. She had known he wouldn't; she had known it since chapter upon chapter of nights at the press, since the permit he'd caught her forging and the sentence he'd said instead of betrayal — *teach me* — which had been, in his language, the plainest declaration of love ever spoken to her. He stood beside her now with his split hands in his pockets and his shoulder touching hers, and they watched the train shrink down the valley, carrying Ferren Alday, born in this parish, father a wheelwright, toward a harbor and a life.

"He's out," Mateo said.

"He's out." Her voice broke on the second word, just slightly, and she let it, because there was no one left on the platform to perform for except the soldiers, and the soldiers were watching the train too.

Below the town, the river ran. Above the town, the bells rang the signing. And in her jacket, against her ribs, the folded proof of the broadside waited — two hundred and six names, every family she'd smuggled, everyone the ledger had condemned, set in her own hand, ready for paste and wall and witness.

"Mateo," she said, when the train had gone and the bells had begun to fade and the platform had emptied down to stragglers and guards. "Get the bucket. Get the brush. Get the wheat-paste from under the counter."

He looked at her. Across the river, the last peal died away, and somewhere in the commission hall a clerk was blotting the final signature dry.

"The signing's done," he said. "Which means in an hour every officer in this town reads the proclamation and starts pretending none of it happened." A slow, fierce grin cut through the exhaustion on his face. "And we're going to tell them exactly what happened. Tonight. On their own door."

"Tonight," Isandro said, and turned toward the shop, and the press, and the work.
