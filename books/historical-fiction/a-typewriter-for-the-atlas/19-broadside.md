## 19. Broadside

The signing was set for ten o'clock, and the bells would tell everyone so.

Isandro knew the hour the way she knew her own pulse now — by the sound the town made around it. All morning the streets had been filling with a strange, held-breath quiet, the kind of quiet that comes before something official happens to people who were never asked. Across the river, in the customs house with its green shutters and its flag newly hung, eleven men in good coats were arranging pens and blotting paper on a table that had been carried in for the purpose. She had seen the table go by on a cart two days ago. She had memorized its grain.

Tonight there would be no river wide enough between them and what they signed.

"You're bleeding again," Mateo said.

He said it without looking up from the paste bucket. That was how they spoke about wounds now — the way other people spoke about weather. Her fingers had split along three knuckles from five nights of setting type, and she had wrapped them in strips of the same rice paper she'd once used for hidden names, because the shop had nothing else soft enough and because it felt correct, somehow, that the paper should serve her hands after her hands had served it so long.

"Then hold the bucket," she said. "If I bleed into the paste, at least it's red on white. Very festive."

Mateo didn't laugh. He hadn't laughed properly since the platform, since Ferren's face had gone small and enormous in the train window, since the bells had rung the hour of signing over an empty berth and a girl in her dead brother's coat standing very still on the boards while the steam swallowed everything. But his hands hadn't stopped either. Neither of theirs had. Work was the only prayer they both knew by heart.

The broadside lay drying across two trestles in the back room, all six feet of it, weighted at the corners with brass furniture from the case. One hundred and forty-three names. Isandro had set them herself, letter by letter, from memory — not the ledger's memory, which was gone, but hers: the memory of writing them into the book one night when her father slept, of copying them onto rice paper folded into the 12-point Garamond, of saying them under her breath like a rosary through every inspection, every knock, every hour she'd stood as Tomás Underhill under a lamp while men asked her questions she answered with her brother's voice.

Some of the spellings she had guessed. Two surnames she'd only ever heard spoken, and she had chosen the letters that sounded most like the mouths that said them. She thought of them as her names now. Not possessions — nothing here was a possession — but charges. A typesetter's word. Things entrusted to her care that could be damaged by careless handling.

She had left room at the bottom. White space, deliberate, the width of two lines.

"What's the space for?" Mateo had asked on the second night, when the composition stick was nearly full.

"Proof," she'd said. "That we don't know everything. The commission will want to believe the list is incomplete, that some of the taken are accounted for elsewhere. Let them believe it. Let them come looking." She had slid another line of lead home. "A list that claims to be whole can be dismissed as propaganda. A list with a hole in it has to be filled."

He had looked at her then the way he'd looked at her the first night at the press, when he caught the forged permit half-dry under her palm and instead of betrayal asked her to teach him. She still didn't have a name for that look. She suspected she never would. Some things you set in lead; some things you leave in white space.

Now it was past nine, and the paste bucket smelled of flour and the cheap glue Mateo had stretched with vinegar, and outside the window the town moved toward the river like iron filings toward a magnet.

"They'll post guards," Isandro said. "At the customs house."

"Of course they'll post guards."

"And the door is oak. Good oak. It'll take the paste badly."

"So we bring more paste." Mateo screwed the lid down on the bucket and stood, rolling his sleeves to the elbow, and for a moment — just a moment — he smiled, and it was the old smile, the apprentice's smile, the one that said *show me the next thing and I'll learn it.* "You've pasted bills to walls your whole life, Tomás. What's one more wall?"

She flinched at the name before she could stop herself. He saw it. He crossed the room and took her wrapped hand, careful of the splits, careful the way he was careful with type — knowing that what he held could be ruined forever by one wrong pressure.

"Not Tomás tonight," he said quietly. "I only meant—"

"I know what you meant." She breathed. Outside, the church bell began to count the hour, and she counted with it, because counting was how she kept the fear in its place, each stroke a sort, each sort a letter, each letter a name. Nine. "But not tonight. Tonight I'd like to walk to that door as whoever I am. Even if I don't know yet who that is. Even if it takes me years to find out."

The bell finished. Somewhere across the river, faintly, a band struck up something martial and cheerful, and the sound crossed the water thin and strange, like a rumor of a country none of them lived in anymore.

"We go at the signing," Isandro said. "Not before. While every eye is on the pens."

* * *

They went as mourners, because mourners could move freely on a day like this and were beneath suspicion — grief being the one uniform the occupying command had never thought to requisition. Mateo wore his father's old black coat, too big in the shoulders, and Isandro wore the same coat she always wore, Tomás's coat, but underneath it, against her skin where no inspection would ever reach, she had pinned the last scrap of rice paper with the final name she'd copied: her father's. Elias Underhill. In case the broadside failed. In case the ink ran. In case the world required her to carry him across the river herself.

The bridge was crowded. Farmers from the valley, shopkeepers, women in black who had been in black so long the color had gone from mourning to mere habit. They flowed toward the customs house the way water flows toward a drain, drawn by the gravity of official things, and Isandro let herself be carried in the current with the paste bucket under Mateo's arm disguised inside a market basket, its lid weighted with cabbages they had no intention of eating.

On the far bank, the square before the customs house had been cleared and roped. Soldiers stood at intervals with their chins up and their eyes forward, performing vigilance for the delegates' windows. Beyond the rope, on the steps, clerks hurried with documents; and above them all, on the green-shuttered façade, the flag hung flat in the windless morning like a thing waiting to be told what shape to take.

"There," Mateo murmured.

Isandro followed his chin. To the left of the main doors, set into the stone, was a smaller door — a service entrance, half-shadowed by an awning, unguarded because no ceremony ever entered by a servant's way. And beside it, bolted at eye height, a board: *COMMISSION OF ARMISTICE AND INQUIRY.* Fresh paint. The words still sharp-edged, still smelling of the brush.

*Inquiry.* She almost laughed. They had named themselves the thing they refused to do.

"Their own door," she said. "Their own board. We paste it beside the word *inquiry* and let the two argue with each other for a hundred years."

"And if they tear it down?"

"Then they'll have to do it in front of the crowd, and the crowd will read it first. You can't unread a hundred and forty-three names, Mateo. That's the whole point of print. It gets inside you and sets itself."

The band across the water swelled. Somewhere a cannon fired — once, twice, a salute or a signal, the sound rolling up the river valley and breaking against the hills — and the crowd made the low collective sound crowds make when history does something loud nearby. Heads turned toward the bridge. Toward the far bank. Toward the pens.

"Now," Isandro said.

* * *

The awning's shadow took them like water takes a stone. Mateo went first, basket swinging, and Isandro came behind with the broadside rolled in oilcloth under her arm, walking with Tomás's stride, Tomás's set shoulders, the performance she could do asleep now and sometimes did, dreaming in her brother's body while her own slept unwitnessed somewhere behind it.

The service door's latch was painted shut with the rest. Beside it, the board gleamed.

Mateo set down the basket, lifted out the cabbages, handed her the brush without a word. She dipped it — the paste was cold and thick, the consistency of porridge gone wrong — and began at the top edge, laying the paper against the wood in a slow downward sweep, smoothing with the heel of her wrapped hand as she went, working out the bubbles, working out the air, the way she'd been taught at fourteen when her father put the paste brush in her hand and said, *a crooked bill dishonors the message, and a wrinkled one insults the reader.*

One hundred and forty-three names, going up line by line against the commission's own wall.

She worked fast but not rushed. There is a difference, and the difference is everything. Rushing makes errors; errors make tears; tears make excuses. She wanted no excuses given to anyone. The paper went up clean, edge true to edge, and the type beneath the paste began to darken and show through, black on white, rising like something surfacing from deep water.

*Alvarez, Raimunda. Alvarez, Teo.*

The baker's wife. The baker's boy, taken at sixteen for labor and never returned.

*Ardoin, Marguerite.*

Who had hidden in the chapel loft for a month and been found anyway.

*Bellamy, Odette — Bellamy, Odile.*

Sisters. Isandro remembered the mother coming to the shop to pay for funeral notices that couldn't yet be printed, leaving the money anyway, saying, *when it's time, use my coin.* The coin was still in the ledger drawer. The ledger was in the archive forty feet away from where Isandro's brush now moved. Everything in this town came back around like a press cylinder, printing the same grief on new sheets.

Footsteps on gravel. Isandro's hand stopped mid-stroke.

Two soldiers rounding the corner of the building, rifles slung, talking — laughing, even, the laughter of men whose war was ending and who believed ending meant forgiven. Ten paces. Eight. Mateo shifted his weight, angling his body between them and the wet paper, a human screen, and Isandro understood and did not stop working; she smoothed the next section blind, by feel, trusting the straightedge of the board beneath.

The soldiers passed. Their conversation drifted: a girl in the customs house village, a horse won at cards, whether there'd be rations issued for the march home. At fifteen paces one of them glanced sideways — and looked directly at the broadside, at the fresh-pasted sheet with its darkening columns, and his eyes did the arithmetic eyes do, moving down the list, catching a name, losing interest, moving on. He said something to the other. They laughed again and were gone around the corner.

Isandro let out a breath that shook on the way out.

"He saw," she whispered.

"He saw paper," Mateo said. "Paper's invisible until someone stands in front of it and refuses to move."

"Then someone will have to refuse."

"Yes," Mateo said simply. "I'd wondered when you'd get to that part."

She worked the bottom third. The names came slower here, the ones she'd reconstructed from fragments — a surname overheard at the checkpoint, a Christian name from a neighbor's sobbing account, initials from a scrap of requisition paper Ferren had brought her weeks ago, back when the boy still talked, before the bakery, before the execution he'd witnessed had closed his throat like a fist. She had promised him a place in the book. *Ferren,* she had printed, near the end but not last, because last place on a list reads like an afterthought and Ferren had never been an afterthought in his life, only in everyone else's accounting.

Below his name: the blank space. Two lines of white, waiting.

And below the blank space, the imprint, small and centered, the colophon her father had used for thirty years and which Isandro had cut herself in new type for this job alone:

*Printed at the sign of the Underhill Press. Set from memory.*

Let them come to the shop. Let them ask. She was done hiding the hand behind the work.

She pressed the final corner flat. Stepped back. The broadside hung on the commission's wall, six feet of white bearing six feet of black, pasted hard beside the painted word *INQUIRY*, and in the flat gray light of the signing morning it did not look like a protest. It looked like a record. It looked like the one object in the square that told the truth, which is the most radical thing a piece of paper can be.

"It's crooked," Mateo said.

"It is not crooked."

"A hair. Bottom-left corner." He tilted his head. "Your father would notice."

"My father," Isandro said, "is lying upstairs listening for bells, and if he ever sees this he'll say the paste work was the finest I've done and mean it, because he taught me that the measure isn't the corner, it's whether the reader stops." She picked up the brush. "Someone will stop."

* * *

Someone stopped within the hour.

They had not planned to stay — staying was suicide, and Isandro had a father upstairs and a promise downstairs and no intention of dying on the far side of a river she'd spent two years crossing invisibly. But the crowd had thickened after the salute cannons, packed shoulder to shoulder facing the customs house, and the current that had carried them onto the bridge now carried them off it slowly, inch by inch, and so they were still twenty yards away, wedged among the coats and hats, when the doors opened and the delegates emerged onto the steps with their documents bound in ribbon and their faces arranged into expressions suitable for history.

The band played. Hats came off. Somewhere a woman wept openly and was shushed and wept harder, and Isandro understood her completely, because relief and grief had long ago stopped being separate rooms in her chest and become one large cold hall where both echoed.

And then the crowd, turning to disperse, began to turn the other way instead.

It happened the way anything happens in a crowd: one head, then five, then fifty, a slow ripple of attention flowing upstream of itself toward the wall beside the service door, where a man in shirtsleeves — a clerk, young, ink-stained at the cuffs, carrying a dispatch box — had stopped dead in the act of crossing the courtyard and was reading.

Reading the way Isandro had dreamed someone would read: completely. His lips moved. His thumb, absently, tracked the lines of type the way a compositor's thumb tracks a galley proof, and Isandro felt her heart turn over in her chest, because she knew that gesture, she had made that gesture her whole life, and this was a man who had set type himself once, somewhere, before the world gave him a dispatch box instead.

The clerk reached the bottom of the list. Reached the blank space. Frowned at the blank space — and she saw it land on him, the argument she'd built into those two empty lines, the hole that demanded filling — and then reached the colophon.

*Printed at the sign of the Underhill Press. Set from memory.*

His head came up. He looked across the courtyard — past the dispersing crowd, past the soldiers, past everything — and though there was no possible way he could see her, twenty yards deep in a hundred bodies, Isandro felt his gaze pass over her like a hand over a case of type, touching every letter without lifting any.

He knew the face. She knew he knew the face. He had bought ornaments and French Clarendon from her father two winters ago, and he had complimented 'the boy's' spacing, and he had shaken her hand, and her hand was small and he had noticed and said nothing, even then, even in daylight, even before there was anything to protect except a girl who loved lead.

Behind him, a delegate's voice called his name — *Vasseur!* — sharp, official, summoning. The clerk did not move. For three full seconds he stood in the courtyard of the armistice commission, in view of the pens and the ribbons and the flags, looking at a document that accused his employers of one hundred and forty-three disappearances, pasted illegally on his own office wall.

Then Vasseur turned, climbed the steps, and disappeared inside with his dispatch box.

And did not send anyone to tear it down.

"Mateo," Isandro whispered.

"I see it."

"He recognized the type."

"I know." Mateo's hand found hers in the crush of the crowd, found it and held it, splits and paper wrappings and all. "What does that mean? Tell me what that means, because I can't tell if we've just won something or signed our own warrant."

Across the river the bells began — not the signing hour, that had passed, but a peal now, loose and repeated, the town ringing itself hoarse over a peace it could not yet feel in its bones. The crowd surged toward the bridge, singing something ragged, and Isandro let it carry her, the broadside drying at her back in the commission's own shadow, one hundred and forty-three names holding their ground in the wind.

It means, she thought, that ink is patient. It means the clerk will stand at his desk tonight with that colophon in his head, and tomorrow, and the day after, until the blank space at the bottom starts to itch like a missing sort in a locked form. It means the list has entered the building.

It means, she thought, watching the customs house doors swing shut, that we are not finished.

And it means — her hand tightened around Mateo's, and the thought arrived with the weight of a full galley, undeniable, already composed — that sooner or later, a clerk who remembers a printer's handshake is going to come across the river to the sign of the Underhill Press.

And when he does, she intended to answer the door as no one's brother at all.
