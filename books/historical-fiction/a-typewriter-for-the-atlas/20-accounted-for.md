## 20. Accounted For

The broadside came down on the fourth day.

Mateo saw it happen from the shop window — two clerks from the commission, a ladder, a bucket of paste and a scraper. They worked with the efficiency of men doing something shameful quickly. The paper tore in strips. One clerk folded the pieces into his coat rather than let them blow into the street, which Mateo thought was the most honest thing the commission had done since it arrived: they wanted the names gone, but not scattered. Scattered, they could be read by anyone. Gathered, they could be denied.

"They took it down," Isandro said behind him. She was at the stone, rolling ink for a job she had no customer for — a receipt, something small to keep her hands moving. "I expected that."

"You hoped otherwise."

"I planned otherwise." She lifted the roller. "There are forty-two copies pasted between here and the river gate. They can scrape doors all week."

That was the thing about her he still couldn't get used to, after everything — the way hope sat in her like ballast instead of sail. She didn't rise on it. She steered by it.

* * *

The inquiries began before the copies were all down.

First it was a woman from the mill district who came to the shop with her sister's name memorized and nothing else — no papers, no proof, just a name spoken twice so it wouldn't come out wrong. Then a man whose son had been taken from the quarry road in October. Then a pair of grandmothers who arrived together and stood shoulder to shoulder like women entering a courtroom, though there was only a counter and a boy with ink to his elbows.

They didn't ask for anything, exactly. They asked if their people were *on* it.

Mateo learned to answer without lying. He would pull the broadside's proof copy from under the press bed — the one they'd kept, the one Isandro set from memory in a single night while the candles burned down like slow fuses — and he would run his finger down the columns and say either *yes* or *not yet*. Yes meant grief of one kind. Not yet meant grief of another kind, the kind with no floor to it.

A woman wept when her husband's name was there. A man raged when his brother's was not. Both reactions were correct. Isandro had told him this early on, quietly, while setting the type: *"Every name I put down is a wound reopened somewhere. That's what a record is. It isn't mercy. It's memory with its coat off."*

By the end of the first week, the shop had become something it had never been licensed to be: an office of inquiry. People came with scraps — a date, a unit number seen on a truck, a description of an officer's gloves. Ferren, who had come back from the train thin and silent and then, slowly, not silent, took these scraps and organized them into a notebook in a hand far too precise for a boy of eleven. He cross-referenced. He made a grid. When Mateo asked where he'd learned it, Ferren said, "Counting soldiers," and went back to work.

* * *

The commission sent a clerk on the ninth day.

Not soldiers — a clerk, alone, with a leather folder and the apologetic posture of a man who has been sent to do arithmetic in a room full of grieving people. He was young. He stood at the counter and looked at the press, at the type cases along the wall, and Mateo watched him recognize the face of the type — Underhill's old Caslon, cut decades ago, worn into a softness that no other foundry could imitate.

"You printed the list," the clerk said. It wasn't an accusation. It sounded almost like relief, as if he'd been sent to confirm something he already believed.

"A printer prints," Mateo said.

"There are questions." The clerk opened his folder. "Formal ones. Names submitted by families are being entered into the record. There will be a review board. In the capital, after withdrawal." He hesitated. "There is also a form for — corrections. If any name on the broadside was set in error."

Mateo understood then what the form was for. Not corrections. Retractions. An invitation to unprint.

He went to the back room, where Isandro was teaching Ferren to lock a chase — her hands over the boy's smaller ones, guiding the frame together — and he told her, flatly, what the clerk wanted.

She came out wiping ink on her apron. She looked at the form for a long time. Then she asked the clerk one question: "How many families have filed inquiries?"

"Two hundred and six," the clerk said. "As of this morning."

"And how many names are on my broadside?"

"Three hundred and eleven."

"Then your commission has two hundred and six reasons to review the record and a hundred and five families who haven't yet found their way here." She slid the form back across the counter. "Tell them no name comes down until every inquiry is answered. Tell them I'll print corrections for the dead who turn up alive — gladly, publicly, with an apology in the same size type. But I don't retract the missing. The missing stay missing in ink until they're found or buried."

The clerk wrote this down. Mateo had the strange sense, watching him, that the man had come hoping to be refused — that some part of the commission's own machinery wanted the list to stand, because a list that stands forces the question *where did they go*, and someone in the capital wanted that question asked in a voice louder than theirs.

* * *

The withdrawals started as rumors, and rumors in that town had always been more reliable than newspapers.

The garrison at the mill was packing crates. The telegraph office doubled its staff and then halved its business. Trucks that had gone north loaded with requisitioned grain now went north empty, and Ferren — who could not stop counting things, who counted them the way other boys breathed — reported the numbers each evening at the shop table: trucks out, trucks in, the difference growing daily like water draining from a basin.

"They're not waiting for the review board," Mateo said.

"They're avoiding it," Isandro said. "There's a difference. A government can survive answering questions. What it can't survive is standing in the dock while three hundred eleven names are read aloud, one per day, in a town that knows the faces that used to go with them." She turned a sort in her fingers — a lowercase e, worn nearly round with use. "My father used to say a lie needs fresh paper. The truth keeps."

But Mateo saw what she wouldn't say: withdrawal was not justice. The trucks leaving empty meant the grain wasn't coming back. The garrison packing meant the taken weren't coming back either — or not most of them. Victory, when it finally showed its face, looked less like triumph than like an enemy deciding the ground wasn't worth the cost of holding, and leaving the cost behind for everyone else to bury.

* * *

On the twelfth day, the answers started arriving.

Not many. A clerk from the commission's rear office appeared at the door with a ledger of his own — an official one, stamped, real — and read out findings in a voice like a train schedule. Eleven of the taken had been located in labor battalions south of the river; they would be returned within the month. Four had died of fever in transit; the commission offered dates and places of burial, which was a kind of cruelty and a kind of gift at once, because now there were graves to stand at instead of only absences. Three had been conscripted into the retreating army itself and were, officially, *unaccounted* — a word Mateo hated, because it made the army sound like the victim of its own theft.

Eleven found. Four dead with named graves. Three swallowed whole.

Out of eighteen answered, in two weeks, out of three hundred eleven.

Isandro took the figures without expression and entered them in the shop ledger — her father's ledger, the same one that had once endangered them all, repurposed now into something it should have been from the start: a record kept by the people it concerned. She wrote the eleven names again in a new column, headed *RETURNED*, and the four beneath *BURIED*, and the three under a heading she ruled carefully and labeled *OWED*.

"That's the honest column," she said, tapping it. "Everything else is commentary."

Ferren copied the column onto a card and pinned it above the counter where the petitioners could see it. Some of them raged at how short it was. One woman kissed the card anyway, because her son's name was among the eleven, and she left the shop lighter than she'd entered it, and Mateo understood that both of those responses were also correct.

* * *

The last soldiers left on a gray morning three weeks after the signing.

There was no ceremony. They marched to the river gate in loose formation, loaded their trucks, and crossed before the fog lifted, and the town woke to streets that belonged to it again — streets with requisition notices peeling off the walls, streets with boarded windows, streets where every third door bore the ghost-rectangle of a scraped-away broadside, pale patches of wood where the paste had pulled the grain up with it. The town wore its scars in rectangles. Everyone did.

At noon, the church bells rang for the first time in months not to mark a curfew but to mark ordinary hours, and the sound was so strange that people stopped in the street to listen to time passing like a normal thing.

Mateo stood in the doorway of the shop with Isandro and Ferren and watched the last truck disappear. He waited for her to say something — she was the one with words for things, always, words set in lead — but she said nothing, and after a while he realized why. Her hand was pressed flat against the doorframe, and she was breathing carefully, the way you breathe when you refuse to cry in daylight.

"We won," Ferren said, in his flat counting voice.

"No," Isandro said. She straightened. "We accounted. That's different, and it's smaller, and it's what we could do." She looked down the empty street. "Eleven home. Four buried. Two hundred ninety-six owed. Nobody gets to call that winning. But nobody gets to call it losing either, because before the broadside, the number was zero. Before us, nobody was even asking."

Mateo reached over and took her hand off the doorframe and held it, ink-stained against ink-stained, and she let him, there in the open doorway, in daylight, which was itself a kind of victory neither of them had dared a year ago.

"Your father would have—" he started.

"He'll tell me himself," she said. "When he's strong enough to scold me for the kerning on the memorial page. Come inside. We have two hundred and ninety-six names to keep warm."

* * *

They worked through the afternoon relocking the memorial page — the final job, the one Isandro had been composing for weeks — adding the four burial dates in small italics beneath their names, marking the eleven returned with a small asterisk and a line at the bottom: *\* Returned. See ledger.* It was, Mateo thought, the strangest bookkeeping in the history of printing: a page of the dead and missing annotated like a merchant's inventory, except that the merchandise was human and the account would never balance.

Near dusk, a knock at the door. Ferren answered it, and came back with a look on his face Mateo had never seen there — not fear, not calculation, but something almost like awe.

"It's the clerk," Ferren said. "The commission clerk. He says—" The boy stopped, gathered himself. "He says he has the original ledger. Father Underhill's. The one they seized. He says it was scheduled for destruction with the rest of the archive tonight, and he took it out, and he's carrying it, and he wants to know—" Ferren swallowed. "He wants to know if the shop will accept the return of its own property."

From the back room, before anyone could answer, came the sound of a cough — deep, wet, long — and then Isandro's father's voice, thinner than it had ever been but perfectly clear:

"Tell him," he called, "that property returns to its owner. And tell him to mind the corners. That book has carried every soul in this town, and it's tired."

Isandro was already moving toward the door.
