## 21. Setting Her Name

The winter the commission signed its papers, the shop smelled of glue and cold iron. Isandro kept the stove fed because her father had always kept it fed, and because a press that froze was a press that died, and she could not bear to think of anything in that building dying. Not anymore. There had been enough of it.

Father Underhill lay in the back room on the cot they'd moved downstairs so he wouldn't have to climb. The doctor came twice a week now and stayed only long enough to listen to his chest and shake his head in a way that had stopped being medical and become liturgical — the shaking of a head over a finished thing.

"He wants to see the forme," Mateo said one morning, coming back from the cot with a candle stub and an expression he tried and failed to make neutral. "The whole page. He says he wants to check your spacing before God does."

"Then I'd better fix my spacing."

She had been composing the memorial page for eleven days. It lived on the imposing stone by the north window, under a sheet she lifted only when she worked, as if the names were seedlings that needed dark as much as light. Every name from the stolen ledger, every name from memory, every name Ferren had whispered to her in the weeks after he found his voice again — set in ten-point Caslon, justified flush, ragged only where a name refused to fit the line and she refused to cut it shorter. Names did not get abbreviated. That was the whole point of names.

One hundred and forty-one lines. Some were full families. Some were single words, first name only, the surname lost even to memory — *Marta*, alone on her line like a woman standing on a platform after the train has gone. Isandro had wept over Marta once, briefly, at two in the morning, and then dried her eyes on her sleeve and kept setting, because grief that interrupted the work was just another way of losing.

Her father wanted to see it. So she would show him. But not yet. First there was a question she had been avoiding for eleven days, and it sat in the type case at the end of the stone like a sort she couldn't find a home for.

The last line. The blank one.

* * *

He died on a Tuesday, in the hour when the church bells were wrong — the occupiers had altered them, everyone knew, though no one could prove what they'd been altered *to* — and he died the way he had set type: deliberately, without wasted motion, waiting until both of them were in the room.

Mateo held one hand. She held the other. His fingers were ink-stained to the second knuckle, the way they had been for forty years; soap had never gotten all of it and never would, and when they washed him for burial she made sure the undertaker understood that the stain was to remain. It was part of the body. It had been part of the body longer than most of the body's other features.

"I saw the page," he said, near the end. His voice was small but level, the voice of a man reading copy aloud for approval. "Ferren lifted the sheet for me yesterday while you were at the market. All of them there?"

"All of them."

"The widow Cerny. The Varga boys."

"All of them."

"And the last line?" He turned his head on the pillow. His eyes were clear — terrifyingly clear, the lucidity of the dying, which is a cruelty no one warns you about. "You left it blank. I taught you better than to leave blanks, Isandro."

Not Tomás. He had said *Isandro*, and said it easily, the way you say a word you've been saying all along in private and have finally decided to say in company.

"It's not finished," she said.

"Nothing is." He looked past her, at the ceiling beams, at something further. "A printer leaves no blank line. If the page ends, the page ends. You don't leave room for ghosts." A pause, a breath that cost him. "Unless the ghost is real. Unless the ghost has been carrying this shop on her back for three years while wearing her brother's collar."

"Don't," she said, and found she was crying without any warning at all, the tears simply arriving, unbidden and unbudgeted, like requisitioned goods. "Don't make it a eulogy. Just be my father for five more minutes."

"I am being your father." His grip tightened — surprising strength, borrowed from somewhere. "I am telling you what fathers tell printers. Finish the job. Set your name. The one that's yours. Let them read it on the door of the commission itself if they have to." Another breath. "Underhill is not a man's name or a woman's name. It's a printer's name. It was always going to be yours. Take it out of disguise."

Mateo's hand closed over hers where they lay folded around their father's fingers. Neither of them spoke. Outside, the bells rang their wrong note, and inside, the stove ticked, and the type stood in its cases in the dark like a town waiting for morning.

He went between one bell-stroke and the next. No drama. He simply was not there anymore, the way a letter is not there anymore once you distribute it back into the case — everything still present, nothing still assembled.

Isandro sat for a long time. Then she stood, wiped her face with the heel of her hand, and went into the shop, and took down the sheet from the imposing stone.

* * *

They buried him on the Friday, in the thin January ground, with half the town in attendance and the other half watching from windows, because funerals had become political again and people counted attendance the way Ferren used to count soldiers. The commission sent a wreath. Isandro looked at the wreath for a long moment and then let it lie on the grave, because her father had believed in acknowledging correspondence, even hostile correspondence, before deciding what to do with it.

Afterward there was coffee at the shop, weak and bitter, and neighbors who clasped her arm and called her Tomás, and she let them, one last day of it, out of respect for the dead man whose son she had been.

But that night, when the last mourner had gone and Mateo had banked the stove, she unlocked the type case at the end of the row — the one she had kept locked since the interrogation, since the hollow sorts, since the night trust became currency — and took out the sorts she had been saving.

Not blanks. Never blanks. Her father had taught her better than that, and she had known for eleven days what the last line would hold, and had simply not been ready to lock it up.

ISANDRO UNDERHILL. PRINTER.

She set it slowly, each sort chosen by feel, the way her father had taught her — by feel, because a compositor who looks at the case is a compositor who isn't listening to the words. The letters clicked into the composing stick with the small definite sounds of a decision being made permanent. Lead doesn't argue. Lead doesn't take it back. Whatever you set in lead is true until someone melts it down, and she was not going to melt it down.

"You're sure?" Mateo stood in the doorway, sleeves rolled, forearms still damp from washing the press rollers. Three years of watching her bind her chest and lower her voice and answer to a dead boy's name, and all he asked was: *you're sure?*

"No," she said. "Lock it up anyway."

He came and stood beside her and looked at the line in the stick. Then he reached past her, took a quoin key from the bench, and locked the chase himself — firm, even pressure, corners checked twice, the way an apprentice checks his master's work and finds it good.

"It'll print crooked if you don't planer it properly," he said, which was apprentice for *I love you*, and had been since the night at the press, and they both knew it, and neither of them needed to say anything else.

* * *

The proof came off the press on Sunday morning.

She pulled it herself — her father's press, her father's ink, her hands, her name standing at the foot of the page in letters an inch high where no reader could miss them and no official could pretend not to see them. One hundred and forty-two lines. The widow Cerny. The Varga boys. Marta, alone on her line, no longer standing on the platform. Father Underhill, *printer*, beside her, the way he had stood beside her at this stone for twenty years. And beneath them all, the new line, the true line, the one that had cost more to set than all the others combined.

Mateo hung the proof on the shop door behind glass, facing outward, and within an hour there were people standing in the street reading it, and within a day there were people coming from the villages, and within a week the priest asked — asked, formally, hat in hand — whether the shop might print copies for the churches of the valley, because the dead deserved to be named in every parish that had prayed for them without knowing a single name.

They printed four hundred. Then eight hundred. The commission's clerk came personally, the one who remembered Tomás fondly, and stood in front of the press for a long time with his hat in his hands, and finally said only: "The archive will want twelve copies for the record." And Isandro, who had learned to read paperwork the way soldiers read terrain, understood that the record was no longer theirs — that somewhere in the machinery of the armistice, the list had stopped being contraband and become evidence.

"They'll ask why your name is on it," Mateo said that night, locking up. "Tomás Underhill. They'll ask where she comes from."

"Then they'll ask," Isandro said. "And I'll answer. That's the difference between us and them, Mateo. We can afford to answer questions. We've got nothing left to hide except the truth, and the truth prints beautifully."

He laughed — the first real laugh since the funeral, startled out of him — and she laughed too, and the sound went up through the shop among the cases and the stones and the hanging proofs, and if the dead heard it, she hoped they recognized the acoustics.

* * *

Spring came late that year and all at once, the way spring does after a war. The occupiers withdrew in April — not routed, not punished, simply withdrawn, the way a debtor leaves town, and everyone understood that the broadside had done it, that a hundred and forty-two names in ten-point Caslon had weighed more than a battalion, that paper properly set is the heaviest material known to man.

The shop reopened under a painted sign. MATEO RUIZ & ISANDRO UNDERHILL, PRINTERS. Mateo had insisted on the order being alphabetical, and she had insisted on the ampersand, and they had argued about it for two days in a way that was not about typography at all, and the sign-painter had charged them extra for the wait.

On the first morning of May, Isandro distributed the memorial page back into its cases, line by line, name by name — not erasing them, she told herself, not ever that, merely returning them to the common stock of letters from which all future pages would be composed, which is the only immortality a printer believes in. When she reached the last line, her own, she paused with the sorts in her hand.

Then she set them aside. Kept them. Locked them in the drawer of her father's desk with his spectacles and the first travel permit she ever forged, the false one, the one that started everything. A printer keeps her first errors and her first truths in the same drawer. How else would she remember the distance between them?

She was standing at the stone, composing a wedding announcement for the cobbler's daughter — ordinary work, blessed work, work with no blood in it — when Ferren appeared in the doorway.

He was taller. That was the first thing. The second thing was the object under his arm, wrapped in sacking and tied with cord, and the way he carried it: carefully, ceremonially, the way you carry something you've crossed a border with.

"Isandro Underhill," he said, reading the sign above the door, sounding the syllables out loud like a boy proving he could. Then he stepped inside, into the smell of ink and cold iron, and began untying the cord. "I brought you something. It's heavy. I think it's going to change everything."
