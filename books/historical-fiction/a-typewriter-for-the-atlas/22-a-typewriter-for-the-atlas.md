## 22. A Typewriter for the Atlas

The bell over the shop door had been Mateo's idea — salvaged from the old bakery on the corner, the one that had burned in the winter of the withdrawal, its brass cracked but still true enough to ring. For eleven years it had announced customers, apprentices, deliveries of paper and ink. On a Tuesday morning in October, with frost still silvering the cobbles of the Rue des Compositteurs, it announced a man I did not recognize.

He filled the doorway the way weather fills a valley. Tall, broad through the shoulders, sunburned to the color of good leather, with a scar running through one eyebrow that split it like a road forked by a river. He wore a traveling coat too fine for this town and boots too worn for any city, and under one arm he carried a wooden case the size of a bread box, bound shut with two leather straps gone dark from handling.

I was standing at the stone with a composing stick in my hand, setting a wedding announcement for the Delacroix girl — twelve-point Garamond, the family could afford nothing better and wanted nothing more — and I looked up at him the way you look at any stranger, with the polite blankness the trade teaches you.

Then he said, "You've moved the case of question marks."

Eleven years, and his voice had dropped an octave and crossed three borders, and still I knew it. The stick trembled in my hand. A piece of type slipped loose and rang against the stone like a small struck bell.

"Ferren," I said.

"You counted wrong," he said, and grinned, and the grin was eleven years old and unchanged. "There were four soldiers that day at the granary. You always said three. I've been wanting to correct you since 1921."

* * *

He set the case down on the counter with the care of a man handling something that had kept him alive, which, as it turned out, was exactly what it was. He unbuckled the straps. Inside, nested in wool that had once been somebody's sweater, sat a typewriter — not one of ours, not a press or a font of type, but a true machine, black enamel gone grey at the corners, keys worn to the shape of fingers.

"Off the train," he said. "Third carriage, second row. A woman left it behind when they called her name at the border crossing — she'd gotten her papers, she was going home to Lyon, and she stood up so fast she forgot everything she wasn't wearing." He turned it slightly in the light. "Nobody claimed it for six hours. By then we'd crossed into territory where claiming things was complicated, so I claimed it instead. I was fourteen. It seemed like the sort of thing a man does."

"You weren't a man yet," I said.

"No," he agreed. "That's why I practiced."

Mateo came up from the basement then, ink to his elbows, and stopped dead on the bottom stair. I watched my husband look at the boy we had sent away and find the man standing in his place, and I watched something move across his face that I have no type for — grief and pride and the particular ache of parents whose child is grown, though Ferren had never been ours except in every way that mattered.

"You got tall," Mateo said.

"You got old," Ferren said, and crossed the room, and embraced him hard enough that the ink transferred — I saw it later, the black print of Mateo's forearm on the back of Ferren's coat, and neither of them ever mentioned it, and I never washed it out.

* * *

We closed the shop early, which we almost never do. Mateo hung the sign — FERMÉ POUR LA FAMILLE, a joke he'd painted himself years ago because half our customers were family by then whether they'd chosen it or not — and we went upstairs, and Ferren ate everything in the house and told us what eleven years had done with him.

He had gone east first, then south, working as a typesetter in Trieste, a proofreader in Alexandria, a translator of shipping manifests in a port city whose name he still refused to pronounce correctly on principle. He had learned five languages and forgotten two. He had carried the typewriter through all of it.

"Why?" I asked him. "It's heavy. You must have sold ten better ones along the way."

He was quiet a moment. Outside, the frost was turning back to rain; it tapped at the window like someone asking to be let in.

"Because of the list," he said finally. "Your broadside. I stood on the platform, Isandro, I watched you put me on that train, and I didn't understand it — I was angry about it for years, do you know that? Angry that you gave me the berth, angry that you stayed, angry that you made me the one who lived." He turned his cup on the table without drinking. "And then I got to Lyon, and there it was. Your names. All of them. Pasted on the commission's door and copied into the inquiry record and printed in the papers, and there were people in that city who found their brothers because of your type. A woman found her son alive in a labor battalion in Silesia because his name was on your page. She came to find me — I don't know why me, maybe because I was young, maybe because I was crying when she asked — and she held my face in her hands and said, *someone remembered.*"

He looked up.

"I decided that day that whatever I did with my life, I would be someone who remembered. And a machine that remembers — that puts down exactly what you tell it, letter for letter, and never loses count—" He touched the typewriter's lid. "It seemed right. It seemed like yours. Like ours."

Mateo had gone very still. In eleven years of marriage I have learned every silence he owns, and this was the deep one, the one he goes to when feeling is too large for speech.

"The shop is yours if you want it," Mateo said at last. "Half of it. More than half. We're not young—"

"You're ancient," Ferren said, "both of you, it's horrifying," and the tension broke, and we laughed, and the laugh turned wet somewhere in the middle and nobody minded.

"I'm not staying," Ferren said then, gently. "Not yet. There's a commission forming in the south — another one, there's always another one — taking testimony from families on the other border. They need someone who can set testimony in five languages and keep the records straight. I told them I knew a trade." He glanced around the kitchen, at Mateo's ink-stained hands, at my own, at the shelf where father's hand-press sat under its dust cloth like a sleeping animal. "But I want to learn the rest first. Everything I didn't get to learn before the train. Casting. Binding. Whatever you'll teach me."

"And after the commission?"

"After," he said, "I'm coming home. If the home will have me."

"It will," I said, before either of them could make it sentimental. "Bring your machine. We'll need it. Hand-set type is dying, Ferren — the linotype men are coming, the big houses are eating the small ones whole. Father's press won't last another twenty years. But the work will. The work has to. Someone has to keep setting names in lead, or the world will decide none of them existed."

* * *

The next morning he came down before dawn, as an apprentice should, and I taught him at the stone the way Mateo had taught me and father had taught Mateo: how to hold the stick, how to read the type upside-down and backward until your mind turns it right without being asked, how to space a line by feel, how to hear the difference between a justified line and a cramped one.

His hands were wrong for it at first — a traveler's hands, a survivor's hands, quick but unpatient — but by noon they were learning. By evening he had composed his first full line without a single error, and I took it from him and locked it in the chase and said, "Now we print it."

"What is it?" he asked.

"Your name," I said.

He went quiet. I pulled proofs the way father had pulled the proof of my own name eleven years ago, on the night before he died — FERREN, alone, fourteen-point Caslon, no surname because he had never had one we trusted, just the name Mateo and I had given him off a manifest when the clerk demanded something to write.

I hung the proof on the line above the stone, where father used to hang his, where mine still hangs, where someday his children's names might hang if the world permits it.

"There," I said. "Accounted for."

Ferren looked at it a long time. When he spoke, his voice was fourteen again, just for a moment.

"Is my name really in the book?" he asked. The same question, the last words he'd spoken during his silence, the ones that broke it. "The memorial page?"

"Every copy," I said. "Every archive that will take one. Every inquiry record. Your name is in more books than the commission's, and ours are the ones that tell the truth."

* * *

They married us twice, Mateo and I — once in the church for the town, and once in the shop for ourselves, with father's press as our witness and the broadside of names framed above the door. Eleven years now. My hair is going grey at the temples, and Mateo's hands ache in cold weather, and the shop on the Rue des Compositteurs says UNDERHILL & MATEO in gold letters that Mateo repainted last spring, refusing my suggestion that we add a third name.

"Not yet," he'd said. "When he comes back. Then we paint it proper."

So tonight, after Ferren fell asleep in the old cot in the back room — the same cot, we never replaced it — I mixed the gold paint while Mateo cleaned the rollers, and we worked by lamplight like thieves of our own signboard, adding letters carefully beneath the others:

UNDERHILL & MATEO & FERREN

Three names. One seat each. Nobody left uncounted.

From the back room came the small sound of the typewriter — Ferren couldn't sleep either; I heard him writing, letter by letter, remembering something, putting it down so it would stay put — and Mateo put his arm around my waist, and we stood in the doorway of our shop and listened to the future compose itself, one true word at a time.

Somewhere far away, in a city of ledgers, a clerk was opening a file marked UNDERHILL, T. — CLOSED. Somewhere closer, a press waited under its cloth, patient as lead, ready for whatever name came next.

I turned off the lamp.

The bell over the door stirred in the draft, and rang once, softly, like a sentence ending — or beginning.
