## 1. The Reader of Letters

There are three kinds of people in the world, my father used to say: those who make history, those who read about it afterward, and those who carry the post between them. He said it with his mouth full of bread and a twinkle he thought I couldn't see, because I was fourteen and carrying the post.

I was twenty-six now, and I had refined the theory considerably. History was made in London by men whose names appeared in newspapers set in type too small to bother reading properly. History was written down afterward by gentlemen with three names and a study overlooking a park. And history was *carried* — in leather satchels, in motor cars, in the breast pockets of chauffeurs who were never once mentioned in any dispatch.

My name is Xanthe Fairweather, and I was the best-informed invisible person on the Kentish coast.

* * *

The Daimler purred along the marsh road that November morning like a well-fed cat, and I drove it the way I did everything: competently enough to be praised, quietly enough to be forgotten. Lady Verity Ashcombe sat behind me in the tonneau with her correspondence tray on her knees, reading aloud in the voice she used for dictation — clipped, imperious, each sentence sounding as though it had already been typeset.

"Miss Fairweather, take note. To Mrs. Pankhurst's secretary: Lady Ashcombe regrets that ill health prevents—" She paused. "No. Strike 'ill health.' Put 'pressing engagements.' A suffragist does not confess to ill health; it gives the opposition ideas."

"Pressing engagements," I repeated, watching the road. There was no traffic to watch. There was never any traffic. The road ran from Ashcombe House through Marrow Quay village to the tidal causeway, and beyond the causeway there was the mainland, and beyond the mainland there was the war everyone said would be over by Christmas, though no one could say which Christmas.

"And to the editor of *Votes for Women*: the Marrow Quay branch reports forty-seven members in good standing, an increase of nine since Michaelmas, despite—" her voice thinned with satisfaction— "*despite* the vicar's sermon."

"The one about a woman's place?"

"The one where he misquoted Saint Paul so badly even the churchwardens winced. Write 'despite vigorous local opposition,' which is kinder and funnier."

I wrote it. I always wrote it. My shorthand was fast and my handwriting, when I transcribed, was so neat that Verity had once held up a letter of mine beside her own and remarked that if I'd been born thirty years earlier I might have made something of myself. She had meant it as a compliment. I had decided to take it as one, mostly because the alternative was throwing myself into the sea.

That was the arrangement, you understand. I drove the car. I took the dictation. I carried the letters to the pillar box in Marrow Quay, and I collected whatever the morning boat or the carrier brought back. And then, at night—

Well. We will come to the night presently.

The Daimler climbed the last rise before the village, and there below us lay Marrow Quay: two streets, one church, one inn, one shop that sold everything and nothing you wanted, and the grey arm of the causeway reaching out across the flats toward the far shore like a stone jetty stretching after a departing ship. The bridge, everyone called it, though it was really a causeway — a mile of granite and iron laid across the tidal sands by German hands thirty years before, when a young engineer named Brandt had come to the coast with drawings under his arm and ambition in his eyes.

I knew that much about Emil Brandt the way I knew most things: sideways, accidentally, from letters never meant for me. I did not yet know what I would come to know. In November of 1914 I still believed my knowledge was a hobby, like stamp-collecting, harmless and private and mine.

The tide was out that morning, and the sands lay bare and gleaming all the way to the far shore, stitched with the dark threads of channels. Gulls stood about on the mud like committee members waiting for a quorum.

"Stop at the shop," said Verity. "Mrs. Tregowan will have gossip, and gossip is intelligence."

* * *

Mrs. Tregowan's shop smelled of paraffin, boiled sweets, and other people's business, in roughly equal measure. I left Lady Ashcombe inspecting tinned salmon — she inspected it the way she inspected government ministers, with an expression that suggested it had personally disappointed her — and went out to the motor to wait, and this is where I must confess to the first of my sins, which was not the worst of my sins but was the doorway to all the others.

I read the mail.

Not carelessly, not greedily — at least, that is what I told myself. I read it *carefully*. There is a difference, I maintained, between a snoop and a scholar. A snoop wants scandal. A scholar wants the shape of things. I wanted the shape of things. I wanted to know that Mr. Cobb the corn merchant owed money to his brother in Whitstable; that the vicar had been writing anxious letters to the bishop about his nerves; that Lady Ashcombe's London solicitor addressed her as "Dear Madam" in ink so black it seemed to resent having to say anything at all. I wanted the whole great loom of human affairs spread out where I could see the pattern, even if I was only ever permitted to sit beneath it and catch the loose threads.

It began innocently, as these things do. Two years ago, my first winter in service, a letter had arrived for Verity with its seal broken in the post — genuinely broken, torn by some careless sorting clerk — and I had seen half a line of it lying on the hall table and spent the entire evening wondering about the other half and a half. The next week, when a letter came intact, I found the wonder worse than the curiosity. Curiosity, at least, can be satisfied.

So I learned to steam. Every servant who has ever lived has known how to steam a letter; it is practically part of our training, passed down like a recipe, mother to daughter, scullery to scullery. You hold the envelope over the spout of the kettle — not too close, patience, patience — until the gum softens and surrenders. Then a thin knife, flat and unhurried. Then the letter, read twice, folded exactly as it was folded, sealed again with a breath and a press, delivered in the morning looking innocent as a lamb. The kettle asks no questions. The kettle keeps more secrets than any priest.

I want to be clear that I did not feel guilty. This is important, because later I would feel guilty about so many things, truly staggering quantities of guilt, and I would look back on this first autumn and marvel at how comfortable I had been inside my small dishonesties. I felt *useful*. I felt *informed*. When Verity dictated a reply to someone whose previous letter I had read, I composed my shorthand with the secret pleasure of a chess player who has seen four moves ahead. Once she looked up sharply and said, "You anticipate me, Miss Fairweather," and I said, "I try to serve efficiently, my lady," and looked out the window at the gulls, and that was that.

A spectator, I called myself. A spectator with excellent seats.

* * *

That afternoon, back at Ashcombe House, the last proper post of the season came up from the boat — I remember the boatman, Cobb's boy, stamping his boots on the step and complaining that his father said the crossings would stop within the fortnight — and among the ordinary grey drift of it, the bills and the circulars and one furious letter from the District Council about drains, there was an official-looking envelope with a crest I didn't recognize and the words **COUNTY BRIDGE & CAUSEWAY COMMISSION** stamped squarely across the flap.

Verity was resting. She rested every day from four until six, and during those hours the house belonged to Mrs. Nance the cook, to Alice the housemaid, and to me, and I belonged to the letters.

I took them to the still room, which had become mine by unspoken treaty — nobody else wanted it, it smelled faintly of old lavender and older damp, and it had a kettle. I lit the lamp, filled the kettle, and while it grumbled toward a boil I sorted the post into piles the way a customs officer sorts cargo: Verity's personal correspondence (to be read twice), household matters (skimmed), and the official envelope, which I weighed in my hand and regarded with professional interest.

Bridges, I reasoned, closing or otherwise, were public business. Public business was nearly as good as private business. I held the envelope over the steam and watched the seal soften, thinking — I remember thinking this precisely, and remembering it later with a strange tenderness for my former self — *nothing interesting ever happens here.*

The letter inside was typewritten. It said:

> Dear Lady Ashcombe,
>
> The Commission regrets to inform you that the Marrow Quay tidal causeway will close to all traffic on the 30th instant for essential winter repairs to the northern arch and the ironwork of the central span. The works are expected to continue until the spring tides of March, weather permitting.
>
> During the closure, communication with the mainland will be by boat only, and then only in suitable conditions. The Commission advises residents to lay in supplies accordingly.
>
> We have the honour to remain, Madam, your obedient servants,
> Per pro. H. R. Lewthwaite, Secretary

I read it twice, as was my custom. The first time for the facts. The second time for the feeling underneath them, which was the part I actually collected — the way a man named Lewthwaite, sitting in an office in Canterbury, had typed *we have the honour* without any honour being present, the way the word *regrets* did duty for an emotion no one involved had ever felt.

So. The bridge would close. The island of Marrow Quay — for an island is what we became each winter, though we kept the manners of a peninsula — would be cut off until March. No post but what the boat brought, when the boat dared. No trains. No visitors. No escape.

And here is the thing I want you to understand, because it explains almost everything that followed: I was *pleased*.

I sat there in the lavender-smelling still room with the kettle ticking as it cooled, and I felt a warmth that I mistook for contentment. A closed causeway meant a closed world, and a closed world meant that my little kingdom of intercepted knowledge would grow richer and stranger, letters accumulating like flotsam, secrets marooned with me through the winter. I imagined the months ahead: lamplight, kettle-steam, the whole drama of the nation arriving piece by piece at my door, and me reading all of it, knowing all of it, belonging to none of it.

Comfortable invisibility. That was my condition, and I wore it the way Verity wore her medals — pinned on, displayed to no one, heavy in a way I had stopped noticing.

Or perhaps I hadn't quite stopped noticing. Perhaps that was the hollow feeling, the one I got sometimes of an evening, standing in the hall with the day's letters delivered and nothing left over for myself. Everyone else in the house had a place in the story. Verity had her campaign; Mrs. Nance had her kitchen; Alice had her sweetheart in Hythe and her Sunday walks. Even the cat had a chair that was acknowledged as hers. I had a chair too, but it was the kind of chair you stand behind.

History was happening — I could feel it happening, out past the causeway, out past the sandbanks, everywhere at once, enormous and urgent — and my role in it was to hold the envelope over the kettle.

I resealed the Commission's letter, pressed it flat under the ledger weight, and carried the post upstairs to the library, where I left it on the green baize tray in perfect order, bills on top, official notice squared neatly at the corner, nothing disturbed except everything.

* * *

Dinner that evening was quiet in the way that only a large house can be quiet — not silence but a sort of upholstered hush, the sound of a building holding its breath around the people inside it. Verity dined alone in the small dining room; I ate in the housekeeper's room with Mrs. Nance and Alice, and Mrs. Nance reported that the Commission men had been seen measuring the northern arch, and Alice reported that her sweetheart said the war would be over before the bridge reopened, and I reported nothing, because reporting was not my role. I asked questions instead, gently, the way you feed a fire.

"Over before spring, do you think?" I said to Alice.

"Tommy says certainly by Christmas."

"Christmas," said Mrs. Nance, with the contempt of a woman who had fed men through two wars' worth of Christmases, "is a long way off when you're hungry."

Afterward I went up with the coffee tray, because on Thursdays Verity took her coffee in the library and worked late, and Thursday was my favourite evening of the week, though I would have denied it under oath. She sat at her desk with the green-shaded lamp burning, and the Commission's letter lay open before her, and she was wearing the expression she reserved for obstacles: chin lifted, mouth flat, the look of a woman preparing to argue with geography itself.

"They mean to close the bridge," she said, without turning round. I had learned long ago not to startle her; she heard me the way she heard everything, which was selectively but completely.

"So the letter says, my lady."

"Four months." She tapped the page. "Four months cut off from London, from the WSPU offices, from Parliament, at the precise moment—" She stopped. Something crossed her face, some calculation or memory, and she pressed her lips together as if filing it away. "At an inconvenient moment. Well. We shall manage. We have managed worse."

"Yes, my lady."

She turned then and looked at me, and for a moment — just a moment — the imperiousness dropped away and I saw underneath it something tired and old and oddly frightened, like a glimpse of the sea floor when a wave draws back. "Miss Fairweather," she said. "How long have you been with me?"

"Two years and a month, my lady."

"Two years and a month." She repeated it slowly, as though checking it against a ledger of her own. Her eyes went to the window, where the dark had come down over the gardens, and beyond the dark, though neither of us could see it, the causeway lay gleaming in the moonlight with its winter of repairs ahead of it. "And in all that time, have I ever explained to you why I stay here? Why a woman with my connections buries herself on this coast, driving a branch society of forty-seven members like a general commanding a platoon?"

I hesitated. It was the kind of question that had a right answer and a true answer and I suspected they were different. "No, my lady."

"No," she agreed softly. "I haven't. And you've never asked. Which is either discretion or indifference, and I have never quite decided which."

"It's discretion, my lady," I said, and heard how smooth it sounded, and hated myself very slightly, and did it anyway.

Something moved behind her eyes — amusement, perhaps, or its elder relation. "Hm," she said. "There are letters in my writing case, Miss Fairweather, that would interest even a discreet woman. Letters going back a long way. To another country, and another life." She looked at the locked writing case on the shelf above her desk — I had dusted around it for two years and never once seen it opened — and then she looked at me again, longer this time, as if taking a measurement. "Perhaps this winter. If the bridge must keep us all here, we may as well be honest with one another. Good night, Miss Fairweather."

"Good night, my lady."

I carried the tray down through the sleeping house with my heart beating faster than a coffee tray warranted. *Letters going back a long way.* I turned it over as I walked, the way you turn a strange coin. Was it an invitation? A warning? A test?

In the still room I banked the lamp low and sat for a while with my hands around the cooling kettle, doing sums I did not know I was doing. A closed bridge. A locked case. An employer suddenly, unsettlingly gentle. Somewhere out beyond the sandbars, the great machinery of the world was grinding toward whatever came next, and for the first time in two years of spectatorship, I had the odd sensation of the play turning to look at me — of the fourth wall developing a crack, hairline, barely visible, right down the middle of my comfortable invisibility.

I should have paid attention to that crack. But the kettle was warm and the lamp was low and the winter was long, and I blew out the flame believing — comfortably, invisibly, wrongly — that tomorrow would be exactly like today.

Three days later, among the last letters the autumn boat would bring for a long while, there was one with a foreign stamp and handwriting I had never seen before, signed at the bottom in firm black ink:

*Yours always, E. Brandt.*

I held it over the kettle, and my hands were not steady, and I opened it anyway.
