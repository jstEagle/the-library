## 2. Lady Ashcombe's Last Campaign

Verity Ashcombe had outlived three kings, two of them by sheer stubbornness, and she did not intend to be hurried by anything so vulgar as her own body.

She woke, as she had woken for sixty-one years, before the gulls. The house was cold in the way that only very old houses on very exposed headlands could be cold—not merely chilly but *opinionated*, as though the walls themselves disapproved of anyone foolish enough to sleep past dawn in November. She lay still beneath the eiderdown and conducted her morning inventory, a ritual she had begun that autumn without ever quite admitting to herself why.

Hands: both present. The right one ached at the knuckle where a policeman had twisted it outside Holloway in 1910, but it opened when she asked it to. Legs: serviceable, if slow about the stairs. Heart: beating with the irregular insistence of a clock that wanted winding. Mind—

The mind, thank God, remained a fortress.

She rose, dressed without assistance—she would be carried to her grave before she allowed a maid to button her boots—and went to the window. Below, the causeway stretched across the estuary like a grey ribbon laid on wet slate. Emil Brandt's causeway. His bridge, his arches, his mathematics holding back the sea twice a day, every day, for sixteen years, and not one person in Marrow Quay thought to thank him because he had built things that worked, and working things, Verity had long ago observed, were invisible. Only broken things got remembered.

She knew precisely how much of the winter remained before the tides closed the road. Eleven days. She had counted them the way other women her age counted spoons.

*You are being morbid,* she told herself, and went down to breakfast.

* * *

The writing case sat on the shelf of her study where it had sat since 1908: walnut and brass, German-made, with a lock that had defeated two burglars' worth of curiosity from her nieces. It was not locked now. She had unlocked it in October and simply—failed to close it again.

Inside lay papers that would scandalize half of Kent and vindicate the other half, depending on which half read furthest. Correspondence from Pankhurst herself. A photograph of a hunger-striking Verity looking, she thought, remarkably well considering. Deeds. And beneath all of it, wrapped in oilcloth like something infectious, a single document she had never signed.

The deed naming Emil Brandt beneficiary of Bridge Cottage and an annuity of forty pounds a year.

Sixteen years she had let it lie there. Sixteen years, and every autumn she told herself she would execute it after the season closed, and every spring she found reasons—the campaign, the tour, the by-election in Blackburn, the simple animal relief of being needed elsewhere. It was astonishing, she reflected now, how many urgent causes one could find for avoiding a single kindness.

Because that was what it was, of course. Not sentiment. Kindness. She had promised him sanctuary at Ashcombe House should he ever need it, standing on his own unfinished bridge in June of 1908 with the wind pulling her hair loose and her heart behaving in a manner she had since filed under *youthful irregularities*. She had promised, and Emil Brandt had gone home to Hamburg and married someone sensible, and Verity had stayed and fought for the franchise and told herself the promise had expired like a milk ticket.

Promises, it turned out, kept no calendar.

She took the deed out and read it again. Her solicitor's careful language. Emil's name spelled correctly, which had pleased her obscurely at the time. And at the bottom, the blank space where a signature ought to go, white as a held breath.

"You are a coward, Verity Ashcombe," she informed the study aloud. The study declined to argue.

The trouble was that executing the deed required explanation. Explanation required confessing that she, Lady Verity Ashcombe, scourge of four Home Secretaries, had once been undone by a man who drew bridges. That she had loved him not wisely but geographically—he had been *there*, on the island, for eleven months, and she had been twenty-nine and furious with the whole arrangement of the world, and he had listened to her rages as though they were weather reports from a country he intended to visit. He had argued with her too. About load tolerances, about whether beauty was a structural requirement or a luxury, about whether women were persons. He had conceded the last point within a fortnight, which was more than any Englishman had managed in a decade.

And she had promised him, because he had said—lightly, terribly lightly—"If I am ever in trouble in your country, may I knock on your door?" and she had said "You may bring your whole family and my cook will complain and I will ignore her," and they had laughed, and then the season ended and duty collected its debts.

Now there was a war coming. She had watched the newspapers all summer the way one watches a boil: knowing, dreading, unable to look away. If it came—if Germany became the enemy—then Emil Brandt would need that promise more than he had ever needed anything, and she would have less ability than ever to honor it without ruin.

Mortality, Verity had decided over the past several sleepless nights, was a most unsporting opponent. It did not debate. It simply waited, patient as tide, until your arguments ran out.

* * *

At eleven o'clock she sent for Xanthe Fairweather.

This was unusual, and she knew it was unusual, which annoyed her. For six years Xanthe had been exactly what a chauffeur ought to be: punctual, silent, mechanically gifted, and blessedly incurious. Verity had hired her partly for competence and partly because a woman who could strip an engine gave the county something to discuss besides hemlines. What Verity had never done was summon her to the study, in the morning, for conversation.

But she had caught herself watching the girl lately. Watching her carry letters to the post and wondering—not idly—which ones Xanthe read. Because Xanthe *did* read them; Verity had known since the first winter, had noticed the faint smudge of steam on an envelope, the way certain letters came back from their sealing slightly wrong, a flap adhered a hair's breadth off true. A lesser woman might have been outraged. Verity had been fascinated. Here was a girl who believed herself invisible, reading history through other people's mail, convinced she was a spectator at events she was in fact quietly touching. It was like watching someone pick up live coals believing them painted wood.

Verity had said nothing. There are some educations one must allow to complete themselves.

"Sit down, Miss Fairweather."

Xanthe sat, hands folded, face composed into its usual pleasant nothing. Good posture. Better secrets. Verity felt an unexpected tenderness toward her, which she disguised as severity because tenderness, unguarded, made her voice do things.

"I am going to tell you a story," Verity said, "and then I am going to give you an instruction, and you will find the instruction strange, and you will obey it anyway, because unlike most people your age you appear capable of following a thought to its conclusion."

Xanthe's composure flickered—one degree, no more. "Yes, my lady."

"In 1908 this house employed a German engineer. He came to build the causeway bridge. His name was Emil Brandt." She said the name plainly and watched it land somewhere behind the girl's eyes. "He was the finest mind I ever met and the worst conversationalist in three counties, because he believed precision was a form of courtesy. He was also," and here Verity paused, weighing sixty years of discretion against whatever it was she saw forming in the room, "the closest thing to a mistake I ever permitted myself."

Silence. Outside, a gull said something rude.

"The promise I made him is in this case. You will not open the case. You will not ask me what is in it. But you will remember that it exists, and that I intend—before the tides close the road—to settle my affairs properly, as any commander settles accounts before winter quarters. Do you understand?"

"Yes, my lady."

"There is one more thing." Verity rose, crossed to the window, and looked down at the causeway, at the water already beginning its patient daily argument with the road. When she spoke again, her voice had lost its parade-ground edge, and this frightened her more than any symptom had. "I have had two letters this month from my doctor in London, and I have burned both without answering, which tells you everything about their contents. My father died at sixty-eight. My mother at fifty-three. I am seventy-one and I have spent my body like a drunkard spends wages, and I am not fool enough to pretend the bill won't come."

"My lady—"

"Don't." She raised a hand. "I don't want comfort. Comfort is for people who intend to stop working. I want—" She stopped. Started again, more quietly. "I want to know that when the time comes, the right things happen in this house. Not the convenient things. The right ones. Can you distinguish between those two categories, Miss Fairweather?"

Xanthe was quiet a moment. "I think so, my lady."

"I believe you can. That is why you're here." Verity turned and studied her—this competent, secretive, watchable girl whom she had never once thanked for six years of faultless service, and whom she was about, without explaining why, to make a witness. "Winter is coming. The post will thin to nothing. Whatever arrives in these last days before the causeway closes—you will bring it to me directly. Unopened."

A pause, delicate as a tripwire.

"Yes, my lady," Xanthe said, and Verity noted, with something between amusement and alarm, that the girl's ears had gone pink.

*Guilty conscience,* Verity thought, and found, to her own surprise, that she did not mind. Let the girl steam her letters. One day soon there might be no one left whose correspondence was worth the candle—and better, far better, that whatever truth existed in this house should pass through the hands of someone who understood that paper could be a weapon, a mercy, or a promise, depending entirely on who was brave enough to deliver it.

"That will be all," she said. "And Miss Fairweather—"

Xanthe paused at the door.

"When the instruction comes—and it will come sooner than either of us expects—I will need you to act quickly, and ask nothing, and trust that an old woman has arranged her life better than her reputation suggests." She smiled, and it was almost the smile that had unnerved Home Secretaries. "Dismissed."

The door clicked shut. Verity stood alone with her unwritten deed and her unwon argument against the tide, and told herself, firmly, that she had eleven days.

She had nine.

* * *

It was on the second evening—after the doctor's third letter arrived and was, per instruction, brought to her sealed; after she read it alone by lamplight and burned it in the study grate with steady hands and a face like carved oak—that Verity sat down at her desk and began, at last, the letter she had owed for sixteen years.

*Dear Emil,* she wrote. *You will be astonished to hear from me, and more astonished by the reason.*

Outside, the first hard frost of the season silvered the causeway stones, and far off across the estuary, in a country sliding toward war, an engineer she had loved in the only grammar she permitted herself was, at that very moment, taking up his pen.
