## 3. The Bridge Closes

The last day of the causeway arrived the way most important days do — without announcing itself.

Xanthe Fairweather learned of it from Mrs. Tregose at the bakery, who had learned it from her husband, who had it on the authority of the bridge-keeper's boy: the winter repairs were to begin Monday, and the tide road would be closed to all traffic until spring. No carts, no motorcars, no post. Nothing but gulls and the sea going about its business over the top of a mile of cobbles that would spend the next five months pretending to be seabed.

"You'll want to lay in your petrol," Mrs. Tregose said, wrapping a loaf in paper as though she were embalming it. "And candles. And I should think a bit of tinned something, if you can get it. Once we're shut off, we're shut off proper."

"Shut off," Xanthe repeated. She turned the phrase over. Marrow Quay sat on its finger of coast like a stone in a ring, and the causeway was the setting that held it to the mainland — a low tidal road, built by Emil Brandt's drawings and half a century of Cornish masonry, that showed itself twice a day and hid twice a day, so that even in the best of seasons the village lived by the tide table the way other places lived by the church clock. In summer this was romance. Postmen timed their rounds to the ebb; excursionists came across with their hats pinned down; Lady Verity Ashcombe drove her motorcar over the wet stones with her chin lifted as though she personally had ordered the sea to stand aside.

In winter, it was a lid.

"I don't suppose," Xanthe said, "that anyone has told her ladyship."

Mrs. Tregose gave her the look Cornishwomen reserve for questions whose answers are already known. "The bridge-keeper's boy told her ladyship before he told his own mother. She sent back word that the repairs ought to have begun in October and that whoever scheduled them for November wanted sacking."

That sounded like Verity. Xanthe carried the loaf out to the Humber, which stood where she'd left it by the bakery wall, gleaming dully under a sky the color of unpolished pewter. She was fond of the car in the way one is fond of a large animal that cannot love you back: she knew its tempers, fed it the good petrol, spoke to it when no one was listening. For eleven months of the year she drove Lady Verity across that causeway twice a week and sometimes more, and every crossing felt faintly like getting away with something — the sea on either side, the water just beginning its patient crawl over the stones behind them.

Now the stones would lose the argument until April.

She drove home along the coast road thinking about coziness, which is what the village was already calling it. Cozy. Shut in together with the wood smoke and the salt and everyone's business pressed close as pickled onions. There was a version of the winter ahead that sounded almost pleasant from the outside: long evenings, lamplit rooms, the whole peninsula tucked up like a parcel. And underneath it, the small claustrophobic fact that there was nowhere to go. That the tide would decide things now. That if anything happened — illness, fire, a letter that couldn't wait — the sea would simply keep it.

Xanthe had lived at Ashcombe House two years and had never once thought of herself as trapped there. She was beginning to consider it now, the way you consider a locked door only after hearing the key turn.

* * *

Lady Verity received the news of the closure standing at the library window, which was where she received most news, on the theory — Xanthe suspected — that a woman on her feet could not be handed bad tidings sitting down.

"So." Verity looked out at the grey line of the causeway, visible at low water like the spine of some enormous sleeping creature. "Five months of Mrs. Cobbett's stewed mutton and Mr. Penhale's sermons, and not a single fresh newspaper between now and the primroses. Well. We survived the mud of Holloway prison, Xanthe; we shall survive the mud of Cornwall." She said *we* the way she always did, folding her chauffeur into her campaigns without asking leave, and Xanthe, who had long ago stopped correcting her, merely nodded.

"There's the wireless at the inn, my lady. And papers come on the mail van when the weather allows."

"The mail van comes when the weather allows," Verity corrected. "Which between November and February is a philosophical distinction rather than a practical one." She turned from the window. "We shall want stores laid in. Coal. Lamp oil. Doctor Pengelly keeps his own boat, thank God, though why God should be thanked for a man who rows like a crab I couldn't say. Make me a list. And Xanthe—" a pause, the particular pause that meant an instruction of real weight was coming, "—the writing case is to stay locked. If anything should happen to me this winter, and I assure you nothing will, but if it should, the case goes into my solicitor's hands unopened. Do you understand?"

"Perfectly, my lady."

It was such an odd instruction, and delivered so casually, that Xanthe thought about it all through the laying-in of stores, and then thought about it again that night — but we are getting ahead of ourselves. First came the war.

* * *

The war arrived, appropriately enough, late and by sea.

Word had been trickling up from London for days — the Vicar had a cousin in the Admiralty who wrote alarming letters — but nobody in Marrow Quay quite believed a thing until it came in print. The last mainland papers of the season came over the causeway on the Tuesday, carried by the mail van making its final run, and by Wednesday morning the village had read them to pieces. Britain and Germany at war. Belgium invaded. Kitchener asking for men.

Xanthe read the papers too, of course. She read everything; it was her nature and her vice. She read the headlines aloud to Cook while they both pretended to be doing other things, and she watched the news travel outward from the kitchen like ink in water — to the stables, to the cottages, to the church, where Mr. Penhale announced a special service of intercession and managed to make even that sound like a mild scolding.

At Ashcombe House, the war produced one effect above all others: silence. Not the household's — Verity's. Xanthe found her ladyship in the study on Thursday evening, standing very still among her framed photographs of processions and deputations, of women in sashes, of a younger Verity with her arm linked through other women's arms outside Holloway gates. On the desk lay a newspaper folded to a map of Europe, and beside the map, half-hidden by an inkwell, a photograph Xanthe had never seen: a man in shirtsleeves, laughing, his hand resting on the shoulder of a machine Xanthe recognized at once, absurdly, as a typewriter.

"My lady?"

Verity closed the drawer over the photograph without haste and without hurry, the way one closes a door on a room one means to return to. "Germany," she said, as if continuing a conversation. "I have spent thirty years arguing that nations are made of people, and now I am expected to pretend they are made of armies. Half my correspondents will be posting their letters under censorship. The other half—" She stopped. "The other half may not be able to post letters at all."

"It will be over by Christmas," Xanthe offered, because everyone was saying it and someone must.

"Everyone said the causeway would be open till December, and here we are shut in November. Prophecy is a poor trade, Xanthe. Stick to driving." But her voice was elsewhere, in whatever drawer she'd closed, and she dismissed Xanthe with unusual gentleness, which frightened Xanthe more than shouting would have done.

* * *

By the following week the sea had taken the road.

Xanthe walked down to watch it happen, because everyone did; there is something in human beings that requires them to attend closures. A knot of villagers stood at the mainland end — no, at the village end, she corrected herself; Marrow Quay was the world now, and everything beyond the water was abroad — watching the tide come in over the cobbles with the unhurried confidence of an institution resuming its duties. Mr. Tregose had brought his children. Old Mrs. Pascoe had brought a thermos and opinions. The bridge-keeper stood at the barrier he'd erected, a wooden affair with a notice painted on it in letters that managed to look apologetic: CLOSED UNTIL FURTHER NOTICE BY ORDER OF THE COUNTY SURVEYOR.

"Further notice," Mrs. Pascoe sniffed, "will arrive by albatross."

There was laughter, but it was the thin kind. Everyone understood the arithmetic. Five months. One hundred and fifty-odd days of high water twice daily over the only road in or out. The doctor with his rowboat. The wireless at the inn, crackling with bulletins that grew worse by the week. And the post — the post slowing like a clock winding down. The van had come three times the first week of November, twice the second, and then the weather closed its fist, and the letters that did get through came damp and late, smelling of salt, like messages from survivors.

Xanthe felt it first as a professional grievance. Her work was driving, and there was now nowhere to drive. The Humber went into the coach house under a canvas shroud, and she oiled its engine and covered it and felt she had buried something alive. Then, with the driving gone, she discovered how much of her day had been shaped around departures and arrivals — the tide tables, the lists, the runs to the station on the far side. The days opened up like empty rooms.

Cook filled hers with preserving. Verity filled hers with correspondence — what little remained of it — and committees conducted by letter, and long silences at windows. And Xanthe filled hers, in the evenings, with the thing she did not discuss: the reading.

She would not have called it snooping. Snooping implied shame, and Xanthe had none; she had instead a conviction, quiet and complete, that history happened in letters, and that someone ought to witness it. When Lady Verity Ashcombe dictated replies to the great and the near-great of the suffrage movement, when ministers' wives and movement women and once — memorably — a person who signed himself only "A Sympathizer in the Home Office" wrote to the house, those letters passed through Xanthe's hands. They were left on hall tables. They were entrusted to her to post, or to fetch from the tray, or to carry upstairs. And in the evenings, by lamplight, in her narrow room under the eaves, Xanthe steamed them open.

Not all of them. Only the interesting ones. Only the ones that smelled of history — and she had a nose for it now, two years trained. She had read the letter announcing the Cat and Mouse Act before the newspapers had it. She had read three separate accounts of the same riot in Glasgow, each written by a woman claiming to have been calm throughout, each describing the same broken window with unconcealed pride. She had read a proposal of marriage sent to Verity in 1913 by a widowed magistrate, and Verity's reply, which declined the offer in language so courteous it took two readings to confirm the refusal, and which ended, devastatingly, *I remain, sir, yours in the Cause.*

Tonight, the third week of November, the tray held only four items, brought across the water by Doctor Pengelly in his boat, wrapped in oilcloth like contraband: a circular from the Red Cross committee; a bill from the coal merchant, forwarded from the mainland agent, charging rates Xanthe considered piratical; a letter from a Mrs. Fennick in London full of war-work enthusiasm and requests; and a slim envelope, foreign-looking, with stamps Xanthe didn't recognize and handwriting that slanted hard to the right, urgent as a person running.

Xanthe held it up to her lamp.

The name on it was German. She could feel that before she could read it — the shape of the words, the density of them. And beneath the foreign address, in smaller English script added by some postal hand, a forwarding note, and beneath that, in faded ink, a name she knew from the house itself, from the brass plate on a door downstairs, from the drawings rolled in tubes in the study: the engineer of the causeway, the builder of the bridge that was at this very moment drowned under the winter sea.

Emil Brandt.

Xanthe looked at the envelope for a long time. Downstairs, the house ticked and settled. Somewhere below, Verity was awake too — she heard the floorboard in the study, the particular slow pacing that meant her ladyship was not sleeping either.

The lamp hissed softly. Outside, the tide was coming in over the bridge, covering the road, covering the way out, sealing the village like wax seals a letter.

Xanthe put the kettle on.

* * *

She told herself she would only steam it far enough to see whether it was private. This was the ritual lie she told every time, and she observed it faithfully, the way one observes grace before meals one intends to enjoy. The kettle breathed its thin steam over the flap; the glue surrendered, as glue always did, with a sigh; the letter slid out into her hands like a fish.

It was dated the twenty-ninth of July — July! It had been traveling four months, held up, redirected, held up again, passed hand to hand like a fugitive, and it bore the marks of the journey: a censor's stamp, crossed out; another stamp; a corner worn soft from handling. Xanthe unfolded it carefully, and read:

*Dear V.,*

*I write against time and against sense, knowing what is coming and unable to prevent any of it. By the time this reaches you we shall be enemies, which is a word I refuse, but the world is not taking my refusal. Therefore I write while the pens still cross the water.*

*My daughter Greta is nineteen. Her mother is dead these six years. If what is coming comes as I fear, she will be stranded between countries, belonging properly to neither, and there is one place on this earth I promised her she could go. You will remember the promise. I have never doubted it, even in the years you did not write. Send word if you can. Send nothing if you cannot. Either way, she may come, and I ask you only to remember what you said to me under the bridge, in the year it was finished, when we were both young and the century was new.*

*Under the bridge, V. You will know the place.*

*Your friend, if the word survives the season,*
*E.B.*

Xanthe read it twice. Three times. The lamp hissed. The tide, invisible from her attic window but present in her mind, climbed the cobbles of the causeway mile by mile.

A promise. Under the bridge. A daughter named Greta, nineteen, belonging to neither country, coming — perhaps already coming — toward a house at the end of a road that the sea had just swallowed for the winter.

She sat very still, holding the future in both hands, and became aware of two facts simultaneously. The first was that this letter was the most historical thing she had ever touched, and she had touched plenty — she had steamed open the private griefs and triumphs of famous women, and thought herself a spectator at the edge of the stage.

The second fact arrived more slowly, cold as the water out in the dark: this letter was not addressed to her. It was addressed to Lady Verity Ashcombe, and Lady Verity Ashcombe was awake downstairs, and the question of what to do with it — post it to her ladyship's tray like an honest servant, or slide it back into its envelope, reseal it, and let it vanish as if the sea had kept it — that question belonged entirely, terribly, to Xanthe Fairweather.

Downstairs, the floorboards stopped pacing.

Footsteps started up the stairs.
