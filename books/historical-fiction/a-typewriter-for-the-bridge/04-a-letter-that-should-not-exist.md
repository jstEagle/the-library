## 4. A Letter That Should Not Exist

The last post of the season came on a Tuesday, in a canvas sack that smelled of wet wool and lamp oil, and Xanthe Fairweather carried it up the drive with the particular reverence she reserved for other people's news.

There was not much of it. The war had thinned the mail the way frost thins a hedge—everything still standing, but bare, and rattling. Three circulars from agricultural societies. A bill from a seed merchant in Truro. A church newsletter with the vicar's handwriting marching across the envelope like small determined soldiers. And one letter that made her stop halfway up the drive and stand very still, so still that a robin landed on the gatepost beside her and regarded her with professional interest.

The envelope was foreign. Not foreign in the way of the French fashion catalogues Lady Verity pretended not to order—foreign in the heavy, serious way. German stamps. A postmark smudged almost to nothing, as though the letter had been handled by many hands before it found its way to Marrow Quay, each of them reluctant. The ink on the front had bled slightly in some earlier rain.

*Lady V. Ashcombe, Ashcombe House, Marrow Quay, Cornwall.*

And beneath the address, in smaller writing, in English: *By hand of trust.*

Xanthe read the address three times. She was aware of the robin watching her, aware of the tide far below the causeway making its slow grey argument with the land, aware that her hands—the hands that changed tyres and cranked engines and had once, memorably, pulled a constable out of a ditch near Penzance—were trembling over an envelope that weighed less than a wren.

She did not open it there. She was a professional. She had standards. She carried it inside, set it on the silver salver in the hall with the rest, rang for Mrs. Tregose as usual, and then went out to the garage and checked the oil in the Daimler twice, which took her four minutes and told her nothing she didn't already know, because she was thinking about the envelope the whole time.

*By hand of trust.* Who wrote such a thing? Circulars came by post. Bills came by post. Love letters came by post, though Lady Verity's had stopped arriving sometime around the Coronation, or so Mrs. Tregose speculated, wrongly, because Mrs. Tregose speculated about everything except the true things, which were kept in the locked room of the house where nobody went.

Xanthe went there every night.

* * *

It had started, as most corruptions do, innocently, with lamplight.

In her first month at Ashcombe House, two years ago now, she had been asked to carry the evening post up to Lady Verity's study and had found the door ajar and her employer asleep in the wing chair with an unfinished letter sliding off her knee. Xanthe should have woken her. Instead she had stood in the doorway like a fool, and the letter had slid the last few inches, and turned over on the carpet, and she had seen her own name at the top of it.

*Dear Miss Fairweather,* Verity had written. *I am informed by Mrs. Tregose that you can be trusted, which is a phrase I distrust on principle, since everyone who is ever described as trustworthy turns out to be merely untested. I shall test her myself.*

Xanthe had read four lines before shame caught her by the collar. But four lines were enough. Four lines were enough to learn that Lady Verity Ashcombe, terror of two governments and ornament of a thousand platform meetings, thought about her chauffeur when the chauffeur wasn't in the room—and what she thought was neither warm nor cold but *assessing*, the way she assessed everything, the way she'd assessed Mr. Asquith from the front row of a hall in 1908 until he'd reportedly lost his place in his notes.

After that, the letters became a habit the way laudanum becomes a habit: first for curiosity, then for comfort, then because stopping seemed unthinkable. When Lady Verity went down after dinner to wind the clocks, Xanthe would take the day's correspondence to her own room above the coach house, and there, with a kettle of water and a thin knife and a patience she was not proud of, she would lift the seals and read.

She never took anything. She never copied anything. She simply read, the way one watches a play through a window—one is not in the story; one is only near it. That was the arrangement she had made with herself, and arrangements with oneself are the easiest kind to keep, because oneself never checks.

History, she had decided long ago, happened to other people. It happened to Lady Verity, who had been force-fed in Holloway and come out thinner and harder and more magnificent. It happened to generals and chancellors and the men who designed bridges. Xanthe's history was petrol and ignition timing and the particular way Cornwall mud seized a wheel in February. She was the audience. Comfortable, invisible, faintly hollow—that was her seat in the theatre, and she had chosen it herself, and if sometimes at night the hollowness echoed, well, that was the price of a good view.

Tonight she waited until the house settled. Mrs. Tregose's footsteps creaked up the back stair at half past ten, punctual as tide tables. Lady Verity's lamp went out at eleven, always eleven, even on nights when Xanthe could hear her pacing, which lately was most nights.

Xanthe crossed the yard under a sky scoured clean by wind. In her room, she filled the kettle and set the German letter on the table and looked at it for a long moment, the way one looks at a door one has decided to open knowing one cannot close it again.

The seal gave without protest. The paper was thin, aerogramme-fashion, covered on both sides in a small, upright, engineer's hand—each letter formed with the care of someone who drew bridges for a living and could not help drawing them even in grief.

She read it once for the words. Then she read it again because she did not believe the words.

*Honoured lady,—*

*I write against all advice and with little hope, for I know what my country has done and I know what my country therefore now is, in your eyes and in the eyes of England. I will not insult you by saying I am not Germany. I am Germany. I was born there; I love its music and its forests and its stubborn, maddening people; and I love also the coast of your Cornwall, which I surveyed in better years, and one woman upon it.*

*You will have heard, or you will soon hear, what has become of men like me here. My papers are called into question. My work is finished. There are men who come to the house and ask questions in voices too polite, and my neighbours look at me in the street the way one looks at weather that has turned. I do not write to plead for myself. A man who built his life across water should have known that water rises.*

*I write for Greta.*

*My daughter is twenty-three. She has my stubbornness and none of my caution, which is a poor inheritance in this year. If the worst comes—if they take me, or worse—I have nowhere in the world to send her except to you. You gave me your word once, in a summer when we were both younger and the world was wide enough for our arguments. You said: if ever you need sanctuary, Ashcombe House is yours. I have never asked anything of you since. I am asking now, and asking everything.*

*Send for her, honoured lady. Or if you cannot send, receive her. She will come quietly. She will tell no one what she is. Let her be Belgian, let her be French, let her be anything but what she is, which is mine and alone.*

*You will burn this letter. I know you. Burn it, then—but burn it slowly, and remember that a promise does not expire because the world has grown inconvenient.*

*Your servant, in spite of everything,*
*Emil Brandt*

Xanthe sat with the letter in her lap while the kettle behind her came slowly to a boil and nobody poured it.

*A promise does not expire because the world has grown inconvenient.*

She read it a third time, hunting for the trick in it, the forgery, the madness—some seam by which she could unpick it and return to being an audience. There was no seam. The hand was steady and sad and certain, the hand of a man who had measured the span between two cliffs and knew exactly how far the fall was.

Greta. He had a daughter named Greta, and he meant to send her across a war to a house on a tidal causeway, on the strength of a promise Xanthe had never heard of, made in a summer before Xanthe existed in these people's lives.

And Lady Verity—what would Lady Verity do? Xanthe tried to imagine it and found she could imagine two women equally vividly: the Lady Verity of the platform, who had said *the law is made by men, therefore we must make ourselves unbearable to men*, and who burned her bridges with a flourish; and the other Lady Verity, glimpsed rarely, in the lamplight of the study, turning a photograph face-down when Xanthe came in too quickly, a photograph of a young man with an engineer's careful parting to his hair, standing on an unfinished pier with his hat in his hand.

Oh, Xanthe thought. Oh, *that's* who that is.

The kettle screamed. She scalded her thumb rescuing it and swore, quietly, in the vocabulary of garages rather than ladies, and resealed the letter with the flat iron she kept for exactly this purpose, pressing the envelope under her mattress for an hour to flatten the evidence of steam. By midnight it looked innocent again. By midnight it looked like what it no longer was: a closed thing.

She carried it in at breakfast and put it on the salver with the seed bill, and watched Mrs. Tregose bear it away on the tray, and felt the crack go through her somewhere under the ribs—a fine hairline crack, inaudible, structural.

She had read the letter. For two years she had read the letters and told herself it cost nothing, that she was a spectator, that history passed through this house like light through glass and left her unwarmed and unsinged. But she had read this letter, and now she knew something the household did not know, something a man across the sea had entrusted to paper and a dying faith, and knowledge, she discovered, is not a spectator sport. Once you know, you are *in*. There is no aisle seat. There is no programme to hide behind.

* * *

At noon, Lady Verity sent for her.

Not to the study—to the morning room, which was worse. The morning room was where Verity did her kindnesses, and her kindnesses were more alarming than her rages. She sat by the window with the mail sorted into three piles: bills, circulars, and a single letter held in both hands as though it were a bird that might startle.

"Miss Fairweather." Her voice was level. "You drove into Marrow Quay yesterday."

"I did, my lady. For the paraffin."

"And the post office was open?"

"It was, my lady. Just. Mr. Curnow says he'll be closing at two now the boats are irregular."

"Mm." Verity looked down at the letter. Xanthe watched her thumb move along the edge of the envelope, back and forth, a small unconscious circuit, like a woman worrying a ring she had taken off long ago. "Did anything arrive for me yesterday that struck you as unusual?"

The question hung in the air of the morning room with the dust motes and the smell of beeswax.

Here it was, then. Here was the moment Xanthe's double life ended—in exposure, in disgrace, in the walk down the drive with her references and her box. She opened her mouth to confess everything, two years of kettles and seals and stolen lamplight, and what came out instead, to her lasting astonishment, was:

"The seed catalogue, my lady. Mr. Curnow complained the Germans had cornered the market in dahlia tubers."

A silence. Then Lady Verity laughed—one short bark, surprised out of her, gone again immediately, but real. "Curnow would blame the Kaiser for the weather if he thought it would get him sympathy at the inn." She looked at the letter again. Her thumb stopped moving. "No. Nothing unusual. Very well, Miss Fairweather. The Daimler wants exercising, I'm told. The roads won't drive themselves."

Xanthe withdrew. In the hall she paused, and looked back through the morning-room door.

Lady Verity Ashcombe had risen and was standing with her back to the window, so that the light came through her grey hair and made it briefly white fire. In her right hand was the letter. In her left hand, produced from the writing case that lived on the sideboard—the locked writing case, brass-bound, older than the war, older perhaps than Xanthe—was a key.

She went out through the garden door toward the incinerator at the bottom of the wall.

Xanthe did not follow. She got the Daimler out and drove it around the park lanes for forty minutes with great mechanical thoroughness, and when she came back the incinerator was cold and swept and there was a smell of paper-ash on the wind, and Lady Verity was in the study with the blinds half down, sitting very straight, doing nothing at all, which for Lady Verity was the loudest thing she could do.

Burned, then. Burned slowly, as instructed, or quickly, as convenience dictated. Either way, the answer was no—or the answer was fear wearing no's clothes, or the answer was a promise being weighed against a war and found, for the present, lighter.

Xanthe cleaned the car. She wound the garage clock. She ate her supper and climbed her stairs and lay in the dark above the coach house listening to the sea working at the causeway stones, and told herself the sensible things: it was not her business; the letter was ash; whatever promise had been made in 1908 belonged to two people who were young when the world was another shape.

But she had read it. That was the fact she could not burn. *I have nowhere in the world to send her except to you.* Somewhere across the water a daughter named Greta was reading the same silence Verity had just made, and preparing, perhaps, to come anyway—because fathers who draw bridges know that promises, like spans, are built to carry weight whether or not anyone chooses to cross them.

And Xanthe, who had spent two years believing she was only the audience, lay awake and understood, with the particular clarity of one a.m., that she had known the ending of this letter before its author did.

She had read it first. Whatever came next—whatever footsteps came up the drive before the tide sealed the road for winter—she would recognize it. She would know exactly what it was.

Because she had read the letter that should not exist, and could not unknow it, and loyalty, she was beginning to suspect, cracks precisely where you thought it was load-bearing.

Three days later, at dawn, a figure appeared on the Marrow Quay road, walking, carrying a case.
