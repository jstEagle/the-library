## 5. What Was Promised in Peacetime

The summer of 1908 arrived at Marrow Quay the way all good things did there: late, and by water.

Verity Ashcombe was thirty-four that year and had already decided she would not marry. She had made the decision formally, in her diary, on the second of January, with a fountain pen and a certain flourish, as though a decision unrecorded were no decision at all. *I am married to the Cause*, she wrote, and beneath it, after some thought, *and I do not intend to take a lover out of boredom.* She had then underlined both sentences twice, which was how she knew they were true.

She was, in those days, already formidable. The newspapers had begun to spell her name correctly without being told. Her father, Sir Edmund Ashcombe, was three years dead and had left her the house, the marshes, the annuities, and a strong conviction—instilled over decades of dinner-table argument—that Verity would do exactly as she pleased and be damned to anyone who said otherwise. He had meant it as a complaint. She had received it as an inheritance.

So when the county surveyor's office wrote to say that the new tidal causeway bridge would require her cooperation—the approach road crossed two hundred yards of Ashcombe land—she replied that she would receive the engineer on Thursday, that she would give him twenty minutes, and that he should not expect tea.

He arrived on Thursday, gave her forty minutes of his time before she gave him any of hers, and asked for tea at the half hour, which she found so presumptuous she almost granted it.

His name was Emil Brandt. He was from Hamburg by way of three years in Zurich and one disastrous commission in Riga, and he spoke English with a precision that made most Englishmen sound like they were chewing their words before swallowing them. He was not handsome, precisely. He was *assembled*—tall, square through the shoulders, with fair hair going grey early at the temples and a face that seemed designed for concentration rather than charm. He wore his collar too tight and his opinions too openly, and when Verity told him the approach road would not cross her land, he set down his tea cup—he had got the tea after all; her housekeeper Mrs. Tregowan had overruled her, as Mrs. Tregowan overruled everyone—and said:

"That is not an engineering objection. That is a political one."

"It is a property one," said Verity.

"Property is politics wearing a coat," said Brandt, "and in England the coat is very fine indeed."

Verity stared at him. In thirty-four years, no man had ever suggested to her face that her property was merely politics in better tailoring. Her instinct was to have him removed from the premises. Her second instinct, arriving half a heartbeat later and considerably more alarming, was to ask him what else he thought.

"Sit back down," she said. "You have another twenty minutes."

* * *

The bridge took fourteen months to build, and Verity attended the site more often than any engineer could reasonably wish.

She told herself it was oversight. She was, after all, contributing land, and land contributed ought to be watched. But Mrs. Tregowan, who had run Ashcombe House since before Verity could reach the sideboard, took one look at the fourth consecutive Tuesday visit and said nothing, which from Mrs. Tregowan was a sermon.

Brandt, for his part, pretended not to notice her. This was his chief technique and he deployed it masterfully. When she appeared on the staging platform in her grey walking costume, he would nod once—*Fräulein*—no, he corrected himself within the first week, *Lady Ashcombe*, delivered with the faint irony of a man who had looked up exactly what the title meant and found it wanting—and return to his instruments. If she asked a question, he answered it completely and then stopped talking, which no one in her acquaintance had ever done. If she argued, he argued back, and here was the scandal of it: he argued *well*. Not the country-house fencing she was used to, all feints and flattery, but the blunt German school, where you stated your case, cited your figures, and if you were wrong, you said so, and if she was wrong—

Well. She was wrong perhaps twice that summer, and he told her so both times, and she went home both times furious, and lay awake both times re-running the conversation until two in the morning, revising her answers, winning arguments against a man who wasn't there.

"You are behaving like a girl," she informed her mirror in July.

The mirror declined to comment.

They fought about everything. They fought about the span—she wanted stone, he wanted steel, steel won because physics is immune to title deeds. They fought about the tolls—she wanted them abolished for market days, he explained patiently that bridges cost money, and she won that one, because she paid for the difference herself and dared him to object, and he didn't, only looked at her for a moment in a way she chose not to examine. They fought about votes for women, beginning the afternoon she found him reading a newspaper account of the Hyde Park demonstration with his head tilted like a heron considering a fish.

"Well?" she demanded.

"It is a curious thing," he said, folding the paper, "to build a bridge across a river while the people on either bank are still arguing about whether the river exists."

"I don't follow."

"The law says women are persons for taxes and not persons for ballots. A structure cannot stand on two different foundations, Lady Ashcombe. Sooner or later it cracks."

Verity stood very still on the half-built causeway, wind coming off the marsh with the smell of salt and cut peat, and felt something turn over in her chest that she refused, for another six weeks, to name.

"And what does Herr Brandt think should be done about it?"

"What I always do." He picked up his chalk and turned back to the stress calculations on the timber hoarding. "Build it properly, and let it bear its own weight."

* * *

In September the tide came in early and caught them both.

It was nobody's fault—or it was hers, if one assigned blame, since she had insisted on walking the full length of the unfinished span at four o'clock when the tide tables said five, and the tide tables, as Brandt pointed out with a lack of sympathy she found frankly unforgivable, described averages. They took refuge in the site hut, a shed of tarred boards smelling of creosote and cold tea, and watched through the one window as the water came up the marsh channels like something poured, silver and silent, erasing the road home.

"We will be here two hours," said Brandt. "Perhaps three."

"How tedious," said Verity, and sat down on the plan chest, and did not leave for three hours, and neither, she noticed, did he suggest any alternative.

What happened in that hut happened slowly, the way tides do. He showed her the drawings—not the polite versions for the county council but the working ones, covered in his own hand, corrections and second thoughts and a small marginal sketch of a gull done, evidently, out of boredom. She saw that he loved the bridge. Not engineering in the abstract, not Germany or England or progress, but this bridge, these particular piers standing in this particular mud, the exact curve of the arch he had calculated eleven times because ten had not satisfied him.

"Why eleven times?" she asked.

"Because a bridge is a promise," he said. "People will walk across it in the dark, in the rain, carrying children, carrying coffins. They will trust it without thinking of it. That is a great deal of trust to owe strangers. Eleven times is the least I can do."

Verity looked at the marginal gull. She thought of every speech she had ever given, every promise she had ever made to rooms full of strangers—that the world could be rebuilt, that the foundation was sound—and understood suddenly why she kept coming on Tuesdays.

"Emil," she said. It was the first time she had used his Christian name. He went quite still, the way men do when a fortress opens a gate they hadn't known was there. "Why did you come to England? Truly. Riga was a disaster, you said. Zurich paid well. Why here?"

He was quiet long enough that the tide filled the silence.

"Because I heard," he said finally, "that there was a woman in Cornwall who had told the Bishop of Truro, to his face, in front of forty people, that God had made no mistake and neither had she. I wanted to see such a country. I thought: a place that produces this woman may yet produce other things."

"He wrote to me afterwards," said Verity. "Eleven pages."

"Was it worth it?"

"He has never written again."

Brandt laughed—a real laugh, startled out of him, brief and low—and in the lamplight of the hut, with the water climbing the pilings outside, Verity Ashcombe admitted to herself, privately and without underlining anything, that she was in a great deal of trouble.

Nothing happened. That must be recorded plainly, because later, much later, people would assume otherwise, and the truth was stranger and sadder than assumption. Nothing happened, because she would not let it. She sat on the plan chest with her ankles crossed and her hands folded and conducted, internally, the most rigorous audit of her life. There was the Cause. There was the house. There was the name she had promised her father she would keep unborrowed and unbent. And there was this man, this foreigner, this builder of promises, looking at her across three feet of lamplit air as though she were a drawing he intended to get right on the eleventh attempt.

When the tide turned and began to fall, she stood up, straightened her skirt, and became Lady Ashcombe again so completely that even she half believed it.

"There is a cottage," she said, on the walk back along the drying causeway, water draining away around their boots. "On the estate. Trelowen—it's empty, it's been empty since old Mr. Pascoe died. If you want lodgings nearer the site instead of lodging at the inn—"

"I would pay rent."

"You will not. You will keep the gutters clear and complain about my fences, which is rent enough." She stopped walking. The evening was enormous around them, sky going copper over the sea, the half-built bridge behind them black against it. "Emil. I am about to say something, and you are going to want to answer it, and I am asking you not to. Not tonight."

He waited.

"One day," she said, "you may need what I have. Not money—I suspect you'd refuse money on principle, you're insufferable that way—but shelter. A roof, a door that locks from the inside, ground that cannot be taken from you. Men like us—" she caught herself, *men like us*, and pressed on before he could enjoy it, "—people who say things loudly make enemies quietly. Governments change. Borders move. I have read history; it is mostly a record of engineers and troublemakers discovering, too late, whose door they should have knocked on. So. Ashcombe House is large, badly organized, and defended by a housekeeper who frightens me. Whatever happens—war, ruin, disgrace, some minister taking a dislike to you—if you ever need it, it is yours. Not rented. Given. Do you understand me?"

The tide whispered along the flats. Somewhere out on the marsh a curlew called, that falling, grieving note.

"I understand," said Emil Brandt. "And I promise the same. If you ever need a bridge built—anywhere, out of anything—I will come."

"That is a foolish promise. I shall never need a bridge."

"All promises look foolish in peacetime," he said. "That is how you know they are real. Only fools and lovers make them, and I am at minimum one of those."

She did not ask which. She walked on, and he walked beside her, and the space between their hands stayed exactly the width of propriety, and stayed there, and stayed there, all the way home.

* * *

The bridge opened in November 1909. There was a ceremony. The Bishop of Truro came, and Verity shook his hand with impeccable warmth, and Emil Brandt stood at the rail of the new span in his best coat, collar too tight as always, watching the first cart cross with an expression Verity recognized and filed away and did not look at directly for eleven years.

He sailed for Hamburg the following week. At the station in Penzance he held out his hand, and she took it, and neither of them let go quite as briefly as the platform warranted.

"Trelowen stands empty," she said. "Remember."

"Lady Ashcombe," he said, "I remember everything. It is my curse. Ask the bridge."

The train came in. He boarded. Through the window he mouthed something she couldn't hear, and she chose—then, and for years afterward—to believe it was *eleven times*.

She walked back out onto the platform into the Cornish drizzle, alone, unmarried, undefeated, and entirely unaware that the promise she had just made would come due in a winter when the bridge itself would close, when the post would stop, and when keeping her word would cost more than she had ever imagined spending.

She was unaware, too, of the girl who would one day drive her motorcar and read her letters by lamplight, and of the letter already moving toward Marrow Quay through the peacetime mails, slow as a tide, patient as water.

But that is ahead. In 1909 there was only the platform, and the rain, and a woman who had just given away her house to a man who might never ask for it—and who walked home whistling, because she believed, as one believes at thirty-five, that duty and desire could be kept in separate rooms forever.

The tide, as Herr Brandt could have told her, always comes in early.
