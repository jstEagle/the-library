## 6. The Woman at the Gate

The letter had been ash for eleven days, and I had been a liar for exactly as long.

I want to be precise about this, because precision was the one virtue Lady Verity Ashcombe ever demanded of me, and because lying, like driving, is a craft that improves with practice. For the first day or two I told myself I had done nothing wrong. A woman may read what passes under her eyes. A chauffeur standing in a hallway when her employer burns a letter is not complicit; she is furniture. But by the third morning, when Verity rang the bell twice instead of once—a signal we had never agreed upon but both understood—and said, "Miss Fairweather, if anyone asks, you know nothing of any correspondence from Germany," and I heard myself answer, "No, my lady," in a voice as smooth as a gear change, then I knew. Furniture does not say no, my lady. Furniture does not lie so fluently.

I had driven her through the village that afternoon with the letters—innocent ones, rationed ones, a circular about war bonds—held ostentatiously on my knee, and Mrs. Cobb at the post office had looked at me with her small ferret eyes and seen nothing. It should have felt like triumph. It felt like the first cold drop before rain.

* * *

The tide came in early that Thursday. It did this perhaps six times a winter, defying the almanac out of pure spite, and every time it did, Mr. Tregose at the harbour wall would shake his head and say, "She's got a mind of her own, that water," as though the sea were an elderly aunt who had stopped taking her advice.

I was in the garage, degreasing my hands with petrol and rag—the only church I truly believed in—when I heard the bell at the gate. Not the front bell, which rang bright and expectant, but the old iron bell by the sea gate, the one nobody used, which gave off a sound like a spoon dropped down a well.

I dried my hands. I went round the side of the house, past the walled garden where Greta Brandt's cabbages would one day grow, though of course I didn't know it yet, and out along the gravel to the gate.

A woman stood there.

I will try to describe her honestly, without the embellishments memory later added. She was perhaps twenty-three, twenty-four. Tall, but stooped, as though she had been carrying something heavy for a very long time and had forgotten how to stand without it. Her coat was good wool gone grey at the seams, and her boots—I noticed boots the way other women notice hats—her boots had been resoled twice and were now requesting a third mercy. She wore no hat. Her hair, dark blonde going brown at the roots of its exhaustion, had escaped its pins entirely, and she had the look of a person who has walked further today than her body had agreed to.

And in her arms, wrapped in a man's overcoat against the salt wind, she carried a typewriter.

Not a suitcase. Not a bundle of clothes or a loaf of bread or any of the sensible things a refugee carries. A typewriter, black and heavy as a paving stone, held against her chest the way other women hold infants.

"You are," she said, in English accented but precise, each word set down like a stone on a path, "the house of Lady Ashcombe."

It wasn't quite a question, and I didn't quite answer it. I looked behind her, down the long white road toward Marrow Quay, where the tide was already lapping at the shingle in long greedy fingers. The causeway would be sealed within the hour. Whatever she had walked through to get here, she had beaten the water by minutes.

"Where is your luggage?" I asked, which was a stupid thing to ask, and she looked at me with the particular patience people reserve for the stupid, and lifted the typewriter half an inch.

"This is my luggage," she said. "Also I have one change of clothes. Also a photograph." She shifted the machine to one arm and produced, from her coat pocket, a photograph the size of a playing card: a young man with a serious face and wire spectacles, standing proudly beside a bridge that existed only in drawings. Emil Brandt. I recognized him from nothing—I had never seen him—but I recognized him anyway, the way you recognize someone from a story you've been told in confidence.

"Your father," I said.

"My father." She put the photograph away with great care, as if returning it to a safe place inside herself rather than inside her coat. "He wrote to Lady Ashcombe. In October. He asked her—" and here her voice did something complicated, went flat on purpose, "—he asked her to remember a promise. The post since October has been—" she made a gesture, seaward, hopeless. "Slow. Or not coming. So I came myself. I walked from the station at Penlow. Eleven miles. There was a cart for part of it, and the driver asked questions, so I walked the rest."

"Questions about what?"

"About whether I was German." She said it plainly, without self-pity, which somehow made it worse. "I decided it was better not to have an accent for the last four miles. So I did not speak."

Eleven miles, four of them silent, carrying forty pounds of typewriter, to arrive at the gate of a woman whose letter she had never received, because her letter had gone up the chimney in the drawing room while I stood by pretending to be a rug.

"The tide will close the road within the half hour," I said, because facts are easier than feelings. "You've cut it fine."

"I watched the water the whole way," she said. "My father taught me tides. He built bridges. He says—" she corrected herself, and something in her face closed like a door, "—he *said*, a bridge is only a promise the water agrees to keep. Today the water is keeping a different promise." She looked past me, up at the grey bulk of Ashcombe House, its windows reflecting the low sky. "Is Lady Ashcombe at home?"

I should have said yes and shown her in. That was my duty, plain as a printed page. Instead I heard myself say, "Who shall I say is calling?"

And she understood, immediately, what I was asking—not the formality but the trap beneath it—and her chin came up, and for the first time I saw the family resemblance to the man in the photograph, the same stubborn architecture around the jaw.

"Greta Brandt," she said. "Of Berlin. My father is Emil Brandt, who designed the bridge your village crosses, and who is now, I am told, a prisoner in his own country's enemy's country, which is England, which is why I am here." She paused. "Or you may tell Lady Ashcombe I am Belgian. Belgians, I understand, are currently fashionable."

* * *

Lady Verity received her in the library, seated before the fire with a shawl over her knees, and I stood by the door in my capacity as furniture-that-lies.

Verity had been told nothing. I had made sure of it—had met the girl at the gate and brought her round through the garden door precisely so that Verity could not rehearse. I told myself this was fairness. I suspect it was cruelty, or curiosity, or some mixture of the two that I did not care to examine. I wanted to see what the great Lady Ashcombe would do when the promise walked through her door wearing resoled boots.

For a long moment Verity simply looked at her. The fire ticked. Somewhere upstairs a window rattled in its frame, the house clearing its throat.

"You have your father's chin," Verity said at last.

"And his typewriter," said Greta, setting it on the low table between them with a thud that made the fire irons jump. "And his stubbornness, according to my mother, God rest her, who had to live with both."

Something happened to Lady Verity Ashcombe's face then. I had served her three years and I had catalogued her expressions the way a lepidopterist catalogues moths: stern, sterner, ironic, thunderous, and the rare one, the dangerous one, amused. What crossed her face now was none of these. It was older than all of them. It was, I think, grief arriving years late at the correct address.

"He wrote to me," Verity said quietly. "In October."

"Yes," said Greta. "I know. He showed me the copy he kept. He kept copies of everything; it is a disease of engineers." She hesitated, and her voice, for the first time, lost its careful footing. "Did you receive it?"

The silence stretched. I stood by the door and thought, *tell her, tell her, don't make me the only one carrying this,* and Lady Verity Ashcombe, who had chained herself to railings for women she'd never meet, who had faced mobs and magistrates and her own brother's contempt without blinking, looked at the girl from Berlin and lied.

"It did not reach me," she said. "The mails are disrupted. You know how it is."

I did not move. But something in me moved—a hairline crack running through the masonry, quiet as ice forming.

Greta nodded slowly. She was not a fool; I could see her weighing it, the way her father must have weighed loads on spans. But she was twenty-three, exhausted, and at the end of an eleven-mile walk, and hope, when it is all you carry, is heavy enough that you stop inspecting it.

"I am here now," she said simply. "That is what matters. He said—if I could reach you—you would help."

And here Verity rose from her chair, slowly, one hand on the armrest, and did the most extraordinary thing I witnessed that winter, and I witnessed several extraordinary things that winter. She took both of Greta's hands in hers and said:

"Then you will stay. As my guest. Officially—" and here her eyes flicked to me, quick as a card sharp's, enrolling me without asking, "—officially you are a Belgian refugee whom I have taken in through the Refugee Committee, of which I am, conveniently, the district chairwoman. Your papers were delayed. Papers always are, these days. Miss Fairweather will vouch for you, won't you, Miss Fairweather? She knows everything that goes on in this house."

Two lies in one sentence, and the second one aimed at me like a thrown key.

"Yes, my lady," I said.

* * *

I showed Greta to the blue room that evening, the one over the kitchen with the view of the sea wall, and as I lit the lamp for her she stood in the middle of the floor turning slowly, taking inventory, like a surveyor assessing unfamiliar ground.

"Why did you lie for me?" she asked. "You did not hesitate."

"I hesitated," I said. "You just didn't notice."

"But you did it. In England, everyone tells me, one follows rules. Rules are very important here. I am learning this." She sat down on the edge of the bed, and for a moment the stubbornness drained out of her and she just looked tired, tired the way only the young can be tired, all the way down. "My father said Lady Ashcombe was the most honorable woman in England. He said it like a joke and not like a joke. Honorable people, I find, are complicated."

"Honorable people are exhausting," I said. "Get some sleep."

At the door she stopped me. "Miss Fairweather. The letter he sent in October. To Lady Ashcombe." Her eyes were steady and dark and missed nothing, and I understood suddenly where the engineer in the photograph had gone—into this girl, whole. "You know whether it came. Don't you."

The lamp flame stood perfectly still between us. Outside, far off, the tide finished its work and the road to the world went under.

"I know everything that goes on in this house," I said. "Didn't you hear? It's official."

She almost smiled. It was the first almost-smile of many, and each one cost her something, and I began, that night, against every instinct of self-preservation, to count them.

I went downstairs past the drawing room, where firelight still moved, and heard Verity's voice, low, speaking—to the fire, to the portrait of her father, to the ghost of a man with wire spectacles—and caught only five words: "*Well. She has your chin.*"

I stood in the dark hall with my hands smelling faintly of petrol and understood that I had passed through some door myself. Three days a liar, and already promoted to accomplice. And the terrible thing—the thing I turned over all night like a stone with something living under it—was that I did not feel dread.

I felt awake. For the first time in three years of reading other people's histories by lamplight, history had knocked at my own gate, dripping wet, carrying a typewriter.

And it had my name in its mouth.
