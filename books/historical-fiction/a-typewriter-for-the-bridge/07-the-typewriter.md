## 7. The Typewriter

The machine weighed as much as a sleeping child, and Greta Brandt had carried it across half of England.

She had carried it from the boarding house in Hamburg where her father had left it with instructions she was not to open until she reached "the lady at Ashcombe." She had carried it onto the ferry at Rotterdam, where a customs officer had lifted it, frowned at the German maker's plate on its underside, and let it pass only because she wept convincingly enough to make him uncomfortable. She had carried it through London while the city shouted itself hoarse about Belgium, and then by train to the coast, and then on foot along lanes that grew narrower and muddier the closer they came to the sea, until at last she had carried it up the drive of Ashcombe House on legs that shook like wet reeds.

And now Lady Ashcombe—who had received her with a face like a locked door and installed her in the household under the fiction of Belgian nationality—had said, in a voice that permitted no discussion: "You will not touch that machine again. Put it in the linen room. Do not look at me in that way, child. In this house, some things are kept shut for good reasons."

So Greta put it in the linen room.

She did not, however, promise not to think about it. And thinking about it, she discovered, was nearly as dangerous as opening it, because a mind that has been told *do not* becomes a house with one lit window.

* * *

The linen room sat above the kitchen, long and low-ceilinged, smelling of lavender and beeswax and the particular starchiness of pressed tablecloths. It had one window, small and deep-set, that looked out over the kitchen garden and, beyond the wall, the grey ribbon of the marsh running down to the closed causeway. Mrs. Tregowan the cook came up twice a week with linens; the rest of the time the room belonged to moths and to Greta, who had been given the job of sorting and mending because it required no conversation and no English beyond *yes*, *no*, and *thank you*—the last of which she pronounced so carefully that Mrs. Tregowan had patted her hand the first time and said, "Well, you're a polite little thing, whoever you are."

Greta sorted pillowcases. Greta darned napkins. Greta was, on the surface, a model of refugee industry.

But the typewriter sat in the corner under a dust sheet, and every afternoon when the light came slanting through the little window and struck the sheet's folded peak just so, Greta found herself standing before it the way one stands before a grave.

Her father had carried this machine through their whole life together. It had sat on the desk in his study in Kiel, beside the window with the view of the harbour cranes, and she had fallen asleep more than once as a child to the sound of it—the quick confident clatter of his fingers, the bell at the end of each line, the carriage sliding back with a sound like a contented sigh. He had designed bridges on it. He had written lectures on it. And between the bridge drawings and the lectures, year after year, he had written letters on it, and after each letter he would sit quite still for a moment, looking out at the water, and Greta, pretending to do her homework, had watched him do it and never once asked why.

Now she knew the name of the person those letters went to. Now she had seen the lady's face—old, proud, iron-grey, with eyes that had looked at Greta as though Greta were an invoice for goods ordered thirty years ago and never paid for.

On the fourth day of her employment as a Belgian, when the house had settled into its afternoon hush and Xanthe Fairweather had driven Lady Ashcombe into the village, Greta pulled back the dust sheet.

* * *

The case opened with a click she felt in her teeth. Inside, snug in its bed of green baize, the machine gleamed—black enamel, round keys with glass centres, the maker's name in gold. And beneath the platen, folded into the space where paper fed through, there was already a page.

Greta slid it out with both hands, the way one takes a bird from a snare.

It was a letter. In English, but typed, dated the twelfth of June, 1908.

*My dear Emil,—You argue like a Prussian and I am beginning to suspect you enjoy losing arguments to me, which is the most alarming confession a man of engineering can make. You write that the causeway will stand two hundred years. I write to tell you that if it does, it will be because you built it stubbornly, and that stubbornness, Emil, is the one virtue we share...*

Greta read it twice. Then three times. Her father's voice was in it—not the words, but the shape beneath them, the teasing gravity, the way he circled a feeling rather than naming it. Someone had known him. Someone had known him *well*.

She turned the page over. Nothing. She looked into the case. At the bottom, beneath the baize tray, her fingers found an edge—a false bottom, of course; her father had built furniture as a hobby and false bottoms as a philosophy—and inside lay a bundle of letters tied with the plain string her father used for everything, dozens of them, typed on this very machine or answered on another, spanning years. 1908. 1910. 1912. 1913. The last one dated March of this year, barely eight months old.

Greta sat down on a bale of pillowcases and began, with the guilty thoroughness of a lifelong reader, at the beginning.

* * *

They were not love letters. That was the first thing she understood, and the thing that undid her. They were argument letters—about the bridge, about suffrage, about whether beauty was a form of function or function a form of beauty, about Bismarck, about Dickens, about whether a woman could understand load-bearing stresses (Verity's position: better than most men; Emil's position: conceded, with a footnote). They were two minds fencing across the North Sea for six years, and the fencing was so obviously, so painfully, a form of devotion that Greta wanted to shake them both through the paper.

*I have been asked again why I do not marry,* Verity wrote in 1910. *I gave my usual answer—that my work is my marriage—and then I walked home in the rain and was furious with myself for lying to someone who would have known it was a lie.*

And Emil, 1911: *You ask what I would do if I needed sanctuary. I tell you what I told you on the cliff at Marrow Quay: that a promise made in peacetime is the only kind worth having, because it is made without fear. Keep your promise, Verity. I hope never to claim it. But keep it, the way one keeps a lamp lit in a window one hopes never to need.*

Greta read until the light failed. She read by matchlight, crouched behind the linen presses, her heart hammering whenever a floorboard spoke downstairs. She learned things about her father she had never known—that he had almost stayed in England in 1908; that he had sent money anonymously to the Marrow Quay school fund for years; that in the last letter, dated March 1914, his handwriting of the soul had changed:

*They speak of war in the papers as though it were weather—something that happens to other people, somewhere else. If it comes, Verity, remember that I am not Germany and Germany is not me, whatever the newspapers say. Remember the girl. Greta is nineteen now and reads everything and argues like you, God help her. If anything should happen to me—if the world goes mad and men are counted by their passports instead of their names—send for her. She will arrive carrying the machine. She will know what it means.*

Greta pressed the last page flat against her knees and sat in the dark, crying without noise, the way one learns to cry in houses that are not one's own.

He had known. He had known what was coming, and he had packed his whole heart into a black enamel box and put his daughter behind it like a shield, or perhaps like a key.

*Why here?* she thought, angrily, wiping her face on a pillowcase she would later have to re-iron. *Why send me to a woman who looks at me like a debt?*

And then, because she was her father's daughter and therefore incapable of leaving a question alone: *What does the machine mean?*

* * *

She was still holding the March letter when the door opened.

Not slowly, not cautiously—opened, full swing, with the confidence of someone who owned every door in the house, and Greta had exactly enough time to register that the dust sheet was folded on her lap, the letters were spread around her like evidence at a trial, and the matchbox lay open at her feet, before Xanthe Fairweather stood in the doorway with a candlestick in one hand and an expression of absolute astonishment rearranging itself, with visible effort, into something unreadable.

For a long moment neither of them moved. Below them, faintly, Mrs. Tregowan could be heard telling a saucepan exactly what she thought of it.

"You're supposed to be in the village," Greta said, in English, which was reckless, but panic had burned away her caution.

"I was supposed to be in the village," Xanthe agreed. Her eyes went around the room—letters, baize, matches, tears—and Greta watched her perform the same arithmetic any intelligent person would perform, and arrive at the sum: *enemy alien, hidden correspondence, stolen fire.* "Lady Ashcombe forgot her spectacles. I've come back for them. They're in the hall, actually, so I needn't have come up at all, and yet here I am."

"Yes," said Greta. "Here you are."

Xanthe set the candlestick down on the shelf with great deliberation, as though buying herself time by arranging objects. "Those are German letters."

"They are my father's letters."

"To Lady Ashcombe."

"Yes."

"Which she told you not to read."

"She told me not to touch the machine." Greta lifted her chin, which was a habit she'd inherited along with the arguing. "I have touched nothing but paper."

Something happened to Xanthe's mouth at that—a twitch, quickly suppressed, that might in a less guarded creature have become a smile. "That is the finest piece of legal reasoning I've heard since the war began, and I include the newspapers."

"They are *my father's*," Greta said again, and heard her own voice crack on it, and hated that, and pressed on anyway. "He is in Germany and I may never see him again and these are all of him I have, and your lady burned the last letter he sent her—I saw it go into the grate, I saw her burn him—and I will not apologize for reading six years of a man who is possibly about to be shot at by your countrymen for the crime of having designed a bridge that your lady walks across every day of her life."

The silence that followed had texture. Greta became aware of her own breathing, of the candle flame leaning in a draught, of the fact that she had just, in broken English, committed treason of the tongue against the household that was sheltering her under a false name.

Xanthe picked up the topmost letter—the March one, the last one—and read it. Greta watched her read it. Watched her lips move slightly on the sentence about passports and names. Watched her reach the line about the girl who would arrive carrying the machine, and stop, and look up.

"He wrote this in March," Xanthe said quietly.

"In March."

"And she burned his October letter asking the same thing."

"Yes."

Xanthe looked at the typewriter for a long time. When she spoke again, her voice had changed—lower, faster, the voice of someone thinking aloud who had spent too many years thinking silently. "There's a writing case in her room. Locked. She keeps the key on her watch chain and she sleeps with the chain under her pillow, and last Tuesday she took the case out and held it in her lap for an hour and didn't open it, and I know because I was mending the fire and she'd forgotten I was there. She forgets I'm there. Everyone does." A short, unamused laugh. "It's a useful quality. Being furniture."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you read letters," Xanthe said, "and so do I, and I have never once in my life met another person who admitted it." She straightened, briskly, all business now, and began gathering the scattered pages with quick precise hands, squaring their edges. "Mrs. Tregowan comes up Thursday mornings. The maids never come up at all—they say the room's haunted, which suits us. The machine goes back under the sheet. The letters—" she paused, holding the bundle, weighing it, and Greta saw her make a decision the way one steps off a ledge—"the letters go in the hatbox on the top shelf, behind the winter furs. Nobody moves winter furs in wartime; there's no occasion for them."

Greta stared at her. "You are hiding them *with* me."

"I am hiding them *near* me," Xanthe corrected, "which is different and safer for us both, and if you repeat that distinction to anyone I shall deny it with such conviction that you'll believe me yourself within a fortnight." She tucked the bundle into the hatbox, pushed the furs over it, and turned back. For a moment her composure slipped and something younger showed underneath—something eager and frightened and delighted all at once. "The eleventh of June, 1908. That's the earliest?"

"The earliest."

"Read me the part about the cliff at Marrow Quay," Xanthe said. "Before I lose my nerve entirely. Quickly, now—her ladyship will be wondering where her spectacles and I have got to, and I intend to be found innocent."

So Greta, kneeling in a linen room above a kitchen in a country at war with her own, held the candle close and read aloud the words her father had written twenty-six years old and in love and too proud to say so—and watched the chauffeur listen with her whole face, like a girl at a window watching a procession she'd been told would never come down her street.

Neither of them heard the front door below, or the tap of Lady Ashcombe's stick in the hall, or the pause—brief, thoughtful—as the old woman stopped beneath the linen-room stair, head tilted, listening to the murmur of a voice reading German-typed English in her own house.

Then the stick tapped on, toward the drawing room, and the two readers upstairs breathed again, not knowing they had been heard at all.
