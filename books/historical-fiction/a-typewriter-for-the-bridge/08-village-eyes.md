## 8. Village Eyes

There is a particular quality to being watched that I had never understood until that December. Before Greta Brandt came through our gate with her father's typewriter strapped to her back like a soldier's pack, I had been the sort of person nobody looked at twice—the chauffeur, the girl who cranked the motorcar and stood at attention while the gentry argued about votes inside warm rooms. Invisibility had been my profession, my hobby, and my comfort. Now every eye in Marrow Quay seemed to have found its focus, and the focus was us.

It began, as these things do, with Mrs. Tregowan's bakery.

I had gone in for the household bread—two loaves now instead of one, because Verity had declared without explanation that we should be feeding an extra mouth, and Mrs. Tregowan counted loaves the way a miser counts coins. She was already weighing mine when she said, in the tone of a woman remarking on weather, "Your mistress has taken in a refugee, then."

"A Belgian lady," I said. The lie came out smooth as butter, which frightened me more than a stumble would have. "Fled when the Germans came through. Very distressing. She doesn't speak of it."

"Mm." Mrs. Tregowan turned the loaf over, inspecting its base as though Belgium might be written there. "Funny sort of Belgian. Tall. Fair. Stood in my shop Tuesday looking at the ginger nuts like she'd never seen such a thing, and when I asked did she want any, she said *danke* before she caught herself." She set the bread down and looked at me over her spectacles with the mild, terrible patience of a woman who has outlived three vicars and all their secrets. "*Danke*, Miss Fairweather. That's not Walloon, is it?"

"It's very close," I said. "The languages. Practically cousins."

"Practically cousins," she repeated, and wrote something down—I am not joking, she actually produced a pencil stub and made a small mark in the flour-dusted ledger—and I carried my two loaves out into the cold feeling as though I'd left a confession behind me.

By Friday, the whole village knew that Ashcombe House harbored a foreigner of uncertain provenance, and by Saturday they had improved upon it. In the post office queue I heard that our refugee was a Belgian countess; that she was a German spy parachuted onto the marshes (a feat requiring, one would think, equipment no one in Marrow Quay could name); that she was the illegitimate daughter of a Prussian archduke; and—in the version favored by young Alfie Curnow, who delivered fish and fiction in equal measure—that she had arrived carrying a machine that could send messages to Berlin.

That last one was too close to the truth for comfort. The typewriter sat in the linen cupboard on the second floor, wrapped in a pillowcase, and I climbed past it daily with the sensation of walking by a sleeping dog of unknown temper.

"You're looking peaky," said Mrs. Penhale—not Thomas's mother but his aunt, who ran the sweetshop and considered herself entitled by that office to monitor the health of everyone under forty. She pressed a barley sugar into my hand whether I wanted it or not. "And secretive. You used to chatter like a jackdaw, Xanthe Fairweather, and now you've got a look about you. A *keeping* look."

"A keeping look," I said.

"Like a hen that's found something good and doesn't mean to share." She patted my arm. "Well. Whatever it is, keep it kept. That's my advice. But eat something."

I confess I nearly cried into the barley sugar. Kindness, when one is carrying a secret, lands harder than suspicion.

* * *

Thomas Penhale noticed on the following Tuesday, though he had the grace not to say so until Thursday.

He was the village schoolmaster, thirty-one years old, with ink-stained cuffs and a way of standing slightly outside every conversation as though taking notes for some future examination of humanity. He had been kind to me since my first week in Marrow Quay three years before—he'd shown me which lane flooded, which publican watered his beer, and how to tell the tide tables apart from the prayer roster in the church porch—but kindness, in Thomas's case, was simply the shape his manners took, and I had long ago stopped reading anything into it. Or rather, I had read everything into it once, early on, and then carefully taught myself to stop, because a chauffeur who reads things into a schoolmaster's manners is a chauffeur asking for trouble.

I had taken to walking to Ashcombe House from the village by the cliff path rather than the high road—it avoided passing the inn, where strangers sometimes lingered—and on this particular afternoon the path was glazed with frost and Thomas was coming the other way with a satchel of exercise books and the expression of a man rehearsing something.

"Miss Fairweather," he said.

"Mr. Penhale."

We performed this little ceremony, and then, instead of stepping past each other as the path demanded, he fell into step beside me, which the path did not demand at all.

"I hear," he said, eyes on the sea, "that Lady Ashcombe has taken in a refugee."

"The village hears a great deal."

"The village hears nonsense, mostly. What I hear is that Mrs. Tregowan has started a ledger." He said it lightly, but I felt the ground tilt. "She showed it to my aunt. Entries on the household at Ashcombe. Loaves, lamp oil, visitors. She calls it 'for the Relief Committee,' but the Relief Committee hasn't met since October, and half the entries are about a young woman's accent."

My heart was doing something undignified against my ribs. "People are idle," I managed. "Winter idleness."

"Winter idleness," he agreed. "Dangerous thing. Idle minds go looking for work, and if none is provided, they make it." He paused at the stile where the cliff path dropped toward the house, and set his satchel down, and looked at me directly for the first time. His eyes were grey and unhurried. "You've been avoiding the high road for eleven days. You take the bread order yourself now instead of sending the kitchen girl. You went to the post office at seven in the morning last Wednesday, which even I don't do, and I *like* the post office. And yesterday, when Constable Bate asked you at the forge whether your mistress's guest was Belgian or Flemish, you said yes."

I opened my mouth. Closed it. The sea filled the silence, doing what the sea does.

"I'm not asking," Thomas said. "I want that understood. I'm not asking what it is, because I've been a schoolmaster long enough to know that the questions you can't answer are usually the ones with weight in them, and weight belongs to the person carrying it, not to the people watching." He picked up his satchel again. "But if anyone asks *me*—the schoolmaster, who sees you at church and at the library and once helped you chase the Ashcombe dog through the graveyard—if anyone asks me whether there's anything strange about the Ashcombe household, I shall say that Miss Fairweather is the most punctual, most respectable, most tiresomely law-abiding young woman in the parish, and that the only mystery about her is why she wastes her evenings on improving literature when the circulating library gets three novels a month and nobody else borrows them."

"You—" I said. Something in my chest had come loose and was swinging. "You know about the novels."

"Someone returns them with sand between the pages," he said. "Sand, Miss Fairweather. From the cliff path. There are perhaps four people in this village who walk the cliff path, and only one of them reads Mrs. Humphry Ward." He tipped his cap, entirely composed, and went on down toward the schoolhouse, calling back over his shoulder: "Mind the ice by the stile. It's caught two of my pupils already this week and I haven't the bandages."

I stood there in the cold with my heart going like a threshing machine, understanding two things at once and unable to decide which alarmed me more: that Thomas Penhale had been covering for me without ever being asked, or that he had apparently been noticing me—carefully, patiently, with sand between the pages—for years.

* * *

The telegram came on the shortest day's eve, and it came, of course, to the worst possible hands.

I had driven Verity into the village for the Red Cross committee—her last public engagement before the winter truly shut us in—and while she held court in the vestry over the question of bandage wool, I waited with the motorcar outside the post office, which doubled as the telegraph office, which meant that Mr. Pascoe the postmaster read everything first and formed opinions second.

He came out to me personally. That was the first bad sign. Mr. Pascoe did not walk; he presided.

"For Lady Ashcombe," he said, holding the yellow envelope just beyond my reach with the delicacy of a man offering a live eel. "Marked urgent. Home Office, London." He watched my face with the frank appetite of his profession. "Serious business, the Home Office. My cousin's boy is in the postal service in Falmouth, and he says—"

"I'll see she gets it," I said, and took it, and Mr. Pascoe's opinion of me visibly rose several grades, because a girl who intercepts telegrams is a girl worth knowing.

I did not open it. I want that recorded somewhere, in whatever ledger is kept against me: I sat in the cold motorcar with the telegram in my glove and I did not steam it, did not slit it, did not even turn it over, because the seal was official and official seals were suddenly, terribly real to me in a way that private letters had never been. I simply held it and thought about Greta in the kitchen garden, learning the names of things—*kale*, she had repeated that morning, delighted, *kale*—and about the word *enemy alien*, which had begun appearing in the newspapers like a stain spreading through cloth.

When Verity emerged from the vestry, triumphant over the bandage wool, I handed the envelope up to her in silence.

She read it standing beside the car, hatpins flashing in the grey light. I watched her face do very little, which with Verity was the loudest thing it could do. Then she folded the telegram along its creases, once, twice, precisely, and put it in her muff.

"Well?" I said. I had never asked her *well?* before. The winter had made us both careless.

"Hale," she said. "An Inspector Hale of the Home Office. Aliens and registration." She looked out across the harbor, where the causeway bridge stood dark and dripping at low tide, its iron bones naked without the water to dress them. "He will arrive on the mail boat tomorrow, and he will lodge at the inn, and he will stay until spring. Until the bridge opens." Her mouth curved, but it was the old campaign smile, the one she wore before losing a vote. "They are sending a man to overwinter in a village of four hundred souls to find enemies among us, Xanthe. One imagines he will be very bored."

"One imagines," I said, "he will be very thorough."

"Yes." She settled into the seat and pulled the rug over her knees. "Those are generally the same men." Then, after a mile of frozen lanes, without turning her head: "Whatever you are keeping, girl—and do not insult us both by denying that you are keeping something—you had best decide soon whether it can survive being found. Because men like Hale always find. It is the finding they love. The rest is paperwork."

I drove on through the dusk with the headlamps making twin tunnels in the fog, and behind us the village lights came out one by one, warm and gold and full of eyes, and ahead of us the bridge waited for the tide to close it, and I understood that we were now a household under glass—warm inside, perhaps, but visible from every side, and spring was a very long way off.

That night I checked the linen cupboard twice, and moved the typewriter beneath the floorboard of the box room where the Christmas decorations lived, and lay awake listening to the wind come up the channel, thinking: *tomorrow*. Tomorrow the boat. Tomorrow the inspector. Tomorrow the beginning of being seen by exactly the wrong pair of eyes.

And underneath the fear, treacherously, warmly, unhelpfully: *sand between the pages.* Grey eyes. *Tiresomely law-abiding.*

I was going to have to become considerably more interesting than that, and considerably less, all at once.
