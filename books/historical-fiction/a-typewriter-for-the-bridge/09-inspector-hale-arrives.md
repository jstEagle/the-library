## 9. Inspector Hale Arrives

The coach from Ashford took four hours to cover what the timetable promised in two, and Inspector Ambrose Hale used every minute of it productively.

He used the first hour cataloguing his fellow passengers. A fish merchant with ink-stained cuffs who claimed to be a fish merchant and probably was, but whose hands were too clean for the trade — a man who sold fish, perhaps, rather than caught them. Two soldiers on leave, one of whom talked too much about troop movements for a man with a public voice, and whom Hale noted down by regiment and approximate age in a small leather book that had already outlived three smaller leather books. An elderly woman who knitted throughout and said nothing at all, which he found restful, and whom he therefore suspected of nothing, which was itself a kind of intelligence.

In the second hour he read his file. It was a good file. He had built it himself, page by page, over eleven years of service, and it smelled of his own particular brand of India ink and ambition. *Marrow Quay: tidal causeway; population 312 (winter est.); one inn, one church, one school; seasonal isolation Oct–Mar.* At the bottom, in his own hand: *Isolation breeds privacy. Privacy breeds secrets. Secrets are the raw material of this work.*

He had written that sentence in 1911 and had never yet found cause to strike it through.

* * *

The Grey Heron Inn announced itself with a sign so weathered that the heron had become abstract — a grey suggestion of a bird, more weather than wildlife. The landlady, Mrs. Tregowan, was a broad woman with the forearms of someone who had been fighting dough for forty years and winning.

"Inspector Hale," he said. "Home Office. I'm booked until spring."

"Are you," said Mrs. Tregowan, in a tone that made clear this was not a question she had any intention of pursuing. She showed him a room overlooking the marsh, small and cold, with a bed that had been made with military precision or genuine indifference — he could not immediately tell which, and filed the ambiguity away under *landlady, assess further.*

"Supper's at seven," she said. "We don't do supper after seven. We don't do much of anything after seven."

"I keep early hours myself."

"Mm," said Mrs. Tregowan, and went downstairs with the air of a woman closing a ledger.

Hale unpacked with method. Razors left. Brushes right. Files center drawer, locked. He hung his two spare shirts with exactly an inch between them, because an inch between shirts was the difference between a man and a sloven, and slovenliness, in his experience, was where carelessness began, and carelessness was where other people's secrets lived.

Then he sat at the window and looked out at the marsh, silvering under the first hard frost of the season, and felt the familiar thing arrive — the thing he did not have a name for and would not have wanted one. It came whenever he was newly billeted somewhere. The room was clean, the work was laid out, and he was entirely, comprehensively alone.

He dealt with it as he always did. He opened the leather book and began to write.

*Day one. Arrival 4:20 p.m. Landlady: Mrs. E. Tregowan, approx. 55, speaks little, observes much — will bear watching. Room satisfactory. Weather deteriorating.*

Underneath, after some thought:

*Marrow Quay is the sort of place that forgets the war exists. My job is to remind it.*

* * *

Supper was mutton, potatoes, and a silence that broke apart politely when he entered and reassembled behind him. There were five men at the long table: two farmers, the ferryman, a young vicar's curate with soft hands, and a fisherman who ate with the grim efficiency of a man refueling machinery.

"Inspector," said the ferryman, who had introduced himself earlier as Cobb, "come to catch spies, then?"

"Come to make sure there aren't any," said Hale pleasantly.

"And if there aren't?"

"Then I'll have had a quiet winter and you'll have had my custom. Everyone profits."

This was, Hale had found over many years, the correct opening move in any village: charm deployed flatly enough to be disclaimed. The farmers relaxed by degrees. The curate asked him whether he'd seen action, and seemed almost disappointed to learn that Hale's war so far consisted of filing cabinets, though he rallied admirably and said that the pen, etc., etc.

It was Cobb, refilling his own tea, who provided the evening's first entry.

"You'll want to know about the Ashcombe household," Cobb said. "Big house up past the church. Lady Verity Ashcombe — famous suffragist, before all this. Half-paralyzed now since November, poor soul. She keeps servants, and a driver, Miss Fairweather — London girl, sharp as tacks — and since the autumn they've had a refugee."

"A refugee." Hale's pen was in his pocket, but his memory was better than his pen.

"Belgian," said Cobb. "Came over when the Germans went through Belgium. Very sad. Quiet girl. Works in the kitchen garden."

"Quiet," said the older farmer, around his pipe, "and no wonder, poor thing, hearing what she's heard."

"What's her name?" asked Hale.

There was a pause of perhaps half a second. Hale had spent eleven years learning to hear half-seconds; they rang like bells to him.

"Greta," said Cobb. "Greta... something foreign. Can't recall."

"Flemish names take getting used to," offered the curate kindly.

"They do," agreed Hale, and smiled, and let it go — outwardly. Inwardly he wrote: *Refugee, 'Greta,' arrived autumn 1914, no details volunteered, name incomplete, hesitation before surname. Note: villagers defend without being asked. Either kindness or concealment. Often both.*

After supper he climbed to his room, took out the leather book, and wrote it all down properly, adding: *Belgian refugees are registered upon arrival at distribution centers. Cross-check against Kent registry lists when post permits. If she is on the lists, well and good. If she is not—*

He stopped. Looked out at the dark marsh, where the tide was coming in over the causeway road with the soft, absolute authority of water doing what water does.

*If she is not,* he finished, *then someone at Ashcombe House has invented a Belgian. And people only invent Belgians when the truth has a German accent.*

He did not enjoy the sentence. He was not a cruel man — he wished to be quite clear with himself on that point, and revisited the point nightly, the way other men revisited their accounts. He believed in order, in the law, in the plain necessity of the moment: there was a war on, and wars required that things be checked. That a lonely girl working in a kitchen garden might be crushed as a consequence of being checked was regrettable, and he regretted it in advance, thoroughly, the way one regrets weather.

He slept soundly. He always slept soundly. Loneliness, like everything else, responded well to routine.

* * *

His days established themselves within a week, because Hale built routines the way spiders build webs — silently, geometrically, everywhere.

Mornings: the register. Marrow Quay contained, by his count, three persons of enemy-alien origin (two elderly brothers named Kessel, naturalized in 1889, who grew apples and had done nothing more suspicious than graft them for thirty years), one deserter from the Royal Fusiliers currently hiding in a hay barn and doing it badly (Hale knew about the hay barn by day three and decided, for reasons he examined and approved of, to let the boy shiver another week while he confirmed the paperwork), and an unknown quantity of loose talk, which he collected in the manner of a man collecting rainwater — a little here, a little there, mostly useful for washing things in.

He interviewed the Kessels on Thursday. They received him in a parlor smelling of apple pulp, pressed him cider he declined, and answered every question with such patient, transparent honesty that he left feeling as though he had been gently scolded. *Kessel, Hermann & Otto. Nothing. Genuinely, provably nothing. Almost insulting,* he wrote, and then, in fairness, added: *Which is itself data. Most men hide something. These men appear to have been too busy with apples.*

He attended church on Sunday, sat in the back row, sang nothing, and watched the congregation instead of God. He learned: that the schoolmaster, a Mr. Penhale, had a way of looking at the Fairweather woman during the hymns that would have been invisible to anyone not professionally trained to notice looking; that the Fairweather woman herself sat very straight and turned the pages of her hymnal slightly early, as one does when one is not reading the words; and that a young woman in a plain coat and headscarf — sitting with the Ashcombe servants' families, slightly apart — mouthed the responses with a faint, telltale precision of someone translating on the fly.

Flemish, he thought, watching her. Or something adjacent to Flemish.

After the service he made a point of being introduced. It cost him nothing to be charming; charm was simply politeness with its sleeves rolled up.

"Miss—?" he said, extending his hand.

"Greta," she said. "Greta Verhoeven." And then, with a small, careful smile that he would think about later, more than he wanted to: "You are the inspector? We heard you had come. In Belgium we had inspectors also. Before."

Her English was excellent. Slightly accented — very slightly. The accent was northern European, flat-voweled, and could plausibly be Flemish. Could plausibly be several things. The word *Verhoeven* was delivered with perfect confidence, the confidence of a name rehearsed.

"How do you find England, Miss Verhoeven?"

"Cold," she said, "and kind. Mostly cold." A beat. "Mostly kind."

She shook his hand with a grip that was stronger than her coat suggested, met his eyes directly — one second, no more, exactly the duration of an honest person, or of a liar who had practiced — and moved off toward the Fairweather woman and the schoolmaster, and the three of them stood together in the churchyard frost like a painting called *Nothing to See Here*, and Hale stood looking at the painting, and wrote nothing down because he didn't need to.

That evening he wrote to the county registry office requesting the Belgian arrival lists for Kent, August through October 1914, and walked down to post it, and stood a while at the edge of the causeway road, where the tide was sealing the village off for the night, black water sliding smooth as poured ink across the roadbed.

*Isolation breeds privacy,* he thought. *Privacy breeds secrets.*

Somewhere up past the church, behind the big house's lit windows, somebody was keeping a secret warm by the fire. He could feel it the way sailors feel weather coming. Eleven years of filing cabinets had given him that, if nothing else.

* * *

On the twelfth morning, Mrs. Tregowan set his breakfast down and lingered, which she never did.

"There's a parcel come for you," she said. "Post's slow, but it got through. From your office, by the look of it."

"Thank you, Mrs. Tregowan."

She didn't go. She wiped her hands on her apron, once, twice. "That girl up at Ashcombe's," she said, to the middle distance. "Greta. She comes down Tuesdays for the market, does the Ashcombes' buying. Good manners. Pays exact. Carries old Mrs. Pentreath's basket home for her, every week, both ways up the hill."

"That's very civic of her," said Hale.

"It is," agreed Mrs. Tregowan. "Village thinks a lot of her." She gathered up his empty teacup with terrible gentleness. "Just so you know how the land lies, Inspector, before you start digging in it."

And she went out, leaving Hale alone with his kipper and his mail, admiring her — genuinely, professionally admiring her — and understanding that the first shot of the winter had just been fired, and it had been fired by the landlady, and it had been a warning shot, fired across his bow, politely.

He opened the office envelope. Inside was the Kent registry extract he'd requested, and he ran his finger down the column of names — Adriaenssens, Claes, De Vos, Maes, Peeters, Willems — thirty-one Belgian women settled in the county, and not one of them named Verhoeven, and not one of them recorded anywhere near Marrow Quay.

He read the list twice. Then he closed it, squared its edges against the table, and looked out the window at the frost-bound garden, feeling the particular cold satisfaction of a man who has confirmed what he already knew, mixed — he was honest enough with himself to admit it — with something less comfortable underneath, something that had no business in an inspector's chest and that he firmly escorted from the premises.

*No record. No papers. No Belgian.*

He took out the leather book, uncapped his pen, and wrote, in his smallest, neatest hand:

*The Ashcombe 'refugee' has no papers because she was never issued any. Next: establish who knows, who doesn't, and who is lying. Begin with the household. Begin with the driver — Miss Fairweather. Servants see everything and repeat selectively; drivers see even more and are trained to be discreet. Discretion is merely secrecy with a wage attached.*

He blotted the page, and was reaching to close the book when a knock came at the door — Mrs. Tregowan again, holding out a folded note.

"This just come up from the school," she said. "Mr. Penhale sent his boy over special. Says — let me see—" she squinted at it, unasked and unashamed — "says he'd be pleased to call upon you, Inspector, seeing as the school's the natural center of the parish and he can save you shoe leather. Thursday suit?"

Hale considered the note. The schoolmaster. The man who looked at Miss Fairweather during hymns. The man now volunteering, unprompted, to walk straight into the inspector's web and sit down in the middle of it with his hat on his knee.

*Either a fool,* Hale wrote mentally, *or a chess player. The village has produced neither so far. I look forward to finding out which.*

"Thursday suits admirably," he said.

He spent the rest of the morning sharpening six pencils, because a man should meet a possible chess player with sharp pencils, and because — though he would never have written it down — the house felt quieter than usual, and sharpened pencils made a satisfying sound, and satisfied sounds were one of the few comforts winter had left him.
