## 10. Rationing the Truth

The kitchen garden at Ashcombe House had gone to sleep believing itself a gentleman, and I was the one who had to wake it.

That is not quite fair. It had simply been left to Mrs. Tregowan's intermittent attentions, and Mrs. Tregowan believed — as a matter of theology, I think — that vegetables ought to be persuaded rather than commanded. By the time the frost came, the beds were a confusion of collapsed bean-poles, bolted lettuce gone bitter as regret, and one pumpkin of such enormous and unexplained size that the village children had begun leaving offerings near it. I spent my first fortnight among the raised beds learning which of Lady Ashcombe's instructions were laws and which were merely weather. "Turn the compost" was a law. "Mind you don't catch chill" was weather, delivered daily, in the same tone, whether or not there was any chill to catch.

I had told them in Hamburg that England would be cold. Nobody had told me it would be damp in a way that got inside words themselves. The Latin on the seed packets softened; the names of tools went round in my mouth like pebbles: *dibber*. *Hoe*. *Cloche*, which I refused for two full days to believe was French and not an Englishwoman's idea of a joke.

My hands changed first. They cracked along the knuckles and darkened at the nails, and when I looked at them of a morning over the pump handle I thought: these are no longer a translator's hands, or a daughter's hands. These are hands that belong to soil. There was something in that which frightened me and something which, if I am honest, steadied me more than any document ever had. In Berlin my usefulness had been a matter of argument — what use was a girl who read four languages and could not yet be trusted to carry a message alone? Here, nobody argued about my use. Here I pulled up a carrot and the carrot existed, blunt and orange and undeniable, because I had pulled it.

The slurs took longer to learn than the tool names, because they were never said to my face.

I heard them the way one hears weather through a wall. The fish-boy, delivering herring to the back door, said something to his companion about "the refugee" with a twist of the mouth that did not require translation. Two women at the lych-gate watched me carry kale to the kitchen and one of them said, clearly enough, "Belgian, is she? Funny sort of Belgian." The other laughed in a way that meant she had understood perfectly well and was choosing, for reasons of her own, to let the lie stand. That laugh confused me more than hostility would have. It took me weeks to understand that in Marrow Quay, mercy and suspicion often wore the same shawl.

Once, directly, a boy — maybe twelve, home from school with mud on his knees and war on his mind — stood in the lane and shouted at me a word he had learned from his father's newspaper. I will not write it here. My German ears caught it instantly; that was the cruelty of it, that they could not pretend not to understand. He ran off before I could decide what face to make.

Mrs. Tregowan, who had seen it from the scullery window, came out wiping her hands. She did not ask if I was all right, which was wise, because the answer would have required a vocabulary neither of us possessed. Instead she said: "He'll be ashamed of that in ten years. Most likely in ten minutes." Then she handed me a dishcloth and sent me in to dry the good china, as though being insulted were a kind of rain and the proper response was simply to come indoors.

It was Xanthe who taught me that the village had rhythms, like tide tables, and that if you learned them you could read almost anything.

* * *

She began coming down to the garden in the late afternoons, after the motorcar was garaged and Lady Ashcombe's correspondence answered. She claimed she came to steal peas. There were no peas after October; she continued to claim it. She would lean on the fence with her hands in her coat pockets and her collar up, watching me work with the particular attention of someone trying not to look interested.

"You hold that fork wrong," she said, the second or third time.

"It is a fork," I said. "One holds it."

"Not in England. In England you hold it like this, so you don't take half the bed with the carrot." She came over the fence — actually over, in one motion, skirts and all, which told me things about her childhood — and put her hands over mine and adjusted my grip, and I felt the warmth of another person's fingers and had to look very hard at the carrot for a moment.

From then on we traded, though neither of us called it teaching. I gave her German the way you slip a child extra cake — casually, sideways, pretending it was nothing.

"*Kohlrabi,*" I'd say, holding up a seed packet. "You have no word. You stole ours."

"We borrowed it," Xanthe said. "There's a difference. Borrowing implies giving it back."

"You will not give it back."

"Not a chance." And she'd say it — *kohlrabi* — flattening the vowels into something English and cheerful, and I would wince theatrically, and she would do it again worse on purpose, until we were both laughing over the cold frames like fools.

In return she gave me Marrow Quay. She taught me its grammar: that Mrs. Penberthy's front room curtain twitching at nine meant gossip, but at eleven meant grief, and one must know which before calling. That Mr. Hosking at the inn watered his beer only in hard winters and could be forgiven everything else. That the church bell rang eight times for services and once, flat and apologetic, when the verger overslept. That when Lady Verity said "we shall see," it meant yes, and when she said "interesting," it meant the conversation was over.

"And this," Xanthe said one grey afternoon, nodding toward the lane where the postman walked his rounds at exactly half past two regardless of weather, war, or the apparent collapse of civilization, "this is the most important rhythm of all. The post. Everything in this village happens either before the post or after it. You plan your whole day around what might be in it."

"What comes now?" I asked. "With the war?"

"Less and less. A newspaper three days old. Circulars about rationing. Letters from mothers to sons, and the answers, when the answers come." She said it lightly, but her eyes went somewhere else for a moment, down some corridor I couldn't follow. Then she shook herself. "*Zwieback*," she said suddenly, pointing at the rusk tin Mrs. Tregowan kept for her teeth. "Go on. Say it."

"*Tsvee-back.*"

"Terrible."

"You are terrible. Your tongue refuses to bend."

"My tongue," Xanthe said, "is famously stubborn. Ask anyone. Ask Lady Ashcombe. Ask the bridge committee." And she smiled, and I noticed — not for the first time, but for the first time letting myself notice — that when she smiled fully, the whole careful structure of her composure shifted, like a house settling, and something younger looked out.

We traded words the way the village traded eggs: small, regular, sustaining. I taught her *Sehnsucht*, and she made me try to explain it, and I failed, and she said, "No, go on, I nearly had it," and I tried again and failed better. She taught me that the Cornish for a narrow lane was a *dray*, and that the villagers would forgive me anything except mispronouncing place-names, which they considered a form of blasphemy. She taught me to say *Porthmarran* correctly, rolling it low in the throat, and the next Sunday old Mr. Curnow at the church door heard me say it and looked at me for a long moment and then nodded once, gravely, as if a gate had opened somewhere that he personally maintained.

Small things. Pebbles. But I was building something with them, though I didn't yet know what the shape would be.

* * *

The jumble sale was announced from the pulpit with the solemnity of a naval mobilization, and I understood immediately that it was both.

It was for the Red Cross, held in the church hall, and by the time Xanthe and I arrived — sent together, which I later suspected was Lady Verity's doing, a small test or a small kindness, possibly both — the hall smelled of wet wool, weak tea, and charity. Trestle tables groaned under the donations of the entire parish: hatpins, hymnals, a taxidermied gull of ferocious expression, forty-one years of *Girl's Own Paper*, a wedding dress described by its donor as "barely worn," which said something about somebody's marriage that the whole village pretended not to hear.

Xanthe moved through the crowd like a native. She greeted everyone — the schoolmaster Mr. Penhale, whom she addressed with a stiffness I found interesting; Mrs. Penberthy, whose curtain I now knew how to read; the vicar, who pressed a tin of toffees on us "for the Belgian lady" with such booming goodwill that several heads turned to see the Belgian lady, and I had to perform gratitude in a language I was supposedly still learning.

"Danke schön," I murmured, and caught myself one heartbeat too late.

Nothing happened. The vicar beamed. But a woman near the haberdashery table — Mrs. Pascoe, Xanthe whispered afterward, who missed nothing and repeated everything — turned her head slowly, the way a weathervane turns, and settled.

I felt the mistake travel out through the hall like a stone dropped in a pond, growing smaller as it spread, until perhaps it vanished entirely. Perhaps. I kept my eyes on the taxidermied gull and breathed.

"Books," Xanthe said firmly, steering me by the elbow toward the far table. "You're looking for books. Refugees love books. Everyone knows this." Her voice was light; her grip was not.

At the book table I found, of all things, a German grammar — some dead clergyman's attempt to arm himself against Goethe — priced at tuppence. I picked it up with genuine delight, and was turning it over when a shadow fell across the table and a voice said, pleasant as pie:

"Now that's a curious thing to find in Cornwall."

Inspector Hale. I knew him by sight already, as the whole village knew him by sight — the man at the inn who asked questions the way other men asked the time, politely, frequently, of everyone. Up close he had pale, patient eyes and the habit of standing slightly too near, as though intimacy were an investigative technique.

"Is it?" Xanthe said, stepping between us with the smoothness of long practice. "Half this parish fought the Kaiser's grammar school, Inspector. Half the hymn books in that box are printed in Germany. Bach wrote in German too — shall we burn the organ?"

"I wasn't suggesting burning anything, Miss—" he consulted some inner card-file, "—Fairweather. Only observing. Curious things, books. They tell you who their owners are." His gaze moved past Xanthe's shoulder to me, rested there without urgency, and moved on. "You've a fine garden at Ashcombe House this year, I hear. Remarkable, for a stranger's first season."

"She's Belgian," said Mrs. Pascoe, arriving like reinforcements, or perhaps like artillery. "Refugee. Terrible business in Belgium."

"So I'm told," Hale said, and smiled, and the smile was the worst thing about him, because it contained nothing cruel and nothing kind — only filing. "Well. Good luck with your grammar, miss." He touched his hat brim to both of us and drifted away toward the taxidermied gull, where he began, I swear it, taking notes.

Xanthe bought the grammar for me with two pennies from her own pocket, and we carried it out into the churchyard like contraband, past the tilted stones, into the raw wind off the sea.

"He knows," I said, when the hall door had shut behind us. "Or he is beginning to."

"He suspects. Suspecting is his profession. Proving is harder, especially here." She stopped by the lych-gate and faced me, wind pulling at her scarf. "Listen to me. From tomorrow, you speak English in public. Bad English. Wonderful, terrible, broken English. Mispronounce *Porthmarran*. Call a dibber a digger. If Mrs. Pascoe asks you a question, smile and say 'please?' until she gives up. Can you act badly on purpose? It's harder than acting well."

"I translated for my father's firm for three years," I said. "I can degrade myself professionally."

Something crossed her face — guilt, I think, quick and unwelcome, at the joke that wasn't quite a joke — and she reached out and straightened my collar with two brisk tugs, the way one straightens a thing one is not allowed to fix properly.

"That's the spirit," she said. And then, quieter, almost to the wind rather than to me: "Nobody in that hall lifted a finger against you today, Greta. Did you notice? Mrs. Pascoe lied for you. To an inspector. She doesn't even like you."

"No," I agreed. "But she dislikes being wrong more. And she has decided — provisionally — that I am hers."

"Provisionally," Xanthe said, "is the strongest word in this village."

We walked home along the cliff path, our one purchase between us, the sea going grey-gold below. At the turn above the house we saw Lady Verity out on the terrace, wrapped in furs despite the hour, standing very straight at the balustrade and looking out at the closed causeway as if it were a horizon she intended to argue with.

"She's been out there every afternoon this week," Xanthe said. "Longer than usual. She stands there like she's waiting for something."

"Perhaps she is waiting for spring."

"Lady Ashcombe," Xanthe said, "does not wait for things. Things wait for her." She said it fondly, but she frowned at the small fur-wrapped figure all the same, and did not stop frowning even when we came through the gate and Verity turned to us with her usual imperious warmth and demanded to know whether we had won anything worth having.

"A grammar," I said.

"German?" Her eyebrows rose, sharp and amused and — I thought, though I could not have said why — a little sad. "Well. One can never have too many languages." And she turned back to the sea, and stood there in the failing light, and did not come in to supper until the lamps were lit, and when she did, her left hand, resting on the balustrade a moment too long, seemed slow to let go.
