## 11. Verity's Collapse

The Red Cross meeting was held in the schoolroom at Marrow Quay, which meant that by half past two the desks had been dragged into rows facing the blackboard, and by quarter to three Lady Verity Ashcombe had rearranged them.

"Rows imply an audience," she said, planting one desk at a stubborn diagonal. "A semicircle implies participation. We are not here to be looked at, Xanthe. We are here to be *useful*."

"Yes, my lady." I had learned years ago that agreement was cheaper than argument and lasted longer.

She was in fine form that afternoon—finer than she'd been in weeks, though I hadn't known to be alarmed by it. There is a kind of vigor in old women that looks like health and is actually something more like a storm front: pressure building where pressure ought not to build. Verity had spent the morning dictating letters with unusual ferocity, correcting my typing of words I had typed correctly, and eating almost nothing, which for her was normal, but also complaining of nothing, which for her was not.

"You've sharpened all six pencils," she observed on the drive down, approvingly. "Good. A meeting without sharp pencils is merely gossip with minutes."

The schoolroom smelled of chalk and wet wool. Forty-odd village women sat in Verity's semicircle, knitting or pretending to knit, while Mrs. Tregowan from the inn read out the subscription list in the voice she used for hymns. Bandages were counted. Wool was allocated. Someone reported that the vicar's wife had knitted a sock so enormous it could only fit a horse, and there was laughter, warm and easy, the laughter of people who have known each other too long to be embarrassed by anything.

I stood at the back by the coat pegs, as I always did. It was my favorite post in any room—the place from which you can see everyone and belong to no one. From there I watched Verity rise to speak, smoothing her skirt with both hands, and I thought, with the comfortable condescension of the young, *She loves this more than she loves anything. More than the cause itself, perhaps. The standing up part.*

She began well. She spoke about lint.

I want to be fair to her: most speakers would have made lint dull. Verity made lint a matter of national honor. She explained how to tear sheets properly, against the seam, so the threads pulled true; she demonstrated with a pillowcase Mrs. Tregowan sacrificed from under her arm; she described the boiling of bandages with such relish that two women near me took notes. Her voice was strong. Her color was high. When she said, "We are told this war will be over by Christmas, and I say to you: good. Let us work as if we have until Christmas and plan as if we have until doomsday," the room applauded, and someone's knitting needles clicked applause as well, entirely by accident.

It was somewhere after that—she had moved on to the question of a second collection box at the church gate—that she stopped.

Not faltered. Stopped. Mid-sentence, between one word and the next, like a clock with the pendulum lifted out. Her right hand, raised for emphasis, stayed raised. Her face did something I had never seen a face do: it slid, very slightly, on one side, as though the left half were trying to leave the room early.

"Lady Ashcombe?" said Mrs. Tregowan, kindly, assuming a lost thread.

Verity's mouth worked. What came out was not a word. It was a sound, flattened and wrong, a syllable with the bones taken out of it—and then her raised hand dropped, and then *she* dropped, folding sideways off the platform in a way no body should fold, and forty women screamed at once, which I mention only because it is remarkable how much a scream sounds like a siren when it comes from forty throats at once.

I don't remember crossing the room. I remember kneeling on the platform boards with her head in my lap, her hat askew, one earring winking at the ceiling. I remember her left hand gripping my wrist with shocking strength while the right lay like something borrowed. I remember thinking, absurdly, of the bridge—of how the causeway closes twice a day whether you are ready or not, and how the water doesn't care what you had planned.

"Don't move her," said a voice, and it was Thomas Penhale, the schoolmaster, already crouched beside me with his sleeves rolled, having come through the connecting door from his classroom at whatever speed a dignified man allows himself. His hands were steady. He loosened her collar, tilted her chin, watched her breathing with the attention he usually spent on long division.

"She's breathing," he said. "Loosen anything tight. Fetch pillows—her own, from home, later. Dr. Merrick is at Pengelly's delivering a baby, they've sent for him." Then, quietly, only to me: "Xanthe. Look at me. You're doing fine."

I was not aware I had been doing anything. That is the strange mercy of emergencies: your hands go on without you.

* * *

They carried her home on a door taken off its hinges, which struck me even then as a thing out of a ballad rather than real life, except that nothing about it felt like a ballad. Six men, Thomas directing, Mrs. Tregowan walking backward in front calling encouragement as though Verity might rally out of sheer irritation. I drove behind with Greta.

Greta had appeared at the schoolroom door halfway through the chaos—I still don't know who fetched her—and had done the useful, invisible things: gathered the scattered wool, calmed Mrs. Pengelly, walked beside the door with her hand resting on Verity's blanket the whole way up the hill as though her touch alone could keep the old woman tethered. Now, in the motor car, she said nothing at all. Her hands were folded in her lap like a woman at church, and her face was white in the way faces are white when they have understood something before everyone else has.

"What is it?" I asked.

"In German," she said slowly, "when a person cannot speak, we say their tongue lies heavy. *Die Zunge liegt schwer.*" She looked at me. "Heavy tongue, heavy heart. It is the same word, sometimes."

I didn't know what she meant. I would know by evening.

Dr. Merrick arrived at Ashcombe House an hour after we did, smelling of someone else's childbirth, and examined Verity in her bedroom for a long time with the door shut. When he came out he took off his spectacles, polished them, put them back on, and looked at us—me, Greta, Mrs. Tregowan, who had appointed herself nurse and refused all relief—as though deciding how much truth a household could carry.

"The left side is affected," he said. "Speech will return in some measure, though perhaps not all of it. Movement—" he turned his hand flat, a gesture like a scale settling "—we shall see. She is strong. Stubbornness counts for a great deal in recovery, and she has thirty years' surplus."

"And her mind?" Mrs. Tregowan asked.

"Her mind," said Merrick, "is exactly as formidable as ever. Which may be the hardest thing of all, for her. She will know precisely what has been taken." He gathered his bag. "No signing of documents. No excitement. No business whatsoever. If anyone brings her papers, I shall have them thrown in the fish pond."

He said it lightly. Everyone laughed the way people laugh when a doctor makes a joke, gratefully, because the alternative is not laughing.

I did not laugh. Because at the edge of my mind, quiet as a moth against a window, something was fluttering: *no signing of documents.* And beneath that, deeper: *the promise.*

* * *

That night the house felt wrong in the way a house feels wrong when its heartbeat has moved rooms. Verity's presence had always been distributed through every floor—the slam of her study drawer, her tread on the stairs, her voice calling corrections through closed doors. Now the house held its breath around her sickroom, and everywhere else was silence with furniture in it.

I sat in the servants' hall with the lamp turned low, ostensibly mending a glove, actually doing nothing at all. Greta came down in her shawl and sat across from me and did not pretend to sew.

"She promised him sanctuary," Greta said at last. "My father. Here. At this house. She wrote it—there are letters, in the typewriter case, you have seen them—"

"I've seen them."

"If she dies—" Greta stopped. Started again, more carefully, the way one walks on ice. "If she cannot speak, cannot sign, cannot *say*—then the promise exists only in paper that proves nothing, in a dead man's machine, and in two women's memories. Mine and yours." She leaned forward. "Xanthe. Do you understand? The promise has no author now. Only witnesses. And witnesses are worth nothing."

"That's not true," I said, because it was the sort of thing one says.

"It is exactly true. English law does not take the word of a German girl and a chauffeur against an empty page." She wasn't angry. That was the terrible part. She was simply stating the weather.

I should have comforted her. Instead I heard myself say, in a voice gone strange and thin, "She'll recover. Dr. Merrick said—stubbornness counts—"

"Stubbornness," said Greta gently, "does not un-stroke a brain. You know that. You knew it in the schoolroom before I did, I think. Your face has been knowing it since four o'clock."

I looked down at my mending. The glove had come apart entirely under my hands; I had unpicked it to ruin without noticing. I set it down.

"I read her letters," I said. It came out of me sideways, confession-shaped, though Greta already knew—had known since the typewriter, since the night we hid it together behind the linen press. Still, saying it aloud in the lamplight made it different, heavier, mine. "Every letter she carries, I read. Every letter she sends. For years. And I told myself it was nothing—a quirk, an indulgence, that history was happening to other people and I was only the reader. And now—" My voice did something embarrassing. I steadied it. "Now there may be no more letters. No author. And everything she meant to say, everything she *promised*, is sitting in that house unsigned, and I am the last person who knows the whole of it."

Greta reached across the table and took my ruined glove, and began, calmly, to mend it herself, her stitches small and even.

"Then we must become authors," she said.

I looked up. "What?"

"Later." Her eyes went past me, toward the dark window, toward the sea sound beyond it. "First we watch. First we learn exactly what she can still do. A mind like hers does not surrender; it finds new roads." She tied off the thread and handed the glove back, repaired. "And Xanthe—if there are witnesses enough, if we are careful, if we act while there is still light to act by—perhaps the promise need not die with her voice."

There was a noise above us then: a thump, a chair, Mrs. Tregowan's quick footsteps crossing the landing overhead. Both of us were on the stairs before either of us decided to be, taking the steps two at a time in our stockinged feet.

We found Verity sitting up in bed, gray-faced and furious, having knocked the water jug to the floor in an attempt to reach her bedside table. On the table lay the pen tray she had demanded and Mrs. Tregowan had refused to give her. Her left side would not obey her; her right hand was stretched out toward the pens, shaking, straining, an inch short.

Her mouth shaped words. What came out was a rough vowel, then another, effortful, mangled—but I had read her correspondence for six years, I knew her sentences the way I knew my own handwriting, and I swear to God I understood her. Understood the sentence she was fighting to build, the one her broken tongue kept collapsing under.

*Bring me the writing case. Bring me the writing case before I lose the rest.*
