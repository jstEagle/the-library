## 12. The Writing Case

The writing case sat on the shelf in Lady Ashcombe's study like a sleeping animal—black leather gone soft at the corners, brass fittings dulled to the color of old honey, and a lock that had defeated every casual attempt at curiosity for as long as Greta Brandt had been in the house.

Greta had been counting the days since Verity's stroke the way one counts stitches in knitting: not because she wanted to know the total, but because her hands needed something to do while her mind refused to sit still. Eleven days now. Eleven days of Mrs. Tregowan's broths, of Xanthe reading aloud at the bedside in her clear chauffeur's voice, of the house holding its breath around a woman who had never once held hers.

It was Xanthe who came to Greta on the twelfth morning, still wearing her coat, snow melting off the shoulders onto the flagstones of the back passage, and said without preamble: "She asked for it."

"Asked for what?"

"The writing case." Xanthe unwound her scarf with fingers that were not quite steady. "I was sitting with her this morning and she woke up properly for the first time since—the first time. And she looked at me and said, 'The case, girl. On the shelf.' Just like that. Half her mouth doesn't work anymore but she made herself understood well enough." Xanthe paused. "Then she said something else."

Greta waited. In the kitchen beyond, Mrs. Tregowan was attacking a turnip with the violence of a woman who had opinions about root vegetables and no other outlet for them.

"She said, 'Bring it to me when I'm stronger. Not before.'" Xanthe's jaw tightened. "And then she went back to sleep. But Greta—" She stopped, started again. "I've driven that woman every day for two years. I've read her letters over her shoulder so long she thinks I'm furniture. And I have never seen her look frightened. Not at magistrates, not at hecklers, not when they threw things at her in Truro. This morning she looked frightened."

* * *

They did not open it that day. They did not open it the next day either, though both of them found reasons to walk past the shelf and look at it, the way villagers look at a house where someone has died. It was on the third evening—Verity sleeping soundly under Mrs. Tregowan's watch, the fire burned down to a red heart, the wind worrying at the shutters—that Xanthe appeared in the doorway of the little room Greta shared with the linen press and held up a key ring.

"Where did you get those?" Greta whispered.

"The housekeeper's cupboard. There are forty keys on this ring and I have tried eleven of them against that lock already tonight. I am going to try the rest. You may come and watch, or you may go back to your mending, but I am going to do it either way, because I have decided that being a spectator is a character flaw and I am reforming."

Greta put down the sock she had been darning—a sock belonging to Thomas Penhale, who left his mending with Xanthe, who passed it to Greta, an arrangement none of them had ever discussed aloud—and followed her down the corridor.

In the study, Xanthe set the case on the desk between them and stood over it with the key ring in both hands like a surgeon deciding where to cut.

"We shouldn't," Greta said, because someone had to say it, and it seemed to be her job lately.

"No," Xanthe agreed pleasantly, and knelt to try the twelfth key.

The lock resisted. The thirteenth key fit but would not turn. The fourteenth turned halfway and stuck. Xanthe swore under her breath in a vocabulary she had clearly acquired from stable boys, and Greta found herself kneeling beside her on the cold rug, close enough to smell coal smoke and engine oil in her hair, holding the lamp so its light fell into the keyhole.

"You could pick it," Greta said. "My father taught me. He said any lock is just a small stubborn person, and you must be more patient than it is."

Xanthe looked up at her. In the lamplight her face was all angles and attention. "Your father taught you to pick locks."

"He taught me many impractical things. He said practical people build the world and impractical people make it worth living in." Greta heard her own voice wobble slightly on the last word and steadied it. "He also taught me to play chess badly and to identify forty species of bird by their calls, which has been useless in Cornwall, where everything sounds like rain."

"Show me," said Xanthe, and handed her a hairpin from her own bun without a moment's hesitation, which told Greta something about Xanthe Fairweather that she suspected very few people knew: that beneath the composure there was a girl who had been waiting all her life for permission to be reckless.

* * *

The lock was indeed a small stubborn person. It took them the better part of an hour—two hairpins, a hatpin borrowed from a hat neither of them liked, one bent teaspoon, and a period of profound silence during which Greta's hands shook so badly that Xanthe covered them with her own and said, "Breathe. It's only paper inside. Whatever it is, it can't be worse than what we're imagining."

That was the trouble, Greta thought. She had been imagining for three years, ever since the letters in the typewriter had run dry and her father had begun saying *when the war comes* in the voice other men used for *when I die*. She had imagined deeds and wills and confessions and apologies. She had imagined nothing at all, which was worst of all.

The lock gave with a click so small it seemed indecent after such effort.

Inside, the case smelled of cedar and ink. There were papers—bundles tied with ribbon, folders fat with correspondence, a leather notebook whose cover bore initials that were not Verity's. And on top of everything, alone in a shallow tray as if placed there deliberately, within reach of a hand that might one day open this case in a hurry or in weakness or in the last hour of a long life:

A document.

Greta lifted it out. The paper was good stock, heavy, the kind used for legal matters. At the top, in a solicitor's careful print: *Deed of Gift and Covenant.*

She read it once in silence. Then she read it again, moving her lips, because the words kept rearranging themselves into impossibility and she wanted to be sure.

"*I, Verity Mary Ashcombe of Ashcombe House, Marrow Quay, in the County of Cornwall,*" she read aloud, because some things needed air, "*do hereby grant and convey unto Emil Johann Brandt of Cologne, engineer, the freehold of Sea Cottage together with its garden and foreshore rights, and do further covenant to pay unto the said Emil Johann Brandt an annuity of sixty pounds per annum for the term of his natural life.*"

The fire ticked. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard settled.

"There's more," Xanthe said quietly. "Read the rest."

But Greta had already seen it. Her eyes had gone to it first, the way a tongue goes to a broken tooth. Below the body of the deed, below the clause about *witnesses attesting*, below even the line reserved for the date—

Nothing. Two blank spaces where signatures should be. A blank space where the date should be. The whole instrument hanging unfinished, like a bridge built to the middle of a channel and abandoned there.

"It's unsigned," Greta said. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears. "It's undated. It's—it's nothing. It's a piece of paper with wishes written on it."

"It's a piece of paper with *her* written on it," Xanthe said. "Look at the ink. Look at how the pen pressed through on the *f* in foreshore. She wrote this herself, or dictated it word by word to someone who copied it exactly. This isn't a draft someone else prepared. This is her hand, her intention, her—"

"Her intention isn't worth sixty pounds," Greta said, and heard the bitterness arrive in her voice like weather. "Intention doesn't hold freehold. Intention doesn't pay annuities. My father spent three years believing in her intention, and I crossed a war to stand in front of it, and it turns out it needs a signature. It needs a signature and a date and two witnesses and a solicitor and a woman who can hold a pen, and she can't hold a pen, Xanthe. She can't hold a spoon."

She hadn't meant to say the last part loudly. It came out loudly anyway, bouncing off the bookshelves, and then she was crying—not prettily, not the way heroines cried in novels, but with the ugly gasping of someone who had been rationing her tears for months and had run out of restraint at exactly the wrong moment.

Xanthe did not tell her to hush. She did not pat her shoulder or offer a handkerchief with sensible advice attached. She simply sat down on the rug beside her, back against the desk, and let her cry, and after a while she said, "My mother used to say that hope is like milk. It goes off if you leave it out too long, but it's still the same milk."

"That's a terrible saying."

"It's a Cornish saying. We specialize in terrible sayings. My grandmother's best one was 'never trust a calm sea,' which she applied to husbands, weather, and soup indiscriminately." Xanthe took the deed gently from Greta's hands and smoothed it flat on her knee. "Listen to me. This paper says she meant to give him the cottage. Not charity—the cottage, and the money, forever. Do you know what Sea Cottage is? It's the best thing she owns after this house. It's where she was born. Mrs. Tregowan told me the Ashcombes have owned it since before anyone can remember, and Lady Ashcombe has refused to sell it to three different people who offered her absurd sums, including a man who wanted to turn it into a tea room, which she described as 'a fate worse than conscription.'"

"I don't understand why she didn't sign it."

"I do," Xanthe said slowly. "Or I think I do. Because signing it makes it real, and real things can be taken away. If she signed it in 1910, and he'd been arrested as a spy, the paper would hang her. If she signed it last year, Hale would find it in five minutes. She kept it unsigned because an unsigned promise is invisible, and she has survived thirty years of making trouble by knowing exactly what is visible." She looked down at the blank lines. "She was waiting for the war to end. She was waiting for a world where giving a German man a cottage wouldn't be treason. And the war didn't wait for her."

Greta wiped her face with her sleeve. Outside, the wind had changed direction and was coming off the marshes with a rawer note, the sound of deep winter arriving ahead of schedule.

"So we've opened the case," she said, "and we've found the treasure, and the treasure is a ghost. Is that the sum of it? We broke a lock for a ghost?"

"We broke a lock," said Xanthe, "for information. And information, my grandmother also said, is the one thing you can't unlearn, so you may as well use it." She tapped the deed. "This proves the promise existed. Not sentiment—paper. If it ever comes to a question of what was promised to your father, this is evidence. Worthless as a deed, perhaps. Not worthless as truth."

"And truth," Greta said, "has been doing wonderfully well for us so far."

For a moment Xanthe almost smiled; then her eyes went past Greta's shoulder to the window, and the smile died.

"Lamp," she said sharply. "Kill the lamp."

Greta cupped the flame dead. Through the gap in the shutters, far down the white lane that led toward the village, a lantern was coming toward the house. Moving slowly. Stopping at intervals, the way a man stops when he is looking at footprints, or counting windows, or committing a house to memory.

"Hale walks at night," Xanthe breathed. "Tregowan mentioned it. He says it aids his digestion." She was already sliding the deed back into the tray, arranging the ribbons, closing the case with a care that made no sound at all. "We were never here. The lock was stiff when we found it and stiffer when we left it. Say nothing. Especially to me—I'm a dreadful liar and you'll see it in my face."

The lantern light swung across the shutter gap, lingered, and moved on toward the gate, unhurried as tide.

They knelt in the dark study, two girls and a ghost of a promise between them, and Greta understood with sudden clarity that the impossible thing was not the unsigned paper. The impossible thing was that they had ever believed they could keep secrets in a village with one road, one inn, and one man whose digestion required him to count their windows by night.

Upstairs, faintly, Lady Verity Ashcombe called out in her sleep—a slurred, half-paralyzed syllable that might have been a name.

Neither of them moved to answer it. Both of them heard it.
