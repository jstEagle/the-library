## 13. Hale's Questions

He came on a Tuesday, because of course he did. Hale was the sort of man who conducted his inspections on Tuesdays, the way tides conducted themselves on schedules and God conducted Himself on Sundays. Xanthe saw him coming up the drive from the kitchen window, a black figure moving at a clerk's pace against the grey sky, and she had time—fully four minutes—to arrange her face into something between a parlourmaid's blankness and a chauffeur's competence.

She chose competence. It was the only thing she'd ever been good at that couldn't be taken from her by a stroke or a war.

"Miss Fairweather." He removed his hat in the hall, revealing hair combed with geometric precision, and produced a notebook before he had finished removing it, as though the notebook were attached to his hand by some municipal string. "Inspector Hale. Home Office. I wonder if I might trouble you for a quarter of an hour."

"Lady Ashcombe is unwell," Xanthe said. "I'm afraid she receives no one."

"I'm not here for Lady Ashcombe." He smiled. It was a small smile, well-maintained, like everything else about him—the boots, the collar, the little leather case that probably contained forms for every catastrophe the Crown could imagine. "I'm here for the household. You are the household's—forgive me—driver? Secretary? Both?"

"Both."

"How economical." He wrote something. She could not see what. That was the whole trick of the notebook, she realized; it did not matter what went into it, only that you watched it happen and spent the rest of your life wondering.

* * *

They sat in the morning room, because it was the room with the least in it. Greta was in the kitchen garden, where she belonged, where she had been sent twenty minutes ago with instructions to pull leeks until further notice. Verity was upstairs in her chair by the window, half-paralyzed and fiercely awake, having communicated via slate and furious gesture that if Xanthe let the man anywhere near her bedroom she would find some way, paralyzed or no, to make Xanthe's life a short one.

Xanthe poured tea because pouring tea gave her hands something honest to do.

"You've been with Lady Ashcombe how long?"

"Four years."

"And before that?"

"I was at a garage in Faversham. Engines."

"A woman mechanic." He noted this too. She wondered what category she'd been filed under. *Ecclesiastical curiosity*, possibly. *Local colour.*

"The household consists of yourself, Lady Ashcombe, and—" he consulted the notebook anyway, though Xanthe would have bet her last shilling he knew it by heart, "—the refugee. The Belgian lady."

"Miss Verhoeven," Xanthe said, and heard herself say it too quickly, the way you say a word you've rehearsed in front of a mirror. One beat too crisp. She watched him hear it too.

"Miss Verhoeven," he agreed pleasantly. "From where in Belgium, precisely?"

The question hung there like a coat on a hook nobody had meant to hang anything on. Xanthe reached for the answer they had prepared—*Liège*—and found, to her horror, that the prepared answers had begun to dissolve under the simple heat of his attention. Liège had fallen in August. Anyone from Liège would have stories. Did Greta have stories? They had never drilled the stories. They had drilled the name, the arrival date, the fiction of the parish priest's letter—which did not exist—and left the rest to improvisation, and improvisation was exactly what Xanthe was worst at, because she had built her entire character out of not being noticed, and lying required a certain theatrical appetite she had never cultivated.

"She doesn't speak much," Xanthe said. "Of the war. Of anything. She's—quiet. Traumatized."

"Naturally." He stirred his tea though there was nothing in it to stir. "It must be difficult for her. Being among strangers. Not speaking the language."

"She gets on well enough."

"In English?"

The trap was so small and so neatly sprung that Xanthe almost admired it. If Miss Verhoeven spoke fluent English, why had no one in Marrow Quay heard her do so? If she didn't, how had Xanthe just implied a conversation?

"She's learning," Xanthe said. "From books. She reads."

"What does she read?"

"Novels." This was true, which was a mercy. It was also, she remembered one heartbeat later, dangerously specific: Greta read Goethe in the original and English novels with a German-English dictionary, mouthing the words, and if Inspector Hale ever got a look at the margins—

"German novels?" he asked mildly.

"English ones."

"Mm." He made a small mark, a tick or a cross, impossible to tell. Then he closed the notebook, which was somehow more frightening than opening it, folded his hands, and delivered the sentence Xanthe would spend the rest of the afternoon trying to unhear.

"We have a report," he said, "of a young woman named Greta Brandt. Daughter of Emil Brandt, engineer, lately resident in Folkestone, now detained under the Defence of the Realm Act. The daughter applied to travel to Kent in November. To claim, I believe, a legacy." He looked up, and his eyes were not unkind, which was the terrible part; unkindness she could have fought. "The application was refused. But refused is a fluid condition in wartime, Miss Fairweather. People move. Tides move. And this village, as I understand it, has been sealed since the causeway closed—no one in, no one out, no post worth the name. A very convenient place, I imagine, for someone who wished to be nowhere."

The clock on the mantel ticked. Somewhere above them, floorboards creaked—Verity's nurse, or Verity herself, dragging her deadened side along the rail.

"You think our Miss Verhoeven," Xanthe said carefully, "is this Brandt girl."

"I think," said Hale, "that Belgium has many Verhoevens and few of them arrive on foot carrying typewriters manufactured in Berlin."

*The typewriter.* Of course. Of course, of course, of course. A machine with a serial number, a manufacturer's plate, a paper trail stitched into its very keys—and they had hidden it in the study like a corpse under floorboards that any constable with a warrant and a crowbar could lift. Her tea had gone cold in her hands. She set the cup down without a sound, because a sound was all she could trust herself not to make.

"I couldn't speak to what she carries," Xanthe said. "Luggage isn't my department."

"No," Hale agreed. "Your department appears to be engines, correspondence, and whatever else Lady Ashcombe requires. Which is rather a great deal, I should think, now that she cannot write her own name." He rose, replaced his hat, and paused at the door with the air of a man remembering a courtesy. "I'll call again. I find that in villages like this, the truth tends to come up slowly, like a tide over a causeway—one hour at a time, until quite suddenly the road is gone." He smiled his small maintained smile. "You've lived behind that bridge all winter, Miss Fairweather. You'll know the phenomenon better than anyone."

And then he was gone, down the drive at his clerk's pace, leaving Xanthe standing in the morning room with two cold cups of tea and the distinct sensation that the floor beneath her was no longer entirely land.

* * *

Verity listened to the account in silence, which was worse than shouting. Since the stroke she had recovered speech in fragments—words arriving like survivors of a shipwreck, some whole, some broken—and when the fragments failed her she used the slate, and the slate made everything sound either like a telegram or a curse. Now she gripped the chalk in a fist that shook and wrote:

**HE KNOWS THE NAME**

"He suspects the name," Xanthe corrected. "There's a difference. Suspicion isn't proof."

Verity wiped the slate with her sleeve—a gesture shockingly informal, the old campaigner breaking through the invalid—and wrote again:

**SUSPICION HANGED ROGER CASEMENT**

"That was rather more than suspicion," Xanthe began, and stopped, because arguing precedent with Lady Verity Ashcombe was like arguing tides with the moon.

**GRETA LEAVES**, the slate said. **TONIGHT. MARSH PATH. BEFORE HE RETURNS.**

"She can't. The marshes in January—if the fog comes off the water she'll walk straight into a channel, and if the frost gives way under her—"

Verity flung the slate onto the bed. It skidded to the counterpane's edge and hung there, teetering, and for one absurd moment Xanthe thought: *even the slate wants to leave.* Then Verity caught her good hand around Xanthe's wrist—not hard, her strength was mostly gone, but with the precision of a woman who had once held crowds of ten thousand still with nothing but her voice.

"Gret-a," Verity said, each syllable hauled up from somewhere deep. "Safe. Here. Than—dead. Out there." Her eyes, the only part of her face still fully obedient, burned with the old ferocity, and Xanthe understood suddenly that this was not cruelty. This was the same arithmetic Verity had run her whole life: what must be risked against what must be saved. The promise had an author after all, even now, even like this.

Before Xanthe could answer, there came a knock, and Thomas Penhale stood in the doorway with snow melting on his shoulders and an expression she had never seen on him before—schoolmaster composed on top, and underneath it something urgent, something that had been running.

"Beg pardon—I saw the inspector go down the drive. Miss Fairweather, might I have a word? It's—it concerns you particularly."

* * *

They walked as far as the gatehouse wall, out of earshot of windows, which in Marrow Quay meant out of earshot of perhaps forty percent of the village and none of the rest. Thomas pulled off one glove with his teeth and drew from inside his coat a folded paper.

"This came on today's mail sledge. Addressed to you. At the house." He hesitated. "The postmaster's boy knows me. Knows I walk past the inn most days. He asked—well. He asked whether I'd save himself the trip up the hill."

Xanthe took the paper. It was an official envelope, Home Office stamp, her name spelled correctly, which somehow made it worse—Hale had checked the spelling. She opened it with fingers that felt borrowed.

*A summons. To attend the inn on Friday next, at eleven o'clock, for interview respecting the registration of aliens within the parish. Signed, J. Hale, Inspector.*

"It's just an interview," Thomas said. "You've already had—"

"He interviewed me this morning. Informally." She stared at the summons. "This is the formal one. This is the kind you bring a lawyer to, or fail to attend at your peril. Thomas—how did he know my name? My proper name, spelled right? There's no register of me anywhere. I'm a chauffeur. Chauffeurs don't get summoned."

Thomas was quiet for a moment. Snow ticked softly in the yew hedge.

"There's a list," he said at last. "Of household staff. Parishes keep them—it's an old form, predates the war, but Hale will have requisitioned every scrap of paper in the district. Your name will be on the Ashcombe household return. Four years back, maybe five." He paused. "Xanthe—may I say something that isn't about lists?"

"You may say anything you like. It seems everyone else is."

"Hale spoke to me yesterday. At the school. Officially—he wanted the school attendance ledger, checking ages against exemptions, boys who might have lied about enlistment." Thomas turned his hat in his hands, a habit she had seen a hundred times and never once wondered about until now. "But halfway through, quite casually, he asked about you. How long I'd known you. Whether you had family. Whether you were"—a small, pained smile—"settled. Whether I thought you were happy here."

"What did you tell him?"

"That you were indispensable, discreet, and entirely without secrets." Thomas met her eyes then, and the gentleness in his face was almost unbearable, because they both knew what he had just done: he had lied to a Home Office inspector, flatly, for her sake, without asking what the secret was or whether she deserved the lie. "He wrote it down. Everything gets written down now. That's the whole war, really, as far as I can tell—a war of notebooks."

Xanthe looked down at the summons. Eleven o'clock, Friday. Two days. In two days she would sit across from that maintained smile and that municipal notebook, and every rehearsed answer she owned had already shown its seams this morning, and somewhere in a file in London—or worse, in the leather case under his arm—was the name *Greta Brandt* with an arrow drawn toward their sealed, snowed-in, one-road village.

"Thomas," she said slowly, "why did you lie for me?"

"Because you'd lie for her." He nodded back toward the house, toward the girl pulling leeks in the frozen garden who was neither Belgian nor Verhoeven nor safe. "I watch people, Xanthe. It's the trade I'm actually in—teaching is only watching with a blackboard. And for weeks I've watched you carry something heavier than you should be carrying alone, and I've covered for you without knowing why, and I'm not going to stop now just because the reason has a name and a serial number." He put his glove back on. "Friday, then. Don't face him alone."

"I don't understand you," Xanthe said, and it came out harsher than she meant. "I don't understand any of this—your covering, your ledgers, your—"

"No," Thomas agreed quietly. "You don't. That's rather the trouble." He glanced at the house, then back at her, and whatever else he had been about to say he folded away, neat as a lesson plan. "Friday. Eleven o'clock. I'll be in the village anyway—there are forms for the school stove, coal allotments, the usual glories of wartime bureaucracy. Walk down with me. Let him see you're not a woman who walks anywhere alone."

He went. Snow filled his footprints almost before he'd cleared the gate, the village erasing him efficiently, as it erased everything eventually—or kept everything, depending on the season, depending on the tide.

Xanthe stood at the wall with the summons in her hand until the ink seemed to blur, and then she did the thing she had done every night for four years with other people's letters: she read it again. *Interview respecting the registration of aliens.* Not her name in the accusative. Not yet. Just her name, spelled correctly, summoned to the table where the questions would be asked, because Hale had understood something about her before she had: that the way into the Ashcombe house was never through Lady Verity, never through the locked writing case, never through the girl in the garden.

It was through the reader of letters.

Upstairs, faintly, the slate scratched against its frame. Down the hill, at the inn, a light came on in a first-floor window—Hale's window, she supposed, where he sat each evening with his files and his tea, transcribing the tide.

Two days.

She turned back toward the house, and found Greta standing at the garden gate, leek tops in one hand, watching her with an expression that needed no translation in any language.

"He asked about me," Greta said. Not a question.

"He asked about everyone." Xanthe folded the summons into her pocket, where it lay against her heart like a coal. "Greta—go inside. And take the leeks. For heaven's sake, take the leeks."

But Greta didn't move. Behind her, at an upper window, Verity's silhouette stood rigid against the lamplight, watching the road as though the road might yet be argued with—and Xanthe thought, with a calm that frightened her more than panic would have done: *Friday we lose, unless Thursday we become criminals properly, deliberately, together.*

She just didn't yet know what the crime would be.
