## 14. Thomas's Offer

Thomas Penhale had taught arithmetic to the children of Marrow Quay for eleven years, and in all that time he had learned one lesson more thoroughly than any he had ever set before a class: that the hardest sums are not done with numbers.

He was grading exercise books on Thursday evening when he heard the knock—not at his door but at the schoolhouse gate, the iron latch lifting with its particular complaint, a sound he knew as intimately as his own heartbeat. He knew, too, who it would be. He had watched her walk up the lane from Ashcombe House every second Saturday for three years, always at dusk, always alone, always with her collar turned up against whatever weather the estuary chose to offer. She came to return books. That was what she said. And Thomas, who could spot an evasion at forty paces—teaching children will do that to a man—had never once called it anything but returning books.

He opened the door before she knocked.

"Miss Fairweather. It's a filthy evening."

"It's drizzling."

"It's the North Sea pretending to be weather." He stood back to let her in. "Come and sit by the stove before the sea finishes the job."

The schoolhouse smelled of chalk dust and coal smoke and the particular sweetness of thirty children's lunches steamed into the plaster over decades—a smell visitors found odd and Thomas found homelike. Xanthe shook the damp from her coat and held out two volumes wrapped in brown paper.

"*The Mill on the Floss.* Again. You've read it four times."

"I like watching Maggie Tulliver make the same mistakes," she said. "It's restful, knowing how it ends."

"And does it end well?"

She looked at him then, and something crossed her face like a cloud crossing water—there and gone, but it darkened everything beneath it for a moment.

"Don't tell me," she said. "I'm only halfway through."

"You're halfway through and you already know how it ends. Everyone does. That's the trouble with George Eliot; she can't resist a moral." He took the paper parcel and set it on the desk among the exercise books. "Sit down. There's tea in the pot if you don't mind it stewed."

"I mind nothing tonight."

That, he thought, was the truest thing she'd said in three years of borrowed novels.

* * *

They sat either side of the stove like bookends, and Thomas let the silence do its work. He had learned patience from the slowest boy in his class, a fisherman's son named Walter Cuddiford who took eleven minutes to divide 144 by 12 but arrived, every time, at the correct answer. Patience was the whole art of teaching, and the whole art of waiting for a woman to speak.

She spoke eventually, staring into the fire.

"The inspector came to the house today."

"Hale?"

"You know his name."

"The village knows his name. The village knows his boot size and his breakfast preferences and that he takes his mutton without mustard, which Mrs. Treloar considers a character flaw of the first magnitude." He poured the tea. "What did he want with you?"

"To ask me questions. Ordinary questions. How long I'd been employed at Ashcombe House. Whether I handle the post. Whether Lady Ashcombe receives visitors." Xanthe turned her cup in her hands. "He asked whether I'd ever seen the refugee write anything down."

"What did you say?"

"That I hadn't." She met his eyes over the rim of the cup. "It was the truth, as it happens. But it felt like a lie, because I wanted it to be true so badly."

Thomas set down his own cup. Here it was, then—the thing he had been circling for weeks the way you circle a wasp's nest, wanting to help and knowing that help meant getting stung.

"Xanthe." He said her name plainly, no title attached, because this next part required plainness. "You know I don't ask questions."

"I know."

"You come every fortnight, you borrow my books, you sit by my stove, and you tell me exactly as much as you choose to tell me, which is generally the weather and your opinion of George Eliot. And I have never asked why Lady Ashcombe's chauffeur reads Victorian novels, or why her Belgian refugee has a north German accent that wouldn't fool a customs officer in Calais, or why you've started looking over your shoulder on the lane like a woman being followed."

Her cup had stopped turning. "No," she said quietly. "You haven't."

"I'm going to say something now, and I want you to hear all of it before you answer, because I've been composing it since the tide went out this morning and I shall lose my nerve entirely if you interrupt." He leaned forward, elbows on knees. "I have loved you since the day you arrived. Do you remember the day you arrived? Of course you don't; it was nothing to you. It was raining sideways and you got out of Lady Ashcombe's Daimler to ask me directions to the church hall, and your hat blew off, and you caught it, and you laughed—at yourself, not at the weather, not at me—and I thought: there is a person who laughs at her own misfortunes. I am going to be in a great deal of trouble over this person."

Xanthe did not move. The fire ticked in the grate.

"Three years," Thomas said. "I have said nothing for three years because you were a spectator here and I was a fixture, and fixtures don't presume. But things have changed, haven't they? You're not spectating anymore. Whatever is happening in that house—you're inside it now, up to your neck, and Inspector Hale is asking about the post you carry, and I'll warrant my last shilling that the danger isn't Lady Ashcombe's. It's yours."

"Thomas—"

"No. Hear the rest." His voice stayed level; eleven years of small boys throwing inkwells had given him that. "I am offering you marriage. Not courtship—we haven't time for courtship; courtship is a peacetime luxury, like sugar in one's tea. Marriage. A paper shield. If you are my wife, Hale cannot compel testimony from you against yourself, and half his questions become improper. If Greta Brandt—I beg your pardon, if the *refugee*—is kin to the schoolmaster's household, she acquires a respectability that no file of his can easily puncture. A parish has ways of protecting its own, and a married schoolmaster is about as much its own as a man can be."

The fire settled. Somewhere outside, the tide was coming in over the flats with its long hush, the sound the village slept to, the sound that sealed them off from England every twelve hours.

"There," Thomas said. "All of it. I told you not to interrupt, and you didn't, which is the most obedient thing anyone has done in this schoolhouse all term."

* * *

Xanthe set her teacup down with great care, as though it might go off.

"You've rehearsed that," she said.

"Since low tide. I told you."

"Not the speech. The feeling behind it." She was looking at him fully now, and he saw that her eyes were bright and furious and frightened all at once, which was a combination he had seen on the faces of children who had been accused wrongly and dared not say so. "Three years, Thomas. Three years you've known, and said nothing, and now—what? Now it's *useful*? Now the Home Office makes my hand worth having?"

"That is not—"

"Isn't it?" She stood up, and the stove-light made her shadow enormous on the schoolroom wall, looming over the alphabet cards. "Because it sounds exactly like it. You dress it up beautifully—I'll grant you, it's a very fine speech, the hat detail was devastating—but underneath it, it's arithmetic. Marry the schoolmaster, acquire legal cover, protect the girl, baffle the inspector. You've worked out the sum and I'm the figure that makes it balance."

"If you believe that," Thomas said, "then I have wasted eleven years learning to wait, because a woman who believes that hasn't heard a word I've said in three years of fortnightly visits."

"Then what am I meant to believe? That you chose tonight—the night after Hale sat me down in the drawing room like a suspect—to declare yourself? Out of pure romance?"

"Yes!" It came out louder than he intended, loud enough to startle them both, and he made himself continue, quieter. "Out of pure romance, and pure terror, and the perfectly ordinary cowardice of a man who waited until a war forced the issue because he couldn't find the courage to force it himself. All of those things are true at once, Xanthe. People aren't clean motives. I'm a schoolmaster; I know how messy a slate gets."

She stood very still. Outside the rain had thickened, drumming on the slates, and beneath it the tide-sound swelled.

"You cannot answer tonight," Thomas said. "I know that. I didn't expect you to; I'd have been suspicious of you if you had. But I want you to carry one thing home across that dark lane, and it isn't the proposal. It's this: whatever you decide, whatever you're carrying in that house—Greta, the letters, the promise, whatever it is Verity Ashcombe has got you holding—you don't carry it alone anymore. Not if you don't wish to. That's the offer. The ring is just the paperwork."

Xanthe's face did something complicated. For a moment he thought she would cry, and then he thought she would laugh, and then she did neither; she simply nodded, once, the way she acknowledged a point in an argument she intended to win later.

"Thank you for the books," she said. Her voice was steady. Only her hands, gathering her coat, were not. "And thank you for—for saying it plainly. Nobody says anything plainly anymore. Everything's coded. The refugee is coded. The promise is coded. Even Verity's silences are coded." She pulled the door open onto the rain. "You were the only plain thing left, Thomas Penhale. Don't spoil it by expecting an answer."

"I don't," he said. "Expecting things is for inspectors."

She almost smiled. Then she was out into the wet dark, and he watched from the doorway as she went down the lane, collar up, unhurried, the lamplight catching her twice before the hedge swallowed her—and he noticed, with the particular ache of a man who notices everything about one person, that she glanced over her shoulder only once, and that it was back toward him.

* * *

He did not sleep. He marked exercise books instead, badly, and drank tea gone bitter as bark, and somewhere past midnight he heard the sea change its note—the deep, dragging withdrawal that meant the causeway was opening, that Marrow Quay was briefly an island no longer.

At dawn there was a knock at the schoolhouse door, early, earlier than any pupil.

It was Mrs. Treloar from the inn, breathless, her shawl pinned crooked.

"You'd best come, Mr. Penhale. That inspector gentleman's at the post office, and he's got a telegram, and he's asking after Miss Fairweather by name—and there's a constable with him."
