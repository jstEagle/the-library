## 15. Midwinter Tide

The longest night of the year came down on Marrow Quay like a lid.

Greta had learned to measure the winter in small inventories. Six eggs in the basket, five if the speckled hen sulked, which she usually did. Half a sack of coal. One candle stub that smelled of beeswax and church. And one typewriter, wrapped in oilcloth beneath the loose floorboard of the still-room, holding more of her father's heart than any letter he had been permitted to send her since August.

She had also learned to measure it in sounds. The tick of the kitchen clock, which Lady Verity's cook swore ran slow and refused to have corrected. The groan of the tide going out across the flats at dawn, vast and unhurried, like an old man turning over in bed. And lately—newly—the sound of Xanthe Fairweather laughing at something Thomas Penhale said on the schoolhouse path, a sound Greta heard through glass and told herself she did not mind hearing.

Tonight there was almost no sound at all. Snow had fallen since dusk without wind, the polite, insistent snow that means to stay, and the whole world beyond the kitchen window was a soft grey-white nothing. The household had gone to bed early. Cook because her knees ached; Mrs. Tregowan the housekeeper because her opinions required rest; Lady Verity because everything tired her now, though she would never admit to being tired, only to "considering."

Greta sat at the kitchen table with her coat already on.

She had not decided it, exactly. Decisions were things other people made—her father, deciding to send his daughter across a war rather than keep her where he could watch over her; Lady Verity, deciding that a girl from Kiel could pass for Belgian if she kept her mouth shut about anything but potatoes. Greta had simply been making small arrangements for three days without naming them to herself: bread saved from supper wrapped in a napkin. Her mother's shawl, the good wool one, folded into a bundle. The matches moved from the tin by the stove into her pocket, so no one would notice their absence until morning.

The marshes were frozen. Mrs. Tregowan said so twice at supper, disapprovingly, as though the weather were a German plot. *Hard enough to walk to the church on*, she'd said, *and folk do, saving their boots*. Which meant, Greta thought now, that the flats between the house and the mainland road were hard enough to cross. Six miles of frozen saltings and shingle, then the coast road, then—she didn't know what came next. A port. A ship. Another country willing to hold her. She would work it out walking, the way her father worked out bridge loads: one assumption at a time, tested before you trusted it.

Because every day she stayed, the danger grew legs.

She had seen it plainly at the jumble sale, the way Mrs. Cobb's eyes had tracked her hands as she folded tablecloths—as if German fingers might be recognized by their folding. She had heard Inspector Hale's voice at the inn when she passed with the egg basket, mild and courteous and terrible: *"And how long has the Ashcombe household employed its refugee? No papers, you say. Curious."* She knew what happened to households that sheltered the undocumented. It was in all the newspapers Cook read aloud with relish. Fines. Prison. Lady Verity, half-paralyzed, wheeled before some tribunal in London while the village watched from behind its curtains.

And Xanthe. Xanthe, who had hidden the typewriter under floorboards with her own hands and lied to Hale with a face like carved soap. Xanthe, whose whole future was being spent, coin by coin, on a stranger who had arrived at the gate owing nothing to anyone.

That was the arithmetic Greta could not make come right. Her father had taught her that a structure is only as honest as its load paths—that every weight must go somewhere, or the whole thing fails. Her weight was going into Xanthe. Into Verity. Into Thomas Penhale, who intercepted telegrams with the cheerful efficiency of a man who had found a use for love. She could feel the strain in them all, the little deflections, the way Cook's smile tightened when Hale's name came up.

A bridge takes the weight or it breaks. It does not get to be loved while it does both.

So: the marshes. Tonight, while the snow covered footprints. By morning she would be a problem belonging to some other parish.

She rose, took up the bundle, and let herself out through the scullery door with the particular silence she had practiced all winter—the silence of a person trying not to exist.

* * *

The cold outside was not an absence of warmth; it was a presence, a thing with weight and opinion. It went through her coat, her shawl, the two jumpers beneath, and settled against her ribs like a hand checking for contraband. The snow squeaked underfoot. Overhead the sky had cleared between clouds, and stars stood out in such numbers that for one foolish moment Greta forgot she was fleeing and simply looked, the way her father had taught her to look—at the Pole Star first, low and steady above the black line of the sea wall, everything else arranged around it like evidence around a fact.

She went down past the boathouse, past the oyster beds sleeping under ice, and out along the sea wall until the wall ended and the marsh began.

Here the world stopped pretending. The flats stretched away grey-white to the distant channel, stitched with the dark threads of creeks frozen mid-curve, and the tide was far out, and the silence was enormous. Somewhere ahead, six miles of nothing, lay the road. Somewhere behind her lay the lit kitchen window of Ashcombe House, a smudge of gold in the snow-gloom, getting smaller with each step.

She was perhaps four hundred yards out when she realized she was not alone.

"Greta Brandt."

The voice carried flat across the ice, stripped of all its usual music. Greta turned. A dark figure came toward her along her own footprints—not hurrying, which was somehow worse than hurrying—and resolved into Xanthe in a man's overcoat over her nightdress, hair escaping its plait, breath smoking.

"How," Greta said.

"You took the matches." Xanthe stopped an arm's length away. "Cook counts them like money. I've watched her do it for a year." Her voice shook, and Greta understood suddenly that it wasn't from cold. "I thought—I told myself you were making toast. At midnight. In your coat."

"So you followed me."

"So I followed you." Xanthe looked past her, out at the flats, and her jaw set. "Do you know what's out there? Do you actually know?"

"I know it is frozen. Mrs. Tregowan complains of it weekly."

"Mrs. Tregowan walks to the *church*." Xanthe flung an arm at the darkness ahead. "Two hundred yards of firm ground by the wall. Out there—" the arm swept toward the channel line, invisible but audible somehow, a faint creaking whisper under the stars, "—out there the tide comes back at three in the morning whether you've reached the road or not. There are creeks under that snow with water moving in them, warm enough at the bottom to melt the ice from beneath, so the crust looks solid and is not. The oyster men won't cross after dark in *December*. And they've lived here their whole lives and they can *swim*."

"Then I will follow the wall."

"The wall ends at the sluice gate, and the sluice gate is broken, and everyone knows it, and you didn't, because you have been here six months and you still don't know where the church keeps its ladder or why we burn the stubble or—" Xanthe stopped herself. Breathed. When she spoke again her voice was lower and worse. "You would get a mile, maybe two. And then either the marsh takes you, or it doesn't, and they find your body at low tide with your name sewn in your collar, and Inspector Hale gets exactly what he wants handed to him on a plate, and Lady Verity—"

"Don't." 

"—Verity spends whatever she has left answering questions about why a Belgian refugee with a German sewing kit—"

"I said *don't*." It came out louder than Greta meant, loud enough to startle a bird off the sea wall, a dark flap into the dark. "Don't tell me what happens to her. I know. That is why I am *going*."

"Going *where*?" For the first time Xanthe's voice cracked clean open. "Where, Greta? Name me the place. You have sixpence and a heel of bread and a shawl. You speak English like a very formal book. Every port in this country has a man with a list, and your name is on all of them, because I have read the lists, it is literally my job, I read everything—" She pressed her mittened hand over her mouth for a second. "I'm sorry. I'm sorry, that was—"

"No." Something in Greta's chest had gone very still and very hot. "No, say it. Say what you think. That I am stupid. That I owe—"

"That is not what I said."

"It is what you mean! All winter!" The words were coming out of her now in German and English tangled together, and she let them. "Every kindness with a ledger behind it. The typewriter hidden—yes, thank you. The lie maintained—yes, thank you. And always underneath, the counting. What it costs. What I cost. Well, I am settling the account! I am removing the expense! Is that not generous? Is that not what the books require?"

Xanthe stared at her. The wind had died entirely; the whole marsh seemed to lean in.

"You think I count," Xanthe said quietly.

"I think everyone counts. Your mistress counted my father's letters for thirty years and burned the last one."

"And I read it first." 

The words dropped into the silence between them like a stone into a well, and Greta heard it hit bottom.

"You—" She took a step backward on the ice. "*You.*"

"I steamed it open in September. Before she burned it. I have read every letter that has crossed this house since I was nineteen years old, and I told myself it was nothing, I was furniture, history happened to other people—" Xanthe's laugh came out jagged. "And then you walked up the drive carrying your father's machine and I understood that I had never once been furniture in my life. I have been a witness. I just hadn't noticed what I was witnessing."

The tide-mist was beginning to rise off the channel, ghosting across the flats, and the cold found new ways in. Greta pulled the shawl tighter and discovered she was crying, which was inconvenient and freezing and absolutely unstoppable.

"My father sent me here," she said, "because a woman promised him sanctuary in 1908 and he believed her. Do you know what that costs a man like my father? To believe? He designs things that must hold. He checks and rechecks. And he checked *her*, for years, letters and letters, and concluded: sound. Reliable. Load-bearing." She wiped her face with the back of her mitten, furiously. "And now she cannot sign her own name, and the promise is a piece of paper nobody will honor, and I am supposed to stand in her kitchen peeling potatoes while the whole structure comes down, and be *grateful*."

"Yes," Xanthe said. "All of that. And none of it changes what's out there." She pointed at the flats again, but her voice had changed—steadier now, the chauffeur's voice, the voice that knew roads and distances and what vehicles could survive them. "Greta. Look at me. I have driven this county in every weather since 1911. If you make me choose between watching you walk out onto that ice and physically sitting on you until spring, I want you to understand that I have considered the logistics of the second option quite seriously, and Cook has a spare mattress, and I am stronger than I look."

Despite everything—a hiccup of laughter tore out of Greta, shocking her, steaming in the air between them.

"You are insane," Greta said. "Englishwomen are all insane. It is the weather."

"Probably." Xanthe stepped closer. And then, gently, in the voice she used for Verity on bad days: "Why tonight? Truly. What happened today?"

And because the night was enormous and the truth had been sitting in Greta's chest all evening like a swallowed stone, she said it. "He wrote again. My father. A letter came in the December post, the one Thomas—Mr. Penhale—held back for me. Three lines. The camp is cold. The food is short. He writes: *Tell Fräulein Ashcombe the promise need not be honored. I release her from it. A promise made in peacetime should not burden anyone in wartime.*" Greta's breath hitched. "He is releasing *her*. From his sickbed, in an internment camp, he is managing her conscience. That is my father. That is what he does. He carries bridges so other people don't have to."

The mist curled between them. Somewhere far off, the channel ice boomed once, deeply, like a struck bell.

"He's wrong, you know," Xanthe said.

"What?"

"About the promise. About whose burden it is." Xanthe stamped her feet against the cold, briskly, as if warming up for something. "I used to think promises were debts. You pay them and they're finished. But I've watched Verity this winter—what's left of her—and the promise isn't weighing her down. It's the only thing keeping her upright. It's the last thing she chose for *herself*, do you see? Not duty, not the Cause, not England. Him. And you want to take even that off her by leaving, and call it sacrifice, and it isn't. It's theft. It's stealing her last campaign."

"She cannot fight it. She cannot even sign—"

"Not yet." Xanthe said it fast, too fast, and then stopped, and Greta saw something move behind her eyes—some plan, half-formed, dangerous, not yet spoken. "Not yet," she repeated, more carefully. "But Greta—if you leave tonight, there is no 'yet' for anyone. If you stay, then whatever comes next, we face it with the full strength of the household. Both of us. All of us. Even Cook, who would deny it under oath but has been putting extra butter in your bread since October and thinks nobody's noticed."

Greta laughed again, wetter this time. "She thinks I don't notice."

"Cook has never successfully deceived anyone in her life. It's why she's a treasure." Xanthe held out her hand, mitten and all. "Come inside. Please. Come and be cold somewhere warmer, and eat the heel of bread you were going to spend on the marsh, because honestly, six miles on one heel of bread—you'd have collapsed at the sluice gate and I'd have had to carry you back, and then we'd *both* have been ridiculous."

Greta looked at the offered hand. Then past it, at the flats—the great white silence where her escape had died six hundred yards from its beginning. She felt the failure of it, and beneath the failure, unexpectedly, something else: relief, vast and shameful, like setting down a weight she'd insisted on carrying alone.

"My father would say I failed the calculation," she said.

"Your father," said Xanthe, "would say the calculation was wrong, not the engineer. Bridges aren't cowardice, Greta. Knowing the load is too heavy for one span—that's engineering." She wiggled her mitten. "Take my hand. We'll freeze out here and then Verity will have two invalids instead of one, and Cook will resign, and honestly the household cannot sustain it."

Greta took the hand. Through two layers of wool she could feel the grip anyway, firm and certain, and they turned together back toward the sea wall and the small gold window growing larger as they walked.

Halfway to the wall, Xanthe spoke again, quietly, not looking at her.

"There's a thing I haven't told you. About the writing case. Greta and I—we opened it, weeks ago, and there's a deed in there. Your father's name on it. Cottage, annuity, everything. Unsigned. Undated. Worthless." A pause. "Unless."

"Unless what?"

Xanthe was silent for three steps. Four. When she answered, her voice had the careful evenness of someone stepping onto ice they weren't entirely sure of.

"Unless someone makes it true," she said. "And I think I know how. But I'll need Verity lucid, and I'll need you brave, and I'll need—" she glanced back once, at the dark shape of the house against the snow, at the single lit window, "—I'll need us all to agree that some promises matter more than the law. Tomorrow night. After Cook's gone to bed."

They climbed the sea wall. Behind them the marsh lay silent and treacherous and beautiful, keeping its secrets under the snow, and ahead the kitchen door opened spilling yellow light, and Cook's silhouette filled it, arms folded, saying without words what she would certainly say with words: *midnight, both of you, in this weather.*

But Greta, stamping snow from her boots on the step, was not listening to Cook. She was thinking about the word *unless*, and the way Xanthe had said it—like an engineer, testing a load, and finding it might, just possibly, hold.
