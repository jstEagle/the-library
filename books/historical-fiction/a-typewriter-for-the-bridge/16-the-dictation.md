## 16. The Dictation

The stroke had taken the right side of Lady Verity Ashcombe the way a tide takes a sandcastle: not all at once, but thoroughly, and with an indifference that felt almost personal. Her left hand still worked, though it had grown unsteady, and her face had gone lopsided in a way that made her look perpetually about to deliver an insult—which, given Verity, she often was.

It was Greta who noticed first that the insults were coming back.

"She called my bread 'a crime against wheat,'" Greta reported one evening in late January, setting down the tray. "In German, mind you, so Mrs. Tregowan wouldn't understand. But I understood."

"Is that a good sign or a bad one?"

"It means," Greta said, "that whatever broke inside her head is mending itself in pieces. And the pieces are choosing their own order." She paused at the door. "She asked for you today. Not for me. Not for Dr. Penrose. For you."

I found this more alarming than any summons to drive through weather. Verity did not ask. Verity commanded, and if you were very lucky she made the command sound like a favor she was doing *you*.

* * *

Her bedroom had been rearranged for illness—the bed pulled from the wall, the heavy curtains half-drawn, a screen hiding the washstand. It smelled of camphor and beeswax and, beneath it, faintly of the lavender water she'd used for as long as I'd known her. She was propped against pillows like a monument under restoration, and when I came in her eyes tracked me across the room with the same precision they'd once used to track Cabinet ministers.

"You're limping," she said. The words came slowly, dragged out of her like nails from timber, each one costing something. "Left boot. Heel worn."

"I've been meaning to have it mended."

"Don't mend it. A woman should have one flaw the village can discuss. Otherwise they invent worse ones." She moved her left hand—an imperial little gesture—and indicated the chair beside the bed. "Sit. No. Closer. I am not going to shout, and neither, apparently, are you going to hear unless you're within arm's reach."

I sat. From this distance I could see how the stroke had aged her beyond its actual damage—not just the slack side of her mouth but a certain surrender in the shoulders of a woman who had spent fifty years carrying herself like a drawn blade.

"You missed my meeting," she said.

"They told me about it. I'm sorry. If I'd driven you—"

"If you'd driven me I would have fallen out of the car on the way home instead of onto the vicarage floor, and Mrs. Pengelly would have had twice the gossip and none of the sympathy. You did nothing wrong." She closed her eyes briefly. "Xanthe. Do you know what the worst part is?"

"The pain?"

"Pain is honest. The worst part is that I can't sign my name." She said it flatly, without self-pity, which somehow made it worse. "Fifty years of petitions. I signed the first one when I was nineteen—my father thrashed me for it, and I signed another the next week out of spite. My whole life is a signature, girl. And now—" She raised the ruined right hand and let it fall. "Now I am a woman of property with no legal voice, and every clerk in Cornwall could tell you what that makes me. Nothing. It makes me what I spent my life fighting to not be."

"Verity—"

"Lady Ashcombe," she corrected automatically, and then, with a ghost of the old humor, "though I suppose after thirty years you've earned the first name. Don't get comfortable with it."

* * *

She didn't speak again for two days. When she sent for me the third time, there was a different quality to the summons—Mrs. Tregowan delivered it with her eyes down and her voice lowered, the way one announces a death in the family or the arrival of a bishop.

Verity had been moved to sit upright in bed, propped with pillows, and someone—I suspected Greta—had pinned her hair with more care than usual. On the coverlet lay the writing case. Unlocked. Open.

I stared at it. So did she, watching me stare.

"Yes," she said. "That case. You know what's in it."

I could have lied. I considered lying. But her eyes held mine with the terrible patience of a woman who had interrogated suffragettes, MPs, and one memorable bishop, and I heard myself say, "The deed. Naming Emil Brandt."

Something moved across her face—anger, I thought, and then not anger. Recognition. Of course she knew I read her letters; perhaps she had always known; perhaps that was why she'd hired a chauffeur who loved books rather than engines.

"Emil Brandt," she repeated. The name came out broken, syllable by syllable, but she gave it weight enough for a eulogy. "You read the letter. The one from him, before the post stopped. Before I burned it."

"Yes."

"Burned it," she agreed, "and kept the deed unsigned for six years, which is the act of either a coward or a sentimentalist, and I refuse to be the former at my age." She drew a breath that seemed to cost her the price of a train ticket. "Greta tells me you helped her hide the typewriter. From Hale's man, when he searched the house. Is that true?"

"It's true."

"Why?"

"Because it was hers. Because it was his. Because it wasn't evidence of anything except love and engineering, and I don't think either of those is a crime yet."

A sound escaped her—rusty, cracked, unmistakably a laugh. "Oh, you're wasted on engines." Then the laugh folded away and she became grave in a way I had never seen her, and I understood that we had arrived somewhere she had been traveling toward alone for a long time.

"I am going to dictate," she said, "my testament. My true testament. And you are going to write it down. Every word. Even the ones that shame me. *Especially* those."

* * *

We began that afternoon and continued over four days, because the fragments of her lucidity came and went like light through moving cloud. In the clear hours she spoke with the old iron authority, marshaling sentences the length of causeways; in the dim hours she drifted, and I learned to stop writing and simply hold her hand until the tide of her attention came back in.

She began, characteristically, with dispositions. The house to her nephew in Canada, who had never seen it and probably imagined England as a larger version of a churchyard. The London lease to the WSPU. But these were the public document, she said, already lodged with solicitors, and what she wanted now was the other thing—the true testament, the one no court would honor and therefore the only one that mattered.

"Write: I, Verity Margaret Ashcombe, being of sound mind and unsound body—"

"Shall I write 'unsound'?"

"Write exactly what I say. I have been polite for seventy years and it got me a partially paralyzed face and a government that ignores me. Begin again."

I wrote: *I, Verity Margaret Ashcombe, being of sound mind and unsound body, do set down the truth, which has waited long enough.*

"In 1908," she dictated, "a man named Emil Brandt came to Marrow Quay to build a bridge. He argued with me about everything—mathematics, politics, the proper way to brew tea—and he was wrong about all of them except the mathematics. I promised him sanctuary in this house should he ever need it. I made this promise freely, in peacetime, before there was any war to excuse me from keeping it. A promise that requires a war to break it was never a promise. It was a costume I put on and called duty."

She paused. Outside, wind worried the shutters. Her left hand lay open on the coverlet, palm up, as if waiting for something to be placed in it.

"He wrote to me last autumn," she went on. "He asked me to receive his daughter. He reminded me of my word. And I burned his letter, because I was afraid—of Inspector Hale, of the village, of being caught in an act of loyalty when loyalty had gone out of fashion. Write that. Write that I was afraid. I have never once written that sentence in my life and I find it fits my hand surprisingly well."

My pen was shaking. I steadied it against the page.

"Now the hard part," she said, and for a moment I thought she had drifted—but her eyes were clear, clearer than they'd been since before the stroke, fixed on the middle distance where 1908 presumably still stood.

"Emil Brandt was the love of my life," she dictated. "There. It is written now, and the world may do with it what it likes. Duty cost him to me, and I let it, because duty was the only lover I'd ever been offered and I believed it would be faithful. It was faithful. That was the worst of it. Duty never leaves you; it simply sits in the house, taking up rooms, until you forget there was ever space for anything else." Her voice thinned. "He married. I campaigned. We both kept our appointments. And I told myself for forty years that I had chosen correctly, which is the lie women of my generation were taught to tell so fluently we mistook it for our own handwriting."

She stopped. Breathed. When she resumed, it was in a different register—legal, deliberate, each clause laid like brick.

"Therefore I direct the following. The cottage at the bridge-head, known as the Engineer's Cottage, together with an annuity of sixty pounds yearly charged upon my estate, I give to Greta Maria Brandt, daughter of Emil Brandt, whom I hereby declare a guest of this house by my own invitation, extended before the war and honored now. Whatever her nation's quarrel with Britain, my quarrel is not with her, nor hers with me. Let no one in this parish mistake her presence here for anything less than the keeping of my word."

She fell silent for so long I thought she'd finished. Then:

"One matter more. The girl Xanthe Fairweather, who reads other people's letters and believes history happens to other people—" I looked up sharply; she was watching me with one eyebrow, the working one, arched in unmistakable amusement. "Yes, I knew. Steam wrinkles paper, child; you press your letters badly. Write the rest. —whom I have watched for three years reading my correspondence and believing herself invisible, I name witness to this testament, and I charge her with its delivery, because she has already carried everything else I ever wrote, and because history does not happen to other people. It happened to her the day she drove through the gate and decided to stay."

I could not write for a moment. The ink pooled under the nib and I blotted it with my sleeve like an amateur.

"Sentimental," I managed.

"Appallingly," she agreed. "Strike it if you like. I'll know."

* * *

On the fourth evening, the fragments aligned. She was fully lucid—sharp, impatient, alive—and she summoned us both, Greta and me, into the lamplit room. The pages of dictation lay on the coverlet in my hand, and she looked at them the way a general looks at a map of ground she cannot reach.

"There remains the difficulty," she said. "I cannot sign. Without signature it is paper. With Hale circling, paper is dangerous and worthless at once. Unless—" and here she turned her head with effort toward the wardrobe, toward the trunk beneath it where the typewriter slept under Greta's spare shawl, "—unless it is typed."

Greta went white. "Lady Ashcombe, the machine—it's Father's. The typeface, Hale will—"

"Hale already knows the typeface, or will. That battle comes later, and we shall fight it in front of witnesses, which is the only place I have ever won anything." Her left hand lifted, trembling, and reached across the blanket. "But the machine was Emil's, and the words are mine, and the hands—" she looked at me, then at Greta, then back to me, and the amusement and the gravity in her face were, for once, the same expression— "the hands will be yours, Miss Fairweather. Both of yours. Type my testament on Emil Brandt's machine. Let his letters and my words come off the same keys at last. That is not forgery. That is a duet."

The wind pressed at the windows. Downstairs a door closed; Mrs. Tregowan banking the fire, going up to bed, the ordinary sounds of a house holding its breath.

"And if I refuse?" I asked, though we both knew.

"Then you will be the woman who drove me faithfully for three years and stopped at the last mile," she said, gently, which was crueler than anything sharp she'd ever said. "And I will haunt you, and I have excellent references for haunting."

Greta was already lifting the trunk-lid. The machine emerged into the candlelight—black, scarred, patient, its keys gleaming like a small dark keyboard of promises—and Greta carried it to the bedside table with the tenderness of someone handling a relic, which is precisely what it was.

Verity arranged herself against the pillows. Her left hand crept across the blanket and closed over mine where it rested on the machine's carriage.

"Page one," she said. "And this time, child, write it exactly as I say it. History is listening."

I rolled the first sheet into the platen, and somewhere below us the tide turned against the causeway, sealing the road, sealing the village, sealing us in with what we were about to do—while upstairs, by three candles and one dying lamp, the keys of Emil Brandt's typewriter began, at last, to answer to Lady Verity Ashcombe's voice.
