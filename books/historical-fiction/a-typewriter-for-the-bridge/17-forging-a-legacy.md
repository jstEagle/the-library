## 17. Forging a Legacy

I had learned three things about English law by the third week of December, all of them from Thomas Penhale, who had a way of delivering terrible news as though it were a grammar lesson.

The first was that a will must be signed by the person whose will it was. The second was that "signed" meant more than ink on paper — it meant intent, witnessed, witnessed properly, in the presence of two persons who were not beneficiaries. The third was that my father's cottage and annuity — the deed Lady Ashcombe had drawn up eleven years ago and then locked away like a letter she could not bear to post — was worth exactly nothing without that signature, because the hand that would have signed it now lay curled and silent against her breast, and the doctors at the inn said what everyone already knew: she would not write again.

"She can speak," Thomas said, standing in the schoolhouse doorway with his hat in his hands, snow melting on his shoulders. "Fragments. Dr. Loxley says the mind is whole behind it. But Greta — the law does not accept fragments."

"Then the law," I said, "has never tried to have a conversation with her."

He almost smiled. Almost. "No. I expect it hasn't."

* * *

I carried this arithmetic back to Ashcombe House through the falling dark: one unsigned deed, one dying woman, one typewriter, one girl who had crossed a continent to claim something that existed only if someone committed a crime on her behalf.

Xanthe was waiting for me in the scullery, which had become our parliament, our confessional, and — since rationing reduced the household to turnips, barley, and optimism — our kitchen. She was mending a glove by candlelight, badly. She looked up when I came in, and I saw that she had been waiting not just for me but for an answer she had already composed.

"We're going to do it," she said. It was not quite a question.

"I don't know how," I said honestly. "She cannot hold a pen. You cannot hold a pen *for* her. Thomas explained it until my ears bled."

Xanthe put down the glove. She had that look I had begun to know and half dread — the chauffeur's look, the look of a woman accustomed to solving problems with her hands. "Greta. What did your father do for a living?"

"He built bridges. You know that."

"No." She shook her head slowly. "Think again. What did he *do*? Every day. In his study in Dresden, with you reading on the sofa pretending to do your homework."

And I understood her before I understood myself, and felt my stomach drop through the floorboards like a stone down a well.

"He typed," I said. "He typed everything. He said the pen was for poets and the machine was for men who intended to be *understood*."

"Yes." Xanthe's eyes were very bright in the candlelight, bright and slightly appalled at herself. "So we ask her. We ask Lady Ashcombe whether she wants the deed finished. If she says yes — if she *makes* it yes, with whatever voice and whatever strength she has — then we put her hand on the keys."

"That isn't signing."

"It is if she guides it. Her hand over yours over mine. Her intention, moving the machine. Not a forgery, Greta. A dictation. There is a difference, and the difference is everything, and the difference may also be nothing at all if Hale ever sees it, and I am aware of both facts simultaneously, thank you."

"You've thought about this."

"Since Tuesday," she admitted. "I haven't slept well since Tuesday."

* * *

We went up to Verity Ashcombe's room that same evening, because Xanthe said there was no sense in letting me sleep on it either, and because — though neither of us said this — the days were no longer a thing either of us trusted to still be there tomorrow.

Lady Ashcombe lay propped against pillows like a monument to herself, which is to say magnificently. The stroke had taken her left side and most of her patience but none of her authority; her right hand rested on the coverlet where it could command the room, and her good eye found us at the door and fixed us there with the expression she must once have used on Cabinet ministers.

"Well?" she said. The word came out slurred at the edges, worn smooth in the middle, but it was unmistakably a summons.

I had rehearsed nothing. I discovered this was better. I knelt by the bed — the floor was cold through my skirt, and I thought, absurdly, of my father kneeling to check the footings of some bridge, testing whether the ground would hold.

"Lady Ashcombe. My name is Greta Brandt. Emil Brandt is my father. And I have come to ask you about a promise."

Her eye moved from me to Xanthe and back. Something happened in her face — not softening exactly; settling. Like a house acknowledging its own weight.

"The cottage," she managed. Each word cost her visibly, and she spent them anyway. "And the — money. Yearly. For him. Drawn it up. Nineteen-oh-eight. Never — " Her jaw worked. "Never finished it."

"Because he never asked."

"Because I never — offered." A sound that might have been a laugh, bitter and small. "Pride. Forty years of it. Damn fool. All of it."

I took a breath. This was the part I had crossed the sea for and now nearly could not say aloud. "If you wish it finished — if you wish him to have it — there is a way. But it is not a safe way, and it is not entirely an honest-looking way, and I will not let Xanthe carry it alone. So I am asking you plainly: do you want your will done?"

The silence lasted long enough that I heard the fire settle and, far off, the tide turning under the ice. Then Verity Ashcombe lifted her right hand — slowly, tremblingly, with the effort of a woman raising a banner — and laid it flat upon the coverlet, palm up. An offering. An oath. An old suffragist's gesture; I had seen photographs of hands held up exactly so outside prison gates.

"*Yes*," she said, and the word rang off the walls like something struck.

* * *

It took us four evenings to prepare, and I will record honestly that two of those evenings were consumed by argument.

Thomas, when told — because we could hardly forge a testament without the village's only lawyer-adjacent man, and because Xanthe had decided, in her infuriating way, that secrets kept from allies were just future betrayals on layaway — objected on every ground available to a man who loved one conspirator and was beginning to love the other.

"It won't stand," he said, pacing the schoolroom between desks like a boy kept in. "A typed will, guided by three hands, witnessed by the schoolmaster and the chauffeur — Hale will tear it apart in an afternoon."

"Then it should be torn apart defending something true rather than abandoned defending nothing," Xanthe said, darning — always darning; I began to suspect the darning was how she prayed.

"And if it goes to assizes—"

"Thomas." Xanthe looked up. "You taught me to read. Well — you taught the village children, and I sat in the back because I'd already read the primers twice. Do you remember?"

He stopped pacing. "You were eight. You corrected my Latin."

"You told us law was memory made public. So make it public. Witnesses. Dates. Her thumbprint if it comes to that. Let the whole thing be so *open* that hiding becomes impossible." She returned to her darning. "Fraud needs darkness, Thomas. Fidelity doesn't."

He stood there a moment, hat turning in his hands, and I watched a man decide to be brave mostly because he had run out of sensible alternatives. "Two witnesses," he said finally. "Dr. Loxley cannot be spared from the sick and Mrs. Cobb has opinions, but Mrs. Cobb's opinions are also incorruptible. And the document must state its own circumstances — dictated, guided, witnessed — so no one can later pretend it was anything else."

"You want us to confess inside the confession," I said.

"I want," said Thomas Penhale, with the first real smile I had seen on him, "to give Inspector Hale a document so honest it insults him."

* * *

On the night itself — the twenty-second of December, the longest night of the year, which Xanthe said was fitting and I said was showing off — we assembled in Lady Ashcombe's room like a very small, very nervous church congregation.

The candles were new ones hoarded from the estate stores; Mrs. Cobb had produced them along with a plate of shortbread that constituted, by December standards, reckless extravagance. Thomas sat by the window with the deed and a fresh sheet of foolscap. Mrs. Cobb sat with her knitting, needles still, which anyone who knew Mrs. Cobb understood to be the equivalent of a magistrate's bench. Xanthe carried in the typewriter — my father's typewriter, the Adler he had carried across Belgium and France and the Channel on his daughter's behalf, the machine that contained nine years of letters between these two stubborn people — and set it on the blanket across Lady Ashcombe's knees.

She looked at it a long time.

Then she raised her right hand and touched the keys, one finger, lightly, as a blind woman touches a face.

"Emil's," she said. Just that. Her eye was wet and furious about being wet.

"Yours now," I said. "For tonight."

Here is what we did, and I set it down plainly so that no one — not Hale, not history, not I myself on some harder morning — can call it anything but what it was:

Xanthe sat at the machine. I sat beside her with the text we had drafted together, in plain words, saying everything: that Verity Mary Ashcombe, being of sound mind (and here Mrs. Cobb snorted in a manner suggesting the soundness was debatable), did declare the cottage at Marrow Quay and an annuity of forty pounds to go to Greta Brandt, daughter of Emil Brandt, in fulfillment of a promise made in 1908; that this instrument was dictated by the testatrix, who could not sign by reason of paralysis; that she guided the hand that struck each key.

And then Verity Ashcombe placed her right hand over Xanthe Fairweather's hand, and Xanthe's hand found the keys, and the old woman *typed her will*.

I will not pretend it was swift or graceful. Each letter was an expedition. When her strength failed mid-word — and it failed often — Xanthe waited, hands hovering, until the pressure resumed, because they had agreed beforehand that nothing would be struck except under Verity's own force. Twice the old woman's hand dragged and doubled a letter, and Thomas ruled, gravely, that the errors should stand, as evidence of exactly what they were. Once she pressed so hard the carriage jumped, and Mrs. Cobb said, "Steady, my lady, you'll have the thing off the rails," and Verity Ashcombe laughed — a cracked, delighted sound — and typed on.

Near the end, she gathered herself for a line none of us had drafted.

"Read — it — back," she commanded.

Thomas read it back, every clause, every error. Then Verity's hand rose again, and with Xanthe beneath it, she added a final sentence, hunting the keys one by one while the whole room held its breath:

*I HAVE WAITED TOO LONG FOR PERMISSION AND AM DONE WITH IT.*

Mrs. Cobb blew her nose with the report of a starting pistol.

Then the witnesses signed — Thomas in his careful schoolmaster's copperplate, Mrs. Cobb in a fist like a blacksmith's, each writing the date and the words "present throughout." And last, because Thomas insisted and because Verity, when it was explained, pressed her lips together in something that was almost her old smile, she inked her right thumb on the pad Thomas had brought for the purpose and pressed it below the signatures: a great purple whorl, undeniable, hers.

"There," said Lady Ashcombe. She was grey with exhaustion and shining with it too. "Filed. Under protest. To — posterity."

Xanthe folded the pages with the reverence other people reserve for flags.

* * *

Afterwards, Mrs. Cobb and Thomas went down, and Xanthe stayed to bank the fire, and I stayed because I could not yet make my legs agree to ordinary purposes. The room smelled of candlewax and shortbread and something older, like a church after a wedding.

"Greta." Xanthe spoke quietly, so as not to wake the sleeping lady. "Do you know what we actually did tonight?"

"We committed a crime," I said, "very politely, in front of witnesses."

"We delivered a letter." She straightened, and the candlelight moved on her face. "Eleven years late, but delivered. Your father used to say the pen was for poets and the machine was for men who wanted to be understood. Well. Tonight the machine was for a woman who wanted to be *obeyed*, at last, by everybody including herself." She paused. "I think it may be the truest document I shall ever touch."

I meant to say something equal to it. What came out was: "Your stitching is crooked," gesturing at the glove on her lap, and she laughed — really laughed, tired and warm — and threw the glove at me, and I caught it, and somewhere downstairs the house settled around its new secret like snow around a lamp.

It was past midnight when I drew back the curtain to check the weather and saw, instead, a figure crossing the inn yard below through the moonlit snow: a tall man, coat collared high, walking with a lantern held at the precise height of a man reading a document.

Inspector Hale, coming home late from the mainland mail boat, a satchel under his arm.

He paused in the yard. He looked up — not at my window, I told myself; at the lit window of Lady Ashcombe's room, where the candles still burned past midnight above a house that ought by rights to have been asleep.

Then he went inside out of the cold, unhurried, and the light in his own window came on, and stayed on, a long time.
