## 18. Discovery

Inspector Edwin Hale had a theory about paper.

People believed that secrets lived in locked drawers, in whispered conversations behind closed doors, in the glances exchanged across a parish hall. But Hale knew better. Secrets lived in paper, and paper was patient. Paper waited in files and folios and biscuit tins, and if a man was willing to read enough of it—and Hale was willing to read all of it—then sooner or later the paper confessed.

He had been at Marrow Quay since November, billeted at the Anchor & Hope under an arrangement Mrs. Tregowan described as "temporary" with the particular firmness of a woman who intended it to be permanent, because permanent meant regular payment. His room overlooked the harbour, which froze in interesting geometric patterns and offered him nothing else. He had catalogued forty-one residents, two registered aliens (both elderly, both harmless, both German-born bakers whose loyalties were to their ovens), one deserter who had turned out to be merely a man with a stammer who avoided conversation, and a quantity of loose talk sufficient to fill three notebooks.

And he had a file on Ashcombe House that would not close.

It was not anything he could name. That was what troubled him. A good investigation was like a good bridge—every element bearing its proper load, nothing hanging in air. But the Ashcombe file hung in air. The household's "Belgian refugee" had arrived without papers, without identity documents, without so much as a ration card issued anywhere Hale could trace. Her French, when she spoke it—which was rarely, and never when Hale was within earshot—had an accent he could not place. She worked in the kitchen garden like a woman who had done such work before, efficiently and without complaint, and she kept her eyes down in the village, which Hale had learned over twenty years of service was not the posture of innocence but the discipline of the watched.

He had written to Kent. He had written to the alien registration bureaux, to the Belgian relief committees, to every office whose stamp might conceivably appear on a refugee's papers. The replies came back on grey forms, and they said *no record*, and *no record*, and *no such person entered*.

Then, in the second week of January, a reply came from the detention depot at Knockaloe that did not say no.

*Re: Brandt, Emil Gustav, engineer, aged 61, interned under D.O.R.A. Reg. 14. Effects inventoried upon admission per standing order. Inventory enclosed. Note: item 7, portable typewriter (Continental, c. 1909), reported missing from effects prior to transfer. Daughter, Greta Elisabeth Brandt, whereabouts unknown; last address Hamburg; presumed attempting passage. Any information to be forwarded forthwith.*

Hale sat for a long while with the inventory in his cold room above the harbour, reading item 7 until the words lost their shape and reformed into something else entirely.

A typewriter. Missing from the effects of a detained German national. And somewhere in his notebooks—because Hale wrote everything down, because paper was patient—there was a note he had made in December, half a sentence really, filed away and almost forgotten: *Ashcombe servant reports household typewriter, foreign make, used by Lady A. for correspondence. Machine reportedly brought by "refugee."*

Brought by the refugee.

He got out the notebook and found the entry. He had noted it idly at the time, one domestic detail among hundreds, the kind of thing a housekeeper says when asked what furniture is in a room. Now it stood up off the page and looked at him.

Hale was not a dramatic man. His superiors had twice passed him over for promotion on precisely those grounds—that he lacked flair, that he proceeded rather than pounced. He considered this the highest compliment available. Flair broke cases. Procedure finished them.

So he did not ride out to Ashcombe House that afternoon. He put on his coat, walked down to the inn, ate Mrs. Tregowan's mutton stew without tasting it, and spent the evening doing what he always did: writing letters. To the depot at Knockaloe, requesting a specimen of Emil Brandt's typing—a letter, a petition, anything the man had produced on the machine before detention. To the Home Office, requesting authority to search premises at Ashcombe House. To the county constabulary, requesting an escort, because a man did not serve papers alone on a great lady's household, whatever the season, whatever the tide.

The tide. That was the other thing about Marrow Quay. The causeway governed everything here like a slow god. Twice a day the road went under, and the village became an island, and whatever you began had to be finished before the water decided otherwise. Hale had come to respect the causeway the way one respects weather or Parliament—not affectionately, but absolutely.

The specimen letter from Knockaloe arrived nine days later, along with his warrant.

He spread the two documents side by side on the little desk beneath the window, where the light was best even though the light was only grey.

The specimen was a petition Brandt had written in October, pleading for release on grounds of age and health. It was typed, not handwritten—the man had evidently been allowed his machine briefly before the inventory—and it was signed in a careful, old-fashioned hand: *Emil G. Brandt, Ingenieur.*

Hale took out his magnifying glass, which he kept wrapped in chamois leather like a relic, and bent over the pages.

Type is like handwriting, only more honest. A pen trembles with mood; type trembles only with the machine itself—with worn keys, with misaligned hammers, with the small injuries of long use. Every machine has its signature. The *e* sits a hair high. The *t* strikes faintly on the left arm. The capital *G* carries a nick, a tiny white scar in the ink where the metal has been chipped, perhaps by a fall, perhaps by decades of use, and it prints that scar faithfully on every page forever after.

The petition's capital *G* carried a scar.

Hale set the magnifying glass down. His hands were steady—he prided himself on steady hands—but something behind his sternum had begun, very quietly, to ache. It was not triumph. He had felt triumph before, small clean doses of it, and this was not that. This was closer to what he imagined a doctor feels when the symptoms assemble themselves into the diagnosis he has dreaded: satisfaction and sorrow arriving in the same envelope, addressed to him.

Because he had met the girl. That was the trouble with small places. In London he could have built this case in a filing cabinet and never once seen the face attached to it. Here he had seen her. He had seen her at the jumble sale, standing at the mending stall with her hands full of someone's darned stockings, laughing at something the Fairweather woman had said—a real laugh, brief, quickly suppressed, but real. He had seen her in the churchyard, laying cut evergreen on a grave that was not, by any record he could find, a relation's. He had seen her bow her head under the village's stares with a composure that was not servile and not proud but simply enduring, the way a post endures.

She was the daughter. Almost certainly, she was the daughter. And the daughter of an interned enemy alien had no right to be living unregistered in a private household under a false nationality, in possession—or in proximity to possession—of her father's property, which had been inventoried and ought to have been impounded.

And there was more. There was the matter that had been nagging at the edge of the file for weeks, the thing he had circled and not landed on. Lady Ashcombe had suffered her stroke in December, and since then no document of hers had surfaced—not a will, not a codicil, nothing executed in those strange weeks when a formidable woman's affairs should have been settled in a flurry of solicitors and signatures. For a woman of Lady Ashcombe's means and notorious precision, the silence was unnatural.

Paper was patient. But paper could also be manufactured. And a typewriter, Hale thought, looking at the scarred capital G, was a very convenient instrument for manufacturing paper.

* * *

Constable Pengelly was nineteen years old, freckled, earnest, and terrified of Lady Ashcombe, whom he had known all his life as a small girl who once sold him a hamster and now as a legend who had once, according to his mother, stared down a government minister until the man apologized. Hale chose him anyway, partly because he was the only available constable and partly because terror, in Hale's experience, made young men punctual.

They rode out on the morning of the twenty-second of January, a hard bright day, the marsh iron-grey and glittering, the tide low enough to leave the causeway glistening and exposed like the spine of some enormous sleeping animal. Frost furred the hedgerows. Somewhere inland a cockerel was announcing the morning to an audience of crows.

"You'll do the talking, sir," Pengelly said. It was not a question.

"I'll do the talking."

"Only she's—" He stopped, started again. "Lady Ashcombe can't speak now, sir. Since her stroke. So I suppose it won't be her."

"Who answers the door, then?"

"The Fairweather woman. Miss Xanthe. She drives for her ladyship. Well. Drove." Pengelly glanced sideways. "You've spoken to her before, sir."

"I have," said Hale.

He had indeed. Three times, formally, in the parlour at Ashcombe House, with tea he did not want and questions she answered with a smoothness that had begun, by the third interview, to feel less like smoothness than like lacquer—layer upon layer applied carefully over something, he could not say what. She was not a liar. That was what unsettled him. Liars were easy; liars overreached, embellished, reached for details they hadn't been given. Miss Fairweather did none of that. She gave him exactly as much truth as his question required and not one grain more, and she did it while looking him directly in the eye, and she poured his tea correctly, and once—only once, and he might have imagined it—she had smiled at some private thought in the middle of her own careful answer, as if the whole performance amused some part of her that he was not permitted to meet.

He had written in his notebook, after the third interview: *Miss F. loyal, intelligent, protecting something or someone. Determine object of protection.*

The notebook knew. The notebook had known for weeks. It was the rest of the world that had been slow.

They came up through the parkland, past the bare elms, and Ashcombe House appeared the way it always appeared to Hale: not grand, exactly, but settled—settled into its ground the way a stone settles into a streambed, with the certainty of centuries. Smoke rose from the kitchen chimney. A wren scolded them from the gatepost. Everything about the place announced ordinary life going on in an orderly fashion, and everything about the place, to Hale's eye now, was evidence.

Pengelly rang the bell. From inside came the sound of footsteps, unhurried, and Hale arranged his face into the expression he wore for all reckonings: polite, apologetic, immovable.

The door opened. Miss Fairweather stood in it, dressed in her dark driving coat though there was no car to drive and had not been since autumn, her hands folded, her chin level. Behind her, in the dim hall, Hale caught movement—a skirt vanishing around the stairwell, quick and quiet—and he noted it without appearing to note it, the way he did everything.

"Inspector Hale," she said. "And Constable Pengelly. Good morning. Is this about the ration returns? Because I've told the requisition officer we've given up our sugar entirely, and I have it in writing—"

"It isn't about sugar, miss." He produced the warrant and held it out. "I have a warrant to search these premises. For a typewriter."

The pause lasted perhaps a second and a half. Later, testifying to himself in the privacy of his own mind, Hale would swear it lasted longer—that in that second and a half he saw everything move behind her eyes at once: the calculation, the fear, the fury, and underneath all of it something that looked almost like relief, as though a woman who had been holding a door shut against a storm had finally heard the storm arrive and could stop pushing.

"A typewriter," she repeated.

"A Continental portable, circa 1909, with a chipped capital G. Property of a detained person. Reported missing." He stepped forward, gently, and she stepped back, and he crossed the threshold into the hall that smelled of beeswax and woodsmoke and last night's fire. "I'm sorry, Miss Fairweather. Truly. I suspect you may be the least guilty person in this house. But I find that doesn't tend to matter."

From the back of the hall, from the direction of the drawing room, came a sound Hale had not heard before and would remember all his life: the sharp, imperative double-rap of a stick on the floor. Once. Twice. Insistent.

Lady Ashcombe wanted to see who had come.

And from the stairs above, where she had gone instead of running, came the sound of a second pair of feet, stopping—caught between flight and loyalty—and then, deliberately, descending.

Miss Fairweather squared her shoulders. "Well then, Inspector," she said, in the voice of a woman who has driven through worse weather than this, "you'd better come into the drawing room. Both of you. And I'll thank you to wipe your boots—the floor's just been done."

It was, Hale reflected, following her down the hall toward the sound of the tapping stick, exactly what he would have written in his notebook, if he had had time: *Subject composed. Subjects converging. Everyone intends to be present.*

Which meant, though he did not yet know how, that everyone intended to fight.

He wiped his boots thoroughly on the mat. It seemed only courteous. Whatever happened next deserved a fair start.
