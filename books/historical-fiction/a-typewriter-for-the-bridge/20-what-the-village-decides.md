## 20. What the Village Decides

Thomas Penhale had taught arithmetic to the children of Marrow Quay for eleven years, and in all that time he had never once seen the schoolroom so full on a weekday morning that people were standing along the back wall with their hats in their hands like churchgoers at a funeral.

The magistrate had commandeered it because it was the only room in the village large enough to hold a hearing and small enough to feel like one. The blackboard still bore yesterday's lesson — *If 6 men can dig 4 yards of trench in a day, how long for 40 yards?* — and Thomas, standing near the stove with his arms folded to keep his hands from betraying him, found himself staring at it and thinking: *the war asks this question every hour of every day, and nobody has worked out the answer.*

At the front of the room sat Magistrate Cobbett, who was also the churchwarden and also, by ancient arrangement nobody remembered consenting to, the keeper of the parish register. He was seventy-three years old and had the face of a man perpetually listening for a bell he half hoped would not ring. Beside him, upright and immaculate, sat Inspector Hale with his file open before him like a hymn book, squared paper, neat columns, everything accounted for except, Thomas thought, the human beings the columns referred to.

And in the middle of the room, alone on two chairs pushed together, sat Miss Greta Brandt, her hands folded in her lap, her chin up, looking for all the world as though she had been asked to recite and was merely waiting to be told which verse.

Behind her, in the second row, sat Lady Verity Ashcombe in her wheeled chair, a rug over her knees and Mrs. Tregenna the cook stationed behind her like a fortress wall. Verity's left side had not come back to her since the stroke, but her right hand gripped the arm of her chair, and her eyes went around the room once, slowly, the way she used to survey a hall full of hostile questioners before a suffrage meeting — *I have faced worse crowds than you,* said the look, *and I have outlasted every one.*

Xanthe Fairweather stood at the back beside Thomas. She was not called yet. She looked, he thought, like a woman holding her breath in a house where all the doors have been locked from outside.

"Right," said Cobbett, and rapped the table with his knuckles, having forgotten or never owned a gavel. "This is an inquiry into the status of the person calling herself—" he consulted a paper, winced slightly, "—Greta Brandt. Inspector Hale has brought matters to my attention. I'll hear him first."

Hale rose. He was not a tall man but he stood as though height were a matter of posture rather than inches, and Thomas had to grant him this: he did not enjoy what he was doing. That was almost worse. A cruel man you could hate; a dutiful man you could only dread.

"I'll be brief," Hale said, and was true to his word, which was somehow the most frightening thing about him. A German national's daughter, arrived under a false nationality. A typewriter belonging to a detained enemy alien, found in her possession. A testament typed on that machine, executed under circumstances no solicitor had witnessed. "I have no doubt these women believe they acted kindly," he concluded. "Kindness is not the law. The law exists precisely for the days when kindness and safety pull in different directions."

He sat down. There was a silence with a great deal of weather in it.

Cobbett cleared his throat. "Miss Brandt. You understand why you're here?"

"Yes, sir." Her English was careful and accented, each word set down like a stone crossing a stream. "I am here because of the war, which I did not make, and because of papers, which are not the same thing as a person."

A ripple went down the room. Someone at the back — Thomas was fairly sure it was Dan Hosking the crabber — made a sound that might have been a cough or might have been the beginning of a laugh, strangled in the egg.

Cobbett rubbed his nose. "Quite," he said, in the tone of a man who suspects he is being outmaneuvered by a foreigner and cannot decide whether to mind.

Hale leaned forward. "Miss Brandt. Your father, Emil Brandt, was arrested in November as an enemy alien under the Defence of the Realm Act. Is that correct?"

"It is correct that he was arrested."

"You do not dispute that he is German?"

"He was German the way I am German, sir. It is where we were born. My father built your bridge." She turned, and her finger indicated — through the frosted window, past the yard, toward the grey line of the causeway where the tide was coming in over forty years of his mathematics. "He loved this coast more than any German ever loved anything, if love can be measured in drawings. There are four hundred of them in my trunk. Every bolt. Every stone. He drew your bridge on our honeymoon, sir, from memory, because my mother complained he drew it at the wedding breakfast."

The laugh got out this time. Dan Hosking gave up entirely and laughed like a gull, and half the room followed before Cobbett banged the table again.

Hale waited, patient as tide. "And the typewriter?"

"My father's. He sent me with it because it was the only thing of his I could carry, and because—" she hesitated, and here her composure moved for the first time, a hairline crack, "—because there were letters inside it. From Lady Ashcombe. Letters I was told to read only if I needed to understand something I could not otherwise understand."

"And did you need to?"

"Yes," said Greta simply. "I needed to know who I was walking toward."

Thomas saw Xanthe's head dip slightly at that — a nod so small it might have been a trick of the lamplight, but he knew her, had known her for three winters of stolen glances across the schoolyard gate, and he knew when she was agreeing with someone from the bottom of herself.

"The testament," Hale pressed on, gently now, which was his most dangerous register. "Lady Ashcombe cannot sign her name. The document bears no signature. Only a thumbprint, added after the fact, in front of witnesses who all have an interest in its contents. You must see the difficulty."

"I see it," said Greta. "But sir — my father always said, a bridge is not the stones. A bridge is whether people decide to cross." 

*She's been talking to Xanthe,* Thomas thought, absurdly, *or Xanthe to her,* because that was exactly the sort of sentence Xanthe would carry home like a shell from the beach and turn over all night.

Cobbett took off his spectacles and polished them on his sleeve, a manoeuvre Thomas recognized from a hundred parish meetings as the prelude to either wisdom or retreat. Before he could choose, however, the door at the back opened, letting in a knife of cold air and Mrs. Penruddock the postmistress, who entered with the unhurried confidence of a woman who reads everyone's business professionally and considered this hearing a natural extension of her rounds.

"Beg pardon, Mr. Cobbett," she said, not begging pardon at all. "I couldn't hear properly from the passage."

"You're not required to hear at all, Mrs. Penruddock."

"No, sir," she agreed, and planted herself against the wall beside Dan Hosking.

Cobbett sighed the sigh of a man surrendering a position he had never really held. He looked around the room. "Does anyone wish to speak to the character of this young woman? Properly speaking I ought to confine myself to the documents, but—" and here he glanced at Hale's neat file with something that was very nearly distaste, "—documents don't live here. People do. Very well. Who'll speak?"

There was a pause. Thomas understood the pause. Speaking up in February 1915, with the casualty lists in the church porch growing weekly, meant declaring yourself, and declaration was not a habit the village indulged.

It was Mrs. Tregenna who broke it. She came forward from behind Verity's chair with the rolling gait of a woman who had been carrying heavy things since before the magistrate was born, and she planted herself in front of the table.

"She's worked in my kitchen garden since October," she announced, in the voice she used for announcing that dinner was ready and would be eaten whether you liked it or not. "Turned ground that hadn't been turned since the old rector died. Learned to salt beans. Never once took the biggest potato off her own plate, which is more than I can say for some Christians present."

"We're aware of your views on the congregation, Mrs. Tregenna," said Cobbett.

"They're accurate views, Mr. Cobbett. And another thing." She pointed a flour-dusted finger at Hale. "That girl sat up four nights running when Lady Ashcombe had her stroke. Turned her, fed her, read to her. Read to her, mind, while certain persons were writing letters to the Home Office." She did not look at anyone in particular, which in Marrow Quay was the loudest way possible of looking at somebody in particular.

"Thank you, Mrs. Tregenna," said Cobbett. "Anyone else?"

Dan Hosking shuffled forward, cap in both fists. "I'll say a word." He cleared his throat, embarrassed. "My lad Tom's at the front, and I'll not pretend I haven't wondered — a German among us, and my boy facing Germans. I wondered it out loud, too, in the Anchor, and I'm not proud of it." He turned to Greta. "But then my nets tore in December, and I'm sixty-one and my shoulders are shot, and this girl mended them. Four nights, by lantern, net-mending being work my own mother did and I never learned. She'd never touched a net before. She watched me two days and then did it better than me." He shrugged, defeated by his own evidence. "You don't learn that quick unless you mean well. That's all I've got. Nets don't lie."

"Nets don't lie," repeated Cobbett, writing it down, and Thomas saw the corner of Hale's mouth tighten at finding his meticulous file quoted back to him in the idiom of fishing tackle.

Then Mrs. Penruddock stepped forward unbidden, which made even Cobbett sit straighter, because the postmistress volunteering testimony was like the tide volunteering to come in twice.

"I'll speak to the letters," she said. "Being as letters are my trade. Since October, that girl has walked to my window every Tuesday and Friday with the household post, and she waits while I frank it, and she thanks me, and in all that time she has never once — never once — tried to read over my shoulder. Do you know how rare that is, Mr. Cobbett? Half this village would read the Lord's Prayer upside-down given half a chance, and have done." Scattered laughter, nervous but real. "A person who respects other people's letters has a straight back, whatever name's on her passport."

*Oh, Xanthe,* Thomas thought, glancing sideways, *she doesn't know what she just said* — because the irony of defending Greta Brandt's epistolary discretion while Xanthe Fairweather, professional steamer-open of correspondence, stood six feet away was almost too much for a schoolmaster's composure. But Xanthe's face was perfectly grave, and only her eyes, meeting his for half a second, admitted they were both thinking it.

More voices now, the dam gone. The butcher's wife vouched for Greta's patience with the queue. Young Mrs. Curnow testified that Greta had sat with her through the worst night of her baby's croup and had sung to the child — "in German, mind, and the baby didn't object" — which raised a laugh that had grief underneath it, because everyone in that room had lost someone to a war started by men in rooms far from any sea.

And then, from the second row, Verity Ashcombe lifted her good hand, and the room went quiet the way water goes flat.

She could not speak reliably. Everyone knew it; the stroke had taken her words and scattered them like dropped change. But she gestured — imperiously, unmistakably — until Mrs. Tregenna wheeled her forward to the table, and then she reached into the basket on her chair and produced a sheet of paper, typed, and laid it before Cobbett with the flourish of a queen presenting terms.

Cobbett read it. His eyebrows climbed. He read it again. Then, in the manner of a man deciding that if he must drown he will drown with dignity, he began to read aloud:

"'To the inquiry concerning Greta Brandt. I, Verity Ashcombe, being of sound mind and imperfect body, state the following. In 1908 I promised sanctuary at Ashcombe House to Emil Brandt and his family, in writing, in letters he kept for six years. I have spent fifty years fighting for the principle that a promise is worth more than the fear that argues against it. I do not intend to be defeated by my own principles in my own parish. Greta Brandt is the daughter of the finest engineer this coast has ever employed and the only man whose arguments I ever enjoyed losing. Register her as what she is: my guest, my debt, and my heir. Any further questions may be addressed to me in writing, as I am told I now compose better than I speak.'"

He stopped. The room made a sound — not applause, Marrow Quay didn't applaud, but a collective exhale, the sound of a village deciding something all at once and together, the way a shoal turns.

Hale stood. "Magistrate. With respect. A document dictated by a paralyzed woman, typed by her employee, presented by the interested party—"

"With respect, Inspector," said Cobbett, and something in his voice suggested respect was about to be stretched to its legal limits, "you've given us your file, and it's a tidy file. But I've lived in this parish seventy-three years, and I've heard from every soul in it this morning, and not one of them — not the crabber, not the cook, not the postmistress, not the widow Curnow — has said a word against this girl, and several of them have said things for her that I'd take to the assizes. Meanwhile your file says 'no Belgian papers,' which I'll observe is a strange crime for a girl who never claimed to be Belgian in front of anyone but yourselves." He folded his spectacles. "The Defence of the Realm Act lets me register aliens. It does not oblige me to call a young woman an enemy because her father was born on the wrong side of a map. Greta Brandt will be entered in the parish register as a ward of Marrow Quay — labor registered, movements reported to me fortnightly, and if the Home Office cares to overrule the entire village of Marrow Quay, they're welcome to send someone down to explain to Mrs. Penruddock why her franking is insufficient."

Mrs. Penruddock, addressed directly, looked as though she might faint from joy.

"Greta Brandt," said Cobbett, "stand up."

Greta stood. Her composure, which had held through accusation and testimony alike, finally broke — not dramatically, just a trembling of the chin, tears she refused quite successfully to let fall, hands pressed flat to her skirt.

"Do you promise to keep the peace, keep the register honest, and give the parish no cause to regret this?"

"I promise," Greta said. "In English, and I will learn to say it in your way too."

"Then that's the matter settled, pending the Home Office making fools of themselves." Cobbett rapped the table. "Hearing closed. And somebody open a window before we all suffocate."

* * *

Afterward the room emptied slowly, the way a congregation empties when nobody wants the sermon to have been the end of it. Villagers paused by Greta's chair to touch her shoulder, say nothing much, and move on — the English liturgy of sympathy. Dan Hosking shook her hand as though closing a business deal. Mrs. Curnow cried a little and pretended it was the cold.

Hale packed his file with unhurried precision. As he passed Thomas, he paused.

"You teach the village children, Mr. Penhale?"

"I do."

"A pity," Hale said, and there was something in it that was not quite threat and not quite envy — something lonelier than either. "You have a remarkable community here. In my experience, communities like this are usually hiding something. Sometimes," he added, buckling his case, "what they're hiding is only kindness. But a man can't build a career on that." He nodded, polite as frost, and went out into the grey.

Thomas let out a breath he felt he'd been holding since November.

Xanthe was still at the back, watching Greta being embraced by a roomful of people who eight weeks ago had crossed the street to avoid her. Something in Xanthe's face was undone and remade at once, and Thomas — who had rehearsed a hundred conversations with her and delivered approximately none of them — found his feet carrying him over before his courage could file an objection.

"Well," he said. "Forty yards of trench, six men a day. I never could make the class see why we'd want the trench at all."

Xanthe blinked, then laughed — really laughed, the first he'd heard from her since the stroke, bright and startled out of her. "Mr. Penhale, that is the worst congratulation I have ever received, and I have been congratulated by Lady Ashcombe, which is its own genre."

"Miss Fairweather," Thomas said, and his heart was going like the school bell, "there's something I've been meaning to ask you. All winter, as it happens. Possibly longer, if we're counting honestly, and I find I'd like to start counting honestly."

But before she could answer — before he could even discover whether her expression was hope or alarm — Mrs. Tregenna came barreling across the room with a face like thunder wrapped in triumph, saying, "Come quick, both of you. It's Lady Ashcombe. She's trying to say something, and I think — I think it's about the bridge."
