## 21. Spring Tide

The bridge reopened on a Tuesday, which Xanthe thought was fitting. Great events, in her experience, preferred to arrive on Tuesdays and Thursdays, when nobody had the presence of mind to meet them properly. The workmen had pulled their barricades on Monday evening and gone off to the Crown for pints, and by Tuesday morning the causeway stood bare and gleaming at low tide, its stones scrubbed pale by a winter's worth of salt, looking—Xanthe thought—rather pleased with itself. Like an old dog that has been to the vet and cannot understand why everyone keeps touching it.

The first mail came up from the mainland at eleven o'clock: four letters, two parcels, and a newspaper folded around a fish so the smell would not offend. Thomas Penhale carried the sack himself, having appointed himself unofficial postmaster of Marrow Quay sometime in February, and he came into the Ashcombe kitchen with his cap in his hand and his ears red from the wind.

"Nothing from the Home Office," he said, before anyone could ask. He said it the way a man announces that the sea has declined to come indoors. "Which I take as a good sign."

"Take it as whatever you like," said Mrs. Tredinnick, who was kneading bread with the violence of a woman settling old scores against flour. "But put the letters down first."

There were three for Verity—one from her solicitor in Truro, one from a woman whose name Xanthe recognized from the suffrage correspondence, and one with no return address at all, only a postmark she did not recognize and handwriting that made her stomach drop before she had even turned it over.

She knew the hand. She had read enough of Emil Brandt's letters, there in the lamplight of the writing room, to know it anywhere: the careful upright script of a man who had learned English from grammar books and loved it like a second sweetheart.

She took the letters up to Verity's room without opening any of them. That was the arrangement now, since the hearing—no more steaming envelopes over the kettle, no more reading by stolen lamplight. Verity, half-paralyzed and fiercely lucid in her fragments, had decreed that Xanthe should read aloud whatever came, and they should decide together what deserved an answer. It was, Xanthe reflected, the strangest promotion of her working life: from chauffeur to reader to, apparently, co-author of a baroness's correspondence.

Verity was propped against her pillows looking out at the water, wrapped in a shawl the color of wet slate. Greta sat at the window mending one of Verity's blouses, her needle moving with the patience of someone who had learned, over a winter, that waiting is also a form of work.

"The mail," Xanthe said. "Three letters."

"Greta's needle," Verity observed, in her slurred but imperious way, "has stopped."

Greta looked down at her own hands as though they had betrayed her. They had. Xanthe saw why a moment later, when she slit the unmarked envelope with the letter knife and unfolded the page. The handwriting was not Emil Brandt's after all. It was a stranger's—the round, official script of a clerk—and the heading at the top named an internment camp on the Isle of Man.

Xanthe read it standing, because sitting down seemed suddenly impossible.

It was brief. Such letters are always brief; grief arrives by government post in fewer than a hundred words. Mr. Emil Brandt, it said. Detained under the relevant regulations. Died of pneumonia on the 4th of March. Every effort was made. The remains have been interred locally. The camp authorities extend their condolences.

*Every effort was made.* Xanthe thought of the phrase the way you think of a stone you have picked up on a beach—turning it over, finding it cold on both sides.

"He is dead," Greta said. Not a question. She had read the shape of the letter in Xanthe's face before a word was spoken aloud. Her needle slipped from her fingers and rolled along the floorboards and stopped against the wainscot, and she let it lie.

"Greta—" Xanthe began.

"No." Greta held up a hand. She rose, crossed to the window, and stood with her back to them both, looking down at the causeway where the tide was beginning to turn, the water sliding in over the stones like something returning home after a long absence. "No, don't. Let me be a moment. Only a moment."

Verity reached out with her good hand and gripped the coverlet. "Read it again," she commanded. "All of it. Slowly."

So Xanthe read it again, slowly, every bureaucratic syllable, while Greta stood at the window and did not turn around, and the tide crept up the causeway below, and somewhere in the village a dog began barking at gulls with the wholehearted commitment of a creature who had never once considered history.

When Xanthe finished, Greta spoke to the window rather than to them.

"He wrote to me every week. From the camp. Did you know that? Every week, even when they limited his paper. Little letters, half a page, always about the same things. The birds he could see. The other men—there was a violinist, he wrote about the violinist constantly, as if a man imprisoned behind wire could still conduct an orchestra if only he described it well enough." Her voice caught, steadied. "He never once wrote *I am afraid*. Not once. And I used to think—how brave. How German and brave. But now I think perhaps he was simply saving me the postage of worrying."

"Greta." Xanthe crossed the room and stood beside her at the window. Below, the sea had nearly covered the causeway; the bridge had opened and closed within the space of an hour, and the world beyond was already cut off again until the tide turned. It seemed suddenly very funny and very terrible, the way the water did that, deciding for everyone who could come and go.

"I am not sorry," Greta said.

Xanthe went still.

"Do not look at me like that. Listen." Greta turned to face her, and her eyes were wet but her chin was level, and Xanthe thought: *she is her father's daughter, she has his exact stubbornness, she has carried it across a war like luggage.* "If he had lived—if he had been released when this stupid war ends and come here—he would have arrived with his hat and his grammar books and his terrible jokes about English weather, and he would have spent the rest of his life being *grateful*. Grateful to Lady Ashcombe, grateful to the magistrate, grateful to England for permitting him to exist. He would have shrunk. I watched men shrink, in the last years in Germany. My father was not a shrinking man. He was a man who built bridges." Her breath hitched. "Now he will always be a man who built bridges. That is how the village remembers him. That is how *you* remember him. You never saw him diminished. I am grieving, Xanthe, do not mistake me—I am grieving so hard I can hardly stand—but underneath it, underneath everything, I am *grateful*, because you all kept him large."

Verity made a sound from the bed—not quite a word. Greta went to her at once, kneeling so that her face was level with the baroness's.

"You kept your promise," Greta said. "Both of you did. He knew it. His last letter—his actual last letter, the one that came three weeks ago—you remember, the one about the violinist—he ended it with a line in English. Just one line. *Tell V. the tide always turns.*" She pressed Verity's good hand between both of hers. "He meant you. He meant all of this. He knew."

Verity's eyes were bright and furious and full. "Stubborn man," she managed, each word dragged up like a bucket from a deep well. "Never once. In thirty years. Never once said—" She stopped, out of air or out of permission.

"Out loud," Greta supplied gently. "No. Neither did you. Some things are said better in bridges."

* * *

They buried nothing, because there was nothing to bury in Cornwall; Emil Brandt lay under a Manx sky in a plot the government had assigned him. But the village decided—without being asked, without being told, in the way of villages—that something must be done, and so on the following Sunday the church bell rang thirty times, once for each year of his acquaintance with the parish, though half the ringers could not have said precisely what they were counting. Mrs. Tredinnick baked bread and sent it up to the house with a note that said only, *For the family,* and Xanthe, reading the note, understood that the word *family* had been redefined over the winter by common consent, and that no one would ever bother to announce it.

Greta wore black. She stood in the kitchen garden in the thin spring sun and pulled weeds with a steadiness that frightened Xanthe a little, until Xanthe went out and knelt beside her in the dirt and said nothing at all, which was the correct thing, and Greta leaned sideways until their shoulders touched, which was the correct answer.

"He would have liked the bell," Greta said eventually. "He would have counted the rings and found one wrong and told everyone about it for a decade."

"He sounds insufferable," Xanthe said.

"He was entirely insufferable. It was his finest quality." A pause. A weed surrendered. "You loved him too. Don't deny it. You read his letters for years. You know a man through his letters better than through his face. His face lies; ink doesn't."

"I never met him," Xanthe said. "And I loved him anyway. So yes. Probably."

* * *

Verity died on the first of May.

She did not die dramatically, which would have disappointed her profoundly, and she would have been the first to say so. There was no second stroke, no final campaign, no last speech delivered from the bed with the window open to the sea. She simply grew quieter through April, the way the tide pools grow quieter as summer comes on, and one warm evening after supper she asked to have her chair moved to the window, and asked Xanthe to read to her—not the correspondence, not the movement's letters, but a novel, something frivolous, because, she said, with tremendous effort and tremendous dignity, "I have earned the right to die having finished a silly book."

She finished the silly book. She died two days later, in her sleep, with the window open and the curtain breathing in and out like something alive, and when Xanthe came in with the morning tea she knew at once, from the quality of the silence, and stood for a long moment in the doorway holding a tray that no longer had anywhere to go.

Greta found her there and took the tray from her hands without a word, because that was the kind of friendship the winter had built—wordless, load-bearing—and then she put her arms around Xanthe and let her cry, which Xanthe did, thoroughly and undignifiedly, into the shoulder of a blouse she herself had helped mend.

"She'd have hated the crying," Xanthe managed.

"She'd have hated it enormously. Cry harder. It's what she'd have wanted."

They buried Verity Ashcombe on a bright cold morning in the churchyard above the quay, and the entire village came, and the bell rang, and Thomas Penhale read the lesson in a voice that only shook twice. The suffragists sent wreaths from London and Manchester and one from as far as Edinburgh, and the vicar, who had disagreed with Lady Ashcombe on approximately every subject available to disagreement, preached a sermon in which he called her *a thorn in the side of this parish for forty years, and thank God for it,* which got a laugh even from the mourners and would, Xanthe felt certain, have pleased Verity more than any eulogy.

At the graveside, as the earth went in, Greta took Xanthe's arm.

"There is a letter," she said quietly. "In the writing case. He left instructions with me—last autumn, before I came. *When V. dies, give Xanthe the letter.* He said you'd know which one."

* * *

That evening, Xanthe climbed to the writing room alone. The house had gone quiet in the particular way houses go quiet after a funeral, every floorboard creaking like a held breath released. On the desk sat Emil Brandt's typewriter, and beside it the locked writing case, which Greta had unlocked for her downstairs and then, with great delicacy, refused to accompany her further.

Inside the case, beneath the deed and the testament and the bundle of letters spanning thirty years, lay a single envelope addressed in that careful upright hand: *For Miss Fairweather, who reads.*

Xanthe sat down. She held the letter a long moment without opening it, feeling the weight of it, feeling—strangely—seen, the way she had felt seen at the hearing, standing before the whole village claiming Verity's voice as her own.

Then she opened it.

*Miss Fairweather,* the letter began. *You do not know me, and I know you entirely. V. has told me about you in every letter these six years—the girl who drives her, the girl who reads. She thinks she hides you from me. She hides nothing; she is constitutionally incapable of it. I know that you read her correspondence, because I know that she lets you, because I know that she trusts you with everything and pretends otherwise, because pride is the last coat she will ever take off.*

*I am writing this to be given to you after she dies, which means that if you are reading it, we are both of us bereaved, and I am sorry for it, and I hope the village is being kind to you. They were kind to me once, in 1908, before the world organized itself into enemies.*

*Xanthe Fairweather, I want to tell you what I told her that summer, on the unfinished bridge. She believed history belonged to other people—to parliaments, to newspapers, to men in rooms. I told her: history is made by whoever is standing nearest when it happens, and writes itself down afterwards in whatever hand is available. You have spent years believing yourself a spectator. You are not. You drove the car. You read the letters. When it mattered, you typed. There is no such thing as a bystander with clean hands; there are only hands, and what they choose to hold.*

*Keep the machine. Keep the archive. Write it down—all of it, the promise and the forgery and the thumbprint and the village that chose mercy. Someone must, and it should be someone who knows that history happened to her.*

*With respect and gratitude, and apologies for the state of my English, which V. says I never improved—*

*Emil Brandt*

Xanthe read it twice. Then she sat in the lamplight with the letter in her lap and looked at the typewriter, and at the window beyond it where the tide had turned and the causeway was emerging stone by stone from the sea, gleaming in the moonlight like something being handed back.

Downstairs, a door closed softly. Footsteps on the stair—unhurried, familiar, pausing at the landing as they always paused, as though the house itself expected them.

Xanthe folded the letter, set it inside the writing case, and put her fingers on the keys.
