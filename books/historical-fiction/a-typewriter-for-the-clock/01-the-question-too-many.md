## 1. The Question Too Many

The census taker came up from the mainland on the last calm day of September, riding the mail boat with her boots planted wide against the swell and a leather satchel clutched to her chest as though the sea might want it. Felix Eriksen saw her from the lamp gallery while he was polishing the storm panes, and he watched the whole way in — the way she stood at the bow without holding the rail, the way she looked up at the tower before the boat had even turned toward the jetty. He had kept this light for twenty-two years and had learned to read people at a distance the way sailors read cloud banks. Most visitors looked at the lighthouse with their mouths slightly open, awed or seasick or both. This woman looked at it the way a clerk looks at an unbalanced column.

He went down to meet her anyway. That was the duty of it. The island belonged to no one, but the light belonged to the Crown, and the Crown sent its people when it pleased.

"You'll be Eriksen," she said on the jetty, before he had said anything at all. She was younger than he'd expected — thirty, perhaps, with wind-flattened brown hair escaping a sensible knot and eyes that were already writing things down behind their own glass. "Ada Lindqvist. District enumeration office, Havnvik." She produced a folded paper stamped with an official seal, held it up the way a preacher holds a psalm, and did not quite hand it over. "I'm to count every soul north of the narrows before the ice comes."

"Then you've come to the right rock," Felix said. "We're few enough that I could count us for you and save you the climb." He smiled when he said it. He had a fisherman's smile, easy and creased, the kind that made strangers trust him within a minute. Marta used to say he could sell a lie to a priest with that smile, and she said it fondly, because in the years of their flight that smile had kept them both alive.

But Ada Lindqvist only looked at him a moment longer than the joke required, and then followed him up the path.

* * *

The keeper's house sat hunched against the tower's east side like a barnacle, low-roofed and stone-walled, warm with the smell of bread and lamp oil. Signe was at the table doing sums in pencil — she was seventeen now, all sharp elbows and sharper arithmetic, her mother's dark hair pinned back with whatever was nearest — and Ida, twelve years old and small for it, was mending a net by the window with the fierce concentration of a child who has decided that being useful is the same as being safe.

Felix made the introductions standing up, which was unusual for him; he was a man who liked people settled in chairs with coffee in front of them. But something about the leather satchel made him keep his feet.

"The household of the Skerries Light," Ada said, opening a ledger whose pages had been ruled by hand, neat columns waiting to be filled. "Names, ages, relations, occupations. Born where, married where, buried where — though I hope not to need the last one." She glanced up. "You've held the appointment long?"

"Twenty-two years. Before that I was deckhand out of Havnvik, and before that—" He shrugged. "The sea doesn't ask where you were born."

"But the Crown does." She wrote his name, spelled it aloud to be sure, wrote his age. Then Signe's: born at the light itself, delivered by the midwife from Vesterhavn who was dead now herself these six years. Then Ida's, and here Felix watched carefully, because Ida was not blood.

"Ward," he said. "Orphaned girl. Her people were lost on the *Solveig* when she was small. No kin ever claimed her, so we did."

"That's generous," Ada said, and wrote it, and there was nothing in her voice but pleasantness. Yet Felix noticed she did not write *ward* immediately. She wrote something shorter first, then crossed it out, then wrote *ward*. He told himself it meant nothing. Clerks crossed things out all day.

Signe answered her questions in a flat, competent voice, giving the year of her birth without hesitation, and Felix felt the particular pride he always felt watching his daughter handle strangers — she gave them exactly what they asked and never a grain more. She had inherited that from him too, though neither of them would have called it inheritance. It was training. Twenty years of training in what you say and what you don't, drilled as gently as lamplight into a child who thought she was merely learning manners.

Ida, asked where she was born, looked at Felix first. Just for a heartbeat. A quick flick of the eyes, asking permission without knowing she was asking it.

"A fishing village down the coast," Felix said smoothly. "She wouldn't remember it."

"I remember the boats," Ida offered shyly. "And a dog. There was a dog."

"There's always a dog," Signe said, and Ida smiled, and the tension in the room loosened half a turn.

Ada Lindqvist poured the tea Felix's silence had finally prompted Signe to make, and for a quarter hour the interview became almost ordinary. She admired the clock in the corner — an old brass ship's clock, kept wound to the second, because in a house run by a lighthouse everything ran on the clock — and she remarked on the cold coming early this year, and she asked how many sheep they kept and whether the garden yielded enough cabbage to see a family through, and Felix answered all of it easily, expansively, a man with nothing under his floorboards but potatoes.

Then she turned back a page in her ledger, wet her pen, and asked the question.

* * *

"I should say," she began, in the mild tone of someone consulting procedure rather than curiosity, "that the district office has flagged certain households along this coast for supplementary inquiry. Nothing personal. There are properties in the old registers — estates, mostly, big houses that changed hands badly in the troubles twenty years back — where persons connected to those estates may have dispersed afterward. The office wants to know if any such persons passed through, lodged, took work, or settled in the years following." She looked up. Her pen rested on the ruled line, poised. "So: in your twenty-two years here, Eriksen — has anyone named Voss ever lodged at this light?"

The name went through the room like a current through water.

Felix felt it enter him and pass through every part of him and exit nowhere. He had prepared for this question for twenty years, in the way a man prepares for a wave he knows is coming — not by bracing, exactly, but by making sure that when it hit, his hands would do the right thing without him. His hands did the right thing. He reached for the teapot and refilled her cup, unhurried, the spout steady.

"Voss," he repeated, tasting the word like a stranger's coin. "No. Can't say I have. We get the odd fisherman caught out in weather, the occasional surveyor, once a bishop — that was an occasion, I can tell you, the man complained the whole visit that our coffee was boiled twice. But no Voss." He set the pot down. "Name means nothing to me beyond the newspaper stories. Bad business, that fire. Whole family scattered, wasn't it?"

"Some of them," Ada said. "Some died in the fire. One was convicted of setting it." Her pen moved at last, drawing a single line across the empty space where a name would go — the line a clerk draws to close an account. "A servant, I believe. Hanged himself in custody before trial, so the story goes, which the office found irregular. Unfinished business tends to stay unfinished on paper forever. That's why I'm asking every household between the narrows and the point."

"And we're the last household before the point," Felix said, "so you've saved yourself a row." He laughed. The laugh came out exactly right — dry, a little tired, the laugh of a man humoring bureaucracy. Inside, beneath the laugh, some deep cellar of himself had gone dark and silent, the way the lamp room goes when the flame gutters, and he was moving through that darkness on memory alone.

Because the truth was a locked door three rooms away, and behind that door sat a typewriter — a black Remington with a cracked space bar and a ribbon gone silver at the edges — and beneath its carriage, still rolled into the platen after twenty years, a page that named a man who had never been accused. And the woman who had carried that machine across half the country sewn into a mattress, who had slept with her hand on it like a talisman, who had married a lighthouse keeper partly because a lighthouse is the one place in the world where the mainland forgets to look — that woman had been dead nine winters, and her secret was now the shape of his whole life.

Marta Eriksen. Born Marta Voss. Buried under a stone on the north slope that said only *Wife of F. Eriksen*, because even in grief he had kept the discipline she begged of him with her last breath.

"Your wife," Ada said suddenly.

Felix's cup stopped an inch above the saucer.

"She isn't listed among the living, obviously, and I don't see a death entry in the parish abstracts for this district — offshore parishes are terrible about reporting, so that's no mystery." The pen hovered again. "For the record of the household, though. Your late wife's name? Place of birth? The office does like its columns complete."

There it was. Not the question she had been sent to ask — the question underneath it, the one clerks always come to eventually, because a missing wife is a hole in any ledger and holes itch. He had rehearsed this one too. Nine years of rehearsal.

"Marta," he said. "Marta Eriksen. Born — up country. Family were farmers, gone now, all of them. She didn't like to talk much of before." He let a shadow cross his face, and it cost him nothing, because the shadow was true. "She's been dead nine years. Fever, the winter the supply boat couldn't cross. We buried her ourselves when the ground thawed." He looked at his hands. He did not have to manufacture what lived in them.

Across the table, quietly, Signe had stopped doing her sums. Pencil resting on the page, she was watching her father with an expression he could not read and did not try to — an expression, perhaps, of a daughter noticing for the first time that there was a question her father had never answered in all her life, namely: who was my mother, before she was ours?

Ada Lindqvist wrote *Marta Eriksen, deceased 1884*, in her careful hand. Then she paused.

It was a small thing. Anyone else would have missed it entirely. But Felix had spent two decades watching the sea for signs invisible to other men — the color of the water off the shoal, the way gulls sit when a storm is two days out — and so he saw it. The pen stopped. Not the natural stop of a writer thinking of the next word, but the particular stop of a person deciding something. Her nib rested against the paper and made a tiny bloom of ink, no bigger than a lentil, and her eyes stayed down on the ledger while something worked behind them, and then the pen moved on as though it had never stopped at all.

"Burned?" she said lightly. "The farm, I mean. Lots of places burned in those years. Hard winters make careless chimneys."

"No," Felix said. "Just gone. Sold, scattered. Families thin out." He heard his own voice and marveled at it — so level, so bored, so precisely the voice of a man who has never once needed to lie about his wife. Somewhere below his ribs his heart was going like the engine of the mail boat.

"Mm." She closed the ledger partway, then seemed to think better of it and opened it again, flipping back several pages — checking something, or pretending to. "One more formality. The supplementary inquiries require me to note whether the household objects to a follow-up visit. Some folk do. Privacy is a right, even on a rock." She looked up, and for the first time since the jetty she smiled fully, and it was a good smile, open-faced, and Felix understood with a cold professional admiration that she had saved it for last the way he saved his own smile for first.

"We've nothing here but cabbages and secrets about the bishop's coffee," Felix said. "Come back whenever you like. Bring butter next time and you'll be family."

She laughed at that — genuinely, he thought, which was somehow worse — and gathered her papers, and stood, and thanked Signe for the tea, and patted Ida's shoulder on the way past, saying the net-mending was finer work than her own handwriting, which made the child glow like the lens itself.

At the door she paused with her hand on the frame. Wind came in around her skirts, smelling of the first real cold.

"The clock keeps beautiful time," she said, nodding at the brass face. "My father was a railway clerk. He always said you can tell everything about a household by its clock. Whether they're afraid of time, or friends with it." Her eyes moved around the room once — the scrubbed table, the mended nets, the three faces arranged in their ordinary attitudes — and came back to Felix. "Yours looks like a friend."

"It's the most honest member of the family," Felix said.

She went down the path. From the window, Felix watched her stand a moment at the jetty before the boat came, looking not at the light but at the keeper's house — at its one locked door, he realized, the shuttered window of the room off the back stair, the one window in the whole establishment that had been painted shut on Marta's instruction and stayed painted shut to this day. She looked at it for perhaps four seconds. Then the mail boat bumped the pilings, she stepped aboard without help, and the sea took her away south.

* * *

That evening he climbed the hundred and eighteen steps to trim the wick, though the wick wanted little trimming; it was habit, and habit was the architecture of his survival. The great lens turned overhead with its soft hydraulic breathing, throwing its beam out across the blackening water, and Felix stood inside the machine of his life — glass and brass and flame — and admitted to himself what he had refused to admit on the jetty.

The pause of the pen. The bloom of ink. Four seconds on a painted-shut window.

Clerks did not linger like that over nothing. Census takers asked names and ages and occupations, and went home, and forgot. But Ada Lindqvist had asked about the Vosses the way a surgeon asks where it hurts — already knowing, already cutting toward something. And the supplementary inquiries, the flagged registers, the *unfinished business stays unfinished on paper forever* — that was not routine language. That was a hook dressed as a form.

Twenty years. Twenty years of quiet, of seasons turning over the island like slow pages, of raising a daughter and taking in an orphan and keeping a light and keeping a silence, until the silence had grown so large he lived inside it the way a snail lives in its shell, and had nearly mistaken it for safety.

He put his palm flat against the cold lamp-room glass. Below, the lights of the house showed yellow and small: Signe banking the stove, Ida carrying coals, the ordinary liturgy of the evening. On the north slope, under the grass, Marta lay with her secret intact, and he had promised her — God, he had promised her on the last night, her voice worn down to a thread — *never let it leave the island, Felix. Never let them read what I carried. Promise me the machine dies with us.*

"I promised," he said aloud, to the glass, to the dark water, to her.

The beam swung out and swept the horizon, and for one full rotation everything the light touched was revealed and everything it did not touch was night, and Felix Eriksen stood in the middle of that difference and understood, with the dread arriving not like a wave but like the tide — gradual, total, undeniable — that the quiet was over.

Three rooms below, in a locked room behind a painted window, the typewriter waited with its one loaded page, patient as a bullet in a chamber.

And somewhere south of the narrows, a clerk with a leather satchel was writing in her ledger by lamplight, pausing — he was sure of it — far too long over a certain blank space where a name should be.
