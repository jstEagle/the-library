## 2. What the Lamp Remembers

The stairs to the lamp room were one hundred and twelve, and Felix Eriksen had counted them so many times in twenty years that the number had stopped being a number and become a kind of prayer. He climbed them now in the blue hour before dusk, a brass oil can in his right hand and a trimming knife in his apron pocket, and he did not look at the window at the third landing because there was no reason to look at it except that Marta had stood there every evening of her last autumn with her hands folded and her face turned toward the sea, waiting for him to come back down.

He looked anyway. He always looked.

The glass was fogged at the edges where the warm air of the house met the cold coming off the water. Beyond it, the strait lay flat and pewter-colored, stitched here and there with whitecaps like loose thread. The mainland was a gray suggestion to the south, ten miles of open water that would turn to grinding ice by January. Somewhere out there was the world — magistrates, census men, debts, names written in ledgers. Up here there was only the stairwell's smell of lamp oil and cold iron, and the slow turning of his own thoughts, which after two decades had worn grooves as deep as the treads under his boots.

At the top, the lamp room opened around him like the inside of a bell. The great lens stood in its bath of mercury, five feet of cut glass and brass, each prism holding a sliver of the darkening sky. The wick assembly rose through the center, and Felix set down the oil can and began, without hurry, without thought, the work that had ordered his life since he was nineteen years old: check the reservoir, wipe the chimney glass, trim the char from the wick so the flame would stand clean and even when the clockwork lifted it into its rotation at sundown.

The knife whispered along the wick's edge. Char fell away in black curls.

*Never let it leave the island.*

She had said it in this room. That was the thing he could never explain to anyone who had not stood here — that the promise had been made forty feet above the sea, with the lamp burning between them and the wind trying the glass like a burglar testing latches. Her last real winter. She had known it was her last, though neither of them had said so aloud; she had simply begun putting her affairs in order the way other people put away dishes, quietly, thoroughly, while pretending it was nothing.

It had been a Tuesday. He remembered that because Tuesdays were winding days for the rotation clockwork, and he had been kneeling at the base of the mechanism with the crank in both hands when she came up the stairs. She had been thin by then. The illness had taken her in the manner of a tide going out — not all at once but steadily, each week revealing more of the bones beneath. But her eyes had never gone out. They had burned low and steady, like a wick trimmed well, right down to the last week of January.

"Wind it slowly," she had said, standing behind him, her hand light on his shoulder. "You always rush the last quarter-turn."

"The last quarter is what makes it keep time."

"I know." A small laugh, cracked at the edges. "I taught you that, didn't I."

He had wound it slowly. The gears had taken the tension with their patient clicking, and outside the window the first snow of the season had come down over the water in long diagonal strokes, and he had thought, *we will have her until spring at least,* because that was the lie a man tells himself when he cannot afford the truth.

Then she had said, "Felix. Come and sit with me a moment," and he had known, from four words, that something was being decided in her that he would have no part in shaping.

*

They had sat on the bench beneath the lens, the one he still kept oiled though no one sat on it now, and she had held her tea in both hands for the warmth and not drunk any of it. The lamp between them threw their shadows huge against the curved wall, two shapes leaning toward one another like figures on a coin.

"I want to tell you what the machine says," she began. "Not what it says word for word. What it *means.*"

"Marta—"

"Hear me. I haven't the strength to say it twice." Her voice had that quality then, quiet and absolute, of a woman who has finished negotiating with death and turned her attention to the living. "You know my father kept accounts on it. You know what else he kept on it. I have told you the shape of it, these twenty years, and you have been kind enough not to ask for the whole of it. I am going to give you the shape once more, and then I am going to ask you for a promise, and you are going to make it, and we will never speak of it again."

The clockwork ticked above them. It had a sound like a patient heart, and Felix would hear it in his sleep for years afterward.

"My family did not flee Skarvik because they were guilty," she said. "We fled because we were *safe*. Do you understand the difference? My uncle Elias burned the east wing for the insurance, and Henrik Söderlund went to prison for it, and my family packed three wagons in the night and came north across the ice road, and everyone agreed — everyone, my mother, my aunts, Elias himself, weeping into his brandy — that the truth would be safer locked away than spoken. And the only place the whole truth existed, outside of Elias's conscience, which I assure you was a well-appointed room with the windows shuttered — the only place was in the pages my father typed before he died. His account. Dates, amounts, the insurance papers, and at the end, in his own words, *Elias Voss set the fire and let an innocent man hang for it.*"

She had looked at her tea. Steam had risen past her chin.

"Why did your father write it down," Felix asked, "if no one wanted it said?"

"Because he was dying and afraid of God," she said simply. "And because a man who spends his life keeping accounts cannot bear to leave one false entry in the ledger. Even an entry everyone else has agreed to leave false." She smiled then, and it was the smile he had fallen in love with on the ferry from the mainland, when she was twenty-two and carrying everything she owned in one valise. "That is why I loved you, do you know? You are also a man who cannot bear a false entry. You trim the wick so the flame stands true. You count the oil twice."

"And you married me to hide inside my honesty."

"I married you because you gave me a tower to hide in," she said, "and then you never once asked me what I was hiding from. That is a rarer virtue than honesty, Felix. But now I am asking you to stop hiding me. Not to expose me. To *keep* me. There is a difference, and it is the whole difference."

"What do you want me to do?"

"Nothing. That is what I want you to do. Nothing, forever." She set the tea down and took his hands, and her fingers were cold as the railing outside. "When I am gone, the machine stays in the locked room, and the room stays locked, and you tell no one — not Signe, not Anders, not the bishop himself if he comes rowing across the ice — that it exists. Let them think we fled with nothing. Let them think whatever they like. The moment that machine leaves this island, it becomes a thing men fight over. Lawyers, magistrates, the Voss cousins who still write letters asking after Mama's jewelry. While it sits behind my door it is only a dead woman's grief. If it travels, it becomes a weapon, and someone will point it, and the someone standing nearest when it fires might be our daughter."

"Signe doesn't even know the name Voss."

"And she must not, until she is old enough to carry it or refuse it. Promise me."

"Marta, if the truth could clear Henrik Söderlund—"

"Henrik Söderlund died in prison eleven years ago." She said it without flinching, and he understood that she had carried that too, alone, all this time — the name of the man whose freedom had been traded for hers. "Nothing I own can give him back his years. But if that page comes off this island, it can take things *from* the living. From Signe. From you. From Ida, if you ever take in another child, and you will, because you are incapable of letting a child go hungry within sight of this light. So." She squeezed his hands, hard, harder than her body should have allowed. "Promise me the machine never leaves."

He had promised. Of course he had promised. A man does not haggle with a dying woman on a bench beneath a lens, with the snow coming down and the clockwork ticking out the seconds of her remaining autumn. He had promised, and she had leaned her head against his shoulder, and they had watched the sun go down together through the glass, and six weeks later he had buried her above the tideline in the frozen ground, and he had gone back up the hundred and twelve stairs that same night because the lamp does not care whose wife has died.

But he had never told anyone what she asked him. And the machine had stayed.

*

Felix finished the wick and sat back on his heels. The flame caught when he opened the fuel valve, small at first, then rising, steadying, until it stood in the center of the chimney like a held breath. Outside, the last light was draining off the water. He checked the clockwork — three days of tension remaining; he would wind it tomorrow — and wiped his hands on his apron and allowed himself, as he allowed himself most evenings, one minute at the seaward window.

The strait was emptying. A fishing boat was running for the harbor at Kallvik, sails double-reefed, and beyond it the sky had the bruised yellow cast that meant weather gathering somewhere out over the open Baltic. The almanac on his desk downstairs said fair for three days, but almanacs were printed in cities by men who had never watched a northeaster eat a coastline. Felix trusted his knees and the color of the sky, and both of them said *soon.*

Twenty years. Twenty years of trimming this wick, of counting this oil, of walking past that locked door at the end of the upstairs passage four times a day and hearing — nothing, now, nothing but the house settling — and keeping the silence the way he kept the lamp: nightly, methodically, without asking himself anymore whether it deserved keeping. A vow stops being a weight after enough years. It becomes part of the architecture. You build your rooms around it. You forget, mostly, that it holds anything up.

Mostly.

Because tonight, climbing those stairs with the oil can, he had found himself thinking about the census man's visit in September — no, the census *woman*, Lindqvist, with her ink-stained fingers and her questions arranged so neatly, so innocently, like driftwood on a beach that might be just driftwood or might be the wreckage of something sunk. *Any persons lodged at the station during the year? Any lodgers of foreign extraction?* Ordinary words. Ordinary forms. And then, delivered while she smoothed the paper flat, in the same administrative voice: *And any family by the name of Voss? It is a common enough name in the southern parishes. We are correcting the registers.*

He had lied the way he trimmed a wick — smoothly, in one pass, leaving a clean edge. *No Voss. Never heard the name at this station.* She had nodded. She had thanked him. And her pen had paused above the column, just for a moment, a hesitation no longer than the space between waves, before she wrote his answer down.

A pen pause. Twenty years of calm, undone by a pen pause.

Felix pressed his palm flat against the cold glass. Below him the island fell away in dark terraces — the boathouse, the woodshed, the low stone house with its lamplit kitchen windows where Signe would be setting the table and Ida would be pretending to read while listening to everything, the way children do, the way he had taught them to listen without seeming to, though he had never taught them that aloud either. Some inheritances pass without instruction.

Down there in the house was a girl of sixteen who believed her mother's name was Marta Eriksen, full stop, born where? — born nowhere, born of nothing Felix had ever specified, because specifying is how lies unravel. Down there was a locked room containing a typewriter, a tin box of documents, and one page still sitting in the carriage where Marta's father had left it — Felix had never dared remove it; removing it felt like finishing a dead man's sentence — and twenty years of silence stacked around it all like sandbags against a flood.

And somewhere out on that darkening water, in a city of registers and corrections, was a government office that employed people with pens, people who paused.

The lamp behind him completed its first revolution of the night. The beam swung out over the strait, touched the fishing boat, released it, swept the horizon, returned. Steady as a pulse. He had always found comfort in that rhythm, but tonight it sounded less like a heartbeat and more like a countdown — tick, sweep, tick, sweep — the same patient clockwork that had measured out Marta's last winter, measuring out something else now, something he could not yet name.

He took the oil can and the knife and started down the hundred and twelve stairs, and at the third landing he stopped at the fogged window again, longer than usual, looking down at the kitchen light.

Tomorrow he would take inventory of the coal and the oil, as he did every October. Tomorrow he would write the numbers in the household ledger, in his careful keeper's hand, the hand that did not know how to make a false entry. And there was a letter he had received three weeks ago that he had not shown to anyone — a letter he had read once by lamplight and then folded away into the oil locker beneath the stairs, because the oil locker was the one place in the house Signe never went without him, and because a man who cannot bear a false entry in a ledger has learned, over twenty years, to be very good with the entries that appear in none.

The letter was from a solicitor in Kallvik. It concerned debts. It concerned his brother.

Felix stood at the window a moment longer, a dark shape against the lamp's revolving glow, and then he went down into the house, closing the lamp room door softly behind him, the way one closes a door on a sleeping child — or a grave.

* * *

In the kitchen, the smell of barley soup and birch smoke. Signe had laid three places at the table and was standing at the stove with her sleeves pushed up, stirring, and Ida sat in the corner with a scrap of paper and a stub of pencil, lips moving over her letters. Two lamps burned on the mantel. Everything ordinary. Everything exactly as it had been for years, the small warm machinery of a household running on routine and trust.

"Fog coming in behind the wind," Felix said, hanging his cap on its peg. "Double-check the shutters before bed."

"Yes, Father." Signe glanced at him — a quick, measuring glance, and he saw with a small cold surprise that she had learned it from him, that flicker of assessment, that habit of reading a face for what it withheld.

Ida looked up from her paper. "Papa Felix, how do you spell *remember*?"

He hung the oil can on its nail. Behind the wall, at the head of the stairs, the great clock in the passage struck seven, and below the stairs, beneath the treads he had counted ten thousand times, a letter waited in the dark of the oil locker with a solicitor's seal already broken.

"R-E-M," Felix said. "The rest you'll have to work for."
