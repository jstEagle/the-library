## 3. Signe's Arithmetic

The ledger smelled of lamp oil and cold iron, the way everything in the keeper's house smelled of something practical. Signe Eriksen sat at the kitchen table with the book open before her, a stub of pencil behind her ear and another in her hand, and she did what she did every October when the first frost silvered the windowpanes: she counted what they had, and she counted what they would need, and she made the two numbers agree by force of will if nothing else.

Coal first. The shed held eleven sacks against the north wall, stacked three deep with a gap behind them where damp could breathe and not rot the wood. She had measured it herself that morning, breath fogging, boots crunching on the shingle path. Eleven sacks. Last winter they had burned through fourteen between the first of November and the thaw, but last winter had been mild — her father said so, and he was a man who remembered weather the way other men remembered scripture. So: sixteen sacks to be safe, seventeen if the gales came early and the house leaked its heat into the wind the way old houses do.

She wrote *coal: 16* in her small, upright hand, and underlined it once.

Oil next. The oil was the whole argument of their lives, really. Coal kept them warm; oil kept them alive — or rather, kept the ships alive, the fishermen and the packet boats and the occasional fool in a pleasure yawl who thought October water was a thing one could argue with. The light burned through roughly four gallons a night in clear weather, more in fog, and the winter held somewhere between ninety and a hundred nights of burning. She ran the figures twice, because arithmetic done once was arithmetic done hopefully.

Four hundred gallons, then, with margin. The cistern in the oil house stood at just over half. Her father had ordered from the mainland chandler in September; the delivery boat was due within the fortnight, weather permitting, and weather rarely permitted anything in October without being asked twice.

Signe chewed the end of her pencil and looked out the window at the grey water. Somewhere past the point, the ferry was either coming or not coming, and the difference between those two states was the difference between a warm February and a cold one, between enough and almost enough, and almost enough on an island in winter was a long word for danger.

She was nineteen years old and she had been doing this arithmetic since she was nine, since the winter her mother died and her father went quiet in a way that never entirely lifted, and someone had to know how many sacks of coal leaned against the north wall. She had learned it the way she'd learned to trim a wick and read a barometer and wind the hall clock: not because anyone taught her, exactly, but because the island taught you or it buried you, and Signe had no interest in being buried before her time.

There were other things she had learned the same way. Things no one taught and no one spoke of.

She wrote *oil: 400 gal., order confirmed?* and left the question mark standing like a small flag on a hill.

* * *

Her father came down from the lamp room at half past noon, smelling of brass polish and cold air, and ate his bread and salt herring standing up, which he always did, as though sitting were a luxury he hadn't earned yet that day. Felix Eriksen was fifty-three years old and looked older in the flat northern light — a tall man gone stooped at the shoulders from twenty years of climbing stairs, with hands so cracked and weathered they seemed less like flesh than like driftwood that had learned to grip things.

"Delivery boat Thursday, if the wind holds," he said. "I'll want the landing cleared."

"It's cleared. I did it Tuesday." She turned the ledger toward him. "We're short on coal. Sixteen sacks minimum, and the chandler quoted nine crowns the sack last spring. It'll be ten now, maybe eleven."

He studied the page. He always studied her pages properly, which was one of the ways he loved her — not with words, which came hard to him, but with attention. "Eleven," he said finally. "It'll be eleven. Order eighteen."

"Eighteen is forty crowns more than we have after the oil." She said it evenly, without accusation. This too was a language they shared: numbers instead of feelings, figures instead of fears. "Unless the Bureau sends the quarterly early."

"The Bureau sends what the Bureau sends." He set down his plate. "Order eighteen. I'll write to my brother."

Something moved through the room then, quick as a draft under a door. Signe kept her eyes on the ledger.

Anders. Uncle Anders, who arrived twice a year smelling of the mainland and its taverns, who called her *little accountant* and pinched her cheek with fingers that trembled slightly, who owed money to people whose names were never spoken above a certain volume. Her father writing to Anders about money was like writing to the tide about keeping the shore dry.

"You think he'll send it?" she asked, and made her voice a question about arithmetic only.

"He owes me." Her father said it flatly, and then, as if hearing how it sounded, added, "He'll send it."

He took his coat off its peg. "I've letters to post. Back before dark. Keep the lamp wound — the clockwork's been dragging again."

"I know. I oiled it yesterday."

He paused at the door, hand on the latch, and for a moment he looked at her with an expression she couldn't file anywhere — not worry exactly, not guilt, something older than both, something that had been living in his face for years and had simply grown comfortable there, the way lichen grows on stone. Then he nodded once and went out, and the door closed behind him with the soft finality of all doors on this island, and Signe sat alone with her columns of figures and the sound of the sea working at the rocks below like a patient animal.

* * *

She finished the coal column. She finished the oil column. She estimated flour, salt pork, potatoes from the bed behind the house, the jarred fish that would see them through the weeks when no boat could cross. She wrote it all in her upright hand, and the numbers lay on the page looking orderly and sufficient, which was the great lie of bookkeeping: that anything could be made safe by writing it down.

It was while she was blotting the page that she heard her father come back.

Not through the front door. That was the first wrongness. His boots sounded on the side path, the one that passed the oil house, and then there was the scrape of the oil house door — a sound she knew better than most hymns, having oiled that hinge every season since she was twelve — and then a long silence, longer than filling a lamp ever took, and then the scrape again, and his boots going away toward the house, and the front door opening and closing as though he had been home all along.

Signe sat very still.

There was no reason on earth for her father to go into the oil house between noon and one o'clock. The lamps were filled at dusk. The reserve cans were checked weekly, on Saturdays, and today was Wednesday. She knew the rhythm of that building the way she knew the rhythm of her own heartbeat, and this beat was wrong.

She told herself it was nothing. She was very good at telling herself things were nothing; it was practically a family trade. But her pencil stayed where it was, and her feet decided otherwise, and a moment later she was pulling on her coat and stepping out into the raw afternoon with the excuse ready-made in her mouth — checking the cistern level, confirming the order quantity — excuses being another thing the island taught you or buried you for lacking.

The oil house squatted against the hillside, stone-walled, turf-roofed, built to be forgettable. Inside it smelled of kerosene and cold stone and, faintly, of paper.

Paper. In an oil house.

She stood in the doorway and let her eyes adjust. The cistern gleamed dully in the light from the open door. The reserve cans stood in their row, each stenciled with its number in her father's blocky capitals. And against the far wall, behind the third can — the one he always shifted himself, the one she'd never once been allowed to move, *mind your fingers, girl, that one sticks* — the shadows lay differently than they should have. There was a gap there. A fresh gap. Something had been placed behind that can within the last five minutes, something flat and rectangular, and the smell of paper hung in the cold air like a confession.

Signe did not go to it.

She stood in the doorway with her hand on the frame and she performed, consciously and completely, the act she had been practicing her whole life: she looked at the place where the secret was, and then she looked away from it, and she filed both looks somewhere deep and locked.

Because here was the truth of her childhood, the arithmetic beneath all the other arithmetic: her father had secrets the way the lamp had oil, and the family ran on them. She had known it since she was small — since the years when visitors asked questions about her mother and got answers that slid off her father like rain off glass; since the night she'd woken at eleven years old and heard him downstairs talking low to Anders, and the word *Voss* had crossed the kitchen like a bird through an open window, and in the morning no one acknowledged that any window had been open at all. She had learned young that some questions were not refused in this house. They were simply never completed. You began one and the air itself thickened around it, and you learned to leave sentences unfinished the way sailors learn to leave harbors before a storm.

She loved her father. That was the whole of it, and it was not simple, and it did not need to be simple. She loved him the way you love a lighthouse: for the light, and for the endurance, and without asking too precisely what the rock underneath was made of.

So she checked the cistern level — actually checked it, dipping the marked rod, calling the figure aloud to the empty room as though that had been her purpose all along — and she noted that the order needed confirming, and she stepped back out into the grey afternoon and pulled the door shut behind her with the same care she used for everything in this house.

* * *

Supper that evening was salt cod and potatoes, and her father talked about the clockwork mechanism in the lamp, which had been dragging, and about the delivery boat, and about whether the ice on the rain barrel meant an early winter. Ordinary talk. Well-made talk. She recognized the craftsmanship in it now, the way she recognized a well-kept ledger: every line balanced, nothing left open, nothing that invited a second reading.

"Uncle Anders," she said, ladling the last of the potatoes. "Will he come before the freeze?"

Her father's spoon paused — half a second, no more, a pause any stranger would have missed entirely. "Why?"

"He usually comes in autumn. And you said you'd write to him. About the coal."

"I'll write tonight." The spoon resumed. "Whether he comes is his affair."

"His affairs generally arrive on our doorstep," Signe said, and immediately wished the words back, because they were sharper than arithmetic, and sharpness invited conversation, and conversation was the one tool she had sworn an hour ago not to pick up.

But her father only looked at her across the table, and whatever moved behind his eyes he put away quickly, the way he put away everything, in some interior locker behind a can that stuck. "Eat your supper," he said gently. "It's getting cold."

They washed the dishes together in the silence that was their true mother tongue. Outside, the wind had begun to rise out of the northeast, worrying the eaves, and the first spit of sleet ticked against the window like something impatient.

Later, when he had gone up to light the lamp — she heard his tread on the stairs, the familiar liturgy of it, valve and match and the hum of the mantle catching — Signe sat at the kitchen table with the ledger before her and did not write anything.

She thought about the letter behind the third can. She thought about the word *Voss*, twenty years dead and still flying through kitchens. She thought about her mother, whom she remembered as a laugh and a pair of quick hands and a smell of lavender that might have been invented afterward by longing, and about how her father never once, in nineteen years, had spoken her name in the past tense — as though Marta were not gone but merely elsewhere, a fact he was keeping safe along with everything else.

The family silence was not emptiness. She understood that now, sitting in the lamplight that spilled yellow through the kitchen door. It was a fullness. It was a room packed floor to ceiling with things unsaid, and they all lived inside it together, carefully, the way you live in a house where the floors are rotten and you know which boards to step on and which to avoid, and you teach your children the map without ever admitting there is a map.

She loved him for it and she was tired of it, and both of these things were true, and neither of them changed what she did next.

She opened the ledger to a clean page and wrote, in her small upright hand, the day's final entry — *letter received, contents unknown, location: oil house, third can* — and then she looked at what she had written for a long moment, and struck it through with a single firm line until the words were illegible, and blotted the page.

Some accounts you keep and some accounts you keep closed. She was her father's daughter. She knew the difference.

Upstairs, or rather up the tower, the beam swung round and laid its white arm across the window, once every eight seconds, steady as a pulse. Eight seconds of light, eight seconds of dark, all winter long, saying the same single sentence to the darkness over and over: *you are seen, you are seen, you are seen.*

Signe blew out the kitchen candle and climbed to her room, and slept, and dreamed of a door she could not open, and woke to sleet rattling the glass like fingertips.

It was two days later that the census woman came back.

Signe saw her first from the kitchen window — a narrow figure in a grey coat picking her way down the landing path with a leather satchel, unhurried, thorough, the walk of a person who intended to write everything down. Behind her, out on the grey water, the sky had begun to stack itself into the long black shelves of a storm coming north.

Her father was in the tower. She could have called up to him. Instead she stood at the window with her arms folded against the cold coming off the glass, and watched Ada Lindqvist approach the house where every board was mapped and every secret weighed, and felt, beneath the practiced stillness, the first cold thread of a fear she could not enter into any ledger.
