## 4. The Storms Begin

The barometer began to fall on a Tuesday, and by Wednesday morning it had fallen so far that Felix Eriksen stood in the lamp room with his thumb pressed to the glass of the instrument and felt the old familiar tightening in his chest, like a fist closing around a rope. Twenty years at the point had taught him to read pressure the way other men read faces — and this one was telling him a lie was coming off the sea.

He had learned long ago not to trust calm water. Calm water was the sea holding its breath before it spoke. By noon the horizon had gone the color of pewter, flat and hard, and the gulls had come inland in a ragged gray river of them, wheeling over the house and settling into the rocks behind it with their backs to the wind, which was itself a kind of omen. Gulls knew things. They had always known them before Felix did.

"Signe!" he called down the stairwell, and his voice fell through the tower like a stone dropped down a well. "Oil covers on. Lash the boat tight."

He heard her moving below — she had been at the ledgers all morning, her arithmetic neat as stitching — and then the sound of the yard door and her boots on the shingle where the dory lay overturned on its rollers. Good girl. She did not need telling twice, had never needed telling twice since the year she turned nine and he'd put a rope in her hands during a smaller storm and watched her learn faster than fear could teach her otherwise. Marta had been the same. The thought arrived unbidden, as thoughts of Marta always did when the weather turned, and he let it pass through him without stopping, the way you let a cold draft through a room rather than chase it and break something trying to shut it out.

By three o'clock the wind had come around north-northeast and begun to build in earnest. It came across forty miles of open water with nothing to slow it, nothing to bend it, and it struck the point like something with intent. The spray went up the tower face in sheets, freezing as it climbed, and by dusk the lantern's gallery rail wore a rind of ice an inch thick that chimed faintly when the gusts found it, a sound like distant bells rung by a careless hand.

Felix trimmed the wick, checked the rotation of the lens, wiped the salt haze from the seaward panes with a cloth kept for that purpose alone, and settled into the watch. Below, the house crouched against the blow with its shutters buttoned and its chimney drawing hard. Ida had lit the kitchen lamps and he could see the small yellow squares of them from the tower, steady as anything, and that steadiness steadied him too. That was the arrangement of his life, had been since Marta died: the light above, the lamplight below, and himself somewhere between, keeping both.

* * *

It came in the fourth hour of the ebb, near ten o'clock, when the darkness beyond the point had grown so complete that the beam seemed less to cut through it than to carry it along, rolling it ahead of itself like a wave.

He saw the light first. Not his own — another, green and red, low on the water, rising and vanishing in the troughs, and even before his mind assembled what his eyes were reporting, his body had already begun to move. He took the stairs three at a time, shouted into the kitchen without pausing — "Boat in trouble off the point! Signe, warm blankets, hot water! Ida, stay by the stove!" — and was out into the wind before the door had finished slamming behind him.

The night hit him like a wall of wet wool. Wind tore at his coat, filled his mouth, drove the breath back down his throat. He got the dory off its rollers and down the shingle alone, though it cost him, the hull fighting him like a live thing, and he shipped the oars and pulled out into the black.

He had done this perhaps thirty times in twenty years. He could count on one hand the ones he cared to remember. The trick, if there was one, was not courage but arithmetic: distance, set of current, period of the seas, the weight of your boat against the weight of the water. You did the sums and you did not look at the sum of your own life riding in the bilge beneath your boots. The sea did not care about courage. The sea respected only calculation, and sometimes, out of what Felix privately suspected was boredom rather than mercy, it permitted the calculations to be enough.

The fishing boat was a small sloop out of Havnvik, half-swamped, her deck awash, two men clinging to the mast step because the cockpit held four feet of green water. Her name board had torn away. As Felix came alongside in a trough between seas, one of them made a grab for the dory's gunwale and missed, went under, came up coughing, and Felix had him by the collar of his oilskin before the next wave broke, hauling him across the thwarts with a strength that surprised them both. The second man jumped — no choice in it, really, the deck going under as he leapt — and Felix caught the painter he'd thrown and brought him aboard hand over hand.

"Ballast!" he roared over the wind, pointing. "Sit her deep or we swamp!"

They rowed — Felix pulling, the younger man bailing with a bucket that had come with the dory and was worth more than everything they'd left behind — and the sea let them go. That was how Felix thought of it afterward, lying awake: *it let us go.* Not that they had won free, but that the sea, having considered them, had released them, the way a cat releases a thing it has batted about and found insufficiently interesting.

* * *

The younger one was a boy, seventeen maybe, blue-lipped and shaking, wrapped in a blanket before the kitchen fire with a mug of something hot pressed into his hands by Signe, who moved around him with a competence that made Felix's chest ache. The older one was fifty or thereabouts, weathered to the color of old rope, with a broken finger already splinted by Signe from a lath and linen, and eyes that kept moving around the room — taking in the ledgers on the shelf, the locked door at the end of the passage, the way the household arranged itself.

Felix noticed men like that. He had spent twenty years noticing men like that.

"Halvard," the older man said, when Signe asked his name. "Halvard Sund. This is my sister's boy, Petter." Then, because men who have nearly drowned feel obliged to fill silence with everything: "Running the herring south before the freeze. Should have run two days ago. Arrogance." He said it flatly, without self-pity, and Felix marked it — a man who named his own faults plainly was either honest or practiced.

"You'll stay the night," Felix said. "No boat goes back into that until it's done with us."

Sund nodded, and drank, and looked again at the locked door, and said nothing.

It was later, near midnight, the boy asleep on the settle and the wind still worrying the house like a dog with a bone, that Sund sat by the fire with his splinted hand cradled and spoke without looking up.

"You'll have heard, I suppose."

"Heard what?"

"The magistrate." Sund turned the words over like stones, choosing which to throw. "Coming north after the new year. From the capital. There's papers being served down the coast — old papers, twenty years old or more. Voss business." He glanced up then, and his eyes were shrewd in the firelight, and tired. "You'd remember the Voss affair. Everyone does. Estate burned, a man convicted, family scattered. Now someone's asking questions again. Rehn is his name. Young. They say he doesn't drink and doesn't smile and reads everything twice."

The fire popped. In the tower overhead, faint through the floorboards, Felix could hear the mechanism turning, the lens rotating on its bath of mercury, patient as a heart.

"I keep a lighthouse," Felix said. "News comes late here."

But his hands, folded in his lap, had gone very still, and he knew that stillness for what it was — the same stillness that comes over a man who has heard, beneath the storm, a second sound underneath it, and knows it is not the wind.

Sund studied him a moment longer than comfort allowed. Then he shrugged, and looked into the fire, and said, almost to himself:

"Funny thing, the sea. Brings what it brings. Doesn't ask first."

* * *

Near dawn the wind began to fail, dying the way great winds die, in long diminishing sighs, and Felix climbed the tower for the morning watch and found the horizon washed clean and cold, the water lying in long steel-colored swells like the fur of some enormous animal settling back to sleep. The wreckage of the Havnvik sloop would be washing ashore for days — spars, nets, a hatch cover or two — the sea returning what it had taken only after it had finished with it.

He thought about that. The sea as protector; the sea as herald. For twenty years the water had stood between the island and the world, a moat, a silence, a promise kept without effort. Every winter it froze harder and the island became more itself, more sealed, more safe. He had loved the ice for exactly that reason, and had never once said so aloud, because loving ice is a shameful thing for a man whose neighbors depend on boats.

And now the same water had carried him two fishermen, and one of those fishermen had carried a word — *magistrate* — the way driftwood carries a barnacle, unknowing, harmless, and all the more dangerous for it. Rehn. A young man who read everything twice.

Below, smoke rose from the kitchen chimney, straight in the dead calm, a thin gray line climbing into a sky the color of unpolished silver. Somewhere in the house, in the locked room, under dust and oilcloth, the machine sat with its secret still held in the carriage, patient as the ice forming now in the quiet shallows.

Felix pulled his coat tighter and began the descent, and behind him the sun came up over a sea so smooth and so innocent that no one seeing it would ever believe what it had just delivered.
