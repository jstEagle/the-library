## 5. Ada Returns

The storm came in off the water like a debt collector.

Ada Lindqvist had been halfway back across the strait when the sky turned the color of wet slate and the ferryman — a broad, taciturn man named Halvorsen who had spoken perhaps eleven words to her in two crossings — pointed his chin at the horizon and said, "You'll want to turn around."

She had not wanted to. She had a commission to fulfill, forms to file, forty-one households on her list with only nine visited before the weather closed in. But the wind had opinions of its own, and by the time she had argued with it for ten minutes at the dock at Vesterhavn, her hat was somewhere over the river and her ledger was clutched under her coat like an infant.

"The light," Halvorsen said, hauling the tiller hard. "Eriksen will take you in. He takes everyone in."

"Does he?"

Halvorsen looked at her then for the first time all day, and there was something in his look that Ada would spend the rest of the evening turning over like a coin of uncertain mint. "Everyone who needs taking," he said, and said nothing more.

* * *

The lighthouse rose out of the murk the way mountains do — slowly, then all at once — and the boat scraped against the stone landing while rain began in earnest, fat cold drops that stung her cheeks. Felix Eriksen stood at the top of the landing steps holding a lantern, as though he had been standing there for some time, as though he had known.

"Census woman," he said.

"Lindqvist. Ada Lindqvist." She climbed the steps, her boots skidding on wet stone, and he did not offer his hand but did take her satchel, which surprised her more than courtesy would have.

"You'll stay the night. No crossing back in this, and no arguing about it either. The storm means to be here longer than you do."

The keeper's house was attached to the tower's base, low-ceilinged and warm, smelling of coal smoke and boiled fish and lamp oil. A girl of perhaps sixteen was stirring a pot on the stove — Signe, from the autumn visit, tall and grave-eyed, with her mother's coloring if one assumed the mother had been fair. Another girl, younger, small-boned, sat at the table copying letters onto scraps of paper with the ferocious concentration of someone who had come late to learning. She looked up when Ada entered and did not look away again for a long moment, the way children study strangers, cataloguing them against some private list of categories: safe, unsafe, interesting, gone-by-morning.

"Anders isn't here?" Ada asked, unbuttoning her coat. "Your brother. He'd said he might return this week."

Felix's hands stopped moving over the stove for exactly one second. Anyone else would have missed it. Ada Lindqvist missed very little; it was her profession, and increasingly, she suspected, her affliction.

"He comes when he comes," Felix said. "Sit. You're soaked through. Signe, dry clothes for our guest."

"I have a change in my satchel—"

"You have a change that's wetter than what you're wearing. Signe."

The girl went. The younger one — Ida, Ada remembered now, the ward — returned to her letters, but Ada noticed the pencil had slowed, and that Ida's eyes kept sliding toward the door at the back of the kitchen, the one with the iron lock, the one Felix's gaze crossed once, briefly, the way a tongue finds a sore tooth.

* * *

Dinner was fish, bread, tea strong enough to stand a spoon in. Conversation was supplied almost entirely by Ada, which suited her fine; she had learned long ago that people told you everything so long as you asked questions that sounded like nothing.

"How long has the light stood here?" she asked.

"Seventy years. My father kept it before me, and his before him."

"And your wife? She was keeper's wife here how long?"

The spoon in Felix's bowl described half a circle and stopped. "Twenty-two years. She died six winters back."

"I'm sorry. What did she die of?"

"Fever." He said it the way a man reads a line he has read many times, smoothly, without feeling, and that smoothness itself was the thing that caught. Ada had taken testimony from hundreds of people. Liars stumbled. Liars over-explained. But the practiced ones — the ones who had rehearsed a single answer for years until it wore a groove in them — those were the ones whose answers came out polished and hollow, like sea glass.

Signe brought more bread and did not sit. Ida ate quickly and watched everyone. The storm threw itself against the seaward glass and the whole house seemed to lean into it and hold.

After dinner, Felix took the lantern and went up to tend the light. "I'll be an hour," he said. "Don't wander. The gallery walk is slick as ice in this wind."

And then Ada Lindqvist was alone in the kitchen with a locked door and a household ledger sitting on the shelf beside the clock.

She had seen it on her first visit, in September — a thick canvas-bound book, the kind the census office itself issued to stationmasters and harbormasters, meant for recording supplies, visitors, births, deaths, anything the central office might someday wish to know about the comings and goings at an isolated post. She had asked to see it then. Felix had said the ink was poor and the entries dull, and had handed her instead a neat summary page he had apparently prepared, listing the household: himself, Signe, Ida, and a rotating note about Anders's irregular visits.

He had prepared a summary. For a census taker. In advance.

At the time she had thought: a careful man. Now, alone, with the wind screaming like something alive at the windows, she thought: a man who does not want anyone reading backward.

She did not touch the ledger. Not yet. That was important to her, and she was honest enough with herself to know why — not because touching it would be theft, exactly, since the book was arguably a public record and she was arguably a public officer, but because she wanted to know what she would find before she let herself find it. Once she turned those pages she would stop being a woman doing her work and become something else. She could feel the threshold under her feet already.

She washed the dishes instead. She listened to Signe reading aloud to Ida by the stove — some serialized story from a mainland paper, a shipwreck, naturally, which made both girls glance at the windows and smile at each other in a way that suggested they found storms homey rather than frightening. She dried her stockings by the fire. She waited for the sound of Felix's boots descending the tower stair.

They came at last, slow, deliberate, and he paused at the bottom for a full ten seconds before opening the kitchen door. Checking himself, she thought. Assembling his face.

"The spare room's made up," he said. "There'll be soup at dawn whether you go or stay. If the wind drops by noon, Halvorsen may risk it."

"Thank you. Before I sleep — the ledger. I should record your household properly this time. Last visit I relied on your summary, but the office prefers primary entries. Dates, names, changes."

Something moved behind his eyes — not fear, she thought, or not only fear. Recognition. The look of a man watching a wave he had been telling himself would break elsewhere finally turn toward shore.

"It's supply records mostly," he said. "Coal, oil, wicks."

"All the same. It's my commission to see it."

He stood there a moment longer, and Ada understood that she was watching a decision being made, and that whatever he decided would tell her more than the ledger itself. Then he took the book down from the shelf and set it on the table between them, and stepped back, and folded his arms, and became a statue of a man waiting out weather.

"Take what time you need," he said.

* * *

The early pages were indeed dull, and beautifully kept. Felix Eriksen wrote a clear, upright hand, each entry dated, quantities precise, the oil consumption tracked against wind hours with an accountant's devotion. She read through 1888, 1889, the death of the wife recorded in June of '87 with three words — *Marta Eriksen, fever* — no date of burial, no minister's name, though every other entry in the book, even deliveries of salt pork, had witnesses noted.

She turned back further.

The handwriting changed in the winter of 1873. It loosened, quickened — a younger man's hand, or a man writing faster because there was more happening to write about. And there, on a page dated the fourteenth of January, a delivery entry: *One Remington typewriter, crated, from Vesterhavn. To Mrs. E., from her family.*

A typewriter. In 1873, on a rock in the middle of a frozen strait, delivered to the keeper's wife *from her family.*

Ada's pen, which she had been using to take notes, stopped moving. Outside, the storm hit the tower with a sound like a struck bell.

Mrs. E. From her family. A machine that cost more than a keeper earned in a year, sent to a lighthouse where there was no one to type to and no one to type for. Unless there had been something to type. Documents. Letters. Something a family fleeing something might need put down in a hand no graphologist could match to its author.

She turned forward again, hunting. The machine appeared twice more — repairs requested in 1876 (*ribbon, keys sticking*), ribbon ordered again in 1881 — and then, in the visitor log for the spring of 1873, she found what she had been turning pages to find without knowing she was looking for it.

The visitor log listed lodgers, wreck survivors, officials. Most entries were ordinary. But on the ninth of February, 1873, there was a name written in the same younger, quicker hand as everything else that winter:

*M. Voss, lodged.*

And beneath it, in different ink — darker, later, pressed hard enough to emboss the paper — the word *Voss* had been inked out. Not blotted, not corrected. Obliterated. Someone had gone over that name stroke by stroke until it was a black bar, and the blackness of the ink was older than twenty years but not older than twenty-five, and whoever had done it had done it carefully, thoroughly, the way one does a thing one intends never to do twice.

M. Voss. Marta Eriksen, née — what?

Ada sat back. The kitchen was silent except for the tick of the clock and the muffled artillery of the sea. The locked door at the back of the room seemed, in that moment, less like a storeroom door and more like a held breath.

She knew the name Voss. Everyone within a hundred miles knew it, the way everyone knows the name of a burned house. The Voss estate fire of 1872 — the granaries, the east wing, a stable hand convicted of setting it, hanged two years after at Malmö despite his weeping protestations of innocence at trial, which everyone agreed were the usual theater. The estate broken up and sold. The family scattered — Elias Voss to the capital, the daughters married off or vanished, the whole edifice of one of the coast's great fortunes dissolved like sugar in rain.

And a typewriter arriving at a lighthouse fourteen months after the fire, addressed to *Mrs. E.*, *from her family.*

Ada was not a romantic. She distrusted intuition, despised coincidence, and believed with a near-religious fervor that the truth of any matter lived in records — in ledgers, registers, shipping manifests, the patient bureaucratic sediment that human beings left behind them without meaning to. She had built her career on the conviction that people lie and paper doesn't.

But paper, she thought now, staring at the black bar where a name had been, can be made to lie too. It just requires a person. And a person who inks out a name leaves behind the most honest signature of all: the proof that there was something worth hiding.

She heard boots overhead — Felix walking the gallery walk above, his footsteps crossing and recrossing the top of the tower like a man pacing a cell. The light swept past the windows in its long rotation, and each pass laid a stripe of white across the table, across the ledger, across her hands, and took it away again. Sweep, dark. Sweep, dark. As if the house itself were blinking.

*Who are you, Marta Voss?* she thought. *And why does a dead woman's erased name make a living man lock a door?*

She thought of the census question she had asked in September — *has anyone named Voss ever lodged at the light?* — and of how Felix Eriksen had answered *no*, instantly, evenly, without the flicker of a man searching his memory. Because he wasn't searching. He knew. He had known for twenty years, and his answer had been waiting for her the way his lantern had been waiting on the landing tonight.

Her commission was the census. Names, numbers, households. Her instructions ran to eleven pages and contained nothing whatsoever about arson, heirs, or typewriters. The sensible thing — the professional thing — was to finish the household entry, close the book, sleep, cross the strait at dawn, and file her reports, and let the Voss business remain what it had been for two decades: a closed file in a regional archive gathering the dust of official satisfaction.

Ada looked at the black bar a long time.

Then she opened her notebook and wrote, in her own careful hand, the date, and beneath it: *Ledger, p. 112, Feb. 1873. Entry altered. Original name recoverable — impression legible under raking light.* Because she was what she was, and even her conscience filed paperwork.

She closed the ledger, returned it precisely to its place on the shelf, and sat by the dying fire with her notebook on her knee, listening to the storm and to the footsteps pacing overhead, and felt the strange, cold clarity of a decision assembling itself in her the way ice assembles on still water — silently, from the edges inward, unstoppable once begun.

She would come back to this island. Not as census taker. Whatever it cost her, however long it took, she would find out what the keeper was guarding besides the light.

Above her, the footsteps stopped. And in the sudden silence she heard, faintly, from behind the locked door at the back of the kitchen, a sound like a key turning — slow, soft, deliberate — and then the creak of hinges, and then nothing at all.
