## 6. Anders's Debt

The ferry from the mainland ran only twice a week in November, and Anders Eriksen had spent the whole crossing deciding which lie to wear when he stepped off it.

He tried them on the way a poorer man might try on coats. There was the lie of the surprise visit — *I was passing through, brother, and thought of you* — though no one passed through a lighthouse island in the Baltic autumn unless the sea itself had decided it. There was the lie of the errand — *I've brought salt pork and lamp oil, thought you'd be short before the ice* — which had the advantage of being partly true, the crates at his feet attesting to it, and the disadvantage of costing him money he did not have. And there was the oldest lie, the family one, the one their father had worn like a skin until the day he died: the lie of being fine.

Anders looked out at the grey water slapping against the hull and understood, with the clarity that only came to him at the bottom of things, that he was not fine, had not been fine for three years, and had built his whole recent life out of the timber of that one word.

The light grew as they came on. White tower, black gallery, the keeper's cottage hunched at its shoulder like a wife leaning close against the wind. Twenty years he had watched that light from the mainland and thought it the loneliest thing he knew. Now, with the harbour men's voices still in his ears — *we'll want it by Advent, Eriksen, or we'll want something else* — it looked less lonely than safe. A fortress. A place where a man could stand behind a locked door and hear the sea tell him nothing he didn't already owe.

The ferry bumped the pier. Anders took up his crates and climbed down onto the wet stone, and his legs, which had carried him without complaint through forty-eight years, went soft as rope under him.

He had not told Felix he was coming. That, at least, was honest.

* * *

Felix opened the door before Anders had finished knocking, which was its own small rebuke; a keeper heard everything on an island this size, and a brother's tread on his pier was a sound he would know from a thousand others.

"Anders." Not *welcome*, not *it's been four months*. Just the name, set down between them like a stone on a table.

"Felix." Anders hefted the crates. "Pork. Lamp oil. And a bottle of the good stuff, though I'll thank you not to ask what I paid."

"I never ask what you pay." Felix stood back to let him in, and Anders caught it — the flicker of the eyes past his shoulder, down the pier, checking. Checking whether he'd come alone. Twenty years of keeping a light had given his brother the habit of looking for what approached out of the dark, and Anders felt that look go through him like a sounding line, measuring the depth of what he carried.

Inside, the cottage was warm and smelled of bread and lamp oil and, beneath it, that faint mineral coldness that clung to everything on the island, as though the stone itself were always a little damp. A girl — Ida, the ward, grown taller since summer, her hair plaited back severe as a schoolmistress's — took the crates from him with a murmured thanks and carried them to the pantry. Through the inner doorway Anders glimpsed Signe at the table, bent over a ledger, her pen moving in small precise strokes. She looked up. She had Marta's eyes. He had never once been able to look at those eyes without feeling he'd been caught at something.

"Uncle Anders." A nod, courteous, cool. Back to the ledger.

"Signe." He made his voice easy, avuncular. "Working hard, I see. Your father works you like a hired man."

"She works like a keeper's daughter," Felix said, and there was nothing in his tone to argue with, which was Felix's gift: he could close a door in your face so gently you only noticed the draft afterwards.

* * *

Supper was fish, bread, salted pork from Anders's own crate, which Felix had the grace not to remark upon. Ida served and ate quickly and asked, with a hunger she tried to make sound like idle curiosity, questions about the mainland — whether the theatre in the capital was truly lit by electric light, whether the trains were as fast as the papers said. Anders answered generously. It cost nothing to be generous with other men's cities. Signe asked nothing at all, but twice he felt her glance rest on him the way a clerk's glance rests on a figure that doesn't sum.

After the meal Felix dismissed the girls — *Ida, take your slate to the other room; Signe, the oil stores want counting before dark* — with the smoothness of long practice, and then it was the two of them at the table with the lamp between them and the wind beginning to work at the shutters.

"So," Felix said.

"So." Anders turned his glass. He had promised himself he would not drink before he said it, and then he had drunk, and now the words sat in him like stones in a net, all the heavier for being wet.

"You came on the ferry with two crates and a face like a man attending his own funeral," Felix said. "Say it, Anders. Whatever it is. I'm too old and it's too late in the year for the long way round."

Anders looked at his brother. It was a face the sea had made — weathered brown, seamed at the eyes, the beard going iron-grey at the jaw — and it was also, Anders thought with a sudden sour grief, the face of a man who had never in his life wanted anything he couldn't earn with his own two hands. Felix had stood on this rock for twenty years and wanted nothing, and the wanting-nothing had made him rich in the only currency that mattered out here, which was certainty. Anders had wanted everything — a share in the timber trade, a seat at the harbour board, a house in town with a brass plate on the door — and the wanting had made him a beggar wearing a gentleman's coat.

"It's money," he said. Because there was no way to make that word pretty, he said it plainly and let it lie there.

"How much?"

"Four hundred." He watched Felix's face for the flinch and got, instead, stillness, which was worse. "Four hundred crowns, and interest, and it's held by—" He stopped. This was the part he had rehearsed on the ferry, and rehearsing had been useless, because the name still came out of him sideways, like something coughed up. "It's held by men who act for the Voss estate."

The lamp flame stood perfectly still. The wind pressed at the shutters and gave up.

"Voss," Felix said.

"You know the name."

"I know the name."

Anders had known Felix for forty-eight years and had heard him say perhaps ten thousand words, and he could count on one hand the times his brother's voice had gone quiet the way it went quiet now — not soft, but *level*, the way water goes level right before it goes over the edge of something.

"It's an old family," Anders said quickly, filling the silence the way he filled every silence, with talk, with charm, with the sound of himself being all right. "Old money. They hold paper all up and down the coast — shipping shares, warehouse mortgages, half the fishing fleet on the mainland owes them something or other. My creditors sold my paper to their agents in the spring. I've been paying what I could, but the herring failed, you know how it goes, and now they want settlement by Advent or they want—"

"Or they want what?"

"Collateral." Anders made himself smile. "Or a man's future earnings, which amounts to the same. They're not monsters, Felix. They're business. It's business."

"Voss is business," Felix said. The word *Voss* again, and this time Anders heard it — heard what lived underneath it, some old cellar-door of a thing, shut and locked and heavy. "And how does a timber clerk in Karsvik come to owe four hundred crowns to the agents of the Voss estate?"

"Bad luck and worse judgment," Anders said. "The usual recipe. Felix—" He leaned forward. This was the speech. He had built it plank by plank on the ferry and it was rotten through, and he laid it out anyway because there was nothing else. "Brother. You're sitting on the richest thing on this coast and you don't even know it. This island, the light, the position — the Commission would pay a settlement for the lease alone if you ever wanted to come ashore. And there's Marta's things. Her family things. Whatever she brought with her when she came here. Old families like that, they'll pay for their own back. Relics, heirlooms — there's a market for it, there are *agents* for it, I've seen the prices—"

He heard himself and stopped. It sounded exactly like what it was.

Felix had not moved. His hands were flat on the table, and Anders saw that the knuckles had gone pale where they pressed the wood, and that his brother was looking at him with an expression Anders had no name for — not anger, nothing so useful as anger. Something older. Something like a man watching ice go out of a river and knowing what the flood will take with it.

"Her things," Felix said.

"Her things. Her—"

"Marta has been dead six years," Felix said. "And you have never once, in all the years she lived, come across this water to sit at her table. And now, in one autumn, you arrive carrying her name in your mouth like a coin you found in the street." He stood. Not violently. That was the terrible thing — he stood the way he did everything, slowly, with the economy of a man who had learned that every motion on a rock in the sea must be deliberate or it will kill you. "What do they know, Anders? The men who hold your paper. What did you tell them?"

"Nothing." Too fast. "Nothing. I never said a word about— I only asked, I only sounded them, whether there might be—"

"You *sounded* them." Felix turned to the window. Beyond the glass the dusk was coming down over the water, and out in it, right on schedule, the great lamp was waking — Anders could not see it from here but he could feel it, the way you feel a heartbeat through a wall, the whole island's slow pulse beginning its turn. "Do you know what I do, Anders? Every night. I stand in that room up there and I turn a lens that throws a light twenty miles out to sea, so that no man on the water has to die for want of one honest flame. Twenty years. And you come to my island and ask me to sell the one thing my wife ever asked me to keep dark."

Anders's mouth was dry. "She asked you—"

"She asked me nothing that concerns you." Felix said it flat, final, a door closing. "The answer is no. There is nothing of Marta's for sale. There never was. And if those men sent you here thinking a brother's face would open this door—" He shook his head, and for one moment, one awful moment, Anders saw not anger in his brother's face but something that looked almost like pity, and pity was the one coin Anders could not bear to be paid in. "Then they don't know either of us."

"They know I'm good for nothing," Anders said, and heard his own voice crack on the small honesty, and hated it. "That's all they know about me, Felix. That and what I owe."

For a moment — Anders would remember this moment all his life, would turn it over in the dark like a stone with two faces — for one moment Felix's hand came up and rested on his shoulder. The grip was heavy and warm and brief.

"Sleep in the loft," Felix said. "The girls will give you supper again tomorrow if you stay. But Anders—" and the hand was gone, and the warmth with it, and the voice had gone back to that level place above the drop. "Whatever you promised those men. Whatever you told them to buy yourself another month. You'll tell me. Before you leave this island, you'll tell me all of it."

* * *

He did not sleep in the loft. He lay in it, under a blanket that smelled of cedar and Signe's childhood, and stared at the rafters while the wind came up out of the northeast and began, note by note, to build the season's first real gale.

Four hundred crowns. He turned the number over. It was not even the whole of it — the whole of it was the afternoon in the back room of the Harbourmaster's Arms in Karsvik, the man with the grey gloves who had not introduced himself and had not needed to, who had listened to Anders's drunken lament about his rich, secretive brother on the island, and had smiled the way a man smiles at a door he has just discovered is unlocked.

*The Eriksens,* the man had said. *Yes. We have an old interest in the Eriksens.*

Anders had told himself since, in the long sleepless arithmetic of the nights, that he had said nothing. That a drunk man's rambling about debts was not information. That he had never spoken the word *typewriter*, never described the locked room, never — God, never — mentioned the name Marta had been born with. He had only listened. Only nodded. Only let the grey-gloved man believe what he already seemed to believe, because the man had said, standing, pulling on those gloves: *Perhaps an arrangement could be made. A man's debts are a heavy thing to carry into winter. Perhaps your brother's island holds something that could lighten it. You'll let us know what you find.*

*You'll let us know.*

He had let them know. That was the truth he could not drown, though he had tried, in the three weeks since, with aquavit and with work and with the ferry's slow grey crossing. He had sent word from the mainland post office at Hällnäs — a single line, unsigned, the coward's telegraph: *He will not sell. She kept nothing. It is as you thought.* He had told himself it was a kindness, closing the door for them, saving everyone the trouble.

But he had also taken the ferry to this island. He had also sat at his brother's table and put Marta's name in his mouth and asked — asked! — with the crates of pork as his witness, as if a brother's love were a thing that could be invoiced.

And there was another thing, the thing that had been sitting in his chest since the harbour at Karsvik, since before: the grey-gloved man had not been alone. There had been a younger one, a clerk, sharp-faced, taking notes, and when Anders left he had heard the clerk say to the man in the gloves, low, not meant for him but meant for the air: *And the woman from the census office — Lindqvist — she's already flagged the household. She's asking after the name Voss all up this coast.*

Voss. The name Felix had said twice tonight as if setting down something hot.

The wind rose. Below him, through the floor, Anders could hear the cottage settling into its night sounds — a door, the banked stove, Felix's slow tread checking the lamp mechanism one last time before he climbed the tower. And beneath all of it, so faint he might have invented it, from behind the locked door at the end of the passage: nothing. Silence. But a silence, tonight, that felt less like an empty room than like a held breath.

In the morning, he told himself. In the morning I'll tell him everything, the gloves, the post office, all of it, and let him hate me and be done.

But even as he made the promise, Anders knew the shape of his own soul too well to trust it. He was a man who had spent his whole life mistaking the next morning for a country he could actually reach.

Out in the dark, the great lamp turned, and turned, and turned, sweeping its one white eye across a sea that was already, far to the northeast, beginning to blacken with weather — and somewhere behind it, on the mainland he had just left, a woman with a census book and a young magistrate with a warrant were asking after a name that Anders had carried across the water like a fever in his blood.

*Voss.*

By morning, the storm would close the crossing. And by the time it opened again, everything Anders had set loose would already be on its way to the island.
