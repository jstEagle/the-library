## 7. Ida's Lessons

The scraps lived in a biscuit tin under Ida's bed, and she counted them the way other children counted treasures.

There were forty-one now. Forty-one pieces of paper saved from the fire, from the wrapping of parcels, from the backs of old notices nailed up in the mainland post office years ago and brought across the water in Signe's coat pocket like smuggled jewels. On each one, in pencil worn down nearly to the wood, Ida practiced her letters. *A* was easy — a little roof with legs. *E* fought her, all those straight lines that wanted to lean. And *S*, which was her own letter, the first letter of her own small self, took her three winters to make graceful, and even now it sometimes came out looking like a snake that had thought better of things.

She sat by the kitchen window on the last morning before the weather closed in, the tin open on her knees, copying words off the flour sack because Signe had said that was how you learned — you copied what was in front of you until your hand knew it without asking.

*FLOUR.*

*WHEAT.*

*SALT.*

She wrote them twice each, tongue between her teeth, breath fogging the cold pane. Beyond the glass the island lay grey and still under a sky the color of dishwater, and out at the point the lighthouse stood with its black bands and its white eye, patient as a grandmother. Somewhere down there Felix was checking the oil stores, and Signe was doing her arithmetic with the coal ledger, and somewhere —

Somewhere behind the locked door at the end of the passage, something went *tick*.

Ida stopped writing.

It always did this. Not every day — some days the house was silent as a held breath — but often enough that she had built her whole understanding of the world around the sound. It was not a clock, though it ticked like one. Clocks were steady. This was different. This was irregular, quick-quick-slow, quick-quick-quick-slow, like a heart that was thinking about what it felt. Like a heart that had opinions.

Signe said it was mice.

But mice did not stop when you pressed your ear to the wood. Mice did not go utterly, guiltily silent the way the thing behind that door went silent, every single time, as if it knew it had been caught.

Ida blew on her ink-less letters to dry nothing, and thought about the locked room the way she thought about everything: carefully, and alone, and without telling anyone what she was thinking.

She had been seven when they brought her to the island, an orphan off the boat from the mainland with a paper tag pinned to her coat — *IDA HOLM, AGE 7, NO KNOWN FAMILY* — and she could still feel that tag sometimes, its pin pricking through wool, its string scratching her chin. She remembered the crossing. She remembered being sick over the rail while gulls screamed at her like creditors. What she did not remember — what no one would ever tell her, no matter how many ways she arranged the question — was anything at all about before. Who had signed the tag. Who had cried at the dock, if anyone had. Whether anyone had.

The Eriksens did not speak of such things. That was the first law of the house, unspoken but absolute, and Ida had learned it faster than her letters: *the past is a room with a locked door, and we do not knock.*

So instead she knocked in the only language she was allowed.

*Dear Mama,* she wrote on a scrap, and then sat looking at it, because she did not know who Mama had been, and the word looked lonely there at the top of the page, standing at a door that would never open.

She turned the scrap over. Better to practice something useful.

*THE QUICK BROWN FOX,* she wrote. *JUMPS OVER THE LAZY DOG.* Signe had taught her that sentence, saying it contained every letter, and Ida loved it fiercely — loved the idea of a sentence that held the whole alphabet inside itself like a mother holding children, none of them left behind.

If only everything worked that way.

* * *

In the afternoon, with the barometer falling so fast that the needle seemed ashamed of itself, Signe called her to the table for lessons proper.

Lessons were Signe's domain. Felix taught the practical things — knots, lamps, weather, the names of every rock between here and the mainland, which ones lied at high tide — but letters and figures belonged to Signe, who had been meant for the conservatory on the mainland, everyone said so, and whose hands grew restless in winter the way caged birds grow restless at dusk.

"Again," Signe said, tapping the slate. "Copperplate. You're carving, not writing."

"I don't see why it matters." Ida bent over the slate anyway. "Nobody here writes letters."

"People write letters everywhere. Even here. Especially here." Something crossed Signe's face then, quick as a fish under ice — there and gone before Ida could name it. "Papa gets letters."

"Papa burns them."

"He reads them first."

"He burns them after," Ida said. "I've seen him. He stands at the stove and holds each page until the corner catches, and he watches it all the way down, like he's making sure it can't come back. Why would a man burn a letter unless the letter frightened him?"

Signe's chalk stopped.

For a moment the only sound was the wind beginning to find its voice around the eaves, low and testing, a singer clearing its throat. Then Signe said, lightly, too lightly, "Where do you get these ideas?"

"The same place you got yours," Ida said. "From watching."

That was the true difference between them, Ida thought, and it was not age. Signe watched the world the way her father did — as a keeper watches, cataloguing, guarding, deciding what needed tending. Ida watched the way a cat watches a mousehole: patiently, hungrily, waiting for whatever lived inside to come out. She could not help it. A child with no past becomes a creature of the present tense, and the present tense is full of details.

And the details said: *there is something in this house that everyone knows about and no one will say.*

The locked room. The burned letters. The way Felix's hand lingered on that door handle each night on his way past — a touch so brief it might have been accidental, except that it happened every time, the way a man touches a wound he has decided not to complain about.

The way the thing inside ticked.

"Write your sentence," Signe said at last. "*The quick brown fox.* We'll do capitals tomorrow if the weather keeps us in."

Ida wrote it. Her fox leapt cleanly over her dog, and both animals were beautiful, and neither of them asked questions.

* * *

Evening came early, the way it did in November — the dark arriving like a debt collector, no negotiation. The lamp upstairs swung into its rotation, its beam strobing through the kitchen windows in long slow pulses, so that the whole room breathed with light and shadow, light and shadow, and Ida sat in the rhythm of it darning socks that were mostly hole and learning, stitch by stitch, the particular silence of the household.

Felix was in his chair with the Bible he never read aloud. Signe was at the ledger, lips moving over figures. Anders — Felix's brother, who had come across the water two days ago smelling of tobacco and trouble — was absent, gone down to the harbor to look at the boats, or so he'd said, though Ida had noticed he took the road toward the tavern instead, and she had noticed, too, the way Felix's eyes had followed him out, flat and cold as the sea before weather.

The house was quiet enough that Ida could hear it again.

*Tick. Tick-tick. Tick.*

No. Not tonight. Tonight the sound was wrong — it wasn't ticking at all tonight. Tonight there was a slower sound beneath the ticks, a whispering, rhythmic, like pages turning. Like someone in that locked room reading.

Her pencil of courage, the one she kept hidden even from herself, rolled forward.

She set down her darning.

"I'm going to bed," she announced, to no one in particular, in the voice of a girl announcing bedtime, which fooled nobody and was not meant to.

"Mm," said Felix, not looking up.

The passage to the sleeping rooms ran past the locked door. Everyone's route ran past the locked door; that was part of its power, that it stood in the middle of ordinary life like a stone in a stream. Ida walked the passage slowly. The lamp beam swept through the passage window — bright, dark, bright — and in each pulse of brightness she saw the door anew: black oak, iron hinge, a keyhole that had once had a key on Felix's ring and did not anymore, which meant the key lived somewhere else now, somewhere secret, and Ida intended, someday, calmly and without hurry, to know where.

She pressed her ear to the wood, the way she had done a hundred times, expecting the guilty silence, the sudden stopping.

Instead the sound continued. Pages. Turning. And beneath them, fainter than faint, a voice — Felix's voice, so low it was barely more than breath, reading words she could not catch, in a tone she had never heard him use anywhere else in the world.

It was the tone men used at gravesides. It was the tone of a man reading to someone who was not there.

Ida stood in the cold passage with her heart going like a bird in a box, and she understood, with the terrible clarity available only to children and the dying, that she was hearing something she was not supposed to know existed: Felix Eriksen, keeper of the light, keeper of silences, talking to the locked room as if the room could answer.

As if someone in it could.

She slipped away to her alcove before the beam swung round again, and lay in the dark with the biscuit tin hugged to her chest like a doll, and did her bravest and most forbidden arithmetic.

She had no mother. No father. No family, no name beyond the tag, no before. Everything she was had been handed to her secondhand by people who kept their own histories behind locked doors — and she had accepted it, hadn't she, gratefully, quietly, the way you accept bread when you are starving and do not ask what field it grew in.

But she was eleven now. And eleven is the age when hunger stops being polite.

*I will learn what's in that room,* she promised the darkness, and the darkness, being honest, made no promise back. *Not to take it. Just to know it. The way I know my letters. One page at a time.*

Outside, the wind rose another register, and the first spit of rain hit the window like flung gravel, and far off across the blackening sea the ferry horn sounded once — a long, mournful note — signaling the last crossing before the storms closed the strait.

Footsteps passed her door. Felix's, heavy and unhurried, going toward the lamp stairs. But halfway along the passage they stopped, and Ida heard, through the thin wall, the small unmistakable music of a key ring being taken from a hook.

Then the footsteps resumed — not toward the stairs.

Toward the locked room.
