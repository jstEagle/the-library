## 9. Ice on the River

The cold came up through the floorboards first, before it touched anything else. Signe Eriksen learned winter the way other children learned their letters — by feel, by habit, by the slow grammar of a house that stood against the sea. By the middle of November she could read the season in the sound of her own boots: a dry knock on dry wood meant frost had reached the cellar; a wet squeak meant the sea still breathed warmth into the stone.

She sat at the kitchen table with the household ledger open before her and did what she did every morning after breakfast, while her father was still up in the lamp room and Ida was still asleep in the loft. She counted.

Coal, forty-one hundredweight remaining. Oil, sixty-two gallons in the store tanks, plus the five drums under tarp behind the boat shed that Father didn't know she'd found. Lamp wicks, eleven new, cut to size. Salt pork, flour, dried cod enough for four mouths until April if April came late. She wrote the figures small and even, because she had learned from her mother's old ledgers — three of them, kept in the bottom drawer of the desk, in handwriting so fine it looked printed — that a keeper's wife who wrote carelessly was a keeper's wife who ran short in February and wept about it in March.

Signe did not intend to run short or weep.

She dipped her pen, hesitated over the oil column, then added a note in the margin: *Ask Papa if the drum behind the shed is counted.* It was a lie of omission she permitted herself, one small one. She had known about the drum since October. She had known about the letter longer than that.

She thought of it now the way she thought of everything lately — sideways, quickly, before the thought could turn and look at her. The oil locker. The third week of October. She had gone down for a spare burner glass and found her father standing at the locker with his back to the door, and something in his hands — not oil cans, not parts. Paper. He'd heard her on the stair and turned with the speed of a man much younger than fifty-four, and the paper was gone into his coat, and he'd said, *Mind the step, that last one's loose,* as though that were why she'd come down at all.

She had said nothing. She had taken her burner glass and climbed back up and written nothing anywhere, and told no one, not even Ida, especially not Ida, who loved her father with the open-handed love of an orphan and would have asked him straight out, and asking him straight out would have broken something none of them could mend.

*We are a house of quiet,* Signe thought sometimes. *We keep silence the way we keep the lamp.*

She hadn't always understood that. When she was nine she had asked why they never spoke of Mama's family, and her father had said, *Because they are far away,* and she had believed him, because at nine you believe the people who feed you and light your window. At seventeen she believed him differently — she knew the sentence was true and also not true, the way a lamp is lit and also burning down. Somewhere far away there were people connected to Marta Eriksen, and her father carried them in his coat pocket like a letter he could neither read nor burn.

She ruled a line beneath the ledger column and blew on the ink.

Outside, the wind had come around north during the night, and the sea beyond the point had that hammered-metal look it got when the water temperature dropped toward the ice line. Signe had lived her whole life on this rock and had seen the strait freeze twice — once when she was six, once when she was twelve — and both times the world had changed shape around the freezing, boats stopped, supplies stopped, letters stopped, everything stopping except the lamp, which burned regardless, because that was the covenant of the house.

*If it freezes early,* she thought, doing arithmetic she didn't want to do, *the conservatory letters stop too.*

She had been writing to the Academy of Music in Christiania for two years now. Three exchanges of letters with a Frau Dahl, mistress of piano studies, who had heard Signe play at the midsummer market two summers past — Signe had played on a borrowed instrument, a parlor upright wheeled onto a cart, and half the fishing fleet had stood listening with their caps off — and who had written back: *Your touch is unusual for a self-taught player. Come south when you can. We will find a way.* The promise was vague and precious and entirely dependent on the post, which was dependent on the ferry, which was dependent on water staying water.

Signe looked at her figures again. Coal sufficient. Oil sufficient. Food sufficient. Everything sufficient except time, and time was not a thing a ledger could hold.

* * *

Ida came down at seven, as she always did, hair braided before she descended because Felix had taught her that a keeper's household rose finished, not assembled. She was fourteen now and growing tall in a way that made her own limbs seem surprising to her; she crossed the kitchen the way foals cross paddocks, all length and apology.

"You've done the count already," Ida said, seeing the ledger. "Can I see? I'm learning the columns."

"You're learning the columns," Signe agreed, sliding the book over. "What do you make of the oil?"

Ida bent over the page, lips moving slightly as she read. Signe watched her and felt the familiar tightening in her chest that was half tenderness and half grief. Ida Vinter had come to them eight years ago off a boat from the mainland with a bundle of clothes, a dead mother's name, and eyes that catalogued every kindness as though expecting it to be repossessed. She practiced her letters on wrapping paper and fish-box slats and once, memorably, on the inside of a flour sack, and she treated each lesson as though literacy might be revoked without warning.

"Sixty-two gallons," Ida said. "Is that a lot?"

"It's enough." Signe hesitated. "It's more than enough, actually. Papa ordered extra in October."

"Why would he order extra?" Ida frowned at the column. "Unless he thinks the supply boats can't come."

The question hung there, innocent and enormous. Signe took the ledger back and said, "He likes to be sure," and hated how easily the family silence came to her mouth, how it fit her tongue like her own language, because it was her own language, inherited, unexamined, hers.

Felix came in at half past, smelling of lamp oil and cold iron, stamping frost off his boots. He was grayer this autumn than last — Signe saw it now, the way you see weather change only after it has fully arrived — but his hands were steady as ever as he poured coffee, and his voice when he said good morning to each of them was exactly level, exactly warm, exactly calibrated, and Signe thought: *He is doing it right now. The thing with the letter. He is being perfectly normal at us.*

"Papa," she said, "the post boat was due yesterday."

"It'll come today." He drank standing, looking out the window at the gray water. "Wind's fair. Torvald doesn't sail in fog but he sails in most else."

"The strait's going to close soon."

"Soon isn't closed." But something moved behind his face when she said it, some flicker she couldn't name, and he set down his cup harder than necessary. "There's bread dough risen. Bake it before noon."

He went out. Through the window Signe watched him take the cliff path toward the landing stage, walking with his collar up, a man going to meet a boat, or to look for one, or to avoid one. She couldn't tell which, and that inability — the fact that she had lived nineteen years beside this man and could not read him on this one point — sat in her stomach like swallowed river ice.

* * *

Torvald Brandt's boat *Petrel* came around the point at eleven, late and wallowing, flying a scrap of red cloth at her masthead that Signe needed a full minute to interpret.

Red cloth at the masthead was the mainland's telegraph. It meant: *official word aboard.* It meant the district office had sent something, or the shipping board had, and everyone on the island was expected to gather at the landing to hear it read aloud, because half the island couldn't read and the half that could included Signe, who nevertheless waited with the crowd because waiting with the crowd was how you learned what people feared before they said it.

By noon nearly the whole island stood on the shingle above the landing stage — forty souls, give or take, the fishing families and the goat farmers and old Halvardsen who claimed to be ninety and was probably seventy. The sky had gone the color of pewter. Out in the strait the water was smoking, that eerie exhalation of vapor where sea met killing air, and someone near Signe muttered that the smoke-water meant ice within the fortnight.

Torvald came ashore with a folded paper in his fist and a face that gave away less than her father's, which was saying something. He was a broad man gone soft at the middle, and he read slowly, running his finger along the lines, and the crowd quieted the way water quiets before it freezes.

"By order of the District Shipping Board," he read, "noticing all residents of the outer islands: owing to forecast ice conditions in the river channel and approaches thereto, the steam ferry *Vestfold* will make her final crossing of the season on the twenty-sixth day of November, calling at Havnø, Skarholmen, and the mainland quay at Brenna. After the twenty-sixth day of November, no vessel will cross the channel until such time as the ice breaks, being anticipated the first week of April or thereafter. All persons requiring passage south, for trade, medical need, or other lawful purpose, must present themselves at the Havnø landing no later than dawn on the twenty-sixth. Signed under seal."

He lowered the paper. For a moment nobody spoke. Then the whole shingle began talking at once.

Signe stood very still among the noise and did arithmetic.

The twenty-sixth of November. Today was the nineteenth. Seven days.

Seven days in which to decide — decide what? That was the strange part, the part that made her skin prickle. She was nineteen years old, she had a promised place waiting at an academy, and she had assumed for two years that she would go south eventually, next spring perhaps, when the money was saved, when the timing was right. The ferry notice didn't force any decision she hadn't already deferred. And yet her heart was hammering as though the notice had her name on it.

Because it wasn't just her decision, she realized. It was the household's. If she went on the twenty-sixth, she wouldn't return until April. Four months. Her father alone on the light with Ida — no, with Ida it was manageable, Ida was practically a keeper herself. Unless Ida went somewhere. Unless—

She looked across the crowd and found her father.

He was standing apart, as he often did at gatherings, near the boat shed with his arms folded, and he was not looking at Torvald or the notice or the crowd. He was looking at the water. And his face —

Signe had spent her life reading that face the way sailors read the horizon, and what she saw on it now frightened her more than the ice notice did. It wasn't surprise. It wasn't even dread, exactly. It was recognition. He looked like a man who has been told a train's arrival time and has been dreading precisely that train for years.

Their eyes met across the crowd. He held hers for one second, two — and then he smiled, small and reassuring, and turned away, and began walking up the cliff path toward the house with long strides, as though the walk itself were an errand he'd suddenly remembered.

Signe did not follow. Not immediately. She stayed on the shingle and let the crowd wash around her and listened.

"—my brother needs the crossing, his lungs won't take another winter out here—"

"—I'm taking the ewes' wool over, sell it at Brenna before the prices drop—"

"—heard the ice came early upriver, whole fleet stuck at Lillehammer already—"

And then, nearer, lower, two of the older fishermen, Gundersen and his son, heads together: "Magistrate's clerk came through Havnø Tuesday. Asking questions up and down the coast, they say. Old business, somebody said. Twenty-year-old business."

"Old business," the son repeated. "Whose?"

"How should I know? Coast is full of old business."

They moved off. Signe stood in the wind with the taste of iron in her mouth and thought about a letter hidden in an oil locker, and extra oil ordered in October, and her father's face at the words *final crossing*, and the red cloth at the masthead, and she felt — clearly, coldly, for the first time in her life — that she was living inside a story whose earlier chapters had been withheld from her.

*Seven days,* she thought. *Whatever is coming, it comes on the twenty-sixth, same as everything else.*

* * *

That evening she baked the bread, fed the goats, mended the storm shutter hinge on the north window, and played two hours on the cottage piano — the one decent thing in the house, bought used by her father the year Mama died, an extravagance he had never explained and she had never questioned, one of the many soft inexplicable things about him. She played her scales, her études, the Chopin mazurka she was preparing for Frau Dahl, and she played them well and mechanically, and underneath the music her mind ran its columns the way she ran the ledger.

Coal, sufficient. Oil, more than sufficient. Food, sufficient. Time — she struck the column mentally and left it blank, because time was the one entry she could not count anymore. Time now had a date attached. Time now ended on the twenty-sixth of November and resumed in April, and everything unsaid in this house had exactly seven days either to be said or to be sealed under the ice with everything else.

After supper, when Ida had gone up to the loft and her father had taken his lantern out for the evening trimming, Signe opened the desk drawer and took out her mother's three ledgers.

She had gone through them a hundred times for recipes and accounts. Tonight she went through them for something else. She didn't know what — a name, a place, anything that might connect to *old business, twenty years old*. Her mother had written beautifully, densely, recording eggs and oil and weather and, once a year, a single line that Signe had never thought much about:

*Anniversary. Kept indoors. F. kind.*

Every year, the same week — the second week of October. *Kept indoors.* As though the anniversary of something were marked not by mourning openly but by staying inside it.

Signe closed the ledger and pressed her palms flat against its cover and sat in the lamplight listening to her father's boots on the tower stairs overhead, up and up, toward the light.

On the twenty-sixth, the ferry would leave at dawn from Havnø, four miles across the strait by rowboat, and whoever intended to be on it had seven days to become certain. She was almost certain. She wanted the conservatory the way she wanted breath. And yet she could not shake the feeling that the ferry was not merely a ferry — that it was a door, and doors swing both ways, and her father had looked at the ice notice like a man watching a door he had long known would someday open.

Overhead, the lamp swung into its first rotation of the night, and the beam swept the ceiling of the kitchen, once, twice, like a slow searchlight looking for something hidden.

Down in the oil locker, four feet of stone and timber below her chair, a letter waited in the dark with her father's secret folded inside it.

Signe put the ledger away, banked the fire, and made herself a promise with the cold coming up through the floorboards: seven days. Whatever it was — whatever he was keeping — she would have it before the channel froze. Because after the twenty-sixth there would be no crossing, no post, no leaving, no telling, until spring. And she could not spend another winter inside a silence she was only now discovering had walls.

Upstairs, faintly, through the floor, she heard the clock strike ten — and beneath it, or imagined beneath it, from the locked room at the end of the passage she had never once been allowed to enter, something that sounded almost like ticking.
