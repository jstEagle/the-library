## 10. The Magistrate's Warrant

The letter came on a Tuesday, folded inside the district post-bag like a knife wrapped in linen.

Ada Lindqvist read it twice at her desk in the census office at Hammerstad, once for the words and once for what lay beneath them. The paper was heavy stock, cream-colored, stamped with the seal of the district court. It requested — no, it *instructed* — her attendance upon Magistrate Halvard Rehn, who would arrive on the morning steamer to conduct inquiries pertaining to an outstanding matter of property and evidence, reference Voss, estate of, 1871.

Twenty-two years. She had been nine years old when the Voss house burned, old enough to remember the glow on the southern horizon that her father had called a barn fire, though everyone in Hammerstad knew better by morning. She remembered the convicted man's name spoken in the market with a strange hush afterward, as though the syllables themselves might catch.

She set the letter down and looked out the window at the gray water, where the islands sat scattered like dropped stones. Somewhere out there was Skarholmen light, and its keeper, and his ledger with its line of ink drawn careful as a scar through a name that should not have been there at all.

*M. Voss.*

She had turned that inked-out name over in her mind for three weeks now, through the storms that stranded her at the light and through the long boat ride home after. She had told herself it meant nothing. Widows remarried; daughters were born under other roofs; clerks made errors and corrected them. There were a hundred explanations for a struck-through name in a household register, and only one of them was interesting, and interesting things were usually false.

But she had seen Felix Eriksen's face when she asked whether anyone named Voss had ever lodged at the station. He hadn't flinched. That was the trouble. A man with nothing to hide flinches or laughs or asks why you'd think such a thing. Eriksen had done none of these. He had simply gone still, the way water goes still over something deep, and said *no* in a voice so level it could have been used to calibrate instruments. And his pen — he had been mending the lamp wick at the time, but he held a pen in his other hand, always, a habit she'd noticed — his pen had stopped moving against the cloth.

Ada had spent six years taking down what people told her about themselves. She knew every species of human silence: the shy kind, the sullen kind, the kind that came from grief too large for speech. Eriksen's silence was none of these. His was the practiced silence of a man who has answered this question before, in some other year, to some other questioner, and has had two decades to polish his answer smooth.

Now the state had come asking again.

* * *

Magistrate Halvard Rehn arrived looking younger than his office.

That was Ada's first thought when she met him at the steamer pier on Wednesday morning: *he cannot be more than thirty.* He was tall and narrow, with the pale, unweathered face of a man who worked indoors, and he wore his coat buttoned to the throat against the wind though the morning was merely cold, not cruel. He carried a single leather case and held it the way seminarians hold scripture — close to the body, angled slightly inward, as if the world might snatch it.

"Frøken Lindqvist." He shook her hand with a brisk, dry grip. "You received my letter."

"I did. Though I confess I don't understand my part in this. The census is finished for the season. My schedules are filed."

"They are." He fell into step beside her along the pier, matching her pace without being asked, which she noted and did not entirely forgive. "Which is precisely why I want you. Your schedules are the most complete record of who lived where on this coast between August and October. Every household. Every lodger. Every servant and ward." He glanced at her sideways. "You are the only map I have of the autumn."

"A map of what?"

He did not answer at once. They passed the fish sheds, their tar-black walls sweating in the damp, and a boy hauling a barrel up the ramp who touched his cap to the magistrate and got no reply. At the end of the pier Rehn stopped and set his case down on the planks, and only then did he look at her directly. His eyes were light gray, almost colorless, and they had the particular patience of a man accustomed to waiting out witnesses.

"In 1871," he said, "the Voss estate at Solvik burned. A man named Kollen was tried and convicted of arson and the death of the housekeeper's daughter. He is serving his sentence still, in the prison at Tønsberg. Last spring, a petition reached the department — a petition from Kollen himself, sworn before the chaplain, maintaining his innocence and asserting that proof exists which would acquit him. Proof in writing." He paused. "Typed."

"Typed," Ada repeated.

"The Voss family kept one of those new machines — a writing ball, a typewriter, whatever the trade calls them now. It vanished the night of the fire. The original warrant for its recovery was never executed because the family declined to pursue it. Declined, Frøken Lindqvist." Something moved behind the gray eyes, quick as a fish turning. "A family whose house has burned declines to search for the machine that might tell them who burned it. I was twenty years too young to sit in judgment then. But I can read a refusal, even one twenty years cold."

"And you believe this machine is still on the coast."

"I believe it never left the district. Things that vanish into the sea stay lost. Things that vanish into houses—" He picked up his case again. "Houses keep them. Houses are patient."

They walked up from the pier toward the office. Ada found herself slowing, and made herself not slow, and hated that she had to make herself do anything at all.

"What would you have of me?" she asked.

"Your schedules. Names of households on the outer islands. And your recollection — anything unusual you encountered in your rounds. Strangers lodging where strangers shouldn't be. Families that seemed— " he searched — "*arranged*. Assembled for viewing, like a parlor shut all summer and opened when company comes."

Ada opened the office door and stood aside to let him pass, using the motion to buy herself the length of a breath.

In her mind, unbidden, rose the image of the Eriksen kitchen on the night the storm pinned her at Skarholmen: Signe at the stove with her arithmetic books, precise as a clerk herself; the ward, Ida, small and watchful, practicing letters on scraps of brown paper; Anders Eriksen, the brother, loud with mainland talk and mainland debts. And Felix, moving through it all like a lighthouse beam — steady, sweeping, illuminating exactly what he chose and leaving the rest in darkness.

An assembled parlor? No. If anything, the household had felt *under*-assembled, pared down, stripped of anything that might invite a second look. She thought of the locked room off the landing. She thought of the ticking she'd heard through its door in the dead hours — no, that had been Ida who mentioned the ticking, Ida who spoke of the locked room the way children speak of a bear in the woods, half terror and half love.

*A second heart,* the girl had called it.

"You'll find the schedules orderly," Ada said aloud. "I keep good records."

"That is why I asked for you specifically." Rehn set his case on her desk and opened it. Inside lay papers squared with a ruler's precision: the old warrant, yellowed at the folds; a copy of the trial transcript; and a photograph, sepia and foxed, of a great house with white columns and a garden going soft around the edges. Solvik, before the fire. Standing on a table on the veranda, small and dark in the image, sat a machine with round black keys.

Ada looked at it longer than she needed to.

"You've been to Skarholmen already this season," Rehn said. It was not a question.

"I conducted the census there in September."

"And?"

"And the household was as registered. Felix Eriksen, keeper, widower. His daughter Signe. His ward, Ida — parentage unknown, entered as such. His brother Anders, visiting from Hammerstad." Each fact laid down flat and clean, like coins on a counter.

"Lodgers?"

"None."

"Anyone ever named Voss associated with the station?"

And there it was. The same question she had put to Felix Eriksen across his own table, coming back to her now in a magistrate's voice, and she understood with sudden clarity that this was how questions worked — they were never spent, only passed hand to hand until someone finally bled on them.

"No," Ada said.

Rehn watched her. He had a way of watching that made silence feel like an answer being read aloud. She kept her face in the mild professional arrangement she used with reluctant farmers, and after a moment he nodded and turned to the papers, and she let her breath go slowly through her nose.

She had just lied to an officer of the court.

Not a large lie. An omission, a door left unopened. But she felt it in her chest the way one feels a stone in a boot — small, hard, present with every step — and beneath the discomfort something else stirred, something that alarmed her far more: relief. Warm and shameful, like brandy taken before noon.

*Why?*

Because Eriksen had stood bare-handed in a gale to save two drowning fishermen while the sea tried to kill all three of them — she'd heard the story in the tavern within a day of getting back to the mainland, embellished, no doubt, but the bones true. Because his daughter tallied coal like a woman keeping a shop honest. Because the ward had shown Ada her letter scraps without shame, *I can write my name now, see*, and the girl's pride had gone straight through Ada's armor into whatever soft place she still owned. Because twenty years ago the state had convicted a man, and the state was now asking Ada to help it hunt a machine rather than ask itself whether the conviction had been wrong.

Or simply because Felix Eriksen had said *no* in that still, deep-water voice, and she had chosen to believe him, and having chosen, she found she could not stop.

Law and conscience, beginning their long argument in her chest, and she suspected neither would win cleanly.

* * *

They took the mail boat out to Skarholmen on Thursday, because the weather held — barely, grudgingly, the sky the color of dirty wool and the wind backing around north with a promise in it that made the fishermen at the harbor shake their heads.

Rehn stood at the bow rail the whole crossing, coat whipping, watching the light station grow from a smudge to a shape to a place. Ada stood nearer the stern with the boatman, and studied the magistrate when he couldn't see her. There was something in his stance she recognized from her father, who had been a pastor: the posture of a man carrying a message he believes in completely, and knows will not be welcomed. Conviction and dread, sharing one spine.

"He's young for it," the boatman said, following her gaze. He spat leeward. "Young magistrates come north hungry. Old ones come north retired. You watch which kind he turns out to be."

"Which do you hope?"

"Neither." He adjusted the tiller. "I hope he freezes solid and goes home. Nothing good comes ashore with a warrant in its pocket. Ask any island." He said it lightly, but his eyes stayed on the breaking swell past the point, and Ada thought: *they know. Out here they already know, or guess, or smell it on the wind the way they smell weather. The coast has been holding this secret for twenty years and the whole coast is complicit, and no one has ever once said so aloud.*

The channel between the point and the open sea was running white. The boatman took it with the bored competence of a man who had done it ten thousand times, and then the station was above them, gray stone and white trim, the lantern room glinting dully under its cloud cover, and Felix Eriksen was standing at the landing steps as if he had known to be there — which, Ada realized, he probably had. Keepers watched horizons for a living. A strange boat was weather, too.

"Magistrate," Eriksen said, when Rehn introduced himself at the top of the steps. He did not offer his hand, and Rehn did not appear to expect one. "Wind's freshening. You'll want to be off again before evening if the boatman's wise."

"I may need more than an afternoon."

Eriksen's face did nothing at all. "Then you'll want the guest bunk aired. Signe!"

The household assembled in the kitchen the way Ada remembered, and she watched Rehn take its measure the way she had taken hers in September: the daughter, reserved and exact; the ward, pressed against the wall near the woodbox, eyes moving from face to face like a bird tracking cats; the brother, who had arrived from somewhere smelling faintly of the tavern and who greeted the word *magistrate* with a smile a half-second too slow. Only Felix was unreadable. Felix was a closed door with excellent paint.

Rehn stated his business plainly, over coffee Signe poured with a steady hand. He sought certain property of the Voss estate, lost in 1871, believed possibly conveyed to this district. He produced the warrant. He laid the photograph of the typewriter on the Eriksen table, next to the bread board, and Ada saw Ida stare at it — stare, and go white, and look at the ceiling, toward the landing, toward the locked room, with an expression that tore at Ada worse than any adult's fear could have.

"You understand," Rehn said, "this is a matter of evidence in a capital case. The conviction stands. The man sits in prison. If such a machine exists within my jurisdiction, concealing it is a crime — not a technical one. A crime against a man's life."

"We keep lamps and ledgers here," Felix said. "No machines worth a magistrate's crossing."

"You had a visitor in September who asked you about the name Voss."

It was said mildly. Almost gently. But the kitchen changed temperature. Signe's cup paused above its saucer. Anders found urgent business with the window. And Felix Eriksen looked at Ada — only at Ada, one long level look, not accusatory, which was somehow infinitely worse — before returning his gaze to the magistrate.

"People ask keepers many things," he said. "Boats are late, names get confused. No Voss here, Magistrate. Never was, so far as my books show."

"So far as your books show," Rehn repeated softly, and wrote something in his notebook, and the scratch of the pencil was the loudest sound in the room.

* * *

He stayed the night. The storm the boatman had smelled came down early, sealing the channel in spray and dark, and there was nothing for it but the guest bunk and the magistrate's patience.

Near midnight, unable to sleep in the unfamiliar weight of the house's sounds — the tower creaking, the wind worrying the glass, the fog signal breathing offshore — Ada went down to the kitchen for water and found the household ledger lying open on the side table where Felix had left it, perhaps carelessly, perhaps not. She had read it in September. She knew its columns and its cramped, disciplined hand.

She looked at it anyway.

And she saw that the page bearing September's entries had been altered since her visit. Freshly ruled lines. New ink, darker than the old. And where she remembered — she was certain, absolutely certain, she had a census-taker's memory and she trusted it as she trusted her own hands — where she remembered a struck-out name, *M. Voss*, inked over with a single savage line, there was now only blank ruled space, as if the entry had been scraped away entire, the page given a small clean amputation.

Someone had cut the name out of history in the three days since her visit. Or in the hours since the warrant crossed his doorstep.

Ada stood in the dark kitchen with the ledger in her hands and heard, overhead, the faint unmistakable sound of a door being quietly unlocked, and quietly locked again.

She put the ledger down exactly as she'd found it and climbed back to the guest room, and lay listening to the wind, and understood that she had now made her choice twice — once with words, once with silence — and that each time the choice got easier, and that this ease frightened her more than discovery ever could.

Below her, in the locked room, something ticked.

In the morning the storm had passed and Halvard Rehn stood at the landing steps pulling on his gloves, and looked back once at the tower, and at her.

"You'll return to Hammerstad today," he said. "I'll remain on the coast awhile. These islands keep their secrets well, Frøken Lindqvist — but secrets are only records that haven't been read yet. And reading records is, as it happens, both of our professions."

He stepped into the boat. Ada watched the station shrink behind them — Felix at the rail of his gallery, a small gray figure against the brightening sky — and she carried her lie home across the cold water like contraband, knowing it would not stay hidden forever, and knowing, too, that the magistrate had left a man behind on the coast to watch the light.

And that the ice was coming, and the crossings would soon end, and whatever was going to happen out at Skarholmen would happen sealed off from the world, with only four souls, one locked room, and a ticking heart to decide it.
