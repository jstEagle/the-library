## 11. Brother's Bargain

The ferry from the mainland put in at the island's lee landing at half past two in the afternoon, riding low on grey water, and Anders Eriksen stepped off it with his hat pulled low and his brother's name in his mouth like a coin he was ashamed to spend.

He had not come to visit. He told himself that twice on the crossing, gripping the rail while the little steamer wallowed through the chop, spray freezing on his collar. He had come because a man in Kristianstad had said the words *Voss* to him across a card table, had said them quietly, the way a man sets down a knife he intends to use later, and had smiled with all the warmth of an open trap.

"You're Eriksen," the man had said. "Brother to the lighthouse keeper. And your keeper's wife — she was a Voss before she was anything, wasn't she? Before she died."

"I don't know what you mean."

"Of course you don't." The man had gathered the cards, unhurried, a man who owned time the way other men owned land. "But you owe me four hundred kroner, Anders, and I've begun to wonder what else you might owe me instead."

That had been eleven days ago. Eleven days of drinking less than usual so his head would stay clear, which told him more about himself than he wanted to know: that he could give up drink without effort when ruin was the alternative, and that this proved nothing good about him.

Now he stood on the frozen shingle below the light, watching the ferry shrink toward the mainland, and rehearsed what he would say to Felix. He had rehearsed it the whole way over. *I've come for a visit, brother, it's been too long.* A lie so old and well-worn it fit him like his own coat.

* * *

Felix met him at the door of the keeper's cottage with that look he always wore now — the one like a man listening to a sound behind a wall. Twenty years Anders had been coming here and twenty years Felix had been half somewhere else, half down some corridor of his own house where no visitor was permitted.

"Anders." Not warm. Not cold. Felix's voice was the water off this coast: temperature impossible to judge until you were in it. "You're thin."

"I'm working."

"That's what I'm afraid of." Felix stepped back to let him in, and the heat of the stove hit Anders like a hand on his chest. The cottage smelled of lamp oil and bread and, underneath it, that faint mineral smell of the sea that never left anything on this island, that got into wool and wood and grief alike.

Signe was at the table with ledgers spread around her, her arithmetic neat as stitching. She looked up, and Anders saw — with the particular pain of a man who has watched a child grow from a distance — how much of Marta there was in her face. The set of the jaw. The way she looked at you as though totting up a column and finding it short.

"Uncle Anders."

"Signe. Still keeping your father honest?"

"Someone must."

And where was the girl — Ida, the ward, the orphan they'd taken in three winters back? He heard her somewhere above, in the loft, moving about, and then heard something else beneath her footsteps, or thought he did: a small rhythmic ticking, like a clock with a stutter. He'd noticed it once before, years ago, and asked Felix about it, and Felix had said *the house settles*, in the tone of a man closing a drawer.

The house settled. Of course it did. Houses settled, wives died, brothers lied, and the world went on requiring things of men who had nothing left to give but their own worse natures.

They ate salt fish and bread. Signe excused herself to check the oil store, and Ida came down shyly to fetch the plates and was sent back up by Felix with a gentleness that made something twist under Anders's ribs — Felix was capable of tenderness, then; he simply rationed it out like coal in a hard winter.

When the door had shut behind the girl, Anders set down his fork.

"Felix. I need money."

His brother didn't sigh, didn't stiffen. He just looked at him across the table with those pale keeper's eyes that had watched twenty years of weather come in off the water, and waited, and the waiting was worse than any anger.

"How much?"

"Four hundred." He made himself say it plainly. Lies within lies got heavy fast. "I'll not dress it up. Cards. Men in Kristianstad. They're patient men, Felix, but patience isn't kindness. They'll take it out of my hide or my hands, whichever works first."

"Four hundred kroner." Felix said it the way he read barometer numbers. "There is no four hundred kroner here. There's oil and coal and Signe's schooling money, and none of it is mine to gamble away because my brother can't pass a card table without touching it."

"I know what I am." It came out sharper than he meant. Sharper than he'd planned, and the plan had been the whole voyage over. "You needn't say it. Everyone in the family says it in their own way. Father said it. Marta said it kinder, which was somehow worse—"

"Don't." One word, quiet, and the room changed pressure. Felix's hands, flat on the table, had gone very still. "Don't say her name to ask me for money."

"I'm not asking for money anymore." And there it was — the coin, out of his mouth at last, gleaming and filthy. "I'm asking you to sell whatever she left."

The silence that followed had texture. Anders sat inside it and felt it close around him like cold water.

"She left a typewriter," Anders said. His voice sounded far away to himself, a man narrating his own drowning. "Everyone knows the story, Felix, even if nobody says it. Her family's machine, carried out of the Voss estate the night of the fire. A machine like that — old, fine, a Voss machine — collectors would pay. Antiquarians in the city would pay well. Four hundred would be nothing against it."

"You don't know what she left."

"No," Anders admitted. "I know what people say. And lately, Felix, I know who's asking."

He hadn't meant to play that card yet. It slipped out the way cards slip from sweating hands, and he watched it land, watched his brother's face do the thing faces do when a long-feared knock finally sounds — not surprise, only arrival.

"Who's asking," Felix said. Flat. Not a question.

"The same ones holding my paper. They mentioned the name Voss. They knew Marta was a Voss. They knew—" He stopped. Started again, lower. "Felix, there's talk of a magistrate coming north. An official inquiry. Something about property of the Voss estate never recovered after the fire. Something about a warrant."

For a moment — a moment Anders would carry to his grave, though he wouldn't understand its weight until much later — his brother looked not frightened but relieved, the way a man looks whose wound has finally been named. Then the shutter came down, that old keeper's discipline, and Felix's face was only stone again, stone with sea-salt in its cracks.

"There's nothing here," Felix said. "Whatever they think survived that fire, it didn't come to this island. Marta brought nothing but a trunk of clothes and a bad cough."

"Felix—"

"*Nothing came to this island.*" Louder now, and upstairs the floorboards creaked — the girl, listening, poor mite, learning early how this family conducted itself. Felix lowered his voice to a thread. "You'll go back on the next boat. You'll tell your creditors whatever gets you clear. But you will not bring that name into this house again. Not in front of Signe. Not ever. Do you understand me?"

Anders understood. That was the rotten core of it — he understood perfectly, and understanding changed nothing, because four hundred kroner did not care whether he understood.

He stayed the night because the last ferry had gone. He slept on the bench by the stove in his coat and lay awake most of it, listening to the wind work at the point, and once — near three in the morning — he heard Felix rise and go down the hall, and heard the small iron complaint of a lock turning, and then silence, a long silence, and then the lock again.

The locked room. The ticking thing. Whatever Marta Voss had carried out of a burning house twenty years ago, it was still here, still breathing its little mechanical breath behind a door Felix pretended was a cupboard.

Anders stared at the ceiling beams and began, without quite deciding to, to build his bargain.

* * *

The magistrate's name was Rehn. Halvard Rehn, young — younger than Anders expected, thirty perhaps, with a clerk's ink-stained fingers and a face that hadn't yet learned to hide what it thought. Anders found him at the mainland customs house two days later, having crossed on the morning boat wearing his second-best coat and his best expression of reluctant conscience.

Rehn received him in a narrow office that smelled of paper and paraffin. A fire burned too small for the room, as if even warmth here had to justify itself on a form.

"Mr. Eriksen." The young man consulted a file without opening it. "Brother to the keeper at Vardø Light. You've come about your debts."

So they knew already. Of course they knew. The creditors and the crown drank from the same well; half the men Anders owed had cousins in the customs service. He should have felt cornered. Instead he felt strangely calm, the calm of a man who has stopped swimming and let the current decide.

"I've come about yours," Anders said. "Your warrant. Your missing heirloom."

Rehn set down the file. Up close his eyes were lighter than his hair, a washed-out winter blue, and they fixed on Anders with the unblinking attention of a man who had spent his career watching people decide whether to lie.

"Speak carefully," Rehn said. "What you call an heirloom, the court calls evidence. Property removed from the Voss estate prior to the arson of 1873. A Corona typewriter, serial-marked, belonging to Elias Voss. It was never recovered. Certain documents typed on such a machine are material to the original conviction."

"And if I told you where it is?"

"Then I would be obliged to verify it." Rehn leaned back. "And you would be owed nothing. Informants inform, Mr. Eriksen. That's citizenship, not commerce."

"Then we have different ideas of citizenship." Anders sat forward, elbows on knees, and made himself hold the young man's gaze. "I'm not selling you a rumor. I'm telling you the machine sits behind a locked door in my brother's house on that island right now, this minute, and that my brother has kept it there for twenty years, and that his dead wife — Marta Eriksen, born Marta Voss — carried it there herself the winter after the fire. Ask anyone in the old district. Ask the parish records. Ask why a Voss daughter married a lighthouse keeper and vanished onto a rock in the sea."

The fire popped. Somewhere down the corridor a door slammed and boots went by, ordinary sounds, the world continuing its business while two men arranged a betrayal between them.

"Why?" Rehn said at last. Not hostile. Genuinely asking, and that was somehow harder. "Why would you do this to your own brother?"

Anders had prepared answers. *Because the law must have it. Because the truth matters. Because a man was convicted for that fire and maybe wrongly, and justice—* All the sentences a man builds to live in afterward.

What he said was: "Because they'll take everything I have if I don't, and my brother has spent twenty years being noble on that rock, and nobility can spare what I can't."

Something moved behind Rehn's eyes — not approval, nothing like it. Recognition, maybe. A clerk's recognition of a figure that doesn't balance.

"The debts," Rehn said slowly. "Four hundred kroner, is it? To Nilsen and his associates." He turned a page he wasn't reading. "The crown cannot pay private debts. But the crown can note that a certain informant cooperated, and certain creditors, when reminded of certain outstanding irregularities in their own licenses, find their memories of smaller sums become... flexible."

"So that's yes."

"That's the law working as it works, Mr. Eriksen." Rehn closed the file. "If the machine is where you say it is, your debts will trouble you less by spring. If it is not—" He stood, and up close he seemed both older and younger, a boy wearing a magistrate's authority like a borrowed coat he was growing into. "If it is not, you'll have sold your brother for nothing, and I will remember that you tried. Go home, Mr. Eriksen. Take the morning boat. And pray, if you do that sort of thing, that your brother is the man you've described."

* * *

The tavern on the harbor road was called the Anchor and had not seen an anchor in living memory. Anders took a table in the corner where the lamplight didn't reach and ordered aquavit, and then more aquavit, and gave himself permission for the first time in eleven days to stop thinking clearly.

It worked wonderfully. For an hour.

The drink did what drink does: it took the sharp edges off the evening — the washed-out blue eyes, the file snapping shut, the boy-magistrate saying *pray that your brother is the man you've described* — and replaced them with a soft warm fog in which four hundred kroner was only money and brothers were only men and everyone betrayed everyone eventually, it was practically a family tradition, look at the Vosses, look at what *they'd* covered up, look at the fire, look at—

Look at the convicted man. That was the thought the fog couldn't dissolve, the nail in his boot. A man had gone to prison for that fire. Anders had heard the story a hundred times growing up — the Voss estate, the stablehand, the trial, the verdict, all of it gossip sweetened with distance. A man had gone to prison while the real answer, if Marta's machine held what the rumors said it held, sat ticking in a locked room on a rock in the Skagerrak.

And he, Anders Eriksen, had just sold the key to it for the price of his gambling debts.

He ordered another. The serving girl looked at him with the professional pity of her trade and he wanted to tell her: *you don't understand, I had no choice,* and heard, even drunk, even drowning, exactly what that sentence was worth. Nothing. Less than nothing. A man always had a choice; he'd simply chosen the one that cost someone else.

Signe's face came to him — *someone must* — and then Ida's, the thin silent girl on the stairs, and then Felix's hands flat on the table, going still, going stone. Twenty years Felix had kept faith with a dead woman's terror, guarded her secret like a flame in a glass storm-house, fed his whole life into it the way he fed oil to the lamp, and his brother had walked into a customs office and given it away between lunchtime and dusk.

"To Marta," Anders said aloud, raising the glass, because he was drunk enough for sentiment and sober enough to know the toast was obscene. "To Marta, who married the wrong brother."

Had she? He'd thought so once, young and bitter — Felix the steady one, Felix the favored one, Felix who got the posting and the pretty refugee girl with the sad eyes and the fine hands. But Marta had chosen Felix, chosen him knowing what he was, a man who would keep her secret until it killed him or nearly had. She'd chosen the rock and the lamp and the silence. She had never once, in all the years, chosen Anders.

Maybe that was the truest thing the aquavit pried out of him that night: not that he'd betrayed his brother, but that he'd betrayed a woman who had seen him clearly twenty years ago and turned away, and some spiteful ledger in him had never forgiven her for seeing straight.

The tavern emptied around him. The lamplighter went by outside. At some point he put coins on the table — too many, he'd count the damage tomorrow, along with everything else — and stood, and the room tilted like a deck in weather.

Outside, the harbor lay black and still, the ice creeping out from the shallows in white lace. Across the water, faint as a struck match, the Vardø light turned and turned and turned, faithful, blind, sweeping its beam over a brother who had already begun composing his apology.

He'd take the morning boat back, he decided. Get ahead of it. Warn Felix — no, warn him of what, exactly? *Sorry, brother, I sold your wife's ghost to the crown, but I needed the money?* No. He'd go back and say nothing and watch the magistrate's boat come, and stand beside Felix on the shore looking surprised as anyone.

That was the plan. That was the whole wretched architecture of it: go home, say nothing, look surprised.

He was halfway to the lodging house when he saw the notice freshly pasted on the harbor board, lantern-light yellow on the paper, and stopped, and read it twice, and felt the drink leave him all at once like water through a cracked hull.

**By order of the District Magistrate: the river ferry *Svanen* will make her final crossing of the season on Thursday next. After that date, no passage north or south until spring thaw.**

Thursday. Three days.

And pinned beneath it, in a clerk's careful hand, a second notice: **Magistrate H. Rehn departs for the islands Wednesday morning, on official business.**

Wednesday morning. One day ahead of the last boat. One day ahead of the ice.

Anders stood in the cold with his hat in his hands, swaying slightly, watching the distant light turn, and understood that whatever happened next would happen faster than any of them — that the season itself had joined the conspiracy, and the window in which a man might still unsell his soul was closing like water freezing over, outward from the edges, silently, and all the way to the middle.
