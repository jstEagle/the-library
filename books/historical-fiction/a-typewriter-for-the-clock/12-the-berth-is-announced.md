## 12. The Berth Is Announced

The notice came on a Tuesday, nailed to the door of the island's only store by a boy who had rowed across from the mainland ferry landing with his collar up and his teeth chattering, though the wind was not yet cruel enough to justify it. Felix read it twice while the boy waited, stamping his boots, and then a third time because he could not believe the arithmetic of it.

*By order of the Harbor Commission: The icebreaker *Valkyrie* will make her final call at Skarvholm on or about the fifteenth of December. She will carry mail and provisions inbound and passengers outbound. Passengers will be limited to THREE (3). After this crossing the channel will be presumed closed until spring thaw. Persons remaining on the island do so at their own risk.*

Three.

Felix stood in the doorway of Halversen's store with the paper in his mittened hand and let the words settle into him like cold water into boot leather. Around him the store went on with its small noises — the tick of the cheese safe, the scrape of Halversen's broom, two fishermen arguing over the price of herring — and none of them knew what he knew, which was that the number three had just become the most dangerous number on the island.

"Bad news, keeper?" Halversen asked, not really caring. He swept toward Felix and back again like a man pacing inside his own skull.

"Ferry schedule," Felix said, and folded the paper into his coat. "Nothing to trouble you."

But it troubled him all the way home. It troubled him along the shingle path where the tide had left its lacework of ice, and up the winding stair inside the tower, and through the kitchen door where Signe stood at the stove stirring barley into the pot and Ida sat at the table copying letters onto the back of an old supply list, her tongue caught between her teeth in concentration. Four souls in this room. Five if he counted himself, and he did, though lately he had begun to wonder whether he counted for anything at all beyond the lamp.

"Post?" Signe said without turning.

"Notices." He hung his coat on its peg as though it were any ordinary coat on any ordinary day. "The icebreaker comes mid-December. Last crossing before the channel closes."

Ida looked up, pencil poised. "Closed how long?"

"Till thaw. April, maybe May."

"So whoever's here after the fifteenth is here till spring," Ida said. She was eleven and had the habit of stating things plainly that the rest of the family had learned to soften. "Like being shut in a drawer."

"Ida," Signe said mildly, and Ida bent back to her letters, but Felix felt the truth of it go through him like a nail. A drawer. Yes. He had spent twenty years learning how a house could be a drawer, how an island could be one, how silence itself was a kind of furniture you built around a thing until no one remembered there was anything underneath.

*Three berths,* he thought, ladling soup into four bowls because that was what his hands knew how to do. *Five mouths.*

* * *

He told them after supper, when Ida had been sent up to bed — though he suspected she listened at the top of the stairs, because she always did, and because he had never quite found it in himself to stop her. Signe sat across the table from him with the mending basket in her lap, a sock half-darned between her fingers, and when he laid the folded notice flat on the wood she put the needle down.

"Three," she read aloud. Her voice was careful. "Father — we are five."

"We are five."

"Anders makes five?"

"Anders is not always here." Even saying his brother's name left a taste in his mouth these days, like water from a rusty cistern. Anders had been on the island three weeks now, sleeping in the old boatshed, drinking at Halversen's more than any man needed to, and asking — lightly, jokingly, the way men ask when they have already decided — about things Marta had left behind. Felix had begun locking the oil room at night. He had begun locking everything.

Signe turned the notice over as if the reverse might hold different numbers. "Then someone stays. All winter. Maybe longer, if the ice is bad."

"If the ice is bad, till May."

She was silent. Outside, the wind had come around to the northeast and was worrying at the tower like a dog at a bone. The lamp above them turned and turned; Felix could feel it in the floorboards, that patient mechanical heartbeat he had lived inside for twenty years, so familiar now that on quiet nights he could not tell whether the ticking was the clockwork or his own chest.

"I'll stay," Signe said.

"You will not—"

"Father." She looked up, and in the lamplight her face was Marta's face, and it stopped his tongue the way it always did. "I'm the strongest swimmer. I'm the one who keeps the coal count and knows where every spare wick is. If anyone stays, it should be me. Ida goes — she's a child, she shouldn't winter shut in a drawer, as she'd say. You go — they'd never run the light without you and you know the Commission would have your post for it. And Uncle Anders—" she hesitated, choosing honesty over kindness, which was a family trait gone wrong somewhere down the line, "—Uncle Anders will do whatever's easiest, so he'll take a berth if there's room and complain either way."

"That leaves you."

"That leaves me. And I don't mind. Truly." She picked the darning back up, pulled the thread taut. "I mind other things."

He wanted to ask what things. He did not dare. There was a letter hidden in the oil locker with a magistrate's seal on it, and he had taken it out four times since it came and read it four times and each time the words were worse: *inquiry into assets of the Voss estate, outstanding warrant of 1871, heirloom typewriter, reward for information.* They had found something. Twenty years, and the past had finally learned to read a map.

If Signe stayed, she stayed alone in a house that might soon fill with strangers asking questions he had spent two decades keeping off the doorstep.

"We'll decide together," he said, which was a lie, and both of them perhaps knew it, because decisions in this house had never been made together; they had been made by Felix alone, in the dark, the way he trimmed the wick, and everyone else had simply lived inside his choices like weather.

"All right," Signe said, and darned her sock, and the clock ticked, and the light turned, and nothing was settled at all.

* * *

He climbed to the lamp room near midnight, as he did most nights, though there was nothing to trim that he hadn't already trimmed. It was the climbing he needed — the ninety-one steps that took him out of his own thoughts and gave them back to him changed at the top. Tonight he carried the notice with him, unfolded now, soft from handling.

From the gallery the channel was a black ribbon stitched with white. He could see where the ice would come, out there past the point — it always started at the river mouth, pans grinding against each other, and then the whole surface stiffened like a held breath. Three weeks, maybe four, and the sea itself would close the door.

Three berths.

He made himself do the counting properly, standing at the rail with the wind pulling at his beard. It was the arithmetic he had been avoiding since noon, and now at midnight, alone under the great lens turning its slow circles, he could no longer refuse it.

Himself. Fifty-three years old, widowed, the keeper of record. If he stayed, the light stayed lit — that was the argument for him staying, and it was a good one, and it was also, he admitted, the coward's argument dressed in duty's coat. Because the truth was the Commission could send a relief keeper by boat any day the weather allowed, and the truth was also that Felix Eriksen was the man whose name would be on whatever papers the magistrate brought north, and a man who stayed to keep a lamp could equally be a man who stayed because he could not bear to watch his daughter learn her mother's name.

Signe. Nineteen. Promised since childhood to the conservatory on the mainland — Marta's promise, made before the illness, *the girl has hands that must be given to music,* and Felix had kept that promise in a jar on a shelf for six years like a seed he hadn't dared plant. If Signe went, she went to a life. If she stayed, she stayed to a winter of questions she didn't yet know existed. But God, if she went, she went alone, into a world that might hand her a newspaper with Voss printed in it, and Felix would not be there to stand between his daughter and the truth when it arrived.

Anders. Forty-nine and dissolving. His brother had arrived in October with a suitcase and a story about work, and the story had thinned daily until Felix could see through it to the debt beneath. Anders owed money to men in Kristiania, and those men, Felix had slowly understood, were the same men who had inherited the Voss grievances — cousins of the cousins, keepers of the family's spite. Anders had not sold Marta's name yet. But he was for sale, the way a damp match is not yet struck but wants to be. Give Anders a berth and he was off the island, away from temptation, harmless on the mainland among strangers. Keep him here with nothing to do all winter but drink and remember what the machine might fetch, and —

Ida. Eleven years old. Not blood — a foundling, left at the light one frozen March morning in a fish crate lined with sacking, and Felix and Marta had taken her in the way lighthouses take in everything the sea gives up. She had no claim on anything. No papers, no family, no name but the one they had given her. When the Commission counted souls for rescue, Ida would be counted last, because there was no one ashore whose signature said she mattered.

And yet watching her copy her letters at the kitchen table, Felix had seen something that frightened him more than warrants: the girl loved the locked room. She pressed her ear to its door. She had heard the typewriter — of course she had, it ticked like a heart in there whenever the house was quiet — and she had invented a whole tenderness for it, a mystery-mother of her own imagining. Ida did not know what slept behind that door. If Ida stayed, Ida would find out. Children always opened drawers.

Five souls. Three berths. And the machine — the fifth passenger, the one that weighed forty pounds and weighed everything — still had to reach the mainland, reach the magistrate, reach the fire of judgment, or Marta died a fugitive forever and an innocent man rotted on under another man's sentence.

That was the sum of it, done properly, done the way Signe tallied coal: not enough of anything for everyone, and no column that balanced.

* * *

Two mornings later the weather turned and brought the answer closer than Felix wanted it.

It came as a boat — the mail skiff, fighting in ahead of a squall with a single passenger hunched in the stern. Felix saw it from the gallery first, a dark fleck on the grey water, and by the time he reached the landing the skiff was bumping against the tires and the passenger was clambering out: a young man, broad-shouldered, city boots, a document case slung across his chest and sealed against the spray with oilcloth.

"Magistrate Rehn," the young man said, offering a hand that was colder than Felix's own. "Halvard Rehn. District office. I'm told you're Eriksen."

"For twenty years."

"Then you're the man I need." Rehn smiled — young, courteous, a smile that had not yet learned what it cost. Behind him the squall was coming down the channel like a shutter closing, and Felix understood with terrible clarity that the sea had just delivered the future onto his landing stage and would now, conveniently, prevent it leaving.

"You'll want shelter, then," Felix said. "Weather's turned."

"It has." Rehn glanced up at the tower, then back, and there was something in the look that was not about lamps at all. "I'll want a good deal of things, Keeper Eriksen. Perhaps we can discuss them indoors."

Inside, Signe had already set water heating, and Ida stood in the corner with her arms full of kindling, staring at the stranger's city boots as though they were animals of a species she'd only read about. Rehn unwound his scarf and set his document case on the table — deliberately, Felix noted, within everyone's sight, the way a man sets down a gun he wants noticed.

"You've had the notice," Rehn said. "About the icebreaker. Three berths."

"We've had it."

"Then you understand your situation better than most." Rehn accepted the cup Signe offered with a nod of thanks. "I'll be frank, Keeper. My business concerns events of 1871. The Voss estate. I've come north because certain records suggest items of that estate may have passed through this district." His eyes moved around the room — the ledger shelf, the locked door at the end of the passage, the faces. "Perhaps even through this house."

The kettle began to sing. Nobody moved to take it off.

Felix felt twenty years compress themselves into the space of one breath — Marta's hands on the carriage keys, the confession page, the promise made to a dying woman, *never let the machine leave the island* — and against it, the notice folded in his pocket, three berths, five souls, and a girl in the corner holding kindling she had not yet put down.

"The Vosses," Felix said carefully, "were before my time here."

"Records disagree about many things," Rehn said pleasantly. "I find they usually disagree about exactly the things that matter."

Outside, the first snow of the true winter came slanting against the windows, and the channel behind it began, quietly, to close.
