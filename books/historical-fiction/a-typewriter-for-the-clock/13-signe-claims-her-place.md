## 13. Signe Claims Her Place

The letter from the conservatory came on the same morning the gulls went silent.

Signe noticed the silence first — that held-breath quality of an island before weather — and then she noticed her father's hands around the envelope, how he turned it over twice as though the address might change if he looked long enough. The postmark was mainland. The handwriting was fine and slanted, the kind of penmanship that belonged to institutions, to marble staircases and radiators and rooms where music was made by people who had never hauled coal.

"Open it," she said, though it was addressed to him.

Felix Eriksen set the letter on the table between the bread tin and the lamp without a word and went to trim the wicks, as if light were the only correspondence he trusted.

She opened it herself. She told herself later that this was practicality — that waiting for him might mean waiting all day, that the paper would only yellow in its envelope like everything else in that house — but the truth was simpler and less flattering: Signe Eriksen had spent nineteen years learning to take what was slow in coming, and there were things a person could not wait for anymore.

*Dear Herr Eriksen,* it began. *We are pleased to confirm that your daughter's audition recording, submitted by post last spring, has been reviewed by our admissions committee...*

Her eyes skated down the page, caught words like *place* and *winter term* and *piano*, and stopped at one sentence that seemed to burn straight through the paper into the wood beneath:

*Given the lateness of the season, we must receive confirmation of her passage before the river closes.*

Before the river closes.

Signe read the sentence four times. Outside, the gulls still hadn't come back, and somewhere beyond the point the sea was arranging itself into something with intent. She thought about the ferry schedule pinned inside the oil locker door, the one her father consulted the way other men consulted scripture. The last crossing. Everyone on the island spoke of it now in the lowered voice people used for funerals and births: *before the ice takes the channel.* After that, nothing moved until thaw. Not letters. Not pianos. Not girls with plans.

"Papa." Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "It's yes. It's actually yes."

He stood at the lamp with his back to her, and she watched his shoulders do something small and complicated — not quite a flinch, more like a man absorbing a blow he'd been expecting for years.

"I know," he said.

"You *know?*"

"The committee wrote to me in October. I wrote back that you'd accept."

The room tilted slightly. Nineteen years of practicing scales with wrapped fingers in a kitchen cold enough to crack the inkwell; nineteen years of playing on an instrument so far out of tune it was less a piano than a philosophical argument about what a piano should be; nineteen years of her father saying *after winter, next year, when things settle* — and he had written to strangers on the mainland and arranged her whole future without telling her, the way he did everything, silently, behind locked doors.

"Why didn't you say?"

He turned then, and his face was doing the thing it always did, the keeper's face, the face he wore for ships and strangers and grief: level, weathered, giving away nothing but the light it reflected. "Because I wanted to be sure first. Because promises break, and I didn't want you carrying a broken one through a whole winter."

"It didn't break."

"No," Felix said quietly. "It didn't."

And that should have been the end of it — the yes, the joy, the packing. But Signe had grown up in a house where silence was a language spoken fluently by everyone, and she heard what lived underneath his answer, the way you could hear undercurrents beneath ice that looked solid all the way to the far shore.

There was something he wasn't saying.

* * *

She went up to tell Ida.

Ida was in the lean-to where she kept her scraps of paper, bent over her letters with the ferocious concentration of someone building a world out of salvage. She copied sentences off flour sacks and newspaper edges and the backs of hymnals, and she had recently graduated — by her own reckoning — to composing original thoughts, which appeared in careful pencil: *THE SEA IS A DOOR THAT ONLY OPENS OUTWARD.* Signe had read it three times and couldn't decide if it was wisdom or just Ida, being Ida.

"They said yes," Signe announced from the doorway. "The conservatory. Winter term. Me."

Ida looked up, and for one unguarded instant her whole face opened like a window — delight, pure and uncomplicated, the kind of joy that cost nothing and expected nothing back. Then, just as quickly, Signe watched the girl fold the feeling away, neat as laundry, tuck it somewhere out of sight, and arrange her features into something more modest. More appropriate. More *safe*.

"That's wonderful," Ida said. "You'll play on a real piano. With strings that are all the same length they're supposed to be."

"All the same length they're *tuned* to be, you absolute—come here." Signe pulled her up by the wrists and spun her once around the lean-to, scattering paper scraps like startled birds, and Ida laughed — really laughed, the high unpracticed sound of her — and for a minute they were just two girls in a cold shed with the storm coming and no arithmetic at all between them.

It was only afterward, walking back along the cliff path with the wind pushing at their shoulders like a hand urging haste, that Signe felt the wrongness settle in. Because Ida had folded her joy away. Because she had done it fast, expertly, the way you'd snatch your hand back from a stove you'd been burned by before. And because Signe knew — had known for years, in the wordless way you know the shape of your own household — exactly why.

There was one berth.

Everyone knew it by now. The icebreaker *Vasa* would make one run before the channel closed, and the harbor master's notice, nailed to the storehouse door, had been amended in pencil by someone with a bitter sense of humor: *THREE PASSENGERS. NO EXCEPTIONS. DON'T ASK.* Three places for a household of five. Signe had done the sum a dozen times in the past week, the way you worry a loose tooth, and every time she arrived at the same terrible remainder, and every time she had set the sum aside because — because the conservatory letter was coming, because her place was surely different, surely *earned*, surely exempt from the arithmetic that governed everyone else.

Wasn't it?

The wind shoved her again. Down below, whitecaps were stitching themselves across the strait in long urgent rows, and the ferry flag at the landing hung straight out, pointing at the mainland like a finger.

* * *

That evening she found her father in the lamp room and made her declaration standing at the top of the stairs, so that he had to look up at her, so that the light itself stood behind her.

"I'm going," she said. "On the *Vasa*. When it comes."

Felix was cleaning the lens with the chamois cloth he kept for the purpose, moving in slow circles, and he did not stop. "I know," he said.

"You keep saying that. You know everything and you say nothing." She came down the last steps into the lantern's glow. The great lens turned overhead with its patient whisper, throwing its beam seaward, sweeping the dark, sweeping it again. "Papa. Look at me."

He looked at her. His eyes in the lamplight were the color of the sea in January, and just as hard to read depth in.

"I've earned this," she said, and heard her own voice sharpen as she said it, heard the edge arrive and did nothing to blunt it. "Nineteen years. Every winter. You promised me — you and Mama both, when she—" She stopped. Marta's name was a door in this house that nobody opened; it swung shut on its own weight. "When she was alive. You said when the time came, I'd go. That music wasn't a luxury for people like us. That's what you said. Your exact words."

"I remember my exact words."

"Then there's nothing to discuss. I'm going." She crossed her arms, planted her feet, prepared for argument, and was unsettled — deeply, strangely unsettled — when none came.

"Signe." Felix set down the chamois. For a long moment he only looked at her, and something moved behind his face like weather behind fog — grief, or fear, or guilt, some vast thing that never fully surfaced. "You'll go," he said finally. "I'll see to it."

Just that. No conditions, no counterarguments, no mention of Anders or Ida or the pencil-scratched notice on the storehouse door. He picked the chamois back up and returned to his slow circles on the glass, and Signe stood there in the turning light feeling the strangest sensation: victory with a hole in the middle of it.

Because he hadn't asked *how*. Any father negotiating a berth for his daughter against two other souls would ask *how* — would talk berths and priorities and who else needed passage. Her father simply said *I'll see to it*, in the tone he used for trimming wicks and coiling lines and all the other small acts of maintenance by which he kept catastrophe at arm's length, and Signe understood, with a cold that had nothing to do with the draft under the door, that whatever *seeing to it* required, he intended to pay for it himself. Out of whatever account he kept hidden from all of them. Out of whatever it was he never said.

"What will it cost?" she asked.

"Circles on glass," he said mildly. "Nothing you need to count."

But that was the trouble, wasn't it. She *did* count. She was her father's daughter whether either of them liked it or not, raised on tallies of coal and oil and lamp-hours, raised on the arithmetic of keeping a light burning through a northern winter — and she could feel, pressing against the inside of her certainty, the shape of the question she was refusing to ask. *Three berths. Who stays?* If she boarded the *Vasa*, the sum changed for everyone left on the shore. Someone's conservatory was someone else's closed channel, someone else's frozen-in-until-spring, someone else's whole life postponed by a pencil line on a harbor notice.

And underneath that, quieter, worse: the memory of Ida folding her joy away in the lean-to, quick and practiced as a card sharp. Ida, who had already done the sum. Ida, who had already assigned herself the remainder.

Signe pushed it all down. She was good at that; the house had taught her well.

"I start after the new year," she said, softer now. "I'll write. As soon as the thaw opens the channel, I'll write every week."

"I know," Felix said, and the gentleness in it undid her a little, and she hated that too, that he could be gentle and silent in the same breath, that love in this family always arrived wearing a disguise.

She went down the stairs. At the bottom she paused, one hand on the rail, and looked back up through the hatch at her father framed in the lantern's glow — a silhouette against the turning beam, sweeping the dark, sweeping it again, keeping watch over water that gave nothing back.

"Papa," she called. "Whatever it is you're not telling me. Whatever it costs. I'd rather know."

Above her, the lens whispered on. The beam swept the dark, found nothing, swept again.

"There's nothing to tell," said the keeper of lamps and lies, in a voice as steady as the flame, and Signe believed him — chose to believe him, which is a different thing entirely — and went down to pack.

In her room she took out her sheet music, all of it, the warped and salt-stained pages she'd played from for years, and began folding them into her good trunk alongside the conservatory letter. At the bottom of the trunk lay a photograph of her mother, young, unsmiling, holding a hat against a wind Signe had never seen. She studied the face for a long time. There was something in it she'd never noticed before, some cast of the features that didn't match her own reflection, some stranger looking out — but the lamp oil was low and the image was dark, and she told herself it was nothing, and shut the trunk on her mother's face.

Down the hall, faintly, she could hear Ida's pencil scratching out another sentence on a scrap of saved paper.

Outside, the wind had begun to rise, and far off across the blackening strait, the first snow of the season came walking toward them over the water.

And three days later, when the supply boat came in against the last of the calm and her uncle Anders stepped onto the landing with a mainland look in his eye and a grip on Felix's arm too urgent for brotherhood, Signe would learn that the arithmetic had changed again — that berths could be taken without asking, that sums could be settled in taverns, and that the question she had refused to ask had been answered in her absence, badly, by someone else.
