## 14. Ida Overhears

Ida had learned the house the way other children learned hymns — by repetition, by listening, by knowing which silences meant what.

The Eriksen house was not large, but it was built like everything on the island: to withstand. Walls thick as a man's forearm, doors that closed with the sound of a book shutting, floors that announced every footstep as though the house itself were keeping count. Ida had arrived at eleven years old with a single bundle and a name no one asked her to explain, and in the four years since, she had mapped the house's moods the way Signe mapped the tides. She knew that the third stair from the top creaked and could be avoided if you pressed your foot to the outer edge. She knew that Felix's boots made a sound different from Anders's — Felix walked like a man apologizing for his weight, Anders like a man daring the floor to object. She knew that when the wind came from the northeast, the chimney drew badly and the parlor filled with the smell of peat and old smoke, and everyone spoke less, as though the house itself had grown hard of hearing and they'd stopped bothering to raise their voices.

And she knew the locked room.

Everyone knew the locked room, in the way everyone knows a thing they have agreed not to say aloud. It was at the end of the upstairs passage, the door painted the same gray as all the others but somehow grayer, and Felix kept the key on a ring inside his coat, and no one — not Signe, not Anders, not Ida in four years of dusting every other corner of that house — had seen inside it. But Ida had heard it.

Not the room. The machine.

It ticked.

Not always. Not the way a clock ticks, steady and indifferent. It ticked when the weather changed, when the temperature swung and the old house settled around itself, and Ida had decided — privately, in the secret ledger she kept behind her eyes — that it was the metal remembering. Metal remembered cold, she thought. Metal remembered the hands that had touched it. Whatever sat in that locked room had been touched by hands that were gone now, and on certain nights, when the frost climbed the glass and the house groaned like a ship, she could stand in the passage with her ear an inch from the gray door and hear it: a small, irregular ticking, like a heart that had forgotten how to beat evenly.

She had never told anyone. This was not because she was afraid of Felix. She was afraid of Felix, but that was a different thing — the good fear, the fear of a man whose approval mattered, the way you fear the sea not because it is cruel but because it is vast. She had never told anyone because telling would have broken something. She understood this without understanding how she understood it. Some things in the Eriksen house were held up by not being spoken of. The locked room was one. Marta — dead before Ida ever came, her photograph turned to the wall in Felix's grief and turned back around only in the last year — was another. Ida's own mother, perhaps, though that silence belonged to Ida alone and she kept it like a stone in her pocket, turning it over at night.

So she listened, and she did not speak, and she told herself this was the price of belonging, and most days the price felt fair.

The day she overheard, the price stopped feeling fair at all.

* * *

It began with the weather, as everything on the island did.

The ferry had stopped. That was the word that had come up from the harbor on Tuesday, passed along the cliff path by the fisherman's boy with his collar up and his voice pitched to carry over the wind: *Ferry's done till thaw. Icebreaker comes once, maybe twice, then the river closes and that's the winter.* Signe had repeated it at supper in her practical way, tallying it alongside the coal and the oil as though a frozen river were simply another line in her arithmetic, and Felix had nodded and said nothing, and Anders had said nothing too, but his nothing had a different texture. Ida had noticed that. Anders's silences had edges.

Anders had been on the island three weeks now. He had arrived with a suitcase and a smile that didn't reach his eyes, and he'd been charming in a way that reminded Ida of a man selling something — he'd fixed the gate hinge, he'd brought Ida an orange, a whole orange, a fruit so exotic on that rock in November that she'd held it for a day before eating it, turning it in her hands like a small sun. But at night she heard him pacing in the room he shared with the stores, and once she'd heard him talking to himself in a low, urgent voice, and once — this was the one she kept returning to — she'd heard him say a name to Felix in the kitchen, quietly, when they thought the girls were abed: *Rehn.*

She hadn't known who Rehn was. She knew now.

The magistrate had come on Thursday, on the last fishing boat willing to cross before the ice took the channel, a young man in a coat too fine for the weather and a face too smooth for the office he held. Ida had seen him from the upstairs window as he climbed the path from the harbor, and she'd thought, with the uncharitable clarity of a fourteen-year-old, that he looked like a spoon — polished, silver, and shaped for someone else's mouth. He had not come to the house. He had gone instead to the harbor master's office, and then to the tavern, and the island, which had perhaps forty souls and no secrets worth the name, had begun to hum.

Ida had been sent down to the tavern that evening because the household had run short of lamp oil — the storm had delayed the supply skiff — and Signe was busy with the winter tallies, and Felix was at the light, and Anders had said he'd go but then hadn't, and so it fell to her, because it always fell to her, the one with no claim on anyone's protection. She didn't mind. She liked being useful. Usefulness was the currency she paid her rent in, and she paid it gladly and in full.

The tavern was called the Anker, which Ida had always thought was a joke the island told on itself — an anchor, holding nothing, in a harbor that froze. It was low-ceilinged and smelled of tar, herring brine, and the sweet rot of spilled beer soaked so deep into the floorboards that no winter would ever freeze it out. She collected the oil can from the keeper's storeroom at the back, and she was carrying it toward the door — it was heavy, and she was small, and she was moving slowly, the way you move when you're carrying something that matters and you're afraid of dropping it — when she heard her uncle's voice from the shadowed end of the room.

She should have kept walking. She knew that even then. But Anders said her name.

Not to her. About her.

"—the girl hears nothing, sees nothing," Anders was saying, and his voice had the loose, warm quality it got when he'd had drink in him, but underneath the looseness there was something tight, something wound like a spring. "That's the beauty of the arrangement, you understand. The keeper keeps his secrets and his silence, and the girls keep house, and nobody asks anybody anything. Twenty years of nobody asking anything. You could learn from it."

There was a pause. When the other man spoke, Ida knew his voice at once, though she'd heard it only once, across a harbor, in the wind. The spoon. Rehn.

"I didn't come north to learn from a drunk," Rehn said. His voice was young but it didn't waver. "I came for a warrant. The Voss heirloom. A typewriter, Eriksen. Manufactured in Dresden, sold to the Voss estate in the spring of '71, unaccounted for since the fire. The old file says it burned. The old file says a great many things."

"And I'm to know where it is because—"

"Because you wrote to your creditors in September," Rehn said, and now his voice changed, cooled, became something with a blade in it, "and your creditors are the men holding the paper on what's left of the Voss estate, and one of them, a Mr. Holm, received a letter in which you wrote — I have it here — *the machine still exists and I know the hand that keeps it.*"

The tavern noise seemed to fall away. Ida stood with the oil can in her arms and felt the words go through her like cold water through cloth.

*The machine.*

The ticking. The gray door. The key on the ring inside Felix's coat, the key she had seen a hundred times without seeing, the way you see a man's hands without ever counting his fingers. The machine that ticked like a second heart in a room no one entered. The machine that had a name in a letter, and a name in a warrant, and a name — she understood this dimly, the way you understand a storm is coming not from the sky but from the gulls — a name that men wanted badly enough to cross a freezing sea for.

"And what do you want for it?" Rehn asked. "For what you know. Let's not pretend you came to me out of civic feeling, Eriksen. Your debts are what — four hundred crowns? Five?"

There was a silence long enough that Ida could hear the fire ticking in the tavern grate, and the wind worrying at the shutters, and her own heart, which had gone strange and loud in her ears.

"Five hundred and forty," Anders said. "And passage off this rock before the channel closes. I've given this island twenty years of my brother's life and it's given me nothing but salt air and his silence, and I'm done being charitable. You want the machine. I can get you into that room. I can get you the key. My brother trusts me the way fools trust family, and I am — God forgive me — I am tired of being trusted for nothing."

Ida's arms ached. She realized she had been gripping the oil can so hard that the wire handle had bitten a line into her palm, and she made herself loosen her fingers one at a time, slowly, the way Felix had taught her to loosen a grip on the rail in high wind — *panic makes you hold tighter, and holding tighter is what throws you overboard* — and she stood in the smell of herring and spilled beer and understood, in the wordless, formless way that children understand catastrophes before they have the language for them, that the world she lived in was made of something else than she had thought.

The ticking was not a heart. The ticking was a thing men bargained over. The ticking was worth five hundred and forty crowns and a berth on an icebreaker and whatever else lived in the space between *I know the hand that keeps it* and *God forgive me.*

She thought: *it could hang someone.*

She didn't know where the thought came from. It arrived whole, the way gulls arrive at a gutting table, and once it arrived it would not leave. A machine that wrote. A machine whose writing was wanted by a magistrate with a warrant. A machine hidden for twenty years by a man who kept a light for a living and a silence for a wife. You did not hide a sewing machine for twenty years. You did not hide a clock. You hid a thing that could hurt someone — or a thing that could save someone — and the two, she understood with a clarity that frightened her, were often the same thing, turned over. The same stone, the other face.

*It could hang someone or it could save them,* she thought, standing in the tavern doorway with the oil can in her arms, *and everyone in this house knows which, and I am the only one who doesn't, and that is what I am to them. The one who doesn't know. The one who hears nothing, sees nothing.*

*The girl hears nothing, sees nothing.*

Anders had said it like a compliment. Like an asset on a ledger. And she had stood there in the dark of the passage between the barrels and heard her own self being spent, casually, the way a man spends coins he's already written off, and something in her — some small, load-bearing thing — had quietly given way.

"That's your offer, then," Rehn said into the silence. "The key, the room, the machine. For five hundred forty crowns and a berth."

"And you burn whatever's in it unread," Anders said. "That's the other half. Holm's terms. Whatever the machine wrote, it dies with the machine. The Voss name stays clean, the conviction stays closed, and everyone who's been dead for twenty years stays dead and settled. That's the bargain. That's the whole bargain."

*Burn it unread.*

Ida closed her eyes. Behind her eyes, the little ledger she kept — the one where she stored the house's silences, the turned photograph, the gray door, her mother's name that no one said — that ledger seemed to open all its pages at once. Whatever the machine had written, it was a *what*, not a *when*. It was a sentence someone had made with their hands, and there were men who would pay five hundred and forty crowns to make sure that sentence was never read. Sentences were not supposed to be that heavy. Sentences were supposed to be the cheapest thing in the world — she made them herself every day, on scraps, on slate, in the frost on the window glass, and they cost nothing and weighed nothing and belonged to her.

But this sentence had a price. This sentence could hang someone. This sentence was worth more than she was — she understood that too, standing there with her chapped hands and her oil can and her four years of usefulness — this sentence had a berth on an icebreaker and a cash value and men crossing frozen seas to claim it, and she, Ida, had a wire handle's mark on her palm and a name nobody asked her to explain.

She opened her eyes. Through the gap between the barrels she could see Anders's hands on the table, turning his glass, and beyond him the door, and beyond the door the dark, and beyond the dark the light on the point where Felix stood his watch, keeping his flame alive against the wind, keeping it alive the way he kept everything — steadily, silently, at cost.

She did not hate Anders. That surprised her. What she felt was colder and more precise than hate: she looked at her uncle's hands turning his glass and she thought, *you would sell the room, and the key, and the ticking heart of this house, and you would spend me in the sentence where you describe it, and you would sleep afterward.* And she thought, *and I will remember this, and one day — not now, I am small and I am nobody and I have an oil can to carry home — but one day, you will need me to hear nothing, and I will hear everything.*

She turned and walked out. She did not run. Running made noise, and noise made questions, and she had learned the family silence so well that she wore it now even against the family, even as armor against the very people who had taught it to her. She walked out into the wind with the oil can against her chest like a child, and the cold hit her face, and the harbor lay black and moving under the first skin of ice, and up on the point the light swept its slow arm across the water, once, twice, patient as forgiveness.

* * *

The house was warm when she came in. Signe had the stove fed and the tallies spread across the kitchen table, her pen moving in quick sure strokes, and she looked up when Ida came in with the smell of the tavern on her coat, and she said, "You're cold. Sit by the fire," and it was such an ordinary kindness, such an unremarkable, sisterly, unweighted kindness, that Ida's eyes stung and she had to turn toward the pegs and unbutton her coat very slowly until the sting passed.

"Did Anders go with you?" Signe asked.

"He was at the Anker," Ida said. It was true. Every word she said that night would be true. That was her decision, made in the doorway of the tavern and confirmed on the walk home: she would not lie, because lying was the family trade and she had just watched what it cost. But she would not volunteer, either. She would carry what she knew the way Felix carried the key — inside the coat, against the body, shown to no one.

"He's been at the Anker a great deal," Signe said, and there was something in her voice — not suspicion, not yet, but the first faint mineral taste of it — and she looked down at her tallies and added, without looking up, "He asked Father about the oil money on Tuesday. Twice."

Ida hung her coat. She carried the oil can to the shelf. And upstairs, passing the gray door on her way to her room, she stopped.

She didn't put her ear to it. That was a child's ritual, and she understood, standing in the passage with the lamp low and the wind coming off the water, that she had stopped being the child who did that. Instead she laid her palm flat against the wood, the way you'd lay a hand on the flank of a horse you were about to ask something of, and she felt — or imagined she felt, and she would never afterward be sure which it was, and she would decide, eventually, that it didn't matter — the faintest tremor through the panels. The house settling. The metal remembering. The machine keeping its own small time in the dark, ticking out the seconds of whatever was written inside it, patient as a fuse.

"Who are you?" she whispered.

The house said nothing. The house was very good at saying nothing. She had been raised by it, in its way — four years of its held breath, its closed doors, its silences load-bearing as beams. But she was fourteen, and she had an oil can's worth of new knowledge, and the silence that had once felt like belonging now felt like a wall she had been living inside without knowing it was a wall.

Downstairs, the front door opened and closed — Felix, coming off the late watch, his boots making their apologetic sound on the mat — and Ida heard Signe's pen stop, and start again, and she heard Felix say, "All well?" and Signe say, "All well," and she heard the great ordinary machinery of the household grind on, unbroken, seamless, lying by omission in every room.

Anders came home an hour later. She heard him from her bed: the door, the boots, the pause on the stairs where he steadied himself against the wall, the careful tread of a drunk man practicing sobriety. He paused, too, at the gray door. She heard his hand on the handle — trying it, softly, the way a thief tries a window — and she heard the lock hold, and she heard him breathe out something that might have been a laugh or might have been relief, and then his door closed and the house was still.

Ida lay in the dark with her eyes open.

She thought about the convicted man. She didn't know his name — no one on the island said it, another of the silences — but she had pieced him together over four years from the shape of what wasn't said: a man who had burned for the Voss fire, a man whose guilt everyone had agreed upon so long ago that the agreement had hardened into fact. And she thought: *if the machine could hang him, and men would pay to burn it unread, then maybe — maybe — the machine was the only thing in the world that could say he didn't do it.* Maybe the ticking in the gray room was a voice. Maybe it had been trying to speak for twenty years, into the dark, to a house that kept it locked away like a shameful relative, and the only person who could hear it was a girl with her ear to the door.

*The girl hears nothing, sees nothing.*

Well. She heard something now.

And in the morning — she decided this in the dark, with the wind worrying the eaves and the machine ticking its patient, dangerous time below her — in the morning she would begin to watch. Not the way she had watched before, the housekeeper's watch, the useful watch. The other kind. The kind that kept a ledger of its own.

She did not know, lying there, that the river would freeze in nine days. She did not know that the icebreaker would carry exactly three, or that the arithmetic of berths was already being done in her father-figure's head, or that the sentence in the gray room would cost this family nearly everything before it saved any of them. She knew only what she knew, which was suddenly more than she had ever known, and less than she needed, and heavier than an oil can, and hers.

The light on the point swept the window. Once. Twice. She counted it the way she counted everything now, and slept at last the way the island slept: lightly, with one ear open, waiting for the ice.
