## 15. Storm of the Century

The barometer began to fall on a Tuesday, and by Wednesday morning it had fallen so far that Felix Eriksen stopped trusting it and trusted his knees instead. His knees had been reading weather for twenty years, and what they read now was nothing good.

"Signe," he said at breakfast, without looking up from his bread, "double the guys on the lamp room shutters. Ida — fill every can you can carry from the cistern before it fouls with spray."

Signe looked at him over the rim of her cup. She had Marta's way of asking questions with her eyebrows, and he answered them the same way he'd answered Marta's for two decades: not at all.

"The glass is thick," she said.

"The glass was thick in '81 too." He tore his bread and did not eat the pieces. "In '81 we lost three panes and I lost the feeling in two fingers. This will be worse. The gulls went inland yesterday. When the gulls go inland, you listen to them."

Ida stood in the doorway with her shawl half on, watching him the way she'd learned to watch — quietly, taking everything in, giving back as little as possible. She was getting more like Signe every month, or maybe like Marta; he could no longer always tell which silences in that house were inherited and which were learned.

"How bad?" Ida asked.

"Worse than any of you have seen," he said. "Maybe worse than anyone living has seen. If I tell you to get down the tower stairs and into the cellar, you go. No arguing, no waiting for me. Promise me."

They promised. He did not believe them, which was fine; he hadn't believed Marta's promises either, and she'd broken every one of them beautifully, right up until the winter she broke the last one and died keeping it.

By noon the sea had changed color, gone a bruised green-black that Felix had only seen twice before, and the wind came around to the northeast with a sound like a train that never passed. He sent the women down to the cottage, nailed the last shutter himself with hands that remembered how, and climbed the hundred and eight steps of the tower as the first real gusts began to lean against the stone.

The lamp room smelled of brass polish and old smoke, the smell of his whole working life. He trimmed the wick, checked the clockwork rotation, greased the carriage gears, and waited.

At four o'clock the light went out.

Not the flame — the sun. The world outside simply ended, noon collapsing into midnight in the space of ten minutes, and then the storm arrived in earnest and the tower began to sing. It was not a howl. It was a note, held, enormous, a single sustained tone in the masonry itself, as if the lighthouse were a bottle and God were blowing across its mouth.

Felix lit the lamp, set the clockwork turning, and settled in to keep it turning.

* * *

He had kept lights through forty-one storms. He knew the taxonomy of them the way a doctor knows fevers. There was the long grind of a three-day blow, tedious and wearing; there was the short vicious squall that tried to catch you careless between rounds. And once or twice in a lifetime there came the kind that did not want to damage the light but wanted to erase it, wanted it never to have existed, and those storms you survived not with skill but with stubbornness, hour by hour, refusing to be erased alongside it.

This was that kind.

By seven the first pane went. It did not shatter so much as detonate — a sound like a rifle shot, and then a fistful of freezing air and spray through the gap where the northwest quarter of the lantern glass had been, and the flame bent sideways and lay down flat and nearly died. Felix threw himself across the lamp with his coat spread wide, taking the wind's weight on his own back, and felt something in the coat tear and something in his shoulder pull, and held on until the gust spent itself and the flame stood up again, trembling, alive.

He got the storm pane — the spare sheet of thick glass kept for exactly this — wedged into the frame with putty and wire and two hands gone numb past feeling. It would hold or it wouldn't. He had done all there was to do about whether, and the doing of that, the doing-and-then-surrendering-of-it, was the whole of a keeper's religion. Marta had understood that. She used to say the lighthouse taught her more theology than her father ever had, and her father had been a pastor.

Another pane went at nine. Then the gallery rail, torn off whole and hurled away into the dark with a shriek of iron. Then the hatch cover to the service room, banging like a door in a haunted story, until Felix roped it down with the last of the line.

Somewhere below him, faint under the storm's one long note, he could hear the sea hitting the point like artillery. He thought of the fishing fleet that should have run for harbor at the first sign, and prayed without words, the wordless kind that wasn't really prayer so much as a man standing at attention in front of heaven and refusing to leave.

* * *

Midnight. The clockwork jammed.

It was the cold — the temperature had fallen through the floor when the storm dragged arctic air down behind it, and the grease in the rotation gear had gone to glue. Felix heard the change in the rhythm first, the great lens slowing, stuttering, and then stopping altogether, and the beam froze pointing out to sea while the flame burned steady inside it like a heart in a dead body.

A fixed light was worse than no light. Every sailor within twenty miles knew this rock flashed white every eight seconds; a steady white meant something else, somewhere else, somewhere safe, and they would steer for it, and they would die on his rocks believing themselves saved.

He worked on the gear housing with an oilcan and a wooden mallet and his bare hands, because gloves were death at that work — you needed to feel the mechanism, feel what turned and what bound — and the metal took the heat out of his fingers in seconds, the way the sea takes heat out of a drowning man. He got the carriage moving. It seized again. He freed it. It seized again. Each time the interval grew longer, and each time he went back to the frozen metal with his bare skin and coaxed another few minutes of rotation out of it, knowing that coaxing was all it was, that the night was twelve hours long at least, and that his hands were not going to last twelve hours of this.

So be it. His hands were the least expensive thing he owned.

There was a version of this night, Felix admitted somewhere around two in the morning, in which he did not fight at all. In which he let the lamp fail, let the storm have the point, let the record show that the keeper of Vargskær Light was overcome by the greatest storm in living memory — who could blame him? Who would ever look twice? And if the light failed, the island went dark, and if the island went dark, perhaps the magistrate's men could not land, perhaps the warrant could not be served, perhaps the typewriter slept on in its gray room through one more season, one more year, forever—

No.

He caught himself at it, the way you catch yourself stepping toward a ledge, and the shame of it was hotter than the storm was cold. Twenty years he had told himself he kept the light for the fishermen, for the pilots, for every soul on black water. And here, in the worst night of his life, he had discovered that some part of him — a part small enough to hide even from itself — had considered putting it out.

Marta would have known. She always said you learn what you actually believe on the nights when believing costs something.

He went back to the gear with his ruined hands and made the lens turn.

* * *

Around three, between rounds, he heard the ship.

It came through the storm the way voices come through walls — not heard exactly, but sensed, a change in the sea's voice, a deeper concussion against the outer shoal. Then a shape in the murk, blacker than black, sliding down the face of a swell with its running lights gone or drowned, and Felix's whole body went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the weather.

He did the arithmetic instantly and hopelessly: shoal water east of the point, breaking seas, a vessel of that size making perhaps six knots with no steerage left, and his light flashing above her every eight seconds, faithfully, uselessly, like a man shouting directions to someone already deaf.

She struck at twenty past three. The sound reached the tower a full second after the flash of it — a white bloom of spray taller than the lantern itself — and then the sound, vast and final, a door closing on the world.

What happened next, Felix did later in reports and testimony, but what he remembered was simpler and worse: the cries. They carried on the northeast wind with horrible clarity, thin human sounds threaded through the storm's one note, and there was nothing — *nothing* — his light could do for them now, nothing his boat could do in those seas, nothing in his hands or his heart, and he stood in the wrecked lantern room with blood on his palms from the frozen wire and watched the dark where they were dying, and kept the lamp turning.

That was the penance. That was the prayer. Not the kneeling kind — the standing kind. The kind where you do the only thing in the world still given to you to do, and do it perfectly, and know it is not enough, and do it anyway, because the doing is all that stands between you and being the sort of man who does nothing.

He counted flashes. Eight seconds, eight seconds, eight seconds. Somewhere below, men were in the water. Above them, absurdly, immaculately, the beam swept on, marking a rock they had already found, warning no one who could still be warned, shining for no one who could still see it. And Felix Eriksen, keeper of the light, stood his watch over the dead and the drowning alike, because the light did not distinguish and neither could he.

Toward dawn the cries stopped. He kept counting anyway.

* * *

The storm blew itself hoarse near sunrise, the way the great ones do — not ending but exhausting, the wind dropping from murder to menace to mere complaint, the snow turning to a gray sifting silence. Felix came down the hundred and eight steps on legs that belonged to a much older man and opened the cottage door on Signe and Ida, both awake, both wrapped in blankets, both looking at him as though he had come back from somewhere farther than the tower.

"There was a ship," Signe said.

He nodded. His voice, when he found it, sounded like gravel poured slowly. "On the outer shoal. Near three this morning."

"Anyone—"

"I don't know yet. Get the stove hot. Boil water. There'll be work on the shore within the hour, whatever the answer is."

Ida was already moving. But Signe stayed, looking at his hands — he realized only then that they were bandaged in rags soaked red, that he couldn't remember doing it — and her face did something complicated, fear and anger and love fighting for the same ground.

"You could have called us up," she said. "You could have let us help."

"The lamp room isn't big enough for help."

"That's not why." Her eyes were Marta's eyes and there was no hiding from them, there never had been. "You go up there alone because up there is where you do your penance, and you don't think anyone else should have to watch it."

He had no answer prepared for that, and no strength left to build one. He stood dripping on his own threshold, an old man in a young man's coat, and let his daughter see him exactly as he was: faithful and frightened and not at all sure that faithfulness had bought anything but frostbite and grief.

Then the fog bell at the harbor end of the island began to ring — not the regular tolling but the fast urgent pattern, three-and-three, the signal they'd agreed on years ago and never once used — and the moment broke like the lantern glass, and father and daughter ran together into the gray morning, down toward the shore, toward whatever the sea had decided to give back.

The first thing the tide laid at their feet was a ship's boat, splintered, overturned, one oar. The second thing was a man, alive, blue to the lips, clutching to his chest with both frozen arms a canvas-wrapped bundle he would not surrender even unconscious — and when Felix knelt and pried the wrappings open with his ruined hands, he saw the corner of an official seal, and beneath it, printed in ink that the sea had not managed to blur:

*MAGISTRATE'S OFFICE — HALVARD REHN.*

The storm had delivered his judge to his doorstep.
