## 16. Wreckage and Witnesses

The storm had spent itself sometime before dawn, and the sea it left behind was a stranger — flattened, glassy, strewn with the leavings of two nights' violence. Ada Lindqvist stood at the top of the shingle beach below the lighthouse point and understood, in the way one understands a room after a burglary, that she was looking at evidence.

Debris lay along the tideline for half a mile in both directions. Spars. A hatch cover painted green-gold, the paint blistered but legible: *Svanhild*, of some port she couldn't yet read. Rope in coils and tangles, kelp in black ropes of its own, a ship's bell half-buried and silent. Farther down the beach, wedged between two boulders like a bird with a broken wing, sat a ship's boat, overturned, its nameboard splintered away.

She had come down before anyone else woke. That had been deliberate, though she'd dressed it as restlessness when she told herself the truth. She carried her census satchel out of habit and her oilskin coat against the spray, and she walked the line of wreckage the way she'd once walked the rows of a parish register — slowly, left to right, missing nothing.

*You are a recorder,* she reminded herself. *Not an investigator. Not a judge.* But the distinction had been wearing thin since she'd found that inked-out name in Felix Eriksen's ledger, and this morning, with the sea offering up its dead and its goods under a sky the color of pewter, the distinction felt like a courtesy she paid herself to keep walking.

The fishing boat that had foundered in the first gale — the one whose two survivors Felix had pulled from the water — had gone down miles south. This was other wreckage. Storm of the century, the islanders were already calling it, though Ada had learned that every generation renamed its worst weather so. Whatever its true rank, it had chewed through the shipping lanes and spat fragments onto this shore, and among the fragments, she suspected, were answers.

She found the first paper forty minutes in, if you could call it paper. A page of a ship's manifest, pulped to felt, letters ghosting through like bones under skin. She pried it off a rock, held it up to the grey light, and could make out only a column of numbers and what might have been a port authority stamp. She wrapped it in oilcloth anyway and put it in her satchel. A recorder kept things. That much of her office she still believed in.

The wind had died but the cold hadn't; it came up through her boots from the stones as if the whole beach were a slab of iron left out overnight. Her fingers went clumsy. She worked on.

It was nearly eight o'clock — she knew because the light above her had gone dark at dawn, punctual as conscience, and because Signe Eriksen appeared on the path from the house with a lantern still lit and a basket on her arm, picking her way down the shingle with the sure feet of someone raised on this coast.

"You'll want gloves," Signe said, by way of good morning. "And you'll want to start at the north end. The current brings everything in there."

"Thank you." Ada hesitated. "I didn't expect help."

"I'm not helping you." Signe set her lantern on a flat rock, sheltered from the breeze, and began sorting debris into piles with brisk, unsentimental hands: wood, rope, metal, cloth. "I'm keeping the beach from becoming a graveyard. Anything with a name goes to my father's ledger, anything personal goes in the basket, and anything valuable goes in the house until someone claims it or spring comes, whichever is longer. You can walk alongside and write things down. That's different from helping."

Ada almost smiled. There was something in the girl's precision that reminded her of herself at that age, before she'd learned that precision was a kind of armor and wondered whether she'd ever take it off again. "Fair terms," she said.

They worked. The sun made no real attempt; it was a pale disc behind cloud, giving light without warmth, and under it the beach revealed itself piece by piece. A sea chest, stove in, spilling clothing that had soaked and dried and soaked again until it was more mineral than wool. A crate of oranges, every one spoiled, their smell sweet-rotten on the air. A child's shoe. Signe put the shoe in the basket without comment, and Ada wrote *one child's shoe, leather, right foot* in her notebook, and neither woman said the thing they were both thinking, which was that the sea gives back everything except the ones you ask it for.

Near midmorning, working the tide wrack where the seaweed lay thickest, Signe went still.

Ada looked up. The girl was crouched over something dark and rectangular, half-buried in weed, her hands hovering above it without touching.

"What is it?"

"I don't know." Signe's voice had changed. "It's heavy. It's — there's metal on it. Corners."

Together they cleared the weed away. It was a book. Large, bound in black leather gone slick and pale with salt, brass corners green with verdigris, a clasp long since sprung. The cover bore no title, but tooled faintly into the leather, almost worn away, was a design: a tree, its branches spread, its roots rendered with more care than its crown.

"It came out of the sea?" Ada said stupidly.

"The sea keeps libraries," Signe said. "My father says that. Everything ever lost on this coast washes up somewhere between here and the mainland, given enough storms." She glanced at Ada, and something moved behind her eyes — caution, or its cousin. "Do you want it? It's not mine to claim."

"It's nobody's to claim. That's rather the point of me." Ada knelt and took the book in both hands. It weighed as much as a paving stone. Water had gotten in somewhere; when she tilted it, it sloshed faintly, a sound like a small sea trapped inside a smaller one. "We should open it carefully. Let it dry slowly, or the pages will—"

She stopped.

Signe had already eased the covers apart, gently, the way one coaxes a stiff hinge, and the first pages fanned open to a flyleaf dense with faded ink. Even pulped by brine, even blurred, the hand was fine and old-fashioned, the letters tall and narrow. And the words themselves were legible enough, because they'd been written large, the way people write things meant to outlast them:

*Familie-Bibel der Voss.*

Ada felt the cold come up out of the stones and through her knees and into her chest, and it had nothing to do with the morning.

"Voss," she said.

"Yes." Signe's face had closed like a door. She stood, wiping her hands on her apron as though the book had dirtied them. "Well. It's a common enough name."

"It isn't, though. Not here." Ada kept her voice level, mild, the voice of a clerk. "I asked your father about it in autumn. At the census."

"And he said no Voss had ever lodged at the light." Signe picked up her basket. "He's the keeper. He'd know."

"He'd know," Ada agreed.

For a moment the two of them stood over the book with the tide sighing behind them, and Ada understood that she was watching a young woman decide, in real time, how much silence cost and who paid for it. She'd seen that arithmetic before. She performed it daily herself.

"Take it up to the house," Signe said at last. "Dry it by the stove. My father will want to enter it in the ledger — wreck goods, unclaimed. That's the law of the coast." She paused. "He's very exact about the ledger."

"I've noticed," said Ada.

* *

She dried the Bible in the keeper's kitchen, because the kitchen had the stove and the kitchen was neutral ground, and because Felix Eriksen spent his days now on the gallery repairing what the storm had broken, and his evenings sleeping like a man rationing himself, and the odds of meeting him were low. Ida helped. The ward turned out to have careful hands and no fear of old books; she blotted pages with linen rag, one at a time, laying each to dry on lines strung across the room like washing, until the kitchen filled with hanging leaves of paper and the smell of brine and lamp oil and, beneath it, something older — candle smoke and leather, the smell of another century.

Ida did not ask what the book was. She had heard the name on the flyleaf; Ada watched her hear it. The girl's hands slowed, then steadied, and she said nothing, and went on blotting. *Practiced,* Ada thought. *They're all practiced here. What household teaches children silence like catechism?*

By late afternoon enough pages had dried to be turned safely, and Ada allowed herself what she had been refusing all day. She sat at the kitchen table with the Bible open before her and read it as what it plainly was: a record. Family Bibles were records. Births, marriages, deaths — the very stuff of her commission, written by families instead of states, and no warrant required to read them. She told herself this twice. It sounded thinner each time.

The early pages were seventeenth-century German, Gothic script she could barely parse. Then Swedish crept in as the family migrated, as families do, westward and northward, and the entries became readable. Baptisms. Marriages. A column of births in the 1840s and '50s: Elias, born 1841. Greta, 1844. Karl, 1849. Marta —

There it was. Not a column entry but a fuller notice, added later in a different hand, the ink browner:

*Marta Voss, geboren den 3. März 1866 zu Vossgård. Vermählt —*

Married. The date that followed had been scraped. Someone had taken a knife or a coin to the vellum-soft paper and abraded the record away, leaving only a shallow bruise in the fibers and, when Ada tilted the page against the window light, the ghost of the removed ink rising like a bruise rises: two words, partially recoverable, in mirror-image relief against the next page's impression.

She fetched the lamp. Held the page at an angle. Breathed, and did not blink.

The ghosted impression read, as far as anything could be read: *Eriksen*. And beside it, where the groom's residence would have been recorded: *fyr* — the light. The light station. Married at the light.

And below the marriage line, added in yet another hand, cramped and hurried, a note in Swedish that the scrapings had missed entirely because it lived in the margin, low, half off the page:

*Marta Eriksen, född Voss — se även 1873.*

Marta Eriksen, née Voss — see also 1873.

Ada sat back. Outside, the winter light was failing over a sea that had surrendered this book and was, presumably, capable of surrendering more. Somewhere overhead, on the gallery, boots crossed iron plating — the keeper, walking his rounds, keeping his light. Twenty years, Ada thought. Twenty years of a man trimming wicks and logging weather and entering strangers' names in a ledger while one name of his own stayed out of every record he touched. He hadn't lied to her in autumn. He'd done something worse and more careful: he'd answered precisely, and let her mistake precision for truth. No Voss had ever *lodged* at the light. One had married into it.

She looked across the kitchen. Ida had fallen asleep in her chair, cheek on her folded arms, a drying page still pinned under one small palm. Thirteen years old, maybe fourteen; an orphan twice over by whatever quiet catastrophes had made her a ward; dreaming whatever children dream in houses built on held breaths.

Ada thought about Halvard Rehn arriving on the mainland side of the ice with his warrant and his youth and his certainty that law was a machine that produced justice if fed correctly. She thought about the inked-out line in the household ledger — *M. Voss*, erased by a pen pressed hard enough to tear — and about a marriage record scraped with a knife, and about a marginal note written by someone who wanted the truth findable by whoever looked hard enough. Three acts of erasure, and in each one, a failure to erase completely. People were like that, she'd learned. They couldn't quite bring themselves to destroy the proof. Some part of them always wanted to be found out.

Or wanted someone else to be.

She copied the marginal note into her notebook, word for word, and dated it, and signed it, because that was her discipline and her discipline was the last clean thing she owned. Then she closed the Bible, weighted it with two flat stones from the windowsill to keep its swollen covers flat, and sat in the failing light listening to the keeper's boots cross the gallery above her, over and over, patient as tide.

The sea had given up its secret. The question that remained — and she wrote this too, because writing made it bearable — was what she would do when Rehn asked her directly, and whether the answer she gave would serve the law she served or the family she'd been counting.

Above her, the boots stopped crossing. In the pause she heard, faintly, the clock in the front hall strike six, and under the clock's voice, softer, from behind the locked door at the end of the passage, something else: a small mechanical sound, rhythmic, patient, like a heart waiting to be listened to.
