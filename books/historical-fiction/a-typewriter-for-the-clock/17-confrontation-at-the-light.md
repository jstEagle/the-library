## 17. Confrontation at the Light

The Bible came ashore on a Tuesday, though Felix did not learn of it until Thursday, and by then the magistrate already had it.

He heard it first as a change in the wind's voice — a rumor carried up from the village in the mouth of the supply boy, Torvald, who climbed the point with his collar up and his breath steaming and delivered the news the way boys deliver news, too loudly and without any sense of its weight. "They've found something in the wrack, Keeper. Down past the shingle. The magistrate's been reading it all morning like it was scripture."

Felix set down the brass he was polishing and did not let his hands shake until the boy had gone.

Something in the wrack. The storms of that fortnight had given up their dead generously — spars, a ship's boat stove in at the stern, casks of tallow rolling white-eyed in the surf, the figurehead of some foreign trader staring up from the sand with salt-crusted lips. And now this. The sea, which had kept Marta's secret for twenty years along with him, had finally grown tired of holding what men concealed, and had opened its hand.

He knew before he saw it. He knew the way a man knows a knock at the door is the knock he has dreaded — not by evidence but by the sudden coldness of his own blood. There was only one thing the sea could surrender now that mattered.

* * *

He told himself he would not go down. He told himself that a keeper does not abandon his light to walk into a magistrate's parlor, that curiosity is a sin of the idle, that whatever Rehn held in those careful gloved hands was no business of Eriksens. He told himself all of it while pulling on his coat, while winding his scarf twice around his throat, while taking the lamp key off its hook out of pure habit because his hands wanted occupation and would have taken up an axe if nothing else offered.

The path down from the light was glazed with ice, and he took it without care, boots ringing on the frozen ruts, the sea below gray-green and working at the shore like a slow jaw. Smoke stood up straight from the village chimneys in the windless cold. Everything looked ordinary. Everything always did, right up until it didn't.

Ada Lindqvist met him at the edge of the green.

She had been waiting for him — there was no pretending otherwise; she stood with her hands folded in front of her, her census satchel over one shoulder like a soldier's kit, and her face wore the particular stillness of a person who has decided something difficult and is waiting to see if it was the right thing.

"Keeper Eriksen." A nod. Her breath clouded between them.

"Mrs. Lindqvist." He tipped his hat. Twenty years of practice made the gesture smooth. "Cold day for standing about."

"It is." She did not move aside. Behind her, through the window of Halvard Rehn's borrowed rooms above the chandler's, a lamp burned yellow against the early dusk, and a silhouette bent over a table. "I thought you should hear it from me first. Before you hear it from someone with less—" she searched, "—less care."

"Care about what?"

She looked at him then, directly, and Felix understood that whatever softness had passed between them across the autumn — the shared tea during the storm, the ledger she'd returned with an apology for its condition, the long silences two private people can hold comfortably in one room — none of it would purchase him ignorance today. She had come here to be honest with him precisely because honesty was about to become scarce.

"A Bible washed ashore after the last blow. Voss family arms stamped in the cover. Names written inside in three different hands going back sixty years." Her voice stayed level, quiet enough that the words belonged to the two of them alone against the whole empty green. "One of the names is Marta Voss. Married — the ink says — to one Felix Eriksen, keeper of this light."

The wind moved through the bare elms by the churchyard and made its dry rattling sound, like paper turning.

"I don't know anything about that," Felix said.

It came out perfectly. That was the horror of it. Twenty years of lying had worn the skill so smooth that the lie left his mouth looking like the truth, sounding like the truth, wearing the truth's own coat and hat, and he watched Ada Lindqvist receive it and know it for a lie and say nothing — because she had read his household ledger, because she had seen the inked-out line where M. Voss had lived and breathed and typed, and because some part of her had already chosen which side of this she stood on even if she could not yet name it aloud.

"No," she said softly. "I don't suppose you do."

* * *

Rehn sent for him the next morning.

The summons came in writing, which Felix noted was itself a message: not a boy at the door with an oral request between neighbors, but a folded paper bearing the seal of the district court, addressed formally to *Felix Eriksen, Keeper of the Skarholm Light*. Paper meant record. Record meant that whatever happened in that room upstairs would exist afterward, in ink, forever. Rehn was twenty-six years old and already understood that power lives in documents. It was, Felix thought grimly, climbing the chandler's stairs, exactly what Marta had spent her life running from.

The room was small and cold and scrupulously ordered. Charts hung squared on the walls. Books stood in ranks, spines aligned. On the table under the yellow lamp sat the Bible, open, and beside it a warrant in a leather folder, and beside that a cup of coffee gone cold and forgotten, which was the single human touch in the room.

Halvard Rehn stood when Felix entered. Young face, old eyes — Felix had catalogued him that way from the first meeting and the catalogue held. He wore his office like a coat slightly too large, and wore it anyway, buttoned to the throat.

"Keeper Eriksen. Thank you for coming."

"You summoned me, Magistrate. I'm not sure refusal was among my choices."

"It wasn't." No apology in it. "Please sit."

Felix sat. The chair was hard and straight-backed, built for attention rather than comfort. Rehn remained standing a moment longer, hands flat on the table, looking down at the open Bible as if gathering courage from it or perhaps asking permission of it. Then he sat too, opposite Felix, and turned the book gently so its pages faced across the table.

"Before I show you this," Rehn said, "I want you to understand what I am and what I am not. I am not Ada Lindqvist, doing sums for the Crown. I am not your brother Anders, who has debts." Something flickered across his face at that — distaste, quickly mastered. "And I am not the men who prosecuted this case twenty years ago, who wanted a conviction more than they wanted the truth. I am a magistrate. My work is to find out what happened and write it down correctly. That is all my work is. Do you understand me?"

"I understand you're young," Felix said, "and believe the law is a clean instrument."

"And you believe it is not."

"I believe," Felix said, "it is a hammer. Useful for driving nails. Useless for mending things."

For the first time, Rehn smiled — briefly, without warmth, the smile of a man who has heard an argument before and rehearsed his answer. "Then we agree on the danger of my office, if not on its purpose." He touched the Bible's edge but did not yet turn it further. "A fishing crew found this after the great blow, wedged in the rocks below the old wreck-line, wrapped in oilcloth. Oilcloth, Keeper. Somebody wrapped this book in oilcloth before it went into the water. Books don't wrap themselves."

"The sea gives up strange things."

"The sea gives up what it's given." Rehn's eyes were steady and dark and entirely unblinking. "Here is what troubles me. The Voss estate burned in the spring of '73. One man was convicted — a groundskeeper, Joren Holt, who confessed, recanted, confessed again, and hanged himself in custody before trial's end. Convenient man, Holt. Dead men sign nothing. But the fire investigators' notes, which I pulled from the archive last month, describe accelerant poured from the north veranda — poured, in patterns, deliberately — and Holt's own confession places him at the stables at the hour of the fire, a quarter mile south. The notes call this discrepancy *unresolved*. Unresolved, and filed, and forgotten, because the family wanted it forgotten and the family paid for forgetting." He paused. "Now the sea returns a family Bible. Now the census finds a keeper who once lodged a M. Voss and inked her out. Now the brother of that keeper owes money to men who trade in Voss secrets. You'll forgive me, Keeper, but coincidences of that density are not weather. They are architecture."

Felix said nothing. He had learned silence from lighthouses: stand still, stand bright, give away nothing but the fact of your presence. Let the storm spend itself against you.

Rehn studied him for a long moment, then turned the Bible fully around and pushed it across the table.

Inside the front cover, in faded brown ink, ran the names. Generations of them. At the bottom, in a younger hand, smaller than the rest as though the writer had hoped smallness might hide her:

*Marta Voss, wed Felix Eriksen, 19th of June, 1873.*

Four months after the fire.

"Read them," Rehn said quietly. "Take your time."

"I know how names look, Magistrate."

"Do you? Then look at this one and tell me what you see."

Felix looked. He let himself look fully, because refusing to look would be worse — a man who has never seen a thing cannot be accused of recognizing it, but a man who will not look has already confessed in every court that matters. The ink had gone the color of dried blood. The letters leaned slightly rightward, hurried. He knew that hand better than he knew his own. He had watched it fill ledgers and shopping lists and letters never posted, and once — one night, one locked room, one page still sitting in the carriage of a machine that ticked like a second heart — he had watched it write down the truth of a fire, naming names, and he had locked the room again and kept the lock oiled for twenty years.

"It's a name in a book," he said.

"It's your wife's name."

"My wife was Marta Eriksen. She died eleven years ago. She was born in Kristianstad, orphaned young, worked as a governess before I married her. Ask anyone in the parish."

"I have asked everyone in the parish." Rehn leaned forward. "That is rather the problem, Keeper. Your wife appears in no parish register anywhere in this district before 1873. No birth record. No confirmation. No governess position that any household will admit to. It is as though she walked out of the weather one day and into your lighthouse, and every document of her previous existence was either destroyed or never existed at all." He tapped the page once, lightly, almost tenderly. "People who are real leave records. People who are running leave gaps. This—" he indicated the Bible, the warrant folder, the whole cold ordered room, "—is the gap closing."

Felix felt the old dread rise in him, the same animal cold that had stirred the day Ada Lindqvist's pen had paused too long over her census form, and he mastered it the way he had mastered it for twenty years, the way he mastered everything: by breathing slowly and giving the storm nothing.

"If you've brought me here to accuse my dead wife of something," he said, "you should say the accusation plainly. I'll not sit here while you circle it."

"Circling is my profession just now." Rehn sat back. "Very well. Plainly, then. I hold a warrant for the recovery of stolen property belonging to the Voss estate — property removed from the premises prior to the fire of 1873 and never accounted for. The estate's surviving heirs, who are men I have no affection for and less trust in, believe that property to be a typewriting machine containing documents relevant to the arson. They want it back. What they want *from* it, I suspect, is destruction — because a machine that types can type confessions, and confessions cut both ways, and the Voss family has spent twenty years on the safe side of that blade." His voice dropped. "I also believe, Keeper — and this is not the law speaking, this is Halvard Rehn, late of the university, son of a drunkard father whom the law failed utterly — I believe that an innocent man hanged himself in a cell in '73 while the true hand that struck the match slept soundly under good roofs. And I believe the proof of it may be sitting in a locked room in a lighthouse on this island, and has been sitting there for twenty years while a woman died keeping its secret and a man grows old keeping hers."

The room was very quiet. The lamp hissed faintly. Somewhere below, the chandler's dog barked twice at gulls and gave it up.

"Say her name," Felix said.

"Marta."

"Not the surname. Just that." His voice came out lower than he intended. "You've said the other one enough."

Rehn regarded him across the table, and something passed between them then that neither man would ever quite name afterward — the recognition of two stubborn men circling the same fire from opposite sides, each convinced the other would be burned, each unwilling to step back and let the other carry the water.

"I can compel you," Rehn said. "You understand that. I can put men on the ferry, I can ride up that hill with the warrant in my fist, and I can open every door on your island. The law gives me that. What the law does not give me is certainty — and if I break into a keeper's house and find nothing, I have made an enemy of the one man on this coast whom every soul trusts, and the Voss heirs will have bought themselves another twenty years with my blundering. So I am asking instead. Man to man. Once." He drew a breath. "Is the machine in your house?"

Felix met his eyes.

He thought of Marta's hands on his face the last winter, chapped and cold, making him promise. *Never let it leave the island. Promise me, Felix. Whatever comes.* He thought of Signe's arithmetic, coal and oil tallied in her neat schoolgirl figures, learning without knowing it the family discipline of counting what must last. He thought of Ida's ear pressed to the locked door, listening to the ticking, dreaming of a history she'd been denied. He thought of Anders on the mainland selling his brother's silence by the ounce.

"No," Felix said.

Rehn closed his eyes briefly. When he opened them, there was no anger in them, and that was somehow worse.

"You're a good liar," Rehn said. "Twenty years of practice. I saw it the moment I asked — not in your words, your words were perfect, but in your face. Right here." He touched his own cheekbone. "A man tells a lie with his mouth and the truth fights its way out underneath his eye. You blinked twice and swallowed once, and your jaw set like a man bracing against a wave. I grew up watching my father lie to magistrates. I know the geography of a lie better than I know the psalms." He gathered the Bible back toward himself, carefully, reverently, closing it over the names. "So I won't insult us both by asking again."

"What happens now?"

"Now?" Rehn stood, buttoning his coat, and for a moment looked every year of his age and several he hadn't reached. "Now I go back to the archive, and the file, and the discrepancies that no one resolved, and I try to build a case that doesn't depend on the word of a keeper sworn to silence or the testimony of heirs bought and paid for. Now I wait for the ice, which waits for no one. And now, Keeper, I tell you this, and you may make of it what you will." He paused at the door, the gray light behind him putting his young face half in shadow. "If that machine holds what I think it holds, then it isn't stolen property. It's a pardon for a dead man and a verdict against a living one, and there is no warrant in this province that should be allowed to bury it. Bring it to me yourself and I swear to you, on my office and on my father's grave, that it will speak in open court and not die in an heir's strongbox."

"And if I bring you nothing?"

"Then the ice comes," Rehn said, "and the river closes, and whatever truth is in your house winters there with you — and I will spend the spring digging, and I am patient, and I am young, and unlike you, Keeper, I have nothing I love enough to protect at the cost of justice."

He opened the door. Cold air poured in.

"One more thing," he said, not turning. "Your brother boarded the supply ferry yesterday morning. He was seen in the company of two men from the city — men whose employers I can name if I need to. Whatever he sold them, he sold it cheap." Now he did turn, and there was something almost gentle in his expression, the gentleness of a man delivering a prognosis. "Whatever you mean to do, do it soon. You have fewer days than you think, and one fewer friend than you had."

The door closed behind him.

Felix sat alone in the cold ordered room for a long time, listening to the lamp hiss and the sea work at the shore, and in the silence he counted — as he had taught his daughter to count, as the family silence demanded — the days remaining until the river froze, and the hours remaining until Anders's bargain reached the wrong ears, and the single night that might remain in which a man could row a typewriter across breaking ice and keep a promise made to a dying woman.

Then he rose, put on his hat, and went home to open the locked room.
