## 18. Marta's Letters

The night Marta Voss left her father's house, the estate was burning for the second time in a week.

She stood at the edge of the orchard with her back against an apple tree gone silver with frost, watching the east wing glow like a lantern with its shutter knocked out. The fire had started in the counting room — so they would say later, so her father was already saying to the men from the village, his voice carrying across the frozen gravel, steady as a sermon. *Sabotage. The stable hand. I saw him near the lamps.* And the men nodded, because Elias Voss had never in his life needed to be believed twice.

Marta had been in the counting room herself that evening. Not when the fire began — an hour before, folding papers into oilcloth while the household slept. She had seen what was kept there: the ledgers with their two sets of columns, one for the magistrate and one for the truth. The debts her father owed the shipping houses in Stockholm, dressed up in the books as investments. The wages withheld from forty laborers and entered as paid. And beneath the false bottom of the strongbox, the insurance policies — three of them, taken out on the east wing in the autumn, weeks before the first small fire in the hay barn.

Her father was burning his own house to drown his own debts, and he had found a man to hang for it.

She pressed her palm flat against the frosted bark and breathed until the shaking stopped. In the satchel at her hip, wrapped in oilcloth and then in wool, the typewriter rode against her spine like a second heart. It had been her mother's, bought in Leipzig before Marta could walk, and it was the only thing in the house her father had never touched — a woman's toy, he called it, too delicate to matter. He was wrong about its delicacy. It weighed more than grief. But he was right that it did not matter to him, and that was precisely why she had chosen it over the jewels in her mother's case.

Because three days ago, alone in the sewing room with the door barred, she had sat down before it and set her fingers on the keys the way her mother had taught her, and she had written the truth.

Not in ink she could deny. Not in a hand that could be called forgery. The machine made each letter strike with the same indifferent force, the same iron certainty, and no man alive could look at a page it produced and say a woman's trembling hand had shaped it. *On the fourteenth of October,* the page said, *I heard my father instruct Klas Brenner to prepare the east wing for burning, and I saw him count out banknotes into Brenner's hand, and he said the stable hand Anders Holm was already half-believed and would serve.* She had signed it. She had dated it. She had folded it into the platen and locked the carriage cover down over it like a coffin lid over a sleeping child.

Now, across the orchard, torches were moving through the smoke toward the servants' quarters. They would find Holm by morning. They would find him because her father wanted him found, and because a man with no family and no money could not afford the lawyers who might have asked why a stable hand would burn the wing that held his master's own ledgers.

Marta pushed off from the tree and walked north, away from the firelight, toward the river road and the dark.

* * *

She had planned the flight the way she planned everything — badly, and then all at once. There were four hundred kronor sewn into her petticoat, saved from an allowance she had pretended to spend on gloves. There was a cousin in Göteborg who owed her mother a favor and would ask no questions if Marta arrived with a story about a finishing school. And there was the ferry at Kvistholm, twelve miles upriver, that left on the ebb tide whether God himself approved or not.

What she had not planned was the snow.

It came down in the third hour of walking, fat and silent, filling the wheel ruts, erasing the road behind her as though the country itself had agreed to forget her passage. By dawn she could not feel her feet. By midday she had missed the ferry — she watched its stern lamp dwindle down the gray channel from the landing at Kvistholm, and sat down on a bollard with the satchel in her lap and understood that she was going to weep, and refused to.

"You'll want the keeper's cottage, then."

The voice came from behind her, unhurried. A young man stood at the end of the landing with a crate of lamp oil balanced on one shoulder, snow in the pale stubble of his beard. He had the broad, weather-cured build of a man whose work was mostly lifting things against wind, and eyes the color of shallow water over sand — light, watchful, faintly amused.

"The keeper's dead," Marta said. Her voice came out cracked. "Everyone here is dead."

"Not yet." He shifted the crate. "I'm the keeper. Felix Eriksen. The ferry's gone, and the next one's Thursday if the ice holds off, which it won't. There's a stove at the light station and more tea than sense. You can wait out the week there, or you can sit on this bollard until you freeze, and I'll have to carry you either way, and you're heavier than you look."

"I am not."

"No," he agreed, and something at the corner of his mouth moved. "You're not."

Later she would try to remember why she followed him. She would tell herself it was the cold, the exhaustion, the simple animal arithmetic of a warm stove versus a frozen bench. All true, and none of it the truth. The truth was that he looked at her satchel — heavy, odd-shaped, guarded the way she realized she had been guarding it since the orchard — and looked away again without one question. Twenty years old and already he knew the shape of a person carrying something they could not put down.

The light station stood on a black thumb of rock where the river widened toward the sea, a white tower and a low stone house with salt crusted in every seam. Inside it smelled of brass polish, lamp oil, and coffee, and it was so quiet after the burning house that Marta's ears rang. Felix set the crate down, lit the stove, put water on, and then stood with his hands at his sides as if uncertain what a host did next.

"Ferry comes Thursday," he repeated. "Or doesn't."

"Thursday," she said.

He nodded, poured the tea, and went up the tower to trim the wick, and that was the whole of their conversation for a day and a half. She learned the rhythm of the place by listening: his boots on the spiral stair, twice a night, the small metallic music of the clockwork that turned the lens. She learned that he ate standing up. She learned that he talked to the lamp — not prayers, exactly, but reports, the way a man briefs a superior he respects. *Wind's backing northwest. Glass needs wiping on the landward side.*

On the second evening she found the typewriter's case had swollen with damp and opened it before the stove to check the mechanism, and he came down the stair and stopped halfway, and she felt him looking at the page still seated in the carriage — her page, the page she had not dared destroy and could not read aloud to anyone living.

"That's a fine machine," he said.

"It's my mother's."

"She wrote letters on it?"

"Everything." Marta turned the carriage so the page faced away from him, a motion so quick and so guilty that she saw him see it. "Ledgers. Recipes. Letters to me when I was at school. She believed a thing wasn't real until it was written down properly."

"And you?"

"I believe," Marta said, staring into the stove, "that some things are only real once they're written down properly."

Felix was quiet a moment. Then he climbed the rest of the stairs, and above her the clockwork took up its patient ticking, and Marta sat with her hands in her lap and understood that she had just been given something she had not known she was begging for: a room in which no one would ask.

* * *

Thursday's ferry did not come. Nor Friday's. The ice came instead, moving down the channel overnight with a sound like distant artillery, and by morning the river was a single white plain stitched with pressure ridges, and Kvistholm was a week's walk across country Marta did not know, past villages where her father's name was worth more than her life.

"A fortnight, maybe," Felix said, studying the ice from the gallery. "Maybe a month. The mail boat will break through when it can."

"I should go inland," she said. "There's a road—"

"There's a road," he agreed. "And constables on it, asking after a young woman traveling alone with a heavy case. A girl ran off from the Voss place, they're saying. Father's offered a reward." He did not turn around. "I don't listen to gossip. But the wind carries it up the tower all the same."

Marta's hands went cold inside her mittens. "You know who I am."

"I know you're Miss Something, and that Miss Something is afraid, and that whatever's in that case matters more than your jewels would, because you didn't take any." Now he turned, and the winter light off the ice made his pale eyes almost white. "I keep a light, miss. It's not my trade to ask what people are steering away from. Only to make sure they see the rocks."

She should have left. Every prudent instinct said so — go inland, take the reward risk, reach Göteborg, reach her cousin, become someone new. Instead she stayed through the fortnight, and then through the month, and the reasons accumulated the way snow does, flake by flake, until one day she looked down and could not see the ground she'd started from. She stayed because he taught her to trim the wick and her hands remembered her mother steadying them over the keys. She stayed because when the ice groaned at night she stopped being afraid of the sound and started being afraid of how little she'd mind dying somewhere so quiet. She stayed because in February he carried her across the pressure ridge when the mail boat finally broke through — carried her, actually lifted her, laughing at his own absurdity — and set her down on the deck, and neither of them let go quickly enough, and the boat blew its whistle, and the moment passed, and it did not pass.

She got off at the first stop. She told herself it was to wire her cousin. She walked back across the ice at dusk, following his lamp, and when he opened the door he did not look surprised.

"Marry me," Felix said, before she could speak. Then, visibly appalled at himself: "That is — I mean to say—"

"Yes," Marta said.

"—it occurs to me that a married woman traveling under her husband's name is a different creature entirely in the eyes of the law, and I would never presume—" He stopped. "Yes?"

"Yes," she said again, and began, helplessly, to laugh, and then he was laughing too, and the two of them stood in the doorway of the light station laughing like fools while the river froze solid behind her and her father's house burned its way into ash and legend, and Marta Voss ended between one breath and the next, and Mrs. Eriksen of the Kvistholm Light began.

* * *

They were married in March by a pastor on the mainland who asked fewer questions than he was paid not to, and Marta learned the grammar of her new life the way she had once learned Latin: thoroughly, secretly, and with a pleasure that alarmed her. She learned that Felix's parents had died of fever within a year of each other and left him the appointment at twenty-two, too stubborn to give it up. She learned that his brother Anders came twice a year and borrowed money both times, and that Felix lent it knowing, and loved him anyway, and said nothing. She learned that silence in this house was not a wall but a roof — it covered everyone equally, and you lived underneath it together.

And she learned the locked room.

It had been a storeroom; Felix cleared it in April without being asked, without being told why. She moved the typewriter in on a table beneath the seaward window, and beside it a box for her mother's other papers, and a drawer for the pages. Because that was the thing she discovered about herself in the first year of marriage: she could not stop writing.

She wrote the confession again, cleaner, fuller, with dates and sums — the version that would stand in a court if it ever had to, the version that named Klas Brenner and the banknotes and the insurance policies and her father's voice saying *the stable hand will serve.* She wrote the account of the flight, for Signe someday, if there was ever a Signe. She wrote letters to her mother, dead six years, because the machine made writing feel like speaking to someone who answered in the same voice. Page after page, struck letter by letter with that indifferent iron certainty, and every page she locked in the drawer, and every night the house ticked around her like a second heart, and she slept.

Signe came in the spring of the second year, red-faced and furious with the world in general, and Marta held her and thought: *now there are two things I would burn the world to keep.* It did not occur to her yet that the two things might one day require opposite fires.

* * *

The news came north with the supply boat in the fourth year, in a newspaper used to wrap crockery. Marta unwrapped it absently and read her father's name in the shipping notices, and then, lower, in the court reports, the sentence she had known was coming and had somehow still not prepared for:

*Holm, Anders, laborer, convicted of arson in the destruction of the Voss estate, sentenced to eight years' penal servitude.*

Eight years. Not the rope — her father had wanted the rope, but even Elias Voss could not manufacture witnesses enough for murder, only enough for ruin. Eight years for a man who had never touched a lamp to anything but straw. Marta sat down on the kitchen floor with the newspaper in her lap and the kettle going unheeded on the stove, and when Felix came down the stair and found her there, she did not hide it. That was the day he learned everything. She read him the whole of it — her father, the ledgers, the policies, the orchard, the flight — and then she unlocked the room and showed him the pages, and watched her husband, who had married a stranger and suspected as much, understand exactly what he had harbored.

He was quiet for a long time. Down in the lamp room below them the clockwork ticked.

"We send it to the magistrate," he said finally. "Tonight. I'll row it across myself."

"And say what? That the daughter of Elias Voss has been living under your name for four years?" She shook her head. "They'll hang me for perjury before they reopen his case. My father's word against mine, and mine delivered by a woman who lied on a marriage record. He bought the pastor, Felix. He'll buy the assizes." She put her hand flat on the typewriter's case, feeling the cold iron through the wood. "This is not evidence yet. This is a seed. Evidence needs soil — a court that isn't bought, a magistrate who isn't his. Someday the soil will come. Until then it lives here, where no one looks, because no one looks at a lighthouse."

"And if the soil never comes?"

"Then it lives here forever, and I live here forever, and that is a bargain I made gladly." She looked up at him. "Promise me something. Promise that whatever happens — if I die, if the world ends — the machine does not leave this island. Not for the mainland, not for the courts, not for anyone who comes asking with kind eyes and a warrant. It goes nowhere unless I send it. Swear it."

"Marta—"

"*Swear it.*"

He swore it. He was a man who kept lamps and promises with the same dumb fidelity, and she knew, even as she made him kneel to it, that she was handing him a weight he would carry long after she was done carrying anything at all. She swore nothing back. That was her one dishonesty in twenty years of honest silence: she did not tell him that someday the soil might come, and that when it came, it would come asking at his door, and that she had arranged, in the only way her conscience allowed, for the seed to be ready.

* * *

In her last winter — she knew it was her last the way sailors know weather, from a heaviness in the bones no doctor could name — Marta sat up late at the machine while the gales walked the point outside. Signe was seven, asleep in the loft. Felix was aloft, keeping the light against a sea gone white and insane. And Marta typed, slowly now, her fingers stiffening, a final page.

*To whoever opens this drawer,* it began. *My name was Marta Voss. My father burned his own house and gave a blameless man to the courts for it. Everything I know is in these pages, and every word is true, and I have hidden the truth the way he hid his ledgers — well, and selfishly, and at another man's cost. Forgive me the hiding. I chose love over lineage, and I would choose it again, but I was wrong to think the choice could bury the rest. Truth is not a thing you can keep. It is a thing you can only postpone.*

She read it through. Then she did the bravest cowardly thing of her life: she left it in the drawer with the others, locked the room, and put the key on the ring with the kitchen keys, where a curious child might one day find it — and told Felix, when he came down gray-faced with spray and exhaustion, that the machine must never leave the island, and kissed him, and meant it, and let him believe that promise was the same thing as peace.

Outside, the storm was building toward the one that would take her, though neither of them knew it yet. The clockwork ticked. The lamp turned. And in the locked room, in the iron type that could not tremble, the truth waited — patient as ice, and moving, all these years, faster than anyone dreamed.
