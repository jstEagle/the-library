## 19. Anders Takes the Ferry

The supply ferry came on a Thursday, which was God's own joke, because Anders Eriksen had spent every Thursday of his childhood watching his older brother climb the tower stairs to light the lamp while he himself stood below, holding the spare glass, waiting to be needed and never quite being it.

He watched the boat come in now from behind the coal shed, collar up against the wind, and told himself for the fortieth time that morning that what he was doing was not theft. It was arithmetic. There were three berths and four souls, and Felix had spent three weeks not deciding, which meant Felix had decided nothing would change until the ice closed the channel and the decision made itself. Anders was simply helping the arithmetic along. Someone had to go. The men he owed were not famous for their patience, and the winter was famous for its length.

* * *

He had rehearsed the conversation with Rehn's clerk a dozen times in the tavern mirror back home — the set of his shoulders, the weary candor of a man burdened by family loyalty but unable to watch justice miscarry. He had practiced saying *my brother* without flinching. In rehearsal, flinching had won every time.

But that was on the mainland, where the practice didn't count. Here on the island, the only audience was gulls and the ghost of his mother, who had raised two sons and told everyone, always, that Felix got the lamp and Anders got the hands. She'd meant it kindly. That was the worst of it.

The ferry's mate, a boy with chilblains and a ledger under his arm, met him at the landing.

"Passenger manifest," the boy said. "Three berths this crossing. Names?"

Anders opened his mouth to say he wasn't going anywhere.

"Anders Eriksen," he heard himself say instead.

The boy wrote it down. Just like that. Twenty years of standing outside rooms where decisions happened, and the door had finally opened onto a list, and his name had walked through it before the rest of him could object. He signed where the boy pointed. The ink was thin and blue and looked, in the grey morning light, like something spilled rather than written.

"You'll want to board by two bells," the boy said. "Captain doesn't wait for weather or sentiment."

"No," Anders said. "I imagine he doesn't."

* * *

He went up to the house once more, ostensibly to fetch his good coat. What he actually did was stand in the kitchen doorway and watch Signe teach Ida her letters at the table, the two girls bent over a slate, their heads nearly touching, one fair head and one dark, and felt something in his chest turn over like ballast in a hull.

Felix wasn't there. Felix was at the light, doing what Felix did, which was everything, always, first and last and in between.

"I'm walking the point," Anders announced, too loudly.

Signe looked up. She had her mother's way of looking at people, patient and level, as though she could wait out any lie the way tide waits out a seawall. She was seventeen and she already waited better than he ever had.

"Take the oilskin," she said. "There's weather coming from the northwest."

"There's always weather coming from somewhere."

"Yes," Signe said, and went back to the slate.

Ida looked at him a moment longer. The girl missed nothing; that was the trouble with children nobody had bothered to lie to yet. They watched the adults the way sailors watch the horizon, reading weather off faces. He smiled at her. She did not smile back, exactly. She gave him the nod you give someone whose boat is leaving, polite and final.

He took the coat anyway.

* * *

The walk down to the landing took twenty minutes and cost him forty years.

That was how it felt, anyway — each step peeling something off him. First the easy things: the smell of the house, the sound of the clock Felix wound like a heartbeat, the particular creak of the third stair that he'd known since boyhood. Then harder things: Signe's face when she'd been small and he'd lifted her onto his shoulders so she could see over the crowd at the midsummer fire. Felix's hand on his shoulder at their mother's funeral, heavy and wordless, forgiving him in advance for whatever came next, because Felix forgave in advance, always had, and somehow that made everything worse.

Because here was the truth of it, the truth he'd been dressing in better and better clothes for three weeks: the men he owed money to were not merely men. Two of them were cousins of the Voss heirs — the true heirs, the ones the estate had passed to when the courts finished their work and Marta Voss became Marta Eriksen became a name inked out of a household ledger. And those cousins had let it slip, over cards, over brandy, over the slow patient months of his ruin, that there was a piece of property still unaccounted for. A machine. Something that typed. Something that, if it still existed, if it still held what people said it held, was worth more than money.

Worth enough, they'd said, smiling, to settle any debt.

He had told them nothing definite. He wanted that on the record, wherever records were kept — in heaven, in his own conscience, on the ferry manifest. He had said only: *there may be something. Let me see.* And they had said: *see quickly, Anders. Ice comes early this year.*

So this was seeing. This was seeing quickly. This was a man in a borrowed good coat walking down to a boat with his brother's silence folded in his pocket like a stolen letter.

* * *

The harbor master's shed was empty, the stove cold. Good. He didn't want witnesses. He wanted this done the way cowards prefer everything done: swiftly, in bad light, with no one looking.

But Ada Lindqvist was there.

She sat on an overturned crate near the water, wrapped in a grey shawl, writing in her notebook with the little pencil she used, that stub of graphite worn round at the edges. She looked up when his boots sounded on the boards, and for one long moment neither of them spoke.

"Catching the ferry, Mr. Eriksen?" she asked at last.

"The supply run," he said. "Business on the mainland."

"Of course." She clicked her tongue softly. "Everyone has business on the mainland lately. Your brother's household seems very busy for a family of four."

"A family of five," he corrected, then wished he hadn't.

"Mm." She looked back at her notebook. "Five. Yes. The census will want to know about that eventually."

He stood there, feeling the sentence land somewhere near his ribs and stay there. She didn't look up again. She didn't need to. The woman had a way of making a silence do the work of a constable, and he stood inside it for three full seconds before muttering something about checking his trunk and walking away faster than dignity allowed.

Behind him, faintly, he heard the scratch of her pencil resume.

* * *

Two bells came the way all bells come when you're dreading them: instantly.

The ferry sat low in the water, fat with coal and flour and lamp oil for the winter, and the captain — a broad, wind-scoured man named Halvorsen who'd known both Eriksen boys since they were knee-high — took Anders's ticket without comment, which somehow felt worse than comment.

"Your brother well?" Halvorsen asked.

"Keeping the light."

"He always does." The captain handed the ticket back torn. "Tell him the ice report says three weeks early this year. Maybe less. River mouth is already skimming over at night."

"I'll tell him," Anders said, and knew he wouldn't be there to tell him anything.

He boarded. He found his berth — lower starboard, narrow as a coffin and about as cheerful — and stowed his bag, which contained one good coat, one bottle of aquavit bought with money he didn't have, and a folder of notes he'd made over three sleepless nights: dates, names, the location of the locked room in Felix's house, the fact of the machine itself, described as best he could from a single glimpse twenty years ago, when he'd carried Marta's trunk up from the landing and felt, through the wood, the strange dense weight of metal and keys.

He had never told anyone about the trunk. Not even Felix. Especially not Felix.

Now the whole story was in the folder, and the folder was in the bag, and the bag was in the coffin-berth, and by Friday night the whole story would be in the hands of whoever paid for it, and after that it would belong to the world, and Felix's twenty years of careful dark would come apart like wet rope.

He lay back on the bunk and stared at the overhead boards and tried to construct the version of events in which he was the hero.

It went like this: *A man drowning reaches for a rope. The rope happens to be tied to a boat that is also carrying his brother's secrets. Is the drowning man a villain for pulling? The Voss matter is old. The convicted man confessed. Justice was served, or was served at, or was adjacent to. And besides — besides — what good does a truth do anyone, rusting in a locked room? Truth wants daylight. He would be giving the truth daylight. He was not selling his brother out; he was setting the truth free, and incidentally saving his own neck, and these two facts were neighbors, not accomplices.*

It almost worked. It worked right up until he heard, through the deck above him, the muffled sound of the ship's bell, and beneath that, imagined or real, the far-off sweep of a lighthouse beam coming around, around, around — Felix's beam, Felix's light, the light Felix had kept burning through every storm since the year their mother died, the light that had never once gone dark, not even the night their father was buried, and here was Anders taking the last berth out and the family's one great secret with him, and calling it opportunity.

*Cowardice dressed as opportunity.* Where had he heard that phrase? Nowhere. He'd invented it just now, lying in the dark, which meant some part of him already knew the truth and had simply filed it under a heading he could live with until spring.

He got up. He took out the aquavit. He drank half of it straight, standing, in the rocking dark, and felt nothing except colder.

* * *

They cast off at dusk. From the stern rail, Anders watched the island recede — the black hump of the point, the white smudge of the keeper's house, and above it all the tower, and atop the tower, right on schedule, the lamp blooming alive in the failing light, steady as a pulse.

Someone standing at a window of that house had watched him sign the manifest. He was sure of it. Signe, probably. Or Ida with her sailor's eyes. Or Felix, come down from the tower for once, seeing his brother's name on a list and understanding everything in a single glance, because Felix understood everything and said nothing, said nothing the way stone says nothing, said nothing for twenty years, and now the silence was on a boat, sailing away in a folder, and there was no calling it back.

The mainland lights came up an hour later, strung along the shore like a dropped necklace. Somewhere past them, in a warm room that smelled of tobacco and law books, a young magistrate was waiting for information. Somewhere nearer, in a tavern that smelled of worse, the Voss cousins were waiting too, playing cards, playing patience, playing him.

Anders finished the aquavit. He thought of Ida's nod at the door — polite, final, the nod you give a departing boat — and understood suddenly, with the terrible clarity that drink sometimes delivers like a bill presented for payment, that the child had known. Children always know. She had looked at him and seen the whole of it: the folder, the debt, the sale. And she had nodded anyway, because she was kind, because she was eleven, because kindness was all she had to give a man walking away from her.

He gripped the rail. The sea ran black and silver under the stern lantern. Behind him the island's light swept the water once more, touched the foam, moved on.

"Forgive me," he said aloud, to no one, to the water, to a brother who could not hear him over the engine and would have forgiven him anyway, which was the cruelest part.

Then he went below to polish his story for the magistrate, and the island's light swept the empty water again, and again, keeping faith with a coast that no longer had anyone on it worth betraying — except everyone.
