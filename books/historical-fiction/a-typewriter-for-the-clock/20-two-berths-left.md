## 20. Two Berths Left

The note was pinned to the door of the boathouse with a fishhook, which was Anders's idea of a joke and had been for fifty years. Felix stood in the gray light of morning with the hook between his fingers, turning it over as though it might tell him something the paper would not.

*Gone ahead,* the note said, in his brother's looping hand. *Don't wait up. Business on the mainland. Back by thaw if God wills it — A.*

No mention of the ferry. No mention of berths. No mention of the money Anders owed to men whose names were spoken in taverns the way one speaks of weather that has already done its damage. Just *business*, the word doing what it always did in Anders's mouth: standing in front of the truth like a fence in front of a burned field.

Felix read the note three times. Then he walked down to the landing, where the supply ferry's wake was still unspooling toward the mainland, a long pale seam drawn across water the color of old tin. The channel ice had not yet closed. It wouldn't for another day or two, maybe three, and then the river would be shut like a book until spring.

He counted, because counting was all he had left of arithmetic that made sense.

Four souls at the light. Three berths on the icebreaker. And now one of those berths was somewhere out on the open water, carrying his brother toward whatever bargain he thought he could still strike.

Two berths. Three people. Signe. Ida. Himself.

Felix stood at the edge of the landing until his beard stiffened with spray, and he found — this was the shameful part, the part he would carry like a stone in his boot — that beneath the fury there was something moving in him that felt horribly like relief.

* * *

The fury came first, so he let himself have it while he split kindling behind the house, because fury was a thing you could put an axe through without breaking anything that mattered.

Anders had taken the berth without asking. Had crept down before dawn with his sea bag and his debts and his hunger for the mainland, and had simply gone, the way he'd gone from every difficult thing since they were boys — from their father's funeral, from the herring boat when the season soured, from Marta's graveside before the last prayer was finished. Felix swung the axe and thought: *he didn't even ask.* Thought: *he knew I'd say no.* Thought, worse: *he knew I'd say yes, and couldn't bear to owe me that too.*

Because that was the shape of it, wasn't it. Anders hadn't stolen a seat. He had taken the one he knew Felix would surrender anyway, and spared them both the scene of it. His brother had committed cowardice dressed as efficiency, and had even, in some crooked cellar of himself, perhaps believed he was doing Felix a kindness.

The axe bit through a knot and stuck. Felix wrestled it free, breathing hard, steam rising off his shoulders in the cold.

Anders had also, Felix understood now, taken something else aboard that ferry. He'd spent two nights drinking with Rehn's clerk before the magistrate ever set foot on the island; Ida had heard enough to make her go quiet in a way children shouldn't know how to be quiet. Whatever Anders knew about Marta, about the machine, about twenty years of careful silence — he had carried it across the strait in his coat pocket like contraband, to sell.

Felix set another log upright. His hands were steady. That frightened him more than shaking would have.

*Let him sell it,* said a voice in him that sounded like no one he wanted to be. *Let the whole rotten thing come out. Let Rehn come with his warrant. You've kept it long enough. Twenty years of oiling a lock and lying to your daughter and listening to Ida press her ear against that door — let someone else carry it now.*

He brought the axe down harder than he meant to and split the block clean in half, both halves leaping apart into the snow.

That was the relief. Not relief that Anders was gone — never that, whatever the man deserved — but relief that the choosing had been taken out of his hands. For three days Felix had lain awake doing terrible sums. Four souls, three berths. Who goes, who stays, who freezes or starves or drowns on an island that will not see another boat until April. He had looked at Signe asleep and thought *she has a conservatory waiting, she has a life stacked up like cordwood.* He had looked at Ida mending her stockings by the stove and thought *she has nothing waiting, which means she has everything to lose.* He had looked at his own hands and thought *these are keeper's hands, these were never meant for anywhere but here* — and then hated himself, because that was the arithmetic of a man arranging his own sacrifice and calling it love.

Now the sum had changed without his consent. Two berths, three souls, and the missing one already beyond shouting distance. There was still no answer. But the question was smaller, and small questions could be survived.

Felix gathered the split wood, stacked it, and went inside to face the two people he had not yet told.

* * *

They were both at the table when he came in, and they both knew before he spoke. That was the thing about households built on silence: everyone became a reader of faces, fluent in the language of what went unsaid.

"Your uncle's gone," Felix said. "Took the ferry."

Signe didn't look up from her ledger. Her pen moved, stopped, moved again. "Did he take his share of the coal money?"

"He took a berth," Felix said. "One of the three."

The pen stopped for good. Signe laid it down parallel to the ledger's edge, the way she did when she was keeping herself level, and only then raised her eyes. She had Marta's eyes. He had never managed to stop seeing that, and he had never once said it aloud.

"Then there's two," she said.

"There's two."

"And three of us."

"I can count, Signe."

Her jaw tightened. He watched her decide not to say what was in her face — that he was the obvious one to stay, that he belonged to the island the way the lamp belonged to its tower, that no one would grieve long. She was too proud to offer him the easy exit and too angry to pretend she hadn't considered it. Twenty years of family silence, and now every silence between them had teeth.

It was Ida who spoke, small and careful, from the far end of the table. "Is Mr. Anders coming back?"

"No," Felix said. "I don't expect he is."

Ida nodded slowly, absorbing it the way she absorbed everything — twice, once as a child and once as something older being born. Then she asked the question that landed on Felix like a hand on the back of his neck.

"Was it because of the machine?"

The fire ticked in the stove. Signe's head came up sharply. "What machine?"

Ida looked at Felix, and Felix looked at Ida, and the girl understood immediately that she had stepped into a country she hadn't known existed. Her ears went red. "Nothing," she said. "Just — I hear ticking. Sometimes. From the locked room."

"You hear—" Signe began.

"It's the clock," Felix said. "Old houses tick."

But Signe was looking at him now with the particular stillness she had learned from him, and he knew the lie had landed wrong, had fallen between them onto the table like a dropped knife. She didn't push. That was the worst part. She folded her hands and returned to her ledger, and the silence she gave him was not obedience anymore. It was storage. She was putting things away to use later.

* * *

He climbed to the lamp room after dark, because the lamp room was where he did his honest thinking, alone above a sea that kept none of his secrets and repeated none of them either.

The beam swept out, swept home. Out, home. Below him the channel was beginning to skin over at the edges, white lace creeping out from both shores toward the middle, and by Thursday — by Friday at the latest — the river would be sealed. The icebreaker would come up the last open water, take on whoever held berths, and stand off again, and after that the island would be a world entire unto itself: coal, oil, flour, four months, and every truth locked in its room.

Which meant the typewriter had to leave before the ice did. Not with a passenger. Not in Anders's treacherous custody. It had to reach Halvard Rehn — or it reached no one, and the man who had rotted twenty years in a cell for the Voss fire rotted on, and Marta's name stayed buried under the alias Felix had helped her build, and everything — the flight, the marriage, the promise made to a dying woman in a cold bedroom — had been for nothing but their own safety.

*Never let the machine leave the island.* Marta's voice, that last winter, thin as a wick. *Promise me, Felix. They'll use it against my family. Promise me.*

He had promised. He had kept it for two decades the way he kept the lamp: nightly, faithfully, without asking what the darkness it pushed back might contain. And now the same machine that was his wife's dying wish sat in a locked room holding the one document that could hang a dead man's memory — Elias Voss, the true arsonist, protected by his own blood — or save a living man from a false conviction, depending entirely on who got hold of it first. Rehn wanted it under warrant, to bury it in an evidence locker, or worse, to return it to the Voss heirs as *property*. Anders wanted to sell knowledge of it to men who'd melt it down for the brass if they couldn't profit otherwise.

And Felix wanted — God help him — to give it away. To row it across the freezing strait himself, if it came to that, and put it into the hands of the one person on earth who seemed to want it for the reason Marta would have wanted it surrendered: the truth.

He thought of Rehn's face at their confrontation, the young magistrate circling him with the washed-up Bible like two dogs around a bone neither would release. Stubborn. Principled. Uncomfortable in his own warrant. Felix had seen enough men to know the difference between a man following orders and a man looking for permission to do right. Rehn was the second kind. He just didn't know it yet.

Out, home. The beam crossed the new ice and lit it up like a scar.

Two berths. Signe, who had claimed hers fair and square, with years of deferred conservatory dreams stacked behind her claim. Ida, who had offered yesterday — quietly, terribly, while washing dishes — to stay behind, *since Signe had plans and I've only got here.* He had nearly struck the table hearing it. A twelve-year-old girl volunteering herself for winter exile so the family math could balance, and meaning it, and being ashamed of how much lighter she clearly believed it would make everyone.

He would sooner row the girl across the floes on his own back than let her believe she was worth less than a seat.

So: two berths, and his daughter and his ward, and himself uncounted. The solution was standing right there, plain as arithmetic. Give them the seats. Stay himself. Keep the light. Let the island have its keeper through the freeze, as it always had.

Except the typewriter. Except the truth. Except the fact that a man who stayed behind with the evidence did the Voss family's work for them, guarding their secret through the very season when it could be carried nowhere and destroyed anywhere. If Felix stayed, the machine stayed frozen in that room until spring — and springs were long, and men with debts to the Voss heirs were patient, and Anders knew exactly which room it slept in.

Unless the machine didn't stay. Unless it crossed the ice ahead of the freeze, ahead of everything, by whatever means its keeper's own two arms could manage.

Felix gripped the rail and watched the beam sweep the black water, and felt the two halves of himself pull apart with a sound like the river ice cracking. The husband, who had promised. The keeper, who had promised. Both of them bound by oaths to a woman who had also, in her saner hours, typed out the truth and hidden it where it could someday be found — because Marta had never been a woman of one wish only. She had wanted the machine safe *and* the truth preserved. She had trusted him to know which promise mattered more when the day came.

He was no longer sure he knew. But the ice was closing, and the ferry was gone, and the decision was no longer going to wait for him to be ready.

Below, a lantern bobbed along the shore path — Ada Lindqvist, walking the strand in the dark with her census satchel, stopping where the wreck debris had piled. Even now, even stranded, the woman worked. Felix watched her crouch, examine something, write.

And then he saw what she was looking at, and his stomach dropped through the tower floor: the tide had brought in a second thing from the wreck line. Something small, pale, rectangular. Something that had not been there that morning.

A crate. Stenciled lettering, half legible even at this distance. *VOSS & SONS, STATIONERS — STOCKHOLM.*

The sea was not finished giving things up. And whatever Ada Lindqvist had just found on the freezing sand, she was standing over it now with her notebook open, and she was writing fast.
