## 21. Signe Learns the Name

The letter had been in the oil locker for eleven days before Signe finally took it.

She knew the count precisely because she counted everything now — the way she tallied coal and oil and lamp-black in her ledger, the way she tallied the days since Ada Lindqvist had left the island, since the storms had begun arriving one after another like messengers who refused to state their business. Eleven days since she'd watched her father's hands fold a paper small enough to disappear into his coat pocket, watched him cross the yard in the blue hour before dawn and kneel at the oil locker with the furtive economy of a man burying something rather than storing it.

Her father did not hide things. That was what she had believed for twenty years of her life. Her father was a man whose whole character was built out of keeping things visible — the logbook open on its stand, the lamp oil measured twice daily and recorded to the half-pint, the household accounts chalked on a slate by the stove where anyone might read them. Honesty as architecture. She had grown up inside that building and called it home.

And so when she had seen him kneel at the oil locker with a letter, she had done what the family taught: she had said nothing.

Now, alone in the storeroom with the wind worrying at the shutters, she took the letter out.

It was not addressed to him. That was the first thing she noticed — no name on the front at all, only a place: *Rehn & Associates, Advocates, Kristiania.* The second thing she noticed was that the seal had been broken and pressed closed again, carelessly, wax over wax, the work of a man who had never learned to lie with his hands.

Signe stood in the cold of the storeroom holding the envelope and understood, in some wordless animal way beneath thought, that whatever she did next would divide her life into two rooms with no door between them. She could put it back. She could climb the stairs and keep the arithmetic of the household and remain the daughter her father believed he had protected.

Or she could read it.

She read it.

---

*Sir,*

*Our office acts for parties interested in settling the estate of the late Elias Voss of Bergsund. We are given to understand you may be positioned to assist in locating certain property of the deceased, being a typewriting machine together with papers contained therein, removed from the estate premises prior to the fire of 1873.*

*You will appreciate the delicacy of our inquiry. The family does not seek scandal. It seeks only closure.*

*We are aware, further, of the presence at your station of one Marta Eriksen, formerly of Bergsund, whose maiden name may interest you more than ours. Records of the parish are imperfect. Ours are not.*

*We would prefer to deal with you directly and quietly. Failing that, other instruments exist.*

Signe read it three times. The third time she read it aloud, very slowly, because the words kept sliding off her mind like water off oiled canvas, refusing to soak in.

*One Marta Eriksen, formerly of Bergsund.*

Marta Eriksen was her mother. Her mother had been dead for nine years, and the dead do not have former places, and they certainly do not have maiden names that advocates in Kristiania write about in letters beginning *you will appreciate the delicacy of our inquiry.*

But the sentence went on. *Whose maiden name may interest you more than ours.*

Voss.

The letter did not say it. Not anywhere, not in plain ink. But Signe had spent her childhood listening to the mainland gossip that drifted across the strait with the supply boats — the story every child on the coast knew, the great house burned to its foundations, the stable hand hanged for it two years later while the family watched from their carriage with faces like closed doors. *The Vosses,* people said, and lowered their voices, not from grief but from the particular hush reserved for money and fire.

Her mother's name was Marta Eriksen. It had always been Marta Eriksen. It was carved on the stone in the churchyard on the mainland that Felix rowed to visit once a year, alone, in weather too poor for passengers.

*Formerly of Bergsund.*

Signe set the letter down on the coal scale, and her hand was shaking, and she looked at it shaking as though it belonged to someone else, someone standing outside herself watching a woman come apart in a room that smelled of kerosene and winter.

---

She did not go to her father. Not yet. That instinct — the silence, the waiting, the folding of knowledge away like linen in a chest — rose in her so naturally that it frightened her more than the letter did. Where had she learned it? Who had taught her that knowing a thing and speaking a thing were different trades? She stood in the storeroom and realized she had been schooled in this her entire life without ever once attending a lesson. The pauses after certain questions at the supper table. The way conversations about the mainland died when they approached Bergsund, the way the map on the schoolroom wall had seemed to her father to look elsewhere whenever anyone's finger traveled south along the coast. Twenty years of small avoidances, and she had absorbed them the way the walls of the light tower absorbed the sea's pounding — invisibly, structurally, until the avoiding was load-bearing.

She climbed to her own room under the eaves and took down the box where she kept what remained of her mother. There was not much. A woman who dies young leaves behind objects the way a snuffed candle leaves smoke — an impression of a shape rather than the shape itself. Two hairpins. A hymnal with the flyleaf inscribed in a hand Signe had always found oddly formal for a keeper's wife: *Marta — with love, Pappa.* And letters, seven of them, written during the courtship and the first years of marriage, which Signe had read so many times she could recite them.

She read them again now, differently.

*Marta Lindqvist,* said the flyleaf of the hymnal. She had never questioned it. Lindqvist — a common name, a salt-of-the-earth name, a name that meant nothing anywhere. Her mother had been born Marta Lindqvist of some inland parish Signe had never bothered to locate on a map, and she had married Felix Eriksen and become Marta Eriksen, and that was the whole of it, that was the genealogy of a lighthouse family, simple as tide tables.

Except that advocates in Kristiania wrote letters about imperfect parish records and names that might interest a widower more than their own.

Except that *Pappa* — whoever Pappa was, whoever had given a girl a hymnal bound in good calfskin with gilt edges that no fisherman's daughter would ever own — had been able to afford such a book.

Except that in all seven letters, in all those pages of her mother's careful, beautiful script, there was not one mention of a grandmother, an aunt, a cousin, a childhood friend, a village, a road, a tree climbed, a river swum. Seven letters, hundreds of sentences, and not one single anchor thrown backward into the past. Her mother's history began at nineteen, on a ferry crossing, meeting a serious young man with salt in his eyebrows. Everything before that was sea fog.

Signe sat on the floor with the letters spread around her like fallen leaves and felt the wound open — not sharply, not all at once, but with the slow terrible widening of ice giving way under a weight that has stood on it safely for years. Her whole life had been built on her mother's name, and her mother's name had been built on nothing at all. It was an alias. It was a coat turned inside out and worn fresh every morning for twenty years, and she had helped press it smooth, and she had never once asked why the lining was warm.

No. That was not true, and the not-true-ness hurt worse than the rest. She had asked. Once, aged nine or ten, sitting on the kitchen floor winding yarn while her mother wound the other end, she had asked where Mamma grew up, whether there were mountains, whether she'd had sisters. And she remembered — she could feel the memory now, surfacing like something drowned coming up hand over hand along a rope — she remembered how her mother's hands had gone still on the wool. How the room had gone quiet except for the clock. How her mother had smiled and said, *Far away, little one, and better there,* and changed the subject to the yarn, to the gauge, to whether Signe wanted her stockings grey or blue. Blue, Signe had said, and the question had sunk, and the silence had closed over it like water over a coin dropped from a bridge.

Her mother had lied to her. Gently, lovingly, with a smile — but lied. Or rather: had been made to lie, had been required to lie, had carried the lie the way Felix carried the oil up the tower stairs, daily, in all weather, until the carrying became invisible.

And then Signe thought: *until the carrying became inherited.* Because here she was, twenty-three years old, crouched in her own room with a stolen letter, having known for eleven days that her father hid papers in the oil locker and having said nothing, having practiced the family silence so faithfully that she had nearly become its finest graduate.

She stood up. She put the letters back in the box. She put on her heaviest shawl and her storm boots, and she went to find her father.

---

He was in the lamp room. Of course he was; at this hour, between the afternoon trim and the lighting, he was always in the lamp room, and Signe climbed the iron stairs with the letter folded against her breastbone like a blade she intended either to use or to be used upon.

Felix Eriksen stood at the lens with a chamois cloth, polishing the brass fittings with the slow circular attention of a priest tending an altar. He heard her boots on the stairs and did not turn — he knew her step, had known it since she was small enough to ride the stairs on his shoulders — and said, mildly, "Mind the third tread, it's taken to weeping."

"Papa."

That single word, spoken in a voice she did not recognize as her own, made him turn.

Later, trying to reconstruct the moment, Signe would think that he must have known at once. Fathers know. All those years of concealment had honed him into an instrument for reading trouble, and his daughter came up the last steps wearing hers the way a lamp wears its flame. But in the moment itself he only set down the chamois, folded his hands, and waited — the patience of a man who has stood many watches through long nights and knows that everything arrives eventually if you hold position.

"I went to the oil locker," Signe said. "For the spare wicks. I've been meaning to inventory them." A lie, and she saw him register it as a lie, and she pressed on before courage could fail her. "There's a letter there. From Kristiania. From advocates acting for the Voss family."

The lamp room was very quiet. Below them the sea worked at the rocks with its endless patient violence, and above them the great lens stood still in its cage, holding the afternoon light and breaking it into nothing, for it was not lit yet, it was only glass, only potential, only a promise that darkness would be answered.

"Give it to me," Felix said. His voice was level. Too level. She had heard that tone once before, the winter the fishing boat *Søster Anne* went down with all hands and a mainland official had come to ask Felix whether he'd seen distress rockets that night. Level as a frozen lake, and just as full of things beneath it.

"You knew," Signe said. "You've known since before the census woman came. You've known for—" she stopped, recalculated, and the recalculation was its own horror. "How long, Papa? How long have you been hiding letters about my mother in with the lamp oil?"

"Signe—"

"*Formerly of Bergsund,*" she read aloud, from memory, because she had it by heart now and would have it by heart until she died. "*Whose maiden name may interest you more than ours.*" She watched his face as she said it, watched for the flinch, the denial, the explanation — anything. What she got instead was worse than any of them. She got stillness. The stillness of a man watching a flood reach a door he has sandbagged for twenty years and knowing the sandbags were never going to hold forever.

"Marta Lindqvist," Signe said. "That's what the hymnal says. That's what I've said my whole life when anyone asked. Marta Lindqvist, from — from somewhere, from nowhere, from a parish nobody could find. And there is no such person, is there. There never was."

"Sit down," Felix said quietly.

"I will not sit down."

"Then stand," he said, "and hear me. But hear me, Signe. Whatever you believe you've found in that letter, you have found the edge of it only. You don't know what's underneath, and what's underneath is not yours to carry."

"Not mine to—" The words caught in her throat and came out cracked. "My mother's name is not mine? Her history is not mine? Papa, I am *made* of her. Every story I have about myself goes through her — my eyes are hers, my hands are hers, the way I hum when I'm working is hers — and you're telling me the one true thing about her belongs to you?"

"It belongs to no one," Felix said. "That is the whole point of it. That is what she asked of me with her dying breath, and I have kept it through nine winters, and I would keep it through ninety, and if you love me—"

"If I love you?" Something in Signe went very cold and very clear, the way the air goes before the barometer falls. "Is that what we do now, in this family? Is that how the silences get kept — by calling them love? Because I've been thinking about it, Papa. Downstairs, all these years. Why we never talk about Mamma's childhood. Why we never visit her sisters — and she had none, did she, because they were invented, because the whole woman arrived on this rock fully formed at nineteen like something the sea gave us. Why Anders goes quiet when Bergsund comes up. Why the census woman's pen stopped moving when she asked about lodgers, and why you lied to her so smoothly — so *smoothly*, Papa, like a man who has had practice."

"Signe." Her name in his mouth sounded like a warning bell.

"She was a Voss," Signe said. "Wasn't she. My mother was a Voss of Bergsund. The family whose house burned. The family whose stable hand they hanged."

The words fell into the lamp room and lay there, and neither of them moved to pick them up.

Felix Eriksen turned away from her. He walked to the window of the lamp room — the eastern pane, the one that faced the mainland, the one he cleaned most often, she realized, the one he looked through most often — and he stood with his back straight and his hands clasped behind him in the posture he used at funerals, and he said nothing at all.

And that nothing was the answer. That nothing was the confession. Twenty years of her life rearranged themselves around it in the space of ten seconds: the hymnal, the vanished relatives, the ferry-crossing beginning of her mother's story, the locked room at the end of the passage that ticked at night like a second heart. Her mother had not merely been a Voss. Her mother had been a Voss who ran. A Voss who shed her name like a burning garment and married a lighthouse keeper to disappear, and had raised her daughter inside the disappearance, and had made her husband swear to maintain it past the grave.

"I used to think," Signe said, and her voice frightened her, it was so quiet, "that we were poor but honest. That we had nothing but we had the truth of each other. That was the story. That was the story you told me my whole life — that a keeper's word is worth more than another man's fortune." She laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, jagged, unrecognizable. "And all the while we had a fortune's worth of secrets stacked in the oil locker next to the spare wicks."

"There is a page," Felix said, still facing the window, still in that funeral voice, "with writing on it. In the machine. If it leaves this island, people will act on it. Men who are owed things. Men who are afraid of things. Your mother spent her last years teaching me exactly how much ruin a single sheet of paper can carry, and I promised her—"

"Stop." Signe pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "Stop talking about her like she's still here issuing orders. She's dead, Papa. She's been dead nine years, and you've been obeying her ghost instead of raising your actual living daughter, and I am so tired — I am so *tired* — of being protected. Do you know what protection looks like from inside it? It looks like a wall. It looks like a father polishing brass while his daughter stands in the doorway learning that she cannot trust the ground floor of her own life."

When she took her hands from her eyes, Felix had turned around. He looked old. She had never seen him look old — not through the storms, not through the funeral, not through any of the winters — but he looked old now, standing in the cold dome of the lamp room with the gray sea light falling on him, an aging man in a tower, guarding a machine full of ghosts.

"I was going to tell you," he said. "When the season ended. When the icebreaker came and everyone was safe. I was going to choose the moment."

"The moment," Signe repeated. "Of course. The moment. There's always a moment, isn't there? There was a moment for telling the census woman the truth and it passed. There was a moment for telling me when I was nine and asked about mountains, and it passed. There will be a moment after this one, and after that one." She picked her way toward the stairs, backward, unwilling to turn her back on him — unwilling, even now, even with the wound wide open, to give him the small final injury of her departing face. "You keep lamps, Papa. You've always kept lamps. But you keep lies the same way — trimmed, fed, turned on every night so nobody notices the dark you're really living in."

"Signe—"

"And one day," she said, hand on the rail, "one of them is going to burn down the tower. And I want you to remember, when it happens, that I asked. That I stood right here and asked, and offered to carry it with you. That's the difference between us. I would have carried it *with* you."

She went down the stairs. On the third tread — the one that wept — her boot slipped a half-inch on the damp iron, and she caught herself, and kept going, and did not look back.

Behind her, above her, in the cold glass room at the top of the world, her father stood unmoving beside the great dark lens, and the light stayed unlit, and the afternoon went on failing toward evening over the water where the ice was already forming, silent, patient, closing the channel one crystal at a time.

Down in the passage below, behind its locked door, the machine waited with its page still in the carriage — and somewhere beyond the freezing strait, on the mainland, men who signed their letters *other instruments exist* were already counting the days.
