## 22. Daughter Against Father

**Felix**

She came down the stairs like weather.

He heard her before he saw her — the particular tread she had inherited from Marta, that deliberate heel-first strike that meant whatever was coming had been rehearsed. Felix kept his hands on the oil cloth, polishing the lamp's brass collar for the third time though it already shone. There were only so many things in a lighthouse a man could pretend to be doing.

Signe stopped at the foot of the stairs. She had put on her good coat. That detail landed in him like a splinter — she had dressed for this, the way one dressed for church or for a funeral or for leaving.

"Say it," she said.

"Say what?"

"You know what." Her voice was steady. Too steady. He knew that steadiness too; it was the sound of a person holding something heavy very still so it would not spill. "The Bible they pulled from the wrack. The name in it. Ida told me what Ada Lindqvist said when she thought no one was listening. *Marta Eriksen, née Voss.*"

The oil cloth went still under his hands. Outside, the wind worried at the gallery rail with its long grey fingers, and somewhere below the point the ice groaned like an old ship settling.

"Signe—"

"Don't." She crossed the room and stood over him, and for a moment he saw not his daughter but Marta standing exactly there, twenty-two years ago, in a wedding dress bought secondhand from a woman in Skarvik who asked no questions. "Don't do the thing where you look at me kindly and tell me nothing. Don't offer me tea. Don't trim a wick. I have watched you do it my whole life, Papa. Every time a name came too close to the fire you'd pick up some tool and begin working."

Felix set the cloth down. Folded it. Laid it on the bench with the care of a man laying down a weapon he did not trust himself to hold.

"What is it you want me to say?"

"I want you to say whether my mother was a Voss." Her eyes were wet but her jaw was iron — Marta again, always Marta. "I want you to say whether every story I've ever been told about her — the miller's daughter from the inland farms, the girl who loved hymns — whether any of it was true. I want you to say why a census taker asks questions about our house and you lie through your teeth like breathing. And I want you to say it now, because in two days either I'm on that icebreaker or I'm not, and I will not cross that ice carrying a name I don't know the weight of."

The stove ticked. The clock in the passage — Marta's clock, wound every Sunday of his widowed life — counted out the seconds with its small dry patience. Felix looked at his daughter and understood that he had come, without noticing, to the hour he had dreaded for twenty years: the hour when keeping silence would cost more than breaking it.

And still his mouth made the old motions.

"Sit down," he said. "I'll make tea."

Something went out of her face. Not anger — worse than anger. A kind of grief arriving early, the way light dies before a storm.

"There it is," she said quietly. "There it is, right on schedule. Tea."

* * *

**Signe**

He rose and took down the tin and the kettle as if the whole world were ordinary, as if she had asked about coal deliveries, and Signe felt something in her chest tear along a seam she hadn't known was there.

She had spent her childhood reading her father the way sailors read the horizon. She knew the set of his shoulders meant weather three days off. She knew the silence he wore on the anniversary of her mother's death was different from the silence he wore when Anders came asking for money, different again from the silence of the locked room at the end of the east passage, the room she had been told since she could walk was *damp, keep out*. She had built her whole understanding of love on those readings. Love, in this house, meant knowing which silences not to question.

She was done with it.

"No," she said. "No tea. No oil cloth. No trimming the lamp. I am twenty-three years old, Papa, and I found out yesterday — from Ada Lindqvist, from a stranger — that my mother's family name is written in a Bible the sea gave up like evidence at a trial. A Voss. Do you know what the men at the ferry landing say about the Vosses? Do you know what they say burned at that estate? A man is *in prison* for that fire. Has been for twenty years. And my mother was—" Her voice cracked and she hated it, hated the crack, pushed through it. "Was one of them. Whatever that means. And you've known since before I could walk, and you let me grow up on a story."

Felix set the kettle back on its hook. He turned around slowly, and when she looked at his face she saw something she had never seen there before: not the keeper's calm, not the father's warmth, but exhaustion — a bone-deep, salt-cured weariness, the face of a man who has been holding a door shut against a sea for two decades and has just heard the hinges go.

"You're right," he said.

The words stopped her like a slap. She had armed herself for denial. She had speeches prepared for denial, arguments, accusations stacked like cordwood. She had not prepared for surrender.

"I lied," Felix said. "Every word of the story I gave you. Your mother's name was Marta Voss when I married her. She came to this island in the autumn of 1871 with a suitcase, a scar on her left wrist, and a fear of closed carriages that never left her. I knew within a month that she was running from something. I didn't ask what. I made her a promise instead — that whatever she was running from, this island would stand between her and it as long as I drew breath." His voice dropped low, roughened. "That promise cost me nothing for twenty years, because the world let us alone. It isn't letting us alone anymore. And now everything I kept quiet to protect her memory is going to be dragged into the light by men with warrants, and I am trying — God help me, Signe, I am trying — to decide how much of it reaches you before they make it public."

"So don't decide," she said. "Tell me all of it. Tell me yourself."

"It isn't mine to tell."

"Whose, then? Hers? She's dead, Papa. She's eleven years dead and she left you holding every secret in this family like a man left holding a hot iron, and you just — carry it. You carry it and you hand me tea."

"The machine," Felix said.

The words fell into the room and lay there.

Signe frowned. "What machine?"

Her father looked at her for a long moment, and she watched him weigh something — watch him stand at the scale with his heart in one pan and his promise in the other, the way he must have stood a hundred times in her lifetime without her ever seeing it. Then he shook his head, once, slow.

"Not yet. Not tonight."

"Papa—"

"*Not tonight.*" It was not shouted, but it filled the room the way the foghorn fills a fog. Then, gentler, terrible in its gentleness: "You want the truth. I understand that. But truth in this family isn't a thing you ask for and receive, like bread. It's a thing that gets people taken away in boats. You are my daughter and I love you more than the lamp, more than the island, more than my own soul — and because of that, the first thing I will always reach for is the thing that keeps you safe. Even if it's a lie. *Especially* if it's a lie."

"That's not protection," Signe said, and her voice broke clean through now and she let it. "That's just another wall. You've built them so long you can't tell the difference anymore. You sit up here in your tower with your great light sweeping the water, telling yourself you guard people. But you know what you actually guard? Darkness. You keep lamps burning so nobody sees what's inside the house. You're not a keeper of lights, Papa. You're a keeper of lamps and lies."

The words hung between them, smoking.

She saw them land. Saw them go into him below the ribs the way she'd intended, and saw — and this was the worst part, the part that would keep her awake for years — saw that part of him *agreed*. Something behind his eyes nodded, weary and old, even as his jaw set against it.

* * *

**Felix**

*A keeper of lamps and lies.*

He turned it over in himself after she stormed up the stairs, after her door shut with the sound of a verdict. He stood at the window where the great lens threw its beam out across the black water, around and around, faithful as a heartbeat, and he let the sentence cut.

Because it was true. That was the wound of it. He had told himself for twenty years that silence was shelter, that the locked room was a vault, that the false name on the marriage register was a kindness done to a frightened woman. All of it true, and none of it the whole truth. The other half was simpler and uglier: he had liked the quiet. He had liked a life with no questions in it. Marta had brought her war to his island and he had buried it under routine — wick, glass, oil, log — until burying became the work itself, until he was less a keeper of the light than a keeper of the grave dug on top of it.

And now his daughter stood in the wreckage of that grave demanding to know what he had interred, and his every instinct was to build another wall between her and it, because the walls were all he knew how to build.

He climbed the tower. It was not yet time to trim the wick, but the climbing was the only prayer he had left, and the lamp room was the only altar.

From up here the strait showed its new teeth — pans of ice riding down from the north, knitting together at the edges, the river closing its ledger page by page. Two days. Maybe three, if the warm wind held. The icebreaker would come once, take its passengers, and then the island would be a world unto itself until April, sealed like the room at the end of the east passage.

He thought about the berth. One left, after tonight's arithmetic — Signe entitled to hers by years of saved coin and conservatory letters, Ida entitled to nothing except that she had offered, this morning, with those flat grey eyes, to give it up. And himself entitled to nothing at all, which was a kind of freedom.

But beneath the arithmetic ran the other current, the dark one: Rehn on the mainland with his warrant, Anders gone over the ice with whatever he'd sold, and the typewriter sitting in its locked room with a dead woman's confession still gripped in its carriage like a hand refusing to let go.

Signe wanted the truth. His daughter wanted the truth, and he had handed her tea.

He pressed his palm flat against the cold brass of the lens housing and spoke to the glass, to the dark, to the woman eleven years drowned in him.

"You told me never to let it leave the island," he said. "You made me swear it with your fever-hands around mine. But you also told me once — that first winter, when you finally slept without waking — you said, *the truth is the only inheritance I have worth giving.* I chose which half to hear. Twenty years I chose."

The beam swept out, swept back. Down the strait, far off, the ice groaned and shifted, and somewhere beyond it the mainland waited with its warrants and its courts and its appetite.

Below him, faintly, he heard a door open. Soft footsteps in the passage. Not Signe's tread — lighter, hesitant. Ida, moving through the dark house toward the east passage, toward the locked room, drawn perhaps by the same tide that pulled at everyone in this house now that the sea itself had started testifying.

Felix's blood went cold.

He took the stairs two at a time.
