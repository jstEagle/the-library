## 23. Ida's Choice

Ida counted her things twice that morning, though there was hardly anything to count. A comb with two teeth missing. A hair ribbon gone the color of weak tea. A bundle of paper scraps covered in letters she had copied from signs and labels and the spines of books, because scraps were what she had been given and scraps were what she had learned on.

She kept them in a biscuit tin under her cot, and when she opened the lid now, the smell of old shortbread rose up out of them like a ghost of some kitchen she had never cooked in.

*Least entitled.* The phrase was not hers — she had heard it from Anders, back before he left, said carelessly over supper about some mainland cousin who expected a share of nothing. But it had lodged in her the way a splinter lodges, working deeper every time she touched it. She was fourteen years old and she had been practicing the phrase in her head for three days now, saying it over and over like a psalm, until it stopped sounding like despair and started sounding like arithmetic.

Signe had the conservatory. Signe had the promise Felix had made to his daughter when she was nine years old, standing at this same window while the autumn gulls wheeled past — *you'll study music in Christiania, little bird, I swear it on the lamp itself.* Signe had a mother buried under a stone with the wrong name on it, which meant at least she had a mother somewhere in the ground. Signe had a father who looked at her across the supper table as if she were the last lit window in a dark country.

And Ida had a tin of scraps, a ribbon, and no one whose promise she was.

She put the lid on the tin and did not cry. Crying was a luxury of people with places to be mourned from.

* * *

The house had changed since Anders went across on the ferry without asking anyone's leave. It had changed the way a room changes after a lamp is carried out of it — same furniture, same walls, but all the shadows rearranged and none of them friendly.

Felix barely ate. He sat at the head of the table with his hands flat on the wood, staring at the middle distance where the sea would be if the walls were glass, and when Signe spoke to him he answered a beat too late, as though translating from a language only he could hear. Two berths left on the icebreaker. Three souls in the house who needed one. Ida had done the sum so many times it had worn a groove in her, the way the stairs to the lamp room were worn in the centers by twenty years of Felix's boots.

Two berths. Three souls. And everyone pretending not to count.

That pretense, Ida understood, was the cruelest part. Signe sat by the fire working her fingering exercises on a silent keyboard drawn in charcoal on a plank, and every few minutes her eyes flicked up to her father and away again, quick as a bird drinking. Felix watched the fire and did not watch Signe watching him. And Ida knitted — or held knitting, rather, the needles clicking out their small faithful sound while her mind ran its cold arithmetic underneath.

If she stayed, Signe could go. If she stayed, Felix could go, and Felix should go; he was sixty-one years old and his lungs had rattled ever since the night of the great storm, when he'd stood bare-handed in the shattered lamp room holding the light together while the wind tried to peel his skin off. She had seen the burns on his palms heal slow and angry. She had heard him coughing through the wall at night, that deep wet sound that men hide even from themselves.

If she stayed, both Eriksens lived their proper lives, and Ida Voss — no. Ida Lindqvist. Ida nobody. Ida stayed behind with the typewriter and the secret and the winter, and maybe the magistrate came for her and maybe he didn't, and either way the story ended the way stories about girls like her always ended: quietly, offstage, in a sentence someone else got to write.

She was not brave. She wanted to say that clearly, at least inside her own skull, where honesty cost nothing. She was terrified. When she thought about staying — about the ice closing over the channel and the island going dark around her like a swallowed star — her stomach turned over and her hands went cold on the needles. She wanted desperately to be saved. Every night since the berth was announced she lay awake wanting it, wanting it the way the drowning want air, and then hating herself for wanting it, because want was all she seemed to have and it took up too much room.

So she would spend the wanting. That was the trade. You gave up the thing you wanted most and bought something better with it: Signe's future, Felix's safety, a clean conscience in a house that had never once let her forget she was a guest in it — kindly treated, warmly fed, a guest nonetheless.

The needles clicked. The fire settled. Somewhere upstairs behind the locked door, the typewriter sat in its dark like a heart in a chest, ticking with other people's secrets.

Ida set down the knitting, folded her hands, and waited for the right moment the way she had learned to wait for everything — patiently, invisibly, certain it would come to someone else first.

* * *

It came two evenings later, in the blue hour between lamplighting and supper, when Felix came down from the light with frost in his beard and found Signe at the table with the charcoal keyboard and the last of the oil money laid out in little stacks, doing her sums aloud.

"Enough," Signe was saying, "for the coach fare from the ferry landing, and the first term's lodging if I take the attic room, and — Papa, sit down, I've worked it six times—"

"I see it." Felix sat. His voice had that flat carefulness he used when feeling pressed up against the surface of him like water against ice, thin and bright and liable to crack. "I see your sums, little bird."

"Then you'll tell me plainly. Am I going?"

Felix was quiet long enough that Ida, in the doorway with the bread knife still in her hand, felt the silence enter the room like a third person and sit down.

"You're going," Felix said at last. "One way or another, Signe. On the breaker or — I'll find a way. I swear it."

"But the berths—" Signe began, and then stopped herself, and Ida saw her do it: saw her glance at the doorway, at Ida, and close her mouth on the arithmetic like a well-brought-up girl. Like someone who knew, in her bones, that the sum included a person standing right there listening.

Ida had loved Signe for seven years. Loved her fierce and quick and generous, loved her the way you love weather. But love did not stop her hearing what Signe hadn't said: *the berths don't add up unless someone is subtracted.*

And there was only one person in the room whom subtraction wouldn't wound.

She came into the kitchen properly then, set down the knife, and stood at the end of the table with her hands folded, the way she'd practiced. She had rehearsed this speech forty times against the wall of the woodshed, where she went to copy her letters in chalk dust and no one watched. It had sounded steady every time. Her voice, when it came out, was not steady at all, but it came out.

"I'll stay."

Felix turned his head slowly, like a man hearing a floorboard creak in an empty house.

"The two berths are for you and Signe," Ida said. "That's how it works. That's how it should work. Signe has her music and you have — you have to get the machine across, don't you?" She saw him flinch and pressed on before courage failed her. "You can't leave it here. Whatever's in it, whoever it convicts or clears, you have to carry it yourself. I've thought about it and thought about it, and I'm the one it costs least. Signe loses everything if she stays. You lose everything if you stay. I lose—" 

She stopped.

*I lose nothing,* was what the speech said. She had written it that way in chalk dust, forty times, steady as scripture. But standing at the table, looking at Felix's face — looking at the terrible dawning thing in it — she found she couldn't lie in front of him. Not to him. He was the only person alive who had never lied to her, not even kindly, not even once.

"I lose more than nothing," she said quietly. "But less than they do. And I've had practice at losing. That's all I mean to say. I'm not asking to be talked out of it. I'm asking you to let me do one useful thing with my life, and this is it."

The stove ticked. Out in the strait, the ice groaned — a long low sound like a door opening underwater, the sound the whole island had learned to live inside these past weeks.

Felix rose from his chair. He didn't speak. He crossed the room and stood in front of her, this enormous man she had feared a little and adored entirely since the night seven years ago when he'd carried her off a wrecked boat wrapped in his coat, and he looked down at her with an expression she had no name for, because she had never seen it on any face before hers.

It was grief. It was shame. It was a man seeing himself reflected in a child's self-erasure and finding the reflection monstrous.

"Who told you that?" he asked. His voice came out rough. "Who taught you that you were worth least? Because it wasn't me, girl, and I will find whoever it was and I will—"

"No one taught me anything," Ida said. "It's just true. You keep a lamp, Mr. Eriksen. You know about light. You put the brightest beam where it saves the most ships. That's not cruel. That's just keeping."

"That is *arithmetic,*" Felix said, "and you are not a number." His hand came up — she thought for a moment he might strike the table, or weep, or both — and instead it settled on her shoulder, heavy and trembling slightly, the burned palm against her collarbone. "Listen to me. Ida. Look at me."

She looked at him. His eyes were wet and furious and full of something she had spent seven years not daring to call by its name, because calling it hope was how children got destroyed.

"When Marta died," Felix said, "she made me promise two things. Never let the machine leave the island. And never let this house make you into a servant of its silences. Do you hear me? She said — I have thought of her words every day for seven years, girl, I could recite them in my sleep — she said, *the orphan is not our charity, Felix. She is our witness. We give her everything we were too frightened to claim ourselves.*"

Ida's breath stopped somewhere in her chest and stayed there.

"She named you in her letters," Felix went on, softer now, the fury draining out of him and leaving something rawer. "Not as our ward. As our daughter. I have the page upstairs. I have never shown you because showing you felt like — like claiming a treasure I hadn't earned. But I earned nothing and neither did you, that's not how belonging works, it isn't *earned*, it's *given*, and it was given to you before you could walk. So no. You will not stay on this rock so that my conscience can travel warm to the mainland. I'd sooner sink the boat."

Behind them, Signe had gone very still at the table, the charcoal stick motionless above her silent keys.

"Papa," Signe said carefully, "if Ida doesn't stay, then someone does. That's just — that's just true, isn't it? Two berths. Three—"

"Four," Felix said. "Count four. There's always been four of us, and the man who counts three has already decided who doesn't count, and I have spent twenty years watching what happens to families who decide that." He looked from his daughter to Ida and back, and Ida saw the decision arrive in his face like weather — not chosen so much as landed, inevitable, the way storms land on this coast. "No one stays. No one is subtracted. I don't yet know how, but I will find the how, because the alternative is a sum I refuse to do."

He picked up his coat from the peg.

"Where are you going?" Signe asked.

"To talk to Halvard Rehn," Felix said. "Tonight, before I lose my nerve. There's a page in that room upstairs that ends this — all of it, the berths, the warrant, twenty years of silence — and I am done letting a dead man's crime decide what my family gets to be." He paused at the door, hand on the latch, and looked back at Ida one more time. "Marta chose you, girl. Start believing it. That's an order from your keeper."

The door shut behind him. The stove ticked. The ice groaned out in the dark like something vast turning over in its sleep.

Ida stood very still, one hand rising slowly to her collarbone where his palm had been, feeling the warmth of it fade and refusing to let it.

Upstairs, behind the locked door, the typewriter waited — and for the first time in her life, Ida understood that it had been waiting for her too.
