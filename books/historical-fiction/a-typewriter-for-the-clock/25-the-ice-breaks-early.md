## 25. The Ice Breaks Early

The sound woke him before the wind did — a long groan from the river, deep as a whale's mourning, followed by a crack that ran through the bedrock of the island and up through the legs of his cot like a struck bell.

Felix Eriksen was on his feet before he understood why, one hand already reaching for the oilskin by the door, twenty years of habit moving him faster than thought. The window was rimed with frost, but he scraped a circle clear with his thumbnail and looked out at a world gone wrong.

The river, which had been ironing itself flat with cold for three weeks — which had been building its white bridge to the mainland plank by patient plank, promising them all a season of sealed silence in which nothing would have to be decided — had broken open.

He stood there a long time, watching.

The ice sheet that had stretched from the island's northern shingle nearly to the far channel was heaving like something alive. Great plates of it tilted upward, catching the moonlight on their raw undersides, and where they split the water showed through as black seams, widening even as he watched. A pressure ridge was forming out near the old pilings, shards grinding against shards with a sound like teeth. The whole surface breathed and buckled, and beneath it the current ran loud and free for the first time since Advent.

A warm front. It had to be. He could feel it now that he listened for it — the wind had swung around to the southwest sometime in the night, and it carried a smell the winter had banished weeks ago: wet earth, open water, rot. The kind of thaw that came once or twice a season, false spring, a lie the sky told before committing to true cold again.

Except this year the lie had teeth.

Because the icebreaker — the *Valkyrie*, out of Havnø — was scheduled to make her last crossing on Thursday, when the channel should have been frozen hard enough that no boat but an ice-strengthened hull could push through, and after Thursday the river would close like a book until April.

Thursday. Two days off.

And if the ice broke early, the *Valkyrie* would not wait for Thursday. No captain alive burned coal cracking through pack ice when he didn't have to. She'd come tomorrow. Maybe tonight.

Felix pressed his forehead against the cold glass and did the arithmetic he had been refusing to do since the notice had gone up in the ferry shed: four souls in his household. Three berths on the ship. One typewriter that had to reach the mainland or Marta's name would rot in a locked room forever, buried with the man who had taken the rope for a crime she never committed and another man had committed entirely.

Five seats needed. Four existed.

And now the clock, which had been ticking with a certain civilized patience, had begun to run.

* * *

He climbed the tower before dawn because the lamp demanded it and because his hands needed work they could understand. The wick wanted trimming; the glass wanted cleaning; the mechanism wanted oil. These were problems with solutions. He performed each one slowly, gratefully, while below him the river continued its terrible conversation with itself.

From the gallery he could see the whole strait. In the gray half-light the broken ice looked like a map of some ruined country — floes drifting south with the current in loose flotillas, grinding past the point, piling against the headland in ridges taller than a man. Open water everywhere. Black lanes between white islands. Any fisherman fool enough to try walking across now would go under inside a minute, and the water was cold enough that a minute was all the argument a body would ever get.

The crossing was closing not with a freeze but with a flood. It was the cruelest possible version. If the ice had thickened, the *Valkyrie*'s captain might have delayed, waited for better conditions, given them a day's grace. But with the channel breaking up, every hour of delay meant thicker drift, harder going, more risk to his hull and his insurance. He would come early and leave early, and he would take exactly the passengers who stood on his deck when he cast off.

Ada Lindqvist had left two days ago, taking the last ferry before the freeze that wasn't a freeze. She'd shaken his hand at the landing, held it perhaps a half-second longer than a census taker needed to, and said, "Keep well, Keeper Eriksen. I hope the winter is kind to you." And then she'd looked at the house, just briefly, just at the shuttered eastern window of the locked room, and Felix had known that whatever she'd found in his ledger — whatever inked-out name she'd spent three days turning over in her careful mind — she was carrying it south with her like contraband.

He wondered sometimes, in the hours when the lamp hummed and his thoughts were allowed to run loose, whether Ada Lindqvist had been sent or whether she had simply arrived. Whether the state moved with intention or merely with momentum, the way ice moved, the way weather moved — vast, indifferent, crushing whatever stood in the channel without malice and without mercy.

It didn't matter now. What mattered was that Halvard Rehn had a warrant, that Anders had sold his brother for the price of his debts, and that somewhere on the mainland a young magistrate was reading an old file with new eyes and finding in it exactly the shape of a machine that typed in a particular hand.

Felix finished trimming the wick and stood a moment with his palm flat against the lens glass, feeling the faint warmth of the flame on the other side.

"Marta," he said quietly. "The river's breaking."

Somewhere below, the floorboards creaked — Signe, up early too, or Ida. The house was full of people who loved him and whom he loved, and he had managed to build the whole arrangement on top of a secret the way you build a house over a grave, and now the ground was shifting under all of them at once.

Two days had become less than one. And he still hadn't chosen.

* * *

Signe found him at the kitchen table afterward, staring at a chart of the river he'd unrolled without quite knowing why. She was nineteen and moved through rooms the way her mother had — quickly, certainly, with her chin slightly lifted as though listening to music no one else could hear. This morning there was frost in her hair where she'd run down from the loft without her cap, and her eyes were bright with the news everyone on the island would be shouting before noon.

"Papa. The ice." She pulled off her gloves finger by finger. "Old Søren says the *Valkyrie* will signal from the point by nightfall. That they'll sail on the morning tide instead of Thursday." She sat down across from him, and he watched her watch him, watched her read his face the way she'd learned to read weather. "Is it true?"

"It's true."

"Then we go tomorrow." She said it simply, and the simplicity was the wound of it — she had no idea what the sentence cost. "I've been packing for a week. My letters from the conservatory are in order, my references — Papa, I'm ready. I've been ready for years."

"I know you have." His voice came out steady. He'd had practice. "There are things about the passage we need to speak of first. All of us. Tonight, after supper."

Her brow creased. "What things?"

"The berths," he said. "How many there are. Who goes."

She laughed — actually laughed, a short bright note — and began gathering the chart's curling edge to smooth it for him, the way she fussed and organized everything, the way Marta had. "Papa, don't be grim. It's arithmetic, isn't it? You taught me arithmetic on this very table. Four of us, three berths — so you stay, obviously. Someone has to keep the light until spring, and you're the keeper, it's your post, and Ida can come with me and Anders—" she stopped. Recounted. He saw her recount, saw the numbers land behind her eyes one by one like stones dropped down a well. Four souls plus himself, he'd written in his own mind, but aloud, in her counting, it came out different: Signe, Ida, Anders, himself — and the light needing a keeper, and only three seats, and her father already assuming, already arranging himself around the gap the way he arranged everything around duty.

"Papa." All the brightness had gone out of her voice. "You're not staying. You can't—"

"We'll speak of it tonight," he said. "All together. There's no use borrowing trouble before breakfast."

But she didn't laugh again, and she didn't smooth the chart, and when she left the kitchen she left the door open behind her, which Signe never did, and Felix sat alone with the map of the breaking river and understood that his daughter had just done the same arithmetic he had, and arrived at the same terrible sum, and hated it.

* * *

Midmorning, he walked the shore path north to where the pressure ridge had built itself against the headland. The ice there stood in jagged ramparts ten feet high, blue-shadowed, glittering, and beyond it the open water ran black and fast toward the sea. Gulls wheeled over the leads screaming their harsh news. Out on the largest floe, hauled up like something prehistoric, lay a dead seal — drowned or crushed, its dark body riding the tilting ice as the current carried it past the point and out of the world.

Ida was there before him.

She stood at the edge of the ridge with her arms wrapped around herself, no coat over her dress, her breath smoking, watching the floes go by with an expression he recognized and dreaded: the look of a child watching a door close on a room she has never been allowed to enter. She was fourteen. She had come to them six years ago off a boat from the south with a bundle, a cough, and a name nobody wanted, and she had grown up in the spaces between other people's silences, learning letters from scraps, learning love the way you learn a language late — carefully, with an accent, never quite believing you're pronouncing it right.

"You shouldn't be out here without your coat," Felix said.

"The whole island is talking." She didn't turn. "The ship comes tomorrow. Signe's packing. Anders is down at the tavern telling anyone who'll listen he'll be first aboard." A pause. The ice groaned somewhere out in the channel, long and low. "That leaves you and me, doesn't it. For one berth."

Felix felt the sentence go into him like a splinter, deep, past the quick.

"Ida—"

"It's all right." Still not turning. Her voice was level in the way that only children and soldiers manage, a levelness achieved by pressing down hard on something underneath. "I knew it would come to this. I've known since the notice went up. I'm not — I don't have anywhere on the mainland, do I. No conservatory. No debts. Nobody waiting. I'm the one it makes sense for."

"Look at me."

She turned. Her face was pale and set and terribly adult, and her eyes were red-rimmed in a way that told him she had done her crying in private, already, in advance, the way she did everything — preparing her losses ahead of schedule so they wouldn't inconvenience anyone.

"If you say one more word like that," Felix said, "I'll want you to remember that I heard it. And I want you to know that no berth on any ship belongs to anyone yet. Not to Signe. Not to Anders. Not to you. Do you hear me? Nothing is decided."

"But it is decided," Ida said softly. "You decided it the moment you started doing sums instead of sleeping. I see the lamp burning upstairs at two and three in the morning, Uncle Felix. I hear you walking." She pulled her thin arms tighter around herself. "Whatever's in the locked room — whatever you're going to do about it — you should do it soon. Because the ice isn't going to wait for you to be brave."

Then she walked back down the path toward the house, small and straight-backed against the wind, and Felix stood alone above the grinding channel and let her words settle into him like snow into a footprint.

* * *

He went to the tavern at dusk, which he almost never did, because there was a man he needed to see and a thing he needed to confirm before the night took its final shape. The taproom was hot and loud with the news, fishermen gesturing with their mugs, retelling the ice report to each other in escalating versions. Anders was at the corner table where he always sat, and he was drunk in the specific way he got drunk these days — not merry, not maudlin, but busy, drinking the way a man works, methodically, to keep ahead of something chasing him.

Felix sat down across from him without asking.

"Felix." Anders blinked at him, swayed, steadied. Up close his brother looked worse than he had in weeks — the skin of his face gone gray, the tremor in his hands visible even through the mug. "Have you heard? Ship's coming early. Tomorrow. We'll drink to it—"

"Anders. Look at me." He leaned in, lowered his voice beneath the din. "Did Rehn give you a date? When he means to come for the machine. Did he tell you when?"

The drunkness fell away from his brother's face all at once, like a cloth pulled off a table. Underneath was fear, plain and naked.

"He didn't say he was coming here," Anders whispered. "He said — he said once the river opened, once the evidence could be moved safely, there'd be a party of men on the first available crossing. He said the warrant covered seizure at the point of origin." His throat worked. "Felix, I never thought — I only meant the debts, I only meant to get them off my back, I never thought they'd send men to the house—"

"When the river opened," Felix repeated. He looked out the fogged window, at the dark where the channel was, where the black water ran. The river had opened today. Ahead of schedule. Everything ahead of schedule. "So it may be tomorrow. On the same tide as the *Valkyrie*. They could stand on the same deck."

"I can warn you—"

"You've warned me plenty." Felix stood, and his brother flinched as if the movement itself were an accusation, and maybe it was. "Go home, Anders. Sleep it off. Be at the house in the morning. Whatever else you are, you're family, and family boards that ship together or not at all."

He walked home along the cliff road with the southwest wind pushing at his back like a hand between his shoulder blades, hurrying him, and below him the river ran black and broken under the stars, carrying its ice south, opening its channel wider by the hour, making everything possible and therefore everything urgent.

* * *

The household met around the stove after supper, and Felix laid it out plainly because there was no longer time for anything else. Three berths. Five claimants, counting the truth itself, which also needed passage. The ship at dawn or near it. Men with a warrant possibly on the same tide.

He watched their faces as he spoke: Signe rigid with the effort of not arguing, her conservatory dreams visibly colliding with the word *warrant*; Anders staring into the stove door, drunk again by evening, unable to meet anyone's eyes; Ida sitting very straight with her hands folded, wearing the expression of someone prepared to volunteer for sacrifice and disappointed not to have been asked yet.

"There is one more thing," Felix said, when the arithmetic had been argued to its exhausted end and no agreement had been reached, because no agreement could be reached, because the sum refused to come out even no matter how they divided themselves. He rose. "Before this ship sails, there's something in this house you all deserve to see. Something I've kept locked for twenty years. Some of you know what it is. None of you know what's written on it."

The room went absolutely still. Even the stove seemed to hold its fire quiet.

"Papa," Signe breathed.

"I'm going to open the room," Felix said. "Tonight. While the ice is still deciding what it wants to be. Because whatever happens at that landing tomorrow — whoever boards and whoever stays — no one in this family is going to face it on the strength of my silence anymore. Silence is what brought us here. It won't carry us out."

He crossed to the hall, took the ring of keys from the hook — the heavy iron key he had touched ten thousand times and turned perhaps fifty — and walked to the eastern door with three generations of his family watching him and the river booming and cracking outside like a countdown.

The lock turned easier than it had any right to, after twenty years.

Cold air breathed out of the dark room, smelling of paper and machine oil and time, and inside, on the desk beneath the boarded window, the typewriter sat waiting under its dust sheet like a guest who had never left — patient, faithful, loaded with the one page that could hang a memory or raise the dead.

Felix reached out and pulled the sheet away.

Out on the river, the ice gave a long, splitting cry, and somewhere far downstream, faint on the wind, came the first sound of a ship's whistle testing the channel.
