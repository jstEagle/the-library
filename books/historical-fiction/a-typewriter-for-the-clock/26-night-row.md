## 26. Night Row

The ice told him first.

Felix heard it from the gallery at half past nine—a sound like a giant knuckle cracking against the underside of the world, a long groan rolling up out of the strait where the river met the sea. He had kept this light through twenty-two winters and he knew every voice the cold could make: the whistle of wind through the ventilator balls, the tick of cooling iron in the lantern room, the soft collapse of snow sliding off the storehouse roof. But that groan was new. That was the ice going rotten all at once, the warm front from the south pressing its palm flat against two months of winter and feeling it give.

The icebreaker would sail in two days. Maybe less. Rehn's warrant sat folded in the magistrate's breast pocket like a knife in a sheath, and Anders's loose tongue had already shown him where to point it.

Felix went down the stairs without lighting a lamp. He did not need one. His body had learned these steps the way it had learned the shape of Marta's hand—by repetition, by devotion, in the dark.

* * *

The locked room smelled of dust and lamp oil and something fainter underneath, something he had never been able to name in twenty years of opening the door only when he must. Wax, maybe. Old paper. The ghost of a woman's handwriting.

He did not light a candle. He stood in the blackness and let his eyes find the shape he already knew: the small table beneath the east window, the machine sitting square upon it under its oilcloth shroud, patient as a headstone.

He had told himself all evening that he would not do this. He had told Signe at supper—her jaw still set from the quarrel they had not finished, her eyes red-rimmed and furious and so much her mother's that it hurt to look at them—that everything would be settled after the crossing. He had told Ida nothing, because Ida watched him with those wide gray eyes that saw too much for eleven years old, and lies slid off her like rain off glass. He had told himself the typewriter would go aboard the icebreaker in its crate, in daylight, properly, with papers and witnesses, the way an honest man surrendered evidence.

But honest men had let an innocent man hang for the Voss fire. Honest men had kept their mouths shut for twenty years while Marta died not knowing whether the world believed her. And Halvard Rehn was young, and young magistrates followed warrants the way water followed gravity, and if that machine came off the boat into his hands before anyone read what was still in its carriage—if it went into an evidence room, into a drawer, into twenty more years of silence—

No.

It would not go by daylight. It would not go by paper. It would go tonight, across the breaking ice, in a rowboat, in the dark, to the mainland shore where Ada Lindqvist lodged at the harbor inn—the one soul on either side of the water who had looked at him with something other than appetite or suspicion. Let her decide what conscience demanded. But the machine would be beyond Rehn's reach when the sun came up. That much Felix could still do. That much he owed.

He lifted the shroud.

The typewriter sat in the starlight that came grey through the frost-furred window, black iron and round keys, absurdly ordinary, like a sewing machine or a scale for weighing flour. Twenty years he had feared it. Twenty years it had sat in this room ticking its single held note—*the page, the page, the page*—and he had come to think of it as a heart he kept beating in a locked room because he could not bring himself to bury it and could not bear to let it speak.

He slid his hands under it. The iron was cold enough to burn.

"Marta," he said aloud, and his voice cracked like the ice outside. "I'm sorry. You told me never to let it leave the island."

The room said nothing. Rooms always said nothing. It had taken him most of a lifetime to understand that her promise had never been about the machine at all—it had been about Signe, about Ida's mother before her, about everyone who might be burned by the truth she carried. And now the truth was the only thing left that could save any of them.

"I have to break my word," he said, "to keep yours."

He wrapped the machine in the oilcloth, then in the old boat cloak, then lashed the whole bundle into a fish crate with tarred twine, working fast, his fingers stupid with cold and hurry. He carried it down through the house like a man carrying a sleeping child past a room full of light sleepers. Signe's door was shut; lamplight showed in the seam beneath it. She was awake. Of course she was awake. He paused there for the length of one breath, forehead nearly touching the wood, and did not knock.

There were things fathers owed daughters that could only be paid in person, and there were things that could only be paid by coming back alive.

* * *

The boathouse doors groaned open onto a night gone strange. The wind had fallen with the warm front, and the sky had cleared to a hard scatter of stars, and the whole strait lay under a skin of ice that was no longer ice but something between ice and water—grey-white, webbed with dark seams, breathing. As he dragged the dory down the rollers the sound came again, closer now, a vast slow tearing somewhere out toward the channel, and beneath it a hiss and trickle like the sea whispering to itself.

He loaded the crate amidships, wrapped in sacking. He took two oars, the boathook, the bailer. He took no lamp; a light on the water at night would be seen from the point, and Rehn had posted nothing yet, but Rehn did not need to post men to ruin him—only to wake early.

At the waterline he stopped.

The dory's bow pushed into a skim of young ice an inch thick, and the skim flexed under the hull with a sound like cracked crockery. Beyond it, forty yards out, lay the old floes—thick, ridged, blue-black in the starlight—and beyond the floes, the open lead where the current kept the channel clear, a ribbon of black water fifty yards wide, smoking faintly in the cold air like a road to hell paved with steam.

A man could cross that. Men had crossed worse. But men had also drowned in sight of their own chimneys, and the water was at the temperature where the sea stops being water and becomes a hand that closes.

Felix thought of Signe behind her door. Of Ida asleep with her practice letters under her pillow, the scraps of paper covered in careful pencil loops. Of Anders somewhere on the mainland spending his brother's secret like coin. Of Marta in the ground under the rowan tree, twenty years dead and still the bravest person he had ever known.

Then he stepped into the boat and shoved off.

* * *

The first hundred yards were the worst and the easiest both—young ice parting before the bow with a continuous brittle shriek, the oars biting through slush, every stroke a grinding argument. He pulled standing, braced, using his weight, and the cold came up through the boards into his boots and set its teeth in his ankles. Steam rose off his shoulders. His breath tore white rags out of his mouth and left them hanging in the still air behind him.

Halfway to the floes the hull struck something solid and lurched.

He froze. Listened. Water was coming in—he could hear it, that thin insidious chuckle at the bilge—and when he felt along the port garboard seam his fingers found a split, short but deep, weeping cold. Not fatal. Not yet. He got the bailer going with his right hand and rowed with his left, and the arithmetic of it filled his skull: bail faster than she leaks, row faster than the ice closes, reach the lead before his arms quit, cross the lead before the cold quit his heart.

Somewhere out in the darkness a floe the size of the church groaned and turned over, and the wave of it came rolling through the broken ice and lifted the dory and dropped her, and the crate shifted, and Felix's heart went with it.

"Hold," he told the crate, the way you'd tell a frightened animal. "Hold on."

The floes rose around him now on both sides like a ruined city—ridges and canyons, slabs tilted against slabs, pressure heaps where the winter had spent months crushing itself together and the warm front was now unmaking the work. He found a lane where the current ran, black water threaded between white ramparts, and shipped the oars and paddled with them doubled, steering with the sweep of his shoulders, the bailer sloshing against his knee. The stars overhead wheeled slowly, indifferent. The light on the point threw its long white arm across the water behind him, sweeping, sweeping, keeping faith with ships that were not there because he was the ship tonight, he was the fool on the water, and the lamp could not save him and he loved it anyway.

The lead was thirty feet wide. Then fifteen. The floes were closing.

He saw it happen ahead of him—two great pans drifting together on some deep impulse of tide, the gap narrowing with terrible gentleness, no violence in it at all, just arithmetic, just the sea doing sums. Ten feet. Six. If the channel shut with him inside it, the ice would take the dory the way a book takes a flower.

Felix did the thing he had been avoiding since the boathouse. He stood, put the boathook out ahead, and drove the dory into the closing gap with everything left in his back.

The bow went in. The pans touched the hull on both sides at once and began, with the patience of mountains, to squeeze. Wood creaked. The split seam sang. He got the boathook jammed vertical between the pans, a splint for the channel's broken bone, and hauled himself forward hand over hand, dragging the boat through the last six feet of black water while the ice closed behind the stern like a wound healing.

Then he was through. Open water ahead, and beyond it, dimly, the mainland shore—a smear of darker dark, a single yellow window that would be the harbor inn.

He bailed until his arm failed. He rowed until the other one argued. The cold had moved past pain into a kind of bright clarity, and he knew what that meant, and he made himself count strokes aloud, one to a hundred and back down again, because a man who stopped counting started thinking, and a man who started thinking about how good the water would feel was already drowned.

Somewhere in the nineties of one of his hundreds, the dory grounded.

* * *

He did not remember beaching her. He remembered the shock of gravel under the keel, and then he was on his hands and knees in the shallows with the surf breaking over his wrists, hauling the crate, and the water soaked him to the chest and he did not care because it was the last water he would have to argue with. He dragged the crate above the tideline. He dragged himself after it. He knelt there in the freezing wrack of kelp and rope-ends, steaming like a landed fish, and put his forehead against the sacking and laughed once, a single bark with nothing in it, and then was quiet.

Above him, the window burned yellow. Smoke stood straight up from the inn chimney in the windless air. Somewhere a dog spoke once and thought better of it.

Felix tried to stand and found his legs were rumors. He crawled instead, up the shingle, one yard and then another, dragging the crate, leaving a furrow in the frozen sand like something wounded returning to its den. At the top of the beach he collapsed against a beached dinghy's overturned hull, out of the wind that wasn't blowing, out of nothing, simply down, and lay there getting his breath back in great ragged pieces.

Twenty minutes, he told himself. Rest twenty minutes, then carry it to the inn, then knock, then—

"Keeper Eriksen."

The voice came out of the darkness to his left and stopped his heart colder than the strait had managed.

She stepped around the dinghy with a lantern held low, and the light came up over her: Ada Lindqvist, coat buttoned wrong, hair pinned in haste, a shawl thrown over census-taker's wool, and her eyes—her eyes went from his face to the crate to the furrow he had plowed up the beach and back to his face, and he watched her understand all of it in the space of three heartbeats, the way she had understood the inked-out name in his ledger, the way she understood everything, damn her, quietly and completely.

"The ice is going," she said. Not a question. Her breath hung white between them. "I heard it from my room. I've been walking the shore for an hour because I couldn't sleep, and I told myself it was the weather, and then—" She stopped. She was looking at the crate the way people look at coffins.

Felix gathered himself against the hull. Every joint had its own opinion and all of them were against him. "Miss Lindqvist."

"You could have died out there." Her voice shook, and he saw that it was anger wearing grief's coat. "The channel, the floes—in God's name, man, the *water*—"

"Yes," Felix said.

"And you brought it anyway." She knelt, unhurried, as though they were in a parlor, and laid her bare hand flat on the sacking over the crate, over the iron thing inside, and the lantern light trembled with her breath. "You rowed a dying boat through the ice at midnight to put this on my shore. Which means you trust me with it." Her eyes came up. "Or you've got no one else left."

Felix looked at her—this sharp, tired woman sent north to count souls who had ended up counting secrets instead—and found that he had nothing left to lie with. The storm had taken it. The cold had taken it. Twenty years of silence lay back there on the island, and here, on this freezing shingle, with the evidence of everything in a fish crate between them and the yellow window of the inn burning above, there was only the truth, waiting to be read.

"It wants reading," he said. "Before the magistrate finds it. Before anyone else does. Will you read it?"

Ada Lindqvist drew a long breath of the bitter air, and her hand did not move from the crate.

"Light your lantern," she said. "We'll read it now."
