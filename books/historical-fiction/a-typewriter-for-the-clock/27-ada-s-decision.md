## 27. Ada's Decision

Ada Lindqvist had been counting the hours the way other people counted money — carefully, and with a growing suspicion that there was less of it than there ought to be.

She stood at the mainland landing, where the river ice groaned like a ship in a storm that hadn't arrived yet. The warm front had come in overnight, an insult of mild air after weeks of killing cold, and now the whole channel was rotting from within. She could hear it: long cracks traveling under the surface, each one ending in a sound like a door being closed somewhere far away. The icebreaker *Vestfjord* sat at her mooring two hundred yards upriver, steam rising from her funnel in a lazy gray plume, taking on coal for a departure that had been moved up two days. Everyone on the island knew. Everyone was doing arithmetic with the hours.

Ada's commission lay folded inside her coat, next to her breast, where she had carried it since September. *By order of the District Census Office* — though the census office had long since stopped caring what Ada did, and the paper that mattered now was the one in Magistrate Rehn's valise, the warrant with its wax seal and its list of names. Voss. Eriksen. The heirloom machine. She had watched Halvard Rehn read that list aloud to himself on the crossing over, his lips moving like a boy reciting scripture, and she had understood then that she was watching two things at once: a young man in love with the law, and the law itself, which loved no one.

And she had hidden what she knew. That was the fact she kept returning to, turning it over like a stone to see what lived underneath. In October, when Rehn first came north asking after a typewriter reported sold or stolen or vanished twenty years ago, Ada had stood in the magistrate's rented room at the inn and said nothing about a name inked out of a household ledger. Nothing about M. Voss. Nothing about the way Felix Eriksen's pen had hesitated, back in the autumn, one beat too long before he told her no, no Voss had ever lodged at the light.

She had told herself it was procedure. A census taker's findings were her own until filed. But procedure was a coat, and underneath it she had simply chosen, and she knew it, and the knowledge sat in her like swallowed ice.

Now she watched the shore across the strait, and waited for a man who was going to do something foolish.

* * *

She had seen him launch the boat.

Not from the light itself — she'd been ashore since morning, summoned by Rehn to review the wreckage inventory — but from the high ground above the landing, where the pines opened onto the river. She had gone up there ostensibly to breathe, which was what people said when they needed to think and couldn't admit it. And from that height, in the flat pewter light of late afternoon, she had seen a figure come down the island path carrying something long and heavy wrapped in sacking, the way a man carries a body, or a cradle, or both.

Felix Eriksen had loaded it into the small rowing skiff he kept beached below the light. He had checked the wrappings twice. He had looked up at the lamp tower once — a glance, quick, the kind a man gives a sleeping child he is leaving — and then he had pushed off into a river full of breaking ice.

No lantern. No companion. No sense.

Ada had done the arithmetic without wanting to. The crossing was half a mile in open water. The floes were moving south with the current, plates the size of barn floors, grinding against each other with a patience that made her stomach hurt. A man in a skiff could survive that if the ice let him through the channels. If the channels closed while he was between them, the river would fold him in like a book and no one would find the page.

She should have told Rehn. That was the thought that came first, dutiful and cold. The magistrate wanted the machine; the machine was crossing the river in a sacking wrap; here was her commission, here was her duty, here was the moment every clerk in the district would say she had been waiting for. All she had to do was walk down to the inn, say four words — *he's on the water now* — and the law would do the rest. Boats would go out. Warrant satisfied. Career advanced. Conscience quieted by the observation that none of this was hers to carry.

Instead she had walked down to the boathouse, found the old keeper's spare oars, and started a fire in the iron stove so there would be warmth when — *if* — he landed. And then she had stood at the water's edge with her hands jammed in her pockets, watching the darkening strait for a boat that might never arrive, hating herself for hoping, and hating herself more for knowing exactly what she hoped for: not the recovery of evidence, but the survival of a stubborn old man who had lied to her in September and whom she had come, God help her, to respect.

The light went out at dusk. She noticed it the way you notice a heartbeat stopping. Across the strait, the tower that had burned steady through every gale of the season — through the night of shattered glass, through the storm they were already calling the worst in living memory — went dark. Either the lamp had failed, or no one was tending it, and Ada understood with a cold certainty that settled into her bones that both answers meant the same thing: whatever Felix Eriksen valued most in this world was currently floating between the ice floes, and it was not the light.

* * *

He came ashore two hours after full dark, and she nearly missed him.

The river gave him up grudgingly. First she heard the oarlocks — a rhythm broken and restarted, broken and restarted — and then a pale shape resolving out of the dark between two grinding pans of ice, moving with the current whether he willed it or not. She ran along the bank, following, shouting his name against the wind and hearing it torn away, until the skiff caught the edge of a floe and spun sideways and she saw him stand up in it, swaying, one arm locked around the wrapped shape as though it were a railing and he a passenger.

The bow ground onto the shingle ten yards downstream of the boathouse. Ada got there as he stepped out, and his legs went wrong beneath him, and she caught his arm and took most of his weight without deciding to.

"Ice took the rudder," he said. His voice was scraped raw. "Or I took it from the ice. One of those."

"You're bleeding."

"Am I." He looked at his hands as though they belonged to a stranger. The knuckles were opened, the fingers white past saving. He was soaked to the chest. Ice had built itself into his beard. He was sixty years old if he was a day, and he had just crossed a freezing strait alone at night, and Ada realized she was gripping his arm hard enough to bruise because the alternative was letting him fall.

"There's a stove lit," she said. "Boathouse. Walk."

He walked. Halfway there he stopped and turned back toward the water, and Ada saw the raw animal panic cross his face before he mastered it — the skiff, the wrapping, the thing in the wrapping, still beached where the river could reach it.

"I'll bring it," she said. "Go. Warm yourself."

She dragged the sacking bundle up above the tide line herself. It was heavier than it looked, denser, and something inside shifted with a metallic sound she recognized from a hundred government inventories: machinery, precision-made, expensive. She set it down inside the boathouse door and stood looking at it while Felix lowered himself onto a crate beside the stove, steaming gently, shivering in great silent convulsions that he plainly intended to pretend were not happening.

They sat in silence for a long time. The stove ticked. Outside, the river worked at the ice, patient as a debt collector.

"Why ashore?" she finally asked. "Why not keep it on the island? Rehn can't search without cause. You could have held out until spring."

"Spring is a magistrate's word," Felix said. His eyes stayed on the bundle. "Ice doesn't wait for spring. Neither does a warm front. Another week and the strait is soup, and the machine goes into the river with everything else, and twenty years of Marta's silence drowns for nothing." He paused. "Also, my brother sold me."

"I know. Ida heard him bargaining with Rehn. Half the tavern heard him by midnight." Ada pulled off her gloves. "You put it beyond his reach. Beyond the warrant's easy reach. Ashore, it becomes mine to account for, not yours to surrender."

Something moved behind his eyes — surprise, maybe, that she had understood, or disappointment that she had. "You always did read ledgers well, Miss Lindqvist."

"That's the trouble," Ada said. "I read everything well. It's what they pay me for."

She knelt by the bundle. Her fingers found the knot in the sacking. And she stopped, because the moment deserved stopping, and because some part of her wanted Felix to stop her, and knew that he would not.

"If I open this," she said, "I'm no longer a census taker who lost track of a household. I'm an officer of the district holding concealed evidence. Everything I've not said since October becomes concealment too. You understand that."

"I understand that you've been concealing since October regardless," Felix said. "The paper only makes it honest."

The words landed harder than he perhaps intended. She unwrapped the sacking anyway.

The typewriter sat inside like something embalmed. Black iron, gold decals worn to ghosts, a carriage dusted with two decades of island grit — and under the dust, unmistakable even to a woman who had never operated such a machine, the look of a thing built for permanence. Government offices ran on machines like this. Courts ran on them. Every confession, every deposition, every verdict that had ever ruined anyone she'd met came off a machine of exactly this make. She had spent her whole career handling the paper these machines produced and had never once stood this close to the animal itself.

A single page remained in the carriage. Yellowed. Typed, the letters crisp as frost ferns.

"Read it," Felix said quietly. "You'll have to sooner or later. Better by lamplight than by subpoena. And I'd rather it was you than him."

"Why me?"

"Because you asked the question too many in September," he said. "And because you didn't write the answer down."

Ada drew the page free. The stove light was poor; she carried the sheet to the lamp hung by the boathouse door and tilted it, and read.

It was dated twenty-two years ago, in the autumn of the fire. The type began evenly, formally, the language of someone educated and frightened and trying to sound neither:

*I, Elias Voss, being of sound mind and unsound estate, set down what follows so that it may outlive the lies now being typed about me...*

Her breath went shallow. She read on.

The page named the fire at the Voss estate. It named the insured losses, the debts dressed up as robbery, the plan conceived when the family's ruin became certain and only arson could salvage anything from it. It named the servant who was paid to light the lamps and leave, and who was promised pardon and passage and instead was arrested, tried on typed evidence prepared by the family's own solicitor, and convicted in a courtroom that never once questioned why a man accused of burning down a house owned a machine that matched the ransom notes. And it named Elias Voss himself — the eldest son, the heir, the man whose signature Ada had seen on half the shipping manifests in the district archives — as the hand that struck the match.

At the bottom, in place of a signature, the same typed words repeated like a hammer falling: *Let the machine testify, for no man in this family will.*

Below that, one line in different keys — lighter, faster, pressed hard by a different hand, a woman's hand, hurried:

*Marta. They are coming for the house. I am taking this and I am not coming back. Forgive me for choosing to live. — M.V., November '71*

Ada stood very still. Outside, the river boomed somewhere as a floe turned over, and she flinched as though it had happened inside her own chest.

Twenty years. Twenty years this page had sat in a locked room above a lighthouse while an innocent man grew old in a prison cell. Twenty years Marta Eriksen — née Voss, the Bible had said, the sea had said, the whole wrecked winter had said — had carried her sister-in-law's husband's crime in an iron box across a frozen country to marry a lighthouse keeper who would keep it dark for her. And Felix had kept it. Through grief, through the census, through a magistrate with a warrant and a brother with debts. He had kept it the way he kept the lamp: faithfully, thanklessly, and entirely alone.

She looked at him. He was watching her from the crate, wrapped in a horse blanket she'd found, his ruined hands folded, waiting for her verdict the way men wait for weather.

"He's still alive," Ada said. "The convicted man. Torvald Brandt. I checked the prison rolls in October — I don't fully know why, except your ledger made me curious. He's fifty-eight years old. He has served twenty years of a life sentence for a fire he did not set."

"I know," Felix said. "Marta knew. It's why she never destroyed the page, though God knows she wanted to. Burning it felt easy, she said. Leaving it was the penance." He looked down at his hands. "I have kept a lamp burning every night for twenty years so that strangers wouldn't die on that water. And one man died anyway, slowly, in a cell, because I kept a promise to a frightened woman. I have done the arithmetic of that every single night, Miss Lindqvist, and it has never once come out even."

"The law says this page belongs to the court," Ada said. "The warrant says it belongs to Rehn. My commission says—" She stopped. Started again. "My commission says whatever keeps me employed."

"And your conscience?"

The question hung there. She thought of Rehn — young, earnest, genuinely convinced that the law was a machine that produced justice if you fed it correctly, not yet having learned that it produced verdicts, and justice only sometimes, and rarely on schedule. She thought of handing him this page and watching it disappear into an evidence locker for a year while motions crawled and the Voss heirs' solicitors — the same family, the same money, the same solicitor's firm, she'd bet her pension on it — filed objections. She thought of Torvald Brandt, fifty-eight years old, running out of winters.

She thought of the inked-out name in a household ledger, and of how she had already chosen, in October, without admitting it — and of how everything since had merely been catching up to that choice.

"Felix," she said. "Do you trust me?"

His eyebrows rose slightly. "I trust you to read carefully."

"Then listen. If Rehn seizes this page tomorrow, it becomes a exhibit. Exhibits get argued. Arguments take seasons, and the Voss money buys seasons the way other families buy bread. But if the page reaches the record another way — authenticated, witnessed, entered before the family's solicitors know it exists — then the conviction review starts from truth instead of from seizure." She was thinking aloud now, fast, the census taker's mind laying columns of figures. "There's a circuit judge sitting in Hammerstad until Thursday. Judge Kollberg. She owes me nothing, but she signed my mother's widow's certificate twenty years ago and she reads everything I send her, because I have never once sent her anything careless. If I carry this page to her myself, sworn and witnessed—"

"You'd be carrying stolen evidence."

"I'd be carrying testimony," Ada said. "Given freely, by its author, twenty years ago, and delivered by its custodian — if its custodian will swear, before a judge, that he surrenders it voluntarily and has done since the day his wife died. Will you?"

Felix Eriksen looked at her for a long moment. The stove ticked. The river ground its teeth outside. And then he nodded, once, the smallest motion, like a man signing something.

"I've been waiting twenty years for someone to ask me to swear to something true," he said.

Ada wrapped the page in oilcloth, and then in her scarf, and put it inside her coat where her commission used to be. Then, deliberately, she took the commission out and laid it on the crate beside the stove — left it there, face up, in the empty boathouse, in the care of a man she had officially never trusted and privately never doubted.

"When they ask what happened to it," Felix said, nodding at the paper, "tell them the river took it."

"No," Ada said. She pulled the door open, and the wind came in cold and clean, and far upriver the *Vestfjord* blew a single low note across the water — testing her whistle, readying for a dawn departure that would carry three passengers and leave the rest to the ice. "Tell them the truth. It's the one thing we haven't tried yet."

She stepped out into the dark, toward the judge, toward the road, toward the thing she could no longer undo — and behind her, faint under the wind, she heard the lamp tower across the strait flare suddenly bright again, tended now by other hands, burning like a promise kept by inheritance.

And two miles downriver, at the inn, a clerk in the magistrate's employ who owed Ada Lindqvist a small gambling debt was already writing a note: *Saw the census woman on the north road tonight, walking fast, carrying something under her coat.*
