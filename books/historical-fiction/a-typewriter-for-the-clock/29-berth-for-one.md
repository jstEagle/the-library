## 29. Berth for One

The words came up the stairwell like cold water rising, and Felix stood with his hand on the rail and let them break over him.

*She's giving her berth away.*

Of course she was. Of course his daughter — Marta's daughter, Marta through and through, stubborn as a keel in sand — would stand on the ice at dawn and refuse salvation because it arrived dressed as a lie. He had known it, hadn't he, in some chamber of himself he kept locked even tighter than the room with the machine. He had known it when he made the arrangement in the small hours, and he had done it anyway, because a man who has spent twenty years keeping a light will spend twenty more believing that light can be given to someone whether they ask for it or not.

Ada was pulling on her coat below, her movements quick and certain, and Felix heard himself say, from somewhere far above his own voice, "I'll go."

"You shouldn't go alone," Ada said, buttoning to the throat. "If she sees you—"

"She'll see me anyway." He took his coat from its peg, the wool stiff with salt and old winters. "She's been seeing me all her life. That's rather the problem."

He went down past her into the kitchen, where Ida sat wrapped in a blanket at the table, her hands around a cup of tea gone cold, her eyes enormous in the lamplight. The girl looked up at him and something passed between them — not forgiveness, not yet, but recognition. Two people who had conspired in the dark and were now waiting together to learn what the daylight would cost.

"It isn't too late," Ida said quietly. "The captain won't sail until the channel—"

"The channel will close by noon." Felix stood at the window. Eastward, beyond the black shoulder of the headland, the sky had begun to bruise grey, and out on the river he could make out the icebreaker's riding lights, three points of gold swinging gently in the dark water of the lead. "It's now or it's spring, and if it's spring, the warrant comes back with the thaw and everything we've done this week means nothing."

Ida set down her cup. "Then we have to make her board."

"We can't *make* Signe do anything." And there it was — the truth of it, spoken aloud at last in his own kitchen, by his own mouth, after a lifetime of pretending otherwise. "We never could. Not since the day she was born."

* * *

But it had not been true an hour ago, and he knew it, and the knowing sat in him like swallowed stone. Because an hour ago — in the dead dark between three bells and four, when the house slept and the wind had dropped to that exhausted whisper storms leave behind — Felix had gone to the girl's bedside and woken her, and what he had asked of her had been nothing less than making.

He remembered it now with terrible clarity, the way a drowning man remembers the shape of the shore.

"Ida." He had touched her shoulder and she came awake the way she always did, instantly, completely, no child's slow surfacing but a lamp turned up to full flame — the reflex of an orphan who had learned that the world does not wait. "Get your coat. Quietly. We'll wake no one."

They had gone out into the blue-black cold together, down the frozen path toward the boathouse, their breath hanging behind them like two ghosts walking abreast. The stars were brutal and close. The river groaned under its ice, long tectonic complaints, the sound of a thing deciding whether to hold or let go.

"Felix." She never called him anything else; *uncle* was a word the household had quietly retired years ago, though neither of them had ever said so. "What's wrong?"

"I need you to listen," he said, "and then I need you to do the hardest thing anyone has ever asked of you, and I need you to forgive me for asking it."

They stopped at the boathouse door, and he turned to face her, and in the starlight her face was pale and grave and utterly attentive, and he understood — not for the first time, but for the first time *fully* — what Marta had seen in the orphanage ledger all those years ago. Not a charity case. A keeper. Someone who tended things.

"There are three berths on the icebreaker," he said. "There are four of us. You know this arithmetic; you've known it longer than any of us wanted you to. Anders took one berth by theft, which leaves two for three, and Signe has claimed hers for the conservatory, and I—" His voice caught on the simple grammar of it. "And I am not going."

"Not going." She repeated it flatly, testing the weight. "You're staying with the light."

"I'm staying with the light."

"No." It came out of her very softly, and it was worse than shouting. "No, that's not what staying means and you know it. Staying means the magistrate finds you here with a warrant and a Bible and a confession you rowed across a freezing river to hide, and he takes you, and we sail away and watch this island shrink behind us with nobody minding the lamp, and that's not staying, Felix, that's being *left*—"

"Ida." He took her shoulders. Through her coat he could feel her trembling, or perhaps the trembling was his own, transmitted through his hands like current through wire. "Listen to me. In a few hours, Signe will be at the dock. She'll be angry — she has every right to be angrier than she has ever been in her life, and I hope she spends forty years being angry at me if it keeps her warm. She will say she won't go. She may say it loudly. She may stand on that ice until the captain himself comes out to fetch her."

"What do you want me to do?"

"I want you to give her your argument." He had rehearsed this sentence in the dark for two hours and it still came out broken. "You told me once — the night of the great storm, when you thought I was asleep and you and Signe were keeping the lamp — you told her that everyone on this island gets saved by somebody, and that the only sin is refusing to be the somebody when your turn comes. Do you remember saying that?"

Her breath stopped. In the silence the river spoke instead, that long grinding moan beneath the ice.

"You were fourteen," Felix said. "You thought no one heard. But I was coming up the stair with oil, and I stood in the shadow of the door and listened to my daughter and my ward keep my light for me while I pretended to sleep, and Ida — I have never in my life felt so much like a fraud as I did in that doorway. So now it's your turn to be the somebody. Go to Signe. Tell her what you told me last week, when you offered to stay behind so she could go. Tell her that she doesn't get to erase herself to punish me. Tell her that her mother didn't flee across half of Norway carrying a typed page inside her coat lining so that her daughter could stand on a riverbank and trade her future away out of spite for a dead man's silences."

"Marta carried it herself?" Ida whispered. "In her coat?"

Felix closed his eyes. Twenty years of locked rooms, and here he stood unlocking them one by one on a frozen path at four in the morning, handing the contents to a sixteen-year-old because he no longer had the strength to carry them and lie about the weight.

"In her coat lining," he said. "Sewn in. She wore that coat every winter until the day she died, and I burned it the following spring because I couldn't bear to touch it, and I have regretted burning it every winter since. Now go inside and put on your good boots. The ones Signe resoled in October. Wear those, specifically, and when she asks why, tell her it's because you're going to walk onto that boat and you wanted to wear something she made."

"That's cruel," Ida said. Her voice shook. "That's — you're fighting unfair."

"Yes," Felix said. "I learned it from her mother, God rest her, who fought unfairly her whole life and called it love. And I am asking you to fight unfairly too, because the alternative is watching that ship sail with an empty berth and spending the rest of mine knowing I let pride decide for people who deserved better than my pride."

"And you?" Ida's chin came up, and there — there was Marta again, in the line of it, in the refusal to cry in front of men. "What happens to you at the dock, Felix? Who fights for you?"

"No one," he said. "That's the whole design."

* * *

He had left her at the kitchen door with the stars still out, and gone up alone to the lamp room, and done the thing he always did when the world became too large for the ground floor: he climbed.

The tower smelled of brass polish and paraffin and twenty winters of his own sweat soaked into the wood of the stairs. At the top, the lens turned in its slow patient orbit, throwing its beam out over the shattered ice field, and Felix stood in the rotating light and let it pass over him again and again — bright, dark, bright, dark — like the pages of a book turning faster and faster toward its ending.

He had counted the berths a dozen times since the announcement. Three souls the icebreaker could carry; that was the law of her tonnage and the law of the closing channel, and laws, unlike magistrates, did not bend. Four Eriksens there had been, once — himself, Signe, Anders, Ida — and Anders had spent his berth on the supply ferry like a man spending stolen money, and so the arithmetic had come down to two places and three lives, and Felix had lain awake doing sums that always ended in someone left standing on the ice.

Except that he had known, hadn't he, lying there in the dark with the wind dying outside, exactly whose place would be empty. There had never really been a question. A man could fool himself about a great many things — about the righteousness of silence, about the mercy of lies, about whether twenty years of tending a light could burn clean a single old sin — but a man could not fool himself at four in the morning. At four in the morning he knew that Signe was the future and Ida was the future and he was the past, and the past belongs on the shore. The past belongs holding the lamp so the future can see its way out.

So he had gone to Ida's room. So he had handed her the argument like a man passing a lit lamp along a dark gallery, hand to hand, hoping the wind wouldn't take it.

And now the sky was greying, and the beam swept its circle, and below him he could hear Ada's voice low in the kitchen and the clatter of the kettle, and out on the river the icebreaker's bell began to ring — slow, deliberate strokes, half an hour to departure, the sound carrying flat and enormous across the ice like the voice of the century itself.

Felix trimmed the wick. It needed nothing; he had trimmed it an hour ago; he trimmed it anyway, because his hands required occupation or they would shake, and a keeper's hands do not shake. Then he went down the stairs, one hundred and twelve of them, each one a year he had lived on this rock, and stepped out into the first grey light of the last morning.

* * *

The walk to the dock took eleven minutes. He knew because he had walked it ten thousand times and because this time he counted, the way a condemned man counts his steps, wanting the number, needing the number, holding the number against the vast unnumbered dark ahead.

The islanders had gathered along the shore road — the Halvorsens, old Mrs. Brandt wrapped in furs, the fisher boys whose boat he had pulled from the sea in October, standing in a silent ragged line with their caps in their hands. Word had traveled in the night, as word does on an island, that the icebreaker sailed at dawn and that not everyone on the point would be aboard. No one spoke to him. They watched him pass the way people watch a man carry a coffin.

The dock thrust itself out over the broken floes, and beyond it the lead of open water stretched black and narrow toward the ship, and on the ice beside the gangplank stood his daughter.

She had her arms crossed against the cold and her jaw set against everything else, and beside her, small and fierce in her good boots, stood Ida — talking, gesturing, insistent, deploying the argument exactly as instructed, wearing the resoled boots exactly as instructed, and Signe was shaking her head, shaking it, shaking it, and the purser at the gangplank head consulted his watch with the merciless serenity of a man employed by schedules rather than hearts.

"—not boarding," Signe was saying as Felix came near, her voice carrying thin and clear across the frozen air, "and you can stop looking at me like that, Ida Lindgren, I know he sent you, I know every word you've said came out of him, and it doesn't change—"

"Signe."

Everything stopped. The gulls, the wind, the bell — it seemed even the river paused beneath its ice. She turned, and her eyes found him across fifteen feet of frozen dock, and they were Marta's eyes, and they were blazing, and they were full of tears she would die before shedding in public.

"You," she said. "You sent a child to do your begging."

"I sent the only person whose argument you'd hear," Felix said. "Because yours and hers are the same argument, and you both learned it in this house, and I'd hoped — God help me, I actually hoped — that one of you might use it on me someday and I'd have the grace to lose."

"You don't get grace," Signe said, and her voice cracked down the middle of the word like river ice. "You don't get to stand there having decided everything — my whole life decided in locked rooms, my mother's name decided, my future decided tonight while we slept — and then talk about grace. Get out of my way. I'm getting off this dock."

She turned toward the gangplank — and stopped. Because Ida had moved. The girl planted herself directly in her path, boots squared on the planks, and lifted her chin with that terrible inherited stubbornness, and said, in a voice that carried to the islanders on the shore and to the sailors on the deck and to the gulls wheeling white against the dawn:

"He gave me his berth, Signe. He's not sailing. He's staying here to be arrested so that you and I can go free, and if you step off this plank, you make his arrest worth nothing — you make it a waste — and I will not let you do that to him, and I will not let you do it to *me*, because I have been the one left behind my entire life and I am telling you, as the person on this island who knows best what being left behind means—" her voice broke and held, broke and held, "—it is not noble. It is just cold. Take the berth. Take the berth and live, and write to me, and hate him if you have to, but *board the ship*."

The purser looked at his watch. Somewhere on the ship, the engine turned over, a deep cough, then a steady heartbeat. The bell rang again — fifteen minutes.

Signe looked at Ida. She looked at her father. And Felix saw it happen — saw the exact moment his daughter understood the full architecture of what he had built in the dark, saw her realize that she had not been outmaneuvered so much as *outloved*, that every exit led back to the same door — and her face, which had been stone all morning, became something younger and more frightened and infinitely more dangerous.

"You're coming too," she said to Felix, and it wasn't a question, and it wasn't a plea, and it was both. "Both berths. Yours and mine. We leave the machine to the magistrate and we all go, and hang the arithmetic—"

"The arithmetic hangs itself if we try that," Felix said gently. "Two berths, three of us. Someone stays. I've stayed my whole life, Signe. It's the one thing I'm qualified for."

"Father—"

"Ten minutes," said the purser.

Signe stood on the dock with the wind lifting her hair and the dawn breaking grey-gold behind her and the whole frozen world waiting to see what she would do — and Felix, who had made every arrangement, forged every necessity, armed every ally, felt his certainty begin, for the first time, to crack.

Because he had planned for her anger. He had planned for her grief, her defiance, her sense of duty to Ida. He had built the trap of love and baited it with sacrifice and believed, as he had believed for twenty years, that he could control what happened at the end of it.

He had not planned for what she did next.

Signe Eriksen looked at her father one last time — and stepped back off the gangplank.
