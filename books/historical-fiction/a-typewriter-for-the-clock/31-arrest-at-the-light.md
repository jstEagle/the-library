## 31. Arrest at the Light

The morning the magistrate came for him, Felix Eriksen was oiling the clockwork that turned the lens, and he did not stop when he heard boots on the stair.

He had known this morning was coming since the night he rowed. He had felt it in his shoulders ever since — not the ache of the oars, though that was there too, sunk deep into the muscle like a nail driven to the head — but something older than ache. A certainty, quiet as frost forming on glass. You do not hand a magistrate a page that unmakes a conviction and expect to keep your own hands clean. Marta had known it too. *The truth costs someone, Felix. Always. The only question is whether you choose who.*

He had thought, in the dark hours after the rowing, that he had chosen himself.

Now the boots reached the lamp room door and stopped, and Halvard Rehn filled the frame with the gray light of the morning behind him, hat in his hands, which was either courtesy or apology or both.

"Keeper Eriksen."

"Magistrate." Felix kept his fingers on the brass fitting, gave it a last quarter-turn, wiped the wrench with a rag. The mechanism ticked beneath them both, patient as a heart. "You'll want your coat buttoned. The gallery's got a wind off the water that goes through a man like wire."

"I'm not here about the weather."

"No," Felix said. "I didn't suppose you were."

Rehn came out onto the gallery. Below them the strait lay broken and glittering where the ice had cracked, floes drifting seaward like a congregation dispersing, and far off — a gray smudge against the grayer sky — the icebreaker sat at anchor, steam rising from her stack in a straight white column. One berth left aboard her now. Ida's name written on the manifest in Ada Lindqvist's careful hand. Signe had stepped back off the gangplank two days ago and given it away as easily and as terribly as a girl handing over her bread, and Felix still could not think of it without his throat closing.

"You could have told me," Rehn said. "Twenty years ago. Ten years ago. Last month, standing in your kitchen while I showed you the warrant."

"Yes."

"That's all you have to say? Yes?"

Felix set down the rag. He turned to face the younger man fully, and he saw what he had seen on the night they read the confession aloud together: that Rehn was not cruel, and not ambitious, and not stupid, and that every one of those absences made what was coming harder rather than easier. A cruel man you could hate. A stupid one you could evade. Rehn was only a man who believed the law was a load-bearing wall in the world, and Felix had spent twenty years pulling bricks out of it.

"The convicted man's name was Torben Haug," Felix said. "He died in the workhouse at Falstad four winters back. Blind by the end. He used to tell the other men he'd started the fire because it was better than telling them the Voss family paid him to say so and then let him hang for it slowly." He looked out at the water. "I knew Elias Voss set the fire. My wife knew it. She carried the proof out of that house in a carpetbag under her petticoats while her father's house burned behind her, and she married me six months later in a church sixty miles south, and she asked me for one thing only — that the machine never leave the island. That no one ever be hanged twice for the same crime." His voice held level. He was proud of that, in a distant way, the way a man is proud of a beam holding in a storm. "She is dead eleven years, Magistrate. Her name has been dirt for twenty. And the machine stayed put until I rowed it to you."

"And now it's mine to act on," Rehn said quietly. "That's the trouble, Keeper. You've made it mine to act on."

"I know."

"The warrant names an heirloom of the Voss estate, concealed from the crown. It doesn't name you yet. But concealing evidence of a capital crime — harboring it, knowing what it contained—" Rehn stopped. He looked at his hat. "Ada Lindqvist will testify the confession is authentic. She examined the type impression against the machine itself. There's no question it's genuine. Which means there can be no question that you knew it was genuine, and kept it locked in a room while an innocent man served a sentence for murder."

"Arson," Felix said. "No one died in the fire. That was the one mercy in it."

"It changes nothing legally."

"It changes everything otherwise." Felix said it without heat. "You taught me that yourself, standing in my kitchen. You said the law isn't a scale, it's a ledger. Well. Here is my entry."

Rehn put his hat on. When he spoke again his voice had gone formal, the young magistrate's voice, the voice that would carry him through thirty years of courtrooms after this one, and Felix heard the boy who had argued justice over law die into it a little.

"Felix Eriksen, keeper of this light, I am arresting you on the charge of concealing evidence pertaining to the arson of the Voss estate, per the warrant issued at Christiania. You'll come with me on the morning boat. You're entitled to counsel at the assizes. Do you understand the charge?"

"I understand it," Felix said. "I've understood it for twenty years. You've only just caught up."

* * *

They gave him an hour.

He asked for an hour and Rehn, looking old suddenly despite being half Felix's age, granted it. An hour to secure the light, to make his arrangements. Rehn stood in the doorway of the kitchen while Felix wrote, watching without pretending not to watch, and Felix wrote quickly because there was not much to write that had not already been said across twenty years of drills.

To Signe:

*The wick trimmers hang on the third hook. The spare lenses are in the crate marked GLASS — do not trust the crate marked SPARES, your mother mislabeled it in '88 as a joke and we never corrected it. Wind the clockwork every four hours without fail; if you miss a winding the rotation drags and the flash character changes, and a changed character is how ships die. Oil consumption runs high in cold. Keep the log. Write in it plainly — weather, wind, passes, failures. A plain log is worth more than a clever one. If I am not back by thaw, the appointment reverts to the district and you'll be turned out, but turn the light properly until then. Whatever else happens, whoever comes, turn the light properly.*

He read it over. It sounded like what it was — a man instructing his replacement — and he supposed that was the truest thing he could leave her.

Ida found him in the oil locker, counting cans.

"You knew this would happen," she said. She was sixteen and had learned on this island to read men the way she'd learned to read letters, patiently, from scraps. "When you rowed out. You knew."

"I hoped the law had more sense than its own books." He stacked a can. "It usually does. Just slower."

"They can't keep you long. Ada said—"

"Ada says what she believes, and she believes well. But a charge is a charge, Ida-mine. Go and help your sister with the lamp. And mind the third hook."

Ida did not go. She crossed the little room and put her arms around him, hard, her cheek against his chest where she could surely hear the same ticking he carried everywhere now, the clockwork above them, the typewriter's ghost, his own stubborn heart. He held her the way he had held her the day the fishing boat brought her orphaned at nine years old — completely, and knowing it would never be enough.

"You gave us the berths," she said into his coat. "Both of them. Anders took one like a thief and Signe gave hers away and you kept none, and now they're taking you instead. That's not arithmetic, Papa. That's just giving everything away."

"Some things you give away because keeping them would cost more."

"What could cost more than this?"

He looked down at her. "Her name," he said. "Marta's name. It cost twenty years of everything, and it was worth every year, and now it's paid. Don't you dare cry at the price of a thing that was bought fair."

But she cried anyway, and he let her, and outside the window the icebreaker's steam column bent in the wind like a man bowing.

Signe came last. She stood in the doorway of the kitchen with her arms folded and her jaw set — her mother's jaw, God help them both — and she did not hug him and he did not ask her to, because between them stood the wound they had opened together and not yet closed: *keeper of lamps and lies*, she had called him, and neither of them had taken the words back.

"The log book's current," she said. "Through yesterday."

"Good."

"I know the flash character by heart. Four seconds eclipsed, three dark. I could run it asleep."

"I know you could. I taught you."

"Why are you letting them take you?" It burst out of her then, the thing underneath. "You cleared her name. They have the page. What possible justice is there in putting *you* in a cell for hiding a truth everyone wanted hidden? The Vosses hid it first. The court hid it when they convicted Torben Haug on nothing. Why is it the man who kept the proof who pays?"

"Because the others hid it out of shame or greed," Felix said, "and I hid it out of love, and the law doesn't weigh motives. Only acts." He crossed to her. She did not step back. "Your mother asked me for twenty years of silence. I gave it to her gladly. Now the silence is over and the bill has come, and I intend to pay it standing up. That's not letting them take me, Signe. That's choosing the losing side of a bargain I made with open eyes."

"And what am I meant to do?" Her voice cracked and she hated it, he could see her hating it. "Keep your lamps and wait?"

"Keep my lamps," Felix said, "and write the log plainly. That's all any of us were ever asked to do."

For one moment her composure trembled — he saw it go through her like wind through wheat, saw her hands come halfway up toward him — and then she mastered it, because she was her mother's daughter before she was his, and she folded her arms again and lifted her chin.

"Come home by thaw," she said. "Or I'm turning the light wrong out of spite."

Despite everything, Felix laughed. It hurt going out of him, like a splinter leaving flesh, but it came.

"There's my girl," he said.

* * *

They walked down to the landing at ten o'clock, Rehn a pace behind him, the constable Rehn had brought carrying Felix's single valise as if it might contain something dangerous. It contained a change of clothes, Marta's Bible, and a tin of tobacco. Twenty years of a life, packed in a case small enough to carry one-handed.

The island had turned out.

Felix saw them as the path bent toward the shore — the fishermen and their wives, the Hovden brothers from the drying sheds, old Mrs. Kranz who sold eggs, a dozen children silent in a way children almost never were. They lined the shingle above the tideline in the flat white morning, and nobody spoke, and the silence was worse than shouting and better too, because it was the silence of people who knew exactly what was happening and whom it served.

Old Mrs. Kranz stepped forward as he passed. She pressed something into his hand — a twist of paper, warm from her pocket. Salt fish, wrapped tight. Food for the road, given wordlessly, the oldest currency of the coast.

"Takk," Felix said.

She nodded once, sharply, as if to say *don't thank me, thank yourself*, and stepped back into the line.

At the landing the boat waited, and beyond it the icebreaker rode her anchor with her gangway down, and beside the gangway stood two figures who had no business being there and every business being there. Signe. And Ida. They must have cut across the point ahead of the party, and they stood at the foot of the plank like sentries, and when Felix reached them Signe took his hand — took it, gripped it, hers cold as the morning — and said, loud enough for the whole shore to hear:

"This light will be burning tonight. And tomorrow night. And every night until you walk back up that path. Write that in your ledger, Magistrate. Entry one."

Rehn looked at her for a long moment. Then at Felix.

"She means it," Felix said. "She's better at it than I am. Always has been."

The boat pulled away. Felix sat in the stern with his valise between his feet and watched the island recede — the white house, the black tower, the gallery rail where he had stood ten thousand nights — and the figures on the shore did not move, none of them, not even the children, until distance dissolved them into the rock itself, as if the island had grown them and was taking them back.

Then he looked up at the tower one last time and his chest went hollow.

The lamp was dark.

In all the years — through storms, through grief, through the night the glass shattered and he bled on the gallery stones — the light had never once failed to show at dusk, and here it was, midday, dead-eyed, and he understood that it wasn't failure. It was simply that the keeper was leaving, and the light knew it, and the hours until dusk stretched ahead like a held breath.

*Turn the light properly,* he had written. *Whatever else happens.*

The boat rose and fell on the swell. Behind him, faint across the water, he heard a sound that stopped his heart and then restarted it: the creak of the tower door, and — he would swear it to his dying day, though the wind stole most of it — a child's voice calling up the stairwell, *Signe! Third hook!*

He closed his eyes. He did not look back again.

Rehn sat opposite him in the stern, coat collar up, watching the island shrink. For a long time neither man spoke. Then the magistrate reached inside his coat and drew out a folded paper — not the warrant, not the charge sheet. A letter, sealed.

"For the assizes clerk at Trondheim," Rehn said. "A request to expedite the hearing. I've cited the prisoner's cooperation and the risk to navigation." He met Felix's eyes. "Every day you sit in a cell, that coast sits in the dark. I've argued justice over law once already this winter, Keeper. I find I don't much care for the taste of the alternative."

Felix took the letter. His hands, he noticed distantly, were steady — steadier, perhaps, than they had been in twenty years.

"And if the court disagrees with you?" he asked.

"Then the court and I will argue," Rehn said, "and I have Ada Lindqvist, and a page of type, and a dead man's blind testimony waiting in the file. I like our odds."

The boat swung toward the icebreaker. Ahead, the gangway waited, and the gray water chopped against the hull, and somewhere north along the frozen river the assizes already sat like a held breath.

Felix gripped the gunwale and watched his island fall away behind him, smaller and smaller, until it was only a dark tooth in a white sea — with a tower on it that stood unlit for the first time in twenty years, and two girls climbing its stairs against the failing light.
