## 32. The Light Goes Dark

They took him at the tide line, in front of everyone.

Ida watched from the top of the path, her hands still burning from the rope she'd held, though she had not been allowed to hold anything. Felix Eriksen walked down to the magistrate's boat with his coat buttoned wrong — one eyelet off, the collar crooked at the back of his neck — and he did not fix it. He never would have let a lamp be lit with a wick set crooked. He never would have let anything under his care go out into the world misaligned. But he walked down that shingle with his collar crooked and his hands free and empty at his sides, and when he passed Ida he stopped, just for a breath, the way he stopped at every door on his rounds.

"Wind's backing north-northwest," he said quietly. "It'll come round again by full dark. Trim for it."

Then Magistrate Rehn touched his elbow, and they went down to the boat together, and that was all. No speech. No farewell worth the word. The islanders stood along the shore in their dark coats like so many fence posts, and Signe stood beside Ida with her jaw set hard enough to crack, and neither of them said a single thing until the boat was a smudge between the gray water and the grayer sky.

"He told us to trim for the wind," Ida said. Her voice came out strange, small, as if it belonged to someone younger than she was. "He didn't say goodbye."

"That *was* goodbye," Signe said. "That was the whole of it."

* * *

The house felt wrong the moment they stepped inside, and Ida could not at first say why, until she understood: it was the silence of the clock. Not stopped — Felix would have wound it before he left; she had watched him do it through the window, his hand turning the key with the same unhurried patience he gave everything — but unwitnessed. For nineteen years there had always been an ear in this house tuned to that ticking, a man who heard it the way other men heard their own hearts. Now it ticked for no one. It ticked for them, if they chose to listen.

Signe went straight to the kitchen and began laying a fire with hands that shook only a little, which Ida thought was very brave, because her own hands were shaking a great deal and she was holding nothing at all.

"They can't keep him," Signe said, to the kindling more than to Ida. "Rehn isn't — he's not like them. He read the page. Ada read it aloud to him in our own kitchen. You saw his face."

"I saw his face," Ida agreed. She had seen it: the young magistrate standing by the window with the confession in his hands, looking older by twenty years in the space of a paragraph, because Elias Voss's name sat there in typed letters as neat as a hymn. But faces were not verdicts. Faces did not row across a frozen river in spring.

"Then help me with this fire and stop standing there like a ghost at your own funeral," Signe said, and there was something in her voice that cracked halfway through, so that the sharpness broke open into something else, and Ida crossed the room and put her arms around her from behind, cheek against her shoulder blade, and held on while Signe breathed.

After a while Signe said, "The lamp."

"I know."

"It'll need lighting before dusk. He won't be here to light it."

"I know."

"The glass is still boarded where the storm broke it." Signe pulled back and wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, briskly, the way their father dried dishes — no ceremony, work first, feeling after. "Two panes. We'll have to manage."

"We've done the rounds with him a hundred times," Ida said. "You know the order. I know the order. We both know the order."

Signe looked at her then, really looked, and something passed between them that had no words attached — the recognition that they were not practicing anymore. That tonight was not a drill with Felix's voice correcting their grip on the winch handle. Tonight the light was theirs, and the sea did not care that they were a girl of seventeen and a girl of twelve, and the ships out there in the dark did not care either.

"Get the oil skins," Signe said. "We'll eat after. The light comes first."

He had taught them that. *The light comes first.* Said it so many times, so flatly, over so many years, that it had worn a groove in Ida the way water wears stone, and she had never once asked what it meant, because she had known: it meant that whatever you were carrying — grief, fear, a name that wasn't yours, a machine that could hang a man or save him — you carried it up the stairs anyway, and you trimmed the wick, and you kept the flame.

* * *

The stair to the lamp room was ninety-three steps. Ida knew the number the way she knew her own age, because Felix had made her count them aloud on her first climbs, when she was seven and the spiral turned her stomach. *Ninety-three steps, Ida. A keeper counts everything. A keeper who guesses is a keeper who drowns someone.* She counted them now without meaning to, her lips moving in the cold draft of the tower, Signe's boots ringing above her on the iron treads.

The lamp room smelled of brass polish and burned oil and salt, a smell Ida had thought all her life was simply the smell of safety itself. The two broken panes were boarded with planks Felix had fitted himself, and through the gaps the wind came in thin and whistling, carrying the first spit of the rising sea. Below them the strait lay in long steel-colored rollers, and away to the north the ice sheet stretched white to the mainland shore, cracked now in a hundred places since the warm front, stitched with leads of open black water.

"Check the reservoir," Signe said, and Ida knelt at the base of the lamp and opened the little door and sighted along the gauge glass the way she'd been shown a hundred times, a hundred times being the point of Felix Eriksen entirely.

"Three-quarters. Enough till morning."

"Weight the clockwork. Wind it four turns past the mark — no, wait." Signe paused, hand on the crank. "Three turns past. The cold thickens the oil and the mechanism runs slow; he'd want it three."

"He'd want it exactly right," Ida said, "and three turns past is exactly right in this weather," and Signe almost smiled, and wound it.

This was the thing Ida understood now, standing in the lamp room while the light outside died by degrees — this was the thing the whole island perhaps had never understood about Felix Eriksen. He had not drilled them because he expected to die or be taken. He had drilled them because love, in him, had always taken the shape of instruction. He could not say *I love you* without his ears going red; Ida had tested this for years, saying it first and watching the color climb. But he could stand beside a twelve-year-old girl at a winch for an hour in a freezing wind, showing her how to ease the cable without letting it jump, and that hour was the same sentence spoken fluently. Every lesson had been a letter he never mailed. They had simply never needed to open them until now.

"Dusk," Signe said.

The horizon had gone the color of a bruise. Out over the water the last daylight lay in a thin band, and beneath it the sea was already night. Signe struck the match — Felix always let her strike the match, even when she was small, even when her hands were unsteady, because *a keeper lights her own lamp or she isn't a keeper* — and touched it to the wick, and the flame caught, and Ida turned the valve a quarter measure, watching the flame stand up tall and clean, and then they rotated the great lens assembly by hand until the clockwork took the weight, and the beam swung out across the water like something alive.

From below, the light must have looked as it always looked. Steady. White. Unbroken. As if nothing at all had changed on the island, as if the man who had kept it for twenty years were still climbing these stairs, still counting his steps, still trimming his wick against a backing wind.

But inside the lantern, Ida saw what the shore could not: the flame trembling in the draft through the boarded panes, and Signe's face lit gold and shadowed gold, wet-eyed and absolutely steady, and she thought: *this is what inheritance looks like.* Not a will. Not blood. Not a typewriter locked in a room or a name inked out of a ledger. Just this — two people standing a watch they were taught to stand, by a man who taught them without ever saying he was teaching them, because he was too proud to call it love and too honest to leave them unprepared.

"First watch is mine," Signe said. "Go down and eat. Wake me at midnight."

"You'll fall asleep sitting up."

"Yes. Wake me anyway."

* * *

Ida did not sleep either. She tried. She lay in her narrow bed listening to the clock tick and the wind rise, and every creak of the house sounded like boots on stairs, and once — she was nearly sure, though it might have been the wind in the chimney — she heard the faint rhythmic clack from behind the locked door of the back room, as though the typewriter were keeping its own watch, typing to no one, waiting.

Near midnight she got up and climbed instead of waking anyone, and found Signe exactly as predicted, upright in the chair by the lens with her chin dropped to her chest, and Ida did not wake her. She sat on the floor at her sister's feet — not sister by blood, none of them bound by blood except Anders, and look what blood had done, blood had sold them all for a debt — and she kept the watch herself, eyes on the flame, hands ready on the valve, counting the seconds of each rotation of the beam. Four seconds bright toward the north lead. Four seconds dark. Four seconds bright. Somewhere out in that darkness were boats, were men, were a magistrate and a census woman and a brother in a cell of his own making, and the light touched none of them and all of them, impartially, the way Felix had always said truth should burn.

Near dawn Signe stirred, and put her hand on Ida's head without opening her eyes, and said, "You let me sleep."

"You needed it."

"That's not the arrangement."

"The arrangement," Ida said, "was his. We're making new ones."

And Signe, after a moment, laughed — a short, cracked, real laugh, the first since the boat took him — and pulled Ida up onto the bench beside her, and they watched the sun come up over the ice together, two keepers of a light that had not gone dark.

Down the coast, far off across the leads of open water, Ida saw a boat moving toward the mainland, small and stubborn, and she thought of Felix somewhere ahead of it in a cold room with bars, and she made a promise to the flame, quietly, in the language he had given her:

*Ninety-three steps. We'll count them every day until you come home to walk them yourself.*

Behind them, down in the house, the clock struck six — and beneath it, faint and patient, the old machine began, very softly, to tick.
