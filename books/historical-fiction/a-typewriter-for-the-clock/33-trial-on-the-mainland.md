## 33. Trial on the Mainland

The courthouse at Torshavn had been built by men who believed that justice required good light, and so its windows were tall and many, and on the morning of the hearing the winter sun came through them in long pale blades that lay across the floor like drawn swords. Ada Lindqvist stood in the corridor outside the chamber with her commission papers folded in her glove, and she found that her hands would not stay still.

She had testified before. Twice, in smaller rooms, over smaller matters — a disputed boundary, a drowned fisherman whose widow contested the finding. But those had been questions of fact that anyone might answer. This morning she was to speak about a piece of paper that could unmake a conviction twenty years old, and every person in that building would be measuring her words against her office.

*Census taker*, they would think. *A woman who counts heads.* What did she know of ink and provenance?

More than any of them, as it happened. She knew the ledger at the light with its inked-out name. She knew Felix Eriksen's hands, how they moved when he lied and how they stilled when he told the truth. She knew the confession page itself — she had read it twice by lamplight on the night the ice broke, once aloud to hear how it sounded in a human voice, and both times it had seemed to her less like evidence than like a wound that had finally been allowed to speak.

"Fröken Lindqvist." Halvard Rehn stood at the end of the corridor, hat in hand, his young face pale from weeks of preparation. He looked, she thought, as though he had not slept properly since the arrest. "You're certain you wish to do this? They will ask you why you didn't report what you found when you found it. They will make it about you."

"I know what they will do," Ada said. "I've had three weeks to imagine it."

"And?"

She looked at him. He was thirty years old and carrying the weight of an institution that did not want to be wrong in public. She understood now why he had come north alone, without backing, without senior counsel — because no one senior would touch it.

"And I spent four months counting the people of that coast," she said. "Every soul, every household, every name spelled correctly into a book that will sit in an archive until the paper crumbles. I know precisely what a record is worth, Magistrate. It is worth exactly as much as the truth inside it."

Rehn almost smiled. "Then let us go make the record honest."

* * *

The courtroom smelled of stove-iron and wet wool. The gallery was full — more full than Ada had expected, and fuller than propriety usually permitted, because the Voss case had traveled down the coast all winter in the mouths of fishermen and ferry clerks, growing details like a snowball rolling downhill. The arson at the Voss estate. The servant convicted. The lighthouse keeper arrested with the machine that proved everything. Somewhere in the retelling, Marta Eriksen had become a fugitive heroine; somewhere else, a thief. Ada had stopped listening to the versions weeks ago.

At the prosecution's table sat Assessor Krag, a heavy man with a heavy face, who had built his early career on the Voss conviction and who regarded Rehn across the aisle the way one regards a younger cousin who has brought disgrace to the family name.

Felix Eriksen stood in the dock.

Ada had seen him gaunt before — she had seen him stand bare-handed in a gale, she had watched him row a freezing strait at night with death riding the floes beside him — but she had never seen him small. The dock made him small. His hands rested on the rail and they were still, terribly still, the stillness she had learned to read as truthfulness, and his eyes found hers across the room with a look that asked nothing and forgave everything in advance.

*Whatever happens here*, that look said, *you owe me nothing.*

It was the same look, she realized, that he must have given Marta Voss on the night she arrived at his light with a stolen typewriter and a burned family behind her.

The presiding judge called the session to order, and the machinery began.

Krag went first, because the state defends its convictions before it considers abandoning them. He walked the court through the original case with the ease of a man reciting his own biography: the fire at the Voss estate in the spring of 1873; the insured losses; the servant, Jonas Pettersen, found with keys he ought not to have possessed and a grudge he had voiced in drink; the confession extracted, signed, witnessed; the sentence. Twenty years served. Pettersen had died two years ago in the workhouse at Halden, Krag noted, his voice dropping into a register meant to suggest closure, finality, a grave well-tended.

"The state's position is simple," Krag concluded. "A conviction was entered. Evidence has surfaced. Very convenient evidence — produced by the very man now charged with concealing it, authenticated by a census clerk, championed by a magistrate too junior for the case. We are asked to believe that for twenty years the sole proof of a great man's crime sat in a locked room on an island, guarded by a widower, and that no one thought to mention it. Gentlemen, either this is a conspiracy of silence spanning decades, or it is a fabrication assembled in a kitchen over one bad winter. I submit the latter is the more economical explanation."

*Economical.* Ada wrote the word in her notebook and underlined it twice. She had learned on the coast that men loved economy right up until the sea disproved it.

Rehn rose. He did not perform. He laid out the warrant for the heirloom, the chain of discovery, the circumstances of the arrest — and here Krag objected, and here the judges conferred, and here the morning ground through its gears while Ada watched Felix in the dock watching the ceiling as though the answers were written there.

Then the judge said, "Call your first witness," and Rehn turned, and looked at her, and said, "Fröken Ada Lindqvist."

* * *

Walking to the stand felt like walking out onto the ice — that specific knowledge that the surface would hold or would not hold, and that the moment of testing had already begun.

She took the oath. She gave her name, her office, her commission. Krag studied her over his folded hands with the patience of a man who intended to enjoy himself.

"Fröken Lindqvist," Rehn began, "what is your professional training?"

"Clerical. Records, registers, census enumeration. Three years with the district archive at Bergen before my current commission."

"So you are practiced in examining documents. Their age, their handling, their consistency."

"That is most of the work. A record that contradicts itself is telling you something. A record that contradicts its neighbors is telling you something larger."

"And the document you examined last November — the typed page recovered from the accused's keeping — did it contradict anything?"

"It contradicted the official account of the Voss fire," Ada said. "Specifically, the account of where the fire began, at what hour, and who was known to have been in the east wing that night. The page gives details that were never published. Details that appear nowhere in the trial transcript you have before you. Names of servants on duty. The state of the locks on the east wing door, which the original investigation recorded as forced but which the page describes as opened with a key kept in the study — a key whose existence only emerges in a household inventory from 1871, filed after the deaths in the family."

Krag shifted. It was small, a settling of weight, but Ada saw it, and she understood that the inventory was the thing he had not known about.

"How can you attest the page's age?" the judge asked. "Typewriting leaves no ink to date, no fading."

"No ink, Your Honor, but it leaves wear." Ada turned toward the exhibit table, where the typewriter itself sat under a cloth like a caged bird. "May I?"

She was permitted. She carried the machine — heavier than it looked, warm from the stove-light — to the witness rail, and unwrapped it, and let the court see it: black iron, ivory keys gone the color of old teeth, the carriage scarred along one side.

"This is a Halvorsen No. 4, manufactured between 1869 and 1872 — the serial plate confirms 1871. Only a few hundred were sold in this country, nearly all to institutions and wealthy households. The Voss estate purchased one in September of 1871; the invoice survives in the probate file. Now —" she tilted the carriage to the light — "the typebars. Every machine develops faults. A bent bar, a worn letter. This one strikes its lowercase *e* slightly high and to the left. Its *r* carries a nick from a jam, here, cutting a small notch into every *r* struck after roughly 1874. The confession page exhibits both faults, identically positioned. So do eleven surviving letters from the Voss household correspondence, dated between 1875 and 1878, held in the district archive. Whatever wrote those letters wrote this confession. And whatever wrote this confession was not in the possession of Jonas Pettersen, who could not type at all — the trial record establishes he signed his confession with a mark."

She set the machine down. Her voice had been steady. Inside, she was surprised to find herself steady too.

Krag rose for cross-examination, and his voice was honey over gravel.

"Fröken Lindqvist. You examined this document in November. When did you first learn of its existence?"

"On the night of the ice-break, ashore at Nordvik. The accused brought it across the strait."

"You did not report it immediately."

"I reported it within the day. To Magistrate Rehn."

"To the one man already inclined to believe it. Not to the district office. Not to me."

"I reported it to the magistrate with jurisdiction," Ada said evenly, "which is what my commission requires. Though if the assessor wishes to know why I hesitated even that long, I will tell him: because I had spent four months in that man's house. Because I had eaten at his table. Because I had watched him keep a lamp lit for strangers in weather that would kill them, and I needed a night to reconcile the man I knew with the criminal I was being shown. One night, Assessor. I regret the delay. I do not retract a word of what I found afterward."

"And what did you find?" Krag spread his hands. "A paper. Typed on a machine anyone might have owned—"

"Which Pettersen demonstrably could not operate."

"—typed by persons unknown, containing claims against a dead family that cannot defend themselves. You call this proof. I call it a story with a machine attached."

For a moment the courtroom was silent enough that Ada could hear the stove ticking. Then she heard her own voice, calm and clear, and it sounded to her like someone else's:

"When I was trained at Bergen, my instructor told me that a falsified record always wants something. It wants to flatter, or it wants to accuse, or it wants to profit. He taught me to ask what the document wants. So I asked. This document accuses Elias Voss — the wealthiest man on the coast, whose heirs still hold offices and property in this district. It exonerates a dead servant who left no estate and no descendants to benefit. It was hidden for twenty years at enormous cost to the man who hid it, who gained nothing by it, who lost his brother and very nearly his daughter to keeping it. What does it want, Assessor? I have asked it for months. It wants nothing. That is what makes it true."

She stepped down. In the gallery, someone began, softly, to applaud, and was silenced, but the silence afterward had changed shape.

* * *

They recessed at midday, and Ada found Rehn in the corridor eating nothing and staring at nothing, his papers squared before him with a precision that betrayed him.

"Pettersen's cellmate," he said, when she sat down across from him. "The workhouse chaplain located him. He's alive, Fröken Lindqvist. Old, half-deaf, and terrified — but alive. He says Pettersen told him for years that he confessed to a fire he couldn't have set, because he was paid to confess. Paid by whom, Pettersen never said. But he described the money coming in an envelope sealed with green wax, and—" Rehn's jaw tightened. "The Voss estate used green wax. There's a sealing stamp in the probate inventory."

"Will he testify?"

"He'll testify. Tomorrow, if we can get him up from Halden in time." Rehn rubbed his eyes. "Krag will say he's bought. Everyone is bought, in Krag's world. Everyone lies except the dead, and the dead conveniently stay quiet."

"Not everyone," Ada said.

Rehn looked up at her.

"Elias Voss is dead," she said. "But his daughter-in-law kept a machine and a page for twenty years rather than let the lie stand, knowing it would cost her everything. And her husband went to a cell this winter rather than burn that page when burning it would have freed him. You want to know whether people lie, Magistrate? Some do. And some keep a light burning over the truth for twenty years and wait for someone to come count them."

Rehn held her gaze a moment longer than the conversation strictly required, and then, being who he was, looked back down at his papers and said, "The afternoon session. Krag will put up the original investigators. Be ready."

"I am ready," Ada said, "to watch the law bend."

"Bend?"

"Toward justice," she said, standing. "It's the direction it bends slowest, and therefore the direction it must be pushed hardest."

* * *

The afternoon belonged to Krag, and Krag spent it well. He produced the constable who had taken Pettersen's confession twenty years ago, white-haired now, dignified, unshakeable in his memory of the man's guilt. He produced the insurance investigator whose arithmetic had damned Pettersen. He walked the court through the economics of the case — who profited, who lost — until the fire seemed less a tragedy than a ledger, and ledgers, Krag implied, did not require typewriters to be read.

Through all of it, Felix Eriksen stood in the dock and said nothing, because he was not called upon to say anything. His own trial — for concealment, for obstruction — waited behind this one like a second storm behind the first. Ada caught his eye once, mid-afternoon, and saw that he was listening to the description of the fire with an expression she had not seen on him before: not fear, not grief. Recognition. As though, after twenty years of guarding the account of that night, he was hearing it told wrong in public for the first time, and finding the wrongness almost bearable, because tomorrow it would be told right.

Near dusk, the judge adjourned until the following morning, when the cellmate would testify, and when — Rehn told her quietly, gathering his papers — he would make his argument. Not merely against the conviction. Against the manner of it. Procurement. Subornation. The green wax.

"You understand what you're asking them to admit," Ada said. "That the court itself was used as an instrument. That a man died in the workhouse serving a sentence this court handed down on false pretenses."

"I understand," Rehn said, "that if we don't ask them to admit it, then the machinery works perfectly forever, and Jonas Pettersen stays guilty in every book ever printed." He closed his satchel. "A record is worth exactly as much as the truth inside it. Someone wise told me that."

Outside, the light was failing over the rooftops of Torshavn, and somewhere beyond the harbor the sea ice groaned and shifted, and Ada thought of the island lying dark to the north — of Signe and Ida climbing the tower stairs at dusk with oil cans in their hands, keeping the lamp the way Felix had drilled into them, two girls holding back the dark over a channel of broken ice. She thought of the letter she had not yet sent declining the promotion south.

Tomorrow she would take the stand again if Rehn needed her. Tonight, walking back to her lodging through the blue cold, she allowed herself, for the first time since November, to believe it would hold — the testimony, the page, the whole fragile structure they had built across a frozen strait.

Then she reached her door and found the landlady waiting in the hall with a telegram in her hand and an odd look on her face.

"From the island," the woman said. "Came on the evening boat. It's marked urgent."

Ada tore it open beneath the hall lamp. Fourteen words, in a clerk's hurried hand, and the floor seemed to tilt gently beneath her as she read them.

She read them twice. Then she put on her coat again and went back out into the dark to find Halvard Rehn.
