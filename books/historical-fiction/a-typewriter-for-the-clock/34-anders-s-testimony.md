## 34. Anders's Testimony

The courthouse at Sundvik had been a granary once, and it still smelled of rye dust and old grain weevils no amount of whitewash could kill. Anders Eriksen sat on the hard bench at the back and counted the things he had lost in order of size: his boat, sold in October to men whose names he would not say aloud; his room above the tavern, surrendered when the tavern itself changed hands; his coat, which was not truly lost but had been pawned and re-pawned so many times that the broker now knew him better than his brother did. Last of all, smallest of all, hardest to carry: his name. Eriksen. It had meant something once, when Marta was alive and Felix still answered his letters.

Now it was spoken in this room only in connection with the word *accomplice*, or worse, *informer*, depending on who was doing the speaking.

The stove ticked. The magistrate's clerk sharpened pencils with the patience of a man paid by the hour. And at the front, behind the rail, Felix sat with his hands folded like a man waiting for a ferry, as though the charge against him — concealment of evidence, obstruction of a warrant, twenty years of silence bought and paid for — were nothing more than weather to be waited out. His hair had gone fully grey since the storm of the century. His knuckles were scabbed where the bare-handed night with the lamp had taken its tax. He did not look at Anders.

He had not looked at him since the day the constable came to the light with papers naming Anders as the source of the information that had put the warrant in Halvard Rehn's hand.

* * *

Anders had rehearsed this morning a hundred times on the walk down from the mainland, along the coast road where the ice was going rotten and black at the edges, breathing out his testimony into the cold air like smoke. He would be contrite but dignified. He would explain the debts without excusing them — no, he would excuse them, that was the whole point, that was the only thing left — he would make them understand what it was to owe money to men connected to the Voss heirs, men who held paper signed by lawyers in three towns, men who had come to him in November with a simple proposition framed as a kindness: *Your brother is sitting on something valuable, Anders. Something the family has missed for twenty years. Help us recover it and your accounts are settled.*

He had said yes. That was the fact underneath all the other facts, the stone at the bottom of the well. In a tavern in November, with beer he had not paid for sweating under his hand, he had told Elias Voss's agents that the typewriter existed, that it had never left the island, that it sat in a locked room above the oil locker in the keeper's house at Skarv Point. He had drawn them a map of the stairs. He had told them which window stuck.

And then Rehn had come north with a warrant instead of thieves, because the Voss family's money had decided that law was cheaper than burglary, and Anders had drunk himself blind for a week believing the matter closed, believing the machine would simply be seized and carried off and the debts marked paid and no one need ever know whose tongue had wagged.

But the ice had broken early. Felix had rowed. Ada Lindqvist had read the confession by lamplight and chosen conscience over commission. And now here they all were in a converted granary while a clerk sharpened pencils, and the debts were not paid after all, because the Voss agents had washed their hands of the whole affair the moment it became a matter of public record. Ruin, then, complete and total ruin, and nothing left to trade except the one thing he had withheld all along.

The truth about what the Voss family had done.

* * *

"Call Anders Eriksen."

His legs knew the walk from the bench to the stand even though the rest of him felt foreign in it. He swore the oath on a Bible that some previous clerk had probably won in a card game, and Halvard Rehn looked up at him from the magistrate's table with those young, unlined eyes that made Anders feel every one of his fifty-three years like a debt coming due.

"You are the brother of the accused," Rehn said. Not a question. The clerk wrote it anyway.

"I am."

"And you provided information to agents acting for the Voss estate regarding the location of certain property. A typewriter."

"I did." His voice came out steadier than he deserved. "I sold my brother's secret for the clearing of my debts. I'll not dress it up. I've dressed it up enough these twenty years to last me into the grave."

A murmur went through the benches. Somewhere behind him Signe sat — he could feel her the way you feel weather changing, a pressure at his back — and Ida beside her, and the islanders who had rowed over for the trial, forty souls in a room built for storing grain, all of them silent as stones.

Rehn let the murmur die. "You will have opportunity to speak to your own conduct," he said. "Today I want the other matter. You grew up on this coast. You worked, before your brother took the light, at the Voss estate."

"Three years. Stable hand, then grounds. 1868 to 1871."

"And you were present on the night of the fire?"

"I was present the evening of it. I'd gone home to my mother's before it started. Everyone says that, Magistrate, and everyone means it, and half of us are lying by omission." He heard his own voice drop into the register he used in taverns, the confessional register, cheap beer and true words. "So let me stop lying by omission, since that seems to be the fashion here."

The clerk's pencil stopped. Even the stove seemed to hold its breath.

"The night the Voss house burned, I saw Elias Voss riding away from the east road at a pace no man rides unless he's fleeing something or chasing it. I said nothing, because he was the heir and I was a stable boy and my mother needed the wage. Two days later, Torvald Sund was arrested for the arson. Torvald, who'd quarreled with old Herr Voss over wages the summer before, who everyone was happy to hang the story on because Torvald drank and Torvald had a temper and Torvald was poor." Anders's hands had closed into fists on the rail without his permission; he opened them deliberately, finger by finger, the way Felix opened a lock. "Torvald Sund confessed. I've since learned — we've all since learned, from the page in that machine there—" he nodded toward the typewriter, sitting on a side table under a glass cloche like a relic, which in fairness it was, "—that the confession was beaten out of him, or bought, or both. But I knew something even then, even at nineteen, that I have kept shut in me for twenty-four years like a bad tooth."

"State it plainly," Rehn said quietly.

"Elias Voss set the fire himself. For the insurance, and to be rid of a house that carried more memory than profit, and because old Herr Voss was dying and the estate would pass through probate cleaner as ashes than as timber. And the family knew. Not suspected — *knew*. Because I wasn't the only one who saw him on the east road. His own cousin saw him. And the family closed over it like water over a dropped coin, and they let Torvald Sund rot in a cell until he died there, and they spent the next two decades hunting one typewritten page because their son had been fool enough to dictate his sin into a machine." He turned, slowly, and looked at the Voss family's solicitors in their good black coats, three of them, arranged along the wall like crows on a fence. "You people have been trying to buy back a confession for twenty years. Well. Here's mine, free of charge."

* * *

The cross-examination lasted an hour and it was a slaughter, but not the slaughter the solicitors intended.

They called him a drunk. He agreed. They called him a debtor willing to sell anything — *you sold your brother's secret, Mr. Eriksen, why should this court believe you wouldn't sell a story?* — and he leaned into the rail and said, "Because the first sale bought me nothing but shame and a week of sobriety I didn't ask for, and this one buys me less. Ask yourself what a man gains by testifying to murder-by-arson against the family that holds his paper. Nothing. Nothing but the truth being on the record before I die, which is the one currency I've got left, and I'm spending all of it today."

They produced his tavern debts, itemized. He read them over the clerk's shoulder without flinching. "That's accurate," he said. "Add four crowns I owe the widow Nilsen for herring, she's too kind to dun me."

They brought out a letter from the Voss agents swearing Anders had fabricated the sighting of Elias on the east road in exchange for consideration. Anders asked to see it, held it a long time, and laughed — a short, ugly sound with no humor in it. "Gentlemen," he said to the court, "this letter is dated the twelfth of December. I met your agents on the ninth of November and again on the second of December, both times in the back room of the Anchor in Sundvik, both times witnessed, and I'll name the witnesses if the court wants them, though one of them has since gone to sea and the other owes you people money too, so draw your own conclusions. Your letter proves only that you can write. My testimony proves you can burn a man's name off a ledger the way your master burned a house off a hillside." He set the letter down. "Twenty-four years ago I chose my mother's wage. Twenty-two years ago I chose my silence. Two months ago I chose my debts. Every choice I ever made was the small one, the cheap one, the one that kept me fed. This is the first expensive choice of my life. Judge it how you like, but judge it *once*."

When he stepped down, his legs shook so badly he had to grip the bench end, and it was Felix's arm — Felix, who had not looked at him in six weeks — who caught his elbow and steered him to the seat without a word, and sat beside him, close, shoulder touching shoulder, the way they had stood together at their father's funeral thirty years ago.

Anders stared straight ahead at the magistrate's empty chair and let his eyes burn and did not wipe them, because wiping them would have meant admitting they were wet, and he had done enough admitting for one lifetime. But he let his shoulder stay against his brother's, and after a moment Felix's weight leaned fractionally back, and that was all the absolution there was, and all there would ever be, and Anders found — to his genuine surprise — that it was almost enough to live on.

* * *

They gave him a room that night in the courthouse annex, a clerk's cot with a blanket that smelled of mice. Through the thin wall he heard the solicitors arguing in low voices, and then the voices stopped altogether around ten o'clock, which was more telling than any argument. He lay awake listening to the river ice booming out in the dark, great tectonic groans as the thaw worked at it, the sound of a season dying.

Near midnight there was a knock.

He expected the constable. Instead it was Signe, wrapped in a shawl, holding a lantern, her face in the yellow light so exactly her mother's face that Anders's throat closed.

"I'm not allowed to be here," she said. "I don't care."

"No," he said. "You wouldn't."

She came in and stood by the door, not sitting, not softening. "I've spent six weeks hating you. I want you to know that I've decided to stop, and I want you to know that stopping doesn't mean forgiving. Those are different things. Papa taught me the difference without meaning to."

"That sounds like him."

"He wants to see you tomorrow. After the verdict. He wants—" She stopped, and the lantern trembled slightly, and for a moment she was sixteen again, the girl who used to ride on his shoulders at the summer market. "He wants to tell you about Mama. What she told him. All of it. He says you've earned the hearing you gave everyone else. His words. He's terrible at tenderness, our papa. It comes out sounding like a summons."

Anders laughed, and it cracked halfway through and became something else, and Signe crossed the little room and held the lantern while her uncle, who had sold everything else he owned, sat on a mouse-smelling cot and wept like a child in front of the one person whose forgiveness he wanted most and deserved least.

She did not comfort him. She stood very straight and let him do it, and when he was finished she said, "Tomorrow, when the verdict comes — whatever it is — Ida is going to type something. Papa promised her. First fresh page in twenty years." Her voice steadied. "I think it should have your name in it somewhere. Not as a villain. As a witness. There's a difference, and Ida understands differences better than anyone I've ever known. Think about whether you've earned it."

She left. The lantern light swayed away down the corridor, and Anders lay back on the cot and listened to the ice booming, and understood that tomorrow the river would open, and the family would go home across it, and whether there was a place for him on that crossing depended entirely on what a nineteen-year-old girl with a typewriter decided a coward's name was worth.

Outside, far downriver, the ice began — faintly, unmistakably — to crack.
