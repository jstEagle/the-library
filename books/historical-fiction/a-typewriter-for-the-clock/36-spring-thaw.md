## 36. Spring Thaw

The river opened the way a fist opens — slowly at first, a crack along the knuckles, then all at once, groaning and giving, the whole grey sheet of it shuddering into motion. Signe stood on the mainland dock and watched the ice go downstream in plates the size of church floors, tilting, colliding, dissolving. All winter she had thought of the river as a wall. Now she saw it for what it had always been: a road that simply refused to hold still.

"You'll want the morning boat," said the ferryman, who was already untying lines though the morning was an hour away. "Before the wind turns. It's kind now. It won't be kind by noon."

Nothing was kind by noon anymore. She had learned that much this winter.

Behind her, on the bench outside the magistrate's office where they had waited out the trial, Ida sat with their father's coat around her shoulders like a tent, her feet not quite reaching the boards. She was watching the ice too, but Signe suspected she was watching something else entirely — something upstream of the water, some thought moving beneath it. Ida had learned to keep her thoughts under surfaces this winter. They had all learned that. The difference was that now, when one of them surfaced, no one pretended not to see it.

"Signe." Ida's voice was small against the river's noise. "Do you think the lamp went dark? While we were gone?"

The question landed in Signe's chest like a stone dropped down a well. She had asked herself the same thing every night since they'd rowed away from the island behind Halvard Rehn's launch, her father's hands cuffed, his face turned back toward the light tower until the fog took it. Three weeks. Twenty-one nights. A lamp does not forgive neglect; her father had taught her that before she could write her own name. *A lamp is a promise you make to strangers,* he used to say, trimming the wick with those careful hands. *You will never meet them. You will never know what your light saved them from. You make the promise anyway.*

"No," Signe said, and heard how much she wanted it to be true in the shape of the word. "Ida and I kept it lit until the last night. And the wicks were trimmed. The oil was full. If it's dark now, it's only because no one has climbed the stairs."

"Then we climb them first," Ida said, with a fierceness that made Signe smile despite everything. "Before we even unpack. Before Papa even sits down."

"Papa isn't coming home yet."

She hadn't meant to say it so plainly. But the winter had burned the varnish off her tongue, and there it was: the plain wood underneath. Ida looked up at her, and Signe watched the girl do the arithmetic — the same arithmetic Signe herself had done a hundred times over coal and oil and lamp hours, tallying what remained, deciding what could be borne.

"He said he'd take the afternoon boat," Ida said carefully. "He said he'd stand on the dock at Vesterhavn and wait for us to come get him in the skiff."

"He did."

"And he'll come?"

Signe crouched so their eyes were level. This was the new discipline of her life, the one she was still learning: to answer questions honestly even when honesty cost something, especially then. Her whole childhood had been built on answers withheld — *where is Mama from, why don't we have cousins, why does Papa lock that room* — and she would not rebuild the house of silence just because the truth made her throat tight.

"He'll come," she said. "He gave his word to the magistrate, and whatever else our father is, Ida, he has never once broken his word. He'll serve out his reprimand, he'll sign the papers, and he'll take the afternoon boat. And we will be on the dock waiting, both of us, because that is what Eriksens do. We keep watch."

Ida nodded once, solemn as a small minister, and turned back to the river.

* * *

They loaded the boat themselves rather than wait for the ferryman's boy. There wasn't much: two carpetbags, a crate of books Rehn had pressed on Ida with the awkward formality of a man who didn't know how to give gifts, and — wrapped in oilcloth in the bottom of Signe's bag, wrapped the way her father wrapped things that mattered — the letter.

Not Marta's letters. Those were gone now, surrendered, read aloud in a courtroom while Signe sat with her hands pressed flat against her knees and listened to her mother's voice rise off the page in another woman's reading. What Signe carried instead was the verdict. A single typed page — Rehn had insisted on typing it himself, on the very machine, there in his chambers, his fingers moving over the keys with the reverence of a man touching a relic — declaring the conviction of Tomas Brandt vacated, the charges against the late Marta Eriksen née Voss formally withdrawn, her name entered into the record clean.

*Clean.* Signe had read the word forty times on the crossing and it still looked strange to her, like a word in a language she almost knew. Her mother's name had been dirty her entire life without Signe knowing it was dirty. Now it was clean, and her mother was dead, and there was no way to bring her the news except to carry it up the hill to the churchyard and say it aloud to a stone.

That was the arithmetic that would not balance, no matter how many times she ran the figures. Justice had arrived. Truth had won. And the person for whom it mattered most had been twenty years in the ground, denied every day of it.

"She'd have laughed," Ida said suddenly, as if reading the ledger over Signe's shoulder. They were mid-river now, the island rising ahead of them black and white, gulls wheeling off the point. "Mama Marta. In the stories you tell. She'd have thrown back her head and laughed at all of them — the magistrate, the warrant, the whole stupid court — and then she'd have made everyone coffee and told them they were being ridiculous."

Signe laughed, surprising herself, and the laugh cracked something loose in her chest that had been frozen since November. "She would have. She'd have called the typewriter 'that stubborn old hen' and threatened to sell it for kindling, and she'd have meant none of it."

"Did you love her?" Ida asked. Then, quickly, before Signe could answer: "You don't have to—"

"I do have to. That's the whole lesson, isn't it? We have to." Signe shipped the oars for a moment and let the boat drift, the current carrying them toward home the way rivers carry everything eventually. "I loved her the way you love someone whose sadness you can't explain. She was warm, Ida. Warmest person I ever knew. But there was a cold room inside her, a room with a locked door, and I used to think — I used to think the cold was me. That I'd done something. That if I were better, quieter, more helpful, she'd let me in." She looked at the island, closer now, the tower standing against the pale sky like a finger pointed at heaven. "It was never me. It was a fire and a lie and a name she couldn't say. I know that now. Knowing it doesn't stop it from hurting. But it stops it from being my fault, and I didn't know how badly I needed it to not be my fault until someone finally said it out loud."

Ida reached across the thwart and took her hand. Neither of them said anything else. The gulls cried, the ice chunks ticked against the hull, and the island came on.

* * *

The dock was exactly as they'd left it, which felt like a small miracle or a small accusation. The skiff was tied where Felix always tied it, bow line doubled, the knot his knot. The path up from the dock was drifted with old snow going grey and rotten at the edges, and the first green things were pushing through it — snowdrops, stubborn as sin, crowding the stones.

The house was cold. Of course it was cold; no one had banked the stove in three weeks. But it was *their* cold, the particular cold of a place that knows its people are coming back, and Signe got the fire going while Ida hauled water, and within an hour the kitchen smelled of woodsmoke and coffee and the specific, unrepeatable smell of home after absence, which is bread and dust and iron and salt all arguing with each other.

It was Ida who noticed the lamp.

Signe was at the window, looking up the hill toward the tower, when the girl went rigid at the table. "Signe. Signe, look. Look at the gallery rail."

Signe looked. Wrapped around the lantern gallery's railing, bright as a wound against the grey stone, hung a strip of cloth. Even at this distance she knew it — her father's red muffler, the one Marta had knitted him the first winter of their marriage, the one he wore every day of every winter and mended every spring, the wool gone soft as moss with age.

"He left it burning," Ida whispered. "No — he left it *tied*. He tied it there before they took him. As a signal."

"As a promise," Signe corrected, and her eyes were stinging. "A lamp is a promise you make to strangers. A scarf is a promise you make to family. He was telling us: I'm still here. I'm still keeping it. Come home."

They stood at the window together for a long time, neither moving, the fire popping behind them, until the kettle began its low whistle and broke the spell.

* * *

Felix came at dusk.

Signe saw the skiff first, a dark fleck on the brightening water, and then saw him at the oars — slower than she remembered, or perhaps she had only ever seen him through the forgiving lens of daughterhood — pulling steadily across the last open channel. She did not run down to the dock. Some part of her wanted to; some younger, pre-winter part of her wanted to fly down those steps and throw her arms around him and be a child again, forgiven everything, owed nothing.

But she was not a child, and he was not merely her father, and they had things between them now that could only be carried slowly, like furniture down narrow stairs.

So she walked. Ida, who had no such history to carry, did run — flew down the path with her coat flying, and Signe watched from the top step as the girl reached the dock before the skiff was even tied and stood there vibrating with the effort of waiting, and watched her father look up at her, and watched his face do something it had almost never done in Signe's living memory: break, openly, into tears.

By the time Signe reached the dock, Ida had already flung herself at him, and Felix had one arm around her and the other extended, and Signe took it, and for a moment the three of them stood on the wet planks in the failing light, holding on, saying nothing, because nothing needed saying that the holding didn't say better.

"You kept the light," he said at last, hoarse, looking past them up the hill at the tower.

"We kept the light," Signe said.

"The scarf—"

"Was a good signal. Papa. It was a very good signal." She squeezed his arm. "Though if you ever get arrested again, next time leave instructions."

His laugh came out cracked down the middle. "Your mother used to say the same thing about everything. *Leave instructions, Felix.*" He stopped. They all three stopped, the name hanging in the air between them — *your mother* — no longer a locked door but a door standing open onto a lit room.

"Come," Signe said gently. "There's coffee. And Ida has been rehearsing a speech about the state of the oil stores that she means to deliver whether we're ready or not."

"I have *not* been—" Ida began, pink-faced, and Felix laughed again, and they went up the hill together, three abreast, the way they would walk from now on. Not in file. Abreast.

* * *

Later, when the dishes were done and Ida had fallen asleep in the chair by the stove with a book sliding off her knee, Felix and Signe sat across from each other at the kitchen table, the lamp between them turned low, and performed the slow work of becoming honest with each other.

It was harder than Signe had expected. Honesty, she discovered, was like the river ice: it broke in cracks before it broke clean, and the cracking sounded like destruction even when it was release.

"I rehearsed apologies the whole way across," Felix said. His hands were wrapped around his coffee mug the way they wrapped around anything — completely, as if letting go might lose it. "On the boat, in the cell, at the trial. Whole speeches. I had one prepared for tonight that began with the circumstances of your mother's arrival and proceeded chronologically through every silence I ever kept." He shook his head. "And now you're here and I can't remember a word of any of them."

"Good," Signe said.

He looked up, startled.

"Good," she said again. "Because I don't want a speech, Papa. Speeches are finished things. They're typed pages — you read them and you're done. I want—" She searched for it, and found it, and said it plainly, because plainness was the discipline now. "I want the version where you tell me things as you remember them. Not all at once. Not even on purpose. Just — when something comes back to you, you say it. To me. Out loud. Even if it's ugly. Especially if it's ugly. Can you do that?"

Felix was quiet for a long moment. Outside, the wind had begun to rise off the channel, the first breath of the evening sea, familiar as a heartbeat.

"When you were six," he said finally, "you asked me why Mama never went to the mainland market days. Do you remember?"

"I remember you said she didn't like crowds."

"She'd already been recognized once by then. A man from the old country, a cousin of the Voss steward, at the fish stalls. He looked at her and she looked at him and neither of them said a word, and we came straight home, and she was sick for two days from fear, and I told you it was bad shellfish." His jaw worked. "That's the shape of it. That's the ugliness. Twenty years of little lies like that one, each one small enough to seem kind, and all of them together a wall higher than the tower."

Signe found, to her surprise, that she was not angry. Or rather — she was angry, she could feel it sitting in her chest like a banked coal, but it was not the only thing there, and it was not the biggest thing. The biggest thing was this: he was telling her. He was doing it right now, unprompted, unpainted. The wall was coming down brick by brick in front of her, and each brick was heavy, and he was lifting them anyway.

"Tell me about the day she came to the island," Signe said. "The real version. Start there."

And he did. He talked until the lamp burned low and the wind rose and fell, and Signe sat and listened and asked, and somewhere past midnight they ran out of story for one night — not out of truth, never that again, but out of night — and Felix banked the stove, and at the doorway he paused.

"There's one more thing," he said. "Not a confession. An invitation." He glanced toward the sleeping Ida, then back. "That machine in the locked room. Your mother's typewriter. I've kept it locked for twenty years because locking it was the only way I knew to keep her promise. But her promise was to hide the truth, and the truth is out now, free, walking around in daylight wearing its own name." His eyes were wet again, but steady. "It shouldn't stay locked. Nothing in this house should stay locked. Tomorrow — or soon — I want to open that door for good. And I want it to be Ida who sits down at it first."

"Why Ida?"

"Because she's been listening to it tick through that door her whole life," Felix said, "and because she offered to die for this family before anyone offered her a place in it, and because—" his voice caught, steadied— "because your mother would have adored her, and the machine should belong to whoever it would have loved best."

Signe looked at her father — this stubborn, silent, faithful man who had spent twenty years mistaking walls for shelter — and felt the last of the frozen places in her chest begin, at long last, to move.

"Tomorrow, then," she said. "We'll open it tomorrow."

Upstairs, in the room that had been locked longer than Ida had been alive, something seemed to wait — patient as a clock, ready as spring.
