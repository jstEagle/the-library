## 1. The Weight of Quiet Hands

The fog came in off the harbor at dawn the way debts come in on a diver's family—slowly, then all at once, until you couldn't see the water for what was owed against it. I watched it press itself against the office windows of the Halloway Trading Company like a beggar too proud to knock, and I typed.

I typed because typing is what they paid me eleven shillings a week to do, and because a girl who stops typing becomes visible, and visibility, for someone with my surname, is a slow way of drowning.

*Received of J. Merrick & Sons, forty casks salt cod, condition good—*

My fingers knew the keys better than they knew my own name. That was by design. On my first morning, nearly two years ago now, Mrs. Quill—the senior clerk, a woman built like a filing cabinet and about as warm—had told me that hands were the only honest part of a typist, and that everything above them ought to be kept out of the work. She'd meant it as instruction. I'd taken it as scripture. Somewhere between the invoices and the freight manifests, I had become nothing but a pair of hands attached to a plain gray dress, and I had worked very hard, every day since, to keep it that way.

Mirabel Dravenmoor did not exist in this building. In this building there was only *Miss Fen*, hired under a name borrowed from a woman who had died of fever before she could object, a girl from up the coast where nobody asked questions because everybody had answers they liked better. Miss Fen was quiet, competent, forgettable. Miss Fen had no father who had gone down into green water and come up ruined. Miss Fen had no brother hauling rope on the east docks where men were starting to talk. Miss Fen was a fiction I maintained with the discipline other women spent on their souls, and some mornings—and this was one of them—I woke tired before I'd even opened my eyes, tired in the marrow, from the sheer labor of not being anyone.

*—payment due on delivery, terms thirty days, countersigned Halloway Trading Co.*

The typewriter was a Remington No. 10, black as a beetle, and I loved it in the way you love anything that asks nothing of you but your hands. It didn't care whose daughter I was. It didn't care that I could read Greek, a little, from a tutor my father had hired back when pearls still paid for tutors. It wanted ink and rhythm and nothing else. When I sat down at it each morning I felt something close to honesty, which is more than I could say for the rest of my life.

The counting room filled around me by degrees. Mr. Pell arrived first, always, smelling of pipe smoke and grievance, followed by the junior clerks in a rustle of cheap wool and cheaper ambition. The radiators knocked like something trapped in the walls. Somewhere below us, in the warehouse that ran clear down to the water stairs, crates were being shifted on trolleys, and the sound came up through the floorboards as a dull percussion, the company's heartbeat: *goods moving, goods moving, goods.*

I did not look at the floor when it thumped. Looking is how people get caught.

"Miss Fen."

Mrs. Quill, materializing at my shoulder the way fog does, no footsteps involved. "Invoice forty-one for the *Corvina*'s charter provisions. Mr. Voss wants it before ten, and he wants it clean." A pause, thin as paper. "He's been complaining about smudges."

Mr. Voss complained about smudges the way a cat complains about birds—in theory, endlessly, and never to the bird's face. But you didn't say so. You said, "Yes, Mrs. Quill," in a voice sanded smooth of all texture, and you pulled the fresh sheet toward you, and you made your hands be perfect even while the rest of you counted the exits.

There were three. The front stair, the freight lift, and the window over the alley, which would drop you four feet onto a coal shed if you were desperate enough. I had catalogued these on my second day, without quite knowing why, the way a rabbit catalogues brambles. Some knowledge you carry just to prove you're still an animal underneath all the careful gray.

* * *

Ten o'clock came, and with it the letter for the *Corvina* provisions, and with *that* came Mr. Pell's habit of reading over shoulders, which was the closest thing he had to a hobby.

"Forty-one," he said behind me, not asking, announcing, the way he announced weather and the sins of the dockworkers' union. "That the one Voss wants?"

"It's being finished now, Mr. Pell."

"Mm." He leaned closer. I smelled the pipe smoke, the coffee gone cold on his desk, and beneath both something faintly briny that clung to everyone in this building eventually—the harbor got into you, into your clothes, your ledger books, your lungs. "You've quick hands, Miss Fen. Quick and clean. Funny thing." He said *funny thing* the way a man says it when he has already decided the thing is funny and is merely waiting for you to appreciate the joke. "We had a typist before you. Girl named Ada. Slow as Christmas, but her mother used to bring us oysters from the beds off Crowsneck. Knew all the diving families, Ada did. Used to tell me which ones to trust with credit."

The keys clicked under my fingers. Clicked evenly. I will say this for fear, properly trained: it makes you precise.

"Crowsneck's mostly gone now," Pell went on, conversational, terrible. "Diving families scattered after that business with the accident. The Dravenmoor fellow. You'd have been a child. Big scandal at the time—man crippled at the bottom of a pearl bed, company left holding his debts. His kids went somewhere. Boy's on the docks somewhere, I hear. Girl—" He waved a hand, smoke trailing. "Vanished. Or married. They usually marry, or vanish. One or the other."

*Payment due on delivery,* I typed. *Terms thirty days.* My heart was going like the trolley wheels downstairs, but my hands—my hands were Mrs. Quill's honest part, and they did not betray me. I finished the line, rolled the platen, and looked up at him with the face I kept for exactly these occasions: mild, slightly dim, politely interested in nothing.

"I wouldn't know about any of that, Mr. Pell."

"No," he agreed, and smiled, and something in the smile had hooks in it. "No, I suppose you wouldn't."

He moved off toward the radiators to stand and warm his grievance. I let out a breath through my nose, slow, controlled, the breath of a woman who had merely been bored. Inside, where nobody could audit it, I was sixteen again, standing in a doorway watching men carry my father up a beach like salvage, and I understood—with the sick clarity that visits me perhaps once a season and always uninvited—that Mr. Pell had not been making conversation at all.

Pell didn't make conversation. Pell fished. And he had just run his line through the water where I lived to see what rose.

* * *

At half past twelve I took my dinner hour and walked, as I always walked, the long way round—not toward the docks, where my blood walked openly, but up along Merchant Row, past the chandlery windows hung with rope and lantern glass, letting the fog scour my face. Two years of practice had taught me the grammar of invisibility. Walk at the pace of errands, not escape. Meet no one's eyes, but don't dodge them either—a person who dodges eyes is worth remembering. Wear the same gray coat until it becomes furniture. Be furniture, Mirabel. Furniture is never questioned, never searched, never sent away.

It was exhausting. That's the part no one tells you about vanishing. Everyone imagines hiding is restful, a kind of sleep. It isn't. It's a performance with no interval, a role played for an audience that must never once suspect an audience exists. I held my face in place the way divers hold their breath, and some nights I lay in my rented room above the laundry on Tanner Street and felt my cheeks ache from the effort of having been no one all day.

And yet. I won't pretend there wasn't a comfort in it, either, small and shameful as stolen bread. Because the girl I had been—the Dravenmoor girl, the diver's daughter with her dead mother's quick tongue and her father's stubborn chin—that girl had a name that men said in counting rooms in the tone Mr. Pell had just used. That girl owed debts contracted before she could sign her own name. That girl's father sat in a room above the chandlery with legs that no longer answered him and a pride that answered for nothing else. To be Miss Fen was to put all that weight down, six days a week, eleven shillings at a time. To be Miss Fen was to walk through the very building that had broken my family, unseen, unaccounted, close enough to touch its ledgers and far enough to be safe.

Safe. I turned the word over as I walked, testing it the way my father used to test pearls against his tooth. It had a crack in it somewhere. It had had a crack in it for weeks, ever since the dockside talk started reaching even me, filtered through the laundress who took in my washing: men asking questions about Dravenmoors, questions with a company stamp on them somewhere, questions that were not idle.

But the fog was thick, and the fog is generous, and I let it tell me I was safe a little longer.

* * *

Back at my desk by two, I found the afternoon waiting like an unpaid bill. Invoices, a freight abstract, a letter to a shipping agent in Portsmouth that I composed in my head twice before typing because good letters are architecture and bad ones are rubble. Mrs. Quill passed and paused—one beat, two—reading the page upside down with eyes that missed nothing in this world except kindness, then moved on. Pell smoked. The radiators knocked. Below us, the heartbeat: *goods moving, goods moving.*

At a quarter to five, the door to the inner offices opened, and the temperature of the room changed.

I didn't look up. Looking up is a reflex; reflexes are how people get caught. But I registered him the way you register a change in current—Mr. Voss, the secretary to the directors, coming through the clerks' room on his evening circuit. He was a tall man built on long lines, gray at the temples in a way that looked arranged rather than earned, and he had a voice like a well-made lock: precise, expensive, and designed to keep things in. The junior clerks developed sudden industry. Even Pell straightened.

Voss crossed the room unhurried, pausing here and there to turn a page on someone's desk with one finger, and stopped—of course—at mine.

"Invoice forty-one," he said.

"Typed and filed, sir. Copy on your desk at four."

Something flickered behind his eyes—surprise, maybe, or its cousin, interest. Men like Voss are rarely surprised; it's the one luxury they deny themselves. "On time," he said. "Clean, Mrs. Quill reports." He picked up the carbon copy I'd set aside, read it at a glance the way he seemed to do everything, and set it down again, aligning its edge with the desk's edge, precisely, a small violence of tidiness. "You've been with us two years, Miss Fen."

"Two years in March, sir."

"And in two years I don't believe I've heard you express an opinion." He said it without malice, which somehow made it worse—the tone of a man noting inventory. "In my experience, employees who express no opinions are either very wise or very afraid. Occasionally both." He straightened his cuffs. "The directors have correspondence coming that requires discretion. Quiet hands and no opinions." He was already turning away. "Mrs. Quill will brief you tomorrow. Eight sharp."

"Yes, sir."

He paused at the door, half-turned, and looked back at me—one long moment, the fog-gray light from the windows lying flat across his face so I couldn't read what moved behind it. Then he left, and the room exhaled, and Pell lit his pipe with the air of a man restored to his natural element.

I sat very still among my tidy papers.

Discretion. Correspondence requiring it. Quiet hands and no opinions. Any other clerk would have heard a compliment, or a chore. I heard the fog shift. I heard the crack in the word *safe* widen by a hair's breadth, the way ice sounds before it gives.

Because I knew what correspondence required discretion at Halloway Trading Company. Everyone on the docks knew, in the wordless way the poor know things they can't afford to say: there were crates that went down to the water stairs at night and pearls that came ashore that no bed had grown. We had all agreed not to know it, the whole waterfront, a conspiracy of survival. But my family had paid for that traffic once already—paid in my father's spine, paid in a debt that outlived my mother, paid in a name I wasn't allowed to speak in this building. And now the building wanted my hands on its secret paper.

I gathered my shawl. Around me the clerks drained out into the evening, and the gaslights came up one by one, and downstairs the trolleys kept their dull time—*goods moving, goods moving*—like a heart that didn't know yet it was about to be stopped.

Eight sharp, then. Quiet hands.

I locked my desk, aligned the key with the drawer's edge out of a habit I suddenly recognized as his, and went down into the fog, carrying the day home like contraband.
