## 2. Salt in the Ledger

There is a particular arithmetic to salt. It gets into everything, given time—into wood, into wool, into the seams of a family's history—and once it's in, no amount of boiling or weeping or scrubbing will draw it back out. You can only learn to live with the taste.

I was thinking about this on a Tuesday morning in March, because the fog had come in off the harbor thick enough to chew, and the whole of the Halloway Trading Company's counting room smelled of it: the damp wool coats drying on their pegs, the clerks' boots leaving dark crescents on the floorboards, the very paper itself curling at the edges like something trying to escape. Salt in the ledger. My father used to say a ledger kept near the sea always went soft in the end, the numbers bleeding into one another until nobody could say what was owed by whom.

He said it the way he said most things—loudly, and to no one in particular, while my mother rolled her eyes and Tobin and I pretended not to listen.

I entered figures. That was my whole employment, if you could call it that: I entered figures into columns, and I did it neatly, and I did it fast, and I did it in a hand so deliberately unremarkable that it might have belonged to anyone. Mirabel Dravenmoor could not write a sentence without her father's cadence showing in it, could not form an M without the flourish he'd taught her on a slate when she was five. But *M. Dray*—that was another creature entirely. M. Dray wrote like a person who had never been taught anything worth keeping. M. Dray pressed no flourishes. M. Dray was a small, gray, useful person, and the Halloway Trading Company collected small gray useful people the way a barnacle collects on a hull.

"Dray." Mr. Quill, the head clerk, stood at my desk with the particular expression of a man who has found a discrepancy and intends to enjoy it. "The Corvina manifest. Page nine. Your totals don't reconcile."

I looked at page nine. The totals reconciled perfectly. I had checked them twice, once forward and once backward, because that is the discipline of a person who cannot afford to be interesting.

"They do, sir," I said. "If you carry the pearl-weight column into the third decimal."

A pause. Quill's mustache performed a small maneuver, like a caterpillar reconsidering its route. He carried the column. The totals reconciled. He did not apologize, because head clerks do not apologize to temporary copyists, and he did not quite leave, either. He lingered, looking at me the way a man looks at a clock he suspects of running fast.

"Where did you learn arithmetic like that?" he asked.

"Church," I said. "Counting the collection."

It was a good lie. It had the twin virtues of being dull and slightly pious, and Quill, who trusted nothing so much as dullness and piety, nodded and moved on down the row of desks, hunting for smaller discrepancies to devour.

I let out my breath slowly and turned to the window, where the fog was doing its slow work on the harbor, erasing the masts one by one as though the ships had never existed at all. And that, of course, is the trouble with fog. It doesn't only hide things. It reminds you of everything it's hiding.

* * *

My father dove for pearls for thirty-one years.

I want you to understand what that means, because the word *diver* has a romantic ring to it in the drawing rooms of this city, all sunlit water and gleaming shells. The reality is a man in a canvas suit and a copper helmet, walking on the bottom of the bay in the dark, hauling a bag of oysters while the air hose above him hisses and the cold presses in on his chest like a hand. The reality is that for every hundred oysters, perhaps one holds a pearl worth the trouble, and the company that owns the beds owns the diver too—owns his boat, his air, his house, and, by the slow magic of debt, his children's futures.

My father was good at it. That was the tragedy of him, or one of them. He could read the bottom of the bay the way I read a ledger—every bar and channel, every bed of shells, every place the current had dropped something worth finding. The other divers called him the Compass, because he could come up heavy-laden from water where everyone else came up empty, and because, my mother said, he was too proud to ask directions from anyone, God included.

The debt began before I could count, which is to say it began the way most debts begin: with a boat, a bad season, and a company man with soft hands and a soft voice who explained, very kindly, that Halloway Trading was prepared to *help*. The interest was never discussed aloud. It didn't need to be. It lived in the ledger the way salt lives in wood, and every year my father dove harder and the number stayed exactly where it was, or moved a little higher, like a tide that only came in.

I was nine the year my mother sat me down at the kitchen table and taught me to keep accounts. Not schoolwork—she had no patience for the schoolmaster's long division—but real arithmetic: what we owed, what we earned, what the difference was, and how many years of my father's lungs it would take to close the gap. She did this without bitterness, in the same voice she used for teaching me bread, and at the end she said the sentence that has governed my life since:

"A number can't be argued with, Mirabel. Learn to make them, and no one can make you small."

She was wrong about that, as it happens. But it took me eleven years to find out, and I loved her too much, in that moment, to doubt her.

* * *

The accident came in the autumn I was fourteen. I have told the story so many times in my head that it has worn smooth, like a stone carried in a pocket, but I will set it down here as straight as I can, because everything that followed—the office, the false name, the fog I have lived in for six years—came out of one afternoon in October.

There was a storm two days before. Not a great one, but enough to shift the silt, and my father—being the Compass, being too proud to wait for the water to settle—went down the next morning to check his best bed before the company's tally-men could reach it. The theory was that if he could mark his claim first, the season's take would count against the debt before the company could shuffle it elsewhere. My mother begged him to wait. Tobin, who was twelve and already built like a bollard, begged harder. My father laughed, kissed her once on the forehead, and said that the sea had fed him his whole life and he wasn't going to insult it by being late to a meal.

The air hose snagged on a spar of the *Adelaide*, a wreck none of the charts admitted to, down in the channel where the storm had scoured everything loose.

He was down forty minutes. The men on the boat said afterward that the hose went slack at the twenty-minute mark and that they pulled, and pulled, and the line only cut deeper into whatever had hold of it. My father, in his helmet, in the dark, with the pressure of forty feet of water on his chest, understood before anyone above him did what had happened. And here is the part I cannot make smooth no matter how I wear it: he did not panic. He later told us—his voice ruined, a rasp like a file on tin—that he had knelt on the bottom of the bay, in the black, with his air running out, and he had thought, with perfect clarity: *I have a ledger at home, and my wife can read it, and my daughter can make it lie if she has to.*

He got the knife out. He sawed through his own air hose in the dark, on the bottom of the bay, and walked—walked—up the anchor line on the last of his breath.

They pulled him into the boat with his lungs half-full of the bay. He lived. I want to be clear about that, because the story would be cleaner if he hadn't, and my family has never once been permitted a clean story. He lived, but the sea took its toll for the rescue: the pressure had wrecked him somewhere inside, in the places a doctor can point to but not mend, and the Compass, who had walked the bottom of the bay for thirty-one years, would never dive again.

The company's response was prompt and Christian. Mr. Voss—though I did not know his name then, only the soft-voiced man with soft hands who came to the house—expressed the directors' sympathy, noted that the accident had occurred on company time in company waters, and then, with genuine regret, presented my mother with a revised ledger. The rescue had cost wages. The boat had been idle. The bed had gone untended. And the *Adelaide*, it turned out, had been salvaged under contract years ago; my father had no business being near her, and the company could pursue the matter of the damaged hose if it were feeling unkind.

The number at the bottom of the page had grown. It had nearly doubled.

My mother read it through twice. I watched her do it, standing in the doorway with Tobin's hand crushing mine, and I watched her arrive at the understanding that the debt was not a debt anymore but a leash, and that Halloway Trading intended to keep the whole Dravenmoor family on it until the last of us was dead or useful.

Then she did something I did not understand until years later. She looked up at the soft-voiced man and said, "The girl reads and figures. Better than any clerk you've got. You want value out of us—there's your value. Put her in the office and take it out of what we owe."

The man's eyes came to me for the first time. I was fourteen, standing in my dead-season dress, with salt-stiff hair and my father's stubborn jaw, and I watched this man weigh me like cargo.

"She'll need a name that doesn't smell of the beds," he said.

My mother didn't flinch. "Give her one, then."

* * *

So that is how M. Dray came to sit at a desk by the window in the Halloway counting room, six years and one fog-bound Tuesday later, entering figures that reconciled to the third decimal and being thanked for it with a grunt.

I want to say I hated it. The truth is more complicated and less flattering: I was *good* at it. There is a real satisfaction, for a certain kind of mind, in a column that comes out even, in a ledger where every number sits where it belongs and says what it means. My father had the bottom of the bay; I had page nine of the Corvina manifest. We were both of us reading water, in our way, and I had come to understand that grief doesn't evaporate. It settles. It finds the low places in a person and pools there, and if you're careful—if you're very careful—you can pour your whole self into the work and let the work hold the water so nobody sees it sloshing.

Careful. That was my word. I was careful about the name on the payroll, careful about the accent I flattened in the office, careful about the ring I never wore and the stories I never told and the church I claimed to attend. Careful as a diver checking his hose. Because I had learned the other lesson of my father's accident, the one my mother never spoke aloud: the sea doesn't care how good you are. One snag, one slack line, one moment of inattention, and everything you are goes into the dark. So I checked my hose every morning. I checked my name, my hands, my face, my voice. I checked them the way other people pray.

And it worked. For six years it worked. The debt came down—slowly, grudgingly, a shilling at a time, but it came down—and Quill grunted at my columns, and the soft-voiced man, whose name I had by then learned was Voss and whose office sat at the end of the hall like a cold room in a warm house, forgot I existed, which was the highest compliment the Halloway Trading Company paid to anyone.

On that particular Tuesday, I finished the Corvina manifest by half past four and sat for a moment with my hands flat on the desk, listening to the foghorn out in the harbor making its slow, patient complaint, and I permitted myself the day's single luxury: the thought that we might be free of it inside two years. The debt, the leash, the false name. Two more years of careful, and then Mirabel Dravenmoor could walk out of the fog and be somebody again.

The thought had barely finished forming when the door at the end of the hall opened, and the cold came out of Voss's office and walked down the row of desks like a draft with a purpose.

It stopped at my desk.

"Dray." Voss did not use Quill's grunting economy; he used your name the way a man lays a card face-up. "You write a fair hand, I'm told. And you can keep your mouth shut, which is rarer." He set a sealed envelope on my ledger, square and heavy, wax the color of dried blood. "Tomorrow morning, first thing. My office. There's a letter wants typing, and it wants typing by someone who isn't curious."

I looked at the envelope. I looked at him. Somewhere out in the fog, the horn sounded again, patient as tide.

"I'm not curious, sir," I said.

"No," said Voss, and something moved behind his eyes that was not quite a smile. "But you will be."

He walked back down the hall and closed his door, and I sat there with the envelope in front of me, feeling the way my father must have felt at the twenty-minute mark—when the line goes slack, and you understand that something below has shifted, and the water you trusted is not the water you thought it was.

I should have been afraid. I was. But underneath the fear, in the low place where the grief had pooled for six years, something else stirred—something my mother had put there at a kitchen table, in a voice like bread.

*A number can't be argued with.*

Tomorrow I would learn what number they had made of my family.
