## 3. The Sealed Letter

Mr. Voss did not knock. This was a fact I had catalogued in my first week at Halloway Trading Company and never once mentioned aloud, because mentioning it would have required admitting I noticed things about Mr. Voss, and noticing things about Mr. Voss was the occupational hazard of anyone who wished to remain employed by Mr. Voss.

He simply appeared in the doorway of the typing room on Thursday morning with an envelope in one hand and the particular stillness of a man carrying something heavier than paper.

"Miss Fen." My borrowed name. He said it the way he said all names—as if reading them off an invoice he had already decided to dispute. "You'll clear your desk."

"Of course, sir." I set down my pen mid-word, leaving the *g* of *goods received* unfinished. Somewhere in the ledger below me there was now a word with no ending, which felt appropriate.

"Not the desk," he said, watching me stack papers I hadn't been asked to stack. "The surface. Everything off it. You'll work on this alone, and when you're finished you'll bring it to me yourself, sealed, and you'll speak of it to no one. Not the other clerks. Not the boy who runs the coal. Not your—" a pause, thin as a knife laid flat—"*family*, should any exist."

"I have no family, sir."

It was the correct answer. It was the answer I had given for six years, in shops and offices and once, memorably, to a charity sister who had looked at my hands and asked where my people were. I have no family. Four words, worn smooth as sea glass from handling.

Voss studied me for a moment longer than the sentence deserved. Then he placed the envelope on the bare wood of my desk and left without another syllable, his footsteps receding down the hall with the even patience of a tide coming in over something it intended to drown.

* * *

The envelope was thick stock, cream-colored, sealed with black wax rather than the company's usual red. Black wax was for correspondence that would be denied later. I knew this the way I knew the weight of wet rope—by inheritance, by the six years I'd spent learning which documents a trading company treated as permanent and which it treated as provisional truths, to be adjusted when the wind changed.

I broke the seal with my thumbnail and slid out a single sheet covered in Voss's hand—small, upright, ruthless, each letter standing at attention like a soldier who had shot a friend for desertion. There were crossings-out. That alone told me this letter mattered; Voss did not cross out words he was certain of.

The instructions at the top read: *To be typed on the good machine. Original and two carbons. No errors will be tolerated.*

No errors will be tolerated. In eleven years of watching men write things down, I had learned that this phrase meant one of two things: either the document was trivial and the writer was pompous, or the document was a weapon and every comma would someday be examined by someone with power and a grudge. I weighed the cream stock between my fingers. It was not trivial.

I rolled a fresh sheet into the Remington—the good machine, the one kept dusted and oiled and used only for letters that went to men whose names began with Sir—and I began to read.

*To the Underwriters' Syndicate, attention of Mr. Calloway—*

My hands were steady. I want that recorded, even if only here, in the privacy of my own skull: for the first four lines they were perfectly steady, because the first four lines were only pleasantries, and terror takes a moment to recognize its own name.

Then I reached the fifth line, and my father's surname sat there in Voss's small upright hand like a fish pulled gasping onto deck.

*...irregularities in the handling of goods consigned through the Dravenmoor operation, wherein certain pearl shipments entered the company's books bearing marks inconsistent with legitimate salvage...*

The room did not spin. I had expected, in whatever part of me expected anything, that discovery would come with drama—that I would cry out, or faint, or drop the sheet and have to scramble for it while some passing clerk saw too much. None of that happened. The clock on the wall ticked. The radiator knocked twice, as it always did at ten minutes past nine. Outside the fogged window a gull screamed at the harbor as though the harbor owed it money.

And I sat very still with my father's name on a page in front of me, in a letter accusing him of smuggling, written by the company that owned his debt.

* * *

Let me tell you what I know about the Dravenmoor operation, since apparently it has an operation now, with the definite article and everything, as though we were a firm and not a man with ruined lungs, a son hauling crates, and a daughter pretending to be nobody.

My father dove for pearls for nineteen years. Not the romantic kind of diving—not the postcards with the graceful brown boys and the sunlit water. The real kind: a stone weight and a rope and a lungful of air bought at twenty minutes' interest, down among the cold and the dark where the oysters grow in beds like graveyards. He was good at it. He was so good at it that when Halloway's own divers kept coming up empty in the years after the big storms shifted the beds, it was Dravenmoor they sent for, Dravenmoor whose catches filled the company ledgers season after season, Dravenmoor whose name the other divers trusted enough to follow out past the point where the charts went vague.

And then one morning the line went slack.

I was fourteen. I have written elsewhere—I have written nowhere, actually, I have only turned it over in the dark like a stone with a cold side—but I was fourteen, and I remember my mother's face when the boat came back with half its crew and all of its silence. The rope had snagged. The weight had pinned him against a shelf of rock in water deep enough to kill a lesser man outright, and he had hung there for six minutes doing arithmetic with his remaining air, and he had lived, and the living had cost him the use of his chest. A man who cannot expand his lungs cannot dive. A diver who cannot dive, in a company town, is a debt with a heartbeat.

The debt was Halloway's gift. The doctor, the boat, the weeks of care—all of it entered neatly into a column against our name, compounding at interest rates that would have embarrassed a loan shark with a conscience. My mother signed what she signed. I know what she signed, because I have spent six years working two desks away from the ledger where it lives, and I have read it more times than I have ever admitted to anyone, including God.

Item: medical attendance, E. Dravenmoor. Item: loss of vessel hire. Item: advance against future salvage, unrecovered.

And then, in my mother's hand, witnessed by a company clerk who is now a director: *Mirabel Dravenmoor, aged 15, placed in service to Halloway Trading Company as compensation in part.*

In service. As though I were a spoon.

She had bargained them down, my mother. From the workhouse to the office, from the docks to the typing room, and she had done it with nothing but her voice and her refusal to weep until she got home. She put me behind a desk because a girl behind a desk could learn how the machine worked. And then she died before she could see whether the learning had been worth the price, which was, I think, the single most tactless thing she ever did.

So yes. I know the Dravenmoor operation intimately. I am, in point of fact, its most literate asset.

* * *

I read the letter through three times before I touched the keys. This is not piety; it is procedure. You do not type a document like this the way you type an invoice. An invoice forgives. A weapon does not.

The letter ran to nearly two pages. It named the Dravenmoor operation in the fifth line and again in the ninth, and by the second page it had grown specific: crates, dates, consignment numbers, a route through the northern quays that I recognized because I had typed the invoices for that route myself, months ago, believing them ordinary. Pearls received. Pearls shipped. Weights that had seemed unremarkable then and now rearranged themselves in my memory like letters in a cipher, spelling something I had been too well-trained to look at.

Here is what the letter did not say, what it danced around with the grace of a man stepping between puddles: where those pearls had gone, and who had signed for their going. The letter offered irregularities. It offered the Dravenmoor name, attached like a label to a crate already nailed shut. It offered everything except the signature at the top of the route—which, I happened to know, because I had typed the routing slips, belonged to Halloway Trading Company itself.

They were not reporting a theft. They were filing an alibi.

I sat with my hands in my lap and understood, in the slow cold way one understands ice forming, that my family had not merely been robbed. We had been selected. A company drowning in debts it could not show its underwriters does not confess; it finds a weight to throw overboard, and it prefers its weights to be poor, and indebted, and named in its own books. My father's name was already in their ledger. All that remained was to move it from the credit column to the criminal one.

And I was to type it. With my own trained hands. On the good machine.

There is a species of humiliation so complete that it passes through humiliation and comes out the other side as something almost like clarity. I sat in that little room with the radiator knocking and the fog pressed against the glass, and I thought: they chose me. Of all the clerks in this building, they chose the Dravenmoor daughter to type the document that hangs the Dravenmoors. Either Voss didn't know—possible, but Voss struck me as a man who made it his business to know what people were hiding, the way other men made it their business to know the tides—or he knew exactly, and this was the joke, and somewhere beneath his grey composure he was enjoying it.

I have wondered since which it was. I no longer believe it matters. A knife cuts the same whether the hand holding it hates you or has simply never considered you a person.

* * *

My hands shook. I record this too, because the shaking matters more than the steadiness—it matters because I watched it happen and did not stop working.

It began in the fingers, a fine tremor, the kind you get from cold or from holding something back. By the time I had rolled in the original sheet and positioned the carbons, it had climbed to the wrists. When I set my fingers on the home row, the keys chattered softly under my touch like teeth.

I thought of my father's hands. I thought of them specifically, and deliberately, the way you press a thumb into a wound to keep yourself awake. Those hands had held steady at forty feet in green-black water with his chest crushed against stone, because if they shook he died, and he had a family, and so they did not shake. Six minutes. The doctor said most men would have torn loose and drowned in ninety seconds. My father hung in the cold and counted and stayed alive because staying alive was the task and his hands were the instruments of it, and instruments do not tremble; they function or they fail.

Mine were trembling over a typewriter in a heated room. Whatever else was true, this much was not in dispute: the water I was in was warmer than his.

I breathed the way he had taught Tobin to breathe—slow in through the nose, hold, slow out—though Tobin had learned it for diving and I was using it for fraud-adjacent stenography, and Father would have found the application either hilarious or damning. Probably both. My father's laughter always had a verdict hidden in it.

Then I typed.

I will not pretend it was easy. The first line came out clean. The second line I mistyped—*consignments* losing its second *n*—and the error hit me like a slap, because no errors will be tolerated, and I pulled the carriage and corrected it with the little roller Voss permitted for exactly this purpose and no other, and my heart hammered so hard I could hear it in the platen. The third paragraph named my father. I typed the word *Dravenmoor* and the key struck the ribbon and the ribbon struck the paper and there it was, my blood, my name, rendered in perfect even capitals by a machine that did not know and could not care what it was killing.

That is the thing about a typewriter, which I have loved and hated in equal measure for six years: it is honest about everything except consequence. It will render any lie in beautiful type. It will produce treason and invoices with the same tidy efficiency, the same soft mechanical applause, *clack-clack-ding*. The machine does not tremble. Only the hands do. And the hands, if they are disciplined, can go on striking while the rest of the person drowns.

Paragraph by paragraph, I drowned and struck. The irregularities. The marks inconsistent with legitimate salvage. The recommendation that the Syndicate withhold settlement pending investigation, pending prosecution, pending the quiet destruction of everyone who shared my surname. Two pages. Two carbons. I changed the ribbon-position lever at the proper moments, crisp copy, first carbon, second carbon, the way I had been drilled. Somewhere around the middle of the second page my hands stopped shaking—not because the fear left, but because it sank below the waterline, the way heavy things do, and continued dragging at me from underneath where no one could see.

When I finished, I drew out the sheets and read the whole thing once more, comparing it line against line against Voss's hand. Perfect. Every word, every date, every number, exactly as dictated. No errors tolerated; none committed. I had produced a flawless instrument of my family's ruin, executed with the skill of a professional and the obedience of a ghost.

*I have no family,* I had told him. The letter in my hands said otherwise. The letter said I had a family worth framing, which was, in the economy of that building, a kind of promotion.

* * *

I folded the originals together, squared the edges, and sat for a moment with them under my palms, feeling the paper warm slightly from my skin. Through the fogged window the harbor was a grey smear with masts sticking out of it like splinters. Somewhere out there Tobin was probably loading crates off a lighter, laughing at something, unaware that his name—our name—was at this moment travelling across a desk toward men who could end him with a signature.

Tobin. Who still said *Dravenmoor* out loud, stubbornly, whenever anyone asked, because he had decided long ago that hiding was a tax paid by cowards and he'd rather pay in knuckles. Who had a wife now, Nessa, round as a moon and due within the month, and a mouth that got him warned by foremen at least twice a season.

If this letter reached the Syndicate and then the port authorities, it would not stop at my father. It would sweep up Tobin like driftwood after a wreck. And my father—my father would stand in the doorway of our rooms with his ruined chest and his unbowed head and refuse to run, because the sea gave him his living and his name, and he would say it exactly that way, in exactly those words, and the water he trusted would close over him one final time.

All of that was downstream of the envelope in my hands.

I sealed it with the wax stick and the company stamp, pressing the red Halloway seal into the black—a small irony I doubt anyone else would notice or appreciate—and carried it down the hall. Voss's door was shut. I knocked, once, and heard his voice say *enter*, and went in, and laid the envelope on his blotter the way you lay down a card you already know loses.

He glanced at it, then at me. For one moment—one measured, terrible moment—he looked at me the way a jeweler looks at a stone he is deciding whether to cut or discard.

"No difficulties?" he said.

"None, sir. No errors."

"Good." He drew the envelope toward him and dismissed me with a nod, already turning to other papers, and I walked out of that office with my spine straight and my pulse going like a diver's at depth.

Back in the typing room, the wastebasket stood beside my desk where it always stood. And in it, crumpled small, lay the sheet Voss had drafted from—the original of his own hand, with its crossings-out, the thing he had carried in and left behind without a thought for the clerk who emptied baskets.

A careful man would have burned it. But then, a careful man would not have chosen me.

I did not reach for it. Not yet. The corridor had eyes, and morning was the wrong hour, and six years of invisibility had taught me that a stolen document is only useful to someone who is still free to steal the next one.

But I knew it was there. And I knew that tomorrow, when the office emptied for the lunch hour and the fog came in off the water to blind the windows, I would kneel down beside that basket and smooth out every crossed-out line—and find out precisely what Mr. Voss had decided to hide between his drafts.

Because a number can't be argued with. And I intended to count everything.
