## 4. Brother of the Tide

The gulls woke me before the bell did, which was an insult on two counts: first because a man ought to be allowed his dreams, and second because I'd been dreaming of my wife's cooking, and no dream should be evicted by a bird with the manners of a debt collector.

I lay in the dark of our room above the cooper's shop and listened to Sera breathe beside me, one hand resting on the small round hill of her belly where our child was conducting whatever business children conduct before they're born. Five months along now. The midwife said the baby was strong. Sera said the baby kicked hardest whenever I ate, which she took as evidence of character.

Then I got up, dressed in the dark by feel—trousers, shirt, boots, the wool cap that smelled permanently of tar and herring—and went down into the fog.

That's the thing about this city nobody tells you until you've lived here a winter: the fog doesn't lift so much as it takes turns. It lifts off the water and settles over the streets, then trades back again around noon like two merchants swapping goods neither of them wants. By the time I reached Pier Nine the fog had decided to stay at sea level, and the cranes came down out of it in pieces—a hook here, a cable there—as if the harbor were being assembled fresh each morning by some patient, invisible hand.

I liked that idea. I still do, most mornings. It's easier than the truth, which is that the harbor doesn't get built by anybody; it just *is*, the way the tide is, the way debt is, the way the Halloway Trading Company is. Things that were here before you and will be here after, grinding along regardless of whether you eat.

* * *

My work is cargo. Not the fine cargo—the pearls and silks that go up the company stairs in locked boxes—but the honest weight: salt pork in barrels, timber, coal, rope, the thousand dull tons that keep a port alive while the pretty things make other men rich. I'm a stevedore, which is a word meaning I carry things, and I've carried them since I was fourteen years old, when my father's lungs gave out on him and the sea stopped paying our family and started collecting instead.

You'd think a man would resent the water for that. My father never did. He talks about diving the way priests talk about God—with love and grievance mixed so thoroughly you can't separate them without killing both. He lost his hearing in the left ear, half the strength in his right arm, and every shilling we had, down there in the cold green dark. And if you put a boat under him tomorrow he'd go out again, pride being the one cargo he refuses to set down.

Me, I hold no grudge against the sea. My grudge has a name and letterhead, and it sits up the hill in a brick building with brass on the door.

But that morning I didn't know that yet. That morning I just wanted breakfast and a load that didn't split.

* * *

The foreman, a slab of a man called Dreech whose neck was wider than my waist, was already at the gangway ticking names off a board. He ticked mine without looking up, which was normal. Then he did look up, and that wasn't.

"Dravenmoor," he said. Quiet-like. Like the word itself might cost him something.

"That's me," I said. "Last I checked."

"You working Pier Twelve after this?"

"Company moved me over Tuesday. Why?"

Dreech looked past me, down the pier, at nothing in particular. "No reason. Just—" He wiped his nose with the back of his hand. "Keep your head down over there. There's been men asking questions."

"What kind of questions?"

"The kind you don't answer." And he turned back to his board and called the next name, and that was the whole of it.

Now, I want to be fair to myself here. I'm not a stupid man. Slow, maybe, in the way the sea makes you slow—careful, watching weather before you trust it. But I will admit that when a foreman tells you men are asking questions and won't say what about, a smarter man would have pressed. A smarter man would have asked *who*, and *about what*, and *why are you telling me and not the others*.

Instead I picked up my hook and went to work, because the alternative was standing still and thinking, and thinking on an empty stomach has never once done me any good.

* * *

By midmorning I'd forgotten it, mostly. The body is merciful that way. You can't haul four hundred pounds of salt pork and brood at the same time; the shoulders take up all the room worry might otherwise use. What was left of my mind was occupied by ordinary things—the slipperiness of the planks, the rhythm of the winch, the argument between two teamsters over whose cart had the right of way, an argument conducted entirely in gestures since both men were chewing and neither wished to surrender the advantage.

It was the tally clerk who brought it back.

He was a new one, or newish—young fellow with ink-stained cuffs and a ledger he guarded like a Bible, going down the line of us checking faces against his book. When he got to me he stopped. Looked at my face. Looked at his book. Looked at my face again, longer.

"Tobin Dravenmoor?" he said.

"That's what the cap says," I told him, and a couple of the lads laughed, because the cap says nothing, it just smells.

He wrote something. Didn't smile. Moved on.

And there it was again, that little cold fish swimming up my spine. Twice in one morning somebody had said my name like it was a question. I stood there with a barrel on my shoulder doing arithmetic I didn't have numbers for. *Tobin Dravenmoor.* It's not a dangerous name. It's not any name at all, really—it's a dead father's trade and a mother's people and a sister who hasn't used the surname in six years, not even at Christmas, not even whispered. Mirabel goes by Marsh now, or Marren, or whatever she's calling herself this season at the company office; she changes it the way other women change ribbons. She says it's practical. She says a diver's daughter who reads and writes is a lit lamp in a powder store.

She's right, of course. She's always right, my sister, which is one of the many things about her that could try a saint.

So the name *Dravenmoor* shouldn't mean anything to a tally clerk. Except it evidently did.

* * *

Lunch I took on a bollard with my back to the wind, bread and cheese and a pickle Sera had put up in the autumn, sour enough to make your eyes apologize. Halfway through, Ollie Brack sat down beside me. Ollie's been on the docks longer than I've been alive; he claims to have unloaded the very first crate Halloway ever shipped, and he tells the story differently every time, which is how you know it's true.

"You hear?" he said, around his own lunch.

"Hear what?"

"Company's got investigators in town." He nodded toward Pier Twelve, where I was due after the bell. "Two of them. Come off the *Meridian* last week, all city coats and soft hands. Been walking the piers asking after crews. Asking after families, too."

"Families?"

Ollie chewed a while. Ollie does everything a while; it's his philosophy. "There's talk," he said finally, "that something came in on the night boats that isn't on any manifest. Something small and dear." He tapped his ear. "Something that comes out of the water."

I knew what he meant. Everybody on these docks knows what he meant, the same way everybody knows which tavern waters its beer. Pearls. Shell pearls, river pearls, the occasional ocean treasure when a licensed boat gets lucky. Half the divers' families along this coast have sold a few off the books to feed their kids, and the company knows it, and looks away, because the company's own cut comes off the top either way. It's an arrangement older than I am. It only becomes a crime when somebody powerful needs it to be.

"When did this talk start?" I asked.

"Started quiet. Getting louder." Ollie finished his bread and dusted his hands. "Your family dive, don't they? Used to."

"Used to. My father's done."

"Mm." He looked at me with those old gray eyes that have watched forty years of men walk into water and trouble. "Done's a flexible word, on this coast."

He got up and went back to work, and I sat on my bollard with a sour pickle halfway to my mouth and felt the fog come in off the water and settle somewhere behind my ribs.

* * *

Pier Twelve in the afternoon was wrong. I can't tell you exactly how, because wrongness on a dock isn't a fact, it's a texture. The men worked, but quieter. The tally clerk checked his book more often. And there was a fellow leaning against the warehouse wall who wasn't loading anything, wasn't tallying anything, wasn't selling hot pies or mending rope or doing any of the hundred useful-nothing jobs a pier supports. He was just watching. City coat. Soft hands. Watching the men, and—when he thought I wouldn't notice—watching me.

I gave him a nod, the neutral nod you give strangers on a dock, and he gave it back, and we were both satisfied with the lie.

The afternoon's cargo was timber, and I threw myself into it the way you throw yourself into anything when standing still means thinking. Board after board after board. My shoulders burned. The winch groaned. Somewhere out in the fog a ship's bell counted the hours, and I counted with it, and told myself stories:

*It's nothing. Company audits itself every few years, scares everybody, finds nothing, goes home.*

*It's the tax men. It's always the tax men.*

*It's some other Dravenmoor. There must be other Dravenmoors.*

There aren't other Dravenmoors. We're a short list. That's the whole trouble with us—we're a family the size of a lifeboat, and every soul aboard is known by name from one end of this harbor to the other.

* * *

The bell rang us off at six, and I walked home through streets gone blue with evening, and the whole way I argued with myself, and lost.

Here's what I couldn't stop turning over: the questions weren't about *divers*. They were about *the Dravenmoors*. Dreech had said it plain. Men asking about families, and my name read off a page like an entry in a ledger. You don't send investigators across the water to count pearl-divers generally. You send them when you've already decided who the guilty party is, and you're gathering paper to prove what you know.

And who decides such things? Who holds the pen?

The company.

The same company that owned my father's debt. The same company that employed my sister under a borrowed name, though Tobin-the-dockhand wasn't supposed to know the details of that, only the shape of it—Mirabel going up the hill every morning in her good coat, coming home tired in a way that had nothing to do with ledgers, saying *it's fine, Tobin, it's clerical, don't ask*.

I thought about her face when she said *don't ask*. I'd always taken it for shame. Now, walking home in the dark, I wondered for the first time if it might have been fear.

* * *

Sera had soup on when I came in, and the smell of it nearly made me cry, which I mention only because a man ought to be honest about what actually breaks him, and it has never once been danger. It's kindness that undoes me. Always has been.

"How was the water?" she asked, which is what she calls the docks, joking, because I come home smelling of it.

"Wet," I said.

She looked at me over the pot. Seven years married; she can read me like large print. "Tobin."

"It's nothing. Talk on the piers. Investigators, maybe. Ollie says—"

"I heard too." She ladled the soup, sat, rested her hand on her belly. "Mrs. Quill told me at market. She said they were asking at the divers' houses. Asking who sold what, and to whom, and whether anyone named Dravenmoor still went out on the boats."

The soup was good. I tasted none of it.

"They said the name," I said. "At the houses. Out loud."

Sera's hand went still on her belly. "They said it out loud."

We sat with that a moment, the fire ticking, the baby kicking under her palm like a small fist knocking on a door.

"Maybe it's nothing," she said, in the voice women use when they are comforting you and lying to you in the same breath and doing both competently.

"Maybe," I agreed, in the voice men use when they know better and haven't the heart to say so.

Because here's what I knew, sitting there in the warm yellow light of my own kitchen, and here's what I couldn't yet make myself say: my father may be done diving, but *done* is a flexible word on this coast, and my father is a proud man with a boat and thirty years of coves in his head. If there's a cache of pearls sitting somewhere off the books—if there's a nest egg he's been nursing against the day the company squeezes us dry—then my father has been out on the night water. And if the company needed someone to hang its own sins on, well.

A disgraced diver. A family already in its debt. A name everyone knows and no one defends.

You couldn't design a better scapegoat if you tried. Which meant somebody had probably tried.

* * *

I kissed Sera, told her I'd be back by ten, and went out into the fog.

The walk up the hill to my father's house takes twenty minutes if you loiter, and I loitered, because I kept rehearsing how to ask a proud man whether he'd been breaking the law to feed his family, and every version sounded like an accusation. By the time I reached the door I'd settled on saying nothing at all and simply looking at him, which, between a father and son, is often the whole conversation anyway.

He was awake—my father sleeps like a cat, in patches, listening—and he opened the door before I knocked.

"Tobin." His voice, what's left of it, is gravel poured slow. "This hour. Your Sera all right?"

"She's fine. Can I come in?"

He stepped aside, and I saw the state of the room, and everything in me went quiet.

His oilskins were hanging by the stove, soaked through. Not damp—soaked, dripping onto the boards in a little dark map of everywhere he'd been. His boots stood beneath them, wet to the laces. On the table, a lantern turned low, and beside the lantern, a canvas sack with the particular slumped shape of something heavy wrapped in cloth.

My father followed my eyes to the sack. To his credit, he didn't move to hide it. He just stood there in the lamplight, an old broken man with seawater still drying on his gear, and waited to see what I'd say.

"Dad," I managed. "What have you done?"

And outside, far down the hill, through the fog and the dark, I heard—or thought I heard—the sound of boots on cobblestones, more than one pair, moving unhurried in our direction, the way men move when they already know the address.
