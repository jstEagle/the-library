## 6. A Room Above the Chandlery

The stairs above the chandlery were built for men who carried rope for a living, which is to say they were narrow, salt-swollen, and hostile to anyone arriving with a conscience in her chest. I counted them the way I counted everything now—twelve steps, each one a syllable of the speech I had not yet written.

I had rehearsed on the walk over. That was the trouble with being literate: you composed even your disasters in advance, polished them, typed them out in your head with clean margins. *Father, the company is going to hang our name on their crimes like a coat they intend to wear out into the storm.* No. Too literary. He distrusted anything that sounded like it had been written down on purpose.

*Father, we are in danger.* Better. Four words. A man could hear four words without reaching for his pride as a weapon.

The lamp was still burning when I reached the landing. Of course it was. My father slept the way driftwood floats—technically, and only because the water hadn't finished with him yet—and since the accident he kept late hours the way other men kept grudges: close, warm, and entirely by choice.

I knocked twice, the old dockside rhythm—two sharp, one soft—which meant *family* and not *creditor*. From inside came the scrape of a chair, the particular creak of a man rising who has decided rising will hurt but refuses to say so.

"Door's unlocked."

"It's always unlocked," I said, letting myself in. "One day a thief will take advantage and steal nothing, because there's nothing to steal."

"There's the knife." He was standing by the window, lamp behind him, so I got his outline first—the shoulders that used to be a wall and were now more of a fence, leaning somewhere—and then he turned and I got the rest of him. Gray at the temples like frost on a hull. Hands scarred in the pattern only pearl divers have, a topography of rope and shell and pressure. He looked tired in a way that had stopped being temporary years ago and had simply become the architecture.

He looked at me a long moment. Fathers of divers learn to read faces; it's how they know when a boy has gone too deep. Mine read mine in about four seconds.

"You've been typing," he said.

"I type all day."

"Not invoices." He crossed the room, took my chin in one scarred hand—not rough, just certain—and turned my face toward the lamplight. "You've got the look. Your mother had it. The look of a woman carrying news she's already apologized for."

I sat down before my knees could vote independently. The room was small and smelled of tar and tea and the ghost of salt that clings to divers the way perfume clings to widows. On the table, his supper sat untouched—a bowl gone cold, bread torn but uneaten. Beside it, laid out with terrible care, his diving knife. He cleaned it every night now. It was the closest thing he had left to diving.

"Eat something," he said.

"You first."

"I ate."

"Liar. There are divers' families in this city who'd be shocked to learn dishonesty runs in us."

That earned me half a smile, which I treasured briefly and then spent, because there was no way to say what I'd come to say gently, and every delay was just interest accruing on the debt.

"Papa. They're going to name us."

* * *

I told him all of it. The sealed letter. Voss's voice reading the words aloud while I took dictation—*the Dravenmoor operation, known associates, contraband routed through the eastern sheds*—and my hands typing it perfectly while my blood tried to climb out through my fingertips. The carbon copy in the wastebasket. The dates. The manifests I had checked against the harbor logs until midnight, until the arithmetic stopped being deniable: Halloway Trading Company had moved the pearls themselves. Halloway had borrowed against cargo that didn't exist, insured cargo that did exist under false papers, and now, with the auditors coming like weather, Halloway intended to set our name alight and stand behind it, warming themselves.

My father listened the way stone listens to rain. He did not interrupt. When I finished, he went to the window and stood looking down at the harbor, where the fog was doing what fog does in this city—making every ship a rumor of itself.

"The Corvina sails in three weeks," I said, because I couldn't help filling silence; it's the clerk in me, I abhor an empty column. "There'll be ships after. We could go south. Tobin knows captains who owe him—"

"We."

"Yes. We. All of us."

He turned around. And here is the thing about my father that I have spent twenty-six years failing to accept: he is not a stubborn man the way mules are stubborn, or the way secretaries are stubborn, out of appetite or habit. He is stubborn the way a keel is stubborn. It isn't temperament. It's function. Remove it and he stops being a ship and starts being wreckage.

"The sea gave me my living," he said.

"I know."

"It gave me your mother. Gave me this room. Gave me your name—" he said *your name*, and I flinched, because I had been living under a borrowed one for six years and he knew it, he had always known it, we had simply agreed never to make it a fact between us, "—and my name. A man doesn't run from the thing that made him."

"A man doesn't drown for it either, and yet here we are discussing which is likelier."

"That mouth," he said, almost fondly. "Your mother's mouth."

"Papa. Listen to me. This isn't a debt collector or a harbormaster with a grudge. This is Voss. You know what Voss is. The company needs Dravenmoors in irons by the time the auditors arrive, and they will get them, because the papers will say so and the papers are typed"—I heard my own voice crack on *typed*, absurdly, professionally—"and typed things are believed. That's the whole religion of offices, Papa. Ink outruns truth. Every time."

"And what would I be in the south?" he asked quietly. "An old diver with ruined ears and no ledger line. A boarder. A story someone else tells." He picked up the knife from the table, turned it once, put it down. "I stood on the deck of the *Merrow* the morning your mother bargained with old Halloway himself. I let them send you into that office because I told myself it was a ladder, not a leash. I have regretted the shape of that bargain every day since. But I signed it facing them. Whatever comes now, I'll face it the same way."

"Facing them gets you arrested!"

"Perhaps."

"*Perhaps?* Papa, it gets you the Assizes, it gets you—" I stopped, because the next word was *it gets you what Mother got*, and some ammunition you don't fire even when it's loaded and the war is on.

He heard it anyway. He always hears everything; that was the cruelty of him. His jaw did the thing it does, tightening like a cleat taking strain.

"Don't," he said softly. "Don't spend her on this argument."

"Then give me something else to spend! Give me one reason staying is worth more than living!" I was on my feet now, and the room had gotten smaller, the way rooms do when love turns into a negotiation. "Tobin has a wife with a child coming. Does his name mean less than yours? Does hers?"

"Tobin can go wherever he likes. I'll put him on a ship myself if it comes to that."

"But you won't get on it."

"No."

"Why?" It came out of me louder than I meant, loud enough that downstairs the chandler's dog started up, one bark, two, then thought better of it. "Say it plainly. Not the sea gave me my name—I've heard the sermon. Plainly. Why?"

And he said, plainly: "Because if I run, then everything they write about us becomes true. Smugglers run. Thieves run. A Dravenmoor stands where the water meets him and answers for what's his. If I go, they win without firing a shot. If I stay—" he spread those ruined hands, palms up, the map of thirty years of diving offered like evidence, "—then whatever they do to us, they do to innocent men, and innocent men leave records too. Trials leave records. You taught me that much, girl. You're the one who reads."

For one obscene moment I wanted to laugh. He was right, and he was using *my* weapons, the ones I'd sharpened in secret for six years—records, ink, the belief that paper remembers—and he was wielding them to justify walking into the noose. There is a special fury reserved for watching your own tools misused by someone you love.

"You think a trial cares about innocence," I said. "I work for these people. I have seen what happens to innocence in a courtroom. It's exhibit C."

"Then find me better paper than theirs."

"I'm trying—"

"Are you?" He came closer, and his voice dropped into the register he must have used on young divers, the one that carries underwater somehow. "Mirabel." My name. My real one, spoken aloud in that room for perhaps the tenth time since I'd buried it. "You climbed twelve stairs tonight carrying proof against the richest company in this harbor. You didn't come to ask whether we should flee. You came to tell me we're fleeing, and you wanted my blessing so it wouldn't feel like what it is."

"What is it?"

"Running. With extra steps."

* * *

We fought for another hour. I want to report that I won any portion of it, but honesty is supposed to be the theme of this account, so: no one won. He wouldn't sail. I wouldn't stop trying to make him. Somewhere in the middle of it the tea got made—he made it, which was his version of an apology, and I drank it, which was mine—and somewhere near the end we arrived at the place families arrive at when both parties love each other and neither will move: a stalemate with a view of the harbor.

What he did offer, in the small hours, was this:

"The cache," he said. "The real pearls. The ones they shipped and insured twice and never declared. I know where the first lot went into the water, and I know the marks on the crates. If it ever came to a trial—if it ever came to *your* kind of war, paper against paper—an honest manifest beats a forged one, and a witness who saw the crates loaded beats both."

"You'd testify?"

"I'd dive first." He said it lightly, the way he said most terrible things. "There's a crate on the bottom off the old jetty pilings with the Halloway broad-arrow burned under a coat of pitch. I put three of them there myself, six years back, before my ears went. The company thinks the sea ate its mistakes. The sea doesn't eat anything. It keeps accounts." He looked at me then, and for a moment the fence became a wall again, briefly, the old load-bearing wall. "You keep yours, Mirabel. That's the family trade, seems like. Diving or typing—we all bring things up from the deep and put them on tables."

It was the kindest thing he'd said all night, and it was also completely beside the point, and we both knew that, and we let it stand anyway, the way you let a bridge stand that you can see is cracked, because the alternative is swimming.

At the door he stopped me with a hand on my shoulder.

"You'll not do anything foolish," he said. It wasn't a question, so I didn't insult him by answering. "Whatever you're planning—you've got the planning face. Your mother had that too. God rest her, she planned her way into marrying a diver with debts, so mind the lineage."

"I love you," I said, because arguments were exhausted and love was the only coin left with any weight.

"And I you." He kissed the top of my head, once, the way he'd done since I was tall enough to reach his elbow. "Go home. Sleep. And Mirabel—"

"Papa."

"If it comes to choosing—" he began, and then stopped, and I waited, and he looked past me down the dark stairwell toward the black water below the chandlery, and whatever sentence he had been building, he struck it from the record. "Nothing. Go on."

* * *

The fog had thickened while we fought. Walking home along the quay I could see maybe ten feet in any direction, lamps appearing and dissolving like thoughts you couldn't hold onto, and somewhere out in the gray the Corvina's anchor lights burned, patient as a promise.

Three weeks. One berth—I was nearly sure of the one berth, Voss's letter had all but said it, and Voss said nothing accidentally. Three weeks, one berth, and three Dravenmoors: a father who would rather drown on principle, a brother with a child arriving, and a clerk nobody had ever officially met.

I did the arithmetic on the walk home, because arithmetic is where I go to hide from feeling, and it hid me adequately until I reached my door.

Because here was the sum I kept arriving at, no matter how I arranged the figures: Papa would not sail. Tobin could not be spared easily—but he *could* be spared, whereas Papa could not be persuaded, and I had watched persuasion fail for two hours tonight and seen it leave bruises on us both. Which left, when you subtracted pride from one column and a pregnant wife from another, a remainder standing alone at the bottom of the page.

Me.

I was the easiest subtraction. That was the joke of my whole life, wasn't it—I had practiced vanishing so long that I'd become the natural candidate for the final disappearance. Six years of being no one, and now the skill might finally prove useful.

I laughed at that, alone in the fog, and it came out wrong, and a constable across the quay glanced over, and I smoothed my face into clerical blankness and walked on.

Tomorrow I would start on the exit papers. Tonight, one more entry in the ledger: I climbed my own stairs, lit my own lamp, and sat down at my little table with a sheet of good rag paper and Voss's signature burning in my memory like a brand.

If the family trade was bringing things up from the deep, then tomorrow I would begin raising the largest thing yet.

His hand. I would need his hand first.
