## 8. The Arithmetic of Blood

There is a particular kind of arithmetic that no ledger teaches. I learned it at the Halloway Trading Company, second desk from the window, where I have spent four years adding columns and subtracting myself from every room I enter. The arithmetic I mean is the kind you do at night, in the dark, with nothing but your own heartbeat for an abacus.

I did it that night, after the sealed letter. After the carbon paper went into the wastebasket and came out again into my bodice, warm as a stolen egg against my ribs.

The company boarding house had gone quiet the way it always does—floorboards ticking as they cooled, someone's cough three rooms down, the fog pressing at the glass like a debtor at a door. I sat on my narrow bed with a stub of candle and a scrap of paper I'd torn from a blotter, and I did what I was trained to do. I made lists.

Not the Dravenmoors, mind you. I wrote no names. Names are dangerous things; Voss had taught me that with one typed paragraph. I wrote *the father*, *the brother*, *the sister*, because even alone in my own head I had learned to be careful, and because there was something in the coldness of those labels that suited the work.

*The father.* Aldous Dravenmoor, fifty-one years old by my reckoning, though the sea has a way of making men older than their birthdays. His left eardrum burst in the accident two winters past; the doctor at the charity ward said—and I was there, pretending to be a neighbor's girl, because who else would come—that he would never equalize again. Never dive. A pearl diver who cannot dive is not a retired man. He is a monument to himself, and monuments starve. He had a debt to Halloway of thirty-one pounds, fourteen shillings, entered against him the week of his accident by a clerk whose name I knew, because I had later shared a desk with her successor. Interest accruing. Always accruing. My father signs his mark with an X and believes, in his salt-cured heart, that a man's word outweighs a man's paper. Against Mr. Voss, this is not a philosophy. It is a handicap.

*The brother.* Tobin Dravenmoor, twenty-three, strong as a capstan and nearly as easy to turn if you know where the pin is. He works the Halloway docks loading crates he does not open, and last spring he married Sera Quill, who is round with a child due before the month is out—a child who will arrive, if the company has its way, bearing its father's name like a warrant. Tobin cannot run. Not now. A man can vanish; a man with a wife eight months gone cannot vanish anywhere but into ruin. And Sera—Sera has done nothing to anyone except love a Dravenmoor, which the company appears to consider sufficient crime.

*The sister.*

Here my pencil stopped.

Here is the thing about being invisible: it is not only a wound. It is also a skill. For four years I have been Mirabel Fen—the Fen borrowed from a dead woman nobody in this city ever met, the first name chosen because it sounded like a clerk's name, gray and forgettable. I have no friends who know my face and my history together. No debts in my true name. No husband, no child, no mark on any register that connects me to the tide-side of the harbor. If I walked out of Halloway House tomorrow, Mirabel Fen would simply cease, the way fog ceases when the sun manages itself, and no one—not Voss, not the directors, not the port authorities they were so busy tipping off—would know where one woman had gone.

My father could not flee. My brother could not flee. I could vanish like ink in water, and no one would even notice the water change color.

You would think that thought would be a comfort. It was not. It was the coldest sentence I ever composed, sitting on that bed with the candle guttering: *the easiest person to save in my family is the person the family will miss least.* Because that is the truth of invisibility, and I want to be honest about it even now, especially now—it was not entirely sacrifice that warmed toward the idea. There was a part of me, a small shameful pilot light of a part, that heard *you could go* and answered *at last.* Somewhere new, somewhere the name Fen could become anything, or the name Dravenmoor could surface clean, unburdened, mine by choice rather than by hiding. A berth on the Corvina was not merely escape. It was permission. It was the world finally saying: *you may be seen now,* even if only by strangers.

I blew out the candle partly to stop myself from enjoying the idea.

* * *

Voss had said three weeks. The Corvina sailed on the twenty-sixth, tide permitting, and Voss was a man who permitted tides. That gave me nineteen days, and nineteen days is both a fortune and nothing, depending on what must be built inside them.

What must be built was this: papers. Exit papers, port passes, whatever document stood between a Dravenmoor and a constable's hand between here and the gangway. Because I understood something by then that I do not think Voss credited me with understanding—he thought he was dealing with a diver's daughter who had learned to type. He was not. He was dealing with four years of a woman who had read everything that crossed her desk, including the things she was not meant to read, and filed them away in the attic of herself.

And what I knew about papers was this: they are only as strong as their dullest detail. Nobody is fooled by elegant flourishes. Constables are fooled—or rather, un-bothered—by the correct weight of paper, the correct stamp, the correct bored phrasing of a bureaucrat who processed nine identical documents that morning and will process nine more. Forgery is not art. Forgery is clerical work performed with criminal intent, and I had been doing clerical work since I was seventeen.

So I began to study.

The next morning I arrived at Halloway House early, as always—I am always early; invisible people cannot afford lateness—and while the fires were being lit I stood at the filing room shelf where the outbound correspondence waited for the morning pouch, and I read. Not letters. Hands. The port authority's clearance forms came through twice weekly, signed by a customs man named Brackett whose signature was a disaster: the B collapsed into a loop like a snail dragged through ink, the rest of the name trailing off in a fatigue that suggested he signed forty of these before breakfast and hated all of them. A fatigued hand is a gift. Nobody scrutinizes a signature the signer himself clearly resented.

Voss's own signature I already knew by heart—I had watched it cross invoices for four years—but knowing it and reproducing it are different animals, so I studied that too, the way he bore down on the V and then hurried, always hurried, through the rest, as though signing his own name were beneath him. Men who resent their names leave patterns. I collected patterns the way my father once collected pearls: patiently, with an aching back, never sure which would prove valuable.

At midday I took my luncheon not to the corner bench where the other clerks went but to the records annex, where I had a legitimate errand—pulling the shipping manifests for the Corvina's last three sailings, because if I was going to put my brother on that ship I wanted to know its routes, its stops, and whether its master had a reputation for asking questions. The manifests told me the Corvina ran south along the coast to Callow's Reach and then across. They also told me something else, something I noted with the numb diligence of a woman who has stopped being surprised: the Corvina was owned, through two layers of holding companies, by Halloway itself. Even our escape would travel on the company's keel. Voss had arranged it so that the berth was never generosity. It was containment. A Dravenmoor on a company ship, under company papers, could be retrieved whenever retrieval became convenient.

I sat with that knowledge in the dust-smelling quiet of the annex and added it to the arithmetic. *The berth is real,* I wrote in my head, *and it is bait.* But a trap you can see is only a trap if you step in it blind. I intended to walk around it, take the bait, and leave the iron jaws snapping on forged paper.

* * *

That night I began to practice.

I had bought, with coins counted out carefully from my hidden store, three sheets of good rag stock matching the port authority's paper—matching because I had held a genuine clearance form to the light that afternoon until I knew its watermark the way one knows a mole on a lover's shoulder. I had a pen of the right nib. I had a bottle of iron-gall ink mixed a shade too pale, because Brackett's office was notoriously stingy and everyone knew their ink came diluted.

And I had my father's kitchen table, borrowed while he and Tobin were at the docks, and Sera sleeping in the back room with her hands folded over the swell of her.

I wrote *A. Brackett* ninety times.

By the thirtieth attempt my hand had found the snail-loop of the B. By the sixtieth I could produce the trailing fatigue without it looking like fatigue at all—which was the trick, I discovered. You must not imitate tiredness; you must actually grow tired, let your wrist genuinely ache, so the decay in the line is honest. A forgery should not be performed. It should be lived. Somewhere around the seventieth repetition I realized I was no longer thinking of it as forgery at all. It felt like typing. It felt like the work I did every day: taking what existed and rendering it faithfully. Only the source had changed. Instead of copying Voss's dictated lies, I was copying Brackett's careless hand, and the lie underneath was mine instead of his, and somehow that made it cleaner. Mine, at least, was a lie with a true purpose folded inside it, the way a pill coats poison around medicine.

Guilt sat with me at that table. I will not pretend otherwise. Every loop of the B, some voice in me whispered: *this is what they say we are. This is what the frame requires us to be. Smugglers, frauds, people of false paper.* I was building the very evidence that would damn my family, letter by letter, and if I failed—if any constable looked too closely—then the Dravenmoors would hang twice: once for the smuggling they did not do, and once for the forgery I did. I was gambling my family's necks on my own competence, and competence, I knew better than anyone, is just another word for a record that has not yet been audited.

But guilt, I have learned, is like ballast. It weighs you down and keeps you upright in rough water. You do not throw it overboard. You learn to sail with it.

So I sailed. Ninety signatures became a hundred and forty. I practiced the date formats, the stamp placement—I could not forge the stamp itself yet, and this troubled me, until I remembered that half the clearances came through unstamped during busy tides, the stamp applied later at the office, and that a missing stamp read as haste, not fraud. I practiced the phrasing of the pass itself: *Permission is granted to the bearer to proceed to the quay...* dull words, beautiful in their dullness, each one a plank in the bridge I was building out of this city.

Somewhere past midnight, the floorboard in the hall sighed.

I have moved quickly exactly twice in my life, and both times were that week. I swept the practice sheets into the bread tin—my father's bread tin, which I had emptied of its actual bread, a theft I intended to confess someday in a better world—and had the tin's lid seated and my hands folded over it like a churchgoer before the second footstep fell.

It was Tobin.

He stood in the kitchen doorway in his dock clothes, smelling of tar and night air, and looked at me sitting alone at midnight with a bread tin in my arms and ink to the second knuckle.

"You're up," he said.

"Couldn't sleep," I said, which was true, and which he received with the expression of a man who has carried crates all day and has no strength left for his sister's mysteries.

"Sera's asking for you," he said. "She can't get comfortable." Then, pausing in the doorway, quieter: "Mira. Something's wrong on the docks. Men asking after our name. I don't like it."

"I know," I said.

He waited for more. I had ink under my fingernails and a tin full of crimes in my lap, and he waited for more, and I gave him nothing, because the truth was not yet built enough to survive being spoken. He nodded slowly, the nod of a man filing something away beside the crates he doesn't open, and went to fetch me to Sera's side.

I set the tin down and followed him. And as I sat that night with my sister-by-marriage's head heavy against my shoulder, feeling the small insistent knock of her child against my arm—knock, knock, knock, like a fist on a door, like a demand, like the future asking to be let in—I finished the arithmetic.

Nineteen days. One berth. Three names, and only mine light enough to carry away, only theirs worth more than mine to stay. The pass would be ready within the week. The question that remained was not whether I could forge it, nor even whom it would bear—for I was beginning, in the dark, with that small knocking against my arm, to understand whom it must bear—but how to get it past the sharpest eyes in this harbor.

Because tomorrow, I had resolved, I would need to show my work to the one man who could tell me if it would hold.

Tomorrow, I would begin testing my forgeries against Mr. Voss himself.
