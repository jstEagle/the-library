## 9. Ink Under the Fingernails

There is a particular kind of theft that leaves no wound on the victim and every mark on the thief. I know this now. My hands know it best of all, stained faintly violet at the cuticles where the copying ink has worked itself into the skin like a secret the body refuses to keep.

I had stolen paper first. That was easy enough—Halloway Trading Company loses reams to damp, to rats, to clerks who line their drawers with it. No one audits the wastepaper of a firm that doesn't audit its own conscience. Then I stole a nib, a fine one, from the stationery cabinet where Mr. Pell keeps his correspondence pens under lock he believes is cleverness rather than habit. The lock yields to a hairpin. Most locks do, if you stop thinking of them as iron and start thinking of them as questions.

The signatures were harder.

Not because my hand is unsteady—it is not; a typist's hand is steadier than a surgeon's when the work matters—but because forgery is not drawing. Anyone can copy a shape. What you must steal is the *habit* behind the shape: the way a man's wrist turns, the place he hesitates, the small vanities his pen commits without his knowledge. A signature is a confession written so many times its author no longer hears it.

So for six nights I studied Mr. Voss's hand.

* * *

It began with the letterheads. Voss signs perhaps forty documents a week—requisitions, customs declarations, letters of credit—and every one passes across my desk before it passes out of the building. I had spent two years training my eyes not to see what crossed that desk. Now I trained them the other way, which is a discipline like learning to walk again after pretending to be a chair.

I began keeping the carbons. Not all of them—one carbon missing is an accident, five is an investigation—but one in three, folded into my petticoat waistband, carried home through fog that was thick enough that year to be practically conspiratorial. In my room above Mrs. Quill's boarding house, with the lamp turned low and a shawl over the shade so the light wouldn't leak into the alley and invite questions, I spread them out like a fortune-teller reading cards.

Voss, it turns out, is vain in exactly one place. His capital H is a cathedral—lofty, ornamental, drawn with a flourish at the top as though the H were being crowned. But his lowercase letters are cowards. They huddle. His *r*'s are stunted little things, and his *s* closes like a fist. And here was the tell, the thing I'd have missed if grief hadn't already taught me to look for what men hide: whenever he signed under pressure—a deadline noted in the margin, a director's initials scrawled beside his name—the tail of his final *s* jerked upward, sharp as a fishhook. A man's signature is a diary he doesn't know he's keeping. Under pressure, Voss's pen remembered something bitter.

I practiced until my own *H* could be crowned in my sleep. I practiced his pressure—he writes heavy at the downstrokes, light on the connectors, a man who leans into authority and skims past obligation. I practiced on old invoices, on the margins of a newspaper, once, shamefully, on the flyleaf of my mother's prayer book, which I regretted instantly and did not undo, because undoing would have wasted good practice paper and my mother, God rest her practical soul, would have understood.

By the fifth night I could sign Edmund Voss so faithfully that I made myself ill looking at it. There is a sickness in that kind of skill. You hold a piece of yourself up next to the counterfeit and cannot always say which is which.

But exit papers are not signed by Voss alone. They require a countersignature from the harbormaster's office—Harbormaster Grell, whose hand I knew only from stamps until I bribed a junior clerk with two hours of my typewriting (his sweetheart letters; the man proposes by proxy, poor lamb) in exchange for three discarded drafts bearing Grell's actual signature. Grell writes like a man perpetually late: everything slanted forward, as if the words were trying to reach the end of the page before he did. Easy enough. It was Voss who required devotion.

And there was a third name. The shipping agent for the Corvina, one Aldous Renwick, whose signature lived on manifests I typed daily. Renwick I saved for last because Renwick frightened me least, and I needed one name I could forge without my stomach turning.

* * *

Thursday night, the watchman nearly ended everything.

Mrs. Quill's house sits four doors from the harbor road, close enough that the fog comes off the water smelling of tar and dead things, and the night watchman makes his rounds along our street at half past eleven and again at two. I knew his schedule the way I know tide tables. What I did not account for was his cough.

I was deep into the third sheet of a forged transit pass—Renwick's signature drying, Grell's countersign slanting hopefully toward freedom, Voss's crowned H sitting atop the document like a gargoyle—when I heard boots on the stair. Not Mrs. Quill's boots. Mrs. Quill weighs her steps like a woman descending with a tray. These were deliberate boots, patrolling boots, and between them came a wet, rattling cough I recognized from the harbor road: Mr. Tallow, the watchman, doing rounds early because the fog had spooked him or because Mrs. Quill had complained of noises.

My lamp was lit. My papers were spread across the bed like the evidence of a conspiracy, which is precisely what they were. There was nowhere to put them. There was never anywhere to put them; that is the arithmetic of hiding—you are always one opened door from ruin.

I did the only thing my body thought of. I swept the documents under the pillow, sat down at the little writing desk with my legitimate ledger work—company invoices, blessedly real—and began to type. On a machine I did not own. In a room that contained no such machine.

The knock came as I realized this.

"Miss? All quiet?" Tallow's voice through the door, polite, tired, accompanied by that cough like a saw working through green wood.

"All quiet," I called back, and then—God help me—added, "I'm catching up on accounts," as though explaining a lit lamp and the clatter of keys to a man who could hear perfectly well that the clatter was coming from a room where no typewriter lived.

A pause. The pause lasted approximately one geological era.

"Accounts," he said.

"Quarter-end," I said, with the serene confidence of a liar who has decided to commit. "The company runs behind."

Another silence, long enough for me to compose my own obituary. Then the cough again, and the boots retreating, and I sat with my hands hovering over keys that had produced nothing but noise, shaking so hard the desk trembled.

Afterward I laughed. I couldn't help it—sat alone in that narrow room with ink under my fingernails and laughed until the tears came, and then the tears kept coming after the laughing stopped, which is how I learned the difference between relief and grief wearing relief's coat. Because here is what I could not say even to the dark: some part of me had *enjoyed* it. The sweep of the papers under the pillow, the steady lie delivered through a closed door, the whole tightrope performance of it—some part of me, the part I don't like to feed, had found it thrilling. To be someone else on paper is one thing. To feel yourself becoming someone else in the bones is another entirely, and I could not swear which direction the current ran. Was I forging documents to save my family, or was I using my family as the respectable excuse for a woman who had discovered, at twenty-three, that she was very, very good at crime?

I looked at my hands under the lamp. Ink in the creases, ink in the crescents of nail, ink worked so deep into the quick that scrubbing with pumice soap barely faded it. My father's hands carry the sea the same way—salt in the knuckles that never washes out, a white crust in the old scars. Diver's hands. Forger's hands. Both marked by what they go down after.

I washed them anyway. Some rituals you keep so you can tell yourself the marks are temporary.

* * *

Saturday I went to see what Voss's own hand could tell me that his signatures hadn't. This sounds mad—I knew it sounded mad even as I arranged it—but there was a filing task, the quarterly transfer of confidential correspondence to the archive room in the cellar, and I volunteered. Pell looked at me as if I'd offered to lick the cellar floor clean, and let me go gladly, because Pell believes cellars are where careers go to die. He is not wrong about much.

The archive room smells of mildew and candle smoke and, beneath both, the faint mineral ghost of the harbor seeping through stone. Crates of ledgers. Boxes tied with red tape. And in the locked cabinet—hairpin, remember—correspondence in Voss's own fist, letters he wrote rather than dictated, which meant letters that mattered.

I read them standing in the dark with a borrowed match burning low, and what I found was worse than anything I'd expected, because what I found was a man practicing too.

His early letters—from years ago, from before, dated the spring of another administration—are stiff, over-formed, each word chosen like a man selecting weapons he isn't sure he knows how to use. And threaded through them, references. To a ship. To a berth paid in cash. To a sister—"my sister's passage is settled, she sails Thursday, tell her I will follow"—and then, three letters later, nothing. No following. No sister mentioned again in eleven years of correspondence except once, obliquely, in a note about remittances discontinued.

I stood in that cold room holding a dead man's—no, not dead, worse, a living man's abandoned promises—and understood something that rearranged the whole architecture of my anger. Voss had bought his way out once. Had taken a berth and left blood behind, and had built his entire ruthless immaculate self on top of that leaving like a man building on a sinkhole. Every cruelty of his was load-bearing. Take it out and the whole structure of Edmund Voss collapsed into the hole where his sister used to be.

I should have hated him more cleanly after that. Instead I filed his letters back in order, locked the cabinet, and climbed the stairs feeling like a burglar who has broken into a house only to find the owner weeping in the kitchen—which does not make the burglary innocent, mind you. It just makes everyone in it miserable.

* * *

Sunday evening, Tobin came by the chandlery stairs where Father sits his evenings now, hands wrapped around a mug he mostly warms rather than drinks. I brought bread I hadn't baked and a report I'd rehearsed the whole walk over: the papers were ready. Transit pass in Renwick's hand, countersigned Grell, authorized under Voss's crowned H. One berth's worth of legitimacy, forged to the stitch.

Father listened without looking at me, which is his way of looking at you most directly.

"You've been signing other people's names," he said finally.

"I've been borrowing them."

"Borrowing." He turned the word over like a stone he suspected of being a coin. "Your mother's mother was a counterfeiter's daughter in Kinsale. Did I ever tell you?"

"You did not."

"She died honest," he said. "That's the family talent, Mirabel. We die honest. We don't live it."

"The company lives dishonestly every day," I said, "and prospers."

"And you'll prosper too, will you? Signing dead men's names until your own wears thin?" He set down the mug. His hands, those salt-cured ruined wonderful hands, were steady. Mine were not. "I won't ask you to stop. I heard what you said about the papers, and I know why you're doing it, and a man who drowned his own livelihood has no business lecturing anyone about desperation. But hear me: whatever name you sign, make sure you can still answer to yours after."

I walked home along the water with that sentence turning over in me like a keel. Make sure you can still answer to yours. As if the danger were forgetting. As if the peril weren't precisely the opposite—that I remembered my name too well, that every forged flourish of Voss's crowned H felt less like disguise and more like rehearsal for a self I might prefer.

At Mrs. Quill's I lit the lamp, pulled the pillow aside, and checked the documents one final time. All correct. All beautiful. All lies executed with the tenderness of truth.

And pinned under the transit pass, a note in a hand I recognized because I had spent six nights teaching myself to recognize it—small, square, pressed hard into the paper:

*Miss Dravenmoor. My office. Monday, eight o'clock. Come alone.*
