## 10. The Secretary's Own Ghost

There is a particular hour in a port city when the fog decides, for reasons of its own, that the day is over. In Grayharbor it comes early—four o'clock in November, sometimes three—and when it settles over the Halloway Trading Company like a lid on a pot, the clerks begin their slow exodus, and I remain.

I have always remained. That is the whole of my biography as far as this office is concerned: the man who remains. Mr. Halloway himself believes it is devotion. The junior clerks believe it is appetite for their positions. Both are wrong, but I have never troubled to correct either, since being misunderstood at close range is considerably safer than being understood at all.

That evening I remained because of a carbon copy.

It sat in the wastebasket by Miss—by the clerk we call Wren's desk, where it had no earthly business sitting. A confidential letter regarding the Dravenmoor operation does not belong in an open bin within reach of any pair of eyes that happen to pass. It belonged in the iron box in my office, where its sisters live. And yet there it was, retrieved before I could ask for it, and the question of *how* it got from my dictation to her typewriter to the basket to nowhere—that question had been circling me all afternoon like a gull that has decided I am something edible.

I burned the copy myself, in the grate, watching the ink curl. Then I sat down at my desk and did what I do every evening, which is to add up the day.

The arithmetic was not comforting. A clerk who hides her name. A clerk whose hands knew the keys too well for a girl supposedly hired off a farm. A clerk who had typed the word *Dravenmoor* without so much as a stutter in the rhythm—I had listened; I always listen; the typewriter confesses everything if you stand behind it long enough—and then had gone home with her spine held straight as a mast in weather.

I had seen that spine before. That was the trouble.

* * *

Her name, when I knew it, was not Mirabel. It was Liesel, and she was eleven years old, and she stood on the quay at Bremerhaven holding a carpetbag nearly as large as herself while our mother explained to her, with the gentle logic mothers use when they are breaking something, that one berth had been purchased, and one berth only, and that the berth was mine.

"You're the boy," our mother said. "The boy goes first. You send for us."

I was fourteen. I believed her. That is the charitable version. The true version is that I wanted to go, and wanting was enough, and I let the arithmetic of blood do its work: the boy first, the boy has prospects, the boy will send passage money home by spring. I stepped onto the gangway with my father's watch in my pocket and my sister's face on the dock, and I remember thinking—not even guilt, mind you, only observation—that she had stopped crying, which seemed mature of her.

I sent letters. Letters are easy. I sent four in the first year, each promising spring. By the third year I had learned that spring is a season that can be indefinitely deferred if one simply stops naming it, and by the fifth year I had stopped writing altogether, which was easier still, because silence requires no postage and breaks no promises, having made none.

Liesel died in 'ninety-one. Cholera, or typhus—the letter that told me spelled it both ways, as though the writer couldn't decide which death would sit better on the page. My mother wrote it. It was the last thing she ever wrote to me. She did not accuse. That was the worst of it. She wrote *you were the boy* the way one writes *the tide came in*, and then nothing more, ever, and I have spent thirty years being the boy, and I have found that the phrase does not improve with age.

People assume cruelty is a temperament. It is usually a scar that learned to walk around and hold a pen.

* * *

So it was with uncharacteristic care that I arranged, the following Tuesday, to be passing the copying room at the hour when the clerk called Wren worked alone.

I want it understood that I am not a man who arranges to pass places. I am a man who arrives. Passing implies uncertainty about one's destination, and I have never once in my life been uncertain about a destination; I have merely, on one occasion thirty years ago, been criminally certain about the wrong one.

But there I was, passing, with a folder of invoices under my arm as a pretext, and through the open door I watched her work.

She typed the way divers swim—which is to say she did not type the way the other clerks typed, all elbows and prayer. Her hands rested low and moved only as far as they must. Economy. Every stroke paid for in advance. I had trained half the stenographers on this coast and I knew what I was looking at: someone who had learned on a machine older than she was, someone who had taught herself in stolen hours because no one would give them to her honestly. Farm girls did not teach themselves to type. Divers' daughters—

Well. There it was. I had known it since the letter, if I am honest, and I am honest in the privacy of my own skull, where no one can subpoena it.

She sensed me and turned. Good instincts. Most clerks sense me too late.

"Mr. Voss." She rose, smoothing her skirt. "Was there something for transcription?"

"There is a matter I wished to discuss." I entered and closed the door, which made her go very still, and I noted the stillness—another diver's trick, going quiet when the shadow passes overhead. "Sit, please."

She sat. Hands folded. Waiting. I have interrogated smugglers with less composure.

"In three weeks," I said, "the Corvina sails for the southern ports. She carries mail, general cargo, and a passenger list I control. I am prepared to place one berth on that list at the company's expense."

Something moved behind her face—quick, controlled, filed away for later examination. "For whom?"

"For whichever member of the Dravenmoor family understands the situation most clearly."

"And what," she said, "is the situation?"

I gave her the situation. I laid it out the way I lay out everything: the inquiry coming from the customs board, the need for the matter to resolve itself quietly, the regrettable necessity that someone bear the weight of it, and the mercy—call it mercy; I did, with a straight face—of a clean departure versus a public reckoning. I watched her listen. She did not flinch at the word *reckoning*. She did not flinch anywhere. She simply absorbed, the way sand absorbs water, taking everything in and giving back nothing but a slightly darker surface.

"Father won't go," she said at last. Flat. Not asking.

"Then the choice narrows."

"It isn't a choice between us. It's a price list."

I looked at her then—properly, the way I permit myself to look at perhaps two people a year—and what I saw frightened me in the specific way that old things frighten. Because I had stood in a copying room once too. I had been the clever one, the literate one, the one who could become someone else on paper. I had done the arithmetic of blood and arrived at myself, and I had boarded, and I had lived, and living had turned out to be the punishment.

"You may tell them," I said, "that the berth expires. Sailing day. After that, the list closes and the inquiry proceeds on its own schedule, which will not be gentle."

"I understand." She stood. And then, at the door, with her hand on the frame, she turned and said the thing I have been turning over since like a stone with something underneath it:

"Did anyone ever offer *you* a berth, Mr. Voss? Or did you take it yourself?"

And she left before I could answer, which was the correct tactical decision, and also the cruelest thing anyone has said to me since 1886.

* * *

I stayed in the copying room after she left. This is the sort of behavior I despise in novels and performed anyway.

The room smelled of ribbon ink and cold tea. Her desk was tidy in the way of people who expect to be searched. On the blotter, aligned to the edge with a precision I recognized as defensive rather than aesthetic, lay her pens and a small tin of pins, and beneath the blotter's lip—a habit of the careless, or of the very careful pretending to be careless—the corner of a practice sheet.

I should have walked away. A wise man walks away. Instead I drew it out.

Signatures. Rows of them, faint pencil first, then ink: *A. Voss.* Over and over. My own hand, studied, approximated, improving line by line down the page until the last two were good. Not perfect. But good enough to fool a harbor master at dusk, and harbor masters at dusk are the entire audience such documents require.

I stood there holding my own name in a stranger's hand, and I want to report rage, or the cold pleasure of confirmation. What I felt instead was older and much worse. It was 1886 again, and I was fourteen, forging my mother's consent on emigration papers because she couldn't write and wouldn't press her mark to a document sending her son away forever. I had practiced her mark forty times. The last one was good. Good enough to fool an emigration clerk at dusk.

Everyone in this business learns the same lesson eventually: paper doesn't remember hands. Paper remembers intent. And intent, once committed to a page, travels farther than the person who wrote it.

I put the sheet back exactly as I'd found it. Aligned. Defensive. And I went up to my office and poured the whisky I allow myself on Fridays regardless of the day, and I sat with it untouched, doing the sum I have spent thirty years refusing to do:

One berth. Three names. And somewhere in this building, a girl doing sums of her own, arriving—I would wager the deed to my soul—at an answer that would look, from the outside, exactly like mine had. The clever one stays behind to manage the ruin. The clever one sends the others ahead. The clever one writes *spring* in every letter and never means it.

Unless she was better than I had been. Unless she meant to stay and burn the whole company down rather than sail away from it.

I drank the whisky. It tasted of tin and salt, the way everything in Grayharbor tastes eventually.

On my desk the Corvina's passenger manifest waited, one berth blank, sailing in nineteen days. I dipped my pen and hovered it over the empty line—over the space where a name would go, where a life would be decided by a stroke of ink—and for the first time in thirty years, Elias Voss found he could not make his hand move.

Somewhere below, in the copying room, a typewriter began to strike. Fast. Certain. Each key falling like a small verdict.

I listened.
