## 11. Tobin Learns the Truth

### Mirabel

I had rehearsed the telling of it eleven ways by lamplight before I ever climbed the stairs to the room above the chandlery, and every one of the eleven versions had collapsed by the time I reached the third step.

That is the trouble with rehearsed speeches. They assume an audience that sits still. Tobin had never sat still in his life. As a boy he'd been thrown out of Sunday service twice for pacing during the sermon, and once for catching a hymnbook that a deacon's wife had dropped, which the deacon considered showing off and the wife considered a small miracle. Tobin agreed with the wife. He always did agree with the women in any dispute, which made him popular at church and insufferable at home.

So I stood at his door with the fog coming off the harbor in gray ropes and the carbon copy of the letter folded against my ribs like a second heartbeat, and I abandoned all eleven speeches and said, "Tobin. It's me."

He opened the door with the chain still on, looked at me through the gap, and said, "You look like a woman about to confess to arson."

"Let me in and I'll tell you what I'm actually confessing to."

"Forgery, then."

I blinked. He unlatched the chain.

Inside, the room smelled of tar and boiled mutton, and Sera's things were everywhere now — a shawl over the chair, a tin of ginger drops on the sill, a pair of tiny knitted boots no bigger than my two thumbs lying together on the table like a question nobody had answered yet. The baby was due within the month. I looked at those boots and felt the arithmetic I'd been carrying all week shift its weight in my chest, the way cargo shifts in a hold when the sea turns.

Sera was out — evening shift at the cannery, her last week before they'd let her stop standing — so it was only Tobin and me and the lamp and the fog pressing at the window like something that wanted in and had all night to wait.

I sat down. I took the folded paper from inside my coat. And because I had failed to rehearse anything that would survive my brother's face, I simply said it.

"They've named us, Tobin. The company. There's a letter — I typed it myself, God help me — that puts the Dravenmoor operation at the center of a smuggling scheme. Contraband pearls. The customs men are being fed it slowly, and when they've chewed enough, there will be arrests. Every soul with our blood in them."

He didn't move for a long moment. The lamp hissed. Somewhere below us the chandlery dog turned over in its sleep.

Then he said, quietly, in the voice he used for the most dangerous things, "Read it to me."

I read it to him. Every word. The *Dravenmoor operation*, in black type, in the company's own letterhead language, dated and signed by Voss's own hand. I read the part about the *known associates* and the part about the *recovery of outstanding debts* and the part where the word *apprehension* sat in a sentence like a nail waiting for a hammer.

When I finished, Tobin stood very still. I have seen him angry before — at a mate who shorted his pay, at a foreman who called Sera a name, at Father, once, over the question of whether a man owed the sea an apology. But I had never seen him this still. Stillness in Tobin is what storm-swell is to a wave. The wave is just the sea showing off. The swell is the sea making up its mind.

"Say the part again," he said. "The part about who actually moved the crates."

"The company moved them. Through their own warehouse, on their own manifests, under their own seal. Then they wrote our name on the crime. They're in debt up to their necks — the pearl contracts failed, the insurance lapsed — and they needed someone to drown for it, and they picked the family that already owed them and couldn't argue."

"And you typed this."

"I was ordered to type it. I'm a clerk. Clerks type what they're handed."

"You could have refused."

"Yes," I said. "And then they'd have wondered why a clerk named Mirabel Fen refused to type a letter about the Dravenmoors, and they'd have wondered it very loudly, and by Friday we'd all have been in irons a month early."

"You could have *warned* us. Weeks ago. You've known — how long?"

"Six days."

"Six *days*, Mirabel." He said my name the way Father said the word *debt*. "Six days I've been walking the docks with men asking after me — asking after *us*, I thought they were after the rent — and you knew, and you sat on it, and you came here tonight to tell me like it's a weather report."

"I came tonight because I've spent those six days finding out how bad it was. You don't hand a man bad news until you know the size of it."

"You hand a man bad news the *moment* you have it, and you find out the size of it *together*, because that's what together is *for*." He turned away from me, then turned back, which was worse, because I could see his hands. Tobin's hands were Father's hands, big and scarred at the knuckles, and when they were calm they were gentle as anyone's. They were not calm. "What were you going to do? Solve it alone? Type us out of it?"

And there it was — the thing I had not rehearsed, because I had been too much of a coward to rehearse it — so I said it plainly, the way you take off a splint.

"I was going to take the berth."

"The what?"

"Voss offered a berth. On the Corvina. Sailing in three weeks. One berth, for whichever Dravenmoor leaves quietly and never comes back. It's his way of closing the ledger — one of us gone, the other two to take the fall, and his conscience kept warm by the thought that he *offered*. I was going to take it. I'd already started the papers."

The silence after that was the longest of my life, and I have sat through Father's silences, which are geological.

"You were going to *leave*," Tobin said. "You were going to leave me and Da here to hang for a crime the company wrote, and you were going to go off across the sea and start over, and you were going to tell us — when? At the dock? In a letter? A *typed* letter?"

"I hadn't decided when."

"You hadn't *decided when*." He laughed, and it was the worst sound in the world, because there was nothing in it. "You know what I did when I was nine? When Da had his accident? I carried water for the whole street for a month so Mam wouldn't have to. Nine years old. Nobody asked me to. Nobody *decided for me* that I'd do it. I decided, and I told everyone, because that's what a family is — it's not people you protect, it's people you *tell*."

"I was protecting you."

"You were *typing* at us." He picked up the carbon copy from the table — I hadn't noticed him take it — and held it up between two fingers like a dead fish. "You know what this is? This is what you think we are. Ink. Something you can manage on paper. You've spent six years being nobody at that office, Mirabel, six years hiding your own name like it's something you stole, and now you've got so good at it you can hide us too. Decide our whole lives in a back room and never once say *Tobin, what do you think?*"

"That's not fair."

"No," he said, "it isn't. It's worse than fair. Fair would be you asking."

* * *

### Tobin

Here is what my sister doesn't understand, and I love her, God knows I love her, but she doesn't understand it and may never: I don't need protecting. I need *counting*. There's a difference, and it's the whole difference, and she stood there in my kitchen with her clever folded papers and her six days of secret arithmetic and she never once put me in the sum.

I put the paper down before I did something to it. That's another thing about me — I know my own hands. Da taught me that. *A man who doesn't know his own hands*, he said, *ends up owned by them.* So I put the paper down and I made myself look at her, and what I saw made it worse, because she wasn't ashamed. She was *braced*. Standing there like a diver about to go under, waiting for me to finish so she could go back to holding her breath in secret.

"How long," I said, "have you been nobody?"

She blinked. "What?"

"At that office. How long have you been Mirabel Fen, the clerk who can't be seen? Six years? You changed your name at nineteen and you've been holding your breath ever since, and you're so used to it you don't even call it holding your breath. You call it *being careful*." I heard my own voice going up and I didn't stop it. "And now they've got you typing our death warrant and your plan is to die quietly in our place and sail away, and you thought — what? That we'd be *grateful*?"

"I thought someone in this family should live."

"And Da? Does Da get a vote in who lives?"

"You know Da. Da will never take that berth. Da would rather drown in this harbor with his name than breathe on another shore without it."

"Then maybe," I said, and my voice cracked on it, and I hated that it cracked, "maybe you should have *asked him*. Maybe he gets to decide what his name is worth. Maybe we all do. That's the thing about a name, Mirabel — it's not yours alone to spend. It's got all of us on it."

She sat down. Actually sat, right there on the edge of Sera's chair, like her legs had filed for resignation. And for a moment she looked nineteen again, the night Mam made the bargain with the company — I wasn't supposed to know about the bargain, but I carried the water, I carried everything, I knew — the night she stood in the doorway with one bag and said *it's only until the debt clears* and we both knew debts like that don't clear, they just change hands.

"I'm trying to keep you alive," she said, very quietly.

"I know you are." And that was the worst part. I did know. She'd been keeping us alive since she was nineteen, alone, in a fog, with a typewriter for a weapon, and she'd done it so long and so silently that she'd forgotten silence was supposed to be temporary. "But you can't keep us alive *instead of us*, Mirabel. That's not keeping. That's replacing."

She looked at her hands. Ink under the fingernails — I saw it even from across the room, black in the creases, and I thought, *she's been at the forgery already, she's been practicing being someone else, she's so far down this road she's paved it.*

"The berth," I said. "The Corvina. Three weeks."

"Yes."

"Then we've got three weeks. And in three weeks I am not going to be the brother you decided for. I'm going to be the brother who decides *with* you, whether you like it or not, and my first decision is this: we don't run yet. We find out where they moved those crates. You've got the letter; I've got the docks. Between your paper and my eyes we can find where the company's own hands touched the cargo, and if we can find that, we don't need a berth. We need a *witness list*."

"You want to fight them."

"I want us to stop being *typed*," I said. "By them or by you."

* * *

### Mirabel

He left the room first. He said he had the late tide, which was a lie, because the late tide doesn't need a man until midnight and it was barely seven, but I let him have the lie because we had both said enough true things for one evening.

I sat in his kitchen with the lamp burning low and Sera's little boots on the table, and I did the thing I always do when I've been cut, which is to reach for the ledger. But the ledger wouldn't open. Every column I tried to set down — the risk of staying, the risk of running, the cost of the berth against the cost of the gallows — came out crooked, because every column had a person in it now, and people don't sum. That was Tobin's whole argument, and I hated him for it, and he was right.

He was right, and I had typed a letter with our name in it and never once said our name out loud to the one person whose name it also was.

Before I left I straightened the boots on the table so they pointed the same way. It was a foolish thing to do. I did it anyway.

Outside, the fog had thickened until the harbor was only a sound — water working at the pilings, the Corvina's bell somewhere out in the gray, patient as a debt. Three weeks. I had come up those stairs holding one plan, and I was going down them holding a fracture, and I could not yet tell whether the fracture was in my family or in me.

Then I heard footsteps behind me on the chandlery stairs, quick and deliberate, and Tobin caught my arm at the bottom, breathless, and said:

"Mirabel — the man at the corner. Gray coat. He's been outside since before you came, and he followed you up the street, and Mirabel — he was writing something down."
