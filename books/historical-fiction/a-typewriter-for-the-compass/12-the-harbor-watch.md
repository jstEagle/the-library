## 12. The Harbor Watch

My sister tells me I have our father's stubbornness the way other people tell you you have his chin — as a fact of inheritance, not a compliment. She said it that night on the chandlery stairs, after she told me everything, after I finished shouting at her in a whisper because shouting aloud would have brought the gray-coated man up the street to see what all the fuss was about. She said, "You're as bad as Da," and what she meant was: *you will not run when running is the only sensible thing left.*

She was right. I am as bad as Da. Worse, maybe, because Da has an excuse. He was born with saltwater in his lungs and pride in place of a spine. I was born with a wife who hums when she sews and a child coming sometime this month, and every sensible bone in my body has been telling me for three days: *get on the boat, Tobin. Take the berth. Let Mirabel work her miracles with ink; that's her trade, not yours.*

But here is the thing nobody tells you about fear. It doesn't make you careful. It makes you furious. And fury, in my experience, keeps terrible hours.

Which is how I came to be lying flat on my stomach on the roof of the sailmaker's shed across from the Halloway warehouse at half past eleven on a Tuesday night, with tar from the shingles gluing itself to my shirt and the fog coming down so thick I could taste it, like drinking from a wet wool sock.

*Courage outpacing wisdom*, Mirabel would call it, if she knew, which she must never know, which is why I told her I'd be checking crab pots.

* * *

Let me back up, because even in my own head this plan deserves an honest accounting, and an honest accounting begins with the word *stupid*.

After the chandlery stairs — after Mirabel laid it all out for me in that flat, careful voice she uses when she's holding herself together with both hands — after I learned that our family name had been typed onto company paper like an item of inventory, that Voss meant to ship one of us out like contraband and hang the rest — after all that, I did not sleep. I lay in bed next to Ada and listened to her breathing, slow and even, one hand resting on the small round curve of her belly, and I thought: *they put us in a ledger.* Not hanged us, not shot us — worse. Filed us. Somewhere in the Halloway Trading Company there is a drawer, and in that drawer there is a paper, and on that paper we are a line item. Dravenmoor, T. Disposition pending.

I got up before dawn and went to the docks, because the docks are where I think. There's something about rope and tar and men shouting numbers that puts a problem into proportion.

And it was there, among the morning crowd, that I saw it — or rather, heard it. Two of the warehouse night crew, waiting for the gate, talking low the way men talk when they're being paid to talk low. I caught fragments. *Thursday's lighter.* *Not through the front manifest, through the chandlery account.* *Voss wants it logged as repair canvas.*

Repair canvas. I've mended sails since I was nine years old. Nobody logs canvas under the chandlery account unless the thing being hidden weighs about the same as canvas and is worth about forty times more. Pearls. The pearls Da saw loaded that last season — the ones the company swore never existed, the ones they used to bury our name — were still moving, still coming ashore, still passing through Halloway hands while Halloway paper said otherwise.

Mirabel had the fraud on carbon paper. What she didn't have was the route. The route was mine to find, sitting right there at the warehouse gate, wearing two men's careless voices.

So: Thursday night. The roof of the sailmaker's shed. Me, the fog, and a notebook I'd bought off a stationer who assumed I wanted it for tide tables.

*Stupid*, yes. But stupid with a purpose has kept more families fed than wisdom ever did.

* * *

The first hour taught me patience, which I did not want to learn.

The fog worked in layers. Down at street level it moved like livestock, slow and grazing, swallowing the lamplight whole so that the warehouse windows floated in nothing, four pale rectangles hung in the dark. Above, where I lay, the air was clearer by a few feet, enough that I could see the ridge of the warehouse roof and the black shape of its loading door, and beyond it, faintly, the masts along the quay leaning like tired men against a wall.

The Corvina's bell rang once, far out. Three weeks, Mirabel had said. Two and a half now. Every bell ring was a coin dropping out of the purse.

At midnight a light came on in the warehouse office window — second floor, corner room, the one with the green shade I could see silhouetted against the curtain. A man moved behind it. Broad shoulders, precise movements, the kind of man who sets a lamp down instead of putting it. I couldn't see his face. I didn't need to. I'd seen Mr. Voss twice from across the trading floor, and some men announce themselves by their posture alone. He walked like a man auditing the floorboards.

He wasn't alone long. Another shape joined him, smaller, stooped, and they stood over the desk a long time. Papers changed hands. I watched the green-shaded lamp throw their shadows huge against the ceiling, two giants wrestling over what was probably three sheets of foolscap, and I thought about how much of the world's wickedness happens at desks, in rooms like that, by men who have never once gotten their boots wet.

The smaller man left through the side door onto Chandlery Lane. Voss stayed. The lamp burned.

I wrote in my notebook, by feel, in the dark: *12:10. Voss office. Second man, unknown, papers.*

It looked very official. It looked like the sort of thing that would matter. I felt briefly like a general, and then I felt my left arm going numb from the elbow down, and the fog dripped off the shed's eaves onto the back of my neck with the regularity of a metronome, and I remembered that generals get to lie somewhere dry.

* * *

Half past one, the real business started, and it started quiet, the way the worst things always do.

No cart came to the loading door. No lanterns, no shouting of dockside orders. Instead, a lighter — a flat-bottomed barge, no bigger than a rowboat grown ambitious — slid out of the fog along the water side of the warehouse, so softly I heard it before I saw it: just the knock of a fender against pilings, polite as a knock at a door.

Two men aboard. One at the oars, one standing forward with a boathook, and neither of them carrying a lamp. They came alongside the little water-door at the warehouse's rear — the one the divers used, back when Da still dove, the one that opens straight off the tidal channel — and they tied off, and then they waited.

For twenty minutes they did nothing. That was the part that made my skin crawl, afterward, when I understood it. They weren't hiding. They were *scheduled*. This was a train arriving at its platform. The fog, the dark, the silence — none of it was cover. It was just weather happening around a transaction so routine that nobody involved bothered to disguise it. You don't hide what everyone's been paid to ignore.

Then the water-door opened from inside, spilling a wedge of yellow lamplight across the black water, and crates began coming out. Small ones. The size of bread boxes, if bread boxes were built to survive a sea crossing. Each one passed hand to hand down the line — a man inside the doorway, a man at the door's edge, the boathook man, the oarsman — smooth as communion. Four crates. Six. Eight. I counted twelve before they stopped, and I wrote *12* in my notebook and my handwriting came out steady, which surprised me, because my heart was going like a drum in a parade.

And then — this is the part I keep turning over, the part I'll be turning over at my grave — the oarsman spoke, just loud enough to carry across dead water, and I knew the voice.

Rennick. Josiah Rennick, who was diving beside my father the year the company cut costs and let the old safety lines rot. Who sat at Da's bedside after the accident and wept like a brother. Who took company coin afterward and never came near our house again, and I'd told myself he was ashamed, poor devil, ashamed to look at us.

He wasn't ashamed. He was employed.

"Last one Thursday," Rennick said to the doorway. "Then the Corvina takes the lot, and that's the account closed."

A voice from inside — I didn't know it, clipped and clerkish — said, "Voss wants the ledger entries matching the repair canvas order. Nothing else."

"Tell Voss," Rennick said, and here he laughed, short and ugly, "that some of us remember when the Dravenmoors did this work honest."

*Honest.* The word came off the water and hit me harder than any blow ever has. He said it like a joke between thieves — *remember when it was honest* — as if honesty were a season that had passed, a fashion gone out, and the theft were simply the new tailoring. As if the crime were that we'd been caught in the old style, not that the style itself was rotten.

They pushed off. The lighter faded back into the fog going south, toward the channel mouth, and the wedge of lamplight closed like a mouth finishing a sentence, and I lay on the tar roof with my notebook pressed against my chest and understood, finally and completely, what my sister had been carrying alone all these years.

Not the fear. Fear I knew. The *certainty*. The awful clarity of knowing exactly what happened and having nowhere lawful to put it. She'd held this weight in her chest since she read that carbon copy, and she'd gone on typing invoices with steady hands, and I had spent those same weeks feeling sorry for myself in a way I now found physically embarrassing.

* * *

I should have gone home. Any reasonable man would have gone home.

Instead I did the thing I will defend to no one: I followed the lighter. On foot, along the shore path, keeping parallel with the channel through fog so thick I navigated by sound — the creak of oar-locks, the lap of water against stone steps. Twice I lost them entirely and stood listening like a fool until the fog gave the sound back. Once a constable's boot heels rang out on the quay ahead of me and I flattened myself into a doorway between stacked lobster pots and stood there smelling of fish and terror while the law walked past close enough to touch, and I remember thinking, with perfect clarity, *Ada is going to kill me, and she'll be right to.*

The lighter made its landing at a private slip behind the Salt Cellar tavern — Voss's ground, everyone knows the Salt Cellar pours on company credit — and the crates went inside through a cellar hatch, and that was that. Twelve crates of pearls, logged as canvas, sleeping under a tavern until the Corvina carried them out of reach forever.

I stood in the shadow of the slip and wrote the last entry: *Salt Cellar cellar. 2:40. Rennick + 3.*

And then I laughed. Out loud, in the fog, alone, like a madman. Because it was done. Because I had it — not all of it, but enough, the shape of it, the route, the names, the schedule. Mirabel had the paper trail and I had the physical one, and between the two of us we could build something no magistrate could wave away. We wouldn't need the berth. We wouldn't need to run. We could stand, all three of us, Da included, on our own name, in our own harbor, and watch the company drown in its own ledger.

That was the feeling, standing there at ten to three in the morning: invincible. Courage at full sail, every stitch drawing, wind fair.

Wisdom, meanwhile, was nowhere aboard. I checked. Wisdom had jumped ship miles back.

* * *

I got home at four. Ada was awake, of course — she's seven months pregnant; she sleeps the way cats sleep, in shallow watchful installments — and she sat up in the dark and looked at me, soaked through, smelling of tar and salt, notebook still clenched in my fist like a hymnal, and she didn't say a single word.

That's worse than shouting. Ada's silences have tonnage.

"You're awake," I said, brilliantly.

"I'm always awake," she said. "Where were you?"

"Crab pots."

"Tobin." Just my name. She can fit an entire cross-examination into the second syllable.

So I told her. All of it — Mirabel, Voss, the letter, the berth, the frame, and now the lighter and Rennick and the Salt Cellar and the twelve crates bound for the Corvina. I told it fast and low, kneeling by the bed, and somewhere in the middle I realized I wasn't confessing, I was *recruiting*, and that Ada's face had gone from frightened to fierce somewhere around the word *canvas*, because Ada is a harbormaster's daughter and has no more sense than I do.

"You have to tell Mirabel," she said.

"I will."

"Tonight, Tobin. Today. Before she does something alone, because that girl decides alone, it's her one fault, she decides alone and tells you after." Ada put her hand on her belly, where the child turned over, a slow roll like the sea deciding something. "And then you take the berth anyway. Whatever she says. You hear me? Evidence or no evidence, you and I and this baby are getting on that ship if there's the least doubt—"

"There won't be doubt." I said it with the magnificent confidence of a man lying flat on his back looking up at a ceiling that is about to fall on him. "We're going to win, Ada. For once, we're going to win on our own ground."

She looked at me for a long moment. Fog pressed against the window. Somewhere out in the gray, a ship's bell counted the half hour, patient as a debt.

"You sound like your father," she said finally, which from Ada is both a compliment and a diagnosis.

* * *

I slept two hours and woke with the plan fully armored in my mind, which should have warned me. Plans that arrive overnight already polished are usually lying about their past.

I found Mirabel at midday, on the chandlery steps where the gray-coated man couldn't linger without becoming conspicuous, and I pulled her aside under the awning where the smell of hemp and pitch covered conversation, and I gave her my notebook.

She read it standing up, the way she reads everything, fast and complete, and I watched her face do what her face does — go still, go careful, the surface freezing over the current. When she reached the page with Rennick's name her breath actually stopped. When she reached *Corvina takes the lot* her knuckles went white around the binding.

"This is—" She looked up, and for one unguarded instant there was something blazing in her eyes that I hadn't seen since we were children, since the summer before the accident. Hope. Actual hope, naked and dangerous. "Tobin, this is the route. This closes it. Manifest plus movement — Da saw the loading, I have the letters, and now you have the transfer. No magistrate in this port can call it coincidence."

"That's what I thought," I said, grinning like an idiot.

"But." She held up one finger, and the finger was trembling slightly, and I loved her for it and hated what caused it. "But Tobin. If you could find this route in two nights, Voss can find that someone found it. He counts everything. He counts *ink*, Mirabel — he counts paper clips. A missing crate count, a clerk noticing a stranger on the sailmaker's roof, Rennick mentioning the Dravenmoor name out loud on open water—" She closed the notebook and pressed it against her chest the way Ada presses her hands against her belly. "This changes things. This makes it urgent. If he learns anyone is watching the warehouse, he won't wait for the Corvina. He'll move tonight. He'll move *now*."

"Then we move faster."

"We?" Something crossed her face — that fracture from the stairs, still healing crooked. "Tobin, I told you about the berth. One berth. I've decided—"

"You've decided alone," I said. "Ada says it's your one fault."

"It's my only talent."

And we might have fought again, there under the awning, the two of us fracturing along the same old seam — except that she stopped. Mid-word. Her eyes fixed on something past my shoulder, down the street, and every drop of blood in my body went cold before I even turned, because I have known my sister twenty-six years and I know what her face looks like when the world tilts.

I turned.

Coming up the street through the noon fog, unhurried, hands clasped behind his back like a man inspecting his own property, walked Mr. Voss. And he was not looking at the shops, or the harbor, or the sky.

He was looking directly at us. At the chandlery. At the two Dravenmoors standing together in broad daylight, in plain sight, exactly where a man who counts everything would expect to find whatever he'd sent his gray-coated man to write down.

"Mirabel," I said quietly. "How many nights did you say we have?"

Her answer was not a number. It was a whisper: *"None."*
