## 13. A Name Spoken Aloud

The Salt Kettle smelled of tar, spilled ale, and the particular despair of men who had been rained on since dawn and intended to make the whole city pay for it. I had been in it perhaps four minutes, huddled in a corner booth with my collar up, when I understood that coming here was either the bravest thing I had done in six years or the stupidest, and that history would likely record no difference between the two.

I had come to find Tobin. He had not come home the night before—not to our rooms, not to Father's loft above the chandlery—and Tobin missing a night was a thing that had happened exactly once before in his life, and that time he'd spent it bailing out a sinking skiff with his hat and had returned at dawn looking like something the tide had personally insulted.

So I went where dockmen went when they weren't working or sleeping: the Salt Kettle, where the windows were fogged from within and without, so that a person inside could be certain only of her own ignorance regarding everything beyond the glass.

He wasn't there. What was there was worse.

* * *

There is an art to listening in a tavern, and I had learned it young, because my mother had a talent for saying things in front of me that she believed children couldn't parse, and I had made a profession of parsing. You don't stare. You keep your hands busy—I had brought mending, because nothing makes a woman invisible faster than darning a sock, as though a needle were a veil. You tilt your head a degree toward the conversation you want and away from the one you don't. And you memorize voices, not faces, because faces change under lamplight and gin, but a voice is a signature.

Three booths over, a voice I didn't know was talking about my family.

"—asking after them by name," he said. He had a clerk's voice, smooth and unhurried, the kind of voice that reads ledgers aloud for pleasure. "Dravenmoor. The old man, the son, and—" a pause, the sound of a man consulting his memory, "—a daughter, they think. Nobody seems to know where she's got to."

My needle stopped mid-stitch.

"Half the harbor's named Dravenmoor if you go back far enough," said another man, older, wet through and drinking like it was medicine. "Divers breed like mussels."

"Not this lot. This lot's done. Company's finished with them." A coin clicked down on wood. "There's a purse for whoever can say where any of them sleep tonight. More if it's the daughter."

I want to tell you that my first feeling was fear, but honesty compels me to admit the first feeling was something closer to outrage—outrage that they would put a price on us, and a *modest* one, judging by the casual way the coin landed. We were worth perhaps two months of a clerk's wages. My father had pulled pearls up from thirty feet of black water for that company. I typed its invoices in a hand so flawless Mr. Pellingsworth had once held a page to the light and said, *Whoever does this work has no soul, only discipline.* Two months' wages.

The second feeling was fear, and it arrived properly, settling into my ribs like cold water into boot leather.

Because the man asking was not a company man. Not quite. He had the soft hands of one, but he wore them in a tavern among stevedores, which meant Voss wanted this done quietly, deniably, through intermediaries who could be disowned by Thursday. Which meant the net was already closing, and my brother—who had missed a night, who had been doing *something*, God help me, in the dark around the Halloway warehouse—was walking through it blindfolded.

And then Tobin walked in.

* * *

You would think, having imagined this moment on the walk over—the reunion, the relief, possibly a scene involving my brother being dramatically rescued—that I would have had some plan better than *sit very still and pray*. But there was no praying to be done, because Tobin came through the door shaking off rain like a dog, spotted the informant's booth, and headed directly toward it with the expression of a man going to collect a debt.

Tobin has never once in his life entered a room quietly. It is his single greatest flaw and, on the docks, nearly fatal.

I did the arithmetic in the space of three heartbeats. If he reached that table, he would be recognized—his face was known to every man who'd ever loaded a crate beside him—and whatever questions he asked, however cleverly disguised, would confirm what the purse-hunter already suspected: that the Dravenmoors were close enough to touch. Worse, Tobin would ask his questions *loudly*, because Tobin's idea of discretion was lowering his voice only when reminded.

So I stood up.

Understand what that cost me. Six years I had spent teaching myself to be furniture. At Halloway Trading I was Miss—nothing, a girl at the typewriter, a pair of hands attached to a machine, addressed as "you there" and grateful for even that. I had perfected the art of the lowered gaze, the apology-ready posture, the smile that promised I would trouble no one. Invisibility was not merely my habit; it was my craft, my armor, the one inheritance my mother had managed to leave me besides the debt.

And now I crossed a tavern full of dockmen toward a stranger with a clerk's soft hands, and I let them all look at me.

"Tobin." I caught his sleeve at the third booth, and my voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect. "Mother's asking for you. She's been up all night."

Tobin looked at me, and I watched him perform a small miracle: he read my face, understood that the words were false and the fear in them was true, and shut his mouth. For once. "Right," he said. "Sorry. I lost track of—"

"You always do." I turned to the informant, and here is where I should confess that I had planned nothing—no speech, no strategy, just a rising panic and a lifetime of watching how power dressed itself. So I borrowed Voss's manner, cool and faintly bored, the voice of a woman with somewhere better to be. "You're the one asking after the Dravenmoors."

The man's eyebrows rose, pleasant and predatory. "News travels."

"Faster than sense, apparently." I set a coin of my own on the table—my last shilling above rent money, spent now on theater—and leaned in slightly. "Whatever you've heard, you're late. And you're wrong. There's nothing left to find. The family scattered after the father's accident. The old man drinks. The son owes everyone. Whatever purse your principal is offering, he'll get more satisfaction buying himself a decent coat."

"And who might you be, miss, to know all this?"

Every eye in the near booths had found us. Dockmen are not subtle; they are crows, and a scene is a scene. I felt their attention land on me like weather.

And I heard myself say it.

"I'm Mirabel Dravenmoor."

The name came out whole, unbroken, in my own voice, and the strangest thing was not the terror—it was the sound itself. Six years I had signed other names, answered to other names, worn anonymity like a second corset, cinched tight and aching. And the name fit. That was the horror of it. It had always fit. I had simply refused to try it on.

The informant's face did something complicated—triumph wrestling with surprise, surprise losing. "Well," he said softly. "That saves a great deal of legwork."

"It saves you nothing," I said, "because I'm leaving, and my brother with me, and you may tell your employer that if a single hair on anyone's head is touched before the Corvina sails, I will walk into the Custom House and say this name again, loudly, to men with official stationery." I straightened. "Tell him the daughter types. Tell him she keeps copies."

That last part was a bluff. Mostly. The carbon paper in my coat pocket was real; my plan for it was still mostly nerve. But the man's eyes flickered—he was a clerk, clerks respect paperwork the way priests respect relics—and I saw the message land somewhere behind them.

I walked out with Tobin's arm in mine, past forty pairs of eyes, into the rain, and only when we were half a street gone did my knees inform me of their opinion of all this.

* * *

We ducked under the fish-market awning, where the smell could strip paint and no respectable informant would follow, and Tobin rounded on me.

"What in God's name did you do?"

"What you were too slow to do. You were going to *ask him questions*, Tobin. You were going to stroll up to the man hunting us and ask him questions, loudly, in a tavern, like a man ordering dinner."

"I was going to find out who sent him!"

"You found out. I told him who I am, which means by tomorrow morning everyone will have found out, which means—" My breath snagged, and I hated that it snagged, hated that he saw it. "Which means there's no taking it back."

He stared at me. Rain dripped off the awning between us in a thin silver line. "Mira. Do you understand what you've done? They've been looking for us in the dark. Now you've lit a lamp and stood next to it."

"Yes."

"Why?"

It was a fair question and I took my time answering, because the true answer had several layers and I hadn't finished excavating them myself. Because he'd have been taken alone and unprepared. Because a target that announces itself can also announce terms. Because I was so tired—bone-tired, soul-tired—of being a nameless girl in a gray dress while men with soft hands priced my family like salvage.

"Because they were hunting shadows," I said finally. "Shadows can be taken quietly, disappeared, blamed. People can't. Not as easily. Not if the people stand in daylight and say *here I am, and here is what I know.*" I touched the pocket where the carbon copy lay against my hip. "I have proof of what they did, Tobin. Proof needs a witness. A witness needs a name. I've spent six years being nobody, and it kept me safe and useless, and I am done being both."

Tobin was quiet a long moment. Rain ticked on the canvas overhead. When he spoke, his voice had gone rough. "Father's going to kill you."

"Father's going to be furious," I agreed, "and then he's going to be proud, and he'll hate the proud part more than the furious part, and we'll both pretend otherwise."

That earned me half a laugh, strangled though it was. Then his face sobered, and he told me where he'd been—crouched behind salt barrels since midnight, watching crates come off the *Corvina*'s consignment route and go into the Halloway warehouse marked as ballast, watching men whose hands were wrong for dockwork handle boxes too carefully. He'd written it all down. My brother, who signs his name like it costs him money, had filled four pages.

"You've been building a case," I said.

"You've been typing one." He shrugged, awkward. "Seemed only fair I contribute."

* * *

By evening, as I had predicted, the story had outrun us both. Mrs. Calloway downstairs heard it from the fishwife, who heard it from a stevedore who'd been in the Kettle: the Dravenmoor girl had stood up in the Salt Kettle and named herself to a spy's face, dared him to do his worst, and walked out untouched. By the version that reached the chandlery, I had thrown the man into the harbor, which I regretted not being able to claim.

Word spreads in a port city the way oil spreads on water—thinly, everywhere, impossible to recall. And I learned something that night about visibility that no one warns you of: it is not a door you open once. It is a lamp in a window, and lamps draw every moth and every thief in the district.

Some of what it drew was good. An old diver's widow pressed my hand at the market and said, "Your father pulled my husband from the bottom in '04, and I never forgot it, and now maybe someone won't forget you." A boy left a note at the chandlery: *If you need witnesses, half the docks saw the crates.* Kindness, arriving like tide, unasked.

But there was a note of another kind too, unsigned, left under our door, in a hand I knew—smooth, unhurried, a ledger-keeper's script I had studied for six years across a hundred invoices:

*Miss Dravenmoor — The Corvina sails in eighteen days, not three weeks. Berths, unlike names, cannot be reclaimed once given. Come see me. Bring your courage; you will need less of it than you think.*

Voss. He knew. Of course he knew—he had known before the ale in the Kettle went sour, probably; the man collected information the way other men collected debts. And the shortened deadline told me what his letter's politeness concealed: the net was drawing closed, and he was offering me the same terrible mercy he'd offered before, with less rope attached.

I sat by the window a long while with the note in my lap, watching fog swallow the harbor lights one by one. Somewhere out there Tobin was walking home in the dark, wearing a face that half the port now knew belonged to a Dravenmoor. Somewhere out there my father slept above the chandlery, dreaming, I hoped, of water rather than ledgers. And somewhere a man who had once taken a berth like the Corvina's and left a sister behind was waiting for me to choose.

Eighteen days. The fog pressed at the glass like a question.

I took out pen and paper, wrote *Mirabel Anne Dravenmoor* at the top of the page—the first time I had written it in years, the letters strange and familiar as a childhood house—and beneath it began to list everything I knew.
