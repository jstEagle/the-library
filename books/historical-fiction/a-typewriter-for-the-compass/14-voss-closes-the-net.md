## 14. Voss Closes the Net

There is a particular quality to bad news that arrives on a Tuesday. It does not come with the theatrical weight of a Friday disaster, when one has the weekend to compose oneself and rehearse one's grief for the directors. No—Tuesday's bad news arrives while the ink is still wet on ordinary correspondence, while the clerks are still sharpening pencils, while the whole machine of the Halloway Trading Company is humming along in its usual self-satisfied way, entirely unaware that somewhere in its gears a small, patient hand has been at work.

I learned of it from Mr. Quill, my informant at the Anchor & Chain, who earns his keep by drinking other men's secrets and mine by the pint.

"A woman stood up in the taproom last night," he said, settling into the chair across my desk as though it were a confessional and he had come to make a particularly juicy confession. "Called herself Dravenmoor. Out loud. Twice."

I did not look up from the manifest I was annotating. Looking up is a concession, and I had already conceded enough that morning. "Drunk?"

"Sober as a customs officer. She stepped between a man asking questions and her brother like a wall going up. Told the room her name was Mirabel Dravenmoor and that anyone wanting the family would have to go through her." He paused, savoring it. "Half the harbor heard before closing."

"Then half the harbor will be repeating it by noon, and the other half by supper." I set down my pen. "You're certain it was the clerk? The one from the typing pool?"

"Same face. Same voice, once you know what you're listening for." Quill smiled the smile of a man who has just priced something. "Funny thing, Mr. Voss. A clerk who hides her name for years, then hands it out free of charge in a public house. Either she's gone mad, or she's decided hiding costs more than standing."

I thanked him, paid him double, and sat alone for some time after he'd gone, listening to the foghorn out in the roads making its slow, idiot complaint against the morning.

So. The girl had stepped into the light.

I want to say I was surprised. I am not permitted the luxury of surprise; surprise is for men whose plans do not require contingency. But I will confess—and this page is sealed, or ought to be—that for perhaps four minutes I simply looked out the window at the gray water and felt something adjacent to admiration. Four minutes. Then I opened the drawer where I keep the arithmetic and got back to work.

* * *

Let me explain how a man closes a net, since everyone imagines it is done with violence. Violence is what amateurs use. A net is closed with paper.

The Dravenmoor problem had always been, in my estimation, a problem of tidiness rather than danger. An old diver crippled by his own ambition, a son who hauled rope on the docks, a daughter who typed invoices under a false name so unremarkable that I had forgotten it twice. The company needed someone to carry the weight of the contraband should the customs service ever grow curious, and the Dravenmoors—indebted, isolated, and proud in that shabby way of people who have lost everything but their opinions—were the natural choice. One does not frame a family that has friends. Friends complicate testimony.

For eleven years the arrangement had held because no one with the name had ever said it aloud.

And now the daughter had said it aloud in a tavern full of dockmen, informants, and at least two men on the customs payroll.

I called in Mr. Halloway himself—not the founder, who was dead and beyond inconvenience, but his nephew, who chaired the board and possessed the rare gift of being able to hear terrible news without letting it reach his face. We met in the directors' room with the shutters drawn, which in that building meant privacy, and in any other building would have meant farce.

"The girl has exposed herself," I told him. "Voluntarily. Which means she intends to do more than expose herself."

Halloway steepled his fingers, an affectation he had borrowed from his dead uncle along with the chair. "She knows about the shipments."

"She has known about the shipments since I put the letter in front of her. That was the point of the letter—or rather, the point was the letter's existence, and hers was the only hand I trusted to type it without questions." I had explained this reasoning to myself many times and it had never once improved. "What concerns me is not what she knows. It is what she has been doing with the knowing. Forged papers, I suspect. Possibly a manifest. Possibly worse."

"Worse than a manifest?"

"There is nothing worse than a manifest," I said. "A manifest is a confession with a shipping date."

We agreed—I proposed, he blessed, which is how boards function—on three measures. First, the berth on the *Corvina*, which I had offered as a quiet exit for whichever Dravenmoor could be persuaded to vanish, would remain available but its terms would change: sailing in ten days instead of three weeks, and only for a Dravenmoor who left without testimony, without documents, without looking back. Second, the girl would be dismissed from the typing pool immediately, quietly, with a month's wages and a warning dressed as kindness. Third—

Third was the part I did not enjoy saying aloud, though I said it anyway, in the flat voice I reserve for such sentences.

"We tip the port authority. Anonymous information. Sufficient detail to justify warrants for all three Dravenmoors before the *Corvina* sails."

Halloway looked at me over his steeple. "All three? Even the ones who cooperate?"

"Especially the ones who cooperate. Cooperation is only credible if refusing it carries a price." I gathered my papers. "Besides, Mr. Halloway, we are not running a charity for the families we ruin. We are running a trading company. The distinction matters to the shareholders."

He nodded, relieved, as men always are, to learn that their cruelty can be filed under commerce.

* * *

That afternoon I went down to the harbor myself. I dislike going down to the harbor. The air there smells of tar and honest work, both of which disagree with me, and the men who work the cranes look at a man in a good coat the way wolves look at a dog that has gotten above itself. But there are things one cannot delegate, and watching is one of them.

I found the Dravenmoor son at the east quay, coiling rope with the easy brutality of a man whose body has never once betrayed him. Tobin, the records said. Twenty-six. Wife expecting within the month—a fact I knew because knowing such facts is my profession, and I am very good at it. He worked with his head down, but not the way the other dockmen kept their heads down. They kept theirs down to avoid the foreman's eye. His was down the way a man's is when he is listening to everything.

I stood at a respectful distance, beside a stack of crates that smelled of salt pork, and watched him for twenty minutes. He never looked at me directly. But twice, as I shifted position, his shoulders changed—the smallest alteration, a quarter-inch of tension, the body's own ledger recording a debt before the mind has read the entry.

Good instincts, the brother. Instincts are the one form of intelligence that cannot be forged, and therefore the one I trust least.

As I turned to go, a gull screamed overhead with the personal grievance gulls reserve for all mankind, and Tobin Dravenmoor straightened up and looked straight at me across forty feet of wet stone. Not hostile. Not afraid. *Taking my measure.* Writing me into whatever account he kept behind those eyes.

I touched my hat brim—an old habit, meaningless, deniable—and walked away feeling, absurdly, as though I had been the one observed.

* * *

In my office that evening I wrote the letter to the port authority. Anonymous, of course. I have written so many anonymous letters over the years that I sometimes wonder whether they constitute the bulk of my actual correspondence, and whether posterity, sorting through my effects, will conclude that I spent my life writing to no one.

The letter named the Dravenmoor operation. It described the smuggling route with just enough error to seem authentic—errors are the signature of truth, and I am a master forger of errors. It gave dates, berth numbers, the names of two minor accomplices who would hang nicely and knew nothing worth hanging them for. And it requested, in the oblique grammar of informants everywhere, that action be taken *before the departure of the vessel Corvina, presently berthed at Pier Nine.*

Before the departure. You see the elegance. If the authorities move early, the Dravenmoors are arrested and the matter ends in a courtroom where our paperwork is impeccable. If the authorities dawdle—as authorities do, being composed largely of committees—the family flees on the *Corvina* and the matter ends at sea, which is even better. Either way, the net closes. The only outcome I could not permit was the third one: the family staying, talking, testifying. A cornered animal must be given exactly two exits, and both must lead away from you.

I sealed the letter, addressed it in a hand that was not mine, and set it aside for the morning post.

Then I sat with my whiskey and let myself think about my sister.

I do this perhaps twice a year, on schedule, the way other men visit graves. Her name was Lottie. In 1889, when I was nineteen and she was sixteen, we stood in a port not unlike this one—colder, meaner, further north—and I held two tickets for a ship leaving ahead of the constables, and I made the same arithmetic the girl Dravenmoor is presumably making now: one ticket spare, two of us, who goes? I reasoned that I was the one with the head for business, that Lottie could stay with our aunt until I sent for her, that sending for her was a thing that would obviously happen once I had established myself.

I established myself. I did not send for her. There was never a right moment, and then there was a wrong one—fever, the winter of '92, a letter from the aunt written in a hand like a spider drowning—and after that there was no moment at all, only the fact of it, which I folded up and put in the same drawer as the rest of my arithmetic.

People imagine that men like me feel nothing. The opposite is true. We feel everything; we have simply learned to file it. Grief, filed properly, becomes efficiency. Guilt, filed properly, becomes generosity of a very controlled kind—which is why I had offered the berth at all, though I would have shot anyone who suggested it.

And now here was this clerk, this diver's daughter with ink on her fingers, doing openly and defiantly the thing I had done in secret and badly. Choosing who sails. Standing in a tavern and saying her name aloud as though a name were armor.

It occurred to me, pouring the second whiskey, that she might be better at this than I had been.

It occurred to me, pouring the third, that this was intolerable.

* * *

The dismissal notice went to the typing pool the next morning, drafted in the mildest language the firm's solicitor would permit: *redundancy of position, effective immediately, one month's wages in consideration of faithful service.* Faithful service. I signed it and thought of her hands on the keys—those quick, careful hands I had watched for years without seeing, the way one does not see a lock until the day it fails to open.

By noon the port authority had my anonymous letter. By evening, I had confirmation from a clerk in the customs house—another man on the company's quiet payroll—that a warrant was being drawn up. Three names. Mirabel, Tobin, and the father, Aldous Dravenmoor, formerly of the diving fleet, currently of a rented room above the chandlery on Rope Walk, where he sat, according to my information, staring at the sea he could no longer enter, like a man widowed by his own legs.

Ten days until the *Corvina*. Nine, now. The warrant would issue within the week.

Everything was proceeding exactly as designed, and I want it recorded—in this private ledger, in this sealed account of a man's conscience, if such a thing survives—that proceeding exactly as designed has never once made me sleep well.

Because here is the flaw in my design, the flaw I did not see until I saw her standing in that tavern in my mind's eye, saying her name like a woman planting a flag: I had assumed the Dravenmoors would behave like frightened people. Frightened people run, or freeze, or bargain. I had built the net for frightened people.

But the daughter had stopped being frightened somewhere between the sealed letter and the taproom, and I had missed the exact moment of it. That is the trouble with watching people for years—you begin to believe you know them, and the ones who surprise you are always the quiet ones, the ones you filed under *harmless* and never reopened.

There was one more thing. Small, easily dismissed, and therefore not dismissed. My night watchman reported that someone had been in the record room after hours on Thursday—no sign of entry, no sign of theft, but the carbon-copy wastebasket disturbed, and a faint smell of lamp oil that lingered past dawn. He blamed rats. I have found that when a man blames rats, he is usually correct, except on the occasions when he is catastrophically wrong.

If the girl had taken the carbon copy of the letter—if she had proof of what the letter implied—then she was not merely hiding anymore. She was building. Quietly, methodically, with the same patience she had used to type my words perfectly while her hands shook.

I stood at my window a long time, watching the lights of Pier Nine where the *Corvina* lay black against the blacker water, nine days from sailing, and I revised my design the way one revisits a wound: reluctantly, thoroughly.

Two exits for a cornered animal. Both leading away from me.

Unless the animal had decided not to leave at all.

I took out a fresh sheet of paper and wrote a note to my man at the chandlery: *Watch the old diver. Watch the son. And find out what the daughter keeps in her desk.* Then I sat back, and the foghorn sounded out in the roads, long and low and idiotic, and for the first time in eleven years the sound of it seemed less like a warning to ships than a countdown—to something coming ashore.
