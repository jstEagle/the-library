## 15. Father's Last Dive

My father had a saying about the sea, which was that it never lied to you, it only let you lie to yourself. He said it the way other men said grace—automatically, with the conviction of long repetition—and I had spent twenty-three years believing it applied to weather and tides and not at all to daughters who forged manifests in their landlady's kitchen.

The morning after I learned Voss had set his watchers on us, I found my father on the shingle below the chandlery, mending a net he hadn't used in six years. His hands still knew the work. That was the cruelty of it. The sea had taken his ear—that last dive, eleven years ago, the one that paid for my mother's coffin and left him listing to port like an old boat—but it had never once offered to take his hands.

"You've been to see the manifest," he said, without looking up. Not a question. The whole harbor probably smelled the ink on me by now.

"I've been writing letters," I said, which was true, and also the sort of lie the sea permits.

"Mm." He drew the twine through his fingers, tight, deliberate. "Your brother tells me you've gone quiet. Tobin says when you go quiet, it's because you're carrying something heavier than your coat."

"Tobin should mind his own cargo."

"Tobin is minding yours, because you won't." Now he looked up, and one eye—the good one, the one the pressure had spared—held me the way a cleat holds a line. "Mirabel."

Not *girl*. Not *you there*. My name, spoken aloud, in daylight, on open shingle, where anyone might hear. My father had never learned the habit of hiding it the way the rest of us had. He wore *Dravenmoor* like a coat he refused to take off even when the company took everything else.

"There are men watching this house," I said, keeping my voice low. "There will be officers by week's end. Papa, we have perhaps four days."

"Then say what you came to say."

What I'd come to say was: *come away, come away, let the name sink, names can be replaced, fathers cannot.* What I actually said, standing over him with the fog pressing down like a wet wool blanket, was: "The crates are still out there. Aren't they."

His hands went still on the net.

I had pieced it together from Tobin's observations and my father's own mutterings across three years of Sunday dinners—the cache of contraband pearls Halloway's directors had sunk in a weighted crate off Gull Rock, too hot to sell, too valuable to destroy, waiting for the scandal to cool. Waiting for eleven years. And now, with the customs inquiry circling closer and Voss desperate, the company would want that crate recovered and moved—or destroyed—before anyone could match its contents to the shipping ledgers.

"They sent divers down Tuesday," my father said quietly. "Two young ones, company men. They couldn't find it. Currents have shifted since '05; the rock shelf collapsed some. They're diving blind." A pause, and then, with something terrible and gentle in his ruined face: "I could find it in my sleep. I dove that shelf for nineteen years."

"No," I said.

"It isn't a request, Mirabel. It's a fact. There's a crate down there with Halloway's mark burned into the lid, and inside it enough seed pearl to hang three directors. You've built your paper proof—I've seen the girl you've become, you build proofs like other people breathe—but paper can be called forgery. Paper can be denied. Pearls in a company crate cannot." He rose, slowly, the way he rose now, in stages, like a ship taking on water in reverse. "They'll move it ashore soon or dump it in deep water. Either way, it's gone, and so is our name's chance of coming back up with it."

"The currents alone—" My voice cracked and I hated it. "Papa, you can't hold your breath the way you did. Your ear. Your chest. Eleven years, and you're sixty-one—"

"Sixty," he said, with dignity. "And I'm not planning to stay down long. Just long enough to look. Long enough to know where it lies, if they've moved it, if it's marked. Eyes, girl. All I'm giving them is eyes." He smiled then, and it was the smile I remembered from before the accident, the one that meant he'd already won the argument in his own head and was merely allowing me time to catch up. "You taught yourself to read a ledger no one would show you. Where do you think you learned stubbornness? It wasn't your mother. God rest her, she was reasonable as rain."

* * *

I did not sleep. I sat in my room above the chandlery with the forged manifest spread before me, finished except for the one column that required what my father promised to bring up from the dark: confirmation of the crate's position, its markings, its contents. Without it, the document was a strong argument. With it, it was a verdict.

Tobin came at midnight, quiet as tide through the door I'd left unlatched. He didn't ask how I'd known he'd come. We were past that.

"He told you, then," Tobin said. "He told you what he means to do."

"He means to *look*, he says."

"And you believe him?"

I thought about my father's hands on the net, sure and unhurried, and about all the years I'd watched him stand at the harbor wall on rough days, watching water he was forbidden to enter, like a man visiting a wife in prison. "No," I said. "But I believe he'll go whether I believe him or not. And I'd rather he go with someone watching from shore who knows what a drowning man looks like than go alone at slack water with nobody the wiser."

"Then we watch together," Tobin said. "Sela's asleep. She thinks I'm checking nets." He hesitated. "Mira. If he goes under and doesn't come up—"

"Then we'll know exactly what the company cost us," I said, "down to the last pennyweight. And I will spend the rest of my life making them pay it back with interest." I heard my own voice and barely recognized it—not wry, not careful, not the clerk's flat instrument I'd tuned for eleven years. Something older. Something with salt in it.

* * *

We went down at first light, the hour when the fog thins but hasn't committed to leaving, when the harbor belongs to gulls and the occasional guilty fisherman. My father carried his old weight belt over one shoulder like a sleeping child and his knife at his hip, and he walked the shingle with the rolling gait of a man stepping back into a room he'd been locked out of for a decade.

Gull Rock stood a quarter mile offshore, black teeth in gray water. Between us and it, the tide ran slack, the surface smooth as poured pewter. My father stood at the waterline and looked out, and I saw him do the thing divers do—I'd forgotten, or never truly known—reading the water the way I read a ledger, finding the story in the surface.

"Shelf runs out from the north face," he said. "Crate was wedged in the second channel, forty feet, maybe less now with the silt. If they haven't moved it." He began strapping on the belt. "If I'm not up in eight minutes—"

"Papa—"

"—eight minutes, Mirabel. Count them. Don't come in after me. You swim like a typewriter sinks." He grinned at Tobin, gripped his shoulder once, hard, and then, turning to me, he did the thing that undid me entirely: he took my hand, this man who had touched me perhaps twice a year since Mother died, and pressed something small and cold into my palm. His diving knife, wrapped in oilcloth.

"Family keeps what family earns," he said. "Earned it in 1898. Kept it through everything. Now it keeps itself in your desk until I'm back to reclaim it."

"That's superstition," I managed.

"That's collateral," he said. "Now count, daughter. Count like your ledger depends on it. Because it does."

He waded in without ceremony, no splash, no flourish, just a diver entering his element after too long an exile, and then the water closed over his head and he was gone, and I began to count.

One. Two. Three. The fog held its breath. Four. Five. Tobin's jaw worked silently beside me. Six. Seven. A gull screamed somewhere overhead, obscene and indifferent. Eight.

Nothing.

Nine. Ten. The pewter surface lay unbroken, smooth as a closed ledger. Eleven. Twelve, and my counting dissolved into pure animal terror, and I was pulling off my boots when Tobin caught my arm—

And then the water broke.

He came up gasping, coughing, one arm raised—not waving, *pointing*—and we dragged him onto the shingle more than he climbed, and he lay there wheezing like a bellows with a split seam while blood trickled from his burst ear and painted a thin red thread down his jaw.

"Gone," he rasped. "Moved. Three days past—drag marks in the silt, fresh, wide runners. They brought it ashore." He coughed, spat seawater, laughed—a broken, furious sound. "Crates. Plural. I saw the second one. Half-buried at the new drop point. And I saw where they took the first one, Mira. I followed the drag line with my eyes as far as the light would carry." His good hand seized my wrist, grip fierce despite the shaking. "The warehouse. The old salt store behind Halloway's main building. The one they sealed after the fire scare in March."

I knelt there on the freezing shingle, holding my father's head above the wet stones, watching blood thread from his ear into the tide, and I understood with perfect, sickening clarity what he had bought with that burst ear and those eight minutes and thirteen seconds underwater.

Proof. Location. A place I could put my hands on the truth—paper truth, physical truth, truth with Halloway's mark burned into wood.

Also: a father who would never dive again, who might never hear properly again on his one working side, who lay gasping on the stones because his daughter's war needed one more casualty and he had volunteered before anyone thought to ask him.

"You foolish, brave, impossible old man," I whispered, pressing my forehead to his.

"Worth it," he wheezed. "Worth every—" Another cough. "Girl. Listen. The drag marks. Fresh. They'll empty that salt store within days, burn the crates same as they burned the records. Whatever you mean to do with your beautiful forgery—" his eye fixed on mine, fierce and fading in and out of focus, "—do it *fast*."

I looked up at Tobin. Tobin looked down at me. Behind us the fog was finally lifting off the water, and out in the roads the Corvina rode at anchor, three days from sailing, and somewhere in the custom house a clock was running that none of us could see.

"Get him home," I said, standing, wringing the seawater out of my skirt with hands that had stopped shaking somewhere around minute nine and would apparently never shake again. "Get Sela to brew her willow bark. And Tobin—"

"Mira?"

"Fetch me the key to the salt store's yard gate. I don't care whose pocket it sleeps in tonight. By tomorrow it sleeps in mine."

I turned toward the city with my father's knife heavy in my pocket and the fog lifting off the harbor like a curtain going up, and behind me I could hear Tobin swearing softly at the sky, and ahead of me, sealed behind a padlocked door on Anchor Lane, the truth sat in its crate, waiting to be typed.
