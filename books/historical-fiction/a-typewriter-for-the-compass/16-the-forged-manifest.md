## 16. The Forged Manifest

There is a particular kind of silence that settles over a room when a woman is about to commit a crime with a fountain pen. I had become something of an expert in silences by then — the silence of my father's kitchen at dawn, the silence of the typing pool when Mr. Voss walked through it, the silence of the harbor fog swallowing a ship's bell. But this one, in the back room of the chandlery with a lamp turned low and my sleeves rolled to the elbow, was the quietest of all. It was the silence of paper waiting.

Paper is patient. That's what nobody tells you about documents. A lie on paper doesn't sweat, doesn't blink, doesn't shift its weight from foot to foot the way a liar does. It just sits there, inked and immaculate, and lets other people do its trembling for it.

I intended not to be one of those people.

* * *

My father's testimony — if you could call it that; he called it "what I seen," and he said it twice, once for me and once for Tobin, and then refused to say it a third time on the grounds that saying things three times made them gossip — came to me in fragments over two nights of broth and bandages. He sat propped in his chair by the stove, one arm splinted, his shoulder wrapped like a mummy's, and he talked the way divers talk: economically, as though every breath were rationed.

"The crates come off the *Corvina*'s sister ship, the *Pelican*. Night run, no lights. Halloway's own lighter took them upriver past the customs buoy."

"Who signed the lighter out?"

"Halloway's man. Young fellow, nervous. Voss stood on the quay with his coat collar up like he thought the fog was taking notes." My father almost smiled at that. Almost. "Crates went into warehouse three. The pearls come back down two nights later, packed in oyster shells, papers marked 'shell stock, low grade.' Somebody wrote 'Dravenmoor operation' on the manifest because somebody had to write something, and we were cheap."

Cheap. That was the word that did it, more than the smuggling, more than the frame. We had been *cheap*. Convenient. A name to write in the column marked *culprit*, chosen the way you'd choose a pencil stub over a new one.

Tobin brought the rest. He'd spent four nights doing what he called "walking" and what I called "loitering with intent," cataloguing the comings and goings at warehouse three with a fisherman's patience. Times. Faces. The number on the lighter. The fact that the night watchman drank his flask empty by eleven and saw nothing after that but the inside of his own eyelids. Tobin had even charmed the watchman's nephew into showing him the freight book's cover page, so I knew the format of Halloway's internal manifests down to the width of the ruled columns.

"You're going to make them believe this paper," Tobin said, watching me lay out my materials like a surgeon laying out knives. "You understand that. If they look too close—"

"They won't look too close. That's the whole art of it. People don't read documents, Tobin. They *recognize* them. They check that the shape is familiar and then they stop looking, because looking is work and work is unpleasant." I smoothed the blank ledger sheet flat. "I'm going to give them something so familiar it bores them."

"And where does a clerk who hides her own name learn to bore customs officers?"

"Three years of typing invoices, little brother. There is no better school for tedium in all the empire."

* * *

The forgery itself took three days, and I will set down honestly what those days cost me, because history has a habit of sanding the terror off these stories until they gleam like the pearls nobody actually smuggled.

Day one was paper. Halloway used a particular stock — heavy, faintly blue-grey, watermarked with a stag's head that only showed when you held it against the light. I couldn't buy it; buying it would leave a trail wider than the fog bank. So I stole it, which sounds worse than it felt, which is its own confession. I did it during the lunch hour, when the stationery cupboard stood unlocked and the junior clerks were at the pie shop arguing about football. I took nine sheets and a length of the official ribbon, and my heart behaved like a trapped bird the entire time, and my face — thank God for my father's training in keeping your face still while your lungs burn — my face did nothing at all.

Nine sheets, because I knew myself. I would spoil at least six.

Day two was the hand. Not mine — Mr. Aldous Finch's, the shipping clerk whose signature legitimized every manifest that left warehouse three. I had typed beneath that signature for three years. I knew its architecture the way you know a staircase in the dark: the aggressive downstroke on the *A*, the loop on the *d* that never quite closed, the final flourish on the *h* that trailed off like a man leaving a room without saying goodbye. Finch was a hurried man. His signature was hurried. That was the mercy of it — I didn't have to draw it, I had to *rush* it, and rushing is easier to fake than care.

I filled six sheets practicing. I wrote *Aldous Finch* until the name stopped being a name and became a motion, a flick of wrist and pen, until I could produce it half-distracted, while answering a question, while my hands shook. Because my hands would shake. On the night that mattered, they would shake, and the signature had to survive the shaking.

Here is what I learned about forgery that nobody warns you about: it is not deception, mostly. It is *attention*. To copy a man's hand you must study him more closely than his wife ever has. You learn the hesitations in his letters, the places his confidence fails him, the small cowardice in the way he crosses his t's low instead of high. By the end of day two I knew Aldous Finch better than I have ever known any living person, and I felt a strange, unwelcome tenderness toward him. Somewhere across town he was eating his supper, ignorant that a woman had memorized his soul through his penmanship.

Day three was the content. This was the part that frightened me least and should have frightened me most, because content is where forgeries die. A forged hand fools the eye; forged facts fool nothing. Every date, every weight, every entry had to sit inside the truth like a pearl inside an oyster — surrounded, cushioned, indistinguishable from the living thing around it.

So I built the manifest around what was real. The *Pelican*'s arrival was real; Tobin had the date from the harbor master's posted board. The lighter number was real; he'd copied it off the hull himself, standing close enough to touch it. Warehouse three was real, the shell-stock notation was real — I had typed it myself, forty times over two years, never once asking why low-grade shell moved at midnight. The fraud wasn't a document someone had invented. It was a true document with one false column, and my job was to restore the column they'd removed: destination, consignee, and value. The real buyer. The real price. The numbers that would make a customs officer sit down very slowly and send for his superior.

For those figures I used my father's knowledge of what a cargo of that size fetched, and Tobin's count of crates, and one thing more — a detail I lifted from the wastebasket carbon of Voss's sealed letter, the fraction of a figure he'd thought destroyed. Voss had taught me everything I knew about covering tracks. It seemed only courteous to repay him.

Then the dating. Documents carry their birthdates in their bodies, in the ink's age and the crease of the fold. You cannot forge yesterday convincingly. So I dated the manifest three months old and then aged it — tea-staining done thin as breath, folded along lines already worn soft, rubbed at the corners with a cork until the paper looked handled, filed, forgotten. I even gave it a coffee ring, because in my experience no document in a working office survives three months without acquiring one, and a manifest too clean to have been spilled upon would be a manifest too clean to be believed.

I signed it at two in the morning, by lamplight, with my heart going like the engine room of the *Corvina* herself.

And here is the thing I will carry to my grave, whatever else becomes of me: my hands were steady. Perfectly steady. After three years of hiding, of flinching, of typing other people's lies with a straight spine and a closed mouth, my hands had learned the trick of it so well they no longer asked my permission. The fear was all mine, lodged somewhere behind my ribs like a swallowed stone. The hands just worked. Steady hands over a pounding heart — if there is a truer description of a Dravenmoor woman, I have not found it. Perhaps my mother could have written it. She had steadied her hands over worse than ink.

I signed *Aldous Finch* in a rush, exactly as he would have, late on a Friday, thinking of his supper. Then I signed the countersignature line — Voss's initials, small and cold as frost — because the manifest had to pass through his office to be believable, and because I wanted, with a spite that surprised me, to make him initial his own undoing.

* * *

Planting it was the last cruelty, and I won't pretend I enjoyed deciding it, because I didn't. There were only two places a customs officer would find a Halloway internal manifest: the company's own records room, or the files of the harbormaster's court, where duplicates of disputed manifests were deposited for review. The first meant breaking into Halloway. The second meant persuading the harbormaster's clerk to accept a filing from a woman with no standing and no name worth speaking.

Or — third option — it meant letting the file arrive the way most inconvenient truths arrive: by accident, or something shaped very much like one.

Tobin favored the direct approach. "We walk it in. Front door. Hand it to the magistrate ourselves and say where we got it."

"And they ask how a diver with a ruined shoulder and a dockhand came to possess an internal company manifest, and we say my father watched them load it from the water at midnight, and they nod sagely and arrest us both before luncheon. No." I turned the aged page in the lamplight, admiring my own wickedness with a queasy pride. "It has to be found, not given. Found papers are evidence. Given papers are a grievance."

"So where do lost papers turn up?"

"Wherever careless men put them." And I smiled, because I had spent three years watching precisely that.

The customs house shared a coal merchant with three other waterfront offices, including the shipping agent who handled Halloway's filings. Every Friday, the agent's boy ferried a satchel of duplicate manifests across the square to be reviewed and countersigned. Every Friday, in wet weather, the boy cut through the tavern passage to stay dry, and every few Fridays — I had asked, delicately, over weeks, the way you ask about anything you intend to use — a paper or two went astray, blown off a cart, dropped in the mud, fished out later by whoever cared enough to look.

Friday was coming. Rain was coming with it, according to the gulls, who are rarely wrong about weather and never wrong about refuse.

I put the manifest in a plain oilcloth wrapper, the kind the agent's office used, tied with the plain twine they used, addressed in a neutral commercial hand to the reviewing officer with the agent's box number — a hand I practiced for an hour until it looked like exactly what it was meant to look like: nobody's. Then I gave it to Tobin with instructions that made him scowl.

"I'm to *drop* it? In the *mud*?"

"In the passage. Under the bench where the boy cuts through. He'll find it, assume the satchel shed it, and do what boys do with found paperwork, which is deliver it late and take credit for saving it. Nobody interrogates a rescued document, Tobin. Everybody loves a survivor."

"And if the rain doesn't come?"

"The rain will come." I looked out the window at the gulls wheeling low over the chimneys, mad as hats, certain as scripture. "The rain always comes. It's the one promise this city keeps."

He tucked the package inside his coat, and for a moment we just stood there, brother and sister in our father's kitchen, holding between us a piece of paper that would either save our name or hang us with it. He gripped my shoulder — the good one, he knows which one now — and said, "Steady hands, Miri."

"Pounding heart," I said. "But steady hands."

He laughed, once, quietly, and went out into the grey.

* * *

That was Tuesday. I had three days to wait, and waiting, I discovered, is the cruelest labor of all, because there is nothing to steady. I went to the office. I typed invoices. I answered to "the new girl" and "you there" and once, gloriously, to "Draven—" before the supervisor caught himself and corrected to "Miss, there." I watched Mr. Voss cross the typing floor with his coat collar up, and I thought: *initials drying on your own indictment, Mr. Voss. How does the ink taste?*

Wednesday night, my father asked me — not looking at me, looking at the stove, the way he asked all important things — whether the paper was done. I said it was planted, or would be by Friday. He nodded slowly, and then he said the thing I hadn't known I was waiting for anyone to say:

"Your mother would have liked you very much."

"She raised me."

"No." He shook his head, careful of the splint. "She'd have *liked* you. There's a difference. Raising's duty. Liking's recognition." He poked the fire with his good hand. "She hid us all with her quiet. You're the first Dravenmoor to stand up in the open and say our name out loud. Don't tell her I said so, but she'd have been frightened for you. And proud past bearing. Both at once. That's how you know a thing matters — when it frightens and pleases you in the same breath."

I didn't cry. I want that recorded. I stirred the pot and said "hush, old man," and he grunted, and that was the whole ceremony, and it was enough. Divers don't weep at surface depth. There'll be time below, later, where the pressure makes everything come out of you whether you mean it or not.

* * *

Friday came, and the rain came with it, fat and committed, drumming on the chandlery roof like applause or gunfire, depending on your mood. Tobin went out at noon with the oilcloth packet under his coat and came back an hour later soaked to the skin and grinning like a lunatic.

"Done. Right under the bench. Boy was already in the passage sheltering — nearly stepped on it, picked it up, squinted at the label, and ran off toward the agent's office like the hounds of heaven were behind him." He wrung out his cap over the sink. "By tonight it's in the satchel. Monday morning it's in front of the reviewing officer. Tuesday—" 

"Tuesday," I finished, "somebody reads the value column and stops breathing."

We had planned it to the hour. What we had not planned — what no arithmetic of mine had accounted for, and I am beginning to suspect nothing ever accounts for Voss — was the knock at the chandlery door that same evening. Heavy. Unhurried. The knock of a man who knows the door will be opened regardless.

Tobin blew out the lamp. Through the gap in the shutter I saw the figure on the step, collar up, hat brim streaming, and my heart performed a maneuver I did not teach it.

Mr. Voss stood in the rain, alone, and called softly through the wood:

"Miss Dravenmoor. We should talk — about the manifest. Before Friday's boy delivers more than either of us bargained for."
