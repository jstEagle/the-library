## 17. Bargain at Midnight

I had rehearsed the confrontation eleven times on the walk from my father's room above the chandlery to the Halloway offices, and by the twelfth telling I had learned something important about rehearsals: they are lies we tell ourselves in a comfortable voice. In every version, I was calm. In every version, Voss flinched. In none of them did I account for the fog, which had come off the harbor that night thick as wet wool, or for the fact that my heart was beating somewhere up around my ears, making my own thoughts sound like they were being shouted through a speaking tube.

The company offices were dark except for one window on the second floor, where a lamp burned behind frosted glass like a drowned moon. Of course Voss was working late. Men like Voss are always working late; it is how they avoid going home, where the arithmetic of their lives might catch up with them.

I let myself in through the loading door with the key I had copied three weeks ago—a forgery so routine now that I barely felt the transgression of it, which is either progress or damnation, depending on who's keeping the ledger. The stairs smelled of lamp oil and old rope. My boots knew which boards creaked. I had memorized them the way other people memorize psalms.

His door was unlocked. That should have frightened me more than a locked one.

"Miss Fen," he said, without looking up from his papers. He used the false name the way he used everything—precisely, and as a small cruelty. "You're out after curfew."

"So are you, Mr. Voss." I closed the door behind me. "Though I suppose men like you don't have curfews. Only schedules."

He looked up then. Lamplight caught the gray at his temples and the flat, patient eyes beneath, and I understood suddenly why clerks went quiet when he passed: not because he was frightening, but because he looked at people the way an auditor looks at columns—checking for errors, finding them, saying nothing yet.

"You've been in my files," he said.

It wasn't a question, so I didn't treat it as one. "I've been in everyone's files. It's what you pay me for. Or rather, what you pay someone like me for, at a rate you'd be embarrassed to say aloud."

"Sit down, Miss Fen."

"My name," I said, and here was the moment all eleven rehearsals had been building toward, the moment I had imagined would feel like triumph and instead felt like stepping off a dock into black water, "is Mirabel Dravenmoor."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere below us, the tide knocked against the pilings, patient as a creditor.

Voss set down his pen. He aligned it with the edge of the blotter, dead parallel, the way he aligned everything, and when he spoke his voice had not changed temperature at all. "Do you know what happens to girls who say that name in this building?"

"I do. I typed the letter that says it. Your letter. In your words, on your machine, with my hands." I took the carbon copy from inside my coat—the one I'd pulled from his wastebasket, the one that proved Halloway Trading Company had moved the contraband through its own manifests while writing the Dravenmoor name into the margins as a sacrifice—and laid it on his desk between us, face up, like a card I'd been holding for too long. "So let's not pretend either of us is discovering anything tonight."

For a long moment he only looked at it. And I want to record, because it matters, because I have thought about it every day since: he did not look surprised. He looked *tired*. There is a difference, and the difference is everything.

* * *

She put the paper down and I saw at once that she had done her work well, which surprised me less than it should have. I had watched her type for two years. You can tell everything about a person from how they strike a key—whether they hit it or ask it—and Mirabel Fen, or Dravenmoor, or whatever she wished to be called this evening, struck keys the way debt collectors knock: firmly, once, meaning it.

"The manifest," she said. "The real one. The one showing Halloway shipped the pearls through its own holds and wrote my family into the loss column to cover the shortfall. I have it. Or rather, I will have it finished by Thursday, and when it's finished, it goes to the customs house."

"That's extortion, Miss—Dravenmoor."

"Yes," she said pleasantly. "I learned it from watching you."

I have negotiated with shipwrights, insurers, magistrates, and once, memorably, a pirate who turned out to be a customs officer in disguise. None of them unnerved me. This girl unnerved me, and not because of the threat. Because of the stillness. She stood in my office at midnight with her future in her coat pocket and her chin level, and I recognized the posture the way you recognize a scar in a mirror.

My sister had stood like that. In a doorway in Rotterdam, twenty-two years ago, holding a ticket she wouldn't use because there was only one and I had already decided it was mine. She had said, *Go, then, Aldous,* in exactly that voice—level, finished, already grieving—and I had gone, and I had built a life on the far side of the water out of the materials of that departure, and every promotion since has been a payment against a debt no ledger records.

"You'll hang yourself alongside us," I told her. "Forged documents. Breaking into these offices. The customs magistrate doesn't distinguish between the forger and the fraud."

"He will if the fraud is proven first and the forger comes forward voluntarily to testify." She said it as though she had rehearsed it, and I suppose she had; I recognized rehearsal too. "Here is my offer, Mr. Voss, and I'll only make it once. I disappear. Quietly, tonight, before the Corvina sails. No testimony from me, no forged manifest surfacing, no scandal. The directors keep their names clean, the company keeps its charter, and I keep my silence—in exchange for one thing."

"The berth," I said.

"No." And here she leaned forward, both palms flat on my desk, and for the first time all evening her voice cracked—not with fear but with the effort of holding something enormous very carefully. "The berth is mine to give, and I've given it. What I want is the record corrected. A sworn statement, witnessed, entered with the port authority, clearing the Dravenmoor name. My father's debts marked paid. My brother's file closed. You can resign afterward if it costs you your position—I'm told men like you always land somewhere soft. But the name gets cleared, in ink, before I vanish."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then Thursday happens, and we all go down together, and at least I'll go down under my own name."

I looked at her for a long time. Outside, the fog pressed against the glass like something listening.

There were perhaps six ways to end this conversation in my favor. I had used four of them on other people over the years and could have constructed the fifth and sixth without effort. Call the watchman. Deny everything. Point out that a carbon copy proves nothing and a girl with a false name proves less. Offer her money—she was poor; poverty is a lever I have pulled many times.

Instead I heard myself say: "You understand what you're asking me to do. Not the statement. The rest of it. To stay."

She blinked. For the first time, she looked young.

"You're offering to buy back your conscience with your own disappearance," I said, "and you think that's a bargain. It isn't. It's the same trade I made at nineteen, dressed in better grammar. I took the berth and told myself my sister was better off without my hunger dragging her down. I told myself I'd send for her. I want you to hear how those sentences sound, Miss Dravenmoor, because you will be saying versions of them for the rest of your life, and they do not improve with age."

"This isn't the same."

"It never is. That's what makes it work." I rose and went to the window. The harbor was invisible; the fog had taken even the masthead lights, and the whole world was reduced to this room, this desk, this girl who had walked into the lion's den carrying nothing but a piece of paper and her grandfather's name. "My sister died in the winter of 'ninety-four. Cholera, in a lodging house by the Rotterdam docks. I received the letter eight months later, forwarded twice. I read it during a board meeting. I approved the third-quarter shipping figures with tears running down my face, and not one of those men noticed, because I had taught myself by then that grief is a private inefficiency."

I turned back. She hadn't moved. Her hands had curled slightly at her sides, and I saw that she was gripping her own composure like a rail.

"If I give you your statement," I said slowly, feeling my way across ice I had avoided for twenty years, "it will end me. You understand that. The directors will know within a week who signed it. There is no soft landing for the man who confesses in ink. There is Rotterdam, or worse."

"Then don't do it for me," she said. "Do it for her. You've been paying interest on that berth for twenty-two years, Mr. Voss. Here, at last, is the principal."

* * *

We stood there a long while, the two of us, with the fog closing the world down to lamplight and breath, and I realized I was shaking—not from fear, though there was plenty of that sloshing around, but from the strange vertigo of having said true things aloud in a building built entirely of false ones.

Voss went to his safe. I heard the dial turn—he didn't shield it, which I chose to take as a kind of answer—and he came back with a single sheet of heavy paper and laid it beside my carbon copy.

Not the statement. A blank sheet, and his pen.

"You'll dictate," he said. "Every clause. I won't trust my own drafting tonight, and neither should you. We write it together, we sign it together, and it goes to the customs house in the morning by your hand, not mine—if I carry it, it can be lost. If you carry it, it exists."

"And your signature?"

"Is worth more than the paper it ruins me on." His mouth did something that was almost, in poor light, a smile. "That, Miss Dravenmoor, is the entire point of signatures."

I dictated. He wrote. Clause by clause: the contraband route, the altered manifests, the deliberate entry of the Dravenmoor name into the loss column, the debt manufactured against my father's boat, the offer of a single berth designed to remove the family's most dangerous member under cover of mercy. He wrote it all without flinching, and when he came to the last line he paused, pen hovering, and asked me, quietly, how I wished the statement signed.

"Mirabel Dravenmoor," I said. "Clerk, Halloway Trading Company. Witnessed by Aldous Voss, secretary."

He signed. Then he held out the pen to me, nib first, an odd courtesy, and I took it, and I wrote my name in full for the first time in six years, and my hand did not shake, which surprised me more than anything else that night. All that practice at being someone else, it seemed, had finally taught me how to be myself on paper.

"There," Voss said, capping the pen. "Now we are both ruined, and it is nearly one o'clock, and you should be gone before the night watch makes his round." He gathered the statement, sealed it, and held it out. As I took it, his fingers stayed on the envelope a moment longer than necessary.

"A word of advice, from a man who once stood exactly where you're standing," he said. "Whatever you traded to get this—whoever you gave the berth to, whatever you told yourself to make the arithmetic come out—they will hate you for it first and thank you never. Decide now whether you can live with that, because the ship sails in three weeks, and regret does not wait for departures."

"I've already decided," I said.

"No," he said, and there it was again, that terrible tiredness, that mirror. "You've decided what you'll *do*. The other kind takes longer. Go home, Miss Dravenmoor. Tell your brother. And pray he never learns what it cost you, because in my experience brothers always learn."

I carried the statement out through the fog with it pressed against my ribs like a second heartbeat, and I did not let myself think about Tobin's face, or my father's, or the single berth waiting on the Corvina like a chair pulled out at a table set for three.

But Voss's voice followed me down the stairs and out into the dark, and by the time I reached the chandlery I knew he was right about one thing: the deciding was done, and the telling had not even begun.

Tobin's lamp was still burning in the window.
