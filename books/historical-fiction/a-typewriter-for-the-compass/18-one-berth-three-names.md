## 18. One Berth, Three Names

My brother's lamp was burning in the window, and I stood in the street beneath it like a moth doing sums.

The arithmetic was simple. That was the obscenity of it. Three names, one berth, and the mathematics of it came out clean as a ledger line—cleaner than anything I'd ever entered in the Halloway books, which were filthy with other people's losses dressed up as ours. Father could not dive again; the sea had taken that from him along with the hearing in his left ear and most of his patience. Tobin had Serafina swelling with a child due before the fog lifted, a wife who needed feeding and would need more of it soon. And I—

I was the easiest subtraction. That's what I kept telling myself, standing there in the wet dark. Not the noblest. The *easiest*. A clerk can vanish anywhere. A clerk has been practicing vanishing for six years, one erased name at a time. If anyone in this family had been in training for disappearance, it was me, and I resented the aptitude even as I relied on it.

The lamp flickered. Tobin was awake, then. Waiting up, probably, to hear what Voss had said at our midnight conference, though he didn't know yet that it had been a conference at all and not simply another humiliation doled out on schedule. He'd learn soon enough. The telling had not begun, Voss had said, and he'd been right about that the way a knife is right about where it goes.

I went up the stairs.

* * *

Tobin opened the door before I knocked, which told me he'd heard my step from the landing—the Dravenmoors have always walked heavy on their heels, all of us except Mother, who moved through rooms like weather. He looked at my face and something in his own closed up, the way a shopkeeper closes shutters when he sees the tax man coming.

"You've been to see him," he said.

"I've been to see him."

"Come in, then. Sit down. Sera's asleep and I'd thank you not to wake her, she's been sick twice tonight and if she wakes a third time I'll be no use to anybody." He said it gruffly but his hand, when he pulled out the chair for me, was gentle as anything, and I thought—not for the first time—that my brother carried tenderness the way some men carry debt: constantly, invisibly, and with a certain grim pride.

I sat. The room above the chandlery smelled of tallow and dried kelp and, faintly, of Serafina's ginger tea. There was a cradle in the corner that Tobin had built himself, from crate-wood he'd never admit to having lifted off the Halloway docks, and looking at it made the arithmetic easier and worse at once.

"Voss offered the berth," I said. No preamble. Preamble is a luxury for people whose names are still safe. "One berth on the Corvina. Sailing in nineteen days now. For whichever Dravenmoor leaves quietly and doesn't come back."

Tobin sat down across from me slowly, the way a man sits when the chair might be a trap. "Quietly," he repeated.

"Silently. Without testimony. Without the manifest going anywhere useful. I disappear, or one of us does, and Voss lets the rest of the story stay buried under the company's version of it. The version where we're smugglers."

"And you told him—" He stopped. Started again. "What did you tell him?"

I had told Voss nothing, in the end. That was the truth of the bargain at midnight: I had laid out my terms and watched him waver like a man at the edge of deep water who once nearly drowned, and neither of us had signed anything because neither of us trusted the other further than the length of this table. But Tobin deserved better than the shape of an unfinished thing.

"I told him the manifest surfaces whether I sail or sink. I told him I'd trade my silence for the family name cleared. And he—" I laughed, and it came out wrong, thin as paper. "He looked at me like he was looking at someone he used to know. Then he poured himself a drink and said the berth still stood."

"So we take it." Tobin leaned forward, hands flat on the table, and I saw the dock work in them, the rope burns and the salt cracks. "We take the berth and we go. All three of us if we have to, we find a way—"

"Tobin."

"—stow away if we must, I've moved crates on that dock for eleven years, I know every blind spot on the Corvina's hull—"

"*Tobin.* One berth. One. That was the offer and he will not amend it, because amending it costs him nothing and generosity costs him everything. It's one seat at a table set for three, and we are three, and Father won't go—I've already asked him, I asked him weeks ago and he looked at me like I'd suggested he sell his own bones—"

"He'd say that. He says that. He also coughs blood into a rag every morning and calls it 'clearing his chest,' so perhaps we don't take the old man's word on what he can survive."

It was the first time either of us had said it aloud, the blood, and we both flinched. Tobin looked down at his hands. "Sera's due inside the month," he said quietly. "You knew that."

"I knew that."

"So you've been counting."

"I'm a clerk. Counting is the whole of my character."

He almost smiled at that. Almost. Then the smile drowned and he said, "Then count it out loud, Mira. Whatever you've been carrying since you walked in that door. Say it."

And here is the thing nobody tells you about loving people: they can always tell when you've decided something without them. Tobin had known me twenty-six years. He'd known me when I was a child who dove for pennies off the pier and came up with pearls in her fists and lies in her mouth. He watched my face for perhaps four seconds before he said, "Oh no. Oh no, no—you're not—Mira, you've decided it's you."

* * *

I want to record, honestly, what happened next, because later—when the trial was done and the name was cleared and strangers called me brave—I found that everyone assumed the hard part was the danger. It wasn't. The hard part was this room, this table, my brother's voice cracking on the second *no*.

"It should be you," I said. "Not me. You."

"That's insane."

"It's arithmetic."

"It's *suicide dressed as arithmetic*, and you know it, and you did it on purpose because you knew I'd hate the sums." He was on his feet now, and the floorboards complained, and somewhere below us the chandlery dog began to mutter. "Voss wants a Dravenmoor gone. You give him yourself? After everything? After the manifest, after the tavern, after you stood up in front of forty dockmen and said *my name is Mirabel Dravenmoor*—" His voice broke on it, actually cracked, and I realized he had never said my full name aloud either, not in six years, none of us had, and hearing it in his mouth hurt in a place I don't have anatomy for.

"Which is exactly why it has to be you," I said. "They know my face now, Tobin. They know my name. Voss can't use me as a quiet exit anymore—I'm too loud. But you're still just a dockhand with rope burns. You can still vanish. And you'll be taking more than yourself. You'll be taking Sera, and the baby, and the name gets to live somewhere the company can't reach it. That's not surrender. That's the whole point of a name—it's meant to *go on*."

"Not without you it isn't."

"Well." I folded my hands on the table, clerk-fashion, and hated myself for the gesture even as it steadied me. "Father refuses. You know he refuses. He'd sooner let them hang him than leave the water, and arguing with him is arguing with a tide. Sera can't travel in her condition even if she could hide aboard, which she can't, she glows like a lantern and cries at sad songs. So it's you, or it's me, and if it's me, then in nineteen days you're a dockhand with a pregnant wife and a father who coughs blood, sitting in a city where the company has decided your whole family are criminals, waiting to see what reprisal looks like. Whereas if it's you—"

"Don't."

"If it's you, then in nineteen days there is a Dravenmoor alive and free and *starting over*, and I am here, testifying, with witnesses and the manifest and a customs office that owes Halloway money and hates them for it. I'm not safer, Tobin. I'm not pretending I'm safer. I'm saying I'm *armed*, and you're not, and armed people hold the line so unarmed people can run."

He stared at me. In the lamplight he looked suddenly very young, the boy who used to hold my hand crossing the ice-slick stones by the pearl sheds, and I understood that he was doing the same arithmetic I'd done, running the same numbers, and hating them, because they were correct.

"No," he said.

"Yes."

"No, Mira—"

"You will get on that ship."

"You don't get to decide this alone! Not again!" And there it was, the wound from the night I'd told him about the letter, the night he'd raged at me for deciding things in the dark and presenting them at dawn like invoices. "This is exactly—this is *precisely*—"

"Yes," I said. "It is precisely that. And I'm going to do it again, and here's why: because somebody in this family has to decide, and Father decides by refusing, and you decide by fighting things bigger than you until they break you, and I decide by counting. Somebody has to count, Tobin. Somebody has to sit with the numbers long enough to see which ones are people."

He sat back down. Slowly, like an old man. We were silent a long time, and the lamp burned between us, and downstairs the dog gave up and went back to sleep.

"Sera will say no too," he said finally. "She'll dig her heels in. She's worse than Da."

"Sera will say yes," I said, "because Sera is the only one of us who thinks past her own pride. Ask her. Tonight. Before you can talk each other out of it."

"And what do I tell her about you? What do I tell her happens to you?"

"The truth. That I stay and testify and clear our name, and that afterward—" I paused, because the afterward was the one column I hadn't filled in, the blank space at the bottom of the page. "Afterward, I write to you. Care of whatever port you land in. Long letters. You know how I am about letters."

"Mira—"

"And when the baby comes," I said, and my voice held, I'm proud to report, it held like good rope, "you name her for Mother. Or him. Whichever. And you teach the child to dive, and you teach the child to read, and you teach the child our name, and you say it out loud, often, in public, in daylight, so the child never learns what it cost us to keep it."

Tobin put his head in his hands. His shoulders shook once, twice, and then stopped, because Dravenmoor men cry the way it rains in this city—briefly, violently, and in denial.

"Nineteen days," he said into his hands.

"Nineteen days."

"And if the ship sinks."

"Then you'll haunt me specifically, and you know how I feel about ghosts interrupting my work."

That got a wet, broken laugh out of him, which had been the entire purpose of the joke, and after that we sat a while longer not talking, which is its own kind of talk among siblings. At last he rose, went to the shelf, and took down the bottle of plum brandy Father had given him at his wedding—a bottle older than his marriage, saved against some occasion too large for ordinary days—and poured two fingers into two cups.

"To Mother," he said.

"To Mother," I said.

We drank. It tasted like plums and decisions.

* * *

I walked home through the fog with the brandy warm in my chest and my legs heavier than the climb warranted. Outside my lodging I stopped and looked back toward the harbor, where the Corvina lay at her moorings, a shadow with masts, patient as a debt.

Nineteen days. Nineteen days to forge the last papers, plant the manifest, hold Voss to his half of a bargain struck between two people who trusted each other exactly as far as they could throw a typewriter, and testify under my own name in front of men who could end me with a signature.

I had told Tobin I was armed. It was mostly true. But standing there in the fog, I admitted to myself what I hadn't admitted to him: that arms are only as good as the hand holding them, and mine had been shaking since the chandlery stairs.

Then the fog shifted, and down at the harbor I saw lights moving along the quay where no honest lights should be at three in the morning—lanterns, several of them, converging on the Halloway warehouse—and behind the lanterns, shapes. Men. Many men. And one figure at their head, tall and straight-backed, walking with the unhurried certainty of a person who has already sent the letter that makes all this legal.

Customs officers. At night. Ahead of schedule.

Either Voss had honored our bargain early—or he had decided the berth's deadline wasn't the only thing that needed shortening.
