## 19. The Night Before Sailing

The fog came off the water that evening the way it always did, slow and gray and patient, like something that had learned it could take whatever it wanted without hurrying. I stood at the mouth of Pier Nine with my collar up and my hands in my pockets, watching the Corvina's running lights blur and bloom through the mist, and I tried to feel like a man who was going somewhere.

I mostly felt like a thief.

That was the thing nobody warned you about escape. They made you think of open water, of clean starts, of a horizon with your name written across it. Nobody mentioned that the first thing you'd pack, before the shirts and the money and the letters of passage, was a great heavy crate of guilt that you'd be hauling through customs for the rest of your life.

I'd told her no. That was worth remembering. Whatever my sister said later, however she arranged the story in her careful clerk's mind, the record ought to show that Tobin Dravenmoor stood on the dock on his last night in the city and said no.

"You're not going," I'd told her that morning, when she laid it out for me in her flat, reasonable voice, the voice she used for figures. "You're the one who did all of it. The papers, the manifest, the testimony—none of it happens without you. You should be the one on that ship."

"And what would you do?" she'd asked. "Stay here and fight Voss with what? Your fists? He has lawyers, Tobin. He has magistrates who owe him dinners. He has a company older than this harbor."

"I have a wife having our child inside the month," I'd said, "and I'm not leaving her to raise our baby in a city where our name is a warrant."

"No," Mirabel said, and here she did the thing that always undid me—she went quiet. Not the quiet of someone with nothing to say. The quiet of someone choosing which true thing to say next. "No, you're not leaving her to do that. She's coming with you."

"She can't sail. She's too far along—the ship's surgeon won't—"

"The berth is yours," she said. "She'll be passed fit or she won't, but if she isn't, it won't be because you gave your seat away out of pride. Pride is Papa's sin, Tobin. We only get one each."

* * *

Now it was night, and the fog had thickened until the Corvina was less a ship than a rumor of one, three lamps glowing where a ship should be. Our father came down the pier ahead of me, moving slowly, favoring the leg the sea had ruined. He walked like a man visiting a grave he wasn't sure he had the right to mourn at.

He hadn't spoken more than ten words since we left the room above the chandlery. Ten words, counted out like coins from a purse that was nearly empty. I knew why. My father believed—with the whole simple, immovable weight of him—that a Dravenmoor did not run. A Dravenmoor faced the water or went under looking at it. Every step he took toward that gangplank was a step against his own creed, and it cost him more than the bad leg ever had.

We found Mirabel waiting by the bollard nearest the plank. She'd gotten there early, of course. My sister had never once in her life arrived anywhere second, not because she was fast but because she planned her arrivals like campaigns. She wore her good coat and her hair pinned up, and under the fog-lamp she looked—there's no other word for it—official. Like a person the world would have to deal with rather than go around.

"You're late," she said to us both, though we weren't.

"Papa walks as fast as his leg allows," I said.

"Papa always could have gone faster than he allowed himself," she said, but there was no edge in it, just an old grief worn smooth, and our father received it the way he received all weather: without complaint.

Then I made my mistake again, right there on the planks, because apparently once wasn't enough for a man of my character.

"Take the berth yourself," I said. "Mira. Look at me. You've done everything. You typed their sins into their own machine. You forged a manifest so clean the customs men will frame it. You should be on that ship, starting over where nobody knows the name Dravenmoor means anything at all. I'll stay. I'll testify. I've got broad shoulders and a short memory—I'll heal."

"No, you won't," she said. "You'll sulk magnificently for six months and then do something brave and stupid, and Sera will be eight months pregnant in a city where the company still holds our father's debt. No."

"Mira—"

"Tobin." And then she did something worse than argue. She smiled. Small and crooked and absolutely certain. "Do you know how many documents I've signed this month that weren't mine? Forged manifests, false names, letters in Voss's own hand. Do you know the first honest signature I've been able to put to anything in eleven years?" She shook her head. "It's going on a sworn statement tomorrow morning. I'm not wasting my real name on a ticket out. I'm spending it here, where it counts."

"That's not fair," I said. "Using sense on me."

"Never is," she said. "Papa, tell him."

Our father had been standing apart, facing the water, and now he turned around, and I saw that he'd brought something wrapped in oilcloth, held against his chest the way other men hold infants.

"A diver knows," he said, "when to come up."

"Papa—"

"He doesn't ask permission of the water. He reads it. Today the water says: this child goes east. Tomorrow—" he shrugged, and the shrug cost his shoulder something, I could see it—"tomorrow the water says other things to other people. A diver who argues with the water drowns proud. There are enough proud drowned men at the bottom of this bay. Don't add to them."

And then he unwrapped the oilcloth, and I forgot every clever thing I'd been saving up.

It was his diving knife. The one from the accident year, the one I'd grown up thinking was lost with everything else—hilt of whalebone worn smooth as sea-glass, blade dark where the salt had gotten into the steel forever. The knife he'd cut himself free with when the lines fouled, the knife that had come up out of the green dark in his fist while the boat crew hauled him half-dead into the light. It had hung on the chandlery wall my whole childhood, hanging there like a saint's picture, and I had never once been allowed to touch it.

He took my hand—my big clumsy stevedore's hand—and folded my fingers around the grip, and closed them, and pressed.

"It has cut rope," he said. "It has cut net. It has cut me, once, when the rope deserved cutting more." His voice was gravel over deep water. "A Dravenmoor does not run, Tobin. But crossing water to keep your family alive—that was never running. That is what the diving always was. Go down. Come back up. Bring what you find to the surface." He pressed my fingers shut harder. "Bring them to the surface."

I am not ashamed to say my eyes were burning, because the fog was thick, and any man will tell you the same.

"Papa," I managed, "the baby—if it's a boy—"

"Name him after whoever deserves it," my father said. "Not after me. I've had my name long enough. Give him a fresh one. Let him start clean." And then, quieter, almost drowned by the fog-horn out on the water: "Let him start clean."

* * *

Mirabel and I walked the last stretch of pier together while Papa followed, stopping twice to let his leg decide the pace. She didn't offer me her arm and I didn't offer mine; we are our father's children, and we carry each other by walking beside, not holding on.

"Do you remember," she said suddenly, "the summer you taught me to swim? Off the rocks past the breakwater?"

"I remember nearly drowning you."

"You did nearly drown me. You pushed me in and then panicked and jumped in after, and we both nearly drowned, and Papa fished us out with the boathook and beat you with his sandal in front of God and the harbor." She laughed—a real one, startled out of her, brief as a match strike. "You cried more than I did."

"I was nine."

"You were certain it was your fault. You kept saying, 'I pushed her, I pushed her,' and Papa finally said—" she dropped into her imitation of him, low and rolling—"'Good. Now you know what it costs to push someone into deep water. Never do it halfway.'"

We walked a few steps in silence.

"You're doing it right," she said. "In case you wondered. Pushing me in all those years ago. Going first into the deep water. You're doing it all the way through."

"Somebody has to cry about it," I said. "Since you won't."

"Oh, I'll cry," Mirabel said, mild as a ledger line. "Tomorrow. After the ship clears the point. I've scheduled it between the testimony and lunch."

And I laughed, God help me, standing there on the pier with my whole life packed into one canvas bag, laughing at my sister, because she was the bravest person I knew and she made bravery sound like bookkeeping, and I loved her the way you love a lighthouse: half admiration, half despair, entirely dependent.

At the gangplank, the mate checked our father's paper and mine, and waved Mirabel back with the bored courtesy of a man who processes departures the way tide processes sand. She stood at the foot of the plank with her hands clasped behind her back—clerk's posture, witness's posture, a woman planting herself—and watched us.

Sera was already aboard. She'd gone up with the first group, pale and enormous and furious at everyone including the ship, and I could see her silhouette at the rail, one hand pressed to the small of her back, scanning the pier for me. When she found me she didn't wave. She just nodded, once, the way you nod to a man across a crowded room when the two of you share a secret that's keeping you both upright.

I turned back to Mirabel.

"If Voss—"

"He won't touch me before the hearing. Too many eyes now." She said it steadily, but I saw her glance, quick as a fish, toward the shadowed end of the pier where a man in a company coat had been standing too long, pretending to read a notice board by fog-light. "Go down the plank, Tobin. Come up on the other side. Bring what you find to the surface."

I hugged her then, hard enough that she complained about her ribs, hard enough that I felt her laugh against my chest, and then our father embraced her—one arm, awkward, the way he'd done since the accident changed the geometry of him—and said something to her I didn't hear and never asked about. Some things belong to the person they were said to.

I started up the gangplank. Halfway, I stopped. I don't know why—some animal instinct, some diver's sense inherited without the trade. I turned around.

Mirabel had already turned away from the ship. She was walking back down the pier, alone, straight-backed, into the fog, and the fog was closing behind her the way water closes over a diver, smooth and total, as if she had never been there at all.

And far off, at the harbor mouth, the customs launch was putting out its lights—one lamp, then another—as it slipped in past the point toward the Halloway warehouses, quiet as a knife going into dark water.

Morning, I understood, was going to come up blood-red or clean, and my sister intended to be standing in the middle of it either way.

The mate touched my elbow. "Boarding, sir. Tide won't wait."

"No," I said, gripping the whalebone handle in my pocket until it felt like a hand holding mine back. "It never does."

I climbed.
