## 20. Testimony in the Custom House

The fog lifted at dawn, which I took as a personal insult. All these years it had kept me, wrapped me, let me pass through the city like a rumor—and now, on the one morning I needed it most, it rolled back off the harbor like a curtain going up.

I stood at the edge of the quay with my hands folded to keep them still and watched the *Corvina* take Tobin away from me. She went out slow, the way ships do when the tide is lazy, her funnel trailing a smear of black against a sky gone suddenly, mercilessly blue. Tobin stood at the rail. He did not wave. We had agreed not to wave, because waving is what you do when something is finished, and neither of us could bear to make it look finished. He put his hand flat against his chest instead—the old gesture our father used before a dive, palm over heart, *I am here, I am whole, I come back*—and then the fog of distance took him the way the fog of the harbor never had.

Sera was beside me, both hands pressed to the swell of her belly, saying nothing. There was nothing to say that wouldn't crack us both down the middle.

"He'll write," she said finally.

"Of course he'll write," I said. "He can't spell 'harbor.' He'll need me for that if nothing else."

She laughed, wet and startled, and gripped my arm, and we watched until the *Corvina* was a smudge, then a suggestion, then nothing at all. Then I turned around and walked toward the Custom House, because the tide waits for no one, and neither does a customs inspector named Brack who has been promised a forged manifest by nine o'clock.

I will be honest, since honesty is apparently the theme of the day: every step of that walk, some small cowardly clerk in the back of my skull was typing out reasons to turn around. *You are a woman without standing. You are a diver's daughter. You have spent seven years teaching this city you don't exist; it believed you; why spoil a good lesson?*

But there is a thing that happens in the fingers after enough years at a typewriter. The keys stop being keys. They become decisions. And somewhere between the chandlery and the Custom House steps I understood that my hands had already made their decision and were simply waiting for the rest of me to catch up.

* * *

Brack was exactly what I had expected from our two careful meetings—a man built like a filing cabinet, gray, immovable, with drawers full of other people's sins. He received the manifest without ceremony, spread it on his desk, and read it the way I had learned to read Voss's correspondence: once fast, twice slow, three times looking for the lie.

"You typed this," he said. It wasn't quite an accusation.

"I typed the true one," I said. "Halloway's own manifest, the night they moved the contraband ashore under the *Meridian*'s papers. This is a fair copy. My father saw the crates. Your own watchers logged the cart route. It all agrees."

"And your name?"

Here it was, then. The door I had been walking toward for seven years, standing open.

"Mirabel Dravenmoor," I said.

It should have been harder. I had rehearsed it in the dark, whispered it like a prayer and like a curse, expecting it to taste of rust. But it came out clean. It came out sounding like mine.

Brack looked up at me for a long moment, and I watched him do the arithmetic—he was a man of arithmetic too, though a more honest kind than Voss's. A Dravenmoor. A name the Halloway Company had spent a year nailing to a smuggling charge. Standing in his office volunteering itself as evidence.

"They'll say you're lying," he said. "They'll say a disgraced family invented the whole thing to save its neck."

"Yes," I said. "That's rather their specialty. But they wrote the original in Mr. Voss's own hand, and I kept the carbon. And your men found the crates where my father said they'd be. Lying is expensive, Inspector. Truth, for once, is cheap."

Something moved behind his gray face—approval, maybe, or just appetite. A man like Brack had waited his whole career for a case big enough to fill his drawers.

"Custom House opens at nine," he said. "Directors' offices above the warehouse. We go together. If you're coming, you come all the way."

"Inspector," I said, "I've been halfway places my whole life. Not today."

* * *

I will not pretend the raid was dignified. Raids never are. They are noise and boots and the particular humiliation of important men being made to stand up from their chairs.

We came up on Halloway's offices at half past nine, Brack's officers ahead of him, and I behind, because Brack had decided a witness on hand was worth more than a witness kept safe, and I had insisted hard enough that he'd stopped arguing. The clerks looked up from their ledgers with the expressions of men watching weather change. Old Petcham dropped his pen. Someone's ink bottle went over and bled across a column of figures, ruining someone's quarter, though whose I couldn't say and didn't care to.

Voss was at his desk.

I want to record this precisely, because everything else about that morning blurred, but that image stayed sharp as type: Mr. Voss, secretary of the Halloway Trading Company, sitting perfectly upright behind his desk with his hands flat on the blotter, watching the officers come through his door as though he had ordered them himself. As though, in some private ledger of his own, he had been expecting this entry for thirty years.

His eyes found me past their shoulders. I had braced for fury. What I got was worse and better: recognition. He looked at me the way a man looks at his own handwriting in an old letter—something he'd written once, long ago, and hoped forgotten.

"Miss—" he began, and stopped, because he didn't know what to call me. Seven years of "the girl in the typing room." No name. He had seen to that himself, or so I'd believed until the midnight bargain, when I'd learned he knew exactly whose daughter I was and always had.

"Dravenmoor," I said. "You may use it now. Everyone else is."

Brack laid the manifest on the desk between them. "Mirabel Dravenmoor," he said, giving my name its full weight, letting it fall in that office like a coin on marble, "makes sworn statement that this document, in the company's files, records shipment of contraband pearl under false papers, and that the charge lodged against her father, Aldous Dravenmoor, was manufactured by this firm to cover its debts. We have the crates. We have the cart logs. We have her testimony. What we would like now, Mr. Voss, is yours."

And here is the thing I did not expect—not because I hadn't imagined it, but because imagining it felt too much like hoping: Voss did not deny it. He looked at the manifest for a long time. His thumb moved along its edge, reading it perhaps by touch, the way I sometimes did, checking the weight of paper against memory.

"The directors will claim I acted alone," he said at last, quietly. "That will be a lie. It will also be effective."

"That sounds familiar," I said.

He looked at me again then, fully, and for a moment the office, the officers, the spreading ink and the gaping clerks all fell away, and it was only two people who had both, in different decades, stood at a rail watching a ship carry away the person they loved because arithmetic told them it must be so.

"You could have taken the berth," he said. "I gave you the berth."

"You gave my brother the berth," I said. "I chose to give it. That's the difference, Mr. Voss, and I think you know it's the only one that matters."

His jaw tightened. Somewhere behind the gray composure something shifted—some drawer opening that had been locked a long time. "My sister," he started, and then closed his mouth on it, because some testimonies are not for custom houses.

But I heard it anyway. I had been hearing it since midnight.

"Testify," I said, gentler than I meant to. "Not for me. For whoever you left on that dock."

For a long moment he said nothing. Then Voss turned to Brack and said, in the flat voice of a man signing something against his own interest, "I'll make a statement. In writing. I'll want my own pen." And that, I thought, was the most honest sentence he'd spoken in twenty years: even confessing, he wanted to control the instrument.

* * *

They took my testimony in the great room of the Custom House at noon, because Brack wanted witnesses and daylight and the smell of official tar on everything. The room was high-ceilinged and cold, all varnished wood and tall windows, and the fog outside had burned off entirely, so the harbor lay exposed below us—bright, glittering, shameless. Somewhere out on that water the *Corvina* was making her way east with my brother aboard, and I found that knowing it steadied me rather than shook me. Whatever happened in this room, one Dravenmoor was beyond their reach. That left the rest of us free to be reckless.

The examining magistrate was a dry stick of a man named Corliss who asked his questions like he was counting them out of a purse. The directors' solicitor sat opposite, a smooth article called Fenwick, whose entire profession consisted of making truth sound expensive.

"State your name for the record," Corliss said.

"Mirabel Aldousa Dravenmoor," I said, and heard my father's name inside my own, given to me and nearly buried by me, and I made sure the stenographer caught every syllable.

Fenwick rose early and often. He established that I had worked at Halloway under a false name—*why, Miss Dravenmoor, if you were innocent of anything?* —and I answered that I had hidden from the company that had ruined my family, which seemed to him damning and to everyone else in the room reasonable. He established that I had forged documents—*you admit to forgery*—and I agreed cheerfully enough that several spectators laughed, which he did not enjoy. He established that my father was a convicted smuggler awaiting appeal, and here I stood very straight.

"My father was convicted on papers typed in that office," I said, pointing toward the harbor and the warehouse beyond it, "by hands paid to type whatever the directors needed typed. I know because mine were those hands. Ask yourself, sir—if Halloway would forge a manifest to hang a crime on a crippled diver, what would they not forge?"

Fenwick smiled his smooth smile. "Convenient, that the sole witness to this conspiracy should be the accused man's own daughter."

"Inconvenient, rather," I said. "For you."

There it was again—that laugh through the room. I had spent seven years learning that silence was safety, and here I was discovering that a room full of strangers laughing *with* you feels like sunlight on water. Terrifying. Wonderful. I understood then, standing in the witness box with my heart hammering like a dockside winch, that visibility is not a wound. It is a kind of diving. The surface is cold and the light is blinding and everyone can see you—but the pearls are down there, and no one else is going to fetch them up.

Corliss asked me to walk through the night the contraband came ashore. I did. I described the carbon copy pulled from Voss's wastebasket, the entry in the *Meridian*'s false papers, my father's dive—his last dive, I said, and my voice did not break, though it wanted to—and the crates moved to the warehouse under the directors' seal. I described the berth Voss offered, and the terms, and how the price of my family's silence was to be one quiet departure and a name left swinging for the crime.

"And why," Corliss said, "did you refuse this arrangement? It offered you safety."

I looked at him. I looked past him, at the windows, at the bright harbor, at the place where a ship had been and wasn't anymore.

"Because my father taught me that the sea gives a man his living and his name," I said, "and that you don't trade either to save your skin. I spent seven years trading mine by the hour, Inspector. I know exactly what it costs. More than a berth. More than a boat. I'm not selling it today."

The room was quiet. Even Fenwick had run out of smoothness. Corliss wrote something down at length, which I suspected was either very good or very bad for me, and called the next witness.

* * *

They brought my father in during the afternoon session, and I watched him walk the length of that grand room on his ruined leg, leaning on a stick Tobin had cut and sanded for him, and I watched the whole room watch him—the famous criminal, the smuggler of the Halloway papers—and I watched them revise, one by one, the picture they'd carried in with them. He was not a dangerous man. He was an old diver with salt-bleached hair and hands like knotted rope, who stood when told to stand and said, when asked for his name:

"Aldous Dravenmoor. Pearl diver, forty-one years. Third generation." A pause. "Fourth, if my son's child is a boy. Fifth if she's a girl and stubborn enough."

Laughter, warmer this time. Even Corliss's mouth twitched.

He testified simply, the way he dove—no flourishes, no wasted breath. What he saw in the cove. What was in the crates. Who stood on the warehouse steps the night they came ashore. He named Director Halloway himself, and the room went still as deep water.

Afterward, outside on the Custom House steps, my father found me. He did not say *I'm proud of you*. That was never in him; pride, in my father, was a thing conducted entirely through posture. Instead he put his rope-knotted hand on my shoulder—the same shoulder he'd steadied a hundred times on a boat deck when I was small and the swell was high—and he said:

"When your mother put you in that office, I thought she was burying you alive among the paper men." He looked out at the harbor, squinting against the brightness. "Turns out she armed you."

"She'd have said it was the least she could do after naming me Mirabel," I said. "A name like that is a dare."

He laughed—one short bark, quickly mastered—and squeezed my shoulder, and for a moment neither of us said anything, because the tide was turning below us, and the gulls were wheeling, and the city that had kept my name in its mouth like a stone for seven years was, this afternoon, saying it aloud in courtrooms and taverns and doorways, and getting it right.

Then Brack appeared in the doorway behind us, gray and grim as ever, holding a single sheet of paper.

"Miss Dravenmoor," he said. "A messenger left this at my office an hour ago. Addressed to you." He hesitated—Brack, hesitating, which told me everything before I'd read a word. "It's from Voss. He resigned his post this evening. The directors are screaming for his head, and he's—" Brack consulted his memory rather than the page, delicately. "He's declined to be at his lodgings when anyone comes asking."

I took the letter. My father's hand tightened on my shoulder, and I broke the seal there on the steps, in the last of the light.

The handwriting was Voss's own, of course—precise, upright, every letter a decision. It was four lines long. Four lines, from a man who had spent his life writing other people's ruin in longhand.

*I have signed the statement Brack has. Everything in it is true.*

*You asked me once what the difference was, between taking the berth and giving it away. I told myself for thirty years there was none—that survival is survival. You were right and I was wrong, and I am too old a wreck to say it anywhere but here.*

*The company keeps a second set of ledgers. The key is in the clock in my office. Give them to Brack, and the name Dravenmoor will be clean by spring.*

*You typed your way out. I never learned how.*

Below that, no signature. Just a small blot, as though the pen had rested a moment too long in one place—like a man standing at a rail, watching a ship go, unable to make himself wave.

"Well?" my father asked.

I folded the letter along its crease, carefully, the way I handled anything that mattered, and looked out at the harbor where the fog was rolling back in off the water at last—as if it had only stepped aside for the day's business and meant to resume keeping the rest of the city's secrets tonight.

"There's a clock in Voss's office," I said. "And it seems I'm not done typing."
