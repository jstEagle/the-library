## 21. After the Fog Lifts

The fog lifted on a Tuesday, which struck me as an insult. Fog ought to have the decency to lift on a Sunday, when a body has time to stand at a window and watch it go. Instead it went about its business on a workday morning, and so did I, and by nine o'clock the harbor was visible for the first time in eleven days and nobody had time to look at it.

I looked anyway.

From the clerks' window on the third floor of what was no longer, strictly speaking, the Halloway Trading Company—though the sign had not yet been taken down, and would not be until the receivers came with their ladders—I could see the whole gray sweep of it: the masts, the gulls hanging in the air like punctuation, the *Corvina* gone these five weeks and good riddance to her, though I confess I looked for her masthead every morning all the same, out of habit, the way one touches a tooth that is no longer there.

Tobin was somewhere past that horizon. He had been somewhere past that horizon for thirty-four days. His last letter had come through a shipping agent in Funchal, four lines of his terrible hand telling me the sea was flat as pewter, that Sera's letters said the child kicked, that he had eaten an orange and thought of me, which made no sense whatsoever and therefore made perfect sense. I kept it in my bodice, which was unhygienic and sentimental and entirely typical of me.

The trial had ended the week before—not with trumpets, as I had once foolishly imagined justice might sound, but with a clerk reading charges in a voice like a man reciting laundry lists, and a magistrate who yawned twice, and a verdict delivered in nineteen minutes. Guilty, guilty, guilty. The directors were fined sums that sounded enormous to me and were, I learned later, roughly equivalent to what they spent on claret. Two of them would do a few months in the county gaol, where they would be treated with the courtesy owed to gentlemen. Voss—

Well. Voss was the reason I was standing at that window instead of at my desk, and the reason my desk no longer existed, and the reason I had spent the previous evening drinking tea I did not want while my father watched me with the particular patience of a man who has held his breath underwater for three minutes and considers impatience a character flaw.

* * *

He resigned rather than face trial. That was the phrase everyone used—*resigned*, as though he had stepped down from a committee—and it was printed in the *Gazette* beneath a photograph of him descending the Custom House steps in a gray coat, hat level, stride even, looking for all the world like a man going to collect his laundry. No handcuffs. No mob. The port authorities had accepted his cooperation—the manifest, his testimony against his own directors—in exchange for letting him slip out of the country ahead of the formal charges. They called it efficiency. My father called it something shorter and saltier.

I called it nothing, because I could not decide what to call it, and a woman who cannot decide what to call a thing is better off saying nothing at all. This is a lesson I learned late and paid for dearly, but I did learn it.

The letter came the morning after the verdict. It was left at the chandlery below our rooms, wrapped in oilcloth, addressed not to Miss M. Dravenmoor or to Mirabel Dravenmoor but to *the typist*, which told me two things at once: that he had known who I was far longer than I had ever suspected, and that he still, to the very end, could not bring himself to write my name. A man can be generous with everything except the thing that costs him most.

My father handed it up to me without comment. He had read the address, of course—he could not read, but he could read an envelope's weight and shape the way I read a ledger, and he knew what a letter from a ruined man looks like before anyone tells him. He went back to mending his nets, which he did now more for the motion of it than for any hope of using them, and I sat on the stair with the oilcloth in my lap and let a full minute pass, because I am a coward in small matters and always have been.

Inside, a single sheet. His hand, not typed—which surprised me until I understood it could not have been otherwise. A typed letter from Voss would have been a forgery of himself.

*Mirabel.* Just that, first. No honorific. He had spent the whole letter saving up my name and then spent it all in one line.

*I will keep this brief, as you have earned brevity from me if anyone has. You will hear it said that I fled, and it will be true, and I will not insult you by dressing it in another word.*

*I want you to know one thing, because you are the only person left who deserves to know it and because there is no one else I can tell without it costing me more than I have left to pay. In 1889 I stood in a harbor office very much like yours, holding papers very much like the ones you forged, and I was offered a berth, and I took it. My sister did not take hers, because she believed—as your father believes, and God help them both—that a name is worth standing on shore for. She was right. I have spent thirty years being wrong in comfortable rooms.*

*What you chose at the end—to stay, to speak, to put your name on the record when every instinct I taught you said to vanish—you chose the thing I could not. I do not ask forgiveness. I only note, for the record I am apparently so fond of, that the best clerk this company ever employed was also the only honest person in it, and that we deserved her destruction and received her testimony instead, and that I do not know which of those facts shames me more.*

*The berth I offered you was never mercy. You knew that. But the choice you made with it was real, and real choices are the only currency worth anything, and I am told I owe debts, so consider this payment of one.*

*Yours, etc.—*

*E. Voss*

There was no signature. Of course there was no signature. A man who spends his life signing things he does not mean learns, eventually, to leave the page blank where his soul would go.

I sat on those stairs for a long while. Below me, the chandlery bell rang; above me, a gull argued with the chimney; and in between, I sat with a dead man's confession in my hands, except he wasn't dead, which was somehow worse. If he had drowned I could have hated him cleanly. Living men leak into your sympathy like bilgewater.

My father came up the stairs eventually, because my father always comes up stairs eventually—it is one of his three speeds. He did not ask what the letter said. He sat down beside me on a step built for one, which meant we both hung off the edges like bookends, and he said:

"Is it finished, then?"

"The trial? Yes."

"No." He nodded at the letter. "That."

"I don't know," I said, which was the truth, and he nodded again, satisfied, as though *I don't know* were the answer he'd been hoping for. Perhaps it was. A man who has spent forty years under water knows that you cannot see a thing clearly until the silt settles, and that hurrying the settling only stirs it worse.

Then he said, "Your mother would have wanted to read that," and I said, "Mother couldn't read, Da," and he said, "She'd have wanted it read *to* her, girl, there's a difference, and it's the difference you're for," and I laughed, unexpectedly and wetly, and that was the funeral for the whole affair. We Dravenmoors do not hold wakes for grievances. We let them dry out on a line until they're stiff enough to use again.

* * *

The company collapsed the way companies do: not with a crash but with a sort of sighing, a deflation, men carrying ledgers out to carriages, the brass plate over the door unscrewed one afternoon while I watched from across the street with a meat pie going cold in my hand. I had expected to feel triumphant. I had rehearsed triumphant. I had, in the privacy of my room, composed several speeches of triumphant proportions.

What I felt instead was the specific hollowness of a room after furniture has been removed—where you can see the rectangles on the wallpaper where things used to hang, and the floorboards worn pale in the shape of a desk that is no longer anybody's.

Because here is the arithmetic nobody warns you about. I had given that building six years of my life. Six years of arriving before dawn to warm the typebars, of learning every voice in the office by footstep, of making myself so useful and so invisible that the two became the same thing. And when it fell, I discovered that the fall took my hiding place down with it. I had spent six years being nobody at Halloway's, and Halloway's had been the only place in the world where I knew exactly who to be.

Vindication, I have decided, is a meal served cold, late, and mostly bone.

But. Because there must be a *but*, or the story curdles: there were compensations, and they arrived in odd denominations.

Mrs. Quill from the net-loft stopped me at the fish market and shook my hand—shook it, in public, in front of the oyster wives—and said, "You did right by your father's name, Mirabel Dravenmoor, and don't let anyone tell you different, and here, take the mackerel, you've earned the mackerel." I have never before or since been paid in mackerel for moral courage, and I doubt the rate will ever be matched.

Old Tewson, who had spent twenty years calling me "girl" in the tone one uses for weather, sent word through his grandson that the divers' benevolent fund wanted a secretary, if I wanted work, "seeing as how you've got a head for paper and a spine for trouble, both rare." I had not known I had a reputation for either. It turns out that when you testify under oath in front of the entire port, people revise their files on you.

And the fog itself—well. The fog lifted, and the sun came through, and for one hour the harbor lay there gleaming and ordinary and beautiful, and I stood at the window of an empty office watching it, and I thought: *this is mine too. I live here. I am not hidden in it anymore.*

It cost me nearly everything to be able to think that sentence. I recommend the outcome and would not recommend the price to anyone.

* * *

There remained the matter of the letter, and what to do with it.

I burned the envelope. I kept the letter. These decisions took me four days, which should tell you something about how much of myself was tangled up in a single sheet of another man's handwriting.

I kept it not out of sentiment—or not only out of sentiment—but because of the fourth paragraph, the one about his sister. I read it perhaps fifty times that week, sitting in the chandlery loft while rain worked the roof. *She was right. I have spent thirty years being wrong in comfortable rooms.*

Here is what frightened me about that sentence, and why I kept the letter: I recognized the temptation in it. Not the cruelty—never that, I tell myself, never that—but the *arithmetic*. When Voss had laid that berth on the table, some part of me, some clerk's part, had done the sum instantly and coldly: one seat, three names, choose. I had chosen love, yes, in the end—but I had chosen it the way one chooses anything, by weighing, and the weighing itself was a thing I shared with him. The difference between us was not that I felt and he calculated. The difference was that when the sum came due, I paid in myself instead of others. Most days I believe that difference is enough. On the other days I reread the letter.

A woman alone in a garret with a villain's confession and too much time will either burn it or learn from it. I chose the second, and I have never regretted it, though I have occasionally resented it, which is the tax on wisdom.

* * *

On the last day of the month, the receivers came for the sign.

I went down to watch, because I am honest enough with myself now to admit I wanted to see it. Two men on ladders, a rope, the great brass letters—H A L L O W A Y—unbolted one by one and lowered into a cart like patients being brought down from a fever ward. A small crowd gathered, dockworkers mostly, and someone started a slow clap that was either mockery or mourning, possibly both; in this port those have always been the same accent.

I stood at the edge of it in my good coat, the one with the mended cuff, and a boy of perhaps twelve looked up at the bare wall where the letters had been and asked his mother what would go there instead. She said she didn't know. Nobody knew. That was the whole truth of the port that spring: the sign was down and nobody knew what would go where.

Except that I knew one thing. I knew it the way you know a tide schedule—not as information but as certainty, something in the blood older than literacy.

I walked home along the quay, past the Custom House where I had testified, past the warehouse with its new government seal on the doors, past the spot on the cobbles where Tobin and I had fought and made it up again in the space of one tide. At the chandlery, my father was on the step, mending, squinting at the clear sky as though it had personally deceived him.

"There's a ship Thursday," he said, without preamble. "Out of the eastern roads. Taking on clerks and scriveners, the agent says. Papers-work for the passenger trade." He threaded his needle through the mesh. "Thought you'd want to know. Thought maybe you were done with ships' papers."

I looked at him. He looked at the nets.

"Da," I said, "I'm not done with anything. I'm just getting started."

He grunted—the Dravenmoor grunt that means approval, agreement, and mild indigestion, all at once—and inside, on the table among the mending, I saw the corner of an envelope with a foreign postmark, and my heart went sideways in my chest, and I reached for it—

—and that is when I heard the knock at the street door, firm and unfamiliar, and a voice below calling up: "Miss Dravenmoor? There's a lady here to see you. Says she's come about a berth."
