## 1. The Last Dispatch

The border crossing at Vellund was a wooden gate, a lamp, and a man with a list.

Juniper Eriksen had studied it for three days from the attic window of a farmhouse on the neutral side, watching the guards change shifts, counting the carts that passed and were waved through, noting which ones were opened and which were not. The ones carrying grain went through untouched. The ones carrying furniture were searched. A woman with a market basket of herring had her basket upended on the road and stood with her hands folded while a soldier poked through silver fish with his boot heel. Juniper watched him do it twice and learned what she needed to learn: they were not looking for contraband anymore. They were looking for names.

She sewed the last pouch into her coat by candlelight on the third night, working with a sailmaker's needle she'd bought in a chandlery two towns back, because a sailmaker's needle goes through canvas and does not complain. The pouches were oilskin-wrapped bundles, each no larger than a prayer book, and there were four of them: one flat against the small of her back, one under each arm where a corset panel would be if she were the kind of woman who wore one, and one inside the left breast, over the heart, because whoever had designed this arrangement had a sense of humor she did not appreciate. Each stitch she pulled tight and then tested with a fingernail, and then pulled again. A seam that fails at a checkpoint does not fail quietly. It fails loudly, in front of a soldier with a bayonet, and everything after that is arithmetic you lose.

Her rule was old and simple and she repeated it to herself the way other people said prayers: *never open what you carry.*

Not never betray it. Not never fear for it. *Never open it.* Because the moment you know what's inside, you have an opinion about it, and a courier with opinions is a courier who hesitates, and hesitation at a wooden gate under a lamp is the same as confession.

She did not know what the dispatches said. She knew only their weight — a little over two pounds, distributed across her body like a second skeleton — and their provenance: the foreign ministry of a republic that had stopped existing on Tuesday, carried out through its servants' hands in the eleven days since, piece by piece, like a household fleeing a fire with its silver sewn into petticoats. She knew the cipher clerk who'd sealed them was named Oskar and that his handwriting shook. That was all. That was enough.

* * *

In the morning she put on the coat and became someone else.

The papers said her name was Fru Annelise Brandt, a typist from the capital traveling to Kalshavn to take up a position with a shipping firm — the Halvard yard, though she kept that detail in her mouth like a stone and did not let herself taste it. Typists traveled constantly in wartime or near-wartime; nobody remembered a typist's face; a typist sat in corners with her head down and her fingers moving, and that was precisely the shape of a person nobody interrogated. The papers themselves were good work — she'd paid too much for them and considered it cheap. The ink had been aged with tea and pressed under books. The official stamp had come off a genuine document belonging to a genuine dead woman, transferred with a skill that made Juniper almost nostalgic; forgery was the family trade of every government, and the republic had been excellent at it right up until the end.

She walked the last mile to the gate in the grey early light with her hands in her pockets and her breath making small clouds, and she let her shoulders do what a typist's shoulders do: round slightly, tired, apologetic. She let her walk shorten. She thought about ink stains. She thought about rent due at the end of the month. She filled herself up with the small anxieties of a small life until Annelise Brandt sat behind her eyes like a tenant who had always lived there.

The queue was short. It was always short now, because most people with anything to hide had already found other borders.

The guard at the gate was young — nineteen, twenty — with a new armband over an old uniform, the red-and-black of the Provisional Authority stitched hastily where the republic's blue-and-silver had been picked off. The thread marks were still visible on the wool. Someone had torn the old colors off this man's sleeve within the week, and the ghost of them remained, faint pale stitching in the shape of something that used to mean law.

"Fru Brandt." He read it off her papers without looking up. "Purpose in Vessmark."

"Employment. I'm expected at a shipyard office in Kalshavn."

"Which shipyard."

"Halvard's."

His pen paused. It paused only a fraction — less than a heartbeat — but Juniper had spent six years reading men at desks the way sailors read water, and she saw the pause go down into him like a stone into a well. He wrote the name anyway. He asked nothing else. But somewhere behind his eyes a little bell had rung, and she understood that the name Halvard was already on somebody's paper in some office, and that her forged identity had just tied itself, with one innocent word, to the very thing she was sworn to protect.

There was nothing to do about it. That was the discipline: knowing when there was nothing to do. She took back her papers with steady fingers, thanked him in the flat polite voice of a woman who thinks about train connections, and walked through the gate into Vessmark.

Behind her, on the flagpole above the customs house, two workmen on ladders were unfastening the republic's flag from its ropes. They worked methodically, folding the blue-and-silver into a crate like linens being put away after a death in the family. One of them glanced down at Juniper passing beneath and then quickly away, ashamed, or pretending to be. The empty halyard clanked against the pole in the wind. By evening there would be red and black up there instead, and children would be taught to salute it, and the workmen would collect their wages and drink to whatever regime paid them, and none of it — none of it — would change the fact that Juniper Eriksen was walking into a country with two pounds of its murdered government strapped against her skin.

* * *

The road to Kalshavn ran along the coast, and the coast told the truth faster than any newspaper.

She counted the signs as she went, the way she'd once counted stars from the deck of a ship whose name she hadn't known yet: a village schoolhouse with its noticeboard freshly pasted over, the Provisional Authority's decrees layered three deep; a chapel where the minister was nailing up a proclamation and could not meet her eye as she passed; a burned car in a ditch, still smelling of petrol, its doors open like a man thrown from a horse. Twice she saw soldiers at crossroads checking papers, and both times she slowed her walk, produced Fru Brandt's documents before being asked, and watched the soldiers' boredom do her work for her. Boredom was an ally. Fear was an ally. Any emotion in a checkpoint guard except curiosity.

At midday she shared a bench at a roadside well with an old fisherman mending net, and he talked to her because old fishermen talk to anyone, and she listened because listening cost nothing and told her everything.

"They took the telegraph office in the night," he said, working a knot loose with cracked thumbs. "No shots. Just men with lists. And by morning the harbor master's flying their colors and the same men are drinking coffee in the same chairs." He spat between his feet. "My grandfather lived through the last purge. And his mother through the one before. You know what my grandmother used to say? The country doesn't die. It just changes landlords, and the tenants keep paying."

"And which are you," Juniper said mildly, "a landlord's man or a tenant?"

He looked at her then — a long look, measuring — and grinned with half his teeth. "I'm a fisherman, girl. I go out when the weather allows and come home when the hold's full. Everything else is weather too." He gathered his net. "You're going to Kalshavn. Everyone's going to Kalshavn. There's a ship leaving on tomorrow's tide and a queue a mile long for it, and the new men are letting it sail because it's cheaper than shooting everyone who wants aboard." He shouldered his nets. "Mind the quay, though. Mind it close. That's where they'll make their example, if they're going to make one."

She watched him go down toward the water and thought about the weight against her ribs and the name she'd spoken aloud at the gate — *Halvard's* — and how a word, once given to a stranger, can never be taken back, only survived.

* * *

Kalshavn announced itself first by smell — coal smoke, tar, brine — then by sound: the groan of cranes working beyond the hills, and beneath it a lower sound she didn't identify until she crested the last rise and saw the harbor spread below. Voices. Hundreds of them, low and constant, the sound of a city holding its breath collectively, the way a full room sounds when everyone is afraid but nobody will say so.

Refugees camped along the quays in the drizzle, under tarpaulins and overturned boats, families arranged around small fires with the careful geometry of people conserving everything. Children ran the pilings. Old men played cards with faces like shutters. And down at the east berth, black-sided and patient, lay the ship — the Meridian, though Juniper didn't know her name yet — funnel cold, gangway down, a purser at the rail with a ledger and an expression of profound unhappiness. Even from the hilltop Juniper could see the crowd pressing at the foot of the gangway and see, too, that the purser's ledger was nearly closed. Berths, like everything else in Vessmark that week, were running out.

She stood in the rain on the ridge road and let herself look at it all for exactly sixty seconds — she timed it by her own pulse, thirty beats, a courier's luxury — and then she turned her collar up and started downhill, because a typist arriving for work does not stand on hillsides memorizing harbors.

But underneath Annelise Brandt's tired shoulders, underneath the rented calm, the dread sat where it always sat: low in the stomach, coiled, professional. Not fear of the soldiers. Fear of arithmetic. Four pouches, one coast, one harbor, one ship that might sail without her, and somewhere in this rain-soaked city a dying clerk named Oskar who held the last instructions of a dead government — and somewhere else, in a house she had not entered in twelve years, people whose name she had already, stupidly, irreversibly spoken aloud to a boy with a pen.

The rain thickened. Down on the quay, the empty halyard of a harbor flagpole clanked against metal, over and over, keeping time for a country that no longer knew what flag to raise.

Juniper Eriksen walked down into Kalshavn with her coat buttoned to the throat and her cargo riding silent against her heart, and began to look for a boarding house.
