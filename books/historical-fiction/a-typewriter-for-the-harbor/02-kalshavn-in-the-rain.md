## 2. Kalshavn in the Rain

The road into Kalshavn descended through pine country for an hour before the sea appeared, and when it did, it was grey on grey — a sky the color of wet slate pressing down on water the color of wet iron, so that for a long stretch Juniper could not say where one ended and the other began. Only the gulls broke the agreement, wheeling up white out of nowhere, screaming their grievances at nothing.

She had walked the last six kilometers rather than risk the omnibus. The papers in her coat pocket named her Juni Halversen, a seamstress from Nordvik traveling to stay with an aunt, and seamstresses from Nordvik did not, as a rule, ride first-class coaches with soldiers aboard who looked at hands before they looked at faces. Her own hands were a problem she had thought about often. They were long-fingered and quick, callused at the middle fingertips from years of keys, and no amount of lampblack or lye could make them look like they had spent their lives pushing needles through wool. She kept them buried in her pockets, curled around the warm weight of her passport, and let them be someone else's problem until Nordvik.

The checkpoint at the bridge over the Kalva River had been a tollbooth three weeks ago. Now it was sandbags and a striped barrier arm and two men in the green-grey of the Provisional Authority, one of them holding a list that fluttered in the wind off the water. Juniper joined the line of carters and fishwives and let the rain soak her shoulders while she rehearsed her answers to questions that had not yet been asked.

*Name?* Juni Halversen.

*Business in Kalshavn?* Family.

*Carrying anything to declare?* Nothing but needlework.

It was true, in the way all her best lies were true. The dispatches sewn into the lining of her coat were not *anything*. They were nothing at all, as far as any checkpoint would ever know. Four flat packets of rice paper, ciphered in Oskar's cramped hand, riding against her ribs like a second heartbeat. She had carried things across borders for six years and had never once opened what she carried, never once asked what she carried, and this discipline — not courage, never courage, only discipline — was the entire reason the foreign ministry's last functioning clerk had trusted her with its final breath.

The soldier with the list worked down the line. He was young. His cap was too big and he kept pushing it back, and his boots were wrong for the weather, city boots, already dark with water. When he reached her he looked at her face longer than he needed to, and Juniper gave him back the mild, tired blankness of a woman who has been walking since dawn and wants very much to sit down somewhere dry.

"Purpose?"

"Family," she said. "My aunt on Strandgade. She's ill."

"Strandgade." He glanced at the list, though there was no way a street was on it. Lists held names. "You'll find the city full of aunts these days. Half the country's moving in with somebody."

"Then we'll be crowded and warm," Juniper said, and smiled — a small smile, apologetic, the smile of a woman conscious of having made a joke above her station — and the soldier huffed something through his nose and waved her under the barrier arm.

She did not let her stride change until she was past the bend in the road and the checkpoint was out of sight behind the boathouses. Then she allowed herself one long breath, counted to ten the way Oskar had taught her — *fear is a metronome, girl; set the tempo yourself or it will set it for you* — and walked on into Kalshavn in the rain.

* * *

The city announced itself by smell before sight: coal smoke, tar, brine, and under all of it the sweet rot of fish heads dumped at the harbor mouth. Then the houses rose up out of the drizzle, gabled and salt-bleached, climbing the hillside in crooked ranks like an audience standing to see something better. And below them all, filling the bowl of the bay, the harbor.

Juniper stopped where Strandgade met the quay and stood in the crowd, and let herself look.

She had expected fear. Fear was in the city, certainly — you could read it in the shuttered customs house, in the new stenciled eagles over the old republican crests, paint still tacky enough to shine in the wet. But what filled the quays was something more complicated than fear. It was *waiting*. Hundreds of people camped along the waterfront in a disorder that had already begun to organize itself into streets: families under tarpaulins slung between bollards, trunks stacked into walls that made rooms, a cookfire here and a washline there, children running the improvised lanes with the tireless indifference of children everywhere. An old man had set up a chair beside his luggage and was reading a newspaper dated nine days ago, holding it carefully open at the page he had already finished. A woman was selling coffee from an urn to anyone who could pay, and to several who couldn't, judging by the number of cups that went out and the smallness of the coins coming back.

They were all pointed at the same thing. At the far side of the inner basin, where the deep-water berth lay, the steamer *Meridian* sat loading under a forest of derricks, her black hull streaked with rust, her rails already lined with faces. Crates went up. Bunkers of coal went down her hatch in a black cataract. A gangway planked with fresh lumber ran from the quay to her main deck, and at the foot of it stood a purser's table under an awning, where a clerk in a visor checked papers and a steward marked names into a ledger with the slow gravity of a man writing scripture.

Juniper drifted along the fringe of the camp, doing what she always did first in a strange place: learning its arithmetic. How many people. How much food visible. Where the soldiers were — two at the gangway, four more at the harbor gate, a patrol of three making a slow circuit of the quay with collars up and rifles wrapped in oilcloth against the rain. How many exits: the gate, the fish stairs at the north wall, the row of skiffs tied under the customs shed that might or might not be guarded. She did this without deciding to do it, the way her fingers found a keyboard in the dark. Six years of couriers' work had built the habit into her body, and bodies do not consult oaths before they act.

A boy of perhaps ten fell into step beside her, materializing the way harbor children do, hand out, eyes shrewd. "Matches? Postcards? I can carry your bag."

"I don't have a bag."

"Then I can carry you." He grinned, gap-toothed, unbothered by the rain running off his cap brim. "You're looking at the ship. Everybody looks at the ship."

"What about the ship?"

"She sails at dawn." He said it the way other boys said the name of a football club. "Tide's at half five. My papa says if you're not on her manifest by midnight you're not getting on her at all, because Captain Sørensen doesn't wait for anybody, not even the navy." He lowered his voice with tremendous ceremony. "There's one berth left."

"One?"

"One. First class, cabin C. Everything else is gone. There's a man from the bank who's offered forty crowns for it and the purser won't sell, because—" the boy dropped into an imitation of adult gravity— "*the berth is spoken for.*" He rolled his eyes. "Spoken for by nobody. Papa says that means somebody important hasn't decided yet whether they're leaving. Papa says that's how everything works now. Nobody's decided."

"And what does the berth cost?" Juniper asked, mostly to keep him talking. Information came cheaply from children if you let them perform.

"Dunno. More than we've got." He shrugged, and the shrug contained an entire economic system. "You want your fortune told instead? Two øre."

"No, thank you."

"Luck, then." He spat ceremonially into the gutter, touched his cap, and was gone into the lanes of canvas and trunks, and Juniper stood a moment feeling the odd cold spot his absence left, the way you feel it when a candle goes out in a big room.

One berth. She filed it away with the soldier's boots and the patrol's circuit and the tide time, in the drawer of her mind where useful facts went to wait for the moment they became dangerous.

* * *

The boarding house on Kirkegade had once been respectable and now made do with being warm. Its landlady, a widow named Fru Vang with forearms like a stevedore and a manner like a locked drawer, took Juniper's forged papers without reading them closely, took her week's rent in advance without comment, and led her up three flights to a room under the roof where the rain drummed the dormer window like polite, persistent applause.

"You'll hear the harbor all night," Fru Vang said. "If it keeps you awake, put the wardrobe against the wall. The vibration stops. Don't ask me why it works, it just works." She lingered in the doorway a moment longer than commercial necessity required, looking at Juniper with the frank assessment of a woman who had housed a great many people lately and had learned to sort them fast. "You're not from Nordvik."

Juniper felt her face do nothing at all, which was itself a skill. "No?"

"Nordvik women don't walk like that. Like you're counting doors." Fru Vang's eyes were pale blue and entirely without malice. "I'm not asking. I house a lot of people who aren't from where their papers say. It's Kalshavn. Nobody here is from anywhere anymore." She turned to go, then added, over her shoulder: "Hot water till eight. After that you boil your own. And if a man comes asking after lodgers — thin fellow, government coat, very polite — you never saw him and neither did I."

She went down the stairs, and her footsteps faded into the general percussion of the house, and Juniper stood alone in the little room with the rain applauding at the glass.

She unpacked nothing. She laid the coat over the back of the chair with the lining toward the wall, checked that the four packets rode flat and invisible, and sat on the bed to take stock of herself the way she'd take stock of a route: condition good, fatigue moderate, nerves — she pressed her palms flat against her knees and watched them. Steady. Almost steady. There was a fine tremor in the left hand that had arrived sometime on the road south and refused to explain itself.

Through the dormer window, past the chimney pots, she could see a slice of the harbor: the masts, the crane arms, and the *Meridian*'s single bright stack, and beyond the ship the open water where the blockade ships lay like patient dogs beyond the moles, grey shapes in grey weather, waiting for whatever the morning brought them.

Escape and trap. That was the whole harbor in two words, and both words meant the same thing. Every soul down on those quays was looking at that ship the way the drowned look at a rope — and every soldier, every clerk, every informant the new regime had bought or frightened was looking at the same rope, knowing that whoever grasped it could be caught mid-reach. You didn't need to hunt desperate people. You only needed to stand between them and the water and watch.

And somewhere in that city, in some office that smelled of ink and wet wool, there was a list. Oskar had said it plainly at the border house, coughing into his handkerchief, folding the blood away out of sight like a bad card: *They have a list, Juniper. Names go on it faster than names come off it.* She was to deliver the sealed letter to the Halvards. She was to deliver the dispatches onward. She was to be gone with the tide, ideally, though Oskar had not quite met her eye when he said it, and dying men were entitled to their small mercies of phrasing.

The Halvards.

Juniper got up and stood at the window and let the name work on her the way rain works on stone — slowly, patiently, finding every crack. Twelve years since she had jumped a Halvard collier at Rønnes pier, twelve years old and hollow-eyed with hunger, folded into the cable locker among the tarred ropes. Twelve years since Maren Halvard had found her there — not the bosun, not the mate, but Maren herself, come down to check the moorings before a storm — and had looked at this same face, this feral child's face, and had not called the harbormaster. Had brought bread down the ladder instead. Had said, in the tone of a woman discussing cargo: *You'll eat in the galley, and tomorrow we'll see.*

Tomorrow had become a season. The season had become a year, and the year had become the thing Juniper measured all later belonging against and always found wanting. She had left at fourteen, gently, without farewell — a letter typed, of course, on the office machine, because even at fourteen she had understood that a typed letter could be returned unread but a spoken goodbye could not be survived — and she had told herself the leaving was a gift to them. A stowaway is a debt, she had reasoned, and debts are best canceled early.

She had spent twelve years discovering that she had canceled nothing.

Now the ministry's last clerk had put that family's name in her hands inside a sealed envelope, and the rain came down on Kalshavn like the whole sky had decided to weep on its behalf, and down at the harbor the *Meridian* loaded crates for dawn while one berth sat empty in cabin C, spoken for by nobody, waiting for somebody important to decide.

Juniper pressed her forehead briefly against the cold glass.

First the dispatches onward — that was the oath, and the oath had order to it, and order was the only structure left standing when a republic fell. Then the letter. Then the ship. In, through, and gone, like water through a lock. No visits. No knocking on old doors. No standing in a kitchen where a woman might set another plate down without being asked why.

She repeated it to herself twice, the way she'd repeat a cipher group to fix it in memory.

Then she put on her coat, checked that the packets lay flat, and went down into the rain — because the truth, which she admitted to no one, least of all herself, was that she had already walked past the Halvard shipyard once on the way from the bridge. Just past. Slowly. Looking at the roofline of the loft where she used to sleep, and the yard gate where Anders used to post the day's work-list in his big blunt capitals, and the light burning in the upstairs office window where the old typewriter still sat, as far as she knew, under its dust cover, waiting.

She told herself she was going out to make contact with Oskar's man at the fish market, and this was true.

But her feet, which had learned their habits at the Halvard yard long before they learned their habits for the ministry, turned left at the corner instead of right — and it took her half a street to notice, and another half to decide not to correct them.
