## 4. Instructions in Cipher

The address Oskar had given her was a chandler's shop on Sild Street, wedged between a sailmaker and a shuttered telegraph office, and it smelled of tar and hemp the way every building in Kalshavn did. Juniper passed it once without slowing. Then she walked to the corner, bought a paper from a boy whose cap was too big for him, and read the same headline three times — *PROVISIONAL AUTHORITY ASSUMES FULL POWERS* — before turning back.

The rule was: never approach a meeting place directly. The rule was: count the exits first. She had learned these rules at nineteen from a woman in Rellvik who had since been arrested twice and released once, and Juniper held them now like handrails on a heaving deck. The chandler's had one door onto Sild Street and, if the windows told the truth, another opening onto the alley behind, where crates of rope were stacked under canvas. A cat sat on the sill inside, orange and unbothered. Cats did not sit on sills in shops where men waited with pistols.

She went in.

A bell over the door announced her, which she disliked immediately. Inside was dimness and inventory: coils of line hung from beams like sleeping animals, lanterns in rows, a counter scarred white by decades of knives. Behind the counter stood a young man with flour-white hands who looked up, looked past her to the street, and nodded once — not at her, at something behind her eyes, some recognition that had been arranged in advance.

"Help you?"

"I'm told you have lamp oil for a small boat," Juniper said. "Paraffin, not whale."

"Out of paraffin." He wiped hands that were already clean. "There's a man through the back who might know where some's gone."

* * *

The room behind the shop had been an office once and was now something closer to a sickroom. A stove ticked. A cot had been dragged against the wall, and on the cot, propped upright against two folded sacks, lay a man so thin that his coat seemed to hang on a coat rack rather than shoulders. He was perhaps fifty-five and would not see fifty-six. His skin had the grey translucence of fish flesh, and his eyes — dark, quick, still entirely alive — were already watching her when she came through the door.

"You're late," Oskar said.

"The checkpoints are slow."

"The checkpoints are slow because they want to be slow. It makes people feel governed." He gestured with a hand that trembled until he stilled it deliberately, pressing it flat against the sack beneath him. "Sit. There's a chair. Mind the third leg; I've meant to fix it since Tuesday and I find I've stopped meaning to fix things."

Juniper sat. Up close, the smell of him reached her — camphor and laudanum and beneath both something sweetly wrong, the smell of a body eating itself. She had met dying men before. In the ministry's back rooms in Rellvik, in the last weeks of the old government, there had been a great deal of dying going about, most of it slower and quieter than this.

"You're Oskar Vint," she said. "Third clerk, cipher registry."

"Was. Titles rot faster than men do." A smile touched his mouth without warming it. "You're Eriksen. They said you'd be small and careful. You're smaller than advertised and more careful than they deserve."

"They said you'd be dead by spring."

"Spring is optimistic. My doctor is a coward and flatters me." He coughed then — a dry rattle that went on too long — and turned his head aside with the practiced economy of a man who had done this many hundreds of times, and when he brought his handkerchief away from his lips there was blood on it, bright as sealing wax. He folded the handkerchief closed, unhurried, and put it in his pocket as though pocketing a letter. "Forgive me. Where were we."

"You were telling me my business."

"So I was." He studied her a moment longer than comfort allowed. "Do you know what a registry clerk does, Eriksen? Everyone thinks we file things. We don't file things. We decide what may be found again. Every government is only its memory, and the memory lives in the registries. When this one died last week—" he said *died* the way other men said *changed hands*, "—the new authority seized the buildings and burned nothing. That's what frightens me. They burned nothing. They want the memory intact. They want to know who typed what, who carried what, who signed what, and on which day. And they have clerks of their own now. Young ones. Sober ones. With good lamps and long hours."

Juniper kept her face still. Underneath it, the dread moved the way water moves under ice — present, directional, silent. "And my dispatches?"

"In your coat, unless you've grown sentimental." He almost smiled again. "I know how you carry them. Left side, sewn into the lining, oilcloth, wax seals intact. Don't look surprised; I wrote your route. I wrote everyone's routes, God help us all. You're the ninth courier I've briefed this month and the second I expect to reach Kalshavn alive."

"And the others?"

"Detained. Turned. One drowned herself at Pernau rather than be questioned, which I record here as the only dignified act of our entire administration." He said it flatly, without drama, and somehow that made it worse. "You'll want to know why yours matters more. Or you won't ask, because you're disciplined, and I'll tell you anyway, because I'm not."

He reached beneath the cot — the motion cost him; she watched his jaw set against it — and brought out a wooden document case, plain, unvarnished, the kind any shipping office in the country owned by the hundred. From it he took a single envelope. Heavy paper. Sealed with the foreign ministry's arms pressed in black wax, the ribbon knotted in the old style, the kind of seal that could be checked against the ministry matrix and would check true.

"This goes to the Halvard house. Shipwrights, Kalshavn quay. You know them."

It was not a question, but Juniper answered it anyway, or tried to. "I—"

"Don't explain. I didn't ask." His voice sharpened, then softened just as fast, as if even severity cost him breath. "Deliver it into Maren Halvard's own hands. No intermediary. No drop. If she isn't home, you wait. If she's dead, you burn it. Those are the instructions and they're not mine; they come from above me, from whatever is left of above me."

"What's in it?"

"A name."

Whose name sat on her tongue — *whose?* — and she swallowed it down, because asking twice would mark her, and marked couriers ended their routes early. But Oskar watched her swallow it, and something in his face shifted, a flicker of pity so brief she might have invented it.

"The regime has a list," he said instead. "You understand that much. Names to be taken before the tide changes, before ships leave, before witnesses scatter. The list exists in three copies. One is in the provincial governor's safe. One went south yesterday. And one—" he tapped his own temple with one yellowed finger, "—one is in here, because I copied it out four days ago, before I understood what I was copying, and I have regretted it hourly since."

"You memorized an arrest list."

"I memorized everything for thirty years. It's the whole of my profession. Now I'm dying with state secrets in my head and no vault to keep them in except this." He laughed, and the laugh became coughing, and the coughing bent him double, and Juniper half-rose before he waved her down with two fingers. When he could speak again his voice was thinner, scraped. "Listen. This matters. Listen as though the walls have ears, because in Kalshavn this week, they may."

"I'm listening."

"There are names on that list you'd expect. Officers. Editors. The usual brave fools. And there are names that shouldn't be there — names of people who never opposed anyone, who never signed anything, quiet people, useful people. The Halvards among them." He let that sit. "Why would a new regime arrest shipwrights? Think. Not for what they've done. For what they can do. Ships move people. The Meridian sails on the morning tide, and every berth aboard it is worth its weight in gold to someone who wants out — and worth its weight in blood to someone who wants certain people kept. Whoever controls the harbor controls the list's ending. Understand?"

"I think so."

"No, you understand arithmetic. Understanding comes later, when it's too late to be useful." He pulled the envelope toward him along the cot and rested his hand on it. "This letter is part of it. I don't know how. I was told to have it delivered by a trusted hand, and trusted hands are short this week, and yours came recommended by a man who is now dead, which should tell you exactly how much trust is worth in Vessmark at present."

"Who recommended me?"

"A dead man." His dark eyes held hers, steady and apologetic at once. "That's the whole of the answer I have. I'm sorry. I've spent thirty years distributing ignorance professionally and I find I have none left for myself."

The stove ticked. Somewhere beyond the wall the chandler's boy moved crates, each scrape of wood loud as a knock. Juniper became aware of the envelope the way one becomes aware of a weight in a boat — small, low, capable of capsizing everything.

"Take it," Oskar said.

She took it. The wax seal was cool through her glove; the ribbon was old-style, knotted left over right. Her fingers knew documents the way a pianist's knew keys, and something in the weight of it, the balance of it, whispered wrongness she couldn't yet name and wouldn't examine, because examining it was forbidden by the oldest rule she had.

*Never open what you carry.* She recited it inwardly like a prayer in a language she was beginning to doubt.

"Your dispatches," Oskar said. "The coded ones in your lining. Deliver them to the harbormaster's office, attention the night watchman, before six tomorrow morning. He knows the protocol. After delivery, your commission ends. You're free. Do you hear me? Free. Take whichever ship will have you and don't look back at this sad little country; it doesn't deserve the looking."

"And if the night watchman isn't there?"

"He'll be there."

"And if the regime has the harbormaster's office?"

Oskar looked at her for a long moment, and in that moment she saw him weigh something — whether to lie to her, perhaps, whether lying still mattered to a man with blood in his handkerchief — and decide against it, or decide that the truth was crueler and therefore kinder.

"Then you'll have delivered a government's last words to its gravediggers," he said. "And you'll have done your oath perfectly, and the oath will have been worthless, and you'll spend the rest of your life deciding whether that was your fault. Welcome to the civil service, Miss Eriksen."

* * *

She rose to go. At the door his voice stopped her, weaker now, running down like a clock.

"Eriksen. One more thing."

She turned. He had shifted himself higher against the sacks, and in the lamplight his face had the strange clarity of the very ill, every bone legible.

"The list. I said I'd memorized it. There's a reason I'm telling you that, and it isn't vanity." He breathed carefully, rationing air. "If you're taken — when, if — they will ask you about a sealed letter. They will describe it to you. Black wax, old-style ribbon, Halvard name. They'll offer you your life for confirmation that such a letter exists and traveled by courier. Do you follow me?"

"Yes."

"Do you understand what follows?" His eyes were very bright and very tired. "They can only ask about a letter they know is traveling. Which means somewhere between Rellvik and this room, the knowledge of this letter crossed into enemy hands. Which means either the letter is bait, or the courier is bait, or both, and I am too far into the ground to learn which." He closed his eyes briefly. "I am sorry. I truly am. You were the best of the nine."

"That's not comforting, Herr Vint."

"No," he agreed. "Nothing is, anymore. Go. Tide waits for no one, not even the innocent."

Juniper slipped the envelope inside her coat, against her body, where the dispatches rode sewn in their oilcloth. Two cargoes now: the coded dispatches of a dead republic, and one sealed letter naming — naming whom — a family that had once fed a stowaway child at their own table without ever asking her name.

She went out through the alley door. The rain had thickened; gulls wheeled screaming over the rooftops, and beyond them, invisible in the weather, the Meridian lay loading at her berth with one place still empty.

Behind her, through the wall, she heard Oskar begin to cough again, and did not stop walking, because stopping was a luxury the list had already priced beyond her means.

By morning, she promised herself, the letter would be in Maren Halvard's hands and her part in it finished. She repeated it twice more, like a countersign, and did not quite believe it either time.

The letter rode against her ribs the whole way back through the rain, warm as a coal, patient as a fuse.
