## 5. A Name on Sealed Paper

The boarding house on Strandgata was called the Anker, which Juniper found either a comfort or an irony depending on how the wind came off the water. It stood four doors from the fish market, its façade the color of weak tea, its windows fogged with the breath of a city that had stopped believing in good news. She had taken a room on the third floor under the name Fru Holm of Røstvik, a widow's name she had worn twice before and would wear again. Widowhood was useful. No one asked a widow where her husband was; they only asked if she'd eaten.

She had not eaten.

In her room there was a bed with a quilt that smelled of other people's winters, a washstand, a chair, and one window that looked down on the street where lamplighters were working their way along the gutters in the last grey hour before dark. Rain had begun again — Kalshavn's rain, fine as sifted flour, the kind that never fell so much as hung in the air waiting to be breathed. Below, refugees moved along the quay with bundles tied in bedsheets, and somewhere a ship's bell counted out eight bells, and someone laughed at something, briefly, and then did not laugh anymore.

Juniper set her satchel on the chair and did not touch it for a long moment.

Her rule was simple enough to be carved in stone: *never open what you carry.* Not because she lacked curiosity — curiosity was a luxury of people whose hands stayed clean — but because what you didn't know, you couldn't surrender, couldn't be tortured into telling, couldn't be tricked into confirming. ignorance was armor. For nine years she had carried dispatches across borders in diplomatic pouches sewn into coat linings, in hollowed book spines, once inside a loaf of bread whose crust she had learned to bake herself, and she had never once broken the seal. Other couriers talked about it the way sailors talked about sirens. The temptation always sang. You just kept rowing.

But this letter was different, and she had known it was different since Oskar pressed it into her hand that afternoon with his blood-flecked handkerchief still folded in his fist like a white flag no one would honor.

*Deliver it to Halvard. To the house, not the yard. To Maren, if you can — the wife will know what to do.*

He had said *the wife,* and then corrected himself — *the matriarch* — and the correction had cost him a coughing fit that bent him double over his desk among the dead ministry's living furniture. And when he'd straightened, wiping his mouth, he had looked at her with an expression she could not file under any of her professional categories. It had been pity. Or apology. She had told herself it was neither, that dying men's faces did things they couldn't help.

Now, alone, with the rain writing its long grey sentence against the glass, she took the letter out and laid it on the quilt.

It was heavy stock, cream-colored, the kind the foreign ministry reserved for communications too sensitive even for cipher. The seal was unbroken — a disc of red wax stamped with the ministry's triple-tower crest, pressed deep and clean. The address was written in a clerk's careful hand: *To the House of Halvard, Shipwrights, Kalshavn. By trusted courier. To be opened by the addressee alone.*

*To be opened by the addressee alone.*

She turned it over. Turned it back. She sat on the edge of the bed and held it in both hands and felt the weight of it, which was nothing — paper weighs nothing — and the weight of it, which was everything she owed a family who had fed a half-starved stowaway twelve years ago and asked nothing back but that she eat her soup while it was hot.

There were reasons to open it. She assembled them the way she assembled cover identities, patiently, one plausible piece at a time. The government that issued this letter no longer existed; its ministers were fled or arrested or signing new oaths with old ink. A letter from a dead state might be a dead letter — but it might also be a live grenade, and grenades deserved inspection. Oskar was dying and possibly delirious; dying men had been used before, their instructions tampered with en route. If the contents endangered the Halvards, she had a right — a duty — to know before she carried such a thing through their door. She was not merely a courier now. The chain of command had dissolved around her. She was, in the eyes of whatever God kept books on such things, the last officer of the republic's foreign service still moving, and officers inspected their ordnance.

These were the reasons. They were good reasons. She knew, sitting there in the failing light with her typist's fingers resting on the wax seal like a pianist testing a key, that they were also lies. The truth was smaller and hotter: the way Oskar had said *Halvard*, the pity he hadn't managed to hide, and the simple animal fact that after three days of checkpoints and forged papers and soldiers tearing the old flag down in strips, she needed — needed, the way a drowning thing needs air — to know what she was carrying into that house. Because if it was bad, if it was very bad, she wanted to stand outside their door tonight, in the rain, and decide whether she could knock at all.

She got up and filled the washstand basin from the pitcher. The boarding house pipes groaned somewhere below, a sound like a ship settling.

Steaming a seal open was a trade skill taught by a woman named Vera in a safe flat in Göteborg six years ago, a woman who smelled of cigarettes and taught tradecraft the way nuns taught catechism: without warmth, without exception, with the certainty that every rule existed because someone had already died breaking it. *You hold it over the steam, not in it. You let the wax soften, not melt. You lift with a thin blade, angled, patient. If your hand shakes, stop, wait, begin again. Impatience is how seals tear, and torn seals are how couriers hang.*

Juniper held the kettle she'd borrowed from the landlady over the little spirit burner until it whispered, and she held the letter above the rising steam, rotating it slowly, counting her breaths the way Vera had taught her to count everything — in fours, in patience, in discipline. The wax began to lose its gloss. She slid the blade of her penknife under the edge, angling up, feeling for the moment the wax released its grip on the paper beneath.

Her hand did not shake. That was the terrible thing, looking back. Her hand was perfectly steady, trained steady, professional steady, while the rest of her screamed.

The seal lifted whole, pale side up, a perfect red moon coming away from its sky. She set it on the washstand, face-down, preserving its underside for re-sealing. Then she drew out the folded pages.

Two sheets. Heavy stock. Typed — which surprised her; she had expected handwriting, expected the intimacy of ink. Typed on a machine with a slight defect she noticed before she understood she was noticing it: a lowercase *e* that struck faintly off-center, its eye a hair too high, so that every *e* on the page wore its small blind look slightly upward, like a man pretending not to see what was in front of him.

She unfolded the first page and read.

It began with protocol — references to emergency decree numbers, citations of articles of a constitution currently being used as kindling elsewhere in the capital. Then the second paragraph arrived, and the room went quiet in the specific way rooms go quiet when something inside them changes shape.

*...pursuant to Article Nine, the following persons resident in the Kalshavn district are designated enemies of the restored state and are to be taken into protective custody prior to the sailing of any vessel from said harbor, their presence aboard constituting a risk of flight and continued agitation...*

A list followed. Six names. Dockmen. A schoolteacher. A harbormaster's assistant. She read them the way she read all lists, quickly and without investment, names being weather to a courier, names passing through her and leaving no sediment —

And then the fourth line.

**HALVARD, ANDERS — shipwright.**
**HALVARD, MAREN — household head.**
**HALVARD, LIV — minor.**

Three lines. Three names. One of them sixteen years old.

The page did not change. That was the strangest part. The paper stayed cream-colored and calm, the defective *e*s went on wearing their blind upward look, the words sat where words sit, and none of it burned, none of it bled, none of it acknowledged in any way that it had just reached across twelve years and taken hold of her throat.

Juniper discovered she was standing. She had no memory of standing. Her legs had decided something without consulting her.

She read it again. This time slowly, the way she proofed copy, hunting for error, hunting for the loophole, the misprint, the clerical accident — *Halvard* was not so uncommon a name, there must be others, cousins, strangers, some other Halvard family in the Kalshavn district that owned a shipyard and built the finest hulls on the coast and had once taken in a stowaway because her mother had—

No. There were no other Halvards. There was only Anders, who at fourteen had taught her to splice rope and pretended not to notice when she cried; only Maren, who had set a second plate without a word and made that plate appear every night for a month as though it had always been there; only Liv, whom Juniper had never met, born two years after she'd slipped away in the dark with a stolen kiss on a sleeping child's forehead — Liv, a minor, *a minor*, listed between her parents like cargo on a manifest.

The second page was worse. The second page was instructions — to her, though it did not name her, addressed instead to *the bearer*. The letter the Halvards received was not a warning. It was not aid. Read closely, in the cold light of the lamp she now realized she had lit without noticing, it was a summons dressed as correspondence: the Halvards were to understand that their arrest was scheduled, that the state extended this one courtesy of advance notice, and that the bearer — the courier who carried this — was to confirm receipt and return with word of the family's response.

Return. With word. With names, with reactions, with the location of anything the family might try to hide or move.

She read the paragraph three times before she let herself see what it actually was.

This letter was not a message to the Halvards.

This letter was bait on a hook, and she was the line.

*No,* she thought, and heard Vera's voice over the years of distance, dry as cigarette smoke: *When a document tells you more than it should, ask who benefits from your knowing it. Knowledge is rationed, girl. Free knowledge is the most expensive kind.*

But Vera's voice was far away and the room was close and the rain was on the glass and the names were on the page, and Juniper Eriksen, who had crossed a border with pouches sewn into her coat, who had lied to soldiers with a steady voice, who had never once opened what she carried in nine years of carrying — Juniper stood in a rented room above a street full of fleeing people and shook.

Not gently. It began in her hands, her typist's hands, the fingers that could strike forty clean words a minute in three languages, and they trembled so badly the pages chattered against each other like teeth. She gripped the edge of the washstand. The basin rang softly against the porcelain. She put the letter down on the quilt because she could no longer hold it, because holding it meant holding what it said, and what it said was this:

The state she had served since she was seventeen years old — the state whose flag she had watched being torn down in strips at the border crossing, whose cipher keys she knew by heart, whose oath she had renewed each spring in a marble hall that smelled of floor wax and permanence — had written the name *Halvard* on a list of enemies. Had written *minor* beside a girl she had kissed goodnight once and left. The republic she had given her steadiness to, her silence, her sleepless crossings, the best years of her hands — that republic was gone anyway, gone into the pockets of men with new armbands, and its ghost, its dying clerk, its last loyal remnant, had sent her to deliver the killing paper to the only people who had ever treated her like something worth keeping alive.

And underneath that — deeper, colder, in the place she did not want to look — the other question, the one Oskar's pity had been pointing at all along:

*Did they send me because I'm the best courier left?*

*Or because I'm the one name on this list that means nothing to anyone else?*

She pressed her palms flat against the washstand until the trembling slowed. Outside, the lamplighter finished his row and the street settled into the blue-dark, and down on the quay the Meridian's riding lights had come on, three small flames reflected in black water, the ship that sailed on the morning tide with one berth still unclaimed. Somewhere in that harbor city, in a house she could see from this window if the streets bent the right way, Maren Halvard was probably cooking supper, setting plates, not knowing that her name was typed on cream-colored paper in a rented room four streets away, sealed with her own government's contempt.

Juniper looked at the letter for a long time.

Then she picked it up, folded the pages along their original creases with hands that had almost remembered their discipline, and reached for the wax.

She stopped.

The seal lay face-down on the washstand, and in the lamplight she saw what she should have seen at the start, what exhaustion and dread had blurred past: the underside of the wax, the impression left where the stamp had pressed — and around its rim, faint, nearly invisible, a second mark. Not part of the official stamp. Something added afterward, pressed lightly, the way a forger tests his own work. A tiny device she did not recognize. A private mark, on an official seal.

Vera again, unbidden: *Official seals come from official dies. If a die has two stories in it, girl, one of them is a lie.*

Juniper stood very still in the rented room, holding the letter she had sworn not to open, listening to the rain and the ship's bell and her own heart correcting itself stroke by stroke toward something harder than fear.

Tomorrow she would carry this to the house on the hill, because an oath is an oath and a debt is a debt, and she could not do one without the other.

But first — before dawn, before she let this paper within a mile of Maren's kitchen — she was going to find out whose hand had really written it.

She blew out the lamp, and sat in the dark with the seal in her palm, learning its false face by touch.
