## 7. Sawdust and Sons

The saw had been screaming since dawn, and Anders Halvard was sick of the sound of it.

Not the sound itself — he'd been raised inside that sound, had slept through it as an infant in a drawer lined with shavings while his father planed keel timbers ten feet away. It was what the sound meant now that he hated: they were rushing. The Halvard yard did not rush. The Halvard yard built slow and true, and men paid extra for the privilege of waiting, because a hull that went into the Baltic water in November came out of it whole in April, and that was worth more than speed.

But half his crew hadn't come back after Sunday.

He stood on the staging above the dry dock with his hands braced on the rail, counting heads the way he counted them every morning, and this morning the count came up eleven short. Eleven men out of thirty-four. He knew where they were without looking — down at the quay, standing in the rain with their hats off and their faces turned toward the *Meridian*, that grey-sided refugee ship loading crates and human beings with the same indifferent efficiency. She sailed at dawn tomorrow. Every man who could scrape together passage money, or knew someone who could vouch for him at the purser's desk, was going to be on her.

"Karlsson's gone," said Bram, his foreman, coming up the ladder with two mugs of coffee and the particular slouch of a man delivering news he'd rather have kept. "Left his tools on the bench. Didn't even take his own plane. Twenty years with us."

Anders took the coffee. It was scalded, over-boiled, made by whoever had drawn short straw on the yard stove. He drank it anyway. "His wife's sister is in Göteborg," he said.

"You know that?"

"I know everything about these men. That's the job." He said it without bitterness, mostly. "If I didn't know whose sister was where, I wouldn't know which man needed his wages early in October, or whose boy had the fever, or who shouldn't be put on scaffolding after his funeral last spring."

Bram grunted. Down below, the skeleton of the fishing trawler — the *Solveig*, laid down in June, planked as far as her waterline — sat in her cradle like something butchered mid-surgery. Eleven men gone meant she would not be finished before winter closed the harbor. It might mean she was never finished. Anders looked at her the way he'd look at a wound he couldn't yet decide how to dress.

"The regime's men are at the customs house," Bram said, quieter. "My brother saw them come off the steamer this morning. Grey uniforms. Not army-grey. The other kind."

Anders felt something cold settle behind his breastbone, the way it had been settling all week, each day a degree colder. "Then we keep our heads down and our hands busy. Busy hands don't get counted."

"That your father's wisdom or yours?"

"His. Mine is shorter." He drained the mug. "Get me the list of who's left. If we're going to launch the *Solveig* late, we'll do it with pride, and if we're going to fail, we'll fail on schedule."

Bram went down the ladder laughing the way men laughed in Kalshavn that week — a short bark, quickly smothered, the laugh of people practicing not to be heard.

* * *

The stranger was in his mother's kitchen again.

Anders noticed things; it was how he'd been built. His mother claimed she could tell a bad plank by the smell of it, but Anders had inherited the other sense, the one that read faces and hands and the way a person stood in a room. And there was a woman at his mother's table, third morning running, wearing a coat too good for Kalshavn and hands too rough for the coat, and his mother was setting a plate in front of her as if she'd done it every day of the woman's life.

He stood in the doorway with sawdust in his hair and let himself watch.

She sat with her spine straight but her shoulders drawn slightly in — the posture of someone conserving space, used to rooms where taking up room cost something. Her hands were wrapped around a cup and he watched the fingers: long, precise, the nails trimmed square. A typist's hands, or a clerk's. But there were calluses on the pads that no clerk earned, and when she lifted the cup her wrist turned with a small economy of motion that spoke of practice at being careful.

"Mama," he said. "A word about the *Solveig*'s stern post?"

Maren Halvard looked up at him with an expression of serene, weaponized innocence. "Of course. Juniper, you'll excuse us — eat, there's more bread."

Juniper. So she had a name now. He filed it away with the coat and the hands.

They stepped out onto the side porch, into the drizzle. Below them the yard spread out in its ordered chaos of timber stacks and steam box sheds, and beyond it the harbor lay flat and pewter-coloured, crowded now with boats that should have been out working and weren't.

"Stern post," Maren said pleasantly.

"She's a guest."

"She's a hungry young woman eating breakfast."

"Mama." He kept his voice level. "Grey uniforms came off the steamer this morning. There are lists. Everyone knows there are lists. And three days ago you take in a woman none of us know, who wears a city coat and has boat calluses, and you won't say where you found her."

"I found her on the doorstep, same place I found you, though you were louder about it."

"This isn't a joke."

"No," his mother agreed, and some of the lightness went out of her face, leaving the granite underneath. "It isn't. Listen to me, Anders. In 1899 your uncle Torvald knocked on doors asking questions about a man who'd stayed with us one night. Do you remember what happened to Torvald?"

"He moved to Copenhagen."

"He moved to Copenhagen *because* the man he asked about was hanged anyway, and Torvald carried it until the day he died. Questions are cheap, son. Answers are expensive. You want answers from her, earn them the way you'd earn anything else — by giving her work and watching her do it. A person's hands will tell you what their mouth won't." She touched his cheek, once, briskly. "Now go cut wood. And send her down to the office when she's finished — I told her you needed help with the accounts, since your clerk ran off to the *Meridian* too."

"You did *what*?"

"I volunteered her. She can type." Maren smiled the smile that had governed this household for forty years and went back inside, and Anders stood in the drizzle understanding that he had been managed, thoroughly and with love, by a woman who weighed less than a spinnaker.

* * *

The ship office smelled of pitch, paper, and the iron stove that never quite warmed the far corners. Anders had cleared his dead clerk's ledger off the big desk and left it bare except for the accounts, and when Juniper Eriksen appeared in the doorway — promptly, at the minute his mother had named, which he noted — he gestured her in without ceremony.

"My mother says you can type."

"I can type."

"These are the yard accounts. Wages, materials, debts owed to us, debts we owe. My clerk kept them in his own private shorthand and then abandoned civilization, so half of it needs recopying clean." He pulled out the chair. "You'll be paid the same as any office hand. One kroner fifty a day and your dinner."

"That's generous."

"It's standard. Don't mistake it for trust." He hadn't meant to say it quite so plainly, but there it was, hanging in the air between them, and he found he didn't want to take it back.

Juniper set her cup down. She looked at him straight, grey eyes steady, and he had the uncomfortable sensation of being measured by someone accustomed to measuring. "What would you mistake it for, Mr. Halvard? What would I have to do?"

"Work," he said. "That's the only currency I deal in. My mother trusts easily. I don't. It's nothing personal — I don't trust my own shadow this month, and my shadow has been with me thirty-one years."

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth. Not quite a smile. "Then give me something to do that isn't copying."

"Why?"

"Because copying tells you whether I can spell. You want to know something else. Ask for it."

He studied her a long moment. Outside, the rain ticked against the window and somewhere across the harbor a ship's horn sounded, low and mournful, the voice of the *Meridian* testing herself for the morning.

"All right," he said slowly, and reached under the counter for the drawing roll. "We're planking the *Solveig*. Stealer stuff — the twisty work near the turn of the bilge, where every plank has to be spiled to fit a curve that doesn't exist anywhere else on the hull. My best spiler is drinking coffee with the purser of that grey tub right now. Can you use a saw?"

"Yes."

"A broad hatchet?"

"Yes."

"A rabbet plane, a spiling batten, and a pencil?"

There was a pause of perhaps half a second. "I can learn the batten."

"Honest," he said. "Good. That's rarer than skill." He unrolled the drawings across the desk and weighted the corners. "Here's the station lines. Here's the mould. We cut a spile off the battens, transfer to stock, saw close to the line, fair it with the hatchet, and if it fits the frame without daylight showing, it goes home. If it doesn't fit, we do it again. No filler, no forcing. A forced plank leaks, and a leaking boat drowns people, and I've buried enough people this year already."

Her eyes had gone to the drawing and stayed there, moving along the station lines with a focus he recognized — the focus of someone reading something important.

"Where did you learn tools?" he asked.

"Places," she said. "I worked a summer in a machine works in Rostock. Before that—" she stopped, and he watched her choose not to finish the sentence, watched her do it cleanly, without the tell-tale flush of a liar.

"Before that," he echoed.

"Before that is a long story and you've got a boat to build."

He almost liked her. That annoyed him.

* * *

The steam box shed stank wonderfully of hot oak and wet wool, and the rain drummed the tarred roof like a thousand small impatient fingers. Anders worked beside her all afternoon, and he worked her hard, harder than he'd have worked a man he trusted, because the test wasn't kindness — the test was the joint.

He showed her once how to hold the spiling batten against the frames, how to tick the marks with the compass, how to split the difference on a double curve. Then he handed her the hatchet and stepped back and pretended to be busy with the caulk hammer while she squared up the first plank.

She was wrong-footed at first — anyone would be, going from paper to oak — but she corrected fast, and what struck him wasn't speed, it was attention. She listened to the wood. He could see it in the way she shifted her grip after feeling the grain run off-line, in the way she turned the plank over twice before committing the saw. Some people fought timber and some people negotiated with it. She negotiated.

The sawing was the test proper. A spiled plank near the turn of the bilge has edges that run at angles no square can help you with; you saw freehand, close to the line, and the line curves away from you in two directions at once. Most beginners saw proud — leave too much wood — which is forgivable. Or they saw shy, cut past the line, which ruins the stock and wastes a day, and reveals a mind that rushes.

Juniper Eriksen did neither. She sawed slow, checked the line every few inches by sighting down the blade, and when the waste piece finally fell away, the edge stood a hair proud of the scribe mark all the way along, waiting for the hatchet like a sentence waiting for its final word.

"Fair it," he said, and handed her the hatchet, and stood close enough to watch.

She took off the proud wood in thin curls, turning the plank against the light, checking the bevel against the frame bevel he'd given her. When she thought it was done she held it up — and then, instead of declaring victory, she held it against the frame herself, sighted the gap, frowned, and took off two more whisper-thin shavings before she offered it to him.

"Try it," she said.

He took the plank. He fitted it against the frames at the third station, pressed it home — and it went home. No daylight. No rocking. The land lay fair against its neighbor, the bevels kissed, and somewhere in his chest a door he'd been holding shut with his shoulder all week swung open an inch.

"Well?" she said.

He handed the plank back. "Do six more like it and I'll tell you."

But his voice had changed and he knew she'd heard it change. They worked on through the afternoon in the steam-smell and the rain-noise, and she cut four more planks before the light failed, and three of the four fit first try, and the fourth needed one pass of the plane, and by then neither of them was pretending it was a test anymore. It was just work, the good kind, the kind that fills a body up and quiets the head.

At some point, without discussing it, they fell into talking the way people talk over labor — sideways, eyes on the wood.

"Your grandmother," Juniper said, wrestling a batten around the mould. "Ilsa. Your mother said she keeps asking for me. Why?"

"Because my grandmother collects people the way other women collect buttons. Don't let her interrogate you. She was present at both previous purges and she treats them like weather she survived."

"Three purges," Juniper said quietly. "If you count this one."

"Do we count this one?"

"I count it." She set the batten, marked, straightened. "Mr. Halvard — Anders. Your crew. Are they all accounted for? The ones who left?"

"Gone to the ships, most of them. Karlsson, my clerk, the Nielsen brothers—"

"Not all of them." She said it carefully, still working. "Yesterday, by the coal sheds, there was a man watching the yard. Not one of yours. Not one of the refugees either — refugees watch the ships. This one watched *you*. Then he wrote in a little book."

Anders felt the cold thing behind his breastbone shift, grow teeth. "You're sure."

"I'm sure I saw a man writing in a book. What it means, I don't know. But if I were building ships in a country that had just changed owners, and someone were keeping notes—" she shrugged, driving the pencil home, "—I'd want to know today, not tomorrow."

He stood very still among the shavings. Through the open door of the steam shed he could see down the length of the yard, the dark shapes of the sheds, the crane arm against the bruised evening sky, the lamp in the office window where his mother had left the light burning as she always did. And beyond the yard wall, at the corner by the coal sheds, a figure in a rain-dark coat stood where a figure had no business standing at that hour, and even at eighty yards Anders could feel the stillness of it — the particular patience of a man whose job was to stand and watch and write.

The figure turned and walked unhurried toward the harbor road, and disappeared into the rain.

"Tomorrow," Anders said, "you'll cut the hood ends at the transom. It's the hardest joint in the boat." He picked up his coat. "And tonight, we tell my mother what you saw. All of it. Including whatever it is you're not telling me — because there is something, Juniper Eriksen, and I'm not asking tonight. But the man in the book changes things. Whatever you're carrying, you'd better decide soon whose hands it belongs in."

She had gone white under the tan, he noticed. But her voice, when it came, was level as a scribed line.

"And if I told you it belongs in no one's hands?"

"Then," said Anders, pulling the shed door shut against the rain and the dark and the watching eyes, "we'd finally understand each other. Because that's exactly what I'm afraid of."
