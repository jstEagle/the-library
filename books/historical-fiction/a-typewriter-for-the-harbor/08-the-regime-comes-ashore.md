## 8. The Regime Comes Ashore

The steamer *Vigilant* came into Kalshavn harbor at half past four in the afternoon, riding a grey sea under a greyer sky, and Captain Elias Reval stood at the rail long enough to count the cranes along the quay. Eleven working, two idle, one with its jib cocked at an angle that suggested neglect or sabotage. He wrote the number in his notebook — *11/2/1* — because he had learned long ago that a city announced itself in small failures before it confessed in large ones.

He was forty-four years old and looked younger, which he considered an operational asset. He was of medium height, narrow through the shoulders, with the kind of face that waiters forgot before they reached the kitchen. His uniform was new and pressed to a sharpness that made the harbor officials who came to meet him look, by contrast, like men who had slept in their clothes. He had done so deliberately. First impressions were instruments; you chose them as carefully as a surgeon chose a blade.

The gangway went down. He did not hurry to descend. He let the harbormaster's people wait the full three minutes it took him to finish reviewing a folder, then closed the folder, squared its corners against the rail, and walked down at an even pace.

"Captain Reval." The harbormaster was a broad man named Skovgaard, sweating despite the cold, extending a hand that Reval took briefly and released. "The Provisional Authority's office has been prepared for you above the customs house. We weren't told—"

"You were told yesterday." Reval smiled. It was a good smile, warm at the edges, practiced in a mirror until warmth and practice became indistinguishable. "I understand there were difficulties with the telegraph lines."

"The storm, sir. Two nights ago. Lines are down past Torvik."

"Then I'll want a courier schedule by morning." He handed Skovgaard the folder without looking at him. "And the passenger manifest for the ship *Meridian*, sailing tomorrow at dawn. All names, all berths, all amendments since booking opened. Originals."

Skovgaard blinked. "The manifest? That's — the purser holds those papers, sir, but I'm not sure I have the authority—"

Reval turned his head slightly, the way a man turns when a sound interests him, and looked at the harbormaster properly for the first time. It was not a hostile look. It was the look of a man reading a document.

"Harbormaster," he said pleasantly, "the government that issued your commission no longer exists. The authority that replaced it exists. Your commission continues at our discretion, which is a generous arrangement, and one I intend to honor — provided the paperwork I request arrives when I request it." He glanced out at the water, where gulls wheeled over the fish market's refuse. "This is a fine harbor. I would prefer to administer it than to reorganize it. You can help me decide which."

"Yes, sir," said Skovgaard. "Of course, sir. The manifest will be on your desk within the hour."

"Wonderful." Reval beamed at him. "You see how quickly we've come to understand each other."

* * *

The office above the customs house smelled of cold pipe smoke and other men's decisions. Reval unpacked his own things first: a typewriter, portable, German, cleaned and oiled the night before; a blotter; a photograph of no one; a wooden box containing pens, ink, a magnifying glass, and a pistol he did not expect to need, because pistols were what one used when one had failed at everything else.

By six o'clock the manifest lay before him, and he read it the way other men read scripture — slowly, twice, with attention to what was absent.

Forty-one names. Thirty-eight claimed. Three berths cancelled in the last week: one by a merchant named Pell whose reasons were financial, one by a widow whose son had been arrested in the capital and who had, apparently, decided to stay and share whatever came, and one — this interested him — cancelled yesterday by telephone, no name given, paid in cash, refunded in cash. He circled nothing. He did not write in the margins of documents that might someday be evidence. He copied the anomaly into his notebook instead, in a small hand so regular it might have been typed.

Among the thirty-eight: the Halvard family. He knew the name already, of course. Shipwrights, four generations, a yard on the east quay that employed — he checked his figures — sixty-two men as of spring, perhaps forty now, if the desertions to the refugee ships continued at their current rate. An old family. A respected family. A family whose patriarch had signed the petition of protest against the Provisional Authority's first decree, back when signing such petitions still seemed like a thing a man could survive.

Reval did not believe in coincidence. In twenty years of intelligence work across three governments, he had found that coincidence was simply a pattern observed too early, and that most men's lives were far more legible than they believed. A shipwright family with a yard full of loyalist workers. A ship leaving at dawn. A capital that had sent him here with two lists — one of arrests, one of questions — and a third instruction that existed only in his memory, having never been written down: *find the dispatches*.

The ministry in the capital had lost something in the week of the coup. Not money, not seals, not even men. Dispatches. Coded traffic from the final days of the old republic, the kind of paper that could prove to foreign chancelleries exactly who had financed the new order's rise — and therefore exactly whom to distrust. The generals wanted it burned. The ministers wanted it buried. And someone very senior, someone whose name Reval never spoke aloud even in his own thoughts, wanted it found, because a weapon that could destroy your patron was a weapon you made certain of holding yourself.

So: Kalshavn. One ship. One tide. Whatever left this harbor tomorrow morning would carry the truth out of reach forever, unless it left carrying something else instead.

There was a knock. A clerk entered — young, pale, with the ink-stained fingers of a man who processed other people's futures for a living. Verner, according to the duty roster. Harbormaster's records clerk, eleven years' service, unmarried, a mother in Torvik.

"Captain. The — the lists you asked for. Dock workers with union affiliations. Shipyard employees. And the, ah, the religious census for the parish, though I don't know why you'd—"

"You won't always know why," Reval said kindly. "That's not a criticism. Knowing why is my profession. Yours is knowing where things are filed." He took the papers. "Sit down a moment, Clerk Verner. Do you smoke?"

"I— no, sir."

"A pity. It gives the hands something honest to do." He sorted the papers into piles with quick, economical movements. "Tell me about the Meridian's bookings. Who handles amendments?"

"Purser Halvorsen. But cancellations go through the harbormaster's window, mostly. Cash ones especially. People don't want their names near—" He stopped himself.

"Near what?" Reval asked, with genuine curiosity.

"Nothing, sir."

"Verner." He said the name gently, the way one addresses a nervous horse. "In the coming weeks, many people are going to bring you information. Some of it will be spite. Some of it will be fear dressed up as patriotism. Some of it will be true. My job is to weigh these things, and I am very good at my job. But I cannot weigh what I am not given." He folded his hands. "Now. Yesterday's cash cancellation. Telephone call. No name. What did the voice sound like?"

The clerk's throat moved. "Young, sir. Maybe. Or frightened. Frightened voices sound young."

"Yes," Reval said softly. "They do." He smiled again, and this time the smile did not reach anywhere near his eyes, though it never quite failed to reach his mouth. "Thank you. That will be all. Oh — and Verner? If anyone asks what we discussed, tell them the truth. I have no interest in secrets. Secrets are for people who lack thoroughness."

* * *

He walked the city that evening, alone, hatless, in the rain, because a man in a good coat walking slowly is invisible in any harbor town, and because maps lie about cities in ways that feet do not.

Kalshavn in the rain was herring barrels and tarpaulins and the smell of tar. Refugees camped under the customs shed awnings with their luggage stacked around them like fortifications — a wicker trunk, a birdcage with a cloth over it, a child's rocking horse too large to abandon and too heavy to carry, dragged anyway. They watched him pass with the flat assessment of animals. He nodded to several. None nodded back. He noted that too.

At the east quay he stopped before the Halvard shipyard gates and read the brass plate: *HALVARD & SONS, SHIPWRIGHTS, EST. 1849*. Through the fence he could see the yard's skeleton — ribs of a half-planked hull rising against the lamplight, the smell of sawdust and hot pitch, a handful of men still working late. One of them, broad-shouldered, moving with the certainty of ownership rather than wage, directed two others at the slipway. Anders Halvard, presumably. The eldest. The heir.

Reval watched him for four minutes by his pocket watch. The man worked. That was worth noting. Men who worked with their hands in a crisis were either fools or foundations, and foundations were the dangerous kind.

A girl came down the quay through the rain — sixteen or so, collar up, a canvas message satchel banging her hip — and called something to the gate watch, laughing, and ran on toward the dry dock. Liv, then. The youngest. The one who carried messages around the docks, according to the file, and who therefore saw everything and understood, at sixteen, almost none of it.

Children who see everything grow into adults who remember everything. He wrote the thought down later, at his desk, and underlined it once.

* * *

At nine he received the harbormaster again, along with the police resident and a customs officer, and held his first briefing in the conquered territory, serving coffee he had brought himself because he did not yet trust theirs.

"Gentlemen," he began, "I want to thank you for your cooperation, which I know costs you something. I don't ask you to love the new order. I ask you to be accurate." He set down three copies of a single-page order. "Effective tonight, the harbor gates close at ten and open at five. No civilian vessel departs tomorrow except the *Meridian*. Guards at the gangway from midnight. Passenger names checked against the manifest — against *my* manifest, the corrected copy, which supersedes the purser's."

The police resident, an older man with a veteran's tired eyes, read the page twice. "Corrected how, Captain?"

"Names may be struck," Reval said. "None may be added. If a name on the original does not appear on my copy, that person does not sail, and I will want to speak with them — courteously, I assure you — before they go anywhere at all." He let that settle. "The Meridian carries thirty-eight souls tomorrow. By Friday I intend to know which of them booked in good faith, which of them booked in panic, and which of them booked as cover. Panic is forgivable. Cover is my business."

"And the shipyard families?" the customs officer asked. "The Halvards are — well. Half the town owes them a boat or a favor."

"Then half the town will be watching closely," Reval said, "to see whether favors purchase exceptions. They will not." He rose, signaling the meeting's end, and added, almost as an afterthought, in the tone of a man sharing a confidence: "One more matter. Somewhere in this city is a piece of paper — or something smaller than paper — that certain parties abroad would pay a great deal to recover, and a great deal more to suppress. It may be in a house. It may be in an office. It may be in a machine." He paused on the word just long enough. "If it surfaces, gentlemen, I will know within the hour, because everything surfaces eventually, and the harbor is a very small pond. Good night."

After they had gone he sat for a while with the lamp turned low, listening to the rain work the windows, and reviewed the day as he reviewed every day: entries, observations, anomalies.

Anomalies: one cash cancellation by telephone, voice young or frightened. A shipyard working late under a regime it had publicly opposed. A message girl who laughed in the rain. And somewhere in the pile of routine reports, a line item he had read three times: the customs ledger showed a diplomatic courier's transit stamp from the border station at Torvik, dated four days ago, female, traveling alone, papers in order — *too* in order, he had thought, reading the description of the examination, flawless in the way that only forged papers or genuinely dull travelers ever were.

Dull travelers did not come to collapsing countries in the last week of a coup.

Reval blotted the entry, aligned his pens, and switched off the lamp. Outside, the rain kept falling on the quays, on the refugee camps, on the dark bulk of the *Meridian* riding at her moorings with her dawn tide waiting somewhere out in the black water — and on the lit window of the Halvard house, where, though he could not have known it, a stranger was being welcomed to a table set with an extra plate.

He fell asleep quickly, as he always did. Ruthlessness, he had found, was mostly a matter of good rest.
