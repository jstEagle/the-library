## 10. What the Youngest Saw

Liv Halvard had three rules for running messages, and she'd invented them herself, which made them better than anyone else's rules because she understood the reasons behind them.

Rule one: never run if you can walk fast. Running told everyone you were in a hurry, and a girl in a hurry was either carrying something worth stealing or worth stopping.

Rule two: know every door on your route that opened inward, because an inward door could be barred from inside, and a girl who knew where to bar herself in would never be caught out in the open by anyone she didn't want to be caught by.

Rule three — and this one she'd learned from watching her father argue with the harbor pilots for eleven years without once raising his voice — was that most people told you everything they were going to do if you simply stood near them long enough and looked like you weren't listening.

She was thinking about rule three as she came down off Copper Row with a parcel of invoices under her arm and the smell of her mother's bread still on her sleeves, because the two grey soldiers had been standing at the corner of the fish market for the better part of an hour, and they hadn't bought any fish, and they hadn't spoken to anyone, and they kept looking at things.

Not at people. At *things*. That was what made Liv stop, on the far side of the market square, pretending to adjust the strap of her shoe.

The old men who sold herring off the carts looked at people — at hands, mostly, and pockets, because that was where money lived. The harbormaster's clerks looked at papers. The customs men looked at crates. Everyone in Kalshavn who made his living watching anything had learned to watch the way his trade watched: narrowly, professionally, at the one thing that mattered to him.

These soldiers didn't look like that. They looked the way Anders Halvard looked when he walked through the shipyard at the end of the day, taking inventory of what needed doing — broad, slow sweeps, corner to corner, roofline to waterline, nothing personal about it at all. The way you looked at a thing you intended to own or dismantle.

And the taller one had a camera.

It wasn't a tourist's camera. Tourists came to Kalshavn twice a year on the coastal steamers and photographed the lighthouse and each other in front of the lighthouse, laughing, holding the little boxes sideways. This camera was heavy and black and mounted on a small wooden tripod, and the soldier worked it with the same unhurried competence he applied to standing still — unfolding the legs, leveling them, winding the film forward with his thumb while his eyes went on sweeping.

Liv counted what he photographed.

First the yard gate. Then the yard gate again from a different angle, lower, crouched. Then the long shed where the steamer *Vessa* was half-planked, her ribs open to the sky like a whale's. Then the slipway, empty since August. Then — this was the one that made something cold settle behind Liv's breastbone — the office window. Her father's window. Where the lamp burned late and the family's surplus typewriter sat on its cabinet like a sleeping animal nobody thought about anymore.

He took two photographs of the office window. Then he folded up the tripod, said something to his companion, and both of them turned and began walking toward Copper Row, unhurried, precise, like men following a route they'd already drawn on a map.

Liv stood very still until they passed her, close enough that she could hear the shorter one chewing something, and then she picked up her parcel and walked home at exactly the speed of a girl with no thoughts in her head at all.

* * *

Dinner at the Halvard house was a ceremony that had survived three governments and two purges, and it had survived them by refusing to notice them. There was bread — there was always bread; Maren baked as though the ovens might be confiscated next — and there was barley soup with whatever the fishmonger owed them that week, and there were rules of seating so old nobody remembered inventing them. Ilsa at the head, propped in her wheeled chair, sharp-eyed and sharp-tongued and currently complaining that the soup had too much dill in it, which it did not, and never did, and everyone knew the complaint was simply how Ilsa said *I'm hungry and glad of it.*

Maren at her right hand. Anders at the foot. Juniper Eriksen — the mail courier, the boarder, the stranger with the typist's hands who'd arrived three days ago with a satchel full of other people's letters and a smile that switched off like a lamp — sat where guests sat, between Maren and the wall, and said little and ate everything and watched the door the way sailors watch weather.

Liv slid into her place, broke her bread, and reported the afternoon the way she reported everything, in the flat cheerful tone she used for tide tables and gossip alike.

"There were soldiers photographing the yard today," she said. "At the fish market. They took pictures of the gate and the *Vessa* and Father's window."

The spoon in Ilsa's hand stopped halfway to her mouth.

It was such a small thing, that pause. Liv heard it the way she heard a dropped tool on the dock — not the sound itself but the silence after it, the whole street holding its breath. She looked up.

Her mother's face hadn't changed at all. That was the strange part. Maren continued ladling soup into Juniper's bowl as though Liv had announced rain, and her hands were perfectly steady, and her eyes had gone somewhere else entirely — somewhere behind her face, some room Liv had never been shown.

"Photographs," Anders said. His voice was easy. Too easy. Liv had grown up on that voice; it was the voice he used with creditors and inspectors and drunk captains, the voice that meant the real voice was locked in a drawer somewhere. "Of the yard? What would they want pictures of the yard for?"

"Maybe they're building ships," Ilsa said dryly, and got her spoon moving again. "In their new government. With all those talented soldiers."

"Grey uniforms," Liv said, warming to her subject now, because the table was listening and being listened to was nearly as good as being useful. "New buttons. Flags on the collars — not ours. And the camera was a good one, on a tripod, and the tall one knew how to use it, he wasn't fumbling like the tourists do. He took two pictures of the office window." She frowned slightly, working at it. "Why would anyone want two pictures of a window?"

Nobody answered.

Juniper had gone very still. Not frozen-still, the way a guilty person freezes, but the stillness of someone setting down something fragile very carefully before they move. Liv caught it because rule three worked at dinner tables too: stand near people long enough, look like you aren't listening, and they will tell you everything.

But nobody told her anything. Her father said, "Pass the salt," and her mother asked Juniper whether the mail had come clean over the border, and Ilsa complained about the dill again, and within four minutes the conversation had moved on to the price of tar and whether the *Meridian* would really sail on the morning tide and whether old Brakke the pilot had finally gone deaf or only deafer.

Liv ate her soup and felt the cold spot behind her breastbone slowly warm again, because the table had absorbed her news the way the harbor absorbs a stone — one ring, then smooth water, then nothing.

Except.

Except that after dinner, carrying plates to the kitchen, she heard her parents' voices in the scullery, low and fast in the space between the pump strokes, and she heard her father say, "—the office, Maren, why the *office*—" and her mother say, "—not here—" and then the pump started up again and covered everything, and Liv stood in the doorway with the plates and decided, with the particular stubbornness of sixteen, that she was not going to be managed.

She was the fastest runner in the family. She delivered messages for the shipyard, for the chandlers, for half the offices on the quay. If something was happening at the docks, she saw it first, because seeing things first was literally her job. And tomorrow she would see more, and she would bring it home, and she would put it on the table the way she'd put the soldiers there tonight, plainly, usefully, and everyone could stop whispering behind pumps.

She stacked the plates. Through the kitchen window, past the dark bulk of the woodshed, the harbor lay under its first lamplight, and out on the black water the *Meridian* rode at anchor with her loading lights burning — one bright point among the rigging where the purser's lantern hung by the gangway, marking the last unclaimed berth the way a star marks a grave or a promise, depending on how you read it.

Liv read it as neither. She was sixteen, and the ship was simply the morning's news, and news always came to her eventually.

She just didn't yet know that this time, it had come to her first — and that the two photographs of a lit window were already in a courier's satchel somewhere across town, traveling toward a man named Reval who would look at them tomorrow morning and write, in his neat and courteous hand, a single word beside the Halvard name:

*Confirmed.*

* * *

That night Liv couldn't sleep.

It wasn't fear. She examined the feeling carefully, the way she examined everything, and decided it was more like the night before a launch — the whole hull sitting there finished and impossible, and everyone knowing that tomorrow the wedges come out and the world changes and no one can push the ship back onto the ways. Something had started today at the fish market. She'd felt it start. A wedge knocked loose.

So she did what she always did on sleepless nights: she pulled on her boots and her coat and went up the back stairs to sit outside her great-grandmother's room, because Ilsa couldn't sleep either, ever, and the two of them had an arrangement that consisted entirely of not discussing it. Liv sat against the wall by the door with her knees up. Inside, the bed creaked, and the oil lamp hissed, and after a while Ilsa's voice came through the wood, dry as paper, not raising itself because it didn't need to.

"You're sitting on the floor again. Your mother will find fluff on that coat."

"Coat's fine."

"Hmph." The bed creaked again, settling. "You brought your news to the table tonight."

Liv blinked at the dark. "How'd you know?"

"Sixty-eight years at this table. I know what the air does when you've said something true." A pause. "Soldiers photographing the yard."

"Two of them. Grey ones."

"Mm." Another creak. Liv pictured her great-grandmother's face, the deep seams of it, the eyes that had watched the harbor burn once and the ministry fall twice. "In the purge of 'eighty-one, they drew the yard first," Ilsa said. "Before they came for names, they came for drawings. Your great-grandfather couldn't understand it. *What do they want with our plans,* he said. As though the plans were the point." Her laugh was short and had no humor anywhere in it. "The plans are never the point, girl. The plans tell them what you value. You photograph what you intend to take."

Liv sat with that. Down the hall, the house ticked and settled around her — the sounds she'd slept inside her whole life, beams and pipes and her father's late footsteps below in the office, the lamp still burning behind the frosted glass that a stranger had photographed twice today from the fish market with a tripod and a steady thumb.

"Ilsa," she said quietly. "Are we going to be taken?"

The lamp hissed. Somewhere out in the harbor a buoy bell rang, patient and stupid, counting waves for no one.

"No," Ilsa said at last, and her voice had shifted into the register Liv had only heard twice before — flat, certain, older than the house. "We are going to leave. Or some of us are. That is what we build ships for, in this family. We build ships so people can go on living somewhere else." The bed creaked one final time, decisive. "Now go to bed. And take your coat off the floor. Your mother works enough."

Liv went. But at her own door she paused, looking down through the stairwell window at the harbor, at the *Meridian*'s loading light, at the dark shapes of the yard sheds where her family had built forty years of departures for other people and had never once, in all that time, needed one for themselves.

Tomorrow, she decided, she'd run her usual route. Fish market, customs house, chandlery, quay. Rule three: stand near people, look like you're listening, and they'll show you everything.

Two grey soldiers had already taught her the first lesson of the new government.

They watched what they intended to take.

And tomorrow, she was almost sure of it, they'd be watching the ship.
