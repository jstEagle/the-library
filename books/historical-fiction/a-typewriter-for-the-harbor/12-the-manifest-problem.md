## 12. The Manifest Problem

The news came to Anders the way bad news always came to Kalshavn — sideways, wrapped in something ordinary.

He was in the yard's counting room with the ledgers spread before him, doing arithmetic he already knew would not resolve. Eleven of his forty-two men had left that week for the ships. Three more had asked for their wages early, and when Anders paid them without argument, they had looked at the floor as though the coin itself were a shame they were passing between them. A shipyard could survive a shortage of timber. It could not survive a shortage of hands that believed there would be a future in which the work mattered.

The knock was two beats, soft. He knew it before he opened the door: Halvard knuckles, learned at this same door by every child in the family, always too polite to be family and never polite enough to be strangers.

Verner Kask stood on the step with his cap in his hands. He was one of the old platers, sixty if he was a day, who had riveted half the hulls in the harbor and now spent his days sweeping sawdust because his eyes could no longer hold a chalk line.

"You'd better come to the kitchen," Verner said. "There's a man from the harbormaster's office. He's been asking after the Meridian."

Anders set down his pencil. "Asking what?"

"Who's booked. Who paid. Who hasn't." Verner turned his cap a quarter turn. "Anders — he had the manifest. The whole thing. In his hand, like a shopping list."

* * *

The man from the harbormaster's office did not stay long. By the time Anders crossed the cobbled lane to the house, he was gone, leaving behind a smell of cheap tobacco and a silence that sat on the kitchen like weather.

Maren stood at the stove though nothing was on it. Liv was at the table with her schoolwork pushed aside, her chin on her fists. Juniper Eriksen — the courier, the guest, the woman with the careful face who mended nets faster than any dockhand Anders had ever employed — sat near the window, still as furniture, listening with the particular quality of attention that Anders had begun, uneasily, to recognize as professional.

"Tell him," Maren said to Verner.

So Verner told it again, in the flat voice men used for things they didn't want to make more real by shaping them well. The clerk from the harbormaster's office had come around the boarding houses and the shipping agents' doors all afternoon. Not asking questions — reading answers. He carried a copy of the Meridian's passenger manifest under his arm, and where a name interested him, he made a small mark in a notebook.

"And here's the thing," Verner said, and his hands worked harder at the cap. "Old Sørensen at the chandlery tried to buy two places for his daughters. Paid in full, gold, on the spot. The clerk wrote down the payment." He paused. "He didn't write down the names going on the list. He wrote them down coming off."

The clock in the corner ticked through four full seconds.

"Coming off," Anders repeated.

"The new harbor authority," Verner said. "Captain Reval's office. They've taken control of the manifests. Every name on every ship sailing tomorrow has to pass their desk. The Meridian included. Maybe the Meridian first."

Liv spoke from the table, her voice carefully casual in the way of a girl who had learned that week that casualness was armor. "Then what does a ticket buy you?"

Nobody answered her. That was answer enough.

Anders looked at his mother. Maren had gone very still over the cold stove, and he saw in her shoulders the same thing he'd seen in her back the winter their father died — the posture of a woman deciding, ahead of everyone else, exactly how much fear she could afford to show.

"Thank you, Verner," she said quietly. "Go home. Kiss your wife. Don't tell anyone else tonight."

Verner went. The door closed, and the family remained — Anders, Maren, Liv, Ilsa's empty chair angled toward the warmth of the stove, and Juniper at the window with rain beginning to streak the glass behind her.

"Well," said Ilsa's voice from the doorway, dry as chalk dust. Everyone turned. She stood leaning on her stick, having come the twelve feet from her bed on some instinct older than hearing. "I've lived through two of these purges, and both times the government discovered it could count. It always ends with lists." She lowered herself into her chair by degrees, like a ship settling onto blocks. "The only question worth asking is whose names are on which ones."

* * *

They waited until Liv was sent — over her loud objections — upstairs to bed, and until the lamp in the hall was turned low. Then they gathered in the type room, the long narrow office off the kitchen where the shipyard's papers lived: contracts forty years deep, hull drawings rolled in tubes, and the old ministry typewriter squatting on its cabinet like a fat black hen. Anders lit two lamps. His mother closed the shutters. Juniper stood with her back to the wall, arms folded, and said nothing, but she had not been sent away either, and no one suggested it.

"We can't sail," Anders said. "Or rather — we can't count on sailing. If Reval can strike names from the manifest, then our tickets are worth exactly what he decides they're worth. Which is nothing, or less than nothing, if he wants us on that quay when the soldiers come."

"So we don't be on the quay," Maren said. "We go overland."

"Overland." He'd known she'd say it. He'd spent the afternoon at the ledger doing the sums for it anyway, because he was his father's son and hope required figures. "Mother. Think it through with me. North road first — it's watched since Tuesday; the garrison at Brundvik has pickets on the bridge. East road through the fells — three days' walk to the Swedish border in country that's already snowed in above the tree line. We'd need guides, and the guides are charging twenty crowns a head now, and half of them are selling their clients' routes to the militia by morning."

"Ilsa has walked those fells," Maren said. "She walked them in '82."

"Ilsa," Anders said gently, "is seventy-eight years old, and her heart is—" He stopped. In the wall's direction, faintly, he could hear the grandmother in question coughing once, twice, and then falling silent again as if embarrassed. "You know what her heart is. Dr. Lemm said flat ground and no frights. There are no flat grounds between here and Sweden, Mother, and the whole journey is one long fright."

Maren turned away and busied her hands with the shutter latch, which did not need touching. "Then we split," she said to the wood. "Those who can walk, walk. Those who can't—"

"—stay," Anders finished. "That's the arithmetic, isn't it? That's what we're actually talking about. Who stays."

The lamps hissed. On the desk, the typewriter sat with its keys like a mouthful of small teeth.

Juniper spoke for the first time. Her voice was level, and Anders noticed — the way he'd been noticing all week, the way a man notices a hairline crack in a hull plate — that she kept her hands absolutely still while she talked, as though they were things being managed separately from the rest of her.

"There may be a third road," she said. "A ship doesn't have to carry your names to carry you."

Maren looked at her. "Meaning what?"

"Meaning crews aren't always manifested the way passengers are." Juniper unfolded her arms, then folded them again. "A ship like the Meridian will need deckhands for the crossing whether the passenger list survives Reval's desk or not. Harbors are short of sailors this month. Some harbors are about to get shorter." She let that sit a moment. "It's not clean. But it's a road that runs over water instead of fells."

Anders studied her. "You've thought about this before. Moving people past checkpoints."

Something moved behind her eyes and was put away, like a document returned to a drawer. "I think about roads," she said. "It's my trade."

"It's nobody's trade," Ilsa said from the doorway.

She had come again, uninvited and unstoppable, her shawl over her nightdress, her stick tapping the boards. She crossed to her own chair by the cold little stove in the corner of the type room — the chair she'd sat in through two purges and one bankruptcy and her husband's funeral — and lowered herself into it with the finality of an anchor dropping.

"Now," she said. "Let us say plainly what we are dancing around. There are seven of us in this house who would need to leave. Me. My son's widow. Her three children — one of whom, Liv, cannot keep a secret longer than a tide turn, God love her. Young Erik from the loft. And the courier girl, who is not ours but has eaten at our table, which by my accounting makes her ours." She held up a hand before Anders could speak. "Seven. And we have tickets for five, bought at prices that stripped the yard's cash box, and the tickets themselves are now worth whatever a man named Reval writes in his little book. And the alternative is a frozen mountain range that would kill me by the second day and possibly young Erik too, because the boy has lungs like wet paper."

"Grandmother," Anders started.

"Don't 'grandmother' me, boy, I raised your father and I'll out-argue you from this chair with half my wits tied behind my back." Her eyes glittered, fierce and clear. "I am telling you the sums so you don't have to pretend them. Someone does the arithmetic in every purge. In '94 it was me, sitting in this very chair, deciding which cousins went to sea and which stayed to open the door when they came. I got it right and wrong in equal measure, and I have buried people from both columns." Her voice dropped, and it was worse than the sharpness — it was tired. "The arithmetic is impossible, children. That is its whole nature. You do it anyway, and then you live with the remainders."

Nobody spoke. Rain worked at the shutters. Somewhere down the lane a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

It was Maren who finally broke it, and she broke it the practical way, which was her gift and her armor. "Then we prepare both roads," she said. "We pack as if we're walking, and we watch the harbor as if we're sailing, and we find out — properly find out — what Reval's office is actually doing with that manifest before we stake anyone's life on a guess." She looked at Anders. "You know people at the docks. People who see things."

"People who see things get seen seeing them," Anders said. But he was already thinking of Hallgrim at the customs shed, who owed the yard eleven years of goodwill, and of the boy who swept the Meridian's gangway, who was sweet on Liv and would talk to her for the price of her noticing him.

"Then be careful," Maren said simply. "Carefully, Anders. Whatever you learn, however you learn it — come home after. That's the whole instruction. Come home after."

Juniper shifted against the wall, and Anders caught it — the smallest stiffening, a breath taken and held. He filed it away with the other small things he'd been filing away all week: the way she read the shipping notices upside down across the breakfast table without appearing to look; the calluses on her fingers that came not from rope but from keys; the fact that she had arrived in Kalshavn with no luggage worth the name and no plans worth the telling, in the exact week the republic died.

Later, he thought. Later he would ask her straight out who she was and what she carried. Tonight there were bags to pack for a road they might not take.

* * *

He packed alone in the type room after midnight, because someone had to and because his hands needed work or they would shake.

One bag per person, that was the rule the refugees on the quays had taught the whole city by example. One bag: documents, medicines, photographs, tools of trade, and whatever small object a human being decided was the shape of their whole life. Anders found himself standing before the shelf of hull drawings — forty years of his family's thinking made visible, every vessel they'd ever dreamed into the water — understanding that he could take none of them. Paper was weight. Weight was speed. Speed was survival.

In the end he took the yard's cash ledger, because whoever survived would need to know who owed what; his grandfather's dividers, brass and worn smooth; and the photograph of his father standing on the ways beside the Freja, squinting into a sun that had gone out of the world years ago.

From the bedroom above, through the floorboards, he heard Ilsa's voice, low and rhythmic, and Liv's answering murmur — the grandmother telling stories again, the girl pretending she was too old for them. He heard his mother moving through her own room, drawer by drawer, each one opened and closed softly, the sound of a woman deciding what a life weighed.

He was latching the bag when the typewriter caught the lamplight — that squat ministry machine the family had bought surplus a decade back, that typed every contract and every letter since and had never once been interesting. He rested his hand on its carriage without knowing why. Cold iron, warm room, and beneath his palm a faint rattle, as if somewhere inside it something sat loose that had not sat loose before.

Down the lane, toward the harbor, a whistle blew — one note, held long, the new signal the patrols used at night. Then another, farther off, answering.

Anders turned down the lamp and stood a moment in the dark, listening to his family breathe above him, and did his arithmetic one last time, and got the same impossible remainder.

In the morning, he decided, he would go to the customs shed. He would find out exactly what Captain Reval's little book contained — and whether any name in it could be saved by anything except luck.

Above him, floorboards creaked. Juniper's door, opening and closing without a sound. Going out, or coming in.

He lay down on the type room cot with his boots on, and did not sleep.
