## 13. Midnight in the Type Room

The house slept the way ships sleep — with small sounds beneath the silence, timber settling on its keel, wind testing the seams. Juniper lay in the narrow guest bed under the eaves for two hours before she admitted she would not sleep at all.

Oskar's words had followed her home through the rain like a dog that wouldn't be shooed. *The dispatches were typed on a ministry machine. One sold as surplus. Guess where.* He had coughed then, that wet tearing cough, and gripped her wrist hard enough to leave marks. *If they wanted you to find them, girl, why send you a letter that names the family? Think about what the letter is*for*.*

She had thought about it all evening, across Maren's table, through Anders's careful questions about the roads east, through Liv's chatter about the fish market and the soldiers who'd been loitering near the customs house. She had thought about it while Maren tucked a wool blanket around her shoulders and said, "There's a candle stub in the drawer, if you read at night," as if Juniper were twelve years old and homesick — which, God help her, some buried part of her still was.

And now, past one o'clock, she dressed in the dark and let herself out of the room without letting the third stair creak. She had memorized which stairs creaked on her first night in this house, eleven years ago, when she was not a guest but a stowaway being fed in secret. Some knowledge you keep even when it stops being necessary. It felt like superstition now. It felt like prayer.

The ship office stood across the yard from the main house, a long low building of blackened brick where the Halvards had drafted hulls for sixty years. In daylight it smelled of pencil shavings and linseed oil and the particular salt-rot of old drawings. At night, with the yard lamps out, it was just a darker shape against the darker sky, its windows reflecting nothing.

Juniper crossed the yard with her collar up. The rain had stopped but the cobbles held water in every hollow, and she stepped where she knew the stones were sound. She carried no lamp. Her eyes had been trained for darkness — couriers learned quickly that light was a confession — and besides, she knew what she was looking for. A typewriter. An Underwood, Oskar had said, serial number ending in 7, sold off with a lot of office furniture in 1906 when the ministry reorganized its chancery. The Halvard ship office had bought it. The Halvard ship office had used it for six years to type invoices and charter agreements and letters to suppliers.

And somewhere inside its iron body, if Oskar was right and if Oskar could still be trusted, were the true dispatches of the Republic of Vessmark — typed, character by character, into the machine's own bones. Microfilm, he'd said, or something like it, hidden where only someone who knew the machine's serial number would ever think to look. Not in a drawer. Not behind a picture. Inside the thing itself.

She tried the office door. Unlocked — Anders never locked it; there was nothing in the building worth stealing that a thief could fence, and the yard's dogs were deterrent enough. It swung open on hinges that groaned once, loudly, and she stood frozen in the doorway for a full minute, listening.

Nothing. Rain dripping from the eaves. The dogs shifting in their kennels by the gate, one low woof, then quiet.

Inside, she counted paces to the filing cabinets along the north wall, then to Anders's desk, then to the type room — the small interior chamber where the clerks worked, windowless, so that the lamplight wouldn't fade the drawings hung outside it. Her fingers found the doorframe. The room exhaled its closed-air smell: dust, ink, cold iron.

She had brought matches but hesitated to strike one. Instead she worked by touch, moving along the wall of shallow drawers where the family kept ribbons and carbon paper and spare keys for nothing in particular. Her fingertips read the contents like Braille: paperclips, a tin of pins, rolled tracing paper, a wooden box of nibs.

Then the cabinet.

It stood in the corner where the two walls met, an oak cupboard taller than a man, the kind offices used to store ledgers and confidential correspondence. She knew it was the right one because she remembered it — remembered, absurdly, hiding behind it during a game of blind man's buff with Liv when Liv was five and Juniper was nineteen and already gone from this house three years. The memory arrived whole and unwanted: lantern light, shrieking children, Maren laughing in the doorway with flour on her hands.

Juniper knelt and tried the lower doors. Locked. The upper drawer. Locked. She ran her fingers along the seam and found the keyhole, small, brass-rimmed, worn smooth by decades of use.

Of course it was locked. Everything important was locked; that was how the world told you where to dig.

She took out the little roll of tools she always carried — a courier's kit, flat as a playing card: two picks, a tension wrench, a strip of stiff wire. Her hands knew this work better than her heart knew anything. She inserted the wrench, fed the pick in, and began to read the lock the way she read cipher text, feeling for the pattern underneath the noise.

Five pin stacks. Old brass, forgiving tolerances. Somewhere above her, thunderless lightning flickered faintly through the high windows of the drafting hall, and she thought, irrelevantly, of her mother's hands doing exactly this — she had learned the picks from her mother, in a kitchen much poorer than this one, in a country that had already been eaten once. *Locks are honest,* her mother used to say. *A lock tells you exactly what it is. It's people who lie.*

The third pin set. The fourth. The fifth gave with a soft click she felt in her teeth more than heard, and the cylinder turned.

The cabinet doors opened onto shelves of ledger books, their spines furred with dust, and below them, wrapped in an oilcloth apron against damp, a shape with the unmistakable silhouette of a typewriter — the square shoulders, the ribbon spools like upraised arms.

Juniper struck a match at last. One match, cupped in her palm, burning down fast. The flame showed her the machine plainly: an Underwood No. 5, black enamel chipped at the corners, space bar worn to bare steel in the middle. She tilted it toward the light and found the serial plate, and her breath went thin.

The number ended in 7.

She set the match down on a saucer someone had left on the shelf — clerks' habits die hard — and lifted the typewriter out onto her knees. It weighed as much as a sleeping cat. She turned it over. She examined the carriage, the typebars, the segment where the metal fingers rose to strike the platen.

Nothing visible. Of course nothing visible. Whatever Oskar's people had hidden here in 1906 or whenever it was, they had hidden it for a courier who knew what to look for — and Juniper did not, quite, know yet. She needed the machine alone, unhurried, with proper light and proper tools. She needed to take it apart, and taking apart a typewriter made noise, and noise was the one currency she could not spend tonight.

So she would carry it back to the house. Under her coat, through the yard, up the creaking stairs she had memorized. Simple. Simple things were rarely simple.

She reached for the oilcloth to rewrap it, and that was when she saw the dust.

Or rather, where the dust wasn't.

The shelf the cabinet had sat on bore a clean rectangle — sharp-edged, pale — where the oak base had protected the wood from years of settling grit. But the clean rectangle was smudged. Dragged, actually: four parallel sweeps through the dust at the front edge, as though the cabinet had been pulled forward and pushed back more than once. Recently. Dust didn't settle in hours, but it hadn't had time to resettle fully either — the sweeps' edges were still crisp, the exposed wood not yet dulled.

Someone had opened this cabinet within the last few days.

Juniper knelt very still, the match burning down toward her fingers, and made herself look the way Reval's people would look. Evidence first, conclusions after.

The cabinet's right-hand door hinge was bright where the paint had been scratched — fresh scratches, metal-bright, as if someone had forced the door with a blade or a pry bar before finding or cutting a key. On the floor of the cabinet, among the ledgers, lay a small curl of pale wood, like a shaving, and next to it a smear of grease that smelled of machine oil, not door hinge oil. And pressed into the dust on the top shelf, half-hidden behind the ledgers, a boot print. Small. A man's heel, worn on the outer edge. Not Anders — Anders's boots were enormous, and anyway, why would Anders search his own cabinet?

Her match died. In the dark, the little room seemed to contract around her, walls leaning in like a hand closing.

She struck another match, hating the flare of it, needing it. The boot print again. The greased lock. And now, on closer look, the keyhole itself: the brass rim bore fine circular scratches, concentric, the signature of a key turned roughly, or of a lock picked by someone less careful than she was. Someone had gotten in. Someone had looked inside this cabinet, perhaps at this very typewriter, perhaps touched it with their hands —

— and left it.

That was the part that tightened around her chest like a knot pulled slowly shut. If they had found what they wanted, the film would be gone and the game would be over. If they had found nothing, fine. But to search and leave? That meant they weren't sure. That meant they were waiting. That meant the cabinet was being watched, or would be watched, or — worse — that leaving it untouched was the trap itself, the way a hunter leaves a snare undisturbed so the animal walks into it believing the path safe.

Oskar's voice, half-sentences, blood on his lips: *Why send you a letter naming the family? Think what the letter is for.*

To flush the loyalists out. To make a courier move. To make somebody come creeping to a shipyard office at midnight and put their hands on a machine that certain eyes were already watching.

Had she walked into it? Was there, right now, someone in the dark yard noting the time, noting the light of her matches flickering in the windowless room — no, the room had no windows; they couldn't see the matches — but the door, the groaning door, the unlocked door she had opened like an invitation—

Juniper closed her eyes and breathed once, slow, the way she'd taught herself on checkpoints: four counts in, six counts out. Panic was a luxury item. Panic was for people with fewer debts.

Think.

Who could have searched the cabinet? The regime had been in Kalshavn barely a week. Captain Reval had come ashore with his lists and his courteous manners eight days ago. Eight days was enough time for the searches she'd seen — the customs men going through the harbormaster's records, the officers photographing the yard from the breakwater, Liv reporting soldiers sketching the dry dock in a little notebook. It was enough time to recruit informants, too. Every occupied town grew its informants like mold, overnight, in the dark. A clerk here. A watchman there. Someone with a key ring, or a crowbar, or simply the run of a building everyone trusted.

Someone inside the yard's orbit. Someone the Halvards fed, perhaps. Someone whose face she might have smiled at across Maren's kitchen table.

The thought made her ill, and the illness made her angry, and the anger steadied her. Fine. Let them have searched. They hadn't taken the machine, which meant they either hadn't understood what it was or hadn't been looking for it specifically — and either way, every hour it sat here was an hour it sat in a house already marked for arrest. The letter named them. The letter she had carried. The letter she should burn, except that burning it might be exactly what the forger wanted too, evidence destroyed, trail ended.

Everything she did served someone. That was the discovery of the night, cold as harbor water. Move, and she confirmed the bait. Stay, and the family drowned in the net. There was no innocent action left, only choices about whom she betrayed and by how much.

She wrapped the typewriter in the oilcloth anyway.

Because whatever else was true, the dispatches inside it — if they were still inside it — were the republic's last living testimony, and she would not leave them lying in a cabinet that strangers had already opened. She would take them apart in the loft above the kitchen, where the floorboards were thick and Maren kept the good lamp with the dark green shade. Tomorrow. Tonight, first, the machine had to cross forty yards of open yard.

She stood, tucked the wrapped weight under her coat against her hip, and paused at the door of the type room with her hand on the latch, listening one last time.

And heard it. Faint, out past the kennels, past the gate: footsteps on the quay cobbles. Two sets, unhurried, the rhythm of men walking a beat rather than going anywhere. Patrol boots. And then, closer than the boots, from somewhere near the office's outer wall — the scratch and glow of a pipe being lit, a small red bloom visible through the gap under the door, there and gone.

Someone smoking in the yard at two in the morning. Standing close to the ship office. Close enough to hear a door groan on its hinges, if a door were to groan.

Juniper killed her match with wet fingers and stood in total darkness, the typewriter heavy against her ribs like a second heartbeat, and understood that she was no longer deciding whether to walk into the trap.

She was already inside it, and something on the other side of the wall knew she was there.

* * *

She waited until the pipe smoker moved off toward the harbor road, then longer, counting her own pulse down from a hundred twice. When she finally slipped out, she went not across the open yard but through the boathouse, along the rack of stacked dinghies, out the water door, and around by the sea wall where the tide slapped stone and covered any sound she made. The typewriter rode under her coat like contraband, which was precisely what it was. At the kitchen door she let herself in on memory alone, and stood in the blackness listening to the house breathe.

"You're bleeding the cold in," said a voice from the shadows by the stove. "Shut the door."

Juniper nearly dropped the machine. Then her eyes adjusted and found Liv, sixteen years old and wrapped in a blanket, sitting on the wood box with her knees drawn up, wide awake, entirely unsurprised.

"You're supposed to be asleep."

"So are you." Liv's whisper was matter-of-fact. "I heard you go out. I've been keeping watch. If anyone came to the house while you were gone, I was going to knock on the wall and wake Papa." She said it the way she reported everything — soldiers photographing the shipyard, boats at anchor, the price of herring — as if danger were simply weather to be noted in a logbook.

"Go to bed, Liv."

"In a minute." The girl's eyes dropped to the oilcloth bundle, to the corner of black enamel showing through, and Juniper watched understanding arrive in her face — quick, unchildlike, frightening in its clarity. "That's the office Underwood. The old one. Why would you—" Liv stopped herself. Something in Juniper's expression must have answered the question, because Liv's chin lifted slightly, the way Ilsa's did. "You don't have to tell me. But I'll carry it upstairs if it's heavy. My legs are younger than yours."

For a moment Juniper couldn't speak. Outside, far off toward the harbor gates, a whistle blew twice and stopped — the patrol changing, or signaling, or nothing at all.

"Liv," she said quietly. "Has anyone asked you about the ship office? Anyone at all? The new men, the ones at the customs house?"

The girl considered, honestly, head tilted. "They ask everyone things. That's their job, Mamma says." A pause. "But yesterday one of them bought coffee for Harald Voss, the harbormaster's clerk. Bought it for him special, and stood talking a long while, and afterward Harald wiped his hands on his coat like they were dirty." Liv shrugged. "Harald's always nervous, though. He drops things when my father's in the office. I only mentioned it because it was odd."

Odd. Juniper held the word the way she'd hold a live coal. A clerk, nervous, drinking the occupier's coffee, wiping his hands.

Upstairs, they set the typewriter on the loft floor together, and Liv went back down without asking anything further, pausing at the ladder only to say, "Whatever's in it — I'm glad it's in our house and not theirs."

Then Juniper was alone with the machine and the green-shaded lamp, and the knot in her chest pulled one turn tighter, because she now knew three things at once: the cabinet had been searched by unknown hands; a stranger smoked in the yard at midnight; and somewhere between the harbor office and the customs house, a frightened man named Harald Voss was learning what his fear could buy him.

She picked up her screwdriver and set its edge against the first screw of the typewriter's frame.

Outside, down by the water, the Meridian's riding lights burned steady over the black tide — one berth empty, dawn coming whether anyone was ready or not.
