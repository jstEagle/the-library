## 14. The Informant's Face

Captain Reval arrived in Kalshavn on the evening boat from Torvik, and he was disappointed by how little it had changed.

He had expected a city at war with itself — barricades, burned kiosks, the nervous choreography of a population that knew its masters had changed. Instead there was rain on the harbor, gulls arguing over fish heads, and a woman selling roasted chestnuts under an awning who looked up at him with the flat indifference of someone who had watched three governments come ashore and expected to watch three more. The red-and-gold of the Republic still hung, sodden and half-furled, above the customs house. Someone would take it down by morning. It was not his job to hurry them.

Reval was thirty-nine years old, thin in the way of men who forgot meals rather than denied themselves, and he wore his new regime's grey uniform as though it had been tailored for him personally, which it had. He carried one leather case. Everything else he needed — lists, orders, authority — fit inside his head, and his head was the best-organized room in Vessmark.

His orders were threefold. First: secure the passenger manifest of the *Meridian*, sailing on the morning tide, and assume control of it. Second: identify and detain the named conspirators before that ship left, because a traitor on open water was a traitor with a future, and Reval did not deal in futures he could not inspect. Third — written in the Adjutant-General's own hand, which meant it outranked everything — recover a set of ministry dispatches believed to have left Torvik in courier hands during the last days of the old government.

The dispatches were the point. The arrests were housekeeping.

Reval stood on the quay in the drizzle for a full ten minutes before going anywhere, because he always did this: he let a place look at him first. Cities told you things when they thought you were nobody. He watched the lamps come on along the mole, watched the *Meridian* riding her moorings with her loading lights burning yellow through the wet air, watched stevedores move crates up a gangway in a rhythm too steady for panic and too hurried for comfort. He counted the guards at the harbor gate — two, bored, one smoking — and noted the position of the customs office relative to the gangway, and the sight line from the dry dock crane to the water.

Then he picked up his case and went to find the harbormaster's clerk.

* * *

The clerk's name was Eemil Vaan, and he was exactly what Reval had been promised: a small man of forty-six with ink-stained fingers, a widow's stoop already forming between his shoulders, and the particular damp-eyed anxiety of someone whose competence had always been noticed by everyone except the people who promoted. He had served the harbormaster's office for nineteen years. He knew every berth fee, every tonnage exemption, every captain's drinking habits and every inspector's signature. He was, in the vocabulary of the new government, an asset. In the vocabulary of any government, ever, he was a man waiting to be frightened.

Reval did not frighten him immediately. That was amateur work. Fear applied too early made a man stupid; fear applied at the correct moment made him fluent.

He presented himself at the customs annex at nine o'clock, showed his credentials to the night porter, and asked to see the manifest procedures for civilian departures. He sat in the clerk's cramped office amid towers of ledgers smelling of mildew and pipe smoke, and for forty minutes he asked nothing but procedural questions, in a courteous, unhurried voice, taking notes in a small precise hand. How were berths allocated? By application, then seniority of booking, then purser's discretion. Who held final authority over the passenger list? Technically the purser; practically, since the emergency decrees, the harbormaster's office countersigned. And who, specifically, countersigned?

Vaan's pen hesitated over the ledger.

"I do," he said. "Since Herr Lindqvist took ill. He's been ill since — well." He did not finish. Since the coup, the unfinished sentence said. Since the shells came down on the Ministry quarter and the radio played marches instead of weather reports.

"Since Herr Lindqvist took ill," Reval agreed pleasantly, and wrote something down that meant nothing, and watched Vaan read the nothing anyway.

It was beautiful, really, how it worked. A man like Vaan spent nineteen years being invisible, and invisibility is a kind of safety — no one arrests the furniture. Then a man in a grey uniform sits down across the desk and begins writing, and the furniture discovers it has a name, a signature, a countersignature, a paper trail. Every stamp Vaan had ever pressed onto a departure document was suddenly a decision. He had signed the manifests of half the merchants now fleeing south. He had processed the Halvard shipping papers for twenty years. His handwriting was all over the republic's exit wounds.

"You've handled the *Meridian*'s documentation," Reval said. Not a question.

"The final manifest isn't compiled yet. Berths keep — changing. People buy out, people bribe in, people—" Vaan stopped himself, appalled at his own mouth.

"People bribe in," Reval repeated, without judgment, and turned to a fresh page. "Go on."

There was nothing so productive as letting a frightened man fill silence. Vaan filled it for six minutes: the bribes, the forged medical exemptions, the family that had sold their boat to buy four berths and been cheated into three, the purser's cousin who had appeared from nowhere holding a first-class ticket for a cabin that did not exist on the ship's plans. He spoke faster and faster, this mild man with his ink-stained fingers, denouncing in a low urgent voice every irregularity of his own office — and Reval understood, with the detached admiration he permitted himself on such occasions, that Vaan believed he was demonstrating loyalty. Look how honest I am. Look how I tell you where the bodies are buried, even though I dug some of the graves myself.

Fear manufactures traitors the way damp manufactures mold: not through wickedness, but through the simple availability of a surface.

When Vaan finally ran down, breathless, Reval closed his notebook.

"Herr Vaan. Do you have family?"

The question landed like a dropped glass. "A daughter. In Torvik. She lives with my sister, since my wife—" He stopped again. Everyone in Vessmark was stopping mid-sentence these days; the country had become a nation of unfinished clauses.

"In Torvik," Reval said. "Which is presently administered by the Provisional Authority. Movement between districts requires documentation. Documentation requires processing." He let that sit. "Processing can be slow. Or fast. Depending."

"Depending," Vaan whispered.

"On whether the people doing the processing know you." Reval stood, buttoned his coat, and became, briefly and deliberately, almost kind. It was important that the kindness be real — performed kindness curdles, but genuine kindness, extended from a position of total power, binds a man more tightly than any threat. "You have been very helpful. I will remember it. I am staying at the Grand Harbor Hotel until the situation stabilizes. If anything occurs to you — anything about the ships, the passengers, the families who frequent this harbor — anything that a diligent officer ought to know — you may send word there at any hour. No formality required. Just your name and the information."

"And if—"

"Yes?"

Vaan looked at his ledgers, at nineteen years of neat columns, at the whole paper architecture of his unremarkable life. "Nothing, Captain. Good evening."

Reval paused at the door. "One more thing. The Halvard shipyard. Their papers cross your desk, I imagine."

"They — yes. Always have."

"Their bookings on the *Meridian*. How many?"

Vaan's throat moved. "I couldn't say precisely. The list keeps changing."

"Find out precisely," Reval said, still pleasant, still at the door. "By tomorrow evening."

* * *

He walked back through the rain without hurrying, because hurrying suggested urgency and urgency suggested fear, and Reval had built a career on the observation that fear is a solvent — it dissolves other people long before it dissolves you. The chestnut seller had gone. The lamplighter was working the mole with a long pole, and the flames caught one by one down the black water, little obedient suns.

In his hotel room he laid out his materials the way other men laid out a card hand. The arrest list, thirty-one names, typed in Torvik on a machine he knew by its quirks — a broken *e* that printed high, a flaw he had learned to recognize the way a huntsman knows a particular stag by a chipped antler. The Adjutant-General's letter regarding the missing dispatches. And a single page of his own composition, headed with the date: *Kalshavn assets, preliminary.* One entry. *Vaan, E., harbormaster's clerk. Compliant. Motivated by daughter, Torvik. Estimate: reliable within 48 hours.*

He did not write *traitor*. The word was imprecise. Traitors betrayed principles, and Vaan had none to betray — only attachments. What Vaan would do, Reval reflected as he blotted the page, was what most people did when history put its hand on their shoulder: he would choose the smallest survival available and call it prudence. Nineteen years behind a counter taught a man that the world ran on signatures, and whoever held the stamp held the truth. Vaan would give him names the way a man pays blackmail — gratefully, almost, relieved at last to have the debt named.

The city itself was nearly as compliant. He had spent the afternoon confirming it: the police commissioner had surrendered his armory with a receipt, the newspaper had printed the Provisional Authority's first proclamation beside the shipping notices as though it were a tide table, and the bishop — the bishop had sent a note welcoming the restoration of order, a sentence Reval found so perfectly obscene he had copied it into his notebook for future use. Only the shipyard district had a texture he didn't like. Too many shuttered windows with faces behind them. Too many workers who had stopped answering roll call and taken to the refugee lines instead. And the Halvard yard still running its cranes, still hammering, as if the country's death were a delivery deadline like any other.

Halvard. The name appeared three times on the arrest list — the patriarch's brother-in-law, the eldest son, and the matriarch herself flagged with a marginal query, as if even Torvik wasn't sure whether you arrested grandmothers. Reval suspected the list was bait as much as a net. The Adjutant-General's letter had hinted as much: the dispatches would move toward whatever the loyalists loved most. You did not catch couriers with roadblocks. You caught them with grief.

He opened the window and listened to the harbor. Under the rain and the rigging creak there was another sound, fainter: a typewriter, somewhere across the water, working late in a shipyard office. Rat-tat-tat-tat, patient as rain itself. Someone typing invoices, or letters, or perhaps nothing at all — typing because the rhythm kept the fear out.

Reval smiled thinly at the dark water and made a note to have the sound located by morning.

Below, in the lobby, the night porter was already asleep at his desk, and on the desk lay the day's register, and on the register's newest line a guest had signed in an hour ago — a woman traveling alone, profession given as *typist*, papers clean enough to pass a tired porter's glance.

Reval would not see the register until breakfast. But he had built his life on the certainty that everything a city needed to tell him, it eventually wrote down.

He slept well. He always did.

* * *

Morning brought the manifest.

Vaan delivered it himself at eight sharp, hands slightly unsteady, eyes ringed grey from a sleepless night — Reval approved of the sleepless night; it was evidence of investment. The clerk had done more than fetch the list. He had annotated it. Little pencil ticks beside certain names, and beside the ticks, in his cramped clerk's hand: *paid cash, no references*; *ticket issued post-decree, suspicious*; and beside the Halvard entries — Anders Halvard, Maren Halvard, Ilsa Halvard, Liv Halvard, four berths confirmed against the yard's bonded account — a tick, and then, after a visible struggle visible in the smudge of eraser beneath it, nothing at all.

"These annotations are excellent work," Reval said warmly, and watched the warmth do its work in turn: Vaan flushing, straightening, becoming in real time a collaborator and knowing it and unable to stop, because the alternative was to snatch the pages back, and the pages were already in Reval's hands, and the daughter was in Torvik.

"There's — one thing more, Captain." Vaan lowered his voice as if the ledgers might inform on him. "Yesterday evening a message came to the office. No name. A boy runner brought it. It said only—" He swallowed. "*Tell the tall officer the machine remembers.*"

The rain ticked against the window. Somewhere out on the water a foghorn sounded, long and low, like the harbor clearing its throat.

"Did you bring me this," Reval said softly, "because you thought it mattered, or because you wanted me to know you brought it?"

Vaan stared at him, stricken, understanding for the first time that there was no longer any difference between the two.

Reval folded the manifest into his case, thanked him courteously, and stood looking out at the *Meridian* while the clerk retreated. The ship would sail on the morning tide. Before she did, he would hold her gangway, seal the harbor gates, and let pressure do what soldiers could not. Somewhere in this dripping, decent, tired city, a courier was carrying the truth of the coup in a coat pocket or a suitcase or a head, and every hour he waited, the fear he was so carefully farming in men like Eemil Vaan would grow another season.

*Machine remembers,* he thought. *Yes. They all do.*

He began drafting the order closing the harbor gates for the night.
