## 15. Bread and Questions

The smell woke Juniper before the light did — yeast and woodsmoke rising through the floorboards of the Halvard house like something alive, something patient. She lay a while in the narrow bed that had been hers for four nights now, listening to the sounds beneath her: the knock of a flour scoop against tin, the wet slap of dough being turned, Maren Halvard humming something tuneless and old.

Four nights. Long enough to stop counting the exits. That was the danger of this house, she thought. It made you forget you were a guest in a country that was dying.

She dressed in the dark, folded her coat over the chair with the diplomatic pouches' ghosts still sewn into its lining — she had cut them out the first night, buried them under the woodpile, but she could feel the places where they'd been, like scars on the cloth — and went down to the kitchen.

Maren was already there, sleeves rolled to the elbow, forearms dusted white to the wrist. She was sixty-one years old and she worked dough the way Anders worked timber: without hurry, without mercy, without a single wasted motion. A mountain of flour stood on the scarred pine table beside three empty basins. On the windowsill, forty small loaves already proved under cloth, pale and swollen as sleeping things.

"You're up," Maren said, not turning around. "Good. Wash your hands. Flour's in the bin."

"I can pay for my keep," Juniper said. "There's money."

"There's bread to make." Maren nodded at the basin. "That's the payment. Go on."

Juniper washed at the pump sink, the water cold enough to bite, and came to stand across the table from her. For a moment neither spoke. The stove ticked. Rain worked at the window in fine, businesslike threads, the same rain that had been falling since she'd come into Kalshavn, as if the whole harbor city were being slowly erased and rewritten.

"Forty loaves?" Juniper asked.

"Fifty-two if we're quick." Maren cut the risen dough with a knife she kept for nothing else, dividing it with an eye so practiced the portions came out equal to the gram. "The Meridian sails at dawn tomorrow. Half the families on those quays haven't eaten properly since the coup. You don't sail well hungry, and you don't wait well hungry either. Bread travels. Bread keeps. It's the one kindness you can put in a pocket."

She said it lightly, the way she seemed to say everything that mattered, and Juniper took her portion of dough and began to work it.

Her hands remembered. That was the strange thing — twelve years since she'd stood in this kitchen as a half-starved stowaway of nine, and her hands still knew the turn, the fold, the press of the heel, the quarter-turn. Muscle was loyal longer than memory was. Her mother had baked, before the purges took everything portable away from her; Maren had taught her these motions in another life, in this same room, saying *you knead like you mean it, little one, that's half the loaf*.

She kneaded like she meant it now, and watched Maren watch her, and saw the old woman notice what her hands were doing and say nothing about it, which was its own kind of conversation.

* * *

By seven the first batch was in the oven and the second batch was proving, and the kitchen had filled with the particular golden heat that makes speech easier or harder depending on what needs saying. Liv came down in bare feet with her hair unbrushed, stole the heel of yesterday's loaf, kissed her grandmother's floury cheek, and vanished out the door toward the docks with her message satchel banging against her hip — sixteen years old and faster than any telegraph boy in Vessmark, carrying other people's urgent news on legs that didn't know yet what urgency cost.

Anders passed through once, hat in hand, heading for the yard. He paused at the table, looked at Juniper's dough — she felt the assessment travel through her shoulders and arms like a hand — and gave a single nod. From him it might as well have been a speech. Then he was gone into the rain, and the door banged, and it was just the two of them again, women and bread and weather.

"The dock families," Juniper said, shaping a loaf, tucking the seam underneath. "Do they know it's from this house?"

"They know." Maren slid the tray into the oven and closed the iron door. "In Kalshavn everyone knows who bakes what. That used to be a comfort." She wiped her hands on her apron and looked out the fogged window at the grey water beyond the roofs. "My mother baked for the strike families in 'ninety-three. When the owners came to break the picket lines, they knew her loaves too. They let her keep the oven because taking it would have been worse than leaving it. There's a kind of armor in being known."

Juniper turned her dough. Turned it again.

"And you," Maren said, gently, in the tone of a woman remarking on the weather. "You came here with a letter to deliver and no return address. You flinch when the gate latch lifts. You sleep facing the door — I know because I did it for thirty years after your grandfather drowned. And you knead like someone I taught."

The oven ticked. Somewhere outside a gull argued with the rain.

"Maren—"

"I'm not asking." She held up a flour-white hand, palm out, and Juniper stopped. "I want you to hear that clearly, because I've noticed you brace whenever anyone comes near a question. I'm not asking. But I'll tell you what I see, and then you can do what you like with it."

Juniper kept her eyes on the dough. It was easier than keeping them on the woman's face, which held the terrible patience of someone who had raised sons through two wars and a daughter-in-law through a funeral and a granddaughter through every fever of childhood.

"You work for somebody," Maren said. "Not the new men — you'd have come with soldiers and a smile. Somebody older. Somebody whose paper is worth less every day, and you know it, and you keep working anyway because you promised." She picked up the knife and began scoring the next batch of loaves, three swift strokes each, sure as breathing. "Your mother worked for somebody too. Did you know that? Not the way you do. Smaller. She moved people, not papers. One family at a time, one boat at a time. She never told me names and I never told her mine, and between us we got my brother onto a ship in 1889 with a false manifest and a true prayer."

"You never told me that," Juniper said quietly. "When I was a girl here. Any of it."

"No. Because you were nine years old and frightened, and a child carrying secrets is a child carrying stones." Maren set down the knife. "You're not nine anymore. But I look at you and I see the same weight settling lower on your back, year by year, stone by stone. So I'll say my piece and then we'll get on with the loaves."

She came around the table and stood close, close enough that Juniper could smell the yeast and the lavender soap beneath it, could see the fine map of lines around her eyes.

"You don't have to tell me," Maren said. "You just have to eat."

Something in Juniper's chest went loose all at once, like a knot she hadn't known she'd been tying since the border. She had crossed checkpoints with forged papers sewn against her ribs. She had walked past the torn flag and the new black banners and the soldiers checking papers with gloved hands. She had read a sealed letter naming these people traitors and had not run, had not warned them outright, had carried the secret like a coal in her mouth for days. She had lied to Oskar. She had hidden film inside her own clothing. She had been so competent, so contained, so *professional*, that she had begun to mistake herself for the job.

And this woman, who had every reason to demand answers, offered instead a bowl of soup and a place at the table and the radical suggestion that love might precede explanation rather than require it.

"Maren," Juniper began, and found her voice had gone rough. "If you knew what I—"

"I know you helped raise that dough," Maren said, turning back to the table, brisk again, mercifully brisk. "And that you'll carry baskets to the quay with me when these are out, and that you eat standing up like a cat, and that whoever you serve, you serve honestly, or my kitchen would know it. Kitchens know things." She handed Juniper a basket lined with cloth. "Fill that. Twelve loaves. The Sorensens' shed by the fish market — ask for Petra, she has five children and a husband on the *Meridian*'s crew list."

* * *

They walked the docks together through the late-morning drizzle, Maren with a basket on each arm and Juniper matching her step, and the harbor opened itself around them like a wound learning to be a view.

The *Meridian* rode low at her berth, black-hulled and patient, her decks stacked with trunks and birdcages and a grand piano lashed under canvas that nobody aboard would ever learn to play. Refugees camped along the seawall in rows of tarps and upturned boats — families who had sold houses for passage and now waited out their last night in a city that had stopped being theirs. Juniper saw a man teaching his daughter to whistle; saw an old couple sharing a single bench like it was a pew; saw a woman wash her children's faces with rainwater caught in a barrel, working fast before the morning could make them dirty again.

Maren stopped at every cluster. She knew names. She knew whose cough was bad, whose husband had been taken in the first week of the coup, whose daughter was marrying in Gothenburg and would meet them there. She pressed loaves into hands and refused coins with a shake of the head so final that no one insisted twice. Juniper followed with her own basket and said little, and watched how the dock people watched Maren — not with gratitude exactly, gratitude is a transaction — but with recognition. *There goes the house that built our ships. There goes the kitchen that feeds us while we leave.*

At the Sorensens' shed, Petra Sorensen took the loaves with both hands and held them a moment before setting them down, as if confirming they were real.

"Tell your mother—" she started, then corrected herself, embarrassed, glancing at Juniper. "Tell Mrs. Halvard we'll repay it. When we're settled. When there's anything to repay with."

"She'll say the same thing she always says," Juniper heard herself reply. "'You don't owe me. You owe the next person.'"

Petra smiled, tired and cracked, and something passed over her face — Juniper couldn't name it, some small tightening — and she said, "Aye. That's her," and closed the shed door softly, as though the children were sleeping even in daylight.

They walked on. The rain thickened, then thinned. Near the customs house, Juniper noted without meaning to — courier's habit, courier's curse — that two men in plain coats stood under the arcade, not talking, watching the gangway of the *Meridian*. One of them held a leather folio against his chest the way a priest holds scripture. Neither smoked. Men waiting to be paid don't smoke; men paid to wait do.

Maren followed her glance, then deliberately didn't follow it.

"Baskets are lighter," she said. "Let's go home the long way. Past the yard."

* * *

The Halvard shipyard stood silent behind its gates, and the silence was wrong. Juniper had seen it working on her walk the day before — the ring of adzes, the steam from the bending shed, twenty men where forty should have been. Today the great doors stood open on emptiness. Tools lay where they'd been set down. A half-planked hull sat in its cradle like a patient abandoned mid-operation, rain pooling in the open seams.

"Half gone to the ships," Maren said, reading her look. "Half gone inland. Two arrested last week for nothing anyone could name." She shifted her baskets. "Anders won't say it, but he knows the yard won't outlive the month. A shipyard isn't wood and iron, girl. It's hands that know the wood and iron. Hands walk. Wood stays. That's all a ruin ever is."

They passed the office window, and Juniper slowed without letting herself slow — a smooth deceleration disguised as adjusting a basket strap — and in the grey glass she caught the reflection of the room behind: the desk, the plans, the tall cabinet against the far wall where the surplus ministry typewriter sat under lock. Someone had left the cabinet door ajar. Or someone had left it *ajar-looking*. In a house this careful, nothing was accidental except by design.

She filed it. She kept walking.

"You saw it," Maren said. It wasn't a question.

"The cabinet?"

"Mm." Maren said nothing more for ten steps. Then: "Liv mentioned soldiers drawing the yard last week. Sketching it, she thought. With little black boxes on tripods." She glanced sideways at Juniper, mild as milk. "Cameras, I believe they're called. My son says the new government loves cameras. Loves lists more."

The rain came down between them, fine as sieve-work.

"Maren," Juniper said, "if someone came to this house — someone official, someone with papers — what would you do?"

"What I always do." The old woman smiled faintly. "Put the kettle on. Offer coffee. People are gentler holding something warm." She started toward the house, then stopped, and for a moment all the mildness fell away and Juniper saw the woman who had faced down mill owners and sea captains and the sea itself. "Whatever you're carrying, courier — whatever name it has, whoever wrote it, however heavy — you should know that this family has survived purges by staying exactly what we are. We build ships so people can leave. We bake bread so they don't leave hungry. We do not choose who leaves, and we do not choose who knocks." She resumed walking. "Even couriers. Even ones with stones in their pockets."

Juniper stood a moment in the rain, watching her go, feeling the microfilm press flat and secret against her ribs like a second heartbeat.

* * *

The afternoon went to ovens and trays and the steady arithmetic of fifty-two loaves becoming zero. By dusk the kitchen smelled like the idea of safety itself, and neighbors drifted through to collect theirs — dockers, widows, the lamplighter, a customs clerk Juniper marked and unmarked and marked again — until the cloth-lined baskets hung empty on their pegs and Maren finally sat down at her own table for the first time all day.

She poured two glasses of the sour cherry cordial she kept for occasions nobody announced, and pushed one across to Juniper.

"Eat," she said, meaning drink, meaning rest, meaning *stay*.

Juniper drank. The cordial was sharp and dark and tasted like summers that had happened to other people.

Outside, the light failed over the harbor, and the lamps of the *Meridian* came up one by one along her hull, a ship lighting herself like a lantern for her own burning. Tomorrow at dawn she would take three hundred people out past the point and into the open water and away. Juniper counted berths the way she counted everything — automatically, hopelessly. She had heard the purser's number through a steward's gossip that afternoon: after bribes and cancellations and the harbormaster's "fees," exactly one berth remained unclaimed. One. In a family of six, plus a courier who officially didn't exist.

"You're doing sums," Maren said, watching her over the rim of her glass. "Stop. Sums at this table after dark never come out right."

"I wasn't—"

"You were. You had the face Anders gets." Maren rose, gathered the glasses, and paused with her hand briefly, briefly, on Juniper's shoulder — a touch weighing perhaps an ounce and lasting perhaps a second, and heavier than everything Juniper carried sewn against her body. "Whatever the sums say, remember the loaves. Nobody in this house has ever done the arithmetic alone, and nobody ever will."

She went out to bank the stove. Juniper stayed at the table, listening to the coals settle, and it was then that the knock came — soft, twice, apologetic — and Anders opened the door to a drenched and shaking figure in a harbormaster's clerk coat, a man Juniper recognized from the quay, who stepped inside dripping and would not sit down and would only say, over and over, gripping his cap:

"I couldn't sleep with it. I couldn't. You have to understand — the list changed tonight. They moved it forward. The arrests come at first light — *before* the ship sails — and your names are on it, all of yours, and I gave him names, God forgive me, I gave him names, but this — this I couldn't give him—"

The stove ticked. The rain worked at the glass. And somewhere across the sleeping harbor, in a lit office above the customs house, Captain Reval was sharpening the morning into order.
