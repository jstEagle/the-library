## 16. The Clerk Confesses

The rain had stopped by the time Anders Halvard locked the tool crib, but the yard still dripped from every rafter and gantry, a hundred small clocks ticking down the evening. He stood on the office steps with a lantern in one hand and counted the dark shapes of his dry dock the way other men counted sheep — not for comfort, but because counting was all that was left to do.

Eleven men had left him since the coup. Three more had asked for their papers that afternoon, apologetic, hat in hand, saying what everyone said now: *I have family in the interior, my brother-in-law works for the new administration.* Nobody said *I am afraid of you* out loud, but Anders heard it anyway, under every apology, the way a hull hears water.

The Meridian's masts stood above the warehouse roofs to the east, black against a sky the color of wet slate. Two days to dawn tide. Two days, and after that whatever came.

He was about to go inside when he heard the gate hinge.

It was a sound he knew better than his own heartbeat — the iron gate at the yard's landward corner, which sang a particular flat note when it moved without oil, and it had needed oil since before his father died, and Anders had never once oiled it, because a man who cannot afford to guard his property ought at least to be able to hear it being taken. The note sounded now, softly, and then there were footsteps on the gravel, quick ones, uncertain, stopping, starting again.

Anders set down the lantern and picked up the long caulking mallet that lived behind the office door, and went out into the dripping dark.

"Who's there?"

The footsteps froze. Then, low, pitched to carry no farther than ten paces: "Master Halvard? Please. Don't shout. Don't light anything."

The voice was young and it cracked on the second word. Anders knew it. He spent half his life at the harbormaster's office fighting over tonnage fees and berth assignments, and he knew every clerk's voice the way a doctor knows coughs. This was Vinter — Petter Vinter, the youngest of them, the one who always wore his collar buttoned to the throat even in August, the one who once, two years ago, had quietly corrected a fee entry in the Halvards' favor and refused to explain why.

"The arrest list," Vinter said, when Anders didn't answer. His breath was coming fast. Somewhere across the harbor a dog began to bark and both men flinched. "Tomorrow morning. Not the day after. Tomorrow. Captain Reval signed it this afternoon — I saw the page myself, I was holding it, I was carrying it to the telegraph room like a good little—" He stopped. Swallowed. Started again, lower. "Your name is on it. All your names. Your mother, your grandmother, your sister. And the girl."

*The girl.* Anders felt the word go through him like a bolt driven home. Juniper. They had her name already, then, or enough of one. He made himself stay still. Made himself keep breathing in the slow rhythm of a man who has spent thirty years walking planks over open water, where panic is simply another way of falling.

"Why are you telling me this?" he said. Not unkindly. But plainly. He wanted the truth plain; he'd have no use for any other kind by morning.

A long silence. Rain dripped off the dock crane. When Vinter spoke again his voice had gone strange, thin, as if he were reciting something learned.

"I typed the list. That's my work now — I copy the pages, I stamp them, I file them. There are forty-one names, Master Halvard. Forty-one families, and I know some of them. The Raskens. Old Lindqvist who sells rope. The widow Aune." His breath caught. "I sat at my desk tonight and I stamped forty-one names and then I couldn't remember eating dinner, I couldn't remember whether I'd eaten dinner, and I realized I never would again unless—"

He didn't finish. Anders waited. Out on the water, far off, a ship's bell rang the hour — nine o'clock, soft across the black harbor, indifferent as God.

"They gave me a paper," Vinter said at last. "For informing. Reval's office gives them to clerks — you report names, you get passage on a neutral ship, first priority, they call it *cooperation credit*. My mother is sick, Master Halvard. Her lungs. The doctors say she needs a drier climate and we haven't the money and here is a man offering me a berth on a ship for the price of writing down what I know." A short, awful laugh. "Do you understand what I'm telling you? It isn't hard. It's the easiest thing I've ever done. That's what frightens me. I could do it with my eyes shut."

"But you're here instead."

"I wrote three names on the cooperation form tonight." The words came out flat, rehearsed, as if he had carried them around all day and they had worn grooves. "Names of men I hate. Men who cheated me, who laughed at me. Dead men, men already fled, it doesn't matter — Reval wants quantity, he doesn't check." Another pause, longer. "And then I looked at the arrest list in my own hand and I thought: tomorrow someone types these same names onto a form like mine. Someone's hand. Someone's dinner they won't remember."

He shifted in the gravel. Anders could just make him out now, a narrow pale shape near the gate, coat collar up, a man built entirely of buttons and guilt.

"You should leave the city tonight," Vinter said. "All of you. Before the six o'clock shift change, before the gates open — Reval seals them at seven, I heard the order read aloud. After seven, nothing moves in this harbor without his signature. Nothing."

"And if I ask you to say none of this happened?"

"Then say it. I'll say it too." A pause. "But Master Halvard — I've seen the manifest orders. Reval has control of the Meridian's passenger list. Whatever berth arrangements you've made, whatever you've paid — he can strike any name he wants, for any reason, and the purser will thank him for the clarification. Leaving tonight isn't safety. It's only better odds than staying."

Then he turned and was gone through the gate, quick and light, and the hinge did not sing this time, because he lifted it as he passed, carefully, the way a man lifts a sleeping child, and Anders stood alone in the dripping yard with a caulking mallet in his hand and nine hours between him and the end of everything he knew how to build.

* * *

The kitchen was warm and smelled of yeast and lamp oil, and for one whole minute Anders stood in the doorway watching it and could not make himself speak.

Maren at the table with her ledger and her spectacles, doing the accounts she kept even now, even though money was becoming an idea rather than a substance. Grandmother Ilsa propped in her chair by the stove with her shawl and her sharp chin, listening to Liv read aloud from a shipping gazette — the old woman liked hearing about ships she'd never sail on, she said, it kept her honest about the world. Juniper sat at the end nearest the door, as she always sat, mending a seam in her coat with small precise stitches, and when Anders came in her eyes came up first, before anyone else's, the way they always did.

She knows something's wrong before I open my mouth, he thought. She reads rooms the way I read weather.

"We need the door closed," he said, "and I need to talk fast."

Liv put down the gazette. Maren took off her spectacles slowly, folding them, setting them down — her way of preparing herself, the ritual she performed before bad news the way a surgeon lays out instruments.

Anders told it straight, because there was no kind way and no time to find one: the name on the list, the hour of the arrests, the sealed gates, the manifest. He watched each face take its blow separately. Maren went very still, hands flat on the table. Liv's face went white and then stubborn, the two colors she wore for fear. Ilsa did not move at all, only her eyes, which went bright and hard as rivets.

Juniper's stitching had stopped. Her needle hung in midair.

"Six o'clock," she said quietly. "Gates seal at seven."

"That's what he said."

"Then we have until seven to become invisible." She said it the way she seemed to say everything — level, practical, as if she were reading a freight manifest. But her fingers had gone white around the needle, and Anders, who had spent his life judging wood by how it behaved under stress, saw that she was bent close to breaking and bearing it the way good timber does: silently, visibly, only to someone looking.

"Nobody becomes invisible," Ilsa announced, into the pause. "We're Halvards. We've been visible in this harbor since before the harbor had customs officers. What we can do is be somewhere else when the knocking starts." She fixed Anders with her stare. "Well? Say the plan, boy. You have a plan forming behind your face, I can see it pushing."

"It's not a plan. It's arithmetic." Anders pulled out a chair and sat, heavily, because his knees had opinions. "There's nowhere to run to tonight. Overland is four days to the border with soldiers on every road and grandmother can't walk to the end of the street. The fishing boats won't take passengers past the blockade line — they get boarded, everyone gets shot as smugglers. The Meridian sails at dawn tide and she is the only thing leaving this harbor with legal papers."

"Which Reval holds by the throat," Juniper said.

"Which Reval holds by the throat. So." He spread his big hands on the table, carpenter's hands, honest about their limits. "So we split. Mother takes Ilsa to the Sørensens' cellar tonight — nobody searches a cooper's cellar on day one, they start with the obvious houses, ours. Liv goes with them. I stay here and meet them at the door when they come, because a house with the master absent looks guilty, and a house with the master calmly drinking coffee looks like a mistake on somebody's list. And at dawn tide we present ourselves at the gangway with our papers in order and our faces bored, and we bet everything on the fact that lists are written by clerks, and clerks make errors."

"No," said Ilsa.

Everyone turned. The old woman had levered herself forward in her chair, and there was something in her face that Anders had seen perhaps five times in his life, usually just before she won an argument against his father that his father hadn't known had started.

"I buried a husband after the 'ninety purge and a son after the one before that, and I will tell you what I have learned, since apparently I'm the only one in this room old enough to have learned it." Her voice was dry as oakum. "Families survive scatterings. Families die of bargains. Every time we have tried to save ourselves by dividing — hiding the old ones, splitting the children off, sending one ahead as payment — the regime comes back for the pieces. They count us, boy. They are very good at counting. You hide three and they find three and then they add the hiding to the charges." She thumped the arm of her chair. "We stay together until the tide, and we walk up that gangway together, and if there aren't berths enough then we stand on the quay together and let them see our faces while they do whatever they've come to do. Together. That is the whole of my wisdom. Take it or waste the night arguing."

Silence. The stove ticked. Liv had reached across and taken her great-grandmother's hand without seeming to notice she'd done it.

"There may not be berths at all," Maren said quietly. "If Reval strikes names from the manifest—"

"Then we'll know by standing there," said Ilsa, "instead of by guessing in a cellar."

Anders looked at his mother. Maren looked back at him for a long moment, and then at Ilsa, and something passed between the two women that he could see but not read — some old treaty, some division of authority older than him.

"Together until the tide," Maren said. "But Anders keeps his coffee calm and answers the door. That much we do smartly."

* * *

Later, when the kitchen had emptied and the lamps were trimmed low, Anders found Juniper in the hall, pulling her coat back on. She had her bag over her shoulder. The sight of it went through him like cold water.

"You're leaving," he said.

"For a few hours. There's a man I have to see before morning." She adjusted the strap with those quick typist's fingers and didn't look at him. "Oskar. My handler. If arrests are moving up, he needs to know, and if he's compromised I need to know that too."

"Vinter said they have your name."

"He said a name." She met his eyes then, and he saw how tired she was underneath the steadiness, tired in a way that had years of practice. "Names are cheap in a coup, Anders. Half the list is probably guesswork. But I'm not going to bet your family on probably."

"Our family," Maren said from the kitchen doorway, mildly, as if correcting a figure in a ledger, and then turned back inside before either of them could answer it.

Juniper stood very still. Anders watched her absorb it — watched it land somewhere deep and stay there, the way a nail sinks below the surface and leaves only a small dimple in the grain.

"Be back before the gates seal," he said. "Six o'clock. I don't care what Oskar tells you, I don't care what the truth is worth — you come back through that gate by six or I come looking, and I'm a shipwright, Miss Eriksen, I always find the crack in the hull."

Something almost like a smile crossed her face and was gone. "Five-thirty," she said. "I'll give you half an hour's margin. Shipwrights always overbuild."

She let herself out. Through the window Anders watched her cross the dark yard, small and quick, pausing once at the gate to look back at the house — one breath, maybe two — and then she lifted the hinge so it wouldn't sing, and was gone into the wet streets of Kalshavn.

He banked the stove, checked the lock on the office where the ship ledgers slept, and stood a last moment in the yard under the dripping cranes, listening to the harbor breathe. Out beyond the breakwater the blockade ships rode at anchor with their riding lights burning, patient as hooks. Somewhere in the city a telegraph key was chattering his family's names to men he'd never met.

Tomorrow, then. Six o'clock, and the arithmetic of one night.

He was turning to go in when he saw the paper.

It lay on the gravel by the office steps, weighted with a bolt-head against the wind — a single folded sheet, placed while he'd stood talking in the hall, placed by someone who had been inside his yard within the last quarter hour. He snatched it up and struck a match and read it twice in the trembling circle of light.

Not a threat. An inventory, in a careful clerk's hand: *They searched your office Tuesday. They searched it again today while the workers ate. They are looking for a machine. Tell whoever hid it — Reval does not want the machine. He wants the hands that hid it.*

The match burned down to his fingers. In the east, above the warehouses, the Meridian's masts stood black against the coming dawn, and Anders understood, with the cold clarity of a man reading water before a storm, that whatever was hidden in this yard was worth more than all of them alive.
