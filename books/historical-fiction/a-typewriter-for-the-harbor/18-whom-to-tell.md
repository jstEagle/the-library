## 18. Whom to Tell

The film was the size of a thumbnail and weighed nothing at all, which was how Juniper had learned to measure everything that mattered.

She carried it out of the ship office that night in the hollow of her cheek — not, she understood immediately, a sensible place, but the first place her body had offered, the way a frightened animal offers its mouth before it thinks. She held it there while she said goodnight to Liv on the stairs, while she nodded to Maren through the kitchen doorway, while she crossed the yard where sawdust lay grey under the moon like a fall of dirty snow. Only when she reached the lane did she move it, pressing it flat between two fingers and sliding it into the lining of her left boot, beneath the arch, where she had once carried a key for a man whose name she had never learned.

Her heart was behaving badly. She made it stop.

*Never open what you carry.* The rule had been drilled into her across seven years and four borders, and she had broken it once already this week, steaming open Oskar's sealed letter in a rented room with a kettle hissing on the stove. But breaking a seal was one sin; what she had done tonight was another order of transgression entirely. She had taken apart a machine that belonged to another family, in their own house, by lamplight, and she had found inside it the true dispatches of a murdered government — and now those dispatches were riding against her instep while she walked through a city under coup, thinking about whom to betray.

Because that was the question. There was no version of the night ahead in which she did not betray someone.

She turned onto the harbor road and stopped.

The Meridian rode at anchor beyond the mole, her running lights burning low, loading finished for the dawn tide. One berth. The purser's clerk had confirmed it again that afternoon, apologetic, as though the arithmetic were somehow his fault: cancellations, bribes paid and withdrawn, a manifest Reval's men had already photographed twice. One berth, and a family of six who would each rather drown than say so aloud, and somewhere in Juniper's boot a strip of celluloid that could buy passage for all of them — or hang them.

Oskar had sent her to deliver paper. Paper, sealed, to be handed over and forgotten. He had not sent her to find this. And yet he must have known the typewriter existed; the surplus sale had gone through the ministry's own registry; the serial number had been his to trace. Which meant either Oskar had lied to her about the nature of her errand —

— or someone had lied to Oskar.

That thought arrived quietly, the way ice forms on rigging: no sound at all, just weight accumulating where there had been none. She stood in the dark of the harbor road and let it finish forming.

A dying clerk in a borrowed room above a chandlery, coughing blood into a handkerchief he washed and rewashed because laundry cost money. A man with half a lung and no family and a government that had stopped existing around him sometime last Tuesday. Who watched him? Who brought his medicine? Who had told him, precisely, which shipyard family to send her to — the one family in Kalshavn whose house contained the ministry's lost machine?

Juniper began walking again, faster now, her breath making small clouds that tore away behind her.

It didn't have to mean anything. Oskar had been careless, perhaps. Or the registry had been read by other eyes long ago, and Oskar had simply chosen the only courier he trusted without knowing he was choosing from a list someone else had written. Coincidence existed. She had built a career on the assumption that coincidence existed, because the alternative was a world in which every errand was a hook.

But Captain Reval did not strike her as a man who relied on coincidence. Reval struck her as a man who *manufactured* it.

She looked back once at the black water. Then she turned away from the chandlery district, away from Oskar's room, and walked instead toward the boarding house on Kirkegaten where she had taken a bed under the name of a dead schoolteacher's daughter.

Not yet. Not tonight. Tonight she would hide it properly. Tomorrow — tomorrow she would look at his face when she lied to him, and see if dying men still knew how to lie.

* * *

The boarding house kept its gas lit until eleven, after which the landlady doused the hall lamp and the whole building subsided into the creaking quiet of strangers pretending to sleep. Juniper climbed to her room in the dark by memory, counted the doors as she passed them the way she always did — three occupied, one empty since Thursday, the couple in number nine who argued softly in a language she couldn't place — and locked herself in.

She did not light the lamp.

There were rules even here, in a city where nobody knew her face. You did not handle contraband by lamplight in a windowed room. You did not touch it longer than necessary. You did not, above all, grow attached to it, because attachment made men stupid, and stupid couriers were the ones who ended up in photographs on intelligence officers' desks, smiling faintly at the camera because they hadn't known they should look away.

She knelt at the foot of the bed and worked loose the floorboard she had marked her first night: third from the wall, the nail already half-drawn by some previous tenant with similar habits. Beneath it the joist ran in a channel wide enough for a hand. She had prepared this space with nothing in mind — an old instinct, filling an empty drawer before knowing what would go in it — and now she took from her bag the things she'd gathered without admitting why: a scrap of oilcloth cut from the hem of a rain cape; a twist of waxed thread; a stub of tallow candle, unlit, for the smell. Grease confused dogs. It was a small thing. Dogs were a small thing, next to soldiers.

She peeled the film from her boot lining with fingers that wanted to shake and did not permit it. In the darkness it was invisible; even held to the window's grey square of sky it showed only as a strip of dull amber with markings too fine to read. That was the obscenity of it, she thought. All that weight — ministries, money, the names of men abroad who had financed a coup with signatures and bearer bonds — compressed into something lighter than a stamp. History had gotten so small. When Ilsa Halvard spoke of the purges of her girlhood, the evidence had been ledgers and witnesses, things you could burn in a barrel in the square while the whole city watched. Now the truth of a nation fit in a boot heel, and its destruction required nothing more theatrical than a match.

She wrapped it in the oilcloth, tied the thread twice, greased the knot, and lowered it into the joist channel. Then she sat back on her heels in the dark and did not replace the board.

This was the part no training covered. Her instructors had taught her caches and ciphers, cover stories and border timing, how to walk through a checkpoint with your pulse slowed to a walk's pace. They had never taught her what to do when the thing you carried stopped being cargo and started being a decision. *Never open what you carry.* Fine. But she had opened it. She had opened the letter and then the machine and then the whole rotten architecture of the mission, and now she stood — knelt — inside the opening, and there was no rule for the view.

Option one: give it to Oskar. Complete the mission in spirit if not in letter. Oskar would know what to do with it — or would he? A dying man in a hostile port, with no embassy left to receive him, no cipher room, no safe. If Reval's people were watching him, handing over the film would be delivering it gift-wrapped. And if they weren't watching him, then the film died with him within weeks, coughed into oblivion, and the coup's financiers went on unpunished in Stockholm and Hamburg and wherever else money kept warm.

Option two: destroy it. Oskar might yet ask for exactly that. Burn it, sink it, and let the truth stay buried with the government that produced it. Clean. Almost merciful. Except that the film wasn't only proof anymore — she had understood that the moment the typebars clinked open under her screwdriver. It was leverage. It was the single object in Kalshavn that Reval wanted more than any arrest, and a man who wants something can be bargained with. Six Halvards. One berth. One film. She hated herself for doing the arithmetic and did it anyway, a second time, to make sure the numbers came out the same. They did. The numbers always came out the same. That was what was wrong with numbers.

Option three: use it herself. Buy the berth. Walk away. Let the family scatter as families scatter, as Ilsa said they always had, and carry the truth out through the blockade alone, and someday, in some neutral capital, type it all up and mail it to newspapers that would print a tenth of it.

She put her forehead against the cold floorboard and breathed.

Somewhere below, the landlady's cat moved through the hall, and a door settled in its frame, and the ordinary sounds of a house full of sleeping liars went on. Juniper replaced the board, pressed the carpet down over it with her palm, and lay on the bed fully clothed with her boots beside her, toes pointing at the door, as she had slept in a hundred rooms across four borders.

She slept maybe two hours. In the dream she was twelve years old again, wedged in the rope locker of a Halvard coaster, listening to grown men argue above her about whether to turn her in at Kalshavn — and in the dream, as in life, it was the youngest voice at the table, Anders, nineteen and embarrassed by his own decency, who said: *we don't choose who we shelter, we just shelter them.* In the dream the rope smelled of tar and salt and she had never been afraid in her life.

She woke with wet eyes and lay still until they dried.

* * *

Oskar received her at ten the next morning, propped in the narrow bed like a monument to some defeated cause. The room above the chandlery smelled of camphor and ink; the medicine bottle on the sill had been refilled — she noted it automatically, noting was what she did — by someone. By whom? The pharmacist on Strandgaten, presumably. Presumably.

"You look," Oskar said, "like weather coming in."

"I'm always weather coming in." She set her satchel on the chair and sat, arranging herself in the visitor's posture she'd rehearsed on the walk over: tired but steady, a courier reporting completion, nothing hidden in her face. "I delivered the letter."

"Did you." His eyes were bright the way fever makes eyes bright, and fixed on her with an attention that his ruined body couldn't match. "And?"

"And it was received."

He smiled. It cost him something; she watched the muscles around his mouth pay out the effort like line off a spool. "You know, Eriksen, in thirty years of the foreign service I have never once heard you say anything useful. It is your single greatest quality."

"The letter was delivered," she said. "Sealed. To the address you gave me."

"Mm." He coughed, a long tearing sound, and waved off her half-rise toward the water jug. When he could speak again his voice had dropped into its lower register, the one that came out when the blood was near. "You opened it."

The room went very still. Somewhere below, a bell buoy clanked in the swell.

"No," Juniper said.

"Juniper." He said her name gently, the way Maren said it, and she felt the floor tilt under the resemblance. "I taught you the kettle trick myself, in Riga, winter of '08. You think I don't know my own student? Your hands. You hold them too carefully when you've handled something you shouldn't have. Like a pianist who's touched a hot iron." He closed his eyes briefly. "Did the name surprise you?"

Every instinct she owned screamed at her, in perfect unison, to tell him the truth. That was the horror of Oskar — that he had trained her so well toward honesty-within-loyalty that lying to him felt less like tradecraft and more like sin. And underneath the scream, quieter, colder, the other voice: *who refilled the medicine?*

"It surprised me," she said, which was true, and gave him nothing else.

He studied her for a long moment. Steam of his breath in the cold room; October pressing at the glass. "Good," he said finally. "Surprise means the seal was intact when it mattered. If you'd known beforehand, you'd have hesitated at the door, and hesitation is visible from the street." He turned his head toward the window, toward the harbor he could no longer walk to. "They're good people, the Halvards. Better than the state ever was. Perhaps that's why—" He stopped. The sentence lay there unfinished, and Juniper picked it up and turned it over in her mind and found it had too many possible endings.

"Why what?" she asked.

"Nothing. Old men editorialize." He fumbled at the blanket and she helped him, lifting his shoulders so he could sit higher, feeling through the nightshirt the terrible geography of his ribs. Up close his skin had gone the color of the paper in his dispatch cases. "Listen to me. There may come a time — soon — when someone asks you what happened to the letter. Regime people. Reval's people. They'll offer you things. Passage. Money. Other people's lives, wrapped up as gifts." His grip on her wrist tightened with sudden shocking strength, the strength of the nearly dead. "Whatever you traded, whatever you promised — you tell me first. Do you understand? Whatever happens in this city, it comes through me before it goes anywhere else. That is the last chain of command in Vessmark, Juniper. Me, and you. That's the whole army."

Something in her chest cracked along a hairline.

"Yes," she said. "I understand."

"Say it back."

"Anything that happens comes to you first."

"Good girl." He sank back against the pillows, spent, and for a moment his eyes lost their focus and wandered the ceiling as though reading something written there years ago. Then, lightly, terribly lightly: "The machine, of course, is another matter."

Juniper did not move. She had trained for this moment her entire adult life — the moment when the conversation turns a corner you didn't see and comes back holding a knife — and she used all of it. She let three seconds pass. She frowned, slightly. "Machine?"

"The typewriter." His eyes came back to her, and they were clear, and they were kind, and they were watching. "Adler model, ministry property, sold off in '04 in the great inventory scandal. It typed the original dispatches. The ones that matter." A thin smile. "I assumed you'd want to know. For completeness."

"For completeness," she repeated.

"You haven't seen it?"

"I've seen the ship office," she said. "Desks, charts, a stove that smokes. I didn't inventory their furniture, Oskar. I delivered a letter."

"Mm," he said again. And then, closing his eyes, settling, his voice going soft and far away: "If you happen across it, you'll bring it to me. Won't you. Before anyone else. Whatever else happens."

The bell buoy clanked. Down in the street a soldier's boots rang on cobbles, unhurried, a man walking a beat he owned.

"Before anyone else," Juniper said.

She stayed another twenty minutes. She poured his medicine, straightened his blanket, listened to a story about the Vienna posting in 1899 that she had heard twice before and laughed in the right places. At the door he caught her hand.

"Eriksen." His voice had dropped to almost nothing. "Whom else have you told? About being here. About the mission. Anyone."

"No one," she said.

"That's my girl." He smiled, and his teeth were very yellow, and his eyes were very clear. "Trust no one. It's the whole curriculum, really. Everything else is grammar."

She walked down the stairs of the chandlery slowly, one hand on the rail, keeping her pace at the pace of a tired woman running errands. Outside, the rain had started again, fine as sieved flour. She turned toward the harbor without deciding to, and stood a while under the eave of a net-mender's hut, watching the Meridian's lights through the grey.

*Who refilled the medicine.*

She turned the morning over, piece by piece, the way she'd turned the typebars. He had known about the kettle trick — plausible, he'd taught her himself. He had guessed she'd opened the letter — plausible, he knew her hands. He had mentioned the machine only after extracting her promise — plausible, if he was a careful old man managing a careless young one. Every piece fit. Every piece also fit the other story: a man coached in his convalescence, primed with just enough truth to be convincing, fishing with the bait she herself had carried across the border. *Bring it to me before anyone else.* Reval wouldn't need to torture anyone. He'd only need patience and a dying man and a promise of medicine, or morphine, or mercy — whichever currency the situation required.

Or Oskar was simply Oskar, dying honestly, trying to keep his last courier alive.

Two stories. Same facts. She could not test either without risking everything, because the only way to test whether a handler was compromised was to feed him something false and watch where it surfaced — and anything she fed him would point, eventually, back to the Halvards, back to the loft, back to the boot of a sixteen-year-old girl who photographed harbors without knowing why it was dangerous.

For seven years, Juniper Eriksen had served a republic with her whole self: her legs, her languages, her sleeplessness, her silence. The republic had lasted eleven days past her latest crossing of its border. Its flag was torn down at checkpoints. Its ministers were fled or shot. Its final army, according to its own last officer, was one consumptive clerk and one woman standing in the rain.

And she had just lied to it. First small lie — *no, I didn't open it* — and then the large one, the one that sat in her stomach like swallowed stone: *before anyone else.* She had promised. She had looked a dying man in the eye and sworn the thing she already knew she would not do. Whatever else the film was — proof, weapon, currency, curse — it would not go into that room above the chandlery. Not while the medicine bottle kept refilling itself. Not while *trust no one* was the whole curriculum.

She was still standing under the eave when she saw the boy.

He came along the quay road at a jog, too fast for the rain, a harbormaster's office cap pulled low, glancing back over his shoulder twice before he reached the Meridian's booking shed. Young, nervous, known to her vaguely — the clerk who tallied freight, the one with the ink-stained cuffs who'd confirmed the berth count. He disappeared inside, and a minute later reappeared, and instead of returning to the harbor office he cut sharply inland, toward the shipyard streets, walking fast with his head down like a man going to confess something.

Juniper pushed off from the wall and followed him.
