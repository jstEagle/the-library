## 19. Ilsa's Stories

Liv Halvard had been sent upstairs with a tray she was not allowed to carry up the stairs, which was the kind of instruction Grandmother Ilsa specialized in giving.

"Set it on the landing," Ilsa called through the door before Liv even knocked. "Then knock. Then wait until I tell you to bring it in. A girl who arrives all at once gives a person nothing to look forward to."

"I've been doing it this way for three weeks."

"Three weeks of practice and you still arrive all at once. Come in, then. You're letting the draft in with your hesitation."

The room at the top of the Halvard house smelled of camphor and lavender and, beneath both, the faint iron tang of the harbor that no amount of closed windows could keep out. Ilsa lay propped against pillows that had been rearranged so many times they'd achieved a kind of architecture, her white hair braided into a crown so tight it seemed to be holding something in. Her hands were folded on the quilt — small hands, knuckles swollen, but steady as moored boats.

Liv set the tray down: bread and butter, weak tea, a spoonful of honey doled out by her mother with the gravity of a woman dispensing medicine.

"You eat that toast before it goes cold," Ilsa said. "And don't hover. Hovering makes me feel like a specimen."

"I'm not hovering. I'm sitting." Liv sat on the edge of the bed, careful of the leg beneath the quilt that hadn't worked properly since before Liv was born.

"That's hovering with your whole body. Fine. Stay. It's raining anyway, and you look like you've been running messages in it."

"I have."

"For people who pay you?"

"For people who might."

Ilsa snorted, which was as close as she came to laughter before noon. She broke off a corner of toast and ate it looking out the window — the window that faced the yard, where the saws had gone silent days ago, where half the workforce had already walked to the quay to argue their way onto ships. The other half stood around in knots talking low and fast. The yard looked like a body whose blood was draining toward one wound.

"They'll come back or they won't," Ilsa said, following Liv's eyes. "Don't make a face about it. Men who are frightened aren't traitors. They're just men who are frightened."

"Anders says—"

"Anders says what Anders always says, which is that if everyone would simply do exactly what he told them, the world would hold together. He got that from his grandfather, who died being right about everything except the wave that took him." She brushed crumbs from the quilt with terrible precision. "You came up here for more than toast. Your mother asked you to sit with me, which means your mother is worried about something, which means there is something to worry about, which means you will now ask me whatever it is you've been chewing since you came through the door."

Liv turned the teacup in its saucer. Down in the kitchen, faintly, she could hear Juniper's voice under Maren's — two women talking the way people talked when they weren't saying anything.

"It's the courier," Liv said. "Juniper. She sleeps in the loft and she eats like she's counting every bite and last night I heard her walking the floor at three in the morning. And Papa watches her when he thinks no one sees. And Mama—Mama sets her plate like she's setting a place for someone who might leave before the meal ends." She looked up. "Grandmother. Who is she? Really?"

For a long moment Ilsa said nothing, and Liv knew better than to fill the silence. Her grandmother could hold a silence the way other people held a grudge — indefinitely, and with visible enjoyment.

"You want to know who she is," Ilsa said finally. "That's the wrong question. But it's the question everyone asks first, so we'll start there, and then I'm going to tell you something you actually need, because you're sixteen and this house has decided — without asking either of us — that you're old enough now."

* * *

"The first purge was before your father could walk. That would be 1879, if your arithmetic is as bad as I suspect. There was a minister — call him a minister; he was a customs man with ambitions — who decided that the shipping families were moving money for the republicans, and some of us were, though not the way he meant, and not for the reasons he thought. They came for names. They always come for names. Names are cheaper than proof."

She ate another corner of toast. Liv waited.

"They arrested eleven men from this street. Two of them were Halvards by marriage — my husband's cousin Torsten, and Torsten's wife's brother. Torsten was guilty of nothing except lending money to the wrong man. His brother-in-law was guilty of exactly what they said. It didn't matter. They were both on the list, and the list was the law, because the man who wrote the list had made himself the law's uncle, its banker, and its tailor."

"What happened to them?"

"Torsten came back after fourteen months and never spoke above a whisper again. The brother-in-law came back in a box with the lid nailed crooked." Ilsa said it flatly, the way she reported weather. "And here is what matters, girl, so put down your tea and listen. While they were away, the rest of the family did not hold meetings about loyalty. We didn't interrogate each other about who knew whom. My mother-in-law — a woman who could smell a lie through a wall — fed every stranger who knocked, including two I'd wager were informants, because turning someone hungry away tells them exactly how much you value them, and starving men sell cheap."

"So you just... let them in."

"We fed them. Letting them in is a different thing. You can feed a wolf at the door without opening it." Her eyes were sharp and gray and missed nothing. "The second purge was 1893. That one I remember better, because I was the one carrying things."

"You?" Liv couldn't help it. "You were—"

"I was seventeen, and my legs worked, and I could walk from here to the customs house faster than any constable could write my description. Of course I carried things. Every household has someone whose youth makes her invisible. You know this yourself — you've been slipping past those soldiers all week because they see a girl with a basket and their eyes slide off like rain off oilcloth." She pointed the crust at Liv. "Don't look proud. Invisibility is only an advantage until someone decides to look. Then it's a coffin."

"What happened in '93?"

"This time it wasn't money. It was printing. Pamphlets, manifests, lists of the arrested's families so the families could find each other. The navy seized three of our vessels on suspicion — suspicion, mind, the cheapest metal in the world — and held them at anchor for a season until the hulls fouled and we nearly lost the yard. Your great-grandfather rowed out to negotiate with an admiral in the rain and caught the fever that killed him. And through all of it—" Ilsa paused, and for the first time her voice changed, softened into something almost tender, quickly filed away again. "Through all of it, a woman named Signe Eriksen came and went through our kitchen like weather."

Liv sat very still. The name landed somewhere below her ribs.

"She was a smuggler's daughter out of the northern coast. She ran documents south when the border was still porous, and she used our ships, and we let her, and once — in the winter of '94 — she brought us a boy who wasn't hers and wasn't ours, half-frozen, hidden in a cod barrel, and asked us to keep him until the ice broke. So we did. That's the whole story. She asked. We kept."

"Was that—"

"That was your Uncle Nils. Who lived to be sixty-one and built the dry dock your father is so proud of, and who cried like a child at my wedding. So when you wonder why your mother feeds a stranger who appeared out of the rain with secrets sewn into her coat—" Ilsa shrugged, magnificent and unrepentant. "We have history with women who carry things."

* * *

Liv helped her grandmother drink the tea, holding the cup while Ilsa guided it with both hands, refusing assistance and accepting it simultaneously, which Liv understood was the general condition of being old.

"Now," Ilsa said, when the cup was empty, "the rule. Because you didn't come up here for stories, whatever you think. You came up here because you can feel the house tightening like a fist, and you want to know what your family believes before someone asks you what you believe. Am I wrong?"

"No."

"Good. Honesty costs nothing yet. Here is the rule, and it is older than I am, and your great-great-grandfather carved it into the beam over the office door where your father walks under it every day of his life whether he reads it or not."

She took Liv's hand. Her grip was stronger than it had any right to be.

"*We build ships so people can leave. We do not choose who leaves.*"

Liv repeated it silently, testing it. "But what does it mean? Practically."

"It means we don't decide whose life is worth saving. Not the government's job, not the church's job, and God knows not ours. When a man comes to the quay with passage money, we take his money and carry him across the water. We don't ask what he did to need leaving. When a woman comes with no money and a story, sometimes we carry her anyway. We don't ask what she's carrying. Do you understand what that costs? It means that in '79 we ferried a customs spy out with the same load as the pamphleteers, because he paid and he was afraid, and my husband never forgave himself for checking the manifest afterward and knowing. And we did it *again* the next season anyway. Because the alternative isn't safety. The alternative is becoming the sort of family that chooses, and families that choose start choosing small, and then they're choosing everything, and then they're just another committee deciding who deserves to live."

"But if there's only room for one—"

"There is never only room for one. There is only ever room for whoever fits, and the arithmetic is done by the sea, not by us." Ilsa released her hand and settled back against her pillows, suddenly looking tired in a way that had nothing to do with age. "You're asking because of the ship. Don't deny it — the whole house breathes differently around the word *Meridian*. One berth, or near enough. And everyone in this kitchen is quietly volunteering everyone else, and hating themselves for it, and calling the hatred love."

Liv's throat tightened. "What would you do?"

"I would do what I've done twice before." Ilsa closed her eyes. "Nothing clever. Nothing noble. I would feed whoever came to the door, and I would not ask the sea to explain itself, and I would stay in the bed I chose." She opened one eye. "Which reminds me. If anyone in this house proposes to give my berth away to save me the trouble of dying in my own bed, they will answer to me personally, and I am far more frightening than Captain Whoever-He-Is, because I have nothing left to lose and excellent hearing through floors."

"Reval," Liv said. "Captain Reval."

"Hm. Reval. An Estonian name wearing a uniform it didn't grow up in. Those are always the worst ones — men performing loyalty harder than anyone believes it." She waved a hand. "Go down. Tell your mother I ate everything and lied about the honey. And Liv—"

Liv stopped at the door.

"When you run your messages tomorrow — and you will, because sitting still would kill you faster than any soldier — you keep your eyes open and your mouth shut, and if you see something that frightens you, you bring it home before you bring it anywhere else. This kitchen is where frightening things get survived. Not the docks. Not the streets. Here."

"Yes, Grandmother."

"And Liv." The old woman's voice dropped, and for a moment the sharpness fell away entirely, and what looked out of her face was ninety years of Vessmark — three purges, two wars of paper, one family that kept building ships while empires kept building lists. "Whatever happens with that berth, with the courier, with all of it — nobody in this house gets to decide alone. Not your father, who will try. Not your mother, who won't admit she's trying. Not even me. We decide loud, at a table, with bread in front of us. That's how Halvards survive. We've buried every regime that tried to make us quiet."

Liv went down the stairs slowly, the rule turning over in her mind like a coin she hadn't known she was carrying: *we build ships so people can leave.* At the bottom, the kitchen was warm and yellow-lit, and Juniper stood at the window with her back straight as a mast, watching the harbor, and Maren was setting a fourth plate for supper without having been asked by anyone.

And through the window, past the rooftops, Liv could see the harbor gate — where soldiers were arriving in a slow, deliberate line, unhitching something heavy from a cart, and beginning, methodically, to close it.
