## 21. The Berth Question

The news came the way bad news always came to Kalshavn — sideways, in fragments, through men who spoke too quietly and looked at their boots.

Anders Halvard heard it first from Brenner, the old rigger who had worked the yard since before Anders could walk. Brenner came into the loft office at dusk without knocking, which he never did, and stood there with his cap in his hands like a man at a funeral.

"They've struck eleven names from the Meridian's list," he said. "Paid cancellations. And two of the berths that were open this morning — the purser's sold them to Reval's people. For their own."

Anders set down the pencil he had been holding over the yard ledger. The numbers on the page had stopped meaning anything an hour ago anyway.

"How many left?"

"For civilians?" Brenner turned his cap once, twice. "One."

The word sat between them like a dropped tool.

"One," Anders repeated.

"Out of four hundred and six passengers." Brenner put his cap on now, as if he'd finished saying something and needed to look busy doing it. "I'm sorry, boy. I checked twice. I made him show me the book."

Anders thanked him. He was surprised at how steady his voice came out. It was the voice he used with suppliers and strikebreakers, the flat one, the one that gave nothing away, and it occurred to him distantly that he had spent thirty years building exactly this voice for exactly this kind of moment, though he had always imagined the moments would be about money.

Brenner left. Anders sat in the office above the empty yard — the yard that had been stripped of its tools three days ago to buy a child back from a checkpoint — and listened to the rain work the skylight, and did the arithmetic that had been waiting all week to be done.

Four hundred and six passengers. One berth.

His mother. His grandmother. His daughter. Juniper Eriksen, who was not family but who had come back to them carrying something none of them would name aloud, and who slept in the loft with her boots by the door like a woman expecting to run.

He did the arithmetic again, as if repetition were a kind of prayer, and got the same answer.

* * *

They did not discuss it at dinner.

That was the strange thing, the thing Anders kept turning over afterward like a stone found in a net. Maren set the table as she always did — the good bread, the fish stew thinning toward the end of what it could give, the lamp turned up high because Ilsa complained about her eyes and Maren pretended to mind. Liv talked. That was new enough that they let her; since the search, since the checkpoint, since her father had come home hollow-handed after selling forty years of the family's tools to strangers with rifles, Liv had gone quiet as a closed door, and when the door opened they were grateful for whatever came through it.

"There's a woman on the east quay," Liv said, "who's knitting sweaters for everyone on the ship. She doesn't know how many people are going. She just keeps knitting. She's on her ninth."

"Nine sweaters," said Ilsa, "and one berth. There's a parable in that, if anyone wanted one."

Nobody wanted one. The silence that followed had edges to it. Anders watched his mother's hands — she had stopped eating, he noticed, though her fork stayed where it was, moving peas around a bowl as if the peas required supervision. He watched Juniper across the table, her typist's fingers curled around her cup, her face doing what it always did, which was nothing. He watched his grandmother watch all of them, sharp-eyed under the white hair, missing nothing, saying less.

Afterward Maren washed dishes and Anders dried, which had been their arrangement since he was tall enough to reach the shelf, and neither of them spoke about the berth, and both of them knew the other had heard the news, and the knowing filled the kitchen louder than talk would have.

It was Ilsa who broke it, from her chair by the stove, where she held court these days like an admiral retired but still flying her colors.

"Well," she said. "Are we going to stand here drying dishes like three sensible people while we all pretend we haven't each decided already? Or shall we do it properly?"

"Mormor," Anders began.

"Don't mormor me. I've outlived two purges and your grandfather, and I know how a Halvard argues when the argument matters. We don't argue. We go quiet and we scheme separately and then we all try to lie at the gangway at once and embarrass ourselves in front of the purser." She pulled her shawl tighter. "So. Sit down. Both of you. We're going to say it plainly, once, and then we're going to stop pretending, which is more than most families manage before the end."

Anders hung the dish towel on its hook with great care, as if the towel's position mattered, as if anything mattered except the thing coming next.

* * *

They gathered in the front room, where the light was bad and the furniture was old and every piece of it had crossed water at least once, because that was what Halvards did — they built ships, and then they filled them with everything they loved and sent them somewhere safer than here.

Maren sat with her hands folded. Anders took the window seat. Juniper appeared in the doorway and Ilsa said, "You too, courier. You've got fingers in this whether you admit it or not," and Juniper came in and stood by the wall like a guest at someone else's trial.

"I'll go first," Anders said, "since apparently we're doing this."

He had rehearsed it on the stairs, actually rehearsed it, words arranged in order like planks laid across a gap, and now that he opened his mouth the planks shifted.

"It should be Mormor," he said. "She's eighty-one. She can't make the overland crossing — you all know it, Dr. Vessel said it to my face in March, and he wasn't kind about it. She survived the '89 purge because there was a ship and a berth and someone with sense enough to put her on it. If we send her overland she dies in a farmhouse coughing, or worse, she gets stopped at some border with soldiers who read lists." His voice held. He made it hold. "She built half this harbor's fleet with her ledgers. She taught me to read tide tables before I could write my own name. If this family gets to save exactly one person, it saves the person who kept the accounts of everyone else it ever saved."

"That's very tidy," said Ilsa.

"It's true."

"Tidy things usually are. That's how you tell them apart from honest ones." But she said it gently, and she reached over and squeezed his wrist once, hard, the way she had when he was nine and had broken the neighbor's window and told the truth about it. Then she withdrew into her shawl and waited her turn like a chess player.

"Anders is wrong," Maren said.

"Moder—"

"He's right about the facts and wrong about the conclusion, which is worse." Maren's voice was even, the kitchen voice, the voice that had fed dock families through two strikes and a blockade without ever once sounding tired in public. "Your grandmother is eighty-one and she has told us herself, repeatedly and at volume, that she intends to die in this house. I believe her. I've believed her for years. She says it the way she says everything — as a fact already filed." Maren unfolded her hands and refolded them. "Liv is sixteen. Liv is the one Reval's people photographed outside this house. Liv is the one who was held at a checkpoint because she ran to warn you. Whatever lists exist — and they exist, Anders, don't look at me like that, I bake bread for the whole quay and I hear everything — her name is on them, or near them, or will be. You want to spend our one berth on eighty-one years already fully lived. I want to spend it on sixteen years barely begun. That's the whole argument. There isn't another one."

"She won't go alone," Anders said. "If Mormor stays, someone stays with her—"

"Someone always stays with her. Yes. I've done that arithmetic too." Maren looked at him then, straight on, and he saw that behind the evenness her hands had gone white in her lap. "Do you think I haven't? Do you think any of us hasn't, every night this week, lying in the dark doing sums with human beings instead of kroner?" Her voice cracked, one hairline fracture, and repaired itself. "I am not asking for my daughter because I love her more than you love your grandmother. I'm asking because of the numbers, and the numbers are the only honest thing left in this city."

The lamp hissed softly. Rain worked the windows. From the harbor came the sound Anders had been hearing in his sleep for days — hammers, mooring lines, the groan of a ship settling against its fenders like a horse that smelled the barn.

"My turn," said Ilsa.

* * *

"You're both idiots," Ilsa began, with evident satisfaction, "but you're my idiots, so listen."

Juniper moved slightly by the wall, and Ilsa pointed a finger at her without looking. "You especially, girl. Sit down. If I'm going to settle this family's affairs I'll do it in front of the one member who knows how to keep a secret, since clearly no one else does."

Juniper sat.

"Here is what eighty-one years buys you," Ilsa said. "It buys you the ability to count. So count with me. This family has been scattered twice before. In '61 they scattered us to Rostock and Copenhagen, and we thought it was the end, and within ten years we had a yard again. In '89 they scattered us worse — my sister to America, my husband to prison, me running this business with ink-stained fingers and a baby on my hip. And we thought that was the end. It was not the end. Do you know why?"

No one answered. Ilsa hadn't expected them to.

"Because Halvards survive scatterings. We do not survive bargains. Every time this family has tried to bargain — buy safety, trade favor, hold together at any cost — we've lost more than if we'd simply scattered and trusted each other to regrow. Your grandfather bargained with the customs men in '87. Cost us the brig *Solveig*. My father bargained with the harbor trust in '52. Cost us the dry dock for a generation." She ticked them off on her fingers, crimes and prices, a ledger recited from memory. "We build ships so people can leave. We do not choose who leaves. That's the rule. Your mother wrote it in the front of the first ledger, and I wrote it in the second, and I will not have it amended now by two children who think love means deciding for each other."

"So you'll stay," Anders said. Not a question.

"I'll stay." She said it the way she announced the weather. "I'm eighty-one. The sea crossing would kill me by Thursday and I'd ruin the voyage complaining, which would spoil it for whoever got the berth, and then we'd have wasted the berth AND my death, and I refuse to waste two things at once. Ilsa Halvard wastes nothing."

"Mormor—" Liv was in the doorway now; Anders hadn't seen her arrive. Her face had gone the color of paper.

"Hush, child, I haven't finished." Ilsa gathered herself, and for a moment the performance thinned, and Anders saw underneath it the small, cold, mortal fear that she carried the way she carried everything — silently, and in a locked drawer. "Here's what I want, and you'll all listen because I'm the oldest and age is finally good for something. Nobody gives anybody anything at that gangway. Nobody lifts anybody over rails, nobody lies to pursers, nobody stands on the quay being noble in a way that photographs badly. Whoever goes, goes because we all agree, here, tonight, calmly, like shipwrights reading a plan. Agreed?"

Three heads nodded. Four counting Juniper.

"Good," said Ilsa. "Now we can begin the real argument, which is that none of you will take it, and neither will I, and there is a fifth person in this room who has been suspiciously quiet."

Every head turned to Juniper.

* * *

Juniper looked, Anders thought, like a woman who had walked into the wrong meeting and was calculating the exits. She sat very straight, hands in her lap, and when she spoke her voice was level and quiet.

"The berth isn't mine. I'm not on the manifest. I never was."

"You could be," Maren said. "For the price of a name added to a list, and we still have friends who owe us favors on that ship."

"I have work here."

"Your work," Ilsa said, "is sitting in a harbor full of soldiers waiting for you to make a mistake. I may be old, courier, but I can count soldiers."

"I appreciate—"

"Don't appreciate me. Answer the question. If we put you on that ship tomorrow morning, would you get on it?"

The silence stretched. Anders watched Juniper's hands — those precise, restless, typist's hands — go still in her lap, completely still, the way they only went still when she was holding something dangerous. He had noticed it weeks ago: the stillness was the tell. When Juniper Eriksen went motionless, she was carrying weight.

"No," Juniper said at last. "I wouldn't get on it. And you shouldn't spend the berth on someone who won't use it."

"There," said Ilsa, with grim triumph. "One honest person in the room. Now we're down to the three of us, and I've already refused, so it's the two of you, and you've each refused the other, which means we've achieved precisely nothing except saying aloud what we were all thinking, which — I'll grant — is more than most families manage."

"Mormor," Liv said from the doorway, and her voice cracked on the second syllable, sixteen years old and furious with grief, "why does everyone keep talking like the ship is the only way? Why is nobody talking about the other ways? The fishing boats, the coves, the — Papa knows every inlet from here to the border, he's sailed them his whole life—"

"Liv." Anders stood. "Come here."

She came, reluctantly, and he put his arm around her shoulders, and she was trembling, and he hated that she was trembling, and he hated more that there was nothing in the world he could trade to stop it.

"The fishing boats are being searched," he said quietly. "Every hull, every night, since Tuesday. Reval's men take the engines apart looking for — " he glanced at Juniper, and stopped himself, and Juniper looked at the floor. "Looking for things. The coves are watched. There is one ship, one berth, and one tide, and the tide turns at dawn."

"Then choose fast," Liv said, with a bitterness that sounded far older than she was, and pulled free of his arm, and left the room. They heard her feet on the stairs, and then the small slam of her door, which was somehow worse than shouting.

Maren rose to follow her. At the door she paused and looked back at Anders, and what passed between them was not forgiveness — it was too early for that, maybe too early ever — but it was recognition: two people standing on opposite ends of the same impossible plank, each willing to fall so the other could cross.

"We'll speak again in the morning," Maren said. "All of us. Calmly. Like shipwrights."

Then she was gone up the stairs, and the house settled around its silences.

* * *

Anders did not sleep. He sat in the front room with the lamp low, listening to the harbor breathe, and at some hour past midnight he became aware that Juniper had not gone up either. She stood at the dark window with her coat already on, looking down at the black water, and he realized she had been standing there a long time.

"The berth," Anders said. "You know who it should go to."

"I know who it will go to," Juniper said. "That's different."

"And who's that?"

She didn't answer. Down on the quay, lamplight moved — a patrol, two soldiers and a lantern, walking their slow circuit past the shuttered chandleries. Their shadows crawled up the wall of the house that built ships, and slid off it, and were gone.

"Whatever you're carrying," Anders said quietly, "it's the reason there's only one berth. Isn't it. Everything Reval does — the searches, the manifest, the checkpoints — it all bends around you like current around a keel."

Juniper turned. In the low lamplight her face was unreadable, but her eyes were not; they were the eyes of someone who had already grieved this family once, years ago, from a distance, and had just discovered that grief could be scheduled to happen twice.

"Get some sleep, Mr. Halvard," she said. "Dawn comes early, and the tide won't wait for us to finish arguing about love."

She went up the stairs. Anders sat alone in the lamplight, and outside, far down the quay, a bell buoy rang once against its chain, and the sound of it — patient, indifferent, keeping its own count of hours — seemed suddenly like the only honest voice in Kalshavn.

He looked at the clock. Four hours to dawn.

Somewhere in the sleeping house, he knew, his mother was awake, and his grandmother was awake, and the courier was awake, and every one of them was spending those four hours doing the same arithmetic, arriving at the same impossible sum — and every one of them, he understood with a clarity that felt almost like peace, intended to lie about the answer at the gangway.

The question was which of them would lie best.
