## 22. Oskar's Last Request

The summons came by a child who did not know he was carrying it.

He was one of the dock boys who ran errands for bread and coins, and he found Juniper at the fish market where she had learned to stand without hurrying, to look like a woman comparing herring. He pressed a scrap of paper into her hand the way children press anything into any adult's hand—without ceremony, already running.

*The old clerk asks for you. Room above the chandlery. Come before dark. He says: before dark.*

No signature. None needed. Only two people in Kalshavn still called Oskar Vinter *the old clerk*, and one of them was dying.

Juniper read it twice, then folded it small and fed it to a brazier of coals an old woman was tending against the sea wind. The paper curled, flared orange, and was gone. She watched it go with a feeling she could not immediately name, and then could, and wished she couldn't: envy. The note had been easy to destroy. Everything else in her life that week required more than fire.

She had not seen Oskar in nine days. Nine days since the type room, since the typewriter's guts lay open on the desk like something surgically delivered, since she had lifted out the strip of microfilm no wider than her little finger and felt the whole weight of a republic shift into her hands. Nine days since she had stood in front of him, his breath rattling wetly in the narrow room, and lied.

*I couldn't find it,* she had said. *The machine was stripped. Whatever they hid inside is gone.*

He had looked at her for a long time. His eyes were yellow now, the yellow of old paper, of things kept too long from the light. And then he had said, *Of course,* and turned to the window, and she had walked out into the rain carrying both the film and the certainty that he knew.

That was nine days ago. Now he asked for her before dark, as if there were a deadline even death respected.

---

The chandlery stood on the salt side of the inner harbor, a crooked building of tarred timber that leaned toward the water like a tired sailor. Juniper took three different streets to reach it and doubled back once along the fish-cleaning quay, watching reflections in the black water for anyone who reflected when she didn't. Reval's men had grown subtler in the past week. They no longer stood on corners; they sat in cafés with newspapers they never turned. The city had learned to be watched.

The stairs up to Oskar's room were narrow and smelled of lamp oil and dried kelp. She climbed them slowly, giving her eyes time to adjust to the dimness, giving her hands time to stop shaking. They mostly obeyed. Her hands were the most disciplined part of her; they had been typing other people's secrets since she was nineteen, and they had learned that secrets pass through fingers best when fingers stay steady.

She knocked twice, paused, once—their old rhythm, from the border days, from another country that had existed four weeks ago.

"Enter," said a voice like sand poured slowly.

The room was one window, one bed, one chair, and a smell of medicine that could not quite cover the smell underneath it, which was the smell of a body finishing its work. Oskar lay propped against pillows gone grey with use. He was smaller than she remembered, though she had seen him only nine days ago. Dying does not happen gradually, she thought. It happens all at once and then takes its time informing everyone.

His coat—the same brown ministry coat he'd worn through twenty years of filing and ciphering—hung on the back of the door, absurd and formal, as if he expected to be buried in it or expected to rise and wear it again. On the table beside the bed: a glass of water untouched, a spoon, and a single candle burned down nearly to the brass.

"You came before dark." He tried to smile. His face remembered how but the muscles had begun resigning their posts. "Good courier. Punctual."

"You shouldn't have sent the boy through the market. Reval's people watch it."

"Reval's people watch everything. That is what Reval's people are *for*." He coughed then, a long terrible business, his whole chest convulsing while she moved to hold the glass to his lips, and when it passed he lay back white-faced and faintly amused, as though the cough had been a colleague's bad joke. "Water. Thank you. Sit down, Juniper. I haven't the strength to talk up at you."

There was nowhere to sit but the edge of the bed. She sat. Up close she could see how the illness had mapped itself onto him—the hollows at his temples, the blue tracery of veins at his throat, the way his hands lay on the blanket like tools set down after a long shift.

"I'm going to say this quickly," he said, "while I still say things well. Then I'm going to rest, and you're going to let me rest, and if I die mid-sentence I want you to know that I noticed, in these last days, that you are the finest officer the republic ever failed to promote."

"Oskar—"

"Let me talk. You've spent your whole career listening to instructions. Indulge me one final dispatch."

She folded her hands in her lap and waited, the way she'd waited at checkpoints, the way she'd waited outside ministries, the way she had waited her whole adult life for other people's words to become her burden.

"The film," Oskar said.

Her spine went cold and perfectly straight. "Oskar—"

"*I said let me talk.*" There was, beneath the wreck of his voice, the old iron. Twenty years of telling ministers inconvenient truths had built something in him that sickness could bend but not break. "You have it. Don't insult us both by denying it again. You had it the moment you left the ship office. I watched your face when you told me the machine was stripped. You have never in your life lied that badly, and I include the time you told me my cipher work was elegant."

Juniper looked down at her hands. Steady. Traitors, both of them—her hands and her heart, each keeping its own discipline.

"Why didn't you say so?" she asked quietly.

"Because I wanted to see what you would do." He drew a breath that seemed to come from somewhere below the floorboards. "A courier who finds the cargo and reports nothing—that's either treason or judgment. I needed to know which. I've spent nine days knowing. Do you want to hear my finding?"

"Yes."

"It's judgment." His eyes closed briefly. "You hid it because you understood what I apparently did not until too late: that I sent you into Kalshavn carrying bait, and the bait worked, and now the thing in your possession is worth more dead than every living person on that quay. You hid it because you love the Halvards, and because you are smart enough to be afraid. Both correct. Both survivable. Treason would have been delivering it to me without thinking."

"And now?"

"Now I am going to give you the hardest instruction of your career, and I am going to die before I can explain it properly, and you are going to hate me for that, and I want you to know that I know." He reached for her hand. His grip was astonishing—dry, light, and immovable, like driftwood. "Destroy it, Juniper."

The word went through her like cold water through cloth.

"Destroy—" She stopped. Started again. "It proves the coup was financed abroad. It names the banks. It names the men. If it reaches Stockholm, if it reaches any neutral paper—"

"If," Oskar said. "Yes. That is the entire problem. Listen to me." He pulled himself higher against the pillows, and for a moment the dying man receded and the clerk remained, the man who had spent his life in the machinery of state, who understood machines. "That film is not truth. Truth doesn't fit on microfilm. It's *leverage*. It is worth exactly as much as someone is willing to pay or kill for it, and right now there is a captain of intelligence in this city who would trade forty lives for it, and there is a government-in-exile in Stockholm that would trade the Halvards' silence for it, and there are foreign ministries that would pay in guns, and none of them—not one—wants it for the truth. Truth is what people say afterward to justify what they did to get it."

"So we do nothing. We let it sit."

"Sitting is a decision too. Sitting means Reval keeps hunting. Sitting means every day it exists, it exists near people who will be arrested for its proximity. You know this. You've known it for nine days; it's why you sleep badly—I can see it under your eyes, girl, I was reading faces before your mother was born."

The mention of her mother landed strangely, and he saw it land, and something softened in his ruined face.

"Or," Juniper said slowly, "I use it. One berth on the Meridian. One family that needs five. Reval offered—"

"He made an offer? Already?" For the first time, Oskar laughed—a real laugh, appalled and delighted, which turned into coughing, which cost him visibly. When he recovered there was blood at the corner of his mouth, a thin pink thread he wiped away without looking at it, the way a man brushes off rain. "Of course he did. Of course. He knows about the berth. He knows about the family. He has probably counted the spoons in Maren Halvard's kitchen." He gripped her hand again. "Then hear me, and hear me completely. A bargain with Reval is a coin he mints himself. He will take your film and give you nothing, or worse than nothing—he'll give you exactly enough hope to keep you standing still while he arrests them all. Men like Reval don't renege on bargains. They design bargains that were never going to be kept. That is the whole art of them."

"Then what do you want?" Her voice cracked on *want*, and she hated it. "Tell me plainly. Destroy it—or spend it. Those are the only doors. Choose one for me, Oskar, because I cannot—"

"That," he whispered, "is precisely why I won't."

The candle guttered. Somewhere below, in the chandlery, a man began closing crates for the night, each lid coming down like a slow heartbeat.

"I have served governments for thirty years," Oskar said, when his breath allowed. "I have carried instructions to men who died executing them, and I signed those instructions, and I slept, because signing orders lets a man sleep. That is what instructions are *for*, Juniper—they are how the powerful borrow the consciences of the obedient." His yellow eyes fixed on hers. "I will not do it one last time. I will not die having taught you that your conscience ends where my signature begins. Destroy it or spend it—but decide it yourself, alone, with no dead man's voice over your shoulder telling you it was ordered. Because if you destroy it, you must be able to live with the silence. And if you spend it, you must be able to live with the price. Either way *you* live with it. Not me. Not the ministry. The ministry—" and here his mouth twisted, "—the ministry is a room in Kristianstad with a leaky roof, if it exists at all anymore. The republic is a flag in a drawer. What remains, courier, is you. You are what remains of the government of Vessmark. Congratulations. My condolences."

Juniper sat very still. Through the small window, beyond the rooftops, she could see the masts of the harbor, and among them—the tallest, the one with a lantern already lit at its truck—the Meridian, waiting for dawn and a tide and a passenger list in an enemy's hands.

"If I destroy it," she said, testing the words like rotten planks, "then everyone who died carrying pieces of this—Voss, and the brothers at the border house, and whoever typed that film in the dark—all of it means nothing."

"It means what it always meant," Oskar said gently. "They bought time. Time is the only currency revolutions ever actually deal in."

"And if I spend it—if I buy Liv's place on that ship with it, just Liv, just one child—"

"Then you will have saved one girl and armed a regime, and the regime will arrest the other four Halvards within the week anyway, because Reval does not honor trades, and you will know—I mean you will *know*, at three in the morning, for the rest of your life—that your film paid for the rope." He closed his eyes. "Unless you find a third door. Couriers are good at finding doors. It's why I chose you, twelve years ago, when I found a stowaway who'd memorized a ship's manifest in one afternoon and could lie about it beautifully."

"You didn't choose me. You arrested me."

"I *recruited* you. The arrest was a formality we skipped." The ghost of the smile again. "Find the third door, Juniper. Or don't. But decide it the way Ilsa Halvard decides things—I met her once, at a christening in 'ninety-three; terrifying woman—decide it with your own two hands on the thing itself."

He was tiring rapidly now. She could see it the way one sees a tide going out—not in any single motion but in the level of everything. His voice dropped, and she leaned close.

"One more thing," he murmured. "The letter I gave you. The sealed letter naming the Halvards. You opened it. Good. But did you trace the seal?"

"Wax, ministry pattern, fourth issue—"

"Not the wax. The *typing*." His hand fluttered weakly against the blanket. "Every machine leaves fingerprints. Every typist leaves habits. The letter that named them—it was typed on a ministry Corona, 1909 batch, and the typist strikes the lowercase *h* hard and drags the *r*. I filed that typist's work for six years. I know her hand like a signature." His breath caught. "She defected the week of the coup. She works for Reval now. Which means the letter was written by the enemy's own hand—which means it was never a warrant at all. It was *chum*. Thrown in the water to see what surfaced."

Juniper's mouth went dry. "To see if I surfaced."

"To see if the last loyal courier would come home to warn the family she loves." His eyes opened, and they were very clear and very far away. "You did. So the trap knows your face now. Remember that tonight, whatever you decide. You aren't choosing in safety. You're choosing in a net."

"Oskar." She bent lower. "Which door? If you were me—if you had one night—if the film was in your coat against your ribs right now—"

He smiled then, fully, and it transformed him; for a moment he was the dry ironic clerk of the border years, unkillable, unimpressed by empires.

"If I were you," he said, "I would be younger. And I would be in love with a family of shipwrights. And I would not be asking a dying man to carry my conscience one mile further than it can walk." His fingers tightened on hers with the last of his strength, a pressure like a knot being tied. "Destroy it. Spend it. Find the third door. Whichever—do it *after* I'm gone, so that no one, including you, can ever say Oskar Vinter told her to."

And then his hand went slack.

Not dramatically. No rattle, no last arching breath out of a novel. He simply stopped holding on, the way a man sets down a heavy load at the end of a road, and his head tipped against the pillow, and his breathing—which had been a labor all evening—became suddenly, unmistakably, a memory. Juniper sat with him while it finished settling. Outside, the chandlery man locked his doors and jingled away down the alley whistling something cheerful and stupid.

She closed Oskar's eyes with two fingers, the way couriers seal envelopes. Then she searched the room, because he would have expected it: under the mattress lining, behind the loose brick, inside the hollow curtain rod. In the curtain rod she found his cipher keys, wrapped in oilcloth, and a note in his cramped hand:

*For burning, obviously. — O.*

Even dead, he gave instructions. Even dead, they were the right ones.

---

She stood a long time at the window before she lit the candle stub and held the oilcloth packet to the flame.

The keys burned slowly, brass fittings going soft and dull in the heat, and the smell of it filled the little room. Juniper watched the last cipher of the Republic of Vessmark collapse into ash in a chipped saucer, and she understood—with a clarity that felt less like thought than like weather—that she was orphaned. Not of parents; that loss was old and healed into scar. Orphaned of *instruction*. For twelve years there had been someone above her, someone whose signature converted her choices into duty. Now there was no one. There was a film against her ribs worth forty lives, a harbor full of soldiers, one berth on a departing ship, a family that had set a plate for her without conditions—and a net, drawn tight around all of it, woven by a typist who dragged her *r*'s.

Somewhere out in the dark, Captain Reval believed the pressure was doing his work for him. Let it, Juniper thought. Pressure makes diamonds and it makes cowards, and it makes, occasionally, a third thing that neither side has budgeted for.

She pinched out the candle, went down the stairs past the locked chandlery, and stepped into the wind off the harbor. The Meridian's lantern burned at its masthead like a low star. Dawn was perhaps seven hours away. The tide, less.

She turned toward the shipyard, and her hand rose unconsciously to her coat, to the small flat weight riding over her heart, and she began—to use Oskar's phrase, his last gift, his last refusal—to look for the third door.
