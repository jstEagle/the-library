## 23. Search of the Shipyard

The boots came at seven in the morning.

Juniper knew it first as a sound beneath sound — the particular rhythm of military footsteps on cobbles, not the loose scatter of a patrol but the deliberate cadence of men arriving with a purpose and a paper to justify it. She had been awake since five, lying in the loft above the ship office with her clothes already on, the film flat against her ribs inside a linen wrap she had sewn herself from one of Maren's tea towels. Twelve hours ago she had pried open the typewriter's guts by lamplight while Liv kept watch at the stair, and now the thing she carried weighed nothing and everything. Paper and emulsion. A few grams of truth that could buy a harbor or burn it.

She heard the gate.

Not the yard gate — that had been broken for years and Anders had never fixed it, saying a shipyard should look poor even when it wasn't — but the house gate, the iron one Maren oiled every spring so it swung without complaint for guests. It did not swing without complaint this morning. It shrieked like something butchered, and Juniper understood that Maren had done that too, in some last hour of the night, the way she did everything: quietly, thoroughly, and with an eye toward exactly who would be listening.

Boots in the courtyard. Voices — low, clipped, the accent of the capital flattening its vowels over Kalshavn's softer ones. Then the knock, which was no knock at all but three blows of a rifle butt against the kitchen door, spaced like punctuation.

Juniper moved to the loft window, which looked down through a gap between chimneys onto the yard, and pressed herself into the cold gap of brick where the glass was oldest and thickest with grime. From here she could see the kitchen door, the wash line, the woodpile, and the side entrance of the office below her. From here, if she flattened her breath, she could see everything and be seen by nothing.

Maren opened the door before the third blow finished echoing.

* * *

There were nine of them. Juniper counted twice because counting was what her hands wanted to do instead of shaking. Nine soldiers in the grey-green of the new regime, rifles slung, faces arranged in the careful blankness of men who had been told this morning that they were doing lawful work and would believe it for as long as the telling lasted. Behind them, unhurried, came a lieutenant with a clipboard and a face like a closed ledger, and behind him — this made Juniper's stomach drop through the loft floor — the harbormaster's clerk, Vossen, wringing his hat in both hands like a man holding a bird he was afraid to release or crush.

So it was true, then. The clerk had talked, or been made to talk, or had traded whatever he had for whatever he'd been promised. Oskar had warned her: *a regime doesn't need informants everywhere, only one man in each room who wants to live more than he wants anything else.*

The lieutenant spoke. His voice carried up through the chimney gap with terrible clarity.

"Maren Halvard. By authority of the Provisional Authority for Public Order, we are authorized to search these premises for materials belonging to the dissolved Ministry of Foreign Affairs. You will admit us to all rooms."

"I will make coffee," said Maren.

"Maren Halvard—"

"You'll want to be here some hours." Her voice was the voice she used for kneading dough, level and unbothered, addressed slightly past a person rather than at them. "It's cold work, searching. The stove is lit. There are cups enough." A pause Juniper could feel through the bricks. "Or you can go through my house like thieves, and I will make coffee anyway, and you can drink it standing among my broken things and think about which kind of men you are."

Nobody answered. Somewhere below, a soldier shifted his weight, leather creaking.

"Two men on the gate," the lieutenant said finally, to someone else. "Vossen, you wait by the door. The rest — begin with the office. Then the house. Rooms in order. Nothing out of order."

They went in through the office door directly beneath Juniper, and she stepped back from the window as the first boots hit the stairs.

* * *

The film lay against her skin, warm now from her body, and she made herself stand still in the middle of the loft and think the way Oskar had taught her to think eleven days ago, in a back room smelling of cough syrup and ink: *When they come, they will not come for you. They will come for a thing. Your whole art is to be a woman standing next to an empty space.*

But they were coming for a machine, and the machine stood in the office below her, and there was nothing empty about that space at all.

She heard them working. That was the worst part — how methodical it was, how orderly, drawers opened and their contents set out in rows on the floor, papers fanned and read in sequence, a soldier's voice calling measurements of the room to the man with the clipboard. They searched the way Anders laid a keel: everything square, everything checked, nothing skipped. Someone pulled books off the shelf and riffled the pages by thumb, looking for cutouts. Someone found the blueprint drawers and spent a long time with those; she heard drawings being unrolled, held, re-rolled, rejected. Hull lines. Rudder profiles. The beautiful useless geometry of ships.

Then a voice, younger, uncertain: "Sir. The type room."

Her heart stopped, restarted badly, and she crossed to the window again on legs she pretended belonged to someone else.

Through the chimney gap she could see the soldiers carrying the cabinet out into the yard — the locked oak cabinet where the surplus ministry typewriter had lived behind its lock, and beyond it, on the workbench, the typewriter itself, lifted by two men as though it were evidence in a murder, which in a sense it was. They set it on a trestle table in the grey light. One of them fetched a screwdriver from the tool wall without asking permission, and Juniper watched him begin to strip it down with the same unhurried competence he'd give a jammed rifle.

He didn't know what he was taking apart. He thought he was looking for hidden compartments, hollowed keys, a rolled message in the carriage — the tricks of spy fiction. He unscrewed the ribbon spools and held them up. He tipped the machine and shook it. He removed the carriage and peered into the slot with a jeweler's squint. What he did not do — what he could not know to do — was lift the typebar assembly free of its seating and split the two machined halves of its frame, where a strip of microfilm no wider than a thumbnail lay coiled in the one place in Vessmark no one had thought to look until a dying clerk had told a courier about a machine sold as surplus in 1904.

Juniper watched the screwdriver hover near the typebar linkage, watched it turn away, watched the soldier reassemble the machine wrong — one screw left over, dropped into his pocket rather than confessed — and felt something move in her chest that was either relief or contempt and was probably both.

"Nothing," the soldier called up. "Clean civilian machine. Old model. Worn hard."

The lieutenant wrote something on his clipboard. "Bring it anyway."

* * *

They came into the house then, and Juniper left the window because she could not watch what happened next and also could not breathe while watching it, and chose, deliberately, to breathe.

From the loft she could hear most of the house. She heard the parlor chairs dragged across the floorboards. She heard Ilsa's door open — Ilsa, bedridden, sharp-tongued, eighty-one years of purges survived — and braced herself for shouting. Instead she heard Ilsa's voice, dry as kindling, say: "Well? Are you going to stand there or are you going to help me sit up? I'm not being interrogated horizontal, it's undignified." And a young soldier stammering apologies, and the creak of the headboard, and then silence except for Ilsa's breathing, which was a weapon in itself.

She heard Liv's bedroom door open and shut, open and shut, and the small sounds of a girl's life being turned over — hair ribbons, schoolbooks, the tin whistle she played badly on the quay. Liv was downstairs. Liv had been sent to the bakery before dawn, before any of them knew, and Juniper sent a silent thanks after her like a coin thrown in water.

And under all of it, threading through it, the sounds of Maren's kitchen: the clink of the good cups. Water filling the kettle. The scrape of the stove lid. The ordinary domestic percussion of a woman making coffee for the men dismantling her life, performed at exactly the tempo of any other morning.

* * *

At half past eight, Juniper did the bravest and stupidest thing available to her, which was nothing.

Oskar had a phrase for it: *the courier's stillness*. A courier's instinct is always to run — run early, run late, run when the boots come up the stairs — and running is the one confession every regime accepts without question. So she stayed. She sat on the loft cot with her hands folded and the film against her ribs and she listened to the ransacking of the only home she had ever chosen, and she practiced being furniture.

Near nine, footsteps came up the outside stair to the loft landing.

She had known they would, eventually. The loft was listed on the office plans; Vossen had handled the Halvard freight paperwork for twenty years and knew every room of the building better than Anders did. Still, when the door opened she arranged her face into the mild irritation of a seamstress interrupted — she wore her own plainest dress, her courier's papers in the pocket reading Fru Sanna Vik, dressmaker, of Drage Lane, and beside the cot stood her sewing basket with its honest clutter of pins and chalk and a half-mended shirt of Anders's she had taken up precisely for mornings like this.

The soldier who entered was young, maybe nineteen, with the chapped ears of a farm boy and a rifle he held like something borrowed. Two more waited on the landing behind him.

"Fru Vik?" He consulted a paper. "You're lodging here?"

"Since Tuesday." She did not stop sewing. The needle went in, drew thread, went in again. Her fingers were steady because she ordered them to be, and because Anders's shirt needed a real seam and deserved better than her fear. "I'm mending for the family. The mistress pays me by the piece. If you're wanting something mended yourself, you'll have to wait your turn like everyone else."

The boy blinked. He had clearly prepared questions about names, allegiances, suspicious documents, and none of his preparations accounted for a middle-aged dressmaker who sounded mildly put out. He looked at the sewing basket. He looked at the cot, the washstand, the narrow window. He looked at her — at the gray streak in her hair she had added with charcoal two nights ago, at the spectacles with plain glass she wore on a cord — and saw exactly what the papers said he would see.

"The room will need searching, fru."

"Mind the pins on the floor," said Juniper, and turned another page-corner of the shirt, and let them move through her life with their gloved hands while she sewed.

They took up the mattress. They emptied the sewing basket — the boy held up the tomato-shaped pin cushion with genuine bafflement, as though it might be ordnance — and they ran their hands along the walls and looked up the chimney, where the draft, thank God and Maren's banked fire, pushed smoke-smell into their faces and told them nothing. One of them lifted the loose plank under the cot where, until yesterday evening, Juniper's cipher keys had hidden. The space was bare. She had burned the keys in the harbor grate at midnight and scattered the ash in the tide race, and watching the soldier stare into the empty dark she felt a fierce, unholy satisfaction she would later be ashamed of.

"Nothing here," the boy reported down the stairs. And then, to her, awkwardly, almost kindly, as if the words were being extracted from him: "You'll stay where you are, fru. Until we're done."

"I've nowhere else to be," said Juniper, which was true in more ways than he could count, and sewed until the boots receded.

* * *

By ten the office was wrecked in the thorough way of official violence: not smashed, but *opened*, every drawer's contents exposed, every file's order destroyed, the accumulated paper anatomy of forty years of shipbuilding strewn across the floor in rows that meant nothing to anyone but the clipboard man, who copied serial numbers with the diligence of a monk. In the yard the typewriter sat wrapped in sacking on a handcart, confiscated, its secret long since migrated six feet upward and thirty years forward into the body of a woman drinking tea she couldn't taste.

Because Maren had brought her tea.

That was the moment Juniper would keep, afterward, when she needed it. Midmorning, while the lieutenant stood in the yard checking his list against the world, Maren had climbed the loft stairs carrying a tray — teapot, cup, a heel of yesterday's bread with butter — and had walked past the soldier on the landing as though he were a deliveryman, and had set the tray on the cot beside Juniper, and had looked at her for one second. One second, no longer. In that second her eyes went to Juniper's chest — to the exact place under the linen where the film rode against her ribs — and then to her face, and what passed between them was not a question and not an answer but something older than both: *I know what you're carrying. Sit still. I have done this before.*

Three purges, Ilsa said. Maren had been a bride during the second one. Some people learned terror. Maren had learned the other thing.

"Drink it while it's hot," Maren said aloud, for the benefit of the landing, in the voice she used for telling children to wear scarves. "Fru Vik gets faint if she skips her morning tea. Bad for her stitching."

The tray trembled — barely, a millimeter's shiver as it left her hands — and then it didn't, and she was gone down the stairs, and Juniper sat with the cup warming her palms and understood that she had just been given instructions by a woman who was not her handler, had never been her handler, and outranked every handler alive.

* * *

At eleven the lieutenant called his men together in the yard, and Juniper risked the window once more because the ending of a thing is always worth witnessing.

They had found nothing. She knew it from their posture before she knew it from her ears — the slight sag of men whose day has failed its purpose, the clipboard man flipping pages backward looking for an error that wasn't his. They had the typewriter, which was something, but a clean surplus machine proves nothing and convicts no one, and Reval — Captain Reval, whose name had begun appearing in Juniper's dreams wearing different hats — wanted proof, not furniture.

The lieutenant stood before Maren in the courtyard, and the whole household stood arrayed behind her like a painting: Anders grim and red-handed from being made to open his own strongboxes, Ilsa installed improbably in a wheeled chair at the kitchen door with a blanket over her knees and an expression of aristocratic boredom, the clerk Vossen hovering at the gate with his hat ruined in his fists.

"Frau Halvard," the lieutenant said. "A Ministry machine was sold to this office in 1904. It has been located. Its presence here, undisclosed, is itself irregular."

"It was disclosed," said Maren. "To the man who sold it to us. With a receipt. Which your men have just carried off along with my husband's correspondence, my son's accounts, and my daughter's music exercises. When you find the receipt in your own files, you may apologize."

A muscle moved in the lieutenant's jaw. Somewhere in his training there was a rule about old women and he had clearly lost the page.

"This premises remains under observation," he said. "No member of this household will leave Kalshavn. No member will approach the harbor or the vessel *Meridian*. Further inquiries will follow."

"Inquiries," repeated Ilsa from her chair, loudly, to the yard at large. "In 1889 they called it a purge. In 1898 they called it a correction. Now it's inquiries. The country improves its vocabulary and nothing else." Several soldiers developed sudden interests in their bootlaces. The lieutenant decided, visibly, that she was too old to arrest and too deaf to have heard herself, and both decisions were wrong.

They marched out. The gate shrieked again behind them — Maren would oil it tomorrow, defiantly, Juniper thought, or maybe she wouldn't, maybe the shriek was hers now, an alarm she'd bought for the price of a hinge.

* * *

The house took a long time to exhale.

Juniper came down the loft stair at noon, when the last grey-green back had vanished up the hill road, and found the kitchen full of family doing the thing families do after a storm: righting chairs, restacking papers, talking too fast about nothing. Anders was trying to sort his accounts back into months. Ilsa was dictating a complaint to no one. Liv burst in from the bakery with flour still on her sleeves, having met half the neighborhood on the way home all wearing identical expressions of terrified sympathy, and demanded to know everything three times.

Juniper stood in the doorway, the film warm against her ribs, and watched Maren pick up the nine coffee cups the soldiers had left — used, all of them, every one, not one man had refused — and carry them to the basin and wash them slowly, completely, with soap and hot water, as though washing away the morning itself.

"They drank the coffee," Juniper said quietly, when the others had drifted to other rooms.

"Men always drink the coffee." Maren didn't turn. "It's easier to break a house you've sat in. That's why I serve it. Make them comfortable in what they're doing and they have to carry the comfort home with them." She set the last cup upside down to dry and looked at Juniper at last, and her eyes went again, briefly, to the place under Juniper's ribs, and stayed there a fraction longer this time.

"They'll come back," Juniper said. "That was a first pass. The man they answer to doesn't send soldiers twice for the same errand. Next time he comes himself."

"I know." Maren folded the towel. "Anders got a note an hour ago, delivered by a boy who wouldn't say who sent it. Anonymous handwriting. It says the harbormaster's clerk is the one who talked, and it says—" she paused, and for the first time all morning her voice bent, very slightly, like a plank bearing weight— "it says our name has moved up the list. Not the end of the week. Tomorrow."

Outside, gulls wheeled over the yard screaming their one opinion. The Meridian's masts showed above the roofline, rigged and waiting, riding at anchor like a held breath.

Tomorrow.

Juniper put her hand flat against the linen over the film, and began, silently, to count the hours.
