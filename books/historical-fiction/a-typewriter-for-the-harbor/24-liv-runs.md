## 24. Liv Runs

The soldiers had been gone an hour, and the house still smelled of them — boot leather and cigarette smoke and something colder underneath, like the inside of a locked drawer. Liv stood in the doorway of the type room and looked at what they had left of it.

They had been thorough in the way her grandmother said men were thorough when they were afraid of being thought careless. Every drawer of the ship office pulled out and set upside down on the floor, the drawings unrolled and weighted open with bayonets someone had thoughtfully not used. The cabinets stood gaping. The ledgers lay in stacks that were no longer alphabetical. And in the corner, shoved aside like a piece of furniture nobody wanted, sat the typewriter.

Not their typewriter. The old ministry machine, the surplus one with the chipped H that had typed a generation of hull specifications and invoices. They had opened it — she could see where the ribbon spools had been lifted out and set back crooked, where someone with big hands had pried at the carriage and then thought better of it. But it was still here. Whatever they had come to find, they hadn't found it, and the not-finding had made them rougher with everything else.

"Liv." Her mother's voice from below, level in the way it went level only when levelness was costing something. "Come away from there."

She came away. In the kitchen, Maren was washing cups — the soldiers' cups, four of them, because Maren Halvard would sooner die than let another family's dishes sit unwashed in her sink, even a family that had turned her house inside out before breakfast. The coffee pot was already scrubbed and hanging. It was such an ordinary act that it frightened Liv more than the search had.

"Are they coming back?" she asked.

"They didn't say." Her mother set a cup in the rack. "That's usually how it works."

"Anders is at the dry dock. He doesn't know."

"He'll know when he comes home."

"He might not come home." Liv heard her own voice go sharp, and pressed on before anyone could stop her. "If they found nothing here, they'll go to the yard next. Papa's there with half the men who didn't run to the ships, and every tool in the place has the Halvard mark burned into it, and he argues — you know how he argues, Mama, he can't help it, he argues with inspectors about *rivet gauge*—"

"Liv."

"If they take him at the dock we won't even know until dark, and by dark—" She stopped, because the sentence had a shape she couldn't finish without seeing it whole.

Her mother turned off the water. For a moment the kitchen was so quiet Liv could hear the tide against the pilings under the street, the harbor breathing through the floorboards the way it always did, indifferent.

"You're thinking of going," Maren said. Not a question.

"I'm thinking of telling him."

"There's a difference between those things, and you're standing on the wrong side of it."

"I've carried messages on these docks since I was eleven years old. I know which streets the patrols walk and when they walk them. Nobody in this house knows the quay like I do right now, and everybody in this house is pretending that staying indoors is the same thing as being safe, and it isn't, Mama, it's just being *later*." The words came out faster than she meant them to, tumbling, and she hated that, hated sounding young. She made herself slow down. "Ilsa says the family survives its scatterings. But we don't survive anything if we sit in separate rooms waiting to be collected one at a time."

Maren dried her hands on her apron, slowly, looking at her daughter with an expression Liv had seen before on the faces of shipwrights studying a hull that had developed a flaw in a place no one expected. Not anger. Recalculation.

"Your father will be home by six," Maren said. "It's barely past two."

"The patrol changes at three. There's twenty minutes when the quay gate has nobody on it because the afternoon shift comes up from the fish market side." She had watched it all winter without knowing why she was watching. Now she knew.

"Liv."

"Papa would come for us." She said it simply, because it was true, and true things didn't need arguing. "He'd be out that door before his coat was on."

Her mother closed her eyes. When she opened them, she said, "Take the shawl. The grey one, Ilsa's. If a soldier stops you, you are a girl running an errand for her sick great-grandmother, and the errand is camphor ointment from the chemist on Bremer Street, and you have the money for it in your left pocket and the list in your right. Say it back."

Liv said it back. Her heart was pounding so hard she could feel it in her teeth, and beneath the fear ran something else, bright and terrible, like the first cold drop of water finding a crack in a hull: *she believed me.*

"Go the fish market way," her mother said. "And Liv — if the gate is manned, if anything is wrong, you turn around. You hear me? A message that costs the messenger isn't worth carrying. That's not cowardice. That's arithmetic."

"Yes, Mama."

"And don't run. Walking girls get waved through. Running girls get remembered."

* * *

The rain had stopped but the streets hadn't noticed; everything still dripped, gutters ticking, awnings shedding their loads in fat drops that hit the cobbles like small detonations. Liv walked fast with her chin down and the shawl up around her ears, and the city performed its broken pantomime around her.

Kalshavn in the third day of the new regime was a city holding its breath between sentences. Shops open but half-shuttered. Men standing in doorways doing nothing with tremendous concentration. The tricolor of the republic was gone from the customs house, and in its place hung nothing yet — a bare patch of wall slightly less weathered than the rest, like a scar where something had been cut away. Somebody had chalked a small anchor on the customs house steps overnight, low, near the bottom, where you'd only see it if you were looking down. Liv looked down everywhere now. Half the harbor seemed to be marking things for each other in chalk.

At the corner of Salt Lane a woman she'd known all her life — Mrs. Brandt, who sold herring and whose son had sailed on the last Halvard brigantine — caught her eye and then deliberately, elaborately, looked at the sky. Liv took the meaning and crossed to the far sidewalk. Two men in civilian coats were talking outside the chandlery, too still, coats too good, boots too military for the coats. She filed them and kept walking, counting her breaths the way her father had taught her to count fathoms: steady, steady, panic kills more divers than depth does.

The fish market was nearly empty at that hour, the stalls hosed down, gulls standing in judgment on the scales. The smell hit her like memory — brine and diesel and blood — and for one absurd moment she wanted to cry, not from fear but from the sheer ordinariness of it, the market going on as if harbors belonged to the people who worked them rather than to whoever had the guns this season.

Then the quay.

She saw the problem before she saw the gate. The twenty-minute gap she'd counted on was not a gap today. Four soldiers stood at the head of the dry dock ramp where ordinarily there'd have been one bored harbor policeman, and they weren't bored. They had a table. An actual folding table set up across the ramp, with a clerk behind it — she knew him vaguely, the harbormaster's assistant, a soft nervous man named Pell — and a ledger, and behind them, leaning against a bollard with his hands in his pockets like a man admiring the view, an officer she didn't know.

She should have turned around. Her mother's voice was very clear in her head: *if anything is wrong, you turn around.* Everything was wrong. The table was wrong. The officer's stillness was wrong.

But the dry dock was forty meters beyond the checkpoint, and she could see the cranes moving over it, and somewhere under those cranes her father was working, and if the soldiers reached him first, walking in a file with their list—

Courage outran judgment. Later she would understand that this is what courage mostly is — not the absence of the calculation, but the refusal to wait for it to finish.

She angled toward the checkpoint at a walk, an erranding girl, camphor ointment, Bremer Street, money in the left pocket.

"Halt." The soldier wasn't unkind about it, which somehow made it worse. He was young, maybe nineteen, rain-dark hair plastered under a cap two sizes big, and he held up one gloved hand with the air of a boy directing traffic for a game. "Where are you going?"

"Dry dock lane. My great-grandmother needs—" She produced the list from her right pocket, exactly as rehearsed, and heard her own voice do something strange halfway through the word *great-grandmother* — a wobble, a half-tone rise, the sound of a sixteen-year-old girl instead of a courier.

The soldier read the list upside down, which told her he couldn't really read it, and glanced past her shoulder at the clerk's table. "Name?"

"Halvard."

The word left her mouth and she heard it land, and she understood in one lurching instant what she had done. She had given them the name off the list. Their list. The one they'd ransacked her house for that morning. She watched it travel — from the young soldier's face, blank, to the clerk, Pell, whose pen stopped mid-stroke, to the officer by the bollard, whose face did not change at all, which was the most frightening thing she had ever seen.

The officer pushed off the bollard and came over unhurriedly. Up close he was younger than he'd looked from a distance and older in the eyes, with neat fair hair and the kind of politeness that had machinery behind it.

"Halvard," he repeated, pleasantly. "Of the shipyard Halvards?"

"My family builds boats," Liv said. Her mouth had gone dry. *Walking girls get waved through. Running girls get remembered.* She had walked straight into being remembered.

"Then you're exactly the person I've been hoping to meet." He smiled, and it was a real smile, warm even, and that was worse. "My name is Captain Reval. Don't look so alarmed — I don't eat children. I only want to ask you some questions, and then you can be on your way to your chemist. Sit." He gestured to a crate beside the table, and when she hesitated: "Or stand. Standing is fine. Standing is actually better — this won't take long."

Pell the clerk had become very interested in his ledger. Liv noticed that his hand, holding the pen, trembled slightly, and that he wouldn't look at her, and she thought: *he knows my name is on a paper in that book, and he knows what happens to names in that book.*

"Now," Reval said, taking out a small notebook, unhurried, courteous, a man conducting a survey. "Your father. Anders Halvard. Is he at the dry dock at present?"

"I don't keep track of Papa's hours."

"No, of course not. Daughters never do." He wrote something anyway. "And your brother? Your mother?"

"Why do you need to know where my family is?" The question came out smaller than she intended, almost a child's question, and she hated herself for it.

Reval looked at her with genuine, gentle surprise. "Because I'm making a census, Miss Halvard. Nothing more sinister than any government does. We need to know who is in Kalshavn, who is leaving, who is owed what. Your family employs many men. Many men have families. Someone must keep the lists tidy or everything falls into confusion." He turned a page. "Tell me — has anyone visited your house recently? A stranger? Perhaps a young woman you didn't recognize?"

The rain-washed street tilted very slightly. She kept her feet.

"A stranger," Reval repeated softly, watching her face the way her father watched a weld. "Someone who might have brought mail. Old mail. Mail from the ministry, perhaps."

"I deliver the mail on this dock," Liv said. "If it came through me, it wasn't a stranger."

Something flickered behind his pleasantness — respect, maybe, or appetite. "You deliver the mail," he agreed. "Yes, I'd heard that. You're rather famous on this quay, did you know? The little Halvard with the canvas satchel. Everyone trusts you." He closed the notebook. "That's not a criticism. Trust is a beautiful thing in a harbor town. Very hard to build. Very easy to spend."

Behind him, the crane over the dry dock swung a girder across the pale sky, and Liv's stomach clenched — Papa was up there, or near it, close enough that she could see the yard office window, close enough that at any moment he might come down the ramp whistling, straight into the table, the ledger, the name.

"You can go," Reval said suddenly, stepping back with a little bow. "Buy your ointment. Give my regards to your great-grandmother."

She stared at him.

"Oh, don't look so astonished. I told you — questions, then on your way." His smile deepened a fraction. "We'll talk again soon, Miss Halvard. You have my word. In fact—" he raised his voice slightly, pleasantly, so that the soldiers and the clerk and everyone on the quay could hear it— "*someone* should walk you home afterward. It's getting late for a girl alone. Corporal, see that the Halvard child gets safely back up the hill once she's finished her shopping. Take your time, though. No hurry at all."

* * *

She bought the camphor ointment. It cost her eleven öre and she paid it with fingers that wouldn't quite work, and the chemist wrapped the jar and asked after Ilsa and Liv said *she's well* in a voice that came from somewhere very far away.

Corporal Voss — that was his name, he offered it shyly, as if apologizing — walked ten paces behind her the whole length of the quay and up Harbor Hill, hands clasped behind him like a man strolling off his dinner. Twice she slowed, testing. He slowed. Once she stopped entirely at a window and pretended to study a hat. In the glass, warped and silvered, she watched him stop too, patient as weather.

They think I'm bait, she realized. They're not following me. They're following whoever comes to me.

The thought arrived whole and freezing, and with it came its twin: Papa would have to come to her. Because she wasn't home yet, and Mama would be watching the clock, and when the clock passed six and then seven, Anders Halvard would come out of the dry dock asking questions in that voice of his, and every road he took to find her would lead past that folding table and that ledger and that courteous man with the notebook.

She climbed the last stretch of hill with the ointment jar knocking against her ribs and her mind running ahead of her feet like water finding a channel. The chalk anchor on the customs house steps. Mrs. Brandt and her elaborate sky. The fish market way, empty at three, crowded with carts at five. Pell's trembling pen — a man drowning and grabbing at whatever floated, even a girl's name.

There was still time. Barely. If she got word to the yard — not to Papa, they'd expect Papa, they were *fishing* for Papa — but to Joren, the old rigger, who drank at the Anchor and Chain and owed the Halvards thirty years of small kindnesses. If Joren walked out the yard's back gate by the coal shed and cut along the sea wall before the evening shift change—

At her own gate she stopped. Voss stopped too, at a respectful distance, and touched his cap. "Home safe, miss," he said, and for one strange moment his young face did something complicated — not quite guilt, not quite apology, the look of a boy who has begun to suspect what he is carrying and hasn't yet decided whether to put it down.

Liv stood in the gateway with Ilsa's grey shawl dripping rainwater onto the stones, and behind her the harbor ticked and dripped and breathed, and somewhere below, her father was finishing his day's work with no idea that the net had already closed around his daughter.

She turned to the corporal and smiled the sweetest smile she owned.

"Thank you for walking me," she said. "You must be thirsty. Wait right there — I'll bring you out a cup of coffee."

And she went inside to raise the alarm.
