## 25. Negotiation at the Dry Dock

They brought the message to Anders at the dry dock in a bucket.

That was how it arrived — not by word of mouth, not by note, but by a boy of perhaps ten who walked the length of the yard carrying an empty wooden bucket as though he had been sent to fetch water, and who set it down at Anders's feet without meeting his eyes and said, "There's paper under the false bottom," and then walked away again before Anders could ask a single question.

Anders waited until he was alone in the tool shed to kneel and pry up the slats. The folded sheet inside was cheap pulp stock, the kind sold at the chandlery on Quay Street, unmarked but for a single line in a cramped, careful hand:

*The girl is at the customs house on the mole. They will hold her until morning unless someone comes for her tonight. Come alone. Come with what you can carry.*

He read it three times. The handwriting meant nothing to him. The knowledge inside it meant everything — Liv had been detained at the checkpoint by the coal yard at four o'clock that afternoon; Maren had come to the dry dock white-faced with the news, wringing her apron in both fists while the men around them pretended not to hear; and now here was a stranger, some clerk or soldier's mistress or simply a decent frightened person, telling him where she sat and what it would cost to get her back.

*Come with what you can carry.*

He understood at once what could be carried. The yard had been dying for two years, and the coup had finished the job: half the workforce gone to the ships or gone inland, the big orders cancelled, the sheds standing half-empty like mouths with teeth missing. But there remained the tools. The good ones — the Swedish augers, the adzes with handles worn to the shape of his grandfather's grip, the boxwood planes his father had bought from a bankrupt Copenhagen firm in 1898 and never once regretted. There remained the cashbox under the floor of the office: four hundred and sixty kroner, most of it earmarked for wages they would probably never pay. And there remained the last thing of value, the thing he had sworn over his father's coffin he would not sell: the steam plant. The little engine that drove the saws, the pride of the yard, older than he was.

They would not take the steam plant tonight. He would not let himself think about whether they would take it eventually.

He told no one. That was the hardest part — walking past Maren in the yard gate, past her red-rimmed eyes, and saying only, "I'm going into town. I'll have news by midnight." She looked at him the way mothers look at sons who are lying badly, and said nothing, because she had raised him to know that some mercies are silence.

He filled a canvas sack in the tool shed with hands that shook until he noticed them shaking and made them stop. Discipline was a Halvard inheritance too. Augers first — they were the soul of the trade, and if he was going to sell the family's soul he might as well lead with it. Then the adzes. Then the planes wrapped in sacking, each one named silently in his head as it went into the dark: *Father's smoothing plane. Grandfather's jack. The little rabbet Ilsa bought me when I was twelve and cut myself so badly the scar still shows.* The cashbox last, heavy against his hip beneath his coat.

The walk along the mole took eleven minutes. He counted them without meaning to. The rain had stopped and left the cobbles shining under the arc lamps, and the lamps themselves seemed wrong somehow — brighter than usual, as if the regime had decided that darkness was now a form of dissent. Soldiers stood at intervals along the quay, greatcoated and motionless, their breath rising in slow ghosts. One of them watched Anders pass and did not move, and the not-moving was worse than a challenge would have been.

*Come alone.* He had come alone.

The customs house on the mole was a squat brick building with a clock whose hands had been frozen at ten past six since before the war scare of '08. Two guards flanked the door. A lamp burned in the ground-floor window, and through the glass Anders could see a desk, a man seated behind it writing, and — his heart went off like a dropped hammer — the top of Liv's head, visible above the back of a bench, her braids unmistakable even from the street, her shoulders very still in the way of someone trying to be furniture.

"State your business," said the nearer guard. Young. Vessmarki accent scrubbed almost clean, but not quite — a coastal boy, maybe even a Kalshavn boy, wearing another country's uniform.

"My daughter is being held inside. I've come to speak to whoever is in charge."

"There's no one held inside. There's a processing interview underway."

"Then I'll wait for it to conclude." Anders kept his voice level, kept his hands loose at his sides. "I'm Anders Halvard. Of the Halvard shipyard. My family has built boats on this harbor for ninety years, and my daughter has delivered messages on this quay since she was old enough to read. Whatever your list says about us, your own officers will confirm she's a child who runs errands."

The guard hesitated. It was a small hesitation, but Anders had spent thirty years reading men across a workbench, and he knew what it meant: the boy had orders that were not entirely clear to him, and orders that are unclear weigh less than the fear of a superior's displeasure. He pressed the advantage gently.

"Who is conducting the interview?"

"Captain Reval's office. The captain is not present. Lieutenant Sorren has the desk."

"Then may I speak with Lieutenant Sorren?"

Another hesitation, longer this time. Then the guard turned, opened the door, and called something inside, and the door stayed open, and that open door was itself an answer. They wanted him to come in. Someone had decided hours ago that a Halvard should be invited rather than summoned, and the difference between those two words was going to cost him everything he carried.

Inside smelled of ink and cold tea and wet wool. The room was bare except for the desk, two benches, and a filing cabinet that stood against the wall like a witness refusing to testify. Lieutenant Sorren was a narrow man with spectacles and the patient face of an accountant, and he rose when Anders entered and extended his hand as if this were a parish meeting.

"Herr Halvard. Please sit. Your daughter is quite all right — we're just clarifying some details of her movements."

Liv sat on the bench with her hands folded in her lap, and when her eyes found Anders's they were dry, and furious, and terrified in equal measure, and she gave him one tiny shake of the head — *don't* — which he pretended not to see, because pretending not to see things was rapidly becoming his primary skill.

"She's sixteen," Anders said. "Her movements consist of delivering bread and letters. I trust the clarification hasn't tired her."

"Not at all." Sorren sat, laced his fingers. "She's been very helpful. Haven't you, Frøken Halvard?"

"Yes, sir," Liv said, in a flat voice Anders had never heard her use, and hated hearing now.

"The situation is this," Sorren continued pleasantly. "Under the security provisions of the new administration, movement within the harbor district requires registration. Your daughter was unable to produce registration papers. This is not an accusation — the provisions are new, and confusion is natural. However." He paused, and the pause was a craftsman's pause; whatever else Sorren was, he was skilled. "There is also the matter of the shipyard. An inventory is being prepared. Certain materials — machinery, tools, currency held in commercial premises — will need to be assessed for requisition or tax. The assessment can proceed formally, through the requisition office, which is currently processing requests in the order received. Or—" the smallest shrug, "—matters can sometimes be clarified informally, between reasonable men, tonight."

There it was. Not even disguised. Anders felt a heat rise in his chest that had nothing to do with shame and everything to do with the animal wish to put his hands around something, and he mastered it the way he mastered a plank that wanted to spring — slowly, with weight applied along the grain.

"You want the yard's tools," he said.

"I want the yard assessed fairly," Sorren said, with a smile that acknowledged they both understood the transaction perfectly and preferred to keep its receipt unwritten. "Tools, certainly. Any cash on commercial premises would naturally complicate the assessment. Machinery especially — the steam plant, I believe? A fine piece of equipment. The army has use for such things."

"The steam plant is not for sale."

Sorren spread his hands, all reasonableness. "Nothing is for sale, Herr Halvard. Things are either requisitioned or they are clarified. Requisitioned items go to the army depot and are logged, and the logging process is thorough but slow, and meanwhile the yard stands idle, and idle yards attract attention. Clarified arrangements are simpler. Faster. Better for everyone." His eyes flicked, briefly, to Liv. "Especially for families with daughters who forget their papers."

Anders thought of his father. He thought of the photograph on the office wall, the two of them squinting into the sun beside the hull of the *Astrid*, his father's hand on his shoulder, the whole harbor behind them believing in tomorrow. He thought of every story Ilsa told about the purge of '94, the neighbors who informed, the neighbors who bargained, the ones who stood straight and were taken away, and how the family had survived not by its bargains but by its scatterings — and he set all of it aside, every scrap of inherited pride, because sixteen years of freckles and stubbornness were sitting three feet away on a bench, and there was no arithmetic in the world that balanced against that.

"How much," he said.

"A fair contribution to the clarification fund," Sorren said delicately. "Say — five hundred kroner. And the hand tools. The assessment office will want examples of the yard's output, you understand. Representative samples."

Five hundred. He had four hundred and sixty, and the tools, and the tools were worth far more than forty kroner. Sorren knew it too, or guessed it; the figure had been chosen to leave a gap that could only be closed by adding something else, and the something else was obvious to everyone in the room except, apparently, anyone who would say it aloud.

"The cash I have," Anders said. "Four hundred and sixty. The tools are in the sack outside. That is what I can carry, and it is what I carry."

Sorren considered him over the spectacles. Outside, boots crossed the quay; somewhere a whistle blew twice and went silent. When the lieutenant spoke again, his voice had cooled by several degrees, the warmth revealed as costume.

"Herr Halvard, I admire loyalty. But let me describe the alternative plainly, since you seem to prefer plainness. Tomorrow morning, Captain Reval processes the arrest lists. Names are on those lists. Whether a name stays on a list or comes off a list is, again, a matter of clarification. A sixteen-year-old girl held overnight for questioning becomes, by morning, a matter of record, and matters of record go to the captain, and the captain is a thorough man." He removed his spectacles, polished them on his sleeve, replaced them. "I am offering you the cheapest evening of your life."

The silence stretched. Liv was looking at the floor, her jaw tight, and Anders understood suddenly that she had heard every word, that they had not even bothered to send her from the room, that her humiliation was intended as seasoning for his. Something in him went quiet then — not calm, but the particular dead-calm of deep water, below where storms reach.

"I'll bring the rest tomorrow," he heard himself say. "In goods. Bronze fastenings, seasoned oak stock, rigging chain. You can send a man to value it. But my daughter walks out of this door tonight, with me, and no name of mine goes on any list tonight. Those are the terms, and after tonight there is no second offer, because after tonight there will be nothing left in the yard worth clarifying and no Halvard fool enough to bring it."

It was the longest speech he had made in a year, and his voice cracked on the last words, and he hated himself for the crack more than for anything else — more than the sack of his father's tools waiting outside in the rain-dark, more than the cashbox growing damp against his ribs. Sorren studied him for a long moment, weighing, and then nodded once, briskly, a man concluding satisfactory business.

"Done. Sign nothing — there's nothing to sign. That's the beauty of informal arrangements." He stood and offered his hand again, and Anders did not take it, and Sorren withdrew it without offense, as if he had expected exactly that and respected it slightly.

At the door, Anders stopped. "Who wrote to me?" he said. "The note in the bucket."

Sorren's face did something very small and very controlled. "I couldn't say. The captain's office receives many communications." And then, softer, almost inaudible, in the tone of a man setting down a weight he'd carried too long: "Go home, Herr Halvard. Take your daughter home. It's late for children to be out."

* * *

They walked the length of the mole without speaking. Halfway along, Liv's composure failed her all at once, the way a knot fails — not gradually but completely — and she grabbed his arm and buried her face in his sleeve and shook, and Anders put the sack down on the wet cobbles and held her with both arms and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that wasn't either a lie or a wound.

"They asked me who I deliver letters to," she whispered into his coat. "Over and over. Names, names. I said the baker, the widow Sandvik, the harbormaster's office. I didn't say anything wrong, Papa. Did I say anything wrong?"

"You said nothing wrong." He tilted her chin up. Her eyes were swollen and blazing. "You were braver in that room than I was, and I paid money to prove it. Don't ever tell your grandmother I said that."

A ghost of a grin crossed her face and died. "What did you give them?"

"Old tools. We have others."

"We don't, though. I've seen the shed. Those were the good ones —"

"Liv."

She fell silent. She was sixteen and sharp as a new blade and she understood exactly what lay in that sack, and he watched her understand it, watch the knowledge settle on her the way snow settles — softly, and everywhere, and with a weight that accumulates. She picked up one end of the sack without being asked, and they carried it together through the lamplight, father and daughter, thieves in their own yard.

* * *

Maren met them at the gate. She looked at Liv first — mothers always look at the child first — and touched her face with both hands, and asked no questions, which was its own kind of interrogation and its own kind of mercy. Then she looked at the sack, and at Anders's face above it, and whatever she saw there made her reach out and grip his forearm hard, once, the way one grips a beam to test whether it still bears load.

"It holds," she said quietly. It might have meant him. It might have meant anything.

Inside, the kitchen was warm and smelled of the day's baking gone cold. Ilsa's door was closed, a thread of lamplight beneath it. Juniper sat at the long table mending a sailcloth bag, needle flashing, and she looked up when they came in, and her eyes went from Liv to the sagging sack to Anders's hands, which he realized only then were trembling again, openly, uselessly, in front of everyone.

He set the sack on the table. It landed with the sound of metal on wood, the sound of an inventory, and in the silence after it he heard clearly, as if spoken aloud, everything the sound contained: the yard's future sold by the pound. He untied the sacking and laid the tools out in rows, because laying them out was the last act of ownership available to him, and naming them was the last act of grief.

"Grandfather's augers," he said. His voice came out strange and level, a voice from underwater. "His adze. Father's planes. The rabbet Aunt Ilsa gave me." He stopped. Sat down heavily. Put his face in his hands, briefly, and took it away again, because a man who starts cannot always stop.

Nobody spoke. Then Juniper reached across the table, past the rows of dead steel, and rested her fingers for a moment on the back of his wrist — light as a moth, gone again in a breath — and said, in her low courier's voice, "Whatever was in that sack, it bought back something that doesn't weigh anything. You got the better price."

He wanted to believe her. He looked at her — this stranger who was not a stranger, this woman with typist's calluses and a sailor's steady gaze — and he thought, not for the first time and with far more force than before: *she knows what these people are. Whoever she carries for, whoever she runs from, she knows the shape of the machine we've just fed.* And he thought also: *she lied to that lieutenant once herself, somewhere, or she'll lie to one soon, and she'll do it better than I did.*

"Papa," Liv said. She had gone very pale. "Papa — when they searched my pockets at the checkpoint, there was a letter in my coat. Not one of mine. Someone must have slipped it in at the bakery queue — a folded paper, sealed, with a ministry stamp. The lieutenant took it and read it and after that they stopped asking me questions and started being polite. Too polite." Her voice thinned. "I think that's why they held me. I think the letter was the reason, and I don't know who put it in my pocket, or what it said."

The kitchen went cold in a way the stove had nothing to do with. Anders saw Maren's hands stop moving. Across the table, Juniper had risen halfway out of her chair without seeming to notice she'd done it, and her face had changed — not to fear, exactly, but to recognition, the look of a navigator who has just seen a coastline appear where the chart says open water.

"What stamp," Juniper said quietly. "Describe the seal exactly."

Anders watched his daughter frown, watching her memory work, and understood that the evening's price had not yet finished being counted — that somewhere in a ministry office, on a machine he had never seen, someone had typed his family into danger with a keystroke apiece, and that the woman at his table knew more about that machine than she was saying.

"Blue wax," Liv said. "With a crown broken by a diagonal line. And initials at the bottom — T.V., I think. Typed, not signed."

Juniper sat down slowly, all the way, like a mast settling.

"T.V.," she repeated, to no one. "Tove Vik. She used to sit two desks from Oskar."

Outside, the harbor clock began to strike eleven, each note falling into the wet air like something dropped from a height, and no one in the Halvard kitchen moved to count the bells.
