## 27. A Night of Small Preparations

### Maren

Maren Halvard had baked bread in this kitchen through two purges, one bankruptcy, and the winter the harbor froze solid enough to walk on, and she knew the difference between a house that was waiting for morning and a house that was pretending to.

Tonight the house was waiting. She could hear it in the way her sons moved — too carefully, too quietly, as though grief were a sleeping thing on the second floor. She could hear it in the clock, which she had not wound since Tuesday because its tick suddenly seemed like an accusation.

So she baked.

She kneaded at the long table by lamplight, flour to her elbows, because bread was the one language she spoke that could not be intercepted, censored, or misfiled. Four loaves tonight instead of two. Six, then, when she thought of the dock families who would come asking at dawn with their questions dressed up as errands. The ovens of a shipwright's wife had always been the harbor's unofficial telegraph; you could read the whole city's fear in what women asked her to bake.

The letter from Juniper's coat pocket had burned well. Maren had never told the girl she'd seen it — the corner of sealed paper peeking out like a wound — and she never would. Some mercies were better administered without the patient's knowledge.

She heard footsteps on the stairs and did not turn. "There's milk on the stove," she said. "It's for you or it's for the cat, depending on who's standing there."

"It's me." Liv came down barefoot, hair loose, carrying her great-grandmother's shawl folded over both arms like something consecrated. "I couldn't sleep."

"Then your hands might as well work." Maren nodded at the dough. "Punch it. It's been rising all evening and getting ideas above itself."

Liv punched it. Then, softer: "Grandmother says we don't choose who leaves."

"Your grandmother," Maren said, "has been saying true things since before this country existed. Go on."

"But if the berth is mine—"

"The berth is nobody's until it is somebody's, and that arithmetic is not yours to carry. It isn't even mine." Maren shaped a loaf with quick, practiced turns, not looking at her daughter. That was the trick of hard conversations in this family — you had them sideways, hands busy, eyes on anything but each other. "Your job tomorrow is to be where you're told to be, when you're told to be there. Can you do that?"

Liv was quiet a moment. "Juniper could do that," she said. "Carry things. Not look at them."

"Yes." Maren set the loaf down and finally looked at her youngest child, sixteen years old and already shaped like the rest of them, all keel and stubbornness. "And look how much good it's done her, being able to. Wash your hands. Take Ilsa her milk. And Liv — whatever happens at that quay tomorrow—" she stopped herself, because *whatever happens* was a door she didn't want either of them walking through. "Take the shawl. Cold comes off that water even in summer."

Liv went. Maren stood alone in the kitchen with six loaves proofing under cloth and let herself, just once, press both flour-white hands flat against the table and bow her head over them, the way her own mother had done during the purge of '89, the way women in this kitchen had done for a hundred years.

Then she straightened, wiped her hands, and went to mend the coat with the torn shoulder seam, because ruin or no ruin, a Halvard did not leave the house looking poor.

### Anders

In the ship office, Anders sharpened knives he would never use.

He knew it and the knives knew it. A man did not fight an army with a filleting blade and a boat-builder's adze. But his father had sharpened them in '94, and his grandfather in the year of the first coup, and there was something in the whetstone's slow circular whisper that organized a man's thoughts the way nothing else did. You could not sharpen a knife while lying to yourself. The stone found every lie in your hands and ground it flat.

Tomorrow the Meridian sailed at dawn tide, and Reval held the manifest, and somewhere in the dark between here and the quay were soldiers with a list that had *Halvard* written on it in a ministry hand.

He tested the edge against his thumb. Drew no blood. Good enough for bread, anyway.

Through the window he could see the yard, silvered with moonlight, silent in a way it had never been silent in forty years. Half his men had gone to the ships. Two more had not come back from lunch. The molds stood empty along the shed wall like rib cages, and the half-built ketch in Dry Dock Three looked — he allowed himself the thought only because no one could see him — like a body waiting to be claimed.

A sound at the door. He turned with the knife still in his hand and saw Juniper framed in the doorway, dressed, bag packed, clearly on her way to somewhere she hadn't announced.

"You're leaving," he said. Not a question.

"I'm checking the east road. If there are patrols before first light, we need to know now, not at the quay." She hesitated. "I wanted to say—" 

"Don't." He set the knife down. "We're not a family that does farewells indoors. Bad luck. My mother's rule."

"She has a lot of rules."

"She's outlived three governments. Her rules have a certain record." He looked at this woman who had arrived at their door a stranger with mail twelve days ago and who now moved through the house like water finding its old channel. He had tested her with a scarf joint her first week and she had cut it clean, and he had known then what he knew now: some people you hired, and some people you simply recognized. "Eriksen. The joint you cut, that first day. Who taught you?"

"My mother," she said. "Before."

He nodded slowly. "Good teacher."

"She'd have liked you," Juniper said, and was gone down the stairs before he could answer, boots quiet on the boards, the front door closing behind her with barely a sound.

Anders stood a moment in the lamplight. Then he took up the adze — not the knives, the adze, the tool that made ships, the tool that took rough wood and gave it a shape that could survive open water — and carried it downstairs to wrap it in sacking. Whatever happened tomorrow, the yard's tools were going into hiding in the church cellar, because regimes fell and families scattered, but tools buried properly waited, patient as seeds, for the hands that would come back for them.

His grandfather's rule. Also, he suspected, his mother's.

Also, he admitted to the empty office, his own.

### Ilsa

Upstairs, Ilsa Halvard sat propped against her pillows by lamplight, writing farewell letters on the typewriter balanced across her knees, and enjoying herself far more than was seemly.

"To Director Kolb," she dictated aloud to herself, hunting the keys with one crooked finger, "*I regret to inform you that I will be unavailable for arrest, having died in 1911 of extreme old age and disappointment in my government. Please direct future correspondence to the sea.*"

The typewriter clattered. Somewhere below, the floorboards creaked under Anders's heavy pacing. She had heard everything today — she always heard everything; deafness had taken only the polite frequencies — the argument about the berth, the silence after it, Maren crying only once and briefly, into bread dough, where tears were invisible.

Three purges. That was her total. Eighty-one years, and three times she had watched this country eat its own children and spit out the bones with new flags sewn over the stains. The Russians had come for names; the liberals had come for ledgers; and now these bright new men in their gray coats had come for lists. Always paper, in the end. Men with guns were merely men with paper's errands.

Her letters were stacked by the bed: one for each grandchild, one for Liv, two for neighbors, one — short, unsentimental, entirely sincere — for the Republic of Vessmark itself, addressed to whichever ministry survived longest, informing it that she forgave it nothing.

She pulled a fresh sheet and rolled it in. Her finger hovered.

Then she typed, slowly, the family's oldest sentence, the one her father had taught her, the one carved somewhere into every Halvard who ever lived:

*WE BUILD SHIPS SO PEOPLE CAN LEAVE.*

She looked at it a long time.

"We do not choose who leaves," she finished aloud. "Even when they choose wrong. Even when they choose us last." Her voice cracked only slightly on the last word, and she glared at the empty room as if daring it to comment.

From the yard below came the small sounds of Anders burying tools. From the kitchen, the smell of bread. From the harbor, faintly, the foghorn of the Meridian testing itself for dawn.

Ilsa blew out her lamp, lay back against her pillows, and slept better than anyone else in the house.

### Liv

Liv packed one bag and unpacked it four times.

The problem was that she kept packing for the life she was supposed to be leaving and unpacking for the life she refused to believe was ending. A dress. Her drawing pencils — no, the pencils stayed, the pencils were for next week, the pencils were for the commission Anders had promised her, sketches of the new ketch for the yard's catalogue. She put the pencils back in the drawer.

She put them in the bag again.

On top of the wardrobe lay her great-grandmother's shawl, and beneath it, wrapped in oilcloth, the thing she had not shown anyone: three photographs, stolen off a soldier's dropped satchel two weeks ago near the dry dock, showing the shipyard gate, the Meridian's moorings, and — she had studied it until her eyes ached — a man in civilian clothes pointing at the Halvard office windows. She still didn't know why she'd taken them. Instinct, maybe. Or the courier's blood she apparently had in her somewhere, the same blood as Juniper's, the take-this-and-run blood.

She sat on the floor with her back against the bed and held the oilcloth packet and listened to the house.

Every house had a night voice, Liv thought. This one creaked in the third stair, ticked in the dead clock, breathed in the walls like a ship at anchor — because it was built of ship timber, every joist of it, masts and keels in a former life, and it remembered the sea even on land. Tomorrow she might never hear it again. Tomorrow there was one berth and five of them and a grandmother who had already decided something, because Ilsa had kissed her forehead at supper and held it a half-second longer than usual, and Ilsa never held anything longer than necessary except opinions.

Liv unwrapped the photographs one last time and slid them inside the lining of the shawl. If she ran, she'd run with evidence. If she was searched — well. No one searched a girl carrying her great-grandmother's shawl. That was the other thing the blood taught you: the best hiding place in the world was inside something that made people feel tender.

She got into bed without undressing and lay listening to the house breathe, memorizing it, the way you memorized a face at a station platform.

### Juniper

By the east road, Juniper counted patrol lanterns and committed their rhythm to memory, and then, on the walk back, permitted herself ten minutes of a different discipline entirely.

Mending.

She sat in the dark of the loft above the boatshed with her coat across her knees, working a needle by feel, repairing the inner seams where the dispatch pouches had been sewn years ago and were now, gloriously, empty — the coded papers gone, delivered, burned, irrelevant. What remained strapped against her ribs was a strip of film worth more than everyone she loved, and it pressed against her sternum with every breath like a second heartbeat, and she had learned, these past weeks, to sleep beside it, run beside it, laugh at Maren's jokes beside it.

The needle moved. Thread whispered through cloth.

This was what her mother had done on nights before borders, she remembered suddenly — she had forgotten for twenty years, and here it was whole: her mother sewing money and papers into hems by candlelight, humming, calm as Sunday, while young Juniper watched and understood nothing except that Mama's hands never shook when they were working. Fear lived in the face and the voice. The hands belonged to the mission.

Juniper's hands did not shake now. She noted this with a kind of clinical wonder. Tomorrow she would stand at a gangway and give away a berth that five people needed, or refuse to give it, or watch soldiers take it, or — the plan she had told no one, the plan that lived only in the film's cold weight against her ribs — step forward herself and become the trade. Her hands would hold steady for all of it. They had been trained for exactly this night, by a smuggler woman and a dying clerk and a family that fed her without questions, and she was, she realized, grateful in a way that had no bottom to it.

Below her, in the boatshed, Anders was wrapping tools. Across the yard, the house glowed with one lamp — Ilsa's, then out. In the kitchen window, Maren's shadow moved among the loaves.

Ten minutes ended. She tied off the thread, bit it clean, and folded the needle away.

Then she heard, distantly, from the direction of the harbor gates, a sound she had been dreading for two days: engines. Not one engine. Several, in formation, moving slowly, the way authority moved when it wanted to be heard.

Juniper closed her coat over the film and stood up in the dark.

### Reval

Captain Reval did not sleep, because he had never seen the need.

He sat in the requisitioned harbormaster's office with the lamps lit and the manifests squared before him and reviewed the morning the way a surgeon reviewed anatomy: the gangway guard doubled at 0400; the Meridian cleared to sail but not to disembark passengers until his officers confirmed identities against the list; the Halvard arrests scheduled for 0430, before the family could disperse but late enough that the courier — and she was in that house, he would have staked his career on it, he *had* staked his career on it — would make her move in the grey confusion between.

On the blotter lay the item that interested him most: a report from the checkpoint clerk, a soft-handed little man named Pell, noting that a young woman matching no description had passed the east road at 2200 hours, walking fast, and returned at midnight.

Walking fast, returning at midnight. Checking roads meant planning routes. Planning routes meant departure. Departure meant she would be at that gangway, because couriers were creatures of completion, and a courier who abandoned her delivery was no longer a courier but merely a frightened woman, and frightened women were easy, and Reval did not want easy. He wanted the film. And he wanted her to hand it to him believing she had won something, because people who believed they had won told you where the copies were.

He wrote one line in his neat, upright hand — *Proceed as scheduled. Let the tide do our work.* — and rang for the duty officer.

Outside the window, Kalshavn lay black and quiet under a thinning moon, the harbor lights burning low, the Meridian a pale shape at her moorings with steam beginning, very faintly, to rise from her funnel against the stars.

Reval checked his watch, aligned his pens, and settled in to wait for dawn — courteous, meticulous, and utterly certain, as he had been all his life, that order would arrive on schedule.

At 0347, the harbor gate mechanism seized, and nothing in Kalshavn arrived on schedule again.
