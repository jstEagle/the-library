## 30. What Ilsa Decides

Ilsa Halvard had not left her bed for the better part of two years, and she intended to leave it now in the manner of a general taking a field: slowly, with complaint, and on her own terms.

The trouble was her legs. They had opinions of their own these days, and their opinion was *no*. She sat up against her pillows, gripped the iron bedframe with both hands — hands still strong, thank God, even if they'd begun to look like her mother's, all knuckle and map-vein — and addressed them directly.

"You will hold," she told them. "You have held through three purges, two shipwrecks, and forty-one years of marriage to a man who whistled off-key. You will hold for one more hour."

Her legs, characteristically, said nothing. But when she swung them over the edge of the bed and stood, they held.

She dressed herself without ringing for anyone. This took a quarter of an hour and cost her two rests against the washstand, but she would not be carried into this room like cargo. The Halvards did not ship cargo. The Halvards built ships, and she was going to walk into that kitchen under her own tonnage or not at all.

She chose the black wool dress. Not mourning black — she was not dead yet and resented the suggestion — but the good black, the one with the high collar that made her look like what she was: the last living person in Vessmark who remembered the country before it learned to eat its own children.

In the drawer of her nightstand lay six letters, sealed, addressed in her own hand, written over four nights on the machine downstairs in the office — the old ministry typewriter with its stubborn capital T and its habit of striking slightly too hard, so that every page came out looking angry. Good. Let them look angry. They were.

She tucked the bundle into the pocket sewn into her skirt — Maren's doing, years ago, when Ilsa began refusing to be parted from her papers even at night. Her granddaughter-in-law thought it was sentiment. It was not sentiment. It was inventory.

Then she opened her door and called down the stairwell in the voice that had once been heard across the whole of the fitting shed during a storm.

"Supper is cancelled. Come to the kitchen. All of you. And bring the boy from the loft — yes, the courier girl too, she eats here now whether anyone has said so out loud or not."

* * *

They came the way people come when summoned by the dying: quickly, guiltily, arranging their faces.

Maren first, wiping flour from her hands, her eyes doing that quick inventory of Ilsa's face that she thought nobody noticed — cataloguing color, breath, the steadiness of the hands. Anders behind her, sleeves rolled, sawdust still in his hair, looking like a man who had been interrupted mid-drowning. Liv skidding in last in her dock shoes, smelling of tar and cold air, sixteen years old and vibrating like a struck cable. And Juniper Eriksen, hanging back by the door as she always did, one shoulder toward the wall — the stowaway's posture, Ilsa noted, twelve years and no one had trained it out of her yet. Perhaps because no one had tried. Or perhaps because everyone understood it wasn't about doors.

"Sit," Ilsa said. "Not you, Liv. Open the window. If I'm going to say this, I'll have air, and if any of you faint, I want somewhere to throw you."

Liv got the window open. Evening came in off the harbor — gull-cry, the groan of the dry dock, and underneath it, faint but unmistakable, the sound of boots on the quay road. More boots than there should have been. Nobody remarked on it. That was how Kalshavn spoke now: in things it declined to remark upon.

"Three times in my life," Ilsa began, standing at the head of the table because sitting would come soon enough and forever after, "this family has been scattered. The first time I was nine years old. My father put my mother and me on a boat to Rønne with nothing but a ham and a hymnal, and he stayed behind because someone had to mind the yard, and the men who came for him were neighbors he'd built boats for." She let that sit. "He survived. He never sang again."

"The second time I was thirty. My brother Halvard the younger — you all eat off his plates and don't know it — went east ahead of the police, and we didn't learn for eleven years whether he'd lived. He had. He wrote to us from a town whose name I still can't pronounce, and the letter took fourteen months, and when it arrived my mother read it aloud twice and then put it away and never mentioned him again. Because hope, in this family, is something we keep in a locked drawer like everything else worth stealing."

Juniper's hand had gone, very slightly, toward her coat pocket. Ilsa saw it. She saw everything; it was the only sport left available to her.

"And the third time," she said, "was 1898, when a woman named Signe Eriksen smuggled my nephew's son out of this harbor inside a crate marked *tarred rope*, and was never thanked, and never asked to be thanked, and died poor in a country not her own. Some of you know who inherited that debt." She looked at Juniper then, straight on, and watched the young woman's jaw set itself like a keel plate. "Some of you are the debt."

"Ilsa," Maren began gently.

"Maren. I am ninety-one years old and I am talking. You'll get your turn at ninety-one, God help this family."

She laid both hands flat on the table. The knuckles were enormous. They had caulked hulls before women were allowed to hold caulking mallets, back when she'd done it wearing her father's gloves and daring anyone to comment.

"Now. There is one berth left on that ship."

Silence. Even Liv stopped fidgeting.

"And I have listened — don't think I haven't heard, walls in this house are older than the regime and twice as leaky — I have heard each of you argue for somebody else. Anders wants me gone. Maren wants Liv gone. Liv wants everybody gone, which is sweet and arithmetically impossible. And I expect the courier girl wants us all gone and herself drowned, out of some notion of honor she picked up from a government that lasted eleven weeks." A pause. "No offense meant to the government. It did its best, the way a drowning man does his best, which is to say it grabbed at everything and saved no one."

"Ilsa—" Anders started.

"I am not finished, and I am *standing*, which should tell you the gravity of the situation."

She drew a breath. It caught a little on the way down; she rode past it.

"I am not going."

The word landed in the kitchen like dropped ironware.

"I am ninety-one years old. My heart keeps time like a drunk drummer. I cannot walk to the gate without resting, let alone climb a gangway in front of soldiers who enjoy their work. If you spend the berth on me, you will have bought me three days of sea-sickness and a death in a foreign boarding house among strangers who will bury me under a name they mispronounce. That is not rescue. That is *shipping*." She smiled, thin and bright as a knife drawn along a strop. "And I did not marry into a family of shipwrights to be shipped like a barrel of herring."

"But—" Liv's voice cracked. "Grandmother—"

"Liv Halvard. Come here."

Liv came around the table. Ilsa took her granddaughter's face in both hands — the same face, God help them, as her mother's, the same stubborn chin — and held it.

"When your father was small, I told him the rule of this house, and he thought I was being poetic, which is the great danger of poets. So I will tell you, and you will remember it when the wind gets up. *We build ships so that people can leave. We do not choose who leaves.*" She turned Liv's face toward the others. "That means we do not choose by rank, or by usefulness, or by who loves whom loudest. It means the youngest goes, because the youngest carries the most years. It has always meant that. Your great-grandfather sent his own mother on the first boat out in '49 and rowed back alone, and he was right, and he cried about it for the rest of his life, and he was *right*."

"She's right," Anders said quietly, and Ilsa could hear in his voice that he hated it.

"Of course I'm right. I've been right since 1874; ask anyone."

* * *

It was after that — after Maren had pressed tea on everyone and nobody drank it, after Liv had fled upstairs with suspicious speed and returned with reddened eyes and her composure badly repaired — that Ilsa delivered the second item of business, the one she'd been saving like brandy.

"There is a bundle in my pocket," she announced. "Six letters. One for each of you, including the stowaway, who will stop pretending she isn't family or I shall haunt her specifically."

Juniper blinked. For the first time since arriving, the courier's professional mask slipped entirely, and underneath it was just a young woman with wet eyes and no idea where to put her hands.

"You wrote letters?" Maren said. "When?"

"Four nights ago. On the office machine." Ilsa allowed herself the full pleasure of the reveal, drawing it out like a good yarn. "The old ministry typewriter. The one with the temperamental capital T. Yes — *that* one. Don't all look at the door at once, you'll strain something."

Nobody had looked at the door. Everybody had looked at the door.

"It writes beautifully," Ilsa went on, merciless. "Strikes hard. Every sentence comes out looking furious, which suited my material. And before any of you ask — no, I wrote nothing of consequence. Farewells, recipes, instructions for the care of my roses, one account of your grandfather's proposal that will make Anders laugh and make Maren blush, and a list of debts owed to various families on the quay that I expect honored, because charity unrecorded is vanity, not virtue." She patted the pocket. "These were written by an old woman putting her house in order. Which is precisely why I am telling you about them. Because I want you to understand something clearly, all of you, before the soldiers come and make it complicated."

She looked around the table. The lamplight caught the steam rising off five untouched cups of tea.

"They can take the yard. They can take the manifest, the tools, the name off the gate — they'll take the *gate* itself, probably, those people love gates. They cannot take what this family knows how to do, which is build vessels and put loved ones aboard them and stay behind on the dock waving until the shape disappears. We have done it three times. We will do it again. The Halvards do not survive by keeping everyone together. We survive by *scattering well*." She jabbed a finger at Anders. "Scatter well. That is your inheritance. Better than the yard. The yard burns."

Anders swallowed. "Mother—"

"Aunt," she corrected absently. She'd been aunt-mother-grandmother to half this family for so long the titles had worn smooth as sea glass. "And one more thing."

She reached into her pocket and drew out, instead of the letters, a single sheet of paper, folded twice, and laid it in the center of the table like a card played face-up.

"My funeral instructions. Read them aloud later, not now. But I'll summarize: I am to be buried facing the water, in whatever ground will have me, and the stone is to say only my name and the words *she kept the ledger*. Nothing about beloved. Nothing about devoted. Anyone who adds an adjective pays a fine to Liv."

Liv made a sound that was trying very hard to be a laugh and mostly succeeded.

"That's an order, girl. First thing you'll inherit. Spend it on sweets."

* * *

Later — much later, when the kitchen had emptied and the boots on the quay road had multiplied into something none of them would name — Ilsa stood at her window with the lamp turned low, watching the harbor.

The Meridian lay at her berth like a held breath. Searchlights swept the water in slow, patient arcs, the way a man pats his pockets for a document he already knows is lost. Somewhere down there was a gangway, and a purser with a manifest, and one berth, and a family downstairs arguing in whispers about a decision that had already been made — had been made, Ilsa suspected, the moment a woman named Signe Eriksen nailed a crate shut in 1898 and never asked what became of the boy inside.

Foolish, brave woman. Ilsa had written her letter to Juniper anyway. Some debts you pay forward even when the creditor refuses to acknowledge the account.

From below came the sound of the office door opening softly — someone moving in the dark toward the type room. Light footsteps. Two sets. One light as paper, one still learning its weight.

Ilsa's mouth curved. She did not call out. She had spent ninety-one years learning the difference between watching and interfering, and she knew which one kept families alive.

But as she turned back to her bed — her legs complaining, her heart drumming its drunk little rhythm, the six letters heavy in their pocket like ballast — she permitted herself one quiet, fierce, entirely unsentimental thought:

*Let them come to the machine. Everything important in this house has always been hidden inside something ordinary.*

Down in the dark, a lock clicked.
