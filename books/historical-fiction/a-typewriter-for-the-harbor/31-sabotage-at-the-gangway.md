## 31. Sabotage at the Gangway

The harbor gate mechanism lived beneath the customs house in a room nobody had entered willingly since the last king's reign.

Anders knew this because his grandfather had helped install it — a great iron linkage running the length of the quay wall, chains and counterweights and a hand-cranked drum that could raise or lower the timber boom gates across every gangway on the eastern mole at once. In peacetime it was a toll system. The port collected its fees by controlling who walked onto the ships. Anders had heard the story a hundred times at Ilsa's table: old Halvard, drunk on plum brandy, swearing that no gate should ever stand between a sailor and the sea again, and then taking the contract anyway because winter was coming and pride doesn't feed children.

Now the gate was Reval's instrument. Lowered at dusk, raised only by soldiers with stamped papers. Every gangway on the mole funneled through two checkpoints, and at each one stood men with rifles and a ledger of names.

Anders stood in the dark of the net loft above the shipyard, watching the customs house through a gap in the shutters, and did the arithmetic he had been doing all day. Tide turned at 4:40. The Meridian could not safely clear the outer channel before 4:15, when the pilot boat would come alongside. Which meant that if arrests were to happen at dawn — and everyone in Kalshavn now understood they would happen at dawn — there was a window of perhaps ninety minutes between first light and slack water in which the family had to be aboard, cleared, and gone.

Ninety minutes. Or less, if Captain Reval decided not to wait for light at all.

"You've been looking at that gate for an hour," said Torvald Nilsen from the shadows near the loft door. He was sixty-one years old and had caulked hulls for the Halvards since before Anders could walk. He held two bottles of aquavit wrapped in newspaper, which meant he had already decided what kind of night this was going to be. "Either you're planning to buy it or break it."

"Break it," Anders said.

Torvald nodded slowly, as if Anders had announced rain. "Thought so. Been thinking so myself since Tuesday." He set the bottles down. "Who else?"

"That's why you're here."

* * *

They came one at a time over the next hour, slipping into the loft through the yard's back fence like boys sneaking to a dance. Einar Voss, who ran the steam crane and could lift a two-ton engine block with fingertips of rope and steam. Young Petter Lund, nineteen, who had signed on as an apprentice riveter eight months ago and had never in his life done anything worse than steal apples. Old Sander Brakke, who was technically retired but appeared at the yard most mornings anyway because retirement, he said, was for men with nothing left to fix. And Henrik Dahl, the foreman, who arrived last and stood by the door for a long moment before committing himself to the room.

Six men and a lantern turned low. Outside, the rain came off the water in long grey sheets, and the sentries on the mole had pulled their collars up and their shoulders in.

Anders laid out what he knew on a workbench, drawing in sawdust with a carpenter's pencil: the customs house cellar, the drum housing, the main chain run. He drew the counterweight shaft, the ratchet dogs that held the boom gates up against their own weight, the crank assembly that took two strong men ten full turns to lower everything and twenty to raise it again.

"They'll have a guard on the door," Henrik said. It wasn't a question.

"One man inside, maybe two outside under the awning. They're watching the gangways, not the building. Nobody important goes into the customs house at night."

"Nobody important," Torvald repeated, and almost smiled.

"I'm not saying it's nothing." Anders looked around at them — at Petter, whose hands were still soft enough that the riveter's calluses hadn't hardened; at Sander, who had a bad hip and a rifleman's patience; at Henrik, who had three sons and a wife and a house on Fisker Lane that would be the first thing the regime confiscated if this went wrong. "I'm saying if we do nothing, tomorrow at dawn they walk my mother out of her kitchen in handcuffs. They walk my grandmother out of her bed. They put Liv—" His voice snagged and he let it. There was no shame in it here. "There are eleven names on that arrest list that I know of, and nine of them are people who built half the boats in this harbor."

Silence. Rain on the roof.

"The mechanism's iron," Einar said finally, leaning over the sawdust drawing. "You can't cut the main chain quiet — she'd shriek like a banshee and drop both gates at once, and every soldier on the mole comes running."

"We don't cut anything," Anders said. "We take the ratchet dogs out of the drum housing. Four bolts apiece. Without the dogs, the counterweights can't hold — the crank spins free and the whole linkage jams in the half-raised position. Gates stuck open a meter off the cobbles. Too low to walk a gangway plank through clean, too high to reset without a crane and six hours."

Einar studied the drawing. "Bolt heads?"

"Square. Nineteen millimeter." He looked up. "We built the housing, Einar. Grandfather's drawings are in the office safe."

A slow grin spread across the old crane operator's face. "Then we bring our own wrenches."

"They'll know it was done deliberately," Henrik said quietly. "Sabotage isn't weather. Reval will understand exactly what happened, and he'll understand who could have done it. Who else knows that machine? Dockworkers. Shipwrights. Us." He rubbed his jaw. "My boys—"

"Henrik." Anders waited until the foreman met his eyes. "If it goes wrong, tell them I forced you. Tell them I came to your house with a pistol. Everyone says it. Everyone swears to it tonight, so there's no hesitation later."

"And you?" Henrik asked. "What do you say when they ask you?"

Anders looked at the sawdust drawing, at the little circles where the bolts would come out, at the neat pencil line of the quay wall beyond which his family's whole history floated at anchor, waiting for morning.

"I say I'm a traitor," he said. "To whatever it is they think they're the government of."

Sander Brakke laughed — a short, dry bark, quickly smothered. Then he reached out and turned the lantern up a notch, so they could all see each other's faces properly, the way men do when they want to remember.

"All right," Henrik said. "All right. God forgive us. When?"

"Now," Anders said. "Tonight, while it's raining hard and they're miserable. Not tomorrow when they'll be awake and angry and expecting something. Tonight."

* * *

They went out in twos, spaced apart, tools hidden in tool bags and fish crates and, in Sander's case, under a legitimate delivery of oakum for the customs house boiler room — a delivery Sander had talked the night watchman into accepting weeks ago and had simply never completed, because Sander Brakke planned ahead the way other men breathed.

The rain was their ally. It hammered the mole stones and drowned small sounds and kept the sentries huddled under the checkpoint awnings, smoking and stamping their feet and staring out at the black water rather than at the black street. Anders crossed the open ground in front of the customs house flat-out, head down, a workman hauling a heavy bag through weather too foul for questions. Behind him came Einar, whistling — actually whistling, low and tuneless, the sound of a man too old to be frightened anymore.

The cellar door was on the seaward side, below the tide line, behind a stack of tarred barrels. It had a lock, and the lock had been replaced sometime in the last decade with a cheap municipal affair that Sander opened with a strip of spring steel in forty seconds while the rest of them watched the street and felt their own hearts in their ears.

Inside: pitch dark, coal smell, the vast sleeping shape of the mechanism filling the cellar end to end like a beached whale. Anders struck a match, shielded it, found the lamp bracket on the stone wall, and lit the stub of candle someone had left there years ago. The flame steadied and threw the machinery into relief — the great drum wound with anchor chain, the counterweight shaft rising through a slot in the ceiling, and along the drum housing, four square bolt heads to a side, exactly as the old drawings promised, furred with a decade of salt rust.

"Eight bolts total," Einar whispered, running his thumb across them. "Two wrenches. You and me on the top row, Sander and Petter below. Quiet as church mice. If a bolt fights us, we leave it and move to the next."

"What about the dogs themselves?" Petter whispered. His voice cracked once on the word *dogs* and he flushed even in the candlelight.

"They stay in place. We don't touch them — we just free them from the housing. Once the bolts are out, the whole ratchet assembly can shift a centimeter under load, and a centimeter is all it takes. First time they try to raise those gates, the drum slips, the weights slam down, and the linkage binds. She won't break — Halvard iron doesn't break — but she won't work either." Anders handed him a wrench. "That's the point. We're not destroying anything. We're making it honest. It's a broken gate, and we're just letting it admit it."

Petter almost smiled. Then he knelt at his bolt and became, in the space of a breath, entirely silent and entirely focused, and Anders thought: *he'll be a good man. Whatever happens tomorrow, he'll be a good man.*

The first bolt came out stiffly, groaning through a quarter-turn of rust before breaking loose. Anders caught it before it could clatter and passed it back to Torvald, who stood at the foot of the stairs with a sack and the expression of a sexton at a funeral. One by one they went: the second, the third, Einar grunting softly on the fourth, Sander working below with a patience that seemed geological. Somewhere above them, boots crossed the floor of the customs house — a sentry doing rounds — and everyone froze, wrenches mid-turn, candle cupped behind a coat, listening to the footsteps cross and recross and finally fade toward the front of the building.

Seven bolts lay in Torvald's sack.

The eighth fought them. It was the lowest bolt on the seaward side, seated in a pocket of standing rust, and neither Petter nor Sander could break it loose, and Anders took the wrench himself and leaned into it with his whole body, jaw clenched so tight his teeth sang, and the bolt gave with a crack that sounded in that stone cellar like a rifle shot.

Everyone froze. Rain, somewhere far away. Water dripping. A gull complaining on the mole.

Then, overhead, muffled through the floorboards: a voice. "Hear that?"

Another voice, bored: "The building settling. This whole town's falling down."

Footsteps moved on. Anders let his breath out through his teeth, slow, controlled, and discovered his hands were shaking — not from fear, he realized, but from the sheer strangeness of what they had just done. Twenty years of building things. Of measuring twice and cutting once. Of being known in this harbor as a man whose word was iron. And in the space of an hour he had become a saboteur in a cellar, lying still in the dark, waiting for armed men to stop hearing things.

*Ordinary men*, he thought. *This is what it costs, and this is how it's paid. Not by heroes. By caulker's hands and a bad hip and a boy who steals apples.*

He broke the eighth bolt loose gently, gently, and passed it down into the dark.

* * *

They were out of the cellar by two o'clock, the door relocked behind them, the bolts distributed among six coats so that no single search would find anything. They separated in twos again and melted into the rain-washed streets, and Anders walked home the long way, past the Meridian at her berth, her riding lights doubled and trembling in the black water. A sentry paced the gangway above him. Anders did not look up. Looking up was how men got remembered.

At the house, a candle still burned in the kitchen window. He let himself in and found Maren sitting up with bread dough rising under a cloth — baking at two in the morning, because if the world was ending, the neighbors would eat.

"Well?" she asked. She didn't ask what. She'd known him for thirty-five years.

"Done," he said. "Eight bolts. Clean."

She studied his face in the candlelight, and whatever she saw there made her reach out and press flour-dusted fingers briefly to his wrist. "Sit. Eat something. You can't carry a family on an empty stomach."

He sat. The bread was yesterday's, dense and good, and he ate it standing at the sink like a laborer, and Maren went back to her kneading, and for a few minutes the kitchen was the oldest room in the world and nothing in it had ever been in danger.

"Maren," he said finally. "When they come — and they'll come, the gate will buy us time but not forgiveness — when they come, you take Liv and you go out the garden gate and down to the Ostergaard boathouse. There's a skiff there with a tarpaulin over it. You don't argue with me. You don't wait for me. You go."

Maren punched the dough down with a firm, steady blow. "Your grandmother won't go."

"I know."

"And you won't go either, will you. Not until every soul on that list has a chance at the water."

He didn't answer, which was an answer. Maren worked the dough in silence, then said, without turning around: "Your father used to talk in his sleep during the last purge. Do you know what he said? Not prayers. Manifests. Names and berths, over and over. Asleep, he was still counting who fit on which deck." She dusted the board with flour. "You're his son, Anders. Count carefully."

* * *

At 4:10, in the grey beginnings of the false dawn, the first test came.

Anders was on the quay, ostensibly supervising the loading of the Meridian's last cargo — crated machinery the purser had agreed to take at double rate, because the regime's new customs officers were happy to be bribed and happier not to notice. Along the mole, the boom gates hung at their dawn position, lowered across every gangway, and at the central checkpoint Captain Reval himself had appeared, early, precise, buttoned into a grey greatcoat despite the rain, reviewing papers by lamplight while the passengers-to-be waited in a sodden, shuffling line.

At 4:12, a signal came from the customs house — the pilot boat was alongside the outer mole, and the harbormaster's office wanted the gates raised to admit the pilot's launch through the service channel.

Anders watched, from thirty meters, as two soldiers went down into the customs house and began to crank.

He heard it before he saw anything: a deep metallic groan rolling up out of the quay stones, the sound of the great drum taking strain. Then a clatter — the ratchet dogs shifting in their freed housings — then a shriek of iron binding on iron, and the boom gates, which should have risen smoothly on their counterweights, lurched upward a meter and jammed fast, quivering, the chain slack in the drum, the crank handle spinning freely in the soldier's hands like something insane.

The shouting started immediately. Soldiers ran. An officer bellowed for tools. Someone sent a boy sprinting toward the barracks. Reval stood very still amid all of it, and though Anders was too far away to see his face clearly, he saw the captain turn — slowly, deliberately — and look down the length of the quay, past the crowd, past the cranes, directly toward the Halvard shipyard.

Not at Anders. At the yard itself. Reading it. Weighing it.

Anders picked up his manifest clipboard and walked toward the checkpoint with his heart hammering and his face arranged into the bored irritation of a man whose cargo schedule was being ruined, and when Reval's gaze swept past him, he met it for exactly one second — the way any citizen might, the way an innocent man might — and then looked down at his papers.

Behind him, down the mole, the tide had begun to turn. He could feel it in the pilings under his feet, the whole harbor breathing in.

Four hours of darkness left. Maybe five. And somewhere in the city, Juniper Eriksen was walking through the rain with something strapped against her body that every soldier in Vessmark would kill for, and Anders Halvard had just bought her whatever time the iron would give.

He turned back toward the yard and began, calmly, to count the crates.
