## 32. The Film Changes Hands

The fishing boat came at low tide, when the harbor mud lay exposed like a wound and no one with authority cared to walk it.

Juniper had learned of it the way she learned most things now — through Liv, who moved through the docks like a gull, seeing everything, reporting nothing until asked twice. *The *Gannet*,* Liv had said over supper, not looking up from her bowl. *Herring boat out of Skarvik. Captain owes Anders for a rudder repair he never paid for. He's running salted cod north along the coast tomorrow before first light. He doesn't ask what's in his hold anymore. Since the coup, nobody asks anybody anything.*

Now Juniper stood at the edge of the timber yard behind the Halvard office, in the hour the lamplighters had abandoned and the dawn watch hadn't yet begun, and waited for a man named Torvald to make her an accomplice.

She had spent three nights on the copy.

It had not been easy. The film was thin as a promise and twice as fragile, thirty-two frames of microphotography tucked inside the typebar assembly of a 1903 ministry Remington that Ilsa Halvard had used, without ever knowing it, to type farewell letters and grocery lists. Juniper had disassembled the machine a second time on the floor of the loft above the ship office, by the light of a shaded candle, working with a jeweler's loupe she'd bought from a pawnbroker who hadn't looked at her face. She'd built a crude contact printer from two pieces of glass, a picture frame, and the boarding house's reading lamp. She'd handled each frame with tweezers stolen from Maren's sewing kit, breathing through her mouth, the way Oskar had taught her years ago in a basement office that smelled of ink and dying lungs.

*You are not carrying paper,* he had told her once, pressing a pouch into her hands. *You are carrying the difference between a country remembering itself and a country forgetting. Treat it accordingly.*

Thirty-two frames. She had made four copies. Two were flawed — a hair of dust across frame nineteen, a blur where her hands had trembled on the second night, and she had destroyed them both in Maren's stove, feeding the celluloid into the coals and watching it curl and stink and vanish. The third was good. The fourth was better.

The third was going to Sweden.

She heard the *Gannet* before she saw it — the wet knock of a hull against pilings, the mutter of a man talking to his boat because there was no one else worth talking to. Then Torvald rounded the stack of seasoned oak, a barrel-chested man in an oilskin the color of old iron, and stopped when he saw her, and did not pretend surprise.

"You're the one," he said.

"I'm the one."

"Anders said you'd have something for the hold. He didn't say what."

"He didn't know." She held up the tin — a flat tobacco tin, rusted at the corners, its original contents long since smoked by some dead man. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth and packed in cotton wadding torn from a coat lining, lay the copy: twenty frames selected for clarity, sealed against salt water with wax and tape. "It goes under the false bottom of your bait box. It stays there until you're past the Vessmark patrol line. Past the lightship at Nordholm. When you're in Swedish water, you take it out and you put it in the post. There's an address inside the wrapping. You don't read it. You don't tell anyone. You mail it like a letter from a stranger and you never think about it again."

Torvald looked at the tin for a long moment. The harbor breathed around them — halyards ticking against masts, the far-off clang of the gate crew still repairing what Anders's men had broken, the smell of tar and rotting weed and the cold mineral breath of the sea.

"And if they search me?"

"They'll search you. Let them find the cod. Let them find your temper. Nobody searches a bait box twice if the first time smells right." She had packed a few fish heads into the tin's upper compartment for exactly this reason. It was the kind of detail Oskar would have approved of, and the kind of detail that made her feel, briefly, ill.

Torvald took the tin. He weighed it in his hand like a man judging a catch. "Anders says you're paying the debt he owed me."

"Anders is wrong. You're doing this because in 1904 your brother drowned off the Skarvik light and a Halvard pilot went out in weather that killed two other men trying to bring him home. Ask him about it sometime. He'll deny it was anything."

Something moved in the fisherman's face — not quite a smile, but the place where a smile would live on a harder man. "That sounds like Ilsa talking."

"It sounds like this family," Juniper said. "Go. The tide won't wait and neither will the gate crew's hangover."

He went. She watched the *Gannet* work its way out past the breakwater, small and stubborn against the grey swell, and she did not let herself breathe fully until the point swallowed it. Somewhere out there, past the patrols and the new flag flying over the customs house, a fishing boat was carrying the republic's memory toward a neutral shore, toward archives and ministers in exile, toward a future in which someone might be made to answer for the money that had bought the coup.

One copy gone. One copy left, strapped flat against her ribs beneath her shift, taped there with the same surgeon's patience she'd learned crossing borders with documents sewn into her coat.

She touched it once, lightly, the way you touch a bruise to learn its shape. Her only currency now. Worth more than the shipyard, more than the Meridian's manifest, more than every life standing between her and Captain Reval's courtesy.

She turned back toward the house and began to plan a fire.

* * *

Oskar's cipher keys lived in the heel of her left boot, where they had lived since Kalshavn.

They were not keys in the way a house has keys. They were five strips of rice paper, each no wider than a thumbnail, printed with number groups so fine they blurred at arm's length — the rotating ciphers of the foreign ministry's courier service, changed quarterly, valid now only to a government that existed in exile, in hiding, or not at all. With them she could read any dispatch the old republic had sent in the last year. Without them she was just a woman with a limp and good boots.

She had told herself a hundred stories about why she kept them. Tradition. Sentiment. The courier's superstition that papers were safer on the body than in any drawer. But the truth had been sitting in her chest for days now, patient as a stone in a shoe, and tonight she finally let herself look at it:

She had lied to Oskar. And a woman who lies to her handler keeps his ciphers either to betray him further or to protect herself from him — and she no longer knew which.

So she would burn them. Not because burning made her honest, but because honesty was a discipline, and disciplines were all she had left. The mission had died somewhere between the sealed letter and the harbor current; the government it served was a memory wearing uniforms; and if she could not save the dispatches by delivering them, then she would have to become them — carried, hidden, undeliverable, true.

Maren's kitchen was warm and empty at half past ten. Bread had been baked that morning for the dock families — the ovens ran whether or not there was peace to bake for — and the room still held the ghost of it, yeast and woodsmoke and scorched flour. A pot of coffee sat on the iron plate, covered, kept for whoever came in from the cold. Maren kept coffee for whoever came in from the cold the way other houses kept lamps lit.

Juniper fed the stove two lengths of birch and waited for the fire to take.

Then she sat down at the long table, pulled off her left boot, and worked the rice paper free.

Five strips. She laid them out on the scrubbed pine in a row, and for a moment she simply looked at them — at the tiny columns of numbers that had been, for eleven days, the last living tissue of a country's voice. She thought of Oskar's hands on the last morning, spotted and trembling, folding the sealed letter into her palm. *Deliver this to Halvard. Before the tide.* He had coughed then, that wet tearing cough, and had not met her eyes, and she understood now that he had known — or feared — that the letter was poison, and had sent it anyway because a dying clerk obeys whatever orders reach him last, even the ones designed to kill.

Or perhaps he hadn't known. That was the cruelty of it. She would never know. He was either a dupe or an accomplice or a patriot, and the difference had been buried with him in a pauper's trench behind the quarantine wall, unmarked because marking graves invited questions.

"Does it help," Maren said from the doorway, "to stare at it first?"

Juniper didn't startle. She was past startling; her nerves had burned down to embers days ago. "It helps to say goodbye properly."

Maren crossed the kitchen, poured two cups of coffee without asking, and sat down across the table. Her eyes went over the five strips of paper with the frank curiosity of a woman who had raised three children and a shipyard and had stopped being surprised by secrets around 1890.

"Codes?"

"Ciphers. Ministry issue." Juniper turned one strip over. Blank on the reverse. Of course. Even the paper was disciplined.

"The government that sent you here."

"The government that stopped existing here. Yes." Juniper wrapped her hands around the cup. The heat hurt pleasantly. "I've carried these since the border. Every checkpoint I walked through, every soldier who waved me past — I was carrying enough paper in my boot to hang forty people, and none of them knew to look."

"And now you're burning them."

"Now I'm burning them."

"Why?"

Juniper considered lying. It was reflexive, the way flinching was reflexive, and she was tired of reflexes. "Because I lied to the man who gave them to me, and he's dead, and this is the only apology I can make. Because if Reval finds these on me, they confirm everything he suspects. And because—" She stopped. Started again. "Because I'm done being a courier. Couriers deliver things and forget them. I can't forget any of it. So I have to decide what I am instead, and I can't do that carrying his orders in my boot."

Maren drank her coffee. Outside, a patrol boots-and-rifle'd past on the quay road, unhurried, masters of the night. Neither woman looked toward the window. Looking toward windows was how neighbors disappeared.

"When Ilsa's husband was alive," Maren said, "he kept a ledger of every debt the yard owed and was owed. Forty years of it. When he was dying he made me burn it in front of him — page by page, so he could watch. I asked him why, and he said: *because the next generation should owe people kindness, not figures.*" She set her cup down. "Burn your figures, girl. Kindness we'll manage another way."

Juniper fed the strips into the stove one at a time. Rice paper caught fast, flared blue-white, and was gone between one breath and the next. She watched each one go. She did not say the names of the cities whose codes they were. She did not recite the oath she had taken at nineteen in a room smelling of sealing wax. She simply watched, and when the last strip curled and vanished she felt — not lighter, exactly. Emptier, the way a hold feels after cargo is landed. Ready for whatever came aboard next.

"There," Maren said quietly. "Now you're just yourself."

"Yes," Juniper said. "That's the frightening part."

* * *

She slept two hours in the loft above the ship office, dressed, boots on, the copy of the film a flat pressure against her sternum that she had stopped noticing the way you stop noticing your own heartbeat. She dreamed in fragments: Oskar's coughing, the flare of burning paper, Liv's face at dinner the night she'd reported the soldiers photographing the yard — *I only thought someone should say it aloud* — and beneath it all, running like a tide under ice, the arithmetic she could not finish.

One berth. One film. One family. One of her.

She woke before four to the sound of the harbor gate grinding — the crews were still patching what Anders's men had jammed, working through the night on Reval's orders, and the sound of their labor was the sound of the trap being rebuilt link by link. She lay in the dark and listened to it and did her last accounting.

The copy was away. By now the *Gannet* was past the point, running north with the ebb, Torvald cursing his engine and his luck in equal measure, the tin riding silent under the bait. If the patrols took him, the tin would smell of fish heads and he would swear at them and be released, and the truth would sink to the harbor floor in a bait box, and she would have lost nothing but hope. If he made Swedish water, then somewhere within a fortnight, an envelope would arrive at an address in Stockholm, and men in exile would hold the proof that the coup had been financed abroad — proof that might, someday, matter more than any single life in this harbor.

And she kept the original. Not for the archive. Not for the exiles. For the quay at dawn, if it came to that. For trade. For ransom. For the moment when Captain Reval's meticulous courtesy finally showed its teeth and she would need something to put between those teeth and the Halvards' throats.

She rose, checked the straps, checked the knife at her ankle that she had sharpened and would never use, and looked once around the loft — the dismantled typewriter laid out on its cloth like anatomy, the cold candle stub, the window showing the first suggestion of grey over the breakwater.

Below her, the house was beginning to stir. Maren's footsteps in the kitchen, already building the fire. Ilsa's voice, thin and imperious, demanding her tea and complaining about the quality of the century. Anders moving through the yard with that heavy deliberate tread, counting what was left to count.

And somewhere south along the coast road, in a requisitioned customs house with tidy ledgers and worse intentions, Captain Reval was also awake. She was certain of it. Men like Reval slept the way cats did — lightly, and always facing the door.

Today the family would move for the quay. Today the bribed clerk would either hold his nerve or lose it. Today the tide would turn at 6:41, and the Meridian would sail with it or she would not, and every plan Juniper had made in eleven days of fear now compressed itself into a single grey hour of walking distance between this house and the gangway.

She strapped the film tight against her heart, picked up the one bag she was allowed, and went down the stairs into the last dark of the old republic — knowing that at the checkpoint on Harbor Road, in less than an hour, someone was going to look into her face and decide what she was worth.

She intended to be worth everything.
