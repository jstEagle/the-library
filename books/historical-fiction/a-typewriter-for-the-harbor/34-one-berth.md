## 34. One Berth

The grey light had not yet decided whether it was dawn or merely the last hour of the night when they came down the long quay toward the *Meridian*. Juniper counted their footsteps without meaning to — a courier's habit, counting everything that could be counted, because numbers did not lie the way people did. Seven sets of feet on wet stone: her own, Anders beside her with his collar turned up and his jaw set like a keel timber, Maren behind him with her hands folded in front of her as though she were walking into church, Liv half-skipping to keep pace, the shawl of Ilsa's wrapped around her shoulders even though Ilsa herself had refused it. Two dockworkers who owed the Halvards more than wages carried the single trunk between them.

Behind them, at the mouth of the quay, the checkpoint lantern still burned. The bribed clerk had looked away as they passed, his face the color of tallow, his hand trembling on his ledger as he marked something that was not true. Juniper had not thanked him. Thanking a man in front of soldiers was how you killed him. She had only met his eyes for half a second, and in that half second she had seen what fear looked like when it was losing an argument with conscience.

The harbor smelled of tar and cold iron and the green rot of the tide going out. Gulls argued over something near the fish sheds. The *Meridian* lay against the pier like a sleeping animal, black hull streaked with rust, her funnel breathing thin smoke — steam up, ready, waiting only for the tide and the gate repair that Anders and his men had sabotaged in the night. Somewhere beyond the breakwater the blockade cruisers sat like grey headstones. Somewhere behind them, in the customs house, Captain Reval was awake. Juniper could feel it the way you feel weather coming through a bad tooth.

The gangway was guarded by two soldiers with slung rifles who looked bored and dangerous in equal measure, and beyond them stood the purser — a small, round man with a waxed mustache and a manifest board under his arm, checking names as passengers came aboard in a slow, shuffling line of families and bundles and held-back weeping.

Anders went ahead. That was right; it was his name on the tickets, his family's passage bought over years of shipyard profit and one desperate bribe to the shipping line's agent in Rønne. Juniper hung back with Maren, letting the men do the talking, because she was nobody — no ticket, no name on any list, a woman in a man's coat carrying nothing but a satchel and, strapped flat against her ribs beneath her shirt, the thing worth more than every life standing on this quay.

She watched Anders speak to the purser. She watched the purser's mustache move. And then she watched the purser shake his head, once, slowly, with the terrible gentleness of a man delivering news he has delivered too many times this week.

Juniper's stomach dropped through the stones of the quay.

"No," she heard Anders say. "No, check again. Halvard. Four names. I paid—"

"I have checked three times, herr Halvard." The purser's voice carried in the cold air, pitched low but not low enough. "Cancellations, seizures, the ministry took six berths off my list Tuesday night for their own people. Then the Voss family gave theirs up when the grandmother died. There was one berth left after all of it. One. I sold it yesterday evening."

"To whom?" Anders's voice cracked like ice underfoot.

"To no one sailing today, thank God. A merchant from Drage who missed the boat and demanded his money back." The purser licked his thumb and turned the page of his manifest, and Juniper saw his finger stop on a line and hover there. "One berth. Herr Halvard — I can put one name on it. Only one. The rest of you—" He spread his small hands, and the gesture said everything the regulations would not: the rest of you are not my problem, God forgive me, the rest of you are the country's problem now.

Maren made a sound. It was very small, a sound like a stitch pulling tight, and Juniper had spent enough years around the Halvards to know that Maren Halvard did not make sounds like that unless the world had actually come apart.

Liv grabbed her mother's sleeve. "Then we don't go," she said. "Nobody goes. We stay together."

"You'll go," Anders said.

"Then nobody goes," Liv repeated, louder, and several heads turned on the gangway.

Juniper stepped forward before the argument could catch fire, because arguments on quays caught fire faster than tar. "Purser," she said quietly. "A word."

He looked at her — at the coat, at the satchel, at the eyes above them both — and whatever he read there made him step aside with her, out of earshot of the soldiers, under the shadow of the bollard where the mooring lines lay coiled like sleeping snakes.

"One berth," he said again, softer now. "I'm sorry. If I could conjure four—"

"You can't. But you can write a name." She kept her voice level. Twelve years of carrying other people's secrets had taught her that the voice mattered more than the words; a steady voice could walk a lie through a locked door. "Write the name now. Before anyone else decides anything. Write it while the ink is still warm from the last page, and no one will question it."

"Whose name?"

"That," Juniper said, "is being decided right now, apparently by committee."

* * *

It became a committee immediately, and it was the worst kind — the kind where everyone loved everyone else too much to win.

They drew apart from the queue, huddled by the bollard with the trunk between them like a table, and the argument began the way these arguments always begin: with silence, each person looking at the person they had already chosen, hoping someone else would say it first.

Anders said it first. Of course he did. He was the eldest son; he had been carrying the arithmetic of this family since he was nineteen. "Grandmother," he said. "Ilsa. Write Ilsa Halvard."

"She won't go," Maren said.

"She'll go if we tell her it's done. She'll curse us the whole way to Sweden and call us fools in front of the captain, but she'll go, because she's too proud to make a scene getting *off* a boat."

"She is eighty-one years old and she told us last night, in those exact words, that she will not set foot on that deck," Maren said. "You want to carry her here like luggage? You want the soldiers to see an old woman fighting at the rail? They'd pull the whole family off for questioning and then there would be zero berths for anybody."

"Then Liv," Anders said, and his voice broke on the name, and Juniper watched a big man who ran a shipyard and had traded away his pride at the dry dock yesterday simply run out of ways to hold himself together. "Liv. She's sixteen. She's the future of — she's the one with her whole life—"

"I'm not going alone," Liv said, and her chin was up, Ilsa's chin, the whole stubborn genealogy of the Halvard jaw stacked behind it. "I'm not getting on that boat by myself. Mama comes or nobody—"

"Liv—" Maren started.

"—or Papa, or Aunt—"

"I'm not your aunt," Juniper said automatically, and instantly wished she hadn't, because Liv rounded on her with tears standing bright in her eyes and fury underneath them, the particular fury of a young person watching adults fail at the only thing that matters.

"Then *you*," Liv said. "Take it. You've been running messages and risking your neck for weeks. You're the one Reval wants. Get on the boat."

"The soldiers at the gangway have my description," Juniper said. "Probably my photograph by now. I'd be arrested at the top of the plank and the berth would be wasted anyway." It was true, and it wasn't the whole truth, and she knew the whole truth was sitting strapped against her ribs: that she could not leave, that the film was her currency and her cross and her oath all at once, that a courier who abandoned her cargo was no longer a courier but merely a woman running away. But she let them believe the smaller reason, because the smaller reason was kinder.

"Then give it back," Anders said desperately, turning to the purser, who had drifted back within earshot and now wore the expression of a man watching a family drown in shallow water. "Refund it. Sell it to someone else—"

"There is no one else, herr Halvard. The boat sails on the tide. In forty minutes there will be no berth and no refund and no argument, and I will have done my job badly and honestly." The purser closed his manifest with a soft clap. "Forgive me. But someone must be on that boat, or the money and the place and the chance all die together on this quay. Choose. Please. For God's sake, choose quickly."

The gulls screamed. The funnel smoke bent in the wind coming off the water. Down the quay, at the far end near the customs house, a lantern moved — just a lantern, just a man walking with a lantern at that grey hour, and Juniper's heart slammed once against the film strapped to her body and told her the grey hour was ending.

Maren spoke then, quietly, the way she spoke in the kitchen when the bread was rising and the truth could finally be said out loud. "It should be Liv. It was always going to be Liv. Anders, look at me. We built ships so people could leave. That's the whole trade, that's the whole family, that's what your grandmother keeps saying from her bed. We do not choose who leaves — but if the choice has been taken from us, then we choose the one with the most sea left in front of her." Her voice did not waver. "Liv goes. Alone. To Pastor Eklund's congregation in Göteborg, the address is sewn in her coat, we settled all this weeks ago in case — in case." She didn't finish the sentence. Nobody needed her to.

"I won't," Liv whispered.

"You will," said Anders.

"I won't! Not alone, not without—"

And it was at that moment — with the argument spinning like a wheel off its axle, with the purser's pen hovering, with the lantern at the end of the quay growing brighter or nearer or both — that Juniper Eriksen stopped being a courier, stopped being a guest, stopped being the woman who believed she had forfeited her place at this family's table twelve years ago when she climbed out of a lifeboat hold with stolen bread in her pockets.

She acted instead. With her hands, not her words, because words had failed everyone on this quay and hands had never yet failed a Halvard.

She picked Liv up.

Not gently. There was no time for gently and no permission asked. Juniper got one arm under the girl's knees and one across her back — Liv was all elbows and indignation, sixteen years old and lighter than the mail sacks Juniper had carried across three borders — and she lifted her, bodily, off the wet stones of the quay, and walked toward the gangway with her.

Liv shrieked. Actually shrieked, like a child, like the child she still was beneath the dock-runner's swagger. "Put me DOWN — Juniper — Mama — PAPA, make her—"

"Herr Halvard!" Juniper called over the girl's shoulder, striding, boots ringing on the planks. "The name! Write the name NOW!"

"HALVARD," Anders roared behind her, his voice wrecked and certain at once. "LIV HALVARD!"

"Purser — the girl — write it —" The soldiers at the foot of the gangway straightened, confused, hands moving toward their rifles and then away again, because what was happening was so far outside their orders — a woman carrying a screaming teenager up a gangway toward a legitimate ticketed passenger berth — that it did not compute as contraband, as escape, as anything requiring bullets. Boredom and confusion fought on their faces. One of them actually stepped aside.

"Juniper, I swear to God, I will hate you forever," Liv gasped against her shoulder, pounding her fist on the coat, on the satchel strap, on the ribs beneath which the film lay flat and silent.

"No, you won't," Juniper said. "You'll write about it. You'll write it better than it happened, the way your family writes everything."

At the top of the gangway the purser was waiting with his pen already scratching, and Juniper set Liv down on the deck — set her down and kept her hands on the girl's shoulders, holding her there, feeling the tremor running through her like current through a wire.

"Listen to me." Juniper leaned close, forehead nearly touching forehead, the way Maren had once leaned close to her over a bowl of soup in this same harbor twelve years ago. "Pastor Eklund. Göteborg. The address in your coat. You deliver yourself the way I deliver dispatches — intact, no matter what. You don't turn back. You don't argue with sailors. And when it's safe — months, a year, however long — you write to your mother care of the bakery on Strandgaten, and the letters will find us, because your mother can find anyone with a kitchen and a loaf of bread. Do you hear me?"

Liv was crying now, properly, ugly and fifteen-again and furious about it. "This isn't fair."

"Nothing about this year is fair. But you're alive, and alive is the only cargo that matters." Juniper gripped her shoulders once, hard, a carpenter's grip, a Halvard grip. "Tell them in Sweden what happened here. All of it. You saw everything. You're the witness now."

Below, on the quay, Maren had both hands pressed over her mouth. Anders stood with his fists at his sides and his head bowed, a man who had lost and been saved in the same stroke. The dockworkers lifted the trunk and passed it up the plank. The purser called the time remaining until the tide in a voice trained over thirty years not to shake.

And Juniper turned and went down the gangway, fast, before her legs could learn what her heart already knew — that she was leaving the best part of herself standing at that rail.

Liv lunged after her. A sailor caught her arms. From the deck her voice came down over the water, cracking and clear: "JUNIPER! Come with — take the berth, I'll get off, JUNIPER—"

Juniper did not turn around. If she turned around she would go back up that plank, and the film would sail to Sweden, and Reval would burn Kalshavn looking for it, and everything Oskar had coughed blood onto a desk to preserve would die with the city. So she walked. Down the plank, onto the stones, past the soldiers, past the purser closing his manifest, back to the family that remained.

Maren caught her hand as she reached them. Neither woman said anything. Maren's grip said *you did what I could not*, and Juniper's answering squeeze said *it was your bread that raised the hands that did it*, and that was the entire conversation, and it was enough.

Above them, at the rail, Liv stood wrapped in Ilsa's shawl, small against the grey sky, watching them with wet furious eyes that promised hatred and gratitude and letters, years of letters.

The mooring lines began to come loose.

And that was when Juniper saw the car.

It came along the quay road at a speed no civilian car used in occupied Kalshavn — black, mud-splashed, its headlights still burning against the dawn it refused to admit had arrived. It braked fifty meters short of the gangway in a spray of harbour water, and the door opened, and a tall figure stepped out into the grey light with his cap squared and his gloves already on, unhurried, precise, a man arriving exactly when he had always intended to arrive.

Captain Reval stood for a moment surveying the scene — the departing ship, the weeping girl at the rail, the Halvards clustered at the bollard, and last of all Juniper, standing apart from all of them, the way a courier always stands apart.

He removed one glove, finger by finger, and smiled at her across the wet stones.

"Frau Eriksen," he said, pleasantly, in a voice that carried all the way to the ship. "Don't sail on my account. We have business."
