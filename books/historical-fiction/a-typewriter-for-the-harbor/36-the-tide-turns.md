## 36. The Tide Turns

Anders had not slept. None of them had, but he had stopped pretending around three o'clock, when the rain came off the water in long grey slants and the lamps of the harbor gate still burned where they should have been dark. He stood at the loft window of the ship office with his coat already on, watching the gate mechanism that he and Torben and the Ostergaard brothers had broken six hours before.

It was a small piece of sabotage, in the end. A coupling pin drawn from the winch train and dropped into forty feet of black water; a jammed dog clutch packed with oakum and grease; a signal wire cut so cleanly it would look like corrosion. Three generations of Halvards had built the harbor's machinery, and Anders supposed there was a bitter justice in the fact that only a shipwright could have broken it this well. The gate crew had been working on it since midnight. Their lanterns swung and clustered like fireflies over the causeway, and every hour they seemed to make no more progress than the hour before.

Behind him, in the office below, his family waited the way sailors wait out weather — not calmly, but quietly, which is a different thing entirely.

His mother had laid out bread and hard cheese at two in the morning as though it were an ordinary day, as though food were simply something one did while soldiers patrolled the quay outside. Ilsa had refused to come down from her room at all. "I said my goodbyes," she had announced through the door, in the voice she used for ending arguments before they began. "If any of you come up here to make them again, I shall throw my slipper." Liv had laughed — actually laughed — and the sound had gone through Anders like warm water through cold hands.

And Juniper Eriksen sat at the corner table with her back to the wall, facing the door, the way she always sat, with the film strapped somewhere against her body beneath her coat. She had told them everything last night — all of it, the letter, the machine, Reval's offer, the berth — because Maren had been right: you cannot carry a thing alone into a morning like this one. Anders had listened to the whole confession and found, to his surprise, that what he felt was not anger but a kind of grim recognition. She was Halvard in every way that mattered except the name, and the name, tonight, was worth nothing anyway.

"The tide turns at 5:40," Juniper said now, from the doorway of the office, without looking up from the pocket watch she'd borrowed. "The Meridian's master wants lines off by five-fifteen if he means to clear the gate before full flood."

"The gate won't be repaired," Anders said. "Not by five-fifteen. Not by six."

"You're certain?"

"I broke it. I know how it breaks."

She almost smiled. It was a thin thing, worn down to the wire, but it was there. Then it went, and she looked past him toward the window, toward the grey shape of the Meridian riding her moorings with steam already feathering from her funnel, and Anders watched the smile die and understood why. One berth. One girl aboard. Everything else still unresolved, still balanced on the edge of a knife that Captain Reval held by the blade.

* * *

They moved at ten past four, in the last of the dark.

The plan was simplicity itself, because plans made at four in the morning by exhausted people should be simple. Walk to the quay in ones and twos, the way dock families always walked to ships. Maren with Liv's arm through hers, a mother seeing a daughter off. Anders carrying a sea bag that contained, along with clothing, the last of the yard's cash sewn into the lining — bribe money, passage money, whatever the moment demanded. Juniper last, unremarkable, a courier's face that had learned to be furniture.

Ilsa remained upstairs. They had each gone to her door in turn during the night. Anders had meant to say something final and had instead found himself kneeling on the corridor floor while his grandmother gripped his wrist through the gap with a strength that embarrassed him. "You were nine years old," she said, "the first time you told me a lie, and you were terrible at it, and you have improved not at all. Now go and be terrible at whatever they ask you next." He had laughed until it hurt, and then it hurt, and then he went.

The rain had slackened to a drifting mist. The quay stones shone under the work lamps. Soldiers stood at the gangway in pairs, bayonets fixed, breath steaming, and beyond them the purser of the Meridian checked papers under a hooded lamp with the expression of a man doing arithmetic he did not enjoy.

The checkpoint at the head of the quay was where it happened — where the night bent, just slightly, toward mercy. The clerk Vinter was there, the harbormaster's man, holding a clipboard he clearly wished were a shield. When Anders's group approached, Vinter looked at their papers, looked at their faces, and looked very deliberately at the middle distance, waving them through with the mechanical boredom of a man who has decided exactly how much trouble he is willing to be in. His hand shook. His eyes did not. Later, Anders would think about that distinction often.

At the gangway, the arithmetic reasserted itself.

"One berth," the purser said. He did not say it unkindly. He said it the way a man reports a hole below the waterline — as fact, not argument. "Manifest's closed otherwise. Captain's orders, and above the captain's orders besides."

Maren stepped forward first, of course. "My daughter—"

"Mother." Liv's voice cracked across the wet air like a whip. She was sixteen and white-faced and her fists were balled at her sides. "Don't. Don't do this in front of me like I'm cargo."

Anders opened his mouth to argue for her — to say, with the full authority of the eldest son, that Liv would board, that this was settled, that he would put her over the rail himself if it came to it — and then Juniper moved.

He never quite reconstructed the mechanics of it afterward. There was a blur of grey coat, a short fierce scuffle at the rail, and suddenly Juniper Eriksen — five foot four of courier and typist and stowaway's child — had lifted his sister bodily, one arm under her knees, one across her back, and swung her toward the gangway with a strength that must have come from somewhere no one had ever thought to look. The purser caught Liv under the arms. Two deckhands took her weight. And Juniper stepped back, breathing hard, her hair loose, her eyes blazing, and said the only words Anders ever heard her raise her voice to speak:

"She carries the family. That's the whole point of families. Go."

Liv was screaming his name from the deck. Maren had her face pressed into Anders's shoulder. And Anders stood on the quay with his empty hands open at his sides, understanding that something had just ended and something else had just begun, and that both things had happened inside a single breath.

* * *

The soldiers noticed at 5:12.

Perhaps they had been counting heads all along; perhaps the scream carried; perhaps Captain Reval, who missed nothing, had simply chosen his moment. A whistle blew from the harbor gate — one sharp note that cut through the mist — and the pair at the gangway turned, and somewhere behind the warehouses boots began to run in unison, that dreadful drumbeat sound Anders had heard only once before, twelve years ago, from the hold of a ship leaving a burning pier.

"Lines," someone shouted from the Meridian's bridge. "Get her lines off!"

The mooring men froze. Soldiers were coming. This was the arithmetic of fear, and fear was winning, until Anders saw Torben and the Ostergaards already moving along the quay with axes and knives, and understood that his crew had decided the question before he had.

"Cut them!" Anders roared, and ran.

What followed lasted, by his reckoning, less than four minutes, though it would occupy the rest of his life. The heavy hawsers went under the blades, one after another, the great ropes parting with a groan and a snap that he felt in his teeth. The Meridian swung free of the quay with the slow majesty of something enormous changing its mind. Her propeller bit water. Steam roared from her funnel. On her stern deck, tiny in the growing light, Liv Halvard stood with her great-grandmother's shawl clutched to her chest, looking back at all of them, and did not stop looking until the mist took her.

Then the shooting started.

It was wild, mostly — soldiers firing over the water at a departing hull, orders shouted and countermanded, the whole apparatus of the new regime discovering that its authority ended at the waterline. But wild bullets kill as thoroughly as aimed ones. A round struck the capstan beside Anders and threw iron splinters across his cheek. Another punched through the warehouse shutter above his head. He grabbed his mother's arm and pulled her low behind the bollards, and crouched there in the smoke and spray, and that was when he saw Juniper.

She was running — not away from the soldiers, which would have been sensible, but along the quay toward the pilot skiffs moored at the north stair, toward the water, toward the blockade line and the open sea beyond it. Toward Sweden, or toward whatever she intended. Reval's men had seen her too. Two of them broke from the group and gave chase, boots hammering the stones, rifles rising.

Everything Anders did next, he did without deliberation, the way a man catches a falling child — the body deciding before the mind is consulted. He was closer to the skiff stair than anyone. Juniper reached the top step, hauled the painter free, and turned to find the soldiers thirty yards away and closing, and found Anders Halvard standing between her and them with his arms spread wide, an absurd human gate, a shipwright who had spent his life building ways for people to leave.

"Skiff!" he shouted at her. "Go! Row for the fishing fleet — they'll take you past the picket!"

"Anders—"

"That wasn't a request! It's a debt! Twelve years of interest!"

The first soldier reached him. Anders did not fight so much as obstruct, the way timber obstructs, taking a rifle butt across the shoulder and wrapping his arms around the man's barrel chest and simply refusing, with all his considerable weight, to let the passage through happen quickly. The second soldier circled. Somewhere behind them an officer was shouting something about the film, about the courier, about *hold her*, and Anders heard the words as if through water, because Juniper was already at the skiff's thwart, oars in the locks, pulling away from the stair into the grey chop.

She looked back once. Rain and spray had plastered her hair flat. Her face was streaming with water that might have been the weather.

"The archive!" she called — one phrase thrown across the widening gap, pitched to carry, pitched for him alone. "Tell them where it lives!" And then she bent to the oars, and the skiff leapt, and the harbor took her.

A soldier fired. Anders flinched, expecting the impact, and realized the shot had gone wide — over the water, chasing the skiff, striking nothing but sea. The soldier fired again, and again, muzzle flashes stuttering in the mist, and Anders watched the little boat jink and surge through the falling rounds, watched it grow smaller, watched it reach the shadow of the fishing fleet at the harbor mouth where a dozen neutral hulls lay at anchor like a congregation of witnesses —

— and then the current took the story in a way no one expected.

* * *

Afterward, they pieced it together from the fishermen, and the account never varied enough to be doubted.

Juniper Eriksen had reached the fleet. She had hailed a boat — the *Solveig*, old Brandt's ketch — and there had been a brief conference at the rail, voices too low to carry across the water. Then, according to Brandt's boy, she had done something strange. She had taken a small sealed tin from inside her coat, kissed it once — actually kissed it, the boy insisted, though boys insist such things — and handed it to the old fisherman with instructions.

The blockade picket was ahead. The *Solveig* would be boarded; everything would be searched; anything she carried would be found. But the harbor current ran strong through the fleet anchorage, ebbing hard on the turn, straight out through the gate channel into the open Baltic. The tin was watertight — courier's kit, military specification, built to survive immersion. The current was going where no regime could follow.

Juniper Eriksen chose the current.

Brandt argued with her — the boy was certain of that, saw the old man's arms go up, saw hers answer — and lost. She climbed down the *Solveig*'s ladder to the waterline herself, leaned out over the grey sea with the tin held in both hands, and let it go. The current took it under within a heartbeat. By the time the picket boat reached the ketch twenty minutes later, boarded her, turned her out, and found nothing but fish and honest nets, the true dispatches of the Republic of Vessmark — proof of foreign financing, proof of the coup's architects, the whole terrible ledger — were somewhere beneath the gray swells, moving east with the ebb toward deep water, undeliverable to any government, any court, any cause. Ours, Anders thought later. No one's. The sea's.

Reval would rage about it, they said. Reval would spend weeks dragging nets and questioning fishermen and filing reports in his immaculate handwriting, and the reports would all say the same thing in different words: *asset lost at sea, evidence unrecoverable.* He had come to Kalshavn for a film and a family, and he would leave with neither, and there was a satisfaction in that which Anders allowed himself to feel fully for exactly one day before setting it aside, because satisfaction was a luxury and the yard had work.

Juniper herself, the boy said, had been taken off the *Solveig* at gunpoint, calm as Sunday, hands folded, saying nothing. They rowed her back toward the quay as the Meridian's smoke faded south, and Anders watched the small boat cross the grey water with the woman who had given his sister the future sitting straight-backed in the stern between two soldiers, and he raised his hand to her, stupidly, uselessly, knowing she could not see it or would not show it if she could.

The skiff bumped the stair below him. He looked down and found Torben in it, white-faced, holding his side.

"Gate crew got the clutch free," Torben rasped. "Soldiers are coming back up the quay. Anders — they'll want names."

"Yes," Anders said. "They will."

* * *

They came for the family at noon, when the mist had burned away and the harbor lay bright and indifferent under a washed sky. Captain Reval arrived with them, of course — immaculate, unhurried, his boots ringing on the stones of the Halvard yard as though he had inherited it — and stood in the sawdust while his men formed a loose ring, and looked at Anders with something that was almost professional respect.

"The film entered the sea this morning," Reval said. It was not a question, and he did not seem to require an answer. "You understand what that costs me. What it costs everyone." He glanced at Maren, at the kitchen window where she stood with her arms folded, refusing to retreat indoors. "Detain them. The house, the office, the lot — process them as material witnesses pending review." A pause, and then, dryly, to no one in particular: "There will be a great deal of review. Nothing here is worth shooting."

As they walked him out — hands bound loosely, neighbors watching from doorways with the careful blankness of people memorizing faces for later — Anders passed the harbor gate. The repair crew had finally freed the mechanism; the great gates stood open to admit the morning traffic, and the ebb tide was running through them fast and clean toward the open sea. Somewhere under all that moving water, he knew, the truth of the coup was traveling east, patient as sediment, waiting for a century or a change of government to set it down somewhere it could speak.

His cheek still bled where the capstan splinters had cut it. His shoulder would carry the rifle bruise for a month. Behind him his mother was already, incredibly, telling the young soldier at the kitchen door that there was coffee if he wanted it, and ahead of him was a cell, and a winter, and a country that no longer existed — and none of it, none of it, felt like defeat, because out past the lighthouse, small and stubborn and alive, a ship named Meridian was carrying a Halvard girl toward a horizon that three purges had never managed to close.

"We build ships so people can leave," Anders said aloud, to no one, to the tide.

The gate guard looked at him strangely. Anders smiled with his split lip, and let himself be marched into the new regime's custody like a man carrying something they would never find.
