## 1. The Route

There is a particular skill to being forgotten in a small town, and I had perfected it by the age of twenty-four: you must be useful enough that everyone knows your face, and dull enough that no one wonders where you go when the engine cools.

My name — the name on my papers, which is to say the name that exists — is Sebastian Lockridge. I drive for Mrs. Edith Marlowe, who is organizing the women of this county into something the newspapers call a menace and she calls a league, and I am very good at driving. That is the first thing anyone will tell you about me if they tell you anything at all. *Lockridge? Steady hands. Never late. Doesn't say much.*

The second thing they might tell you, if they were paying attention, which they are not, is that I read every letter that passes through that car.

Not because I am curious. Well — partly because I am curious; let's not be sentimental about it. But mostly because a driver who reads is a driver who knows where the movement bleeds, and a movement that doesn't know where it bleeds is a movement that dies of a wound nobody noticed until the funeral.

* * *

Tuesday mornings began at the Marlowe house on Chapel Street, which was not really her house but her sister's, though her sister had been in Brighton since 1914 and the house had quietly become a headquarters the way spare rooms do. I would arrive at half past seven with the engine already warm — cold engines make noise, and noise attracts questions, and questions are how organizations get hanged — and Mrs. Marlowe would come down the front steps with her collar buttoned to her chin and a satchel that weighed like sin.

"Good morning, Lockridge."

"Morning, ma'am."

She never asked whether I'd slept well or eaten breakfast. She asked about roads, weather, and once — memorably — whether I could tell a constable from a special constable at forty yards in fog. I said yes. She nodded as though this settled some question she'd been carrying, and got in.

That morning there were eleven stops on the route. I knew them before she read them out, because I had copied the route book myself two nights prior while she argued politics over supper with the vicar's wife, who was against votes for women but very much in favor of the sherry.

"Harrow Lane first," she said. "Then the mill houses. Miss Pargeter has letters for the weavers' wives."

"Yes, ma'am."

"And we'll take the long way past the school. There's a woman in the third cottage — grey door, blue shutters — who wants to join but won't come to meetings. Her husband works at the depot plans office." She said it lightly, the way one says *we'll take the long way because the road floods*, and I understood that the grey door was now on my list too, along with everything else on every list, filed in the place behind my eyes where other people keep their mothers' birthdays.

I drove. She wrote. The car smelled of petrol and lavender, an combination I have never encountered anywhere else and expect never to again.

* * *

You should understand what the route looked like in the spring of 1917, because by autumn it would be unrecognizable, and someone ought to have written it down while the grey doors were still grey.

Harrow Lane was four cottages and a bakehouse, and the bakehouse was where Mrs. Tull kept the subscription list under the flour ledger, because no sergeant in England would search a flour ledger for fear of being accused of baking. At the mill houses the letters went through Miss Pargeter, a narrow woman with ink-stained cuffs who signed her correspondence with a name that wasn't hers — I know because I read it — and who had once told a recruiting officer, within my hearing, that her brothers were all dead and therefore exempt from patriotism.

At the school end of the long way stood the grey door. The woman who answered it — Mrs. Aldous, though the letters called her only "the friend past the school" — took the envelope with both hands, the way you take something hot out of an oven, and said nothing except "thank you," and closed the door, and I sat in the idling car feeling the peculiar ache of having witnessed a small act of courage that no one would ever applaud.

Eleven stops. Forty-one envelopes. I counted the envelopes the way other men count sheep, and by the time we turned for home I knew the count, the senders, and — because Mrs. Marlowe dictated replies aloud when she thought the engine noise covered her — most of the answers.

"You're quiet today, Lockridge."

"I'm always quiet, ma'am."

"That's why I keep you." She smiled at the window. "A talking driver is a liability. A silent one is furniture."

I have been called many things in my life, most of them in whispers, but *furniture* was the kindest lie anyone ever told me, and I accepted it graciously.

* * *

Here is what nobody in that town knew, including — especially — the woman who trusted me with her life's work several times a week:

Every night, after the car was garaged and the oil checked and the day declared respectable, I sat at the desk in my lodgings above the saddler's shop with a candle turned low and a notebook no bigger than a prayer book, and I copied out what I had memorized on the road. Names. Addresses. Routes. The grey door. Mrs. Tull's flour ledger. Miss Pargeter's false signature. Which cottages had husbands home and which had husbands in France and which had husbands who had stopped writing, a distinction that mattered more than any of us liked to say.

Why did I keep it? In fairness to myself, I asked that question nightly, usually around the second page, usually with the candle guttering and my hand cramping.

Partly because paper remembers better than I do, and I had learned — in another life, under another set of facts — that memory unbacked by paper is just a rumor you tell yourself. Partly because if anything happened to Mrs. Marlowe, or to the satchel, or to the little network of grey-doored women holding their hot envelopes, then the record of who they were and what they risked would exist nowhere except in official files, and official files are written by the people doing the arresting.

And partly — I will admit this to a page no one is meant to read — because writing their names down was the closest thing I had to belonging anywhere.

That sounds self-pitying. It isn't meant to be. Let me put it plainly: I lived in a town of perhaps four thousand souls, and every one of them knew Sebastian Lockridge, and not one of them knew him. He was steady-handed and silent and had no people and no history worth asking after, because a history invites questions, and questions are how organizations — and drivers — get hanged. At the Marlowe house I was furniture. At the saddler's I was the quiet lodger who paid on time. At the garage I was good with engines. I was everywhere and I was nowhere, and the notebook, with its careful columns of other people's dangerous lives, was the only place in England where I existed on purpose.

Some men keep diaries. I kept a census of the resistance, in a hand disguised as a clerk's, in an alphabet of abbreviations I invented myself and can still recite asleep. If that is loneliness, then loneliness is at least productive.

* * *

Wednesday it rained, which was good for the work. Rain keeps windows up and conversations private and constables indoors drinking tea and resenting their careers.

We ran the western loop: the farms, the quarry village, the widow Hartree, who fed us seed cake in her kitchen and talked for twenty minutes about her hens while passing three envelopes across the table as though handing over recipes. Mrs. Marlowe ate the cake. She was constitutionally incapable of refusing food offered by a member, which I privately considered a security flaw, though I never said so. A driver who offers opinions becomes a passenger's equal, and equals get dismissed, and dismissed drivers don't hear dictation over engine noise.

On the way back she was quiet for a long stretch, watching the hedgerows blur, and then she said, without preamble:

"The requisition rumors are getting worse."

"What rumors are those, ma'am?"

"The War Office wants land south of here. Depot ground. Shells, powder, the whole apparatus." She said it the way she said everything, evenly, as though reporting the price of coal. "Half the parish, if the talk is true."

"Talk usually exaggerates."

"Talk usually precedes." She turned from the window and looked at me, and I kept my eyes on the road because her looking-at-you was a thing best endured in profile. "If it happens, Lockridge, the lists matter more than ever. Every name. Where people go, who stays in contact. Dispersed organizations die unless someone holds the thread."

"I'll hold the thread, ma'am."

"I know you will." And then, softer, almost to herself: "It's strange, isn't it. We spend years building something invisible so it can't be seized. And the moment the government wants land, invisibility becomes a problem. You can't claim compensation for a thing that officially never existed."

I made a neutral sound. Inside, something cold turned over, because I had been thinking about seizure — of papers, of names, of histories — since before she'd started, and I did not enjoy hearing my private weather forecast read aloud by the woman it concerned.

But I said nothing. Furniture doesn't.

* * *

Thursday evening, above the saddler's, I did the copying.

The candle was down to its last inch, the rain had moved off toward the coast, and the notebook lay open at a fresh page with the heading I used for nothing else: *W.* — for Wednesday, for west, and, if I'm honest, for the weight of it. Widow Hartree. Quarry cottages, six subscribers. The boy at the quarry gate who carried messages for a penny and didn't know whose messages they were, and God forgive me, I wrote his description down too, because he was fourteen and fourteen-year-olds get questioned roughly.

Forty-one names that week, give or take. Add them to the rest. Two hundred and six entries since January, each one a person who had decided, in the privacy of a kitchen or a bakehouse or a grey-door hallway, that they could no longer stand politely aside — and who had told exactly one stranger, a woman with a satchel, and been entered into history by a driver they believed couldn't spell.

I closed the book. I pressed my thumb briefly against the cover, which is not a ritual, I told myself, merely a habit, merely checking the string was tied.

Below me, through the floorboards, the saddler coughed himself toward sleep, and the town did what towns do — banked its fires, bolted nothing because nothing needed bolting, and trusted the world to stay the shape it was.

I blew out the candle and lay in the dark composing tomorrow's route in my head, stop by stop, envelope by envelope, and it was only near midnight, on the edge of sleep, that I admitted the thing the rain on the roof had been telling me all evening:

Mrs. Marlowe hadn't mentioned the requisition rumors casually. Mrs. Marlowe never did anything casually. She had watched me while she said it, in that profile-enduring way, and she had spoken of lists and threads and records, and she had said *someone holds the thread* while looking directly at the only person in the car.

Which raised a question I did not want to ask at midnight, and would be unable to stop asking by dawn:

How long had the organizer suspected that her furniture could read?

And what, precisely, was she going to trust me with next?
