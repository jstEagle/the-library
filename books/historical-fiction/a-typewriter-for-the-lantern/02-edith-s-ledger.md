## 2. Edith's Ledger

Edith Marlowe had a way of handing you something dangerous as though it were a casserole.

"Take this to Mrs. Fenwick in Harrowgate," she said that morning, setting a wrapped parcel on the seat between us with the casualness of a woman passing along a fruitcake. "Mind the corners of the box. And mind the road out of Millbrook — there's a constable who's developed opinions about women in motorcars."

I looked at the parcel. It was about the size of a hymnal and considerably heavier than any scripture I'd ever been asked to carry, which is saying something, given my upbringing.

"What is it?"

"You know what it is."

She was right, of course. I knew what it was the way you know a thing you've been circling for months without being permitted to touch it — by its weight, by the way she held it slightly apart from her body as though it were warm-blooded, by the fact that she'd chosen me to carry it at all.

The ledger. The master ledger. The book in which Edith Marlowe had spent eleven years writing down the names of every woman who had ever signed a petition, paid a shilling, housed a speaker, or written so much as an angry letter to the *Millbrook Courier* on the subject of votes for women. Members, donors, correspondents. The nervous ones, the fierce ones, the ones who'd joined after their husbands died and the ones who joined over their husbands' living objections. Eleven years of names, addresses, subscriptions paid in half-crowns and eggs and one memorable instance of a goat, all recorded in Edith's small, upright hand, which looked like the handwriting of a woman who had never once doubted where the next word would go.

And she was putting it in my car.

* * *

Let me explain something about Edith Marlowe, since you'll be hearing a great deal about her and history has already begun sanding her down into either a saint or a nuisance depending on which pamphlet you pick up. She was neither. She was a tall, dry, unhurried woman of fifty-three who wore grey because it didn't show dust and spoke in the tone of someone reading a shopping list aloud to herself, even when the list contained items like "we shall need to get Mrs. Ashcombe out before the magistrate issues the warrant." She had buried a husband, raised no children, converted a drawing room into an organizing office, and made the local Liberal association so uncomfortable that two of its members had taken to leaving through the kitchen when they saw her coming up the front path.

She did not trust easily. This is not a character flaw in an organizer; it is the entire job description.

So when she handed me the ledger without ceremony, without even asking me to sign for it — Edith signed for everything, Edith believed in receipts the way other women believed in prayer — I understood that I was being told something. Not asked. Told. The way one tells a doorframe it will now bear weight.

"I'll want it back by Thursday," she said, settling into the passenger seat and arranging her skirt with three precise movements. "Mrs. Fenwick is copying the southern district names onto tissue paper. If we're going to have duplicates, I'd rather have them scattered than concentrated."

"Scattered," I repeated, starting the engine. "Like seed."

"Don't be poetic, Lockridge, it doesn't suit the petrol fumes."

But her mouth did something at the corner that on another woman might have been called a smile, and we drove out of Millbrook with eleven years of the movement riding between us like a sleeping animal I was afraid to look at directly.

* * *

The road to Harrowgate runs through country that pretends to be gentle. Low hills, hedgerows, sheep doing their eternal impression of contentment. In October the light comes sideways and gold, and if you didn't know what was happening — if you hadn't spent the last year driving through it, watching recruiting posters go up on the same walls where suffrage bills had been pasted, watching the young men of four parishes march off to be subtracted — you could almost believe the world was holding still.

I knew better. I drove through this country six days a week and I read its mail at night, and I can tell you that the world does not hold still. It leans. It leans very hard in one direction and pretends to be standing straight.

Here I should confess something, though I confessed it to no one for forty years: I read the letters. All of them. Every envelope Edith entrusted to the car, I opened at night in my room above the livery stable with a kettle of water and a bone folder, read them through twice, sealed them again with a flatiron heated just shy of scorching, and delivered them the next morning looking exactly like a driver who had never once wondered what was inside. It began as caution — I wanted to know what I was carrying, in case carrying it became a crime, which it eventually did — but caution curdled into appetite almost immediately. Because here is the thing about a movement that lives on paper: the paper is more honest than the people. Women wrote things to Edith they never said aloud. They wrote about husbands who'd struck them, about sons who'd enlisted to escape home, about the specific loneliness of being right in a town that preferred you wrong and quiet. They wrote in code sometimes — "my sister's cough is better, tell the doctor" meant the Harrowgate meeting was safe — and I cracked the codes within weeks, because codes are only gossip wearing a disguise, and I have never in my life resisted gossip.

I kept a notebook. A cheap one, oilcloth-covered, bought from the stationer in Millbrook for tuppence, the kind schoolboys use. Into it I copied the network: who knew whom, who could be trusted with money, who had already been arrested once and would be watched, whose husband read her correspondence and therefore required the flatiron treatment most especially. Names, addresses, relationships, weaknesses, strengths. Edith had her ledger; I had mine; neither of us knew about the other's, or rather, Edith suspected hers existed somewhere in the world and I knew mine existed under a floorboard, which is the difference between faith and knowledge.

I thought of myself as a shadow copy. A redundancy. If anything happened to Edith's book, mine would hold.

I want you to notice, because I did not notice at the time, how much I enjoyed this. There was a pleasure in it that I didn't examine for years — the pleasure of mattering invisibly, of being the second copy of something important. I had built a life out of being nobody in particular, and keeping that notebook let me be nobody with a purpose.

* * *

We stopped at the Halfway House outside Corley for tea, because Edith believed in schedules and the schedule included tea, and while she was inside conducting what she would later describe as "a brief exchange" and what the landlord would later describe as "that Marlowe woman interrogating me about his wife's subscription," I did a foolish thing.

I unwrapped the parcel.

Just the top corner. Just enough to see. I sat in the driver's seat with the ledger on my knees under a fold of sacking, and I turned back the brown paper from the corner of the box, and I lifted the cover of the book itself.

The pages were dense. Edith's handwriting marched across them in columns — name, parish, date joined, contributions, notes. Some entries ran to lines; some ran to paragraphs. Beside certain names she'd made small marks I recognized from my own notebook, having invented several of them independently: a cross for "watched," a circle for "reliable," a tiny *w* for "husband." We had arrived, separately and secretly, at the same shorthand for other people's lives. I found that both funny and unsettling, the way you find your own handwriting in a stranger's letter.

I turned pages. 1909, 1911, the great petition year of 1913. And then, near the front, in the section headed *Drivers and Messengers*, I stopped.

There it was. My name.

**Lockridge, S. — driver, retained Jan. 1916. Reliable. Discreet. No family.**

No family. Two words, accurate as far as they went and a lie in every direction they didn't. Edith had vetted me — of course she had, she vetted everyone, probably the vicar had a file — and what her inquiry had turned up was exactly nothing, a clean absence, a man with no past worth mentioning. That was the whole architecture of Sebastian Lockridge: a man composed entirely of negative space. Papers in order, references vague, history smooth as a river stone. No family. No one to write to. No one to ask questions. No one to miss him if he vanished.

I sat there with the engine ticking and read my own entry until the letters stopped meaning anything.

Here is what I felt, and I report it honestly because the honest version is stranger than the dramatic one. I did not feel fear. What I felt was vertigo of a particular paper-colored kind — the sensation of seeing yourself confirmed as a fiction. Somewhere in the world, presumably, there had once been documents proving that the real Sebastian Lockridge existed: a birth record, perhaps, a christening, a school register. I had acquired those papers the way one acquires a coat from a dead man, and I wore them well, and for two years they had fit so perfectly I sometimes forgot they weren't mine. But there in Edith's ledger, reduced to four words in her upright hand, I saw myself as the movement saw me: a convenience. Reliable. Discreet. No family.

A tool that worked. And tools, I understood suddenly, are exactly the things you write down, because writing a tool down costs you nothing. You don't write down what you love. You write down what you use.

The parcel paper rustled. Edith was crossing the yard, teacup abandoned, moving with that long unhurried stride of hers, and I had perhaps ten seconds. I rewrapped the corner, smoothed the paper, set the box precisely where it had been, and got out to open her door like the reliable, discreet, family-less man I was officially documented to be.

"Mrs. Hobbs sends her regards," Edith said, folding herself into the seat, "and her subscription, which is in the parcel with the rest. Don't lose it."

"I never lose anything," I said.

"No," she agreed mildly, looking straight ahead through the windscreen. "You don't. That's why you're here."

I have replayed that sentence perhaps nine thousand times in the intervening decades, searching it for meanings the way a diviner searches a field for water. Was it praise? A warning? Did she know? Could she possibly have known? Edith Marlowe knew things about people the way weather knows things about coastlines — comprehensively, impersonally, and without needing to announce itself. I have decided, finally, that she was simply stating a fact: I was hired to keep things, and I kept them, and whatever else I kept beyond the scope of my employment was my own affair and possibly her own suspicion.

At the time, I merely started the engine and drove.

* * *

Harrowgate took us until mid-afternoon. Mrs. Fenwick was a widow with ink-stained fingers and a house that smelled of camphor and hot starch, and she received the ledger the way priests receive relics — hands washed first, I'm not inventing that detail, she actually washed her hands before touching the box. She and Edith disappeared upstairs to copy names onto tissue paper while I sat in the kitchen drinking tea I didn't want and listening to the clock tick off minutes I couldn't account for.

That's when the dread caught up with the intimacy, the way dread always walks a few steps behind and then arrives all at once.

Because I had seen the entry. I knew now what the ledger was in full: not just a membership roll but a map of everyone the movement had ever touched, annotated with judgments, vulnerabilities, marks for *watched*. Eleven years of trust, transcribed. And it was riding around the county in a motorcar driven by a man who did not exist, guarded by nothing but brown paper and the general disinclination of constables to search respectable vehicles.

If that book were seized — by police, by magistrates, by anyone with authority and malice — it wouldn't be evidence against Edith alone. It would be a sentence for every name in it. Every half-crown donor, every woman who'd sheltered a speaker, every correspondent who'd put treasonous sentiments in ink and posted them to a known address. One box. Hundreds of lives, filed alphabetically.

I had spent a year thinking of my notebook as the backup. Sitting in Mrs. Fenwick's kitchen, I understood for the first time that backups cut both ways. If Edith's ledger fell into the wrong hands, my little oilcloth book would become the second copy of a catastrophe. I was not preserving the movement. I was duplicating its exposure.

And yet — and this is the part I've never fully made peace with — I also understood that the duplication was necessary. Movements die of concentration. Edith knew it; that was the whole point of scattering copies across the country like seed, like ash, like everything that survives by being spread thin. The book had to exist in more than one place, and I was one of the places, and the price of being a place is that you can be burned.

When they came downstairs at dusk, Edith was tying the parcel with fresh string.

"It stays with Mrs. Fenwick tonight," she said. "You'll take it to the printer's tomorrow — Ashcombe's shop, in Millbrook. He's making the duplicate plates." She paused, and looked at me with those pale, level eyes that had assessed half the women in England and found most of them wanting. "Lockridge. Do you understand what you're carrying?"

"A book," I said, "with a great many names in it."

"A country," she corrected. "A small one. The kind of country that gets invaded." She handed me the string ends to knot, which I took for the compliment it probably wasn't. "People fight wars over less than what's in this box. Remember that, the next time you're tempted to leave it on the car seat while you buy cigarettes."

I knotted the string. My hands were steady, which surprised me; inside, something had begun quietly rearranging itself, the way furniture shifts in a house during a distant explosion.

We drove back through the dark with the ledger on the seat between us, and neither of us touched it, and the hedgerows came at us black out of the night, and somewhere behind us in Harrowgate a widow was sleeping one floor below a copy of every name that mattered, and ahead of us in Millbrook a printer was waiting to make more.

I did not yet know that the army was coming. Nobody did. The requisition notices were still ten days away from the telegraph office, and Captain Voss was still a signature on a document in the capital, and the filing room where I would one day stand in the dark was still a drill hall full of band instruments.

All I knew, driving home that night, was that I had looked into the book and found myself written there — four words long, no family, reliable — and that a piece of paper had just told me the truth about my life more plainly than any person ever had.

I existed only where I was written. Which meant that whoever held the writing, held me.

Two weeks later, the soldiers came for the hall, and then for the records, and then for the book.

But I'm ahead of the story, and Edith always hated when I drove ahead of the story.
