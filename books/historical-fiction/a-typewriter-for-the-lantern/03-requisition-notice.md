## 3. Requisition Notice

The notice went up on a Tuesday, which struck me as obscene. Catastrophes should arrive on Sundays, when the church bells are already ringing and no one has to be pulled away from anything. Instead the town of Marleigh learned its fate between the greengrocer's and the ironmonger's, at eye level, in four sheets of paper pasted to a board that had previously held a lost-dog poster and an advertisement for cod liver oil.

I was standing in the queue for bread when the corporal and his two men came down the high street with a bucket of paste and a ladder. You could watch the whole street rearrange itself around them — not dramatically, nothing so honest as a scream, but the way water rearranges itself around a dropped stone. Mrs. Petchey stopped mid-sentence about her rheumatism. The baker leaned out his door until flour dust hung about him like a question. Even the dog outside the tobacconist's went quiet.

The notices were printed on good stock, I'll grant them that. Whoever had drafted them understood that authority is mostly typography. The heading was large and centered: **NOTICE OF REQUISITION UNDER DEFENCE OF THE REALM**. Beneath it, in language so clean it seemed embarrassed by its own content:

*By order of the competent military authority, the town of Marleigh and surrounding lands are required for the establishment of a munitions depot. All residents shall vacate within fourteen (14) days. All books, records, registers, correspondence, and documents of every kind held by any person, business, or organization shall be surrendered for security review. Failure to comply will be treated as conduct prejudicial to His Majesty's forces.*

Fourteen days. Security review. Conduct prejudicial. I read it three times, and each time my eyes snagged on a different phrase like a stocking on a nail. Fourteen days was generous — they wanted you to notice that, wanted you to feel grateful for the courtesy of your own eviction. *Security review* was the one doing the real work. Records. Registers. Correspondence. Documents of every kind.

Of every kind.

I had spent eighteen months learning what lived inside Edith Marlowe's ledger. Not the names alone — anyone could copy names. What lived there were the connections: who gave money through which third party, whose husband was a magistrate and therefore received letters addressed to his sister instead, which farmhouses along the north road would hide a speaker overnight if her train arrived late. Eighteen months of driving Edith to forty-one towns and reading every letter she let me carry, and memorizing, because I am apparently constitutionally incapable of carrying information without also hoarding it like a magpie with a grudge.

And somewhere in that ledger, in the third column from the left under donors, was a name written in Edith's careful hand: *Sebastian Lockridge, driver, £1 per quarter.* My name. The name I had built out of a dead boy's birth certificate and a borrowed accent and three years of practice signing it without hesitation. If the ledger was seized, I did not merely lose proof of my work. I lost proof of myself. There is a particular vertigo in realizing that the state might confiscate your existence before lunch.

"You're staring at it like it owes you money," said the woman behind me in the queue, who turned out to be Ada Quill, the postmistress's sister, holding a basket with two eggs in it as though they were munitions themselves.

"Just reading," I said.

"Everyone's reading." She lowered her voice in a way meant to be overheard. "My Bert says it's temporary. Says once the depot's built they'll let folk come back to work it."

"Does he."

"Bert says a lot of things." She looked at the notice again, and something moved behind her face that I recognized, because I wore it daily: the arithmetic of a person deciding, in advance, how much of herself she can afford to lose. "Fourteen days," she said. "That's not even enough time to argue."

"That," I said, "is rather the point of fourteen days."

She looked at me sharply then, and I smiled my driver's smile — pleasant, vacant, the smile of a man whose opinions extend no further than tire pressure. It is a good smile. I have relied on it more than on any document I own.

* * *

By evening the town had divided itself into three parties, and the division happened so smoothly you'd have thought we'd rehearsed it.

There were those who read the notice and went pale and began packing. There were those who read it and went pink and began explaining why it was necessary — the war effort, the Hun, duty, sacrifice, all the words that function less as arguments than as doors closing. And there were those who did not read it at all but positioned themselves near it, hoping to be seen reading it, in case being seen mattered later.

Mr. Calloway of the ironmonger's belonged to the second party, and announced it within hours. He took down his shutters early and told a gathering crowd that resistance was pointless, that the army had been perfectly civil about it, that in his opinion some of the agitation in this district — and here he paused delicately, letting everyone supply the word *suffragist* themselves — had brought this on us. A place that harbored troublemakers, he said, could hardly complain when the authorities decided it needed looking after.

He got a smattering of agreement, mostly from men who owed him money.

What interested me was not Calloway. Calloways are a constant; you can set your watch by them. What interested me was Mrs. Fenwick, who ran the lending library and had signed Edith's petition twice, standing at the edge of Calloway's audience with her face arranged into thoughtful neutrality, nodding at intervals, and then going home and — I learned this later, and it cost something to learn — writing a letter to the military governor offering full access to her subscription records, "in the interest of transparency."

Transparency. I have come to hate that word the way sailors hate a specific rock that has sunk their friends.

But I want to be fair to Mrs. Fenwick, because fairness is the discipline I imposed on myself when I started keeping names. She had a son at the Front and a daughter-in-law dependent on her, and fear does not make people wicked so much as it makes them small. She chose smallness. Most people do. I have simply never had the option, which is not the same as being braver — it is the same as having less to lose and knowing precisely what it is.

Edith, when I drove her past the notice board that afternoon, did not go pale or pink. She read it the way she read everything, top to bottom, lips barely moving, one gloved finger tracing the air an inch off the paper as though she might correct its punctuation. Then she stepped back and said:

"Records."

"Ma'am?"

"They're not after the houses, Seb. They can requisition empty fields for a depot. They're after the records. Fourteen days is how long it takes to box papers properly." She smoothed her glove. "Someone in the War Office has read our file and wants the rest of it."

I did not say: *ma'am, I have read your file too, cover to cover, in lamplight, while you slept in the passenger seat, and I could recite the donor column backwards.* I said: "Shall I take the long way home, ma'am?" Because that is what I was, officially: a driver who takes the long way home.

"Yes," she said. "Drive slowly. I want to look at my town."

* * *

We drove slowly. It was Edith who named things, always, and she named this one as we passed the schoolhouse: "Occupation. That's the word for it, whatever they print. We are being occupied by our own side."

Marleigh in October light, seen at eight miles an hour: the crooked sign of the Lamb and Flag; the allotments with their last beans gone leathery on the stalk; children chalking hopscotch squares on the pavement, unaware that the pavement had fourteen days left to be theirs. A woman beating a rug over a railing, the dust rising gold. Everything ordinary, and therefore unbearable, because I knew — and now the whole street half-knew — that ordinariness is a loan, not a possession.

At the corner of Mill Lane a soldier was nailing a second notice to the chapel door, and the minister, old Mr. Tredwell, stood watching with his hat in his hands. As we rolled past at funeral speed I saw him ask the soldier a question, and I saw the soldier shrug, and I saw Mr. Tredwell put his hat back on like a man closing up a shop. He would surrender the chapel rolls, I thought. He would call it rendering unto Caesar. And the chapel rolls contained, in Edith's shorthand, the names of eleven women who sheltered speakers, recorded as "sewing circle attendance."

I added Tredwell to the list in my head. Not a list of enemies. A list, simply, of what the next fortnight would do to whom.

* * *

That night I sat in my room above the livery stable with the lamp turned low and my notebook open — the hidden one, the true one, cheap paper sewn into the false cover of a motor maintenance log. Every address I'd ever copied. Every route. Every name Edith had spoken aloud in the car, believing — reasonably, since I was her quiet, reliable Seb — that the car walls kept secrets.

They did keep secrets. Mine better than hers.

I wrote the date at the top of a fresh page and beneath it: *Requisition posted. 14 days. All records seized.* Then I sat with the pen hovering, because the next line was the hard one, the line I'd been circling since morning:

*If the ledger goes to London, Sebastian Lockridge becomes a fact the government owns.*

A man exists on paper or he doesn't. That had been my founding principle, learned young and expensively: papers are armor. I had built mine plank by plank — the brother's certificate, the school references forged in a hand I'd practiced until it cramped, the National Insurance card with its reassuring official stamp. Every document genuine-looking because most of it was genuine, assembled around one small forgery the way a pearl forms around grit. And now the largest archive of my life outside my own head was about to be crated and carried off by clerks who would read it with bored efficiency and file me under *S*.

Unless.

Unless someone copied it first. Unless the record that mattered — the true one, the whole one, names and routes and letters and all — existed somewhere the requisition couldn't reach. Somewhere between my ears, and soon, God help me, on paper again, hidden better than Edith hid hers.

I blew out the lamp and sat in the dark listening to the stable clock and thinking about Mrs. Fenwick's letter and Mr. Calloway's audience and the corporal's excellent typography. The town was cracking open along lines nobody had drawn yet, and everyone was choosing, right now, tonight, which side of their own character to stand on when it split.

In the morning, Edith sent me to collect the ledger from the safe at Ashcott's — and that is how I came to be the one holding it when the soldiers arrived.
