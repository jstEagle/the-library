## 4. What the Sergeant Reads

The requisition notices had been up for six days when I learned the army's greatest weapon was not the bayonet but the filing cabinet.

I want to be precise about that, because precision is what saved me later and damned me sooner. A man with a rifle can only kill you once. A man with a rubber stamp and a ledger can kill you over and over — kill you in the record, kill you retroactively, kill you so thoroughly that your own grandchildren will insist you never existed. I had spent eleven years building a self out of paper, so you would think I'd have understood this instinctively. But there is a difference between knowing a thing and watching a bored sergeant demonstrate it at a folding table with ink on his cuffs.

* * *

The hall had been the Odd Fellows' lodge before it was a records depot, and before that a dance pavilion, and I remember it best as the place where Mrs. Hargrove's nephew fainted during a recitation of "The Wreck of the Hesperus" in 1909. Now it smelled of carbolic soap and cigarette smoke, and its parquet floors were scarred by army boots, and along one whole wall stood the confiscated papers of our town in numbered crates: church registers, school attendance books, the grange minutes, Dr. Prewitt's patient files, the co-operative's accounts.

And Edith Marlowe's ledger.

I had driven it there myself. That is the sentence I keep returning to, the stone in my shoe. Not that the soldiers took it — they took everything, they took the christening spoons off the mantelpieces of houses whose families were still eating breakfast — but that I carried it up those steps in my own arms, because Edith asked, because she said it was safer with me than hidden, because I believed her. There are two kinds of fool: the kind who trusts too little and the kind who trusts exactly the wrong amount. I have been both, sometimes in the same afternoon.

They had kept me on, you see. That was the arrangement I'd volunteered into like a woman stepping into cold water all at once rather than inching in. The army needed drivers who knew the lanes, which flooded, which the milk lorries used, where the culvert on Marsh Road had been sagging since the flood of '12. They retained four of us local men. Two were boys barely shaving; one was Mr. Tredwell, who had decided early that collaboration was simply good housekeeping and said so cheerfully to anyone who'd listen; and the fourth was Sebastian Lockridge, aged twenty-three, steady hands, no family to speak of, no opinions worth noting.

No opinions worth noting. I had worked very hard on that sentence for eleven years.

* * *

On the fourth morning they set me to waiting. This is most of what an army driver does — wait — and I had learned to wait invisibly, hat brim low, hands still, a man-shaped absence near a lorry. But that morning the clerks had spread their work out into the open hall because the side rooms couldn't hold the crates, and so I waited within full view of the table where Sergeant Deel sat with Edith's ledger open before him.

I knew his name already. You learn names the way other people learn weather; it keeps you alive. Sergeant Deel, of the Army Service Corps, records section, seconded to the requisition commission. Forty-ish, sandy, with the complexion of a man who has spent his career indoors arguing about forms. He had a way of reading with his chin propped on his fist that suggested he'd read ten thousand pages this month and expected to read ten thousand more, none of them interesting.

He opened Edith's ledger the way he'd opened the parish register an hour before: licked thumb, turn, glance, stamp, turn, glance, stamp.

And I stood by my lorry twenty feet away and felt something in me go very quiet and very cold, the way a house goes quiet when you hear glass break downstairs.

Here is what he was doing. He was not reading. Reading is what I do — I take words in and they stay, whether I ask them to or not; I could recite you half of Edith's correspondence from memory, including three letters I wish to God I could forget. What Deel did was triage. His eye moved down each column and stopped only where the column stopped being ordinary. Names, subscriptions, addresses: ordinary. Sums above a certain figure: stop, note, stamp. Crossed references between pages: stop, note, stamp. And then, on the third page of the membership roll, his finger actually touched the paper — dragged down it slowly, the way a customs officer pats a coat seam — and I realized he was counting names per line, looking for density, looking for clusters. Looking, though he wouldn't have used the word, for a network.

The word on his form — I saw it later, close enough, when I carried a crate past — was *associations*. Persons noted for associations. It sounded so harmless. So administrative. As if the danger were not the women themselves but their habit of knowing one another, which is, if you think about it, the definition of a town.

* * *

Let me tell you what was under his finger, or near enough.

Edith's ledger held every name we had. Members of the league, yes, but also donors who wished to remain anonymous and were anonymous nowhere except in that book; correspondents in three counties; the wives of two magistrates; a sergeant's wife at the drill hall whose letters arrived in envelopes addressed in her left hand; the schoolmistress; the midwife, who could enter any sickroom in the district without suspicion and therefore carried more paper than any courier; and the girl at the exchange who listened to everything and repeated nothing except to us.

Two hundred and eleven names. I know the number the way you know your own pulse. And threaded among them, in Edith's upright hand, entries like small stones set in mortar:

*L. Ashcombe — see correspondence, March — careful.*
*Mrs. Tredwell — willing, but husband must not know.*

That last one. I thought of Mr. Tredwell across the hall just then, helping the corporals carry crates, cheerful as a churchwarden, and I thought of his wife's handwriting in a book that now lay open under a sergeant's thumb, and I confess I felt something that was not fear first but rage — rage arriving ahead of fear like the lightning before the thunder, and lasting longer.

Because here is what I understood, standing there pretending to check a tyre I had already checked twice: those names were not going into a warehouse. They were going to become charges. Not today, maybe not this month — the depot project wanted labourers and quiet, not martyrs, and someone sensible would argue for caution — but the record would sit in some office in the capital, and offices are patient, and patience is the one virtue armies have never had to requisition. Every name stamped *noted* today was a name that could be summoned in five years, denied a licence in seven, quietly refused a pension in twelve. The army didn't need to hang anyone. It only needed to remember. We forget; files don't. That is the whole horror of paperwork and nobody tells you until it's your name in the drawer.

And mine was in the drawer. Page forty-one, between Rooker and Salter, in Edith's hand: *S. Lockridge, driver.* Proof that I existed, filed alphabetically among people who had been born to theirs.

* * *

You will want to know why I didn't simply steal it back. Everyone asks that eventually, usually with the confidence of someone who has never stood in a hall with thirty soldiers in it. I'll answer anyway, because the answering matters.

First: taking it would confirm its value. Nothing teaches an army a document matters faster than losing it. Second: I didn't yet know what else was in those crates — what else of ours might be recoverable, salvageable, worth the risk of my hands. Third — and I am being honest here even though honesty costs me — I froze. Not long. Perhaps ninety seconds. Long enough for Deel to reach page forty-one and pass over *S. Lockridge, driver,* with no more interest than he'd shown the vicar's Easter collections, while the woman who had written that line watched him do it and could not move her feet.

Then the cold finished arriving, and underneath the cold, something with edges. Purpose, if you want the pretty word. I've never liked the pretty word. What it was, was arithmetic. Two hundred and eleven names. One memory. One notebook hidden in the false bottom of my tool box, wrapped in oilcloth, holding perhaps sixty of those names copied out in my cramped hand. Sixty against two hundred and eleven. The sum was short by a hundred and fifty-one lives, give or take, and the only bank that held the balance was sitting open on a trestle table under a bored sergeant's fist.

So I did what I had trained myself eleven years to do. I memorized.

* * *

Memorization sounds passive. It is the opposite. It is the hardest work I know, and I include typesetting by candlelight and lying to Captain Voss, both of which come later in this account.

What I took from the table that morning, in three passes disguised as errands — fetching my gloves from the cab, checking the water in the radiator, tying and untying a bootlace that needed nothing — was this:

Deel himself: sandy, fortyish, wedding ring, ink on the right cuff, chews the inside of his cheek when he finds a cross-reference, stamps without reading when a page bores him. Left-handed stamping. Sloppy on proper nouns, precise on figures. Which told me sums frightened them and names, alone, did not — not yet — which meant the danger lived in connections, and connections lived in the correspondence files, boxed separately, crate fourteen.

Corporal Ives, the inventory clerk: young, conscientious, numbered everything in sequence, and sequence is a gift. Crate fourteen sat between thirteen and fifteen. Thirteen was the grange. Fifteen was Dr. Prewitt. I have never forgotten a number in my life and I did not begin forgetting them that day.

The filing order itself: alphabetical within category, categories by source — societies, businesses, persons, then a last crate marked *miscellaneous* that held, judging by the corner of paper visible through its slats, loose letters. Loose letters. Unsorted. Unindexed. The army's least-loved children. If anything of ours was going to slip through the net unnoticed, it would live in miscellaneous.

And one more thing, which seemed small at the time and was not: Deel paused at page forty-one. Only a breath. His finger came to rest below the entry and lifted again, and his face did absolutely nothing. I have thought about that non-face many hundreds of times since. At the time I read it as boredom. Boredom, mostly. But there was a flicker — the smallest hitch, a man's eye catching on a familiar shape in a crowd — and I filed it away the way I filed everything, and I did not understand until much later, much too late for comfort, that a driver's name might catch a records man's attention for reasons of its own. Papers travel. Papers get read in the capital by people who compare one pile against another. Somewhere in another file there may have been a Sebastian Lockridge already — a birth, a death, a boy from somewhere I'd have to claim to be from — and if the two papers ever met, they would not match, and mismatched papers are how hanging juries fill their afternoons.

But that morning I only knew: he paused, and passed on. I breathed. I went and checked a radiator that did not need checking.

* * *

At noon they broke for rations and I drove Corporal Ives into town for bread and cigarettes, and he talked the entire way, because he was nineteen and I was furniture.

"You should see the letters," he said, around a cigarette he wasn't allowed to light in the cab. "Crate fifteen, well, sixteen — the misc one. Women write the most extraordinary things when they think no one's keeping them." He laughed. "Sarge won't let me sort that box. Says it's above my station." Another laugh. "It's because he wants first go at it himself, more likely."

"Women write to whom?" I asked, in the flat tone I keep for questions I must not seem to care about.

"Oh — each other, officials, the member for the division, one to the King, I expect. Half of them never posted. Confiscated en route." He said *confiscated en route* with relish, a phrase newly learned and worn like a new coat. "Intercepted mail's all fair game under the Defence regulations. Sarge says a letter tells you more than a confession. People put their true names in letters." He snorted at his own joke. "True names."

I drove. The lanes went by, hedges gone bronze, the hills holding that thin October light that makes even a dying year look honest.

A letter tells you more than a confession. Yes. I could have taught him that lecture myself, and better. For years I had read Edith's post on the sly — every letter she let me carry, and some she hadn't — and I had learned the whole town through its sealed envelopes: who was frightened, who was brave on Tuesdays and cowardly on Fridays, who loved whom, who lied to the census. I had built my notebook out of other people's secrets and called it devotion. There is no clean word for what that is. I've tried several. *Loyalty* is the kindest and it doesn't fit.

But hear the difference, because the difference became my whole life: I had read to keep names safe. Deel read to make names dangerous. Same act, opposite directions — like two men copying the same map, one to find the road home and one to burn the bridges behind everyone else.

Except that isn't quite true either, and I promised myself this account would not flatter me. Part of why I read was hunger. Pure, simple, shameful hunger — the appetite of a person with no history, gorging on everyone else's. I read Edith's letters partly for the network and partly because I was starving for a self, and I took theirs secondhand. When I finally wrote my book I made sure to write that part in. Voss never wrote his appetites down. That is the difference between us, if I get to choose the difference, and I choose it.

* * *

I got the ledger's remaining contents over four days, working the waiting game, and I will spare you the tedium except to say that tedium was the whole trick: the army's great gift to me was that its clerks found our lives boring. Boredom meant speed, and speed meant gaps, and gaps were where I lived. By Friday night my notebook held two hundred and six of the two hundred and eleven names, with addresses where I knew them and marginal notes in my own shorthand where I didn't. Five names escaped me entirely. I lay awake that night hunting them through my memory like a woman counting sheep that kept turning into people, and found four of them by Saturday noon — Mrs. Prewitt's sister-in-law, the two Rooker cousins, the organist — and the fifth stayed lost.

The fifth was the one that mattered most. Of course it was. The missing page, as it were, though it wasn't a page; it was a name attached to a page, a name Edith had guarded so carefully that even in the ledger it appeared only as an initial and a reference to correspondence kept elsewhere — correspondence that had gone into crate fourteen, miscellaneous, unindexed, under the eye of a sergeant who wanted first go at it himself.

An informant inside the army, Edith called her, only to me, only once, only outdoors and walking. Our ear at the drill hall. The reason we always knew a raid a day early. The reason no one had been arrested yet.

Her initial was K. Her page was in crate fourteen. And on Monday morning, when I reported for duty, the crates were being loaded onto a transport for the capital, and crate fourteen was already aboard, roped and stencilled, and Sergeant Deel stood beside it smoking, checking items off a list with his left-handed stamping thoroughness, and the train left at four o'clock.

I had until four o'clock to find a reason to be on it.
