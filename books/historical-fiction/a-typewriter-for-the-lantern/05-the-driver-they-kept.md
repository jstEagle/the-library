## 5. The Driver They Kept

The notices went up on a Tuesday, and by Thursday the army had discovered it needed drivers.

This should not have surprised anyone. Soldiers, in my experience, could requisition anything — houses, horses, hymnals, a town's entire paper record — but they could not drive themselves anywhere. They stood at crossroads with maps folded wrong and looked at the countryside the way a man looks at a wife who has just stopped speaking to him. The roads of Marleigh were mine. I had learned them letter by letter, delivering Edith's correspondence to forty-one addresses across three parishes, and if the army wanted them driven, then the army would have to hire the one person in town with the strongest possible motive for knowing exactly what they carried and where.

I want to be precise about this, because later — when Captain Voss writes his elegant history, or when the inquiry reads its minutes, whichever version survives me — someone will say that Sebastian Lockridge collaborated. Let us agree on the word. Collaborated. It has such a respectable sound, like *consulted*, like *assisted*. What I did was walk into the provost's temporary office at the Bell and Anchor, set my driving papers on the counter, and offer myself to the occupation for wages, on the reasoning that a spy who waits outside the walls learns nothing but the weather.

The clerk behind the desk was a corporal named Dunmore with the complexion of uncooked pastry and the eyes of a man who had counted too many things for too long. He looked at my papers, then at me, then at my papers again, performing the little theatre of suspicion that is the army's substitute for thought.

"Lockridge," he said. "Local man."

"Born here," I said, which was true of Sebastian Lockridge, who had been born here and had died here at eleven months old, leaving behind a birth certificate and a name that fit me like a borrowed coat that had, over years, taken the shape of my shoulders.

"You understand you'll be driving for His Majesty's forces. Under military discipline."

"I understand I'll be paid," I said. "The rest we can discover together."

He didn't smile. None of them smiled. That was the first thing I learned about the men of the 112th Labour and Transport: they did not smile because smiling implied a choice, and they had all been assembled out of choices made elsewhere, by other people, and shipped here in crates marked ORDINANCE. Dunmore stamped my papers, wrote my name in his book — my name, going into their books, which should have frightened me more than it did — and told me to report to the requisitioned assembly hall at six the following morning.

"You'll be on general transport," he said. "Mail, stores, officers' runs. Keep your mouth shut and your engine quiet after dark."

I said I was famous for both.

* * *

The hall had been Edith's cathedral.

I don't use the word carelessly. The Marleigh Assembly Hall was a plain brick building with a leaking roof and a piano missing two octaves, and on Thursday evenings it held more conviction per square foot than any church in the county. I had parked outside it a hundred times while Edith went in with her satchel of letters and came out an hour later having rearranged the moral architecture of three hundred women. I had never been inside during a meeting. Drivers wait outside; that was our arrangement, hers and mine, and I had told myself it suited me fine, this business of belonging to the movement only through its paperwork, only through other people's handwriting in other people's names.

Now the hall belonged to the King's Army, and I drove up to it through a morning fog that smelled of coal smoke and cut grass nobody would be cutting, and there were sentries at the doors where the tea urn used to be.

Inside, the transformation was thorough in the way that only institutional transformations are. The chairs had been stacked into towers along the walls. The stage where Edith had stood — I knew she stood there because I'd heard her rehearse once through the propped door, her voice dropping into that register that made even the word *enrolment* sound like a call to arms — now held a trestle table buried under ledgers. Clerks sat along it like crows on a fence, stamping things. The piano had been shoved into a corner and someone had set a crate of boot polish on top of it, which felt less like disrespect than like arithmetic: the army had measured the piano and found it worth nothing, and was right, and somehow that was worse.

Sergeant Hale ran the floor. You will remember him from the inventory — a broad, patient man with reading glasses he wore pushed up on his forehead like a decoration for valour in the campaign against paperwork. He had skimmed Edith's ledger four days earlier with the bored efficiency of a butcher flipping through a catalogue, and I had watched him do it from across the room and understood, in the way one understands a fall before it lands, that every name in that book was about to become a charge sheet waiting for a signature.

"There's my driver," he said now, when Dunmore pointed me out. He said it without warmth and without coldness, the way a man says *there's my spanner*. "Lockridge. You know these roads."

"Better than the post office does."

"We'll see." He handed me a chit. "First run, mail sacks to the junction. Second run, you come back here and help load the archive wagon. We ship records south Friday week."

"Archive wagon," I repeated, keeping my voice at the flat pitch of a man being given errands.

"The historical section wants everything centralised." He pronounced *centralised* with the faint distaste of a practical man describing a disease. "Captain Voss's department. Very keen on papers, that one. Writes everything down twice." He almost smiled, which from Sergeant Hale was practically a confession of feeling. "You'll load boxes. Heavy ones. Try not to drop history."

I took the chit and went out to the lorry, and my hands on the wheel were perfectly steady, which cost me something, because inside my chest every nerve I owned was standing at attention.

Friday week. Nine days. I had nine days to learn where that ledger would travel, in which crate it would sleep, under whose signature it would pass — or to find some way to make sure it never arrived at all. And my only route into that knowledge ran directly through the front door, wearing a uniform cap, carrying their mail, loading their boxes, being useful.

There are easier ways to betray yourself. I've since catalogued several.

* * *

That first day I drove mail. It sounds trivial and was in fact the whole war, or at least the whole occupation: every order, every requisition, every complaint from a farmer whose fence line had become a security perimeter moved through my hands in canvas sacks that smelled of dust and sealing wax. I did not open anything. I want that recorded too, though no one will believe it. I had learned to read letters through their envelopes years ago — weight, stiffness, the shadow of a seal held to the sun — and I found I could stand in a yard full of intercepted correspondence and touch nothing, because touching was not necessary. The army had already done the reading. There were little pencil ticks on half the envelopes. Some bore small blue slips: *DETAINED — REFERENCE SECTION.*

Detained. As though a letter were a suspect. As though Mrs. Fenwick's careful, misspelled enquiry about her son's allowance had been loitering near the scene of a crime.

I memorised the senders anyway. Old habit. Names are the only cargo I've ever trusted myself to carry without dropping.

At noon I ate ration bread on the lorry's running board and watched the hall's side door, where a procession of clerks ferried document crates into the yard. And at two o'clock, Sergeant Hale came out, wiped his glasses on his sleeve, and looked at the sky as if consulting it, and said the words I had been waiting for with a patience that must have seemed, to anyone watching, like simple obedience:

"If you're finished, Lockridge — the archive wagon won't load itself."

* * *

I will describe the loading of that ledger as accurately as I can, because accuracy is the only inheritance I have to leave, and because I have spent enough nights since trying to reconstruct the moment that I might as well spend one more.

They had boxed the ledger itself separately from the rest. I noticed that before I understood why. The general correspondence went into ordinary pine crates, thirty pounds of envelopes and petitions apiece, nailed shut and stencilled in chalk. But the master membership ledger — Edith's ledger, the book with the marbled boards and the brass corners that she had entrusted to me to transport exactly once, back when transporting it had been an honour instead of a wound — that book lay alone on the trestle table with a paper tag tied to its spine, and beside it a special crate, lined with oilcloth, built to keep damp out. Someone had decided it mattered. Someone upstream, whose name I didn't yet know, had looked at a list of confiscated materials and put a small, deliberate star beside one item.

I lifted it. Fourteen inches by ten, perhaps three inches thick, and heavy as a paving stone, because Edith had written in it the way she did everything: completely. Members, donors, subscribers to the newsletter, correspondents, the little code marks she used for reliability and risk — a single tick, a double tick, a small circle for those who could be approached but never enrolled. Three hundred and eleven names. I knew the number because I had copied every one of them into my own notebook over eighteen months of night work, in my own cipher, in my own hand, and I had thought myself safe in that knowledge until the moment the army seized the original and I understood that a copy is only as good as its source, and sources can be interrogated.

My name was in that book. Not *my* name — Sebastian Lockridge's name, entered in Edith's neat upright script among the drivers and couriers, with a tick beside it that meant reliable. A dead infant's name, vouched for by a woman who believed in him, sitting in a crate bound for the capital where strangers would run their fingers down the columns deciding who had resisted and who had merely been present. Proof that I existed, kept in the custody of the people most equipped to prove otherwise.

"Careful with that one," said a corporal whose name I never learned. "Tagged priority."

"It's just a list of names," I said.

"Everything's a list of names," he said, with the serenity of a man who had stopped thinking about it. "That's how you tell who's who."

I set the ledger into its oilcloth nest. My hands did not shake, and I remember being grateful for that, and I remember also understanding that gratitude was the wrong emotion entirely and being unable to locate the right one. Around me the clerks worked on. Chalk squeaked on crate sides. Somewhere beyond the yard wall a woman — I think it was Ada Prewitt, who sang when she thought no one could hear — was singing something with a lot of low notes, and the fog was burning off, and the army was packing up a revolution in pine boxes to send south for filing.

Sergeant Hale came down the trestle line checking tags against a manifest. When he reached my crate he paused, glanced at the ledger through the open lid, and then at me, and for one second — one — his gaze stayed on my face a beat longer than the manifest required.

"You look pale, Lockridge," he said. "Heavy box?"

"Heavy book," I said.

"Mm." He tied the tag down, closed the lid, and held out the hammer and nails. "Your crate, your nails. Army rule — the last man to handle it signs for it." He turned the manifest toward me and offered the pencil.

So I signed. Sebastian Lockridge, in my careful, borrowed hand, on an army manifest, directly beneath the description *1 vol., membership records, sundry societies, priority.* The last man to handle it. I wrote my own alias onto the chain of custody of my own erasure, and Sergeant Hale watched me do it with those patient eyes above his pushed-up glasses, and I could not decide — and cannot, even now — whether what I saw in his face was boredom or something else. A note. A question. The smallest possible reservation, filed away in a man who filed everything.

"Nail it square," he said. "Voss's people count."

* * *

That evening I drove the last run back through the dark with the engine hooded, past the darkened windows of houses whose families had eleven days left, past the notice board where the requisition order flapped in a wind that smelled of rain that hadn't come yet. At the corner by the chapel I stopped, as one does, to check a wheel, and let the engine idle, and thought about what I had learned.

Nine days. The wagon went south Friday week, probably to the junction, probably by rail from there — I could confirm the timetable from the mail runs. The ledger travelled in a marked priority crate under Voss's historical section, which meant it would land on the desk of the very man whose job was to turn three hundred and eleven names into a footnote reading, as the official phrasing had it, *no organised local resistance.* Unless it landed somewhere else first. Unless a crate could go missing between a hall and a siding, or arrive water-damaged, or be mislabelled in a way that no chalk and no counting could ever undo.

And unless — here was the thought I circled like a moth at glass — unless getting close enough to lose that ledger meant getting close enough to be lost in it. Because I had signed for the crate. My name, the false one, the shield, was now written in their ink on their paper in their file, attached forever to the one object in Marleigh that could hang everyone I had ever silently served. If the crate vanished, the last man to handle it was a local driver with no family, no history, and no one to ask questions on his behalf.

A perfect suspect. Almost as if the universe had designed him.

I pulled away from the corner before the sentry at the chapel end could grow curious, and drove home through streets that had already begun to belong to somebody else, and I sat at my kitchen table with the lamp turned low and opened my notebook to the page headed *HALL — FRIDAY WEEK,* and began, in cipher, to write down everything: the wagon, the siding, the crate number, the tag, the oilcloth lining, Sergeant Hale's one-second pause.

Halfway down the page I stopped, because I had remembered the tick beside my name in that ledger, and I understood suddenly and completely that the book was not evidence of the movement's existence.

It was evidence of mine.

Whatever happened Friday week — whether the ledger burned, drowned, or reached the capital intact — the next chapter of this story would begin with a decision about that crate, and every road to it ran through the army's own yard, in their own lorry, wearing their own cap.

I blew out the lamp and sat in the dark, listening to the occupation settle over Marleigh like snowfall, and told myself what I would keep telling myself for nine days: that I was stealing access, not selling loyalty.

Both were true. That was the trouble. Both were true.
