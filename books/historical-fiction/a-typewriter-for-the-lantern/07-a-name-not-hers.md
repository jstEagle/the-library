## 7. A Name Not Hers

The lieutenant had a way of holding papers as though they might lie to him if he gave them the chance.

He stood in the doorway of the motor pool office with my file in one hand and a cup of tea he wasn't drinking in the other, and he said, "Lockridge," the way a man says a word he's been practicing.

"That's me." I kept my hands busy with a spark plug I didn't need to touch. Idle hands invite questions. Busy hands invite only impatience, and impatience was on my side; the army had forty drivers and half of them were boys who couldn't parallel a lorry between two haystacks.

"Sebastian *Aubrey* Lockridge." He read it out slowly, syllable by syllable, like a priest administering a sacrament to a suspect.

"Aubrey was my father's idea," I said. "I've forgiven him for it."

He did not smile. Lieutenants under thirty rarely do when they've been told to smile at nothing and check everyone for something. "You're listed as local hire. Harrowgate address. Family?"

"Mother died in '09. Father before her. A brother." I let that sit a moment, then added, with the flatness grief deserves and rarely gets, "Killed in '14."

"I'm sorry." He said it automatically, which was the correct amount of sorrow for a brother he'd never met, and then his eyes went back to the paper, because the paper was the thing that mattered. The dead are only ever footnotes to paperwork. I have since become an expert on this.

"Which regiment?" he asked.

"Pardon?"

"The brother. Which regiment?"

And there it was — the trap laid so gently you could have eaten your supper off it. Because I had built Sebastian Lockridge the way Nell Ashcombe's father had once built a pamphlet, line by line, and I knew every word of my own text except the ones I'd never thought anyone would ask for. A name, a birthdate, an address, a mother's maiden name, a church, a school, a scar on the left knee from a bicycle in 1906. I had not given him a regiment, because regiments have muster rolls, and muster rolls have clerks, and clerks have memories, and somewhere down that chain a man might say *no Lockridge served with us* and the whole careful architecture would come down around my ears like wet plaster.

So I did what liars do best. I told the truth about someone else.

"He wasn't army." I wiped my hands slowly on a rag, giving the pause the texture of reluctance rather than calculation. "Merchant marine out of Hull. Went down with the *Cormorant* off Norway. No body, no grave, no regiment." I looked up. "You can check. I'd be glad of it. Nobody believes a sailor's death until the Admiralty stamps it, least of all the family."

Something in him softened — not kindness exactly, but the relief of a man whose checklist has found nothing to tick. Sailors' deaths were a national ache by 1917; every third household had one, and questioning them felt like kicking a crutch.

"No need," he said, and closed the folder. "Routine verification. New security protocols since the requisition. You'll show these papers at the hall gate each morning."

"Yes, sir."

"And Lockridge—" He hesitated at the door. "Your brother's ship. You said the *Cormorant*. My cousin was on a tramp steamer out of Hull. Different ship. But it's all the same sea, isn't it."

"It is, sir," I said. "It's exactly the same sea."

He left. I sat down on the crate beside the workbench and discovered my hands were shaking, which was interesting, because my voice hadn't shaken once, and I made a note to distrust my voice in future. Voices will perform anything. Hands know better.

Then I got up, finished the spark plug, and went back to driving for the men who held my town in a filing cabinet.

* * *

Here is how you build a man.

First you find his grave. That part sounds grim but is actually the easiest step; the war had made graves plentiful and paperwork scarce, and a boy drowned off Norway leaves no body to contradict you. I chose Sebastian Aubrey Lockridge in the spring of 1913, from a parish register in a town two counties over where nobody would ever think to look twice. He'd been born in 1891. He'd gone to sea at sixteen. He was, as far as the world knew, still alive — which was the beauty of it. A dead man invites questions. A live man abroad invites none.

Second, you give him habits. Men are caught not by their names but by their manners. So I taught myself to sit with my knees apart, to shake hands like I was closing a deal, to swear fluently but without imagination, to look at women's faces instead of their collars, and to accept drinks I didn't want. I practiced walking downstairs heavily. I practiced being bored in church. I learned to say *my late father* without my voice catching, because the real grief belonged to a different person entirely, and grief mislaid is grief discovered.

Third — and this is the part no manual tells you — you give him a history you can bear to remember. Because a false identity isn't a costume you put on; it's a story you have to keep telling, and stories rot if you don't love them a little. So I loved Sebastian Lockridge. I gave him a childhood I could recite like scripture: the house on Chandler Street in Harrowgate, the mother who took in sewing, the father who drank and drove a bakery cart and died of his lungs in 1908, the brother who ran away to sea. I gave him loneliness, which was no invention at all. I gave him a talent for engines and an inability to stay anywhere longer than a year, both of which explained everything and investigated nothing.

What I did not give him was a sister. Sisters get asked after. Sisters appear in photographs. Sisters are the loose thread that unravels the whole sweater, and besides — and here is the confession I have never said aloud — some nights, driving Edith Marlowe home through the dark with her letters stacked beside me like ammunition, I forgot myself entirely. Not the role. Myself. There were evenings when *Sebastian* fit so well that the woman underneath went quiet as a banked fire, and I could almost believe she'd never existed, that there had only ever been a quiet young man with grease on his cuffs and no one waiting at home. It was restful, that forgetting. It was also the most dangerous thing I did, more dangerous than reading the letters, more dangerous than copying the ledger. A spy can survive suspicion. What she cannot survive is wanting the disguise more than the face beneath it.

My real name I kept the way you keep a coal alive through winter: banked, fed sparingly, never brought into the open air. Once a month, on the night of the new moon, I wrote it in the back of my notebook and then immediately ruled a line through it. Writing it was the point. Striking it was the discipline. I am aware that this is madness. I am also aware that it worked.

* * *

The trouble with armor is that it weighs.

That evening I drove Captain Voss's clerk — a pale, diligent young man named Prewitt who smelled of ink and fear — from the requisitioned hall out to the telegraph office and back, and he talked the entire way, the way frightened men do, filling silence like sandbags.

"—and the captain says a history is a weapon like any other, only slower, and the colonel wants the first draft before the depot's operational, and there's a printer in town, Ashcombe's, that's to be inventoried tomorrow, type and press and all. The captain's very particular about printers. He says whoever controls the type controls the past." Prewitt laughed, a small nervous bark. "Funny sort of fellow, the captain. Polite. You wouldn't think he'd be in this line of work."

"What line is that?" I asked, watching the road.

"Oh — records. Truth, he calls it. *The administration of truth.*" Prewitt said it the way you'd repeat a phrase in a foreign language, not sure whether to admire it or wash your hands after.

Ashcombe's press. I filed it where I filed everything, in the locked drawer behind my eyes, next to the sergeant's filing order and the gate roster and the fact that Lieutenant Routine-Verification took his tea with too much sugar and too little attention. Tomorrow they would inventory a printing press. Which meant tomorrow the town would have one fewer voice, unless somebody moved faster than the army, and the army moved in lorries while the truth moved on foot.

I dropped Prewitt at the hall, parked the lorry, and walked home the long way, past the shuttered shops and the notice boards furred with proclamations, and I did what I always did on the long way home: I checked my own tailings. Was I followed? Was I known? Did the lieutenant's questions mean anything, or did they mean only that the machine was waking up and beginning to count its parts?

The answer, which arrived three days later in the form of a list posted outside the provost's office — *persons requiring further documentation review* — was: both. My name was not on the list. Sebastian Lockridge had passed clean through. But eleven other names were, and I knew nine of them from the ledger, and one of them was a woman named Mrs. Tarnow who had merely donated two pounds ten to the cause and would now spend a week explaining herself to men with rubber stamps.

Her name was on that list because of a page I had once carried in a canvas satchel. I had carried it carefully. I had carried it honestly, even. But I had carried it, and carrying was enough. That is the lesson of documents, and of identities, and of graves borrowed for safety: nothing you hold stays neutral. Everything you carry carries you somewhere you didn't choose.

* * *

That night I sat at my kitchen table with the notebook open to the page of struck-through names, and I added the day's intelligence in my smallest hand: the inventory order, Ashcombe's press, Voss's phrase about type and the past, the review list with nine familiar names. Then I turned to the last page and wrote my true name, and ruled the line through it, and sat looking at the strikethrough for a long time.

Because here was the arithmetic I could not escape. Every day the disguise grew stronger and the woman grew fainter. Sebastian Lockridge now had employment, papers, a reputation for reliability, a lieutenant's sympathy regarding drowned sailors. The woman underneath had a notebook and a strike-through. If the army ever came for me, they would arrest a man who had never existed, and the woman would simply — evaporate. Unmourned. Unrecorded. Exactly like the people in Edith's ledger, exactly like the letters in the mail sacks, exactly like whatever version of this town Captain Voss intended to publish. I had built myself into the same machinery I was fighting. I was a name on someone else's paper.

Well. Then the answer was obvious, wasn't it? If names on paper were weapons, I would need better paper. And if the army was going to inventory Ashcombe's press tomorrow morning, with its type slugs and its cases and its hidden drawers full of everything a town had ever dared to print —

— then somebody ought to visit the printer's daughter first.

I blew out the lamp, put the notebook inside the false bottom of my tool chest, and lay in the dark rehearsing what I would say to a girl named Nell Ashcombe, who hated Edith Marlowe, and who had no reason on earth to trust a driver with kind eyes and a dead brother.

I would worry about that at dawn. Dawn, as I had learned, is when all disguises are weakest — including, especially, the one you've forgotten you're wearing.
