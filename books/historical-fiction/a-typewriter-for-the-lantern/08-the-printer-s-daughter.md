## 8. The Printer's Daughter

I found the Ashcombe print shop by its smell before I found it by its sign. Ink and oil and cold iron — a smell like a promise that somebody in this town still made things instead of waiting for them to be taken.

The shop sat behind the corn chandler's on Tanner's Row, wedged where the street narrowed and the buildings leaned toward each other like conspirators. The windows had been papered over with newspaper, which struck me as either defiance or surrender; with Ashcombe, I would learn, it was neither. It was inventory. He'd run out of anything worth printing, so he'd printed his own curtains.

The requisition had closed him down in the first week. That was the arithmetic of the army: a hall for drilling, a schoolhouse for billets, a chapel for stores, and a press — because a press could print orders, manifests, notices of seizure, all the paperwork by which a town is unmade politely. They hadn't taken his machines yet. They'd taken his livelihood and left him the machines, which is a cruelty with better manners than most.

I knocked twice. A slot scraped open at eye level, and one eye appeared in it, brown, bloodshot, and unimpressed.

"We're not buying," said the eye.

"I'm not selling."

"Everyone's selling something. Ration cards, silence, sympathy. Go away."

"I need type set," I said. "Quietly."

The eye considered me the way a magistrate considers a beggar with suspiciously good boots. Then the door opened on a chain, and I was looking at Nell Ashcombe — nineteen or twenty, sleeves rolled over forearms inked to the elbow, hair tied back with what appeared to be a strip of printer's waste. She looked me over once, thoroughly, the way her father must have looked over proofs.

"You're the army driver," she said. "Lockridge. You were driving when they posted the notices. Everyone saw the car."

"Everyone sees the car. Nobody looks at the driver. That's rather the point of drivers."

"That," she said, "is exactly what worries me about you." But she unhooked the chain, because curiosity is a chain too, and hers was longer than the one on the door.

* * *

Inside, the shop was a cave of iron. Two presses stood against the wall under dust sheets, and the cases of type ran along one side in ranks, each drawer labeled in a hand so small and even it looked typeset itself. A single lamp burned low. There was no stove lit, though the morning was raw. Either there was no coal or there was no wish to be seen warm.

"Ashcombe's Fine Printing," I read from a framed certificate above the cases. "Established 1888."

"My grandfather established it. My father ruined it. I'm expected to complete the trilogy."

"How did he ruin it?"

She pulled a stool out with her foot and didn't offer me one, which told me something about how long this conversation would be permitted to last. "He printed things people asked him to print. That used to be called a business. Now it's called collaboration, depending on who's asking, and everyone's asking."

"And Edith Marlowe asked."

Something crossed her face then — not anger, exactly. Anger is hot. This was older and colder, a thing kept in a cellar like the presses, brought up into the light only to be shown to visitors.

"Mrs. Marlowe," Nell said, "came here in March. Sat where you're standing. Told my father that printing the suffrage circulars was God's work and England's work and would he do his part. And my father, who had buried a wife and had one daughter and a mortgage, said yes. Because she had a way of saying yes to you before you'd agreed." She turned a drawer of type with one finger, restless. "He printed four hundred circulars. He charged her cost, which was already charity, and she paid it in full and thanked him as if he'd handed her a medal. And then in May the requisition came, and the first thing they wanted printed was the seizure notices. And my father, who had learned from Mrs. Marlowe that a man prints what conscience requires, discovered that conscience has two accents and only one of them pays."

"He printed those too."

"He printed those too." She said it flatly, without excuse. "Four hundred seizure notices. Same type. Same press. Same man. Do you know what it's like to watch your father set the words that turn your neighbors out of their houses, and know that six weeks earlier the same hands set the words that told those neighbors to stand firm?" She looked up. "No. You don't. You drive the car."

That landed where she meant it to land. I let it.

* * *

"Tell me why you hate her," I said. "Not him. Her."

"Because she made it simple." Nell was pacing now among the cases, touching labels as she went — Long Primer, Pica, Nonpareil — like a pianist running scales. "That's her gift, isn't it? I've watched her speak. Everything becomes a choice between standing and kneeling, and anyone who mentions the rent due between the two is a coward. My father knelt and stood both, in the same month, on the same press, and Mrs. Marlowe got her circulars and the army got its notices and we got to live here afterward with both lots of ink on us." She stopped. "She never came back after the requisition. Did you know that? Four hundred circulars' worth of friendship, and when the soldiers walked in she was already gone south. Evacuated, they say. Conveniently."

"She wasn't evacuated."

Nell's head came around slowly. "What did you say?"

I had not intended to say it. But there is a moment in every negotiation — and this was one, whatever else it was — where you must put something real on the table or go home with nothing. "I drove her," I said. "I read the letters she carried. I know the names in her ledger. And she is alive, and she is not evacuated, and she will come back if there is a reason to come back to."

"A reason." Nell laughed, short and hard. "A reason like what?"

"Like a record that survives. The army took her ledger. Every name — members, donors, correspondents, your father among them, I'm sorry to say, listed under 'sympathetic trades.' They've inventoried it. They're boxing it for the capital. In a year there will be an official account of this town, written by men who were never in it, and in that account nobody resisted anything, and the people who did will just be names on a charge sheet. Unless someone rebuilds what was taken. From memory. From letters. From—" I nodded at the cases. "Type."

She was quiet a long moment. In the lamp light the ink on her forearms looked like old bruises.

"You want my father's type," she said finally. "You want his press, his skill, his risk, and his name added to another woman's war. And what does he get?"

"The truth printed."

"The truth!" She spat the word like a pip. "My father needs coal and flour, not truth. Truth doesn't feed anyone I love." But she said it while looking at the presses, not at me, and I have been lied to often enough — mostly by myself — to know what it means when a person argues with her back half turned.

* * *

It was her father who ended the argument, though he never raised his voice above a murmur. He came down the outside stair from the living quarters — a stooped man in a collarless shirt, spectacles pushed up into thin grey hair, moving with the careful economy of someone conserving fuel he couldn't afford. He took in the scene: the open door, the stranger, his daughter's stance.

"Nell," he said mildly. "We don't keep customers standing."

"She's not a customer."

"Everyone's a customer," he said, "of something." And he looked at me with eyes that had spent thirty years measuring other people's copy, and I felt weighed, priced, and provisionally accepted, all inside ten seconds.

I gave him the shorter version — the ledger, the confiscation, the official history being drafted, the need for a press that existed nowhere on any inventory. I left out Edith's whereabouts and my own reasons, which he noticed me leaving out, and filed, I suspect, in a drawer marked LATER.

When I finished, he was silent. Then he walked to the far case, the oldest one, and drew open a drawer near the bottom. Inside lay not type but metal slugs — cast pieces, odd sorts, letters cut deep and mirrored wrong.

"Defective casts," he said. "Rejected years ago. Kept because throwing away metal felt sinful during the slump." He held one up to the lamp. "If a man needed to print something without its being traced to a press — the face slightly wrong, the wear irregular, unmatchable to any shop in the county — these are what he'd use." He set the slug down and closed the drawer with the gentleness of a man closing a coffin. "I'm telling you this as a hypothetical, Miss Lockridge. Or Mister. Forgive me — the papers say one thing and the shoulders say another, and it's no business of mine either way. A pressman learns to see what's actually there. It's why we proofread."

Heat climbed my neck. Nell stared at him, then at me, recalculating everything, and I watched her decide — visibly, like type locking into a chase — that this new fact changed nothing she'd already concluded about me, which was apparently that I was trouble wearing a driver's coat.

"Why are you really doing this?" she asked me. Not hostile now. Worse: genuinely curious.

Because I am a fiction and the ledger proves it, I thought. Because somewhere in a boxed crate is a page with my invented name in Edith's careful hand, and when they rewrite that book, I stop having ever existed at all. Because I have built myself out of paper and the army holds the paper.

"Because I read the letters," I said aloud. "All of them, for two years. I know things about this town that its own people don't know about themselves — who fed whom, who hid whom, who broke and who didn't. If the only account left is theirs, then everything I read dies twice. Once when the letters were taken and once when the lie is printed. I can't allow the second one."

Nell studied me for a long time. Somewhere overhead a soldier's boots passed along Tanner's Row, unhurried, the sound of occupation as background noise, already ordinary.

"If I help," she said at last, "it won't be for Mrs. Marlowe. Understand that. She can burn in whichever southern town she's hiding in."

"Noted."

"And it won't be for you. I don't trust you. You talk like a book and you look like a locked drawer."

"Also noted."

"It'll be for my father. Because they made him print their notices, and a man should get one chance to print the correction." She thrust out her ink-stained hand, and I shook it, and it was the hardest handshake I'd had in years, because it was honest.

Her father saw me to the door. On the step, quietly, he said: "Come Thursday. After the mail lorry goes through. And Miss— Lockridge — bring what you remember exactly as you remember it. Don't tidy it. Memory tidied is memory falsified, and we'll have enough of that from the capital."

* * *

I walked back through streets I had driven a hundred times and never seen at walking pace. The requisitioned hall stood ahead, flags limp, sentries bored, and I thought of the ledger in its crates upstairs and of the drawer of misfit slugs hidden beneath a false bottom of respectability, and I understood that I had just acquired allies who hated the woman whose work I was preserving, on grounds I could not dismiss and might, in their place, have shared.

Edith makes everything simple, Nell had said. Standing or kneeling. And perhaps that was true, and perhaps simplicity was exactly the weapon the moment required — but the people who lived inside the blast radius of her simplicity deserved to have their complication recorded too, and that, I was beginning to see, would fall to me.

I reached the car. A clerk was waiting beside it with a slip of paper and an expression of mild importance.

"Captain Voss requests the driver," he said. "Tomorrow morning. He's interviewing local personnel for the historical record."

I took the slip. My name was spelled correctly on it, which somehow frightened me more than any misspelling would have.

"He asks," the clerk added, turning away, "that you bring your own account of events. In your own words."

In my own words. I folded the slip into my glove and thought of a dead man's papers, a living woman's ledger, and a drawer of crooked type, and wondered which version of myself I could afford to be by morning.
