## 9. The Historian Arrives

He arrived on a Tuesday, which felt right. Catastrophe in this town had learned to keep office hours.

I was polishing the windshield of the staff car when the train came in — the eleven-forty from the capital, carrying mail, a crate of typewriter ribbon, and one passenger who stepped down onto our soot-grey platform as though he'd been expected all his life. He wore a captain's uniform that fit him the way a well-bound book fits its shelf: correctly, and without any suggestion that it might once have contained something else. Small man. Spectacles with round gold frames. A leather valise he carried himself rather than surrender to a porter, the way a priest carries a relic or a thief carries the take.

Sergeant Hale met him at the barrier, and I watched Hale — a man who could chew through a requisition form without swallowing — stand up straighter and speak more slowly. That told me more than the uniform did.

"Captain Voss," I heard him say. "Historical Section."

Historical Section. Of course they had one. Every army keeps priests and every army keeps accountants, and somewhere between the two they keep men whose business is deciding what happened.

I went back to my windshield. There is an art to being unremarkable, and I have practiced it longer than most people have been alive: the lowered eyes, the competent hands, the face arranged into the mild vacancy of someone thinking about carburetors. But I confess my hands were not entirely steady on the cloth, because I had already understood what Captain Voss was for. The army had taken the town, taken the records, taken the ledger with nine hundred names in it — and now it had sent a man to decide what those names would mean. Or whether they would mean anything at all.

You can shoot a woman for her opinions. What you cannot survive is being edited out of the sentence entirely.

* * *

By evening the officers' mess had been rearranged for him — the requisitioned bank on the square, its marble counters now buried under maps and filing cabinets, its vault still holding what remained of Edith Marlowe's confiscated world. Voss took a corner room on the second floor, the one with the tall window facing the press building across the street. Nell Ashcombe's father's old shop, shuttered since the seizure, its windows papered over with army notices. I noted the view and did not like it. Historians like to watch printers' windows. It is a professional jealousy, I imagine, older than either trade.

I learned his habits within three days, because learning people's habits is the only education I've ever trusted. He rose at six and walked the square before breakfast, gloves on, umbrella furled even under clear sky, nodding to soldiers and — this was the remarkable part — to townspeople. He nodded to Mrs. Pell at the baker's. He nodded to the Wexford boy who swept the steps of the chapel. He asked Mrs. Pell whether the rye flour situation had eased, and she said it had not, and he said, "That's a pity; good bread matters to morale," in a tone of such genuine regret that she nearly smiled at him.

That was the danger of him. Not cruelty — we had plenty of cruelty already and had learned its shape. The danger was courtesy. You can hate a boot; you cannot hate a handshake.

On Thursday he required a car.

"Lockridge." Hale jerked his thumb toward where I stood by the garage door. "You're driving Captain Voss today. And tomorrow. However long he wants."

"How long's he staying?"

"As long as it takes," Hale said, and there was something in his voice I hadn't heard before — not fear exactly, but the particular discomfort of a practical man in the presence of a patient one. "He's writing it all down. Everything. The whole campaign." He said *campaign* the way you'd say *infestation*.

I fetched the car.

* * *

His first request was the schoolhouse at Marleigh Cross, four miles out, where the army had posted its original requisition notices. He sat beside me with the valise on his knees and did not open it for the entire drive, which unnerved me more than if he'd spent the time interrogating me about my papers.

"You're a local man," he said, as we came down past the mill pond.

"Yes, sir."

"Born here?"

"Born in Harwick, sir. Came here when I was young." This much was true, if you accepted that the *he* born in Harwick had died at eight years old and been worn since then like a coat cut to measure. My papers said Sebastian Lockridge. Sebastian Lockridge had a birth certificate, a school record, a dead mother, and a grave in Harwick churchyard belonging to a boy who had never drawn breath outside of ink. I had built him myself, plank by plank, years ago, for reasons that were mine alone, and he had held up under customs men, recruiting sergeants, and now, apparently, historians.

"Harwick," Voss repeated. "Good country. Flat." He looked out at our hills, which are anything but flat, and seemed to find them mildly suspect. "How long has your family lived in the county?"

"The Lockridges? Some generations, sir."

"Mm." He opened the valise at last and produced a notebook bound in grey board, and a pencil, sharpened to a point that could have served surgery. "And do you know many of the people being relocated?"

Here it was, then. Sooner than I'd guessed.

"I know everyone, sir. It's a small place."

"Do you?" He turned and looked at me directly, and I want to record accurately what that look was, because I spent a long time afterward taking its measure. It was not suspicion. Suspicion would have been manageable. It was interest — the clean, appetiteless interest of a man examining a document for watermarks. "Names are curious things, Lockridge. They attach themselves to events, and then the events are gone and the names remain, and people build meanings on them. Half the trouble in history comes from a name surviving when the event it belonged to has been misunderstood." He smiled. It reached his eyes, which made it worse. "I'm here to make sure the misunderstanding doesn't happen. In either direction."

"In either direction, sir?"

"People will tell you there was resistance here. Agitation. Sedition, even." He said the words the way a doctor recites symptoms he doesn't believe in. "There will also be people — later, when it's safe — who claim they resisted. Everyone resists, afterward. It's very fashionable. My task is to establish what actually occurred." He tapped the grey notebook against his knee. "Which is why I talk to drivers. Drivers see everything and repeat nothing. It's why I requested a local man."

*Requested.* So Hale hadn't chosen me after all. Somewhere in that marble bank, a file had been pulled and a name had been read aloud, and the name was mine — the false one, but still.

The schoolhouse, when we reached it, stood empty, its noticeboard stripped except for one requisition order curling at the corners. Voss walked the length of it, reading the ghost-edges of notices that had been pasted over each other like sediment, dating them by the curl of the paper. Then he photographed nothing, wrote down three lines, and closed the notebook.

"Notice boards are honest," he remarked, climbing back into the car. "People lie. Paper only curls." And he gave me directions back to town by a route that passed, I noticed, the hall where Edith's ledger now sat boxed in an army filing room.

* * *

Over the following week he interviewed me four times, always in the car, always conversationally, never with the notebook open until the end. He asked about the routes I'd driven before the requisition. About the women who'd used my taxi — and yes, I had driven for Edith openly enough; a chauffeur is furniture, and furniture hears everything. He asked what parcels I'd carried. What letters. Whether I'd ever noticed anything unusual about the correspondence.

"Letters are letters, sir," I said. "Ladies write a great deal of them."

"Ladies do," he agreed pleasantly. "Particularly ladies of strong opinions. Did Mrs. Marlowe dictate hers?"

"I wouldn't know, sir. I drove."

"You drove." He let the repetition sit there like a stone dropped into a well, listening for how deep the water was. Then he opened the notebook and wrote, unhurriedly, three words. I could not read them upside down, which I assure you I tried.

That night I sat in my room above the garage with my own notebook — the hidden one, the true one, the one holding everything I'd copied from Edith's letters and memorized from her ledger — and I added a new entry. Not a name. A description. *V., Capt., Hist. Section. Grey notebook. Asks twice. Writes thrice. Smiles with his eyes. Believes he is already finished.*

Because that was the thing I'd come to understand, watching him work the town like a man proofing pages. Captain Voss wasn't gathering evidence. Evidence implies uncertainty. He was assembling confirmation. He interviewed Mrs. Pell about bread and wrote nothing. He interviewed the Wexford boy about soldiers' conduct and wrote nothing. He interviewed the reeve's clerk about the requisition lists for two hours and filled six pages — but when the clerk mentioned, hesitantly, that some families had refused to sign and had been carried out, Voss's pencil stopped.

"Carried out," he repeated.

"Some of them, sir. Old Mr. Trench. The Hollis sisters."

"And afterward?"

"Afterward they signed, sir."

The pencil resumed. But I saw what he wrote, because the light was behind him and I have excellent eyesight and a shameful talent for reading upside down: *"No significant dissent."*

Four words. Four words and the Hollis sisters, who were carried out of their own kitchen between two corporals, ceased to have happened. Old Trench, who spat at the major's boots and was fined a month's pension for it, became a smooth page in an elegant book. I sat in that car with my hands on the wheel and understood, with a clarity that felt almost physical, that I was looking at the other author. That the ledger in the vault and the notebook on Voss's knee were two versions of the same town, and only one of them was going to reach print, unless —

Unless.

* * *

The fifth time he interviewed me, he did it in his room, over tea, which meant the questions changed shape. The car is a workplace; a room is a confessional.

"You were recommended to me, Lockridge," he said, pouring. "Did you know that?"

"No, sir."

"A reliable local man, familiar with the district, discreet. Discretion is rare. Most people can't wait to tell you things." He handed me the cup. His fingers were ink-stained at the second knuckle, though he typed most nights — I'd seen the lamplight burning in his window across from the dead printer's shop, and heard, more than once, the small iron music of a portable machine going until two in the morning. A historian who types his own pages. I found I respected it and hated it in equal measure.

"Who recommended me, sir, if I may ask?"

"Sergeant Hale. And someone else." He smiled again, that eye-reaching smile. "A name in a file. You come well documented, Lockridge. Birth, schooling, service. Someone was thorough with you."

The tea tasted of tin and nothing else. I kept my face at carburetor-vacancy and my voice at bored gratitude. "My late father liked things in order, sir."

"Your late father." Voss consulted the grey notebook, though I'd have wagered my life he knew the entry by heart. "Deceased Harwick. Your brother also deceased. The family reduced early." He closed it. "Grief makes careful people, I find. Careful people make excellent witnesses." He looked at me over his cup, and for one moment — one — the courtesy thinned and I saw through to the machinery underneath: not malice, nothing so usable as malice. Method. "Tell me, Lockridge. If you discovered that the official account of something differed from what you had seen with your own eyes — which would you trust?"

I considered giving him the soldier's answer. *The official account, sir.* But lying badly to Voss would be worse than not lying at all; he collected discrepancies the way other men collect stamps.

"I'd trust my eyes, sir," I said. "But I'd wonder what my eyes had missed. Seeing's a skill. Most of us only see what we're looking for."

Something moved behind the gold spectacles — approval, I think, and something colder beneath it, the pleasure of a man who has confirmed a watermark. "Precisely," he said. "Precisely. Which is why the record requires professionals. Memory is a townsperson, Lockridge. The archive is an army." He set down his cup. "Thank you for the tea conversation. Tomorrow we'll go out to the depot site. Wear your coat; the wind off the ridge is filthy."

I stood to go, and at the door he added, lightly, the way you'd mention weather:

"One more thing. The Marlowe woman. Before the evacuation — she used private cars. Local drivers. You'd have known her route, I imagine."

I had driven it two hundred times. I knew which hedgerows hid the turning, which farm gate stuck, which cottage on the ridge road kept a lamp lit in the front window on meeting nights — a lantern signal, one flick for all clear.

"I drove where I was pointed, sir," I said.

"Of course you did." He was already turning back to his desk, to the grey notebook, to the version of the world where no one had ever pointed anywhere at all. "Good night, Lockridge."

I walked home through the blackout streets with my heart going like a telegraph key. Across the square, his lamp still burned above the papered-over windows of the Ashcombe press, and beneath it, faint through the glass, the sound of typing — patient, meticulous, tireless, setting into type a town in which none of us had ever existed.

I got out my notebook and began, by candle stub, to plan how I was going to steal the truth back from a man whose weapon was a pencil — and the first thing I needed was Nell Ashcombe, who hated Edith Marlowe more than she hated the army, and who was going to be the hardest ally I ever won.
