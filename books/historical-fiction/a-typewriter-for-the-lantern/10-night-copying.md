## 10. Night Copying

Nell Ashcombe kept her father's type slugs in a biscuit tin under the floorboards of the composing room, and she told me this the way other people confess to murders — quietly, at night, with a lamp turned so low it might as well have been a rumor.

"You understand," she said, prying up the board with a flatiron she'd heated for the purpose, "that if they find these, my father isn't shot for hiding them. He's shot twice."

"He's dead," I said, before I could stop myself.

"Shot twice anyway," Nell said, and lifted out the tin.

I had come to her three nights after our first conversation, which she had spent hating me and I had spent deserving it. She'd hated Edith Marlowe longer and better — four years of practiced grievance, polished like brass — but hatred, it turns out, is a kind of attention. Nobody studies a person they don't care about. Nell could recite every promise Edith had ever made and broken, every name Edith had ever used carelessly, every meeting where the wrong woman had been seen walking in and never quite seen walking out. She was, without knowing it, a second ledger. All I had to do was survive being near her long enough to open it.

The press itself was a corpse we worked inside of. The army had seized the *Ashcombe Courier* in the first week — presses, paper stock, even the ink — on the theory that a town about to become a munitions depot should not be permitted to have opinions. They had left the building standing because demolishing it would have cost labor, and labor was wanted elsewhere. So the type cases sat in their racks gathering dust that fell like slow grey snow, and the stone floor held the ghost-smell of ink and oil, and above us the requisition notice still hung on the door, its paste dried to a map of some country that no longer existed.

We worked by candlelight because lamplight showed through shutters and candlelight, if you were careful, showed through nothing. We worked between eleven and three because the patrol passed the print shop at quarter past and quarter to, and because Nell's brother George slept like the dead two floors up and she preferred he stay that way — asleep, I mean, though the other sense would come to apply later, and neither of us knew it yet.

Here is what we were doing, and I will describe it plainly because plainness is the only honesty left to me: we were rebuilding a book from memory.

Edith's ledger — the master ledger, the one with every member, donor, correspondent, and sympathizer in three counties — had been seized along with everything else, boxed by clerks who did not know what they held, and stored in the requisitioned hall pending transport to the capital. I had seen it go into Box 41. I had carried Box 41 myself, which is the sort of irony I would have found funny in another life. And in my notebook, sewn into the lining of a coat I wore only at night, I had — over months of driving and reading and memorizing — the fragments: addresses copied in my own hand, names recalled from letters, dates from meetings I'd driven women to and waited outside of with the engine ticking.

Between those fragments and Nell's memory of the town — who lived where, who had married whom, whose sister had gone to London in March and never written back — we meant to reconstruct enough of the truth that when Captain Voss's official history said *no significant dissent*, there would exist, somewhere, a document that said otherwise in the voices of the people themselves.

"A wrong name," Nell said, setting the tin on the stone. "That's what frightens you. Say it."

"A wrong name could hang someone," I said. "Or worse. A right name in the wrong column. If I write that Mrs. Aldous donated two pounds when actually she only attended one meeting — if I inflate her — then when Voss's people cross-check against the original ledger, she's not a witness anymore. She's a conspirator. I'd be manufacturing the evidence that ruins her."

"And if you leave her out entirely?"

"Then she never existed, and neither did any of it, and Voss wins by default."

Nell nodded slowly, the way a person nods at a diagnosis. "So there's no safe version."

"There's no version without blood on it," I said. "There's only choosing how much, and whose."

She pulled the lamp closer. "Then let's choose carefully."

* * *

The first night we got through the Hacketts.

It sounds like nothing, put that way — one family, one entry. It took us two hours.

The trouble was not remembering them; the trouble was remembering them *correctly*, and discovering how little of my memory deserved the confidence I'd been lending it. I had Ruth Hackett down as a subscriber — five shillings, quarterly, since the winter meetings began. But Nell frowned at that.

"Ruth didn't subscribe. Ruth couldn't afford to subscribe. Her husband drank half the wages." She said it without malice, the way you'd correct a date. "What Ruth did was let Edith use her kitchen. Every second Thursday. Which is more dangerous than money, if you're making lists of dangerous things."

"Which means she goes under *correspondent* or *host*," I said, "not donor."

"Which means," Nell said, "you write it however the ledger wrote it, or your reconstruction doesn't match the original, and a mismatch is as good as a lie."

And there it was — the trap inside the craft. I wasn't writing truth; I was forging a record, and forgery has its own terrible ethics. Too accurate and I confirmed the army's own document, gave Voss's clerks a ready-made index of suspects. Too soft and I erased the very women I was trying to save. The needle's eye between those two failures was narrow enough to thread only by candlelight and only at three in the morning, and even then my hands shook.

"Ruth Hackett," I dictated to myself, pencil hovering. "*Hosted fortnightly meetings, winter '16–'17. No funds recorded. Husband opposed.*"

"*Husband opposed*," Nell repeated. "Is that in the original?"

"It's in a letter. She wrote to Edith in January asking whether meetings could move because Tom had begun counting the chairs." I closed my eyes and heard the letter again, Edith's voice reading it aloud in the motorcar while I drove and pretended not to listen: *He counts the chairs, Mrs. Marlowe. He has never once counted anything else in his life, but now he counts the chairs.*

Nell laughed — one short, startled bark, quickly smothered against her sleeve. "That's Ruth exactly."

"That's Ruth exactly," I agreed, and wrote it down, because humor is evidence too, and because a record that makes the dead laugh is a record worth the risk of making.

* * *

By the fourth night we had a system, and the system was Nell's father's.

Arthur Ashcombe had run the *Courier* for thirty years, and printers, Nell explained, are the original archivists of small towns — not because they keep records but because they set them, letter by letter, and a man who sets ten thousand lines of parish news learns where every body is buried. Hidden among the ordinary slugs in the biscuit tin — and here Nell's voice went strange with pride and grief fighting for the same breath — were the ones her father had saved when the seizure came: ligatures, special sorts, the odd pieces of display type no jobbing printer could replace. And beneath the false bottom of the tin, wrapped in waxed cloth, a full set of the italic fount he'd used for the society column, hidden the day the soldiers walked in, because — Nell quoted him — *"They can take the press, girl, but they can't take the alphabet."*

He'd died in February. Heart, the doctor said. Nell said otherwise, privately, to me, once, and then never again.

So we worked like compositors instead of writers. I drafted each entry in pencil on scraps of wallpaper lining; Nell checked it against her memory of the town; where we agreed, the entry went into the fair copy; where we disagreed, we argued until one of us remembered something concrete — a face, a date, a funeral — that broke the tie. If neither of us could, the entry went into a separate page marked with a red thread: *uncertain*. Nell insisted on the thread. "A doubt you can see," she said, "is a doubt you can fix. A doubt you've smoothed over is a lie wearing a collar."

I have known editors, generals, and bishops who understood truth less well than a printer's daughter with a spool of red thread.

The intercepted letters were harder. Those I had copied verbatim into my notebook — forty-one of them by then, pulled from the army mail sacks on my runs, letters from townspeople to evacuated relatives describing the requisition, the seizures, the men who'd vanished after the depot survey. Copying them into the manuscript was simple transcription. Deciding whether to include them whole or excerpt them was surgery.

"The whole letter," Nell said, every time. "Always the whole letter."

"Some of them say things that could get the writer identified. Phrases. Habits."

"Phrases and habits are how the families will know them. You excerpt a letter and you've amputated the proof it's real."

"But a whole letter is a confession signed in handwriting."

Nell looked at me across the candle for a long moment. "Whose confession? Theirs, for complaining about soldiers stealing their chickens? Or ours, for printing that they complained?" She tapped the page. "You've already decided to be guilty of something, Lockridge. Decide to be guilty of the whole thing."

We printed the letters whole. I have never regretted it. I have often been afraid of it. Both facts are true, and I have learned not to make them fight.

* * *

Errors found us anyway. That is the part no one tells you about craftsmanship: it is not the absence of error but the ritual of hunting it nightly, knowing the hunt never ends.

On the sixth night I caught my own hand having written *Ellen Pryce* where the ledger — where I *believed* the ledger — had *Ellen Price*, and I sat frozen for the better part of a minute because I could not remember which was true, and both women existed, and one of them had a brother in the depot garrison. A misspelled name in a seized document is a clerical slip. The same misspelled name in a smuggled pamphlet is a death warrant with a typo in it.

"Burn it," Nell said, when I told her. "Rewrite the page. Never trust a correction — corrections carry the ghost of the error."

We burned entries in the stove Nell's father had used to melt lead, and the smell of burning wallpaper and pencil graphite became, for me, permanently the smell of fear. To this day a struck match puts me back in that cellar.

On the eighth night Nell caught mine. I had placed a donation from the Thorndike sisters in October; Nell went very still and said, "The Thorndikes weren't in town in October. They were in York — the aunt's funeral. My father printed the announcement." She fetched the obituary clipping she'd salvaged from the waste pile — printers save everything; it's a disease and a virtue — and there it was, dated the third week of October. I had invented a fact out of the shape of my own certainty, and if she hadn't caught it, the Thorndikes would have had an alibi problem they couldn't explain away.

"How many others?" I asked. Not aloud to her — to myself, staring at the pages. How many errors were sitting in my fair copy wearing the uniform of truth?

"All of them, until proven otherwise," Nell said. She was re-inking her fingers absently, a habit, touching the type even though the press was gone. "That's the only honest way to do this work. Assume you're wrong and make yourself prove you're right."

* * *

Somewhere past midnight on the ninth night — I know it was the ninth because we had begun marking nights the way prisoners mark walls, tallying them on the inside of the coal cupboard — Nell stopped mid-check and looked at me across the candle.

"Why you?"

"Why me what?"

"This." She gestured at the spread of paper, the tin, the red-threaded doubts. "You drive for Marlowe. Fine. Plenty of people drive for causes they barely believe in — it's wages and petrol. But you sit here every night copying letters by hand like a monk with a deadline. You read mail that isn't yours. You memorize filing orders in an army hall." Her eyes narrowed. She had her father's eyes, ink-dark and unsentimental. "Nobody does all that for somebody else's cause. So either you're paid in ways I haven't imagined, or this is yours too."

I considered lying. It was, I reflected, the first time in years the impulse had required actual effort — usually the lies came pre-fitted, seamless, worn smooth. But Nell Ashcombe had spent nine nights watching my hands shake over other women's names, and you cannot lie convincingly to someone who has seen you afraid for strangers.

"I read the letters," I said. "All of them. Before she ever asked me to drive anywhere, I read them."

"I know. You just said—"

"No. I mean that's the whole answer. I read them and I couldn't stop. Every letter was a person insisting they existed, and everyone around me — the army, the clerks, the historians — treating them as inventory. Somebody had to be the one who thought they mattered." I turned the pencil over in my fingers. "It's not noble, Nell. It's compulsion. Some people drink. I archive."

She studied me a moment longer, then snorted — almost a laugh. "Compulsion. My father called it the same thing. Said a printer who doesn't check his spellings twice is committing a sin against people who'll never know his name." She picked up her pencil. "Fine. Compulsion I understand. Patriotism I'd have thrown out."

We worked on. The tally on the coal cupboard grew. The fair copy thickened — fourteen pages, then nineteen — each entry argued into existence, each letter transcribed whole, each uncertain name wearing its red thread like a wound dressing. Outside, the patrols passed at quarter past and quarter to, boots on cobbles, and we froze in our practiced way, candle pinched, hands flat on cold stone, breathing like women who had done nothing all day but sleep.

Once, in the silence of one such freeze, I heard Nell whisper something. When the boots faded I asked her what.

"Hail Mary," she said. "For my father. He'd have loved this. Sneaking the alphabet back out from under their noses." She relit the candle. "He always said the truth keeps badly. Like milk. Somebody has to keep churning it into something that lasts."

"Cheese," I said.

"Don't ruin it."

But I was smiling, and so was she, and for a moment — one candlelit moment in a dead man's print shop, surrounded by forged history and honest doubt — it felt less like smuggling and more like printing. Like the proper, ordinary, stubborn work of putting words on paper and trusting paper to outlive the men who burn it.

Then I reached the next gap in my memory, and stopped smiling.

It was the section I had been circling for three nights, the way you circle a sore tooth. The donations from late autumn — the largest single block in the ledger, the money that had kept the network fed and moving — and threaded through them, in Edith's careful hand, a series of initials I had seen only once, briefly, when she'd shown me the page to explain why certain routes were suddenly safe and certain drivers suddenly retired. Initials attached to sums. Initials belonging to someone inside the requisition itself.

Someone inside the army had been feeding Edith information. Someone inside the army was named in the ledger. And that page — that single, crucial page — was the one thing my memory had refused to hold.

I had assumed it would come back to me, the way the rest had come back, coaxed out by candlelight and argument. It hadn't. It wasn't coming. And every hour we spent perfecting the surrounding entries made the hole more dangerous, because a reconstruction with one deliberate gap looks like carelessness, but a reconstruction missing exactly the page naming the army's informant looks like protection — and protection, discovered, points straight at the informant like a finger.

"Nell," I said. "I have to go back to the hall."

She looked up from the type tin, and in the candlelight her face did something complicated. "When?"

"During the blackout," I said. "Tomorrow night."
