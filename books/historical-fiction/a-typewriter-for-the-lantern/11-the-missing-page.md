## 11. The Missing Page

There is a particular kind of arithmetic that keeps a person awake, and it is not the kind taught in schools. It goes like this: one page of Edith Marlowe's ledger contained forty-one names. I could recite thirty-nine of them in alphabetical order, in order of enlistment, and in order of the dates they'd first written to her. Two were missing from my head entirely — one because I'd read it at the end of a long day and my eyes had skated over it like a stone across water, and one because—

Well. Because of the second reason, which I will come to, and which is worse.

I sat in Nell Ashcombe's back room with a candle stub burning down to its brass dish and went through it again. Thirty-nine. Nell had fallen asleep at the composing table, her cheek on a forme of type she'd be feeling for a week if she didn't move soon, and I envied her the sleep and hated myself for envying it. Outside, the requisitioned town made its new noises — boots on gravel where there used to be dogs, the clank of lorries being unloaded at what had been the corn exchange. A town doesn't die all at once. It dies by substitution.

Thirty-nine names. The fortieth was Mrs. Halloway's cousin or Mrs. Halloway herself; I'd have sworn to either under oath, which is precisely why I couldn't swear to it at all. And the forty-first —

The forty-first was the informant.

* * *

You should understand how Edith ran things, since I am the only one left who does, and even I run on fumes now. She kept two ledgers. The public one — subscriptions, meeting minutes, the harmless scaffolding of any respectable association — she kept locked in a drawer and would show a magistrate with pride. The other ledger lived wrapped in oilcloth inside a biscuit tin inside a hatbox, and it recorded everything: every donor who wished to remain anonymous, every woman whose husband beat her for attending meetings, every letter forwarded through the network, and — on the last leaf, folded once and pasted down at the edge so it looked like part of the binding — the name of the man inside the army itself who fed us the requisition schedules before they were posted.

Edith never told me his name. That was discipline, not distrust; she told no one. But she showed me the page once, when she was teaching me the route, and said, "If anything happens to me, you'll need to know this exists, even if you never learn what it says." Then she let me look for exactly as long as it took to understand that I was looking at something that could get a man shot against a wall, and she closed the book.

I had remembered its existence. I had not remembered its contents. There is a difference between keeping a secret and keeping a fact, and I had kept only the first, like a woman who remembers there was a key without remembering which door.

Now the ledger was boxed in the records office of the requisitioned hall, catalogued by Sergeant Pryce and destined for the capital, and Captain Voss was arriving within days — we'd heard the date from a clerk who liked to talk — to write the campaign's official history, in which no doubt nobody had ever resisted anything, and the forty-first name would become an exhibit, a charge sheet, or worse: a footnote in a book nobody would ever think to check.

And the thirty-ninth name — the one I'd half-glimpsed and couldn't fix — belonged, I was almost certain, to a woman whose brother was a sergeant in the garrison. If I guessed wrong in print, I would either expose an innocent woman or fail to protect a guilty arrangement, and both failures would land on someone's neck.

Nell woke, lifted her face from the type — leaving, I noted, a beautiful backwards impression of the letter E on her cheek, which struck me as the most honest portrait anyone had taken of her — and looked at my list.

"You're missing one," she said.

"I'm missing one," I agreed.

"Can't you just... leave it out?"

"Leave out the name of the man the army will hang when they find him?" I said. "Or leave out the proof that he existed, so that when Voss's history says no one helped us, no document contradicts him?"

Nell rubbed the E off her cheek, which seemed ungrateful of it. "Then you know what you have to do."

I did. I had known since midnight, and I had been negotiating with myself ever since, offering myself smaller errands, easier truths. The self is a poor negotiator. It always ends up agreeing to the thing it set out to avoid.

* * *

Let me describe the moral vertigo properly, because people imagine conscience as a compass, steady and pointing. It is not. It is more like standing on the parapet of a bridge in a high wind, knowing that whichever way you lean, someone below will look up.

To go back into the hall was to serve them again — to put on the driver's coat and the cap and become, once more, the useful local boy the army believed it had hired. Every mile I drove for them bought me a mile closer to their paper. But the paper I needed was the paper that named their traitor. If I took it, I wasn't stealing a record. I was deciding, alone, in a candlelit room with a girl asleep on her type, that a man inside that garrison should live or die according to my memory of a page I'd seen for ten seconds eighteen months ago.

And if I left it — if I let the ledger travel to the capital intact — then Voss would have it, and Voss would read it, and Voss, whatever else he was, was not bored the way Sergeant Pryce was bored. Pryce had skimmed the names looking for quantity. Voss would read looking for meaning. He would find the pasted-down leaf, because a historian finds pasted-down leaves the way water finds low ground.

Either way, I held a man's life in the space between two bad choices, and the only comfort available was the miserable one that at least the choice was mine. Edith would have said something about how comfort isn't the point. Edith, wherever she was evacuated to, was probably saying it right now to some poor suffragist branch in some safe town, and they were nodding, and none of them knew that her biscuit tin was in an army crate with a shipping label on it.

I put out the candle before dawn, the way you do when the light has stopped helping.

* * *

The hall had been a drill hall before it was a corn exchange before it was a drill hall again, which tells you something about the ambitions of towns like ours: we build rooms for war, convert them to commerce, and are always faintly surprised when the army comes to collect. Now it was Records, Section B, and smelled of floor wax laid over eighty years of horse.

Sergeant Pryce ran the inventory with the serene incompetence of a man promoted beyond his curiosity. He liked me. This was the strangest feature of my situation — that among the men occupying our town, dismantling our records, and boxing our neighbors' lives for shipment to the capital, the ones who liked me included a sergeant, two corporals, and a cook who saved me heel ends of bread. Occupation makes odd friendships. You can hate a system perfectly well while liking the tired man standing inside it, and I did both daily, and neither canceled the other, and I had stopped expecting them to.

"Lockridge," Pryce said, looking up from his manifest. "They've got you running the mail run Thursday?"

"And the supply run Friday."

"Efficient." He stamped something. "Half these crates go south Monday. Historian fellow wants the rest kept for his reference — Captain Voss. Fussy man. Wants everything cross-indexed."

My heart performed a small, cold maneuver. "Cross-indexed?"

"Cards. Every name on a card, every card in a drawer." Pryce said it the way another man might say *another day, another dollar*, and I understood that the army was about to do, with better funding and worse motives, exactly what I had been doing at night in Nell's back room. We were rivals, the army and I. Both of us rebuilding a town out of its own paperwork.

"When does the captain arrive?" I asked, lightly. Lightness is a skill. I recommend practicing it on small questions so it holds on large ones.

"When he arrives. Officers are weather." He waved me off toward the yard, and I went, and behind me the crates stood in their rows like a congregation waiting for a sermon none of them wanted to hear.

* * *

Here is what I planned, sitting in the cab of the mail lorry with my hands around the wheel and the engine ticking: Thursday's mail run ended at the hall. Deliveries took twenty minutes. After that, the yard emptied for the shift change, and the records room door — I had watched it for three days — was locked with a key that hung on Pryce's belt, except on Thursdays, when a clerk from the adjutant's office came to collect returns and Pryce left it open because the clerk outranked him in practice if not on paper.

Ten minutes, perhaps twelve, with the pasted leaf. I had done harder things with less planning, though I confess most of those had been lies rather than burglaries, and lying has the great advantage that if it fails, you are merely disbelieved, whereas burglary, failing, gets you shot.

But here is the vertigo again, and I want to be honest about it, because the histories — Voss's especially — will make all of this sound clean. I lay awake that night doing the sum. If I copied the name and burned nothing, the original still traveled to the capital and Voss still found it. To actually protect the man, I would have to take the page itself — remove it from the record — and a record with a hole in it is a confession with the confession torn out. Anyone competent would know a leaf had been cut. Anyone competent would ask who had access. The list was short. It began and nearly ended with Sebastian Lockridge, driver, trusted local, quiet young man.

So: take the page, betray myself. Leave the page, betray him. Copy the page, accomplish nothing. Every road out of that room went through somebody.

There was a third option, and I turned it over the way you turn over a coin you suspect is weighted. The third option was to find the man himself — the informant, whoever he was — and tell him. Let him choose. Run, confess, sabotage, whatever a man chooses when he learns his life has been sitting in a crate with a shipping label. It was the honorable option. It was also insane, because I didn't know his name, which was rather the entire problem, and because telling him meant revealing that the network's most dangerous secret had survived the seizure, which would send him — reasonably — straight to his commanding officer to report that the conspiracy continued, at which point every name in my notebook would be worth a second look.

I lay there in the loft above the stable where the army stabled its horses and I lodged by their sufferance, listening to the animals shift, and I thought: everyone believes they are the reasonable one in the story. Voss believes it. Pryce believes it. Even the men who post requisition notices believe they are the reasonable ones, and the notices say so, politely, in two languages of bureaucracy. The only honest position available to any of us is vertigo — to act anyway, knowing the ground is moving, and to keep acting, and to write down what happened afterward including the parts that don't flatter.

That was the decision, such as it was: I would go in Thursday. I would copy the page, all of it, verbatim, and I would decide about the rest with the page in front of me instead of in my imagination, because imagination is where all the worst versions live.

It was not a noble decision. It was a working one. Nobility, in my experience, is mostly what people call their decisions after everyone affected is dead.

* * *

Thursday came in grey and drizzling, the kind of weather that makes a town look apologetic for itself. I ran the mail route with my usual punctuality, signed the usual chits, exchanged the usual two sentences with the gate sentry about the usual rain. Inside the hall, the crates for Monday's shipment had been chalked with numbers, and I counted them as I passed — habit now, though each count landed in me like a small hammer. Forty-one crates of a town. Somewhere in row three, a biscuit tin wearing a hatbox as a disguise.

The clerk from the adjutant's office arrived at four, as he always did, red-faced and hurried, and Pryce, as he always did, unlocked the records room and left the key in the lock "just till I'm back, mind," and went off to argue with the quartermaster about lamp oil. The clerk buried himself in the returns.

I delivered the last of the mail. I walked back through the corridor with an empty satchel and a full pulse. The records room door stood open six inches. Beyond it, shelves of confiscated paper rose to the ceiling — parish registers, shop ledgers, school rolls, letters, the whole sedimentary record of a town that had existed for two hundred years and now, officially, was being reviewed for security.

I put my hand on the door.

From the far end of the corridor, unhurried, came footsteps — measured, polished-heeled, the tread of a man who has never once hurried because hurrying implies events control him. And with the footsteps, a voice, light and courteous, saying to someone: *"No, no — leave the shelving as it is. One should always reconstruct the original arrangement. A record out of context tells you only what it says. In context, it tells you what it means."*

Captain Voss had arrived four days early.

He rounded the corner into the corridor, saw me — a driver, a satchel, a hand on an open door — and inclined his head with perfect civility.

"Ah," he said pleasantly. "Someone who knows where things are. Splendid. I wonder if you might help me after all — I'm told the Marlowe papers are in here somewhere, and I should very much like to see them before they're crated."

His eyes, mild as milk, rested on mine, and on the door, and on my hand still resting on the door, and I smiled the smile I'd practiced on small questions, and reached for a large one.
