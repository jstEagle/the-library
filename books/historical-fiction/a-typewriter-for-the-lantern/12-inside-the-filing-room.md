## 12. Inside the Filing Room

The blackout came to Marlowe Cross the way most official things did — late, badly organized, and announced by a man with a clipboard.

Air-raid drills, the notice said. Every Thursday and Sunday from eight o'clock until midnight. All lights extinguished. All personnel indoors. The depot's searchlights would be testing their sweep patterns across the valley, so anyone moving out of doors did so at the risk of being mistaken for a signal to the enemy, which was a phrase that could mean anything from a lantern to a cough.

I read it twice and then a third time, because it was the first document the army had produced in four months that worked entirely in my favor.

A town with no lights was a town where I could walk. A records office locked for six hours under regulation rather than judgment was a lock with a schedule. And searchlights sweeping the valley meant the sentries' eyes were aimed at the sky instead of at the woman crossing the yard with a clipboard of her own — requisition form 44-B, *driver assigned to records transport*, which I had copied onto real army paper with Nell Ashcombe's father's best forgery hand and which had already carried me through three checkpoints without a single soldier looking above my chin.

"You're going," Nell said when I told her. It wasn't a question. She said it the way you'd say *you're bleeding*.

"I'm going."

"They'll shoot you. They'll shoot you and they won't even write down that they shot you, because writing things down is apparently my family's profession and nobody else's." She was sharpening a pencil against the heel of her palm, a nervous habit she'd inherited along with her father's type slugs and his bad knee. "What page is worth your life?"

"The one I can't remember."

"Which is?"

"A name." I folded the forged form into my coat. "The name of the person inside the depot who fed Edith the shipping schedules. Edith called her *Lantern* — never anything else, never once, not even in the ledger margins. But there was a page. A single page near the back, filed under correspondence, that tied Lantern to an address, and I read it exactly once while Edith pretended to look at the weather. I was twenty-three pages past it before I understood what I'd read, and by then we were at the Hendersons' and she was asking me to hold her gloves."

"And you can't reconstruct it."

"Nell, I can recite the Hendersons' pantry inventory from that day. I cannot produce that name. My memory keeps everything except the thing I ask it for; it's like a clerk who files brilliantly and then goes home."

She stopped sharpening. "If you find it, and it says what you think it says — that someone inside this depot has been passing information to Edith Marlowe since before the requisition — do you understand what happens if that page falls into the wrong hands? Not Voss's hands. Any hands."

"I understand."

"Because retrieving truth and betraying a person are sometimes the same act, Seb, and the page doesn't care which one you meant."

That was the trouble with Nell Ashcombe. Everyone assumed that because she hated Edith Marlowe she was simple, and everyone who assumed it got a lecture about epistemology instead.

* * *

Thursday, nine minutes past eight, the last lamp in the officers' quarters went dark and the valley went black as a closed book.

I had planned the route for eleven days, walking it in daylight at different hours, wearing different expressions. From the drivers' billet behind the motor pool, across the yard where the coal heap stood — coal heaps don't count against blackout regulations because no one has ever successfully regulated coal — through the gap in the fence that Private Dunmore had cut for his own smuggling purposes and that I knew about because I drove his letters, including the ones to a girl in Harrowgate he believed didn't know about the girl in Leeds. Then along the drainage channel behind the quartermaster's stores to the rear entrance of the old county hall, which the army had converted into its records office and which still had, bless the county surveyor of 1889, a servant's door with a lock designed to keep out burglars of a previous century.

I had picked the lock twice in daylight practice, gloved, timed. Forty seconds. On the night it mattered it took me ninety, because my hands were doing something independent of my intentions, and I stood in the doorway listening to my own breathing and thought, *this is the moment you turn around, Lockridge*, and then thought, *and this is the moment you've been waiting for since they nailed those notices to the church door,* and went in.

The filing room occupied what had been the county hall's reading room. I knew it in its former life: long oak tables, green-shaded lamps, the smell of paste and newsprint, schoolgirls doing homework under the eye of Miss Prewitt, who had once confiscated my pencil for chewing it and returned it at the end of term with the teeth-marks sanded off, which I have always considered the single greatest act of mercy performed in this town. Now the tables held ledger boxes. Rows of them. Army-grey cardboard, stenciled in white: *MUNITIONS DEPOT NO. 9 — CIVIL RECORDS — SECURITY REVIEW.* Our whole civic life, boxed by subject and alphabetized by men who thought a parish was a kind of weather.

The seizure inventory ran to forty-one boxes. Box thirty-four was correspondence, M through R. I'd watched the clerks pack it — that was the point of volunteering as a driver, that was the whole terrible bargain, access bought with suspicion — and I'd memorized the order the way I memorized everything, compulsively, uselessly, until it was suddenly neither.

My torch was a nurse's penlight with a shuttered slide, borrowed from a woman in the hospital annex whose gratitude for a delivered letter had been expressed in practical terms. I slit the shutter to a thread of light and began.

The room was cold enough that my breath showed, which was a problem, and quiet enough that I could hear the building settle, which was worse. Somewhere overhead a beam ticked as it cooled. Outside, right on schedule, the searchlights woke and began their slow idiot ballet across the clouds, and through the high windows the light swept the ceiling in pale arcs like something searching for me by reflection rather than sight.

Box thirty-four. Correspondence, M–R. The folders inside were army-issue now, our papers refiled into their categories: *SUBVERSIVE CORRESPONDENCE*, *SEIZED PERSONAL EFFECTS*, *EVIDENCE — PENDING*. That last category was the one that made my teeth ache. Evidence pending. Pending what? Pending a decision about whether the fact that Mrs. Aldous had written her sister in Birmingham complaining about the price of mutton constituted treason. Pending Captain Voss's editorial judgment, which was the same thing but with footnotes.

I found the Marlowe folder at the back of the box, thick as a Bible, tied with the army's flat grey tape. And beneath it, thinner, mislabeled in a clerk's careless hand as *MARLOWE, E. — MISC.*, the loose sheets.

I went through them with the penlight an inch from the paper, faster than caution approved, because the drill ended at midnight and the sentry rotation gave me — I checked my father's watch, another dead man's gift — fifty-one minutes.

Billet assignments. Requisition appeals. A child's letter to a soldier, intercepted and read and filed, God help us all, under EVIDENCE because the boy had drawn a picture of the depot with the powder magazine marked with an X, which any child could do, every child in the district had done it, we'd all grown up pointing at that hill.

And then the fourth sheet. Edith's hand — I'd know it anywhere, that upright slanted script with its furious little crosses on the t's:

*Lantern confirms: the 3:40 southbound carries priming powder the second Tuesday of each month. Guard detail reduced on those runs. If the committee requires proof beyond this, Lantern will provide it, but I beg the committee to weigh what exposure costs her. — E.M.*

Below that, in a different hand, smaller, rounder, careful as embroidery — the address. Not a name. An address, a room number, a description of the window that faced the parade ground, and beneath the address, in the same embroidered hand, two words that closed the matter forever: *she is frightened.*

There it was. Page found. The name I couldn't remember wasn't a name at all; it was a place, and a person behind a particular window, and I sat down on a packing crate because my knees had opinions and let the relief move through me like fever breaking.

Fifty-one minutes became thirty-eight. I took out my notebook — the real one, the one that lived sewn into the lining of my coat, the one holding four months of names and addresses and routes and the entire reconstructed skeleton of Edith's network — and I copied the page. Verbatim. Both hands. Both sets of words. Edith's fury and Lantern's fear, side by side, exactly as they'd lain in the file, because context is evidence too and I had promised myself I would steal nothing out of proportion.

I was copying the final line — *she is frightened* — when the penlight crossed the top margin of the sheet and caught something that shouldn't have been there.

Pencil. Faint, in the upper corner, in a hand I recognized because I had spent an entire afternoon being interviewed by it: neat, small, unhurried, the handwriting of a man who had never once needed to cross anything out.

Captain Voss had annotated the page.

Not stamped it. Not initialed it in some official capacity. He had taken a pencil to Edith's letter the way a scholar takes a pencil to a manuscript, and in the margin beside *the 3:40 southbound* he had written: *Verified against freight manifests, Feb–June. Accurate.*

Beside the address: *Confirmed by observation. Window described correctly.*

And at the bottom of the page, alone, centered almost, in that same meticulous hand, five words that stopped my heart the way a hand stops a clock:

*Identity probable. Driver, local.*

I did not move. I want that recorded somewhere, even though the only record is this one: for the length of ten breaths I did not move at all, while outside the searchlights swept and swept, and somewhere in the dark between me and the door a floorboard settled with a sound like a knuckle cracking.

He knew. Not certainly — *probable* was doing careful work in that sentence, and Voss struck me as a man who chose words the way a surgeon chooses blades — but he suspected. He had read this page, verified it against manifests, gone and looked at the window himself, and then sat down and reasoned his way outward from a frightened woman behind glass to the network that used her, and from the network to the people who moved through it, and among all the possible movers he had set a small penciled question mark next to the idea of a driver. A local driver. Retained by the army. With access.

Every favor I had traded for, every checkpoint waved through, every bored sergeant who hadn't looked above my chin — all of it had been building a case in somebody's notebook without my knowledge. While I collected names, Voss had been collecting mine.

And yet.

Here is the thing I could not make fit, standing there in the cold with my heart going like a typewriter: he had not acted on it. Weeks. He'd had this page for weeks — the annotation was weeks old, the graphite softened and smudged from handling — and no soldiers had come for me, no questions beyond his courteous interview, no arrest, not even an inconvenient assignment to the far end of a supply route. A man who suspected the driver of being the network's courier had kept driving with the driver. Had interviewed her politely. Had shown her, if I recalled that afternoon correctly, the very layout of his own workroom.

Which meant either that Voss wanted something from me more than he wanted me arrested — or that Voss was not building a prosecution at all.

I looked down at the page in my hands, at Edith's fierce script and the stranger's frightened one and the historian's patient pencil between them, three hands on one sheet of paper, three versions of the same war. Then I folded it back along its original creases, retied the folder's tape in the same flat knot, slid the box shut, and put the crate back exactly one boot-width from where it had stood, because Miss Prewitt taught us that a library survives on exactness.

Thirty-one minutes left. Plenty of time to reach the door.

I was within twelve feet of it when the handle turned.

No knock. No fumble of keys — the lock had been picked, or opened by a key in silence, which amounted to the same signature. The door swung inward a hand's width and stopped, and through the gap came the smell of pipe tobacco and cold wool, and a voice pitched precisely for a small room, courteous as ever, unhurried as ever, a voice that had clearly been waiting in the dark for some time:

"Don't reach for anything, Miss Lockridge. Or should I say —" and here he paused, and I could hear him smiling, hear the pencil tapping once against the doorframe, "*— whichever name you find most convenient.*"

The searchlight swept the ceiling, and in its brief white arc I saw Captain Voss's face, and he was not angry.

He looked, God help me, delighted.
