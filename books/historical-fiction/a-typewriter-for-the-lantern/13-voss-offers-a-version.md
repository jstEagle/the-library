## 13. Voss Offers a Version

Captain Voss sent for me on a Tuesday, which I later decided was deliberate. Tuesdays were the days the requisitioned hall smelled worst — coal smoke and carbolic and the sweet rot of papers left too long in damp corners — and a man who wanted to unsettle you would choose the room at its most honest. Or perhaps he simply liked Tuesdays. Tyrants are rarely as theatrical as we hope they'll be. It would make them easier to hate.

The note came by the usual channel: a folded chit handed to me with the mail sack, instructing *Driver Lockridge to present himself at the records office, 1600 hours, no cargo required.* No cargo. That was the part that made my hands go cold on the steering wheel of the ambulance I was driving that afternoon. Everyone in that building knew what my cargo usually was. A summons without a parcel meant the summons was about me.

I had three hours to decide who to be.

This is the arithmetic of a false life: you are always choosing, moment to moment, which version of yourself to wear, and each choice narrows the wardrobe. Sebastian Lockridge, driver, mildly stupid, agreeably bored — that was the uniform for soldiers. Sebastian Lockridge, observant, careful, quietly ambitious — that one I'd worn for Voss himself, twice, during his interviews for the official history. He had liked that version. He had written things down while I talked, in a hand so small and even it looked typeset, and he had asked questions with the gentle persistence of a man lifting stones to see what underneath them was load-bearing.

I drove the afternoon run with my heart going like a sewing machine and considered running. Considered it honestly, the way you consider anything you know you won't do. The reconstructed ledger sat in Nell Ashcombe's cellar under sacks of flour, four-fifths rebuilt, every page an accusation. If I vanished, Voss wouldn't need to find it. He'd only need to find its absence suspicious, and absence is easier to prove than possession.

So I washed my face in a horse trough, because vanity is a kind of armor too, and presented myself at 1600 hours exactly. Punctuality, I have found, disarms people. They expect either defiance or fear; punctuality suggests a man with nothing on his conscience but his laundry.

* * *

The records office had been the town's subscription library before the army ate it. I remembered it from girlhood — or rather, I remembered inventing memories of it, since the real girl who might have visited such rooms had never existed. I'd given her a childhood in these shelves anyway, when Edith asked where I'd learned my letters. Fiction is habit-forming. Start with a name and soon you're furnishing whole houses.

Voss had cleared a reading table by the window and set out two chairs, facing each other, no desk between us. A man who wants to interrogate you puts furniture between you. A man who wants to recruit you removes it. I noted this with the part of my brain that never stops noting things, the part that has kept me alive, and sat down in the offered chair like a woman with nothing to declare.

"Miss Lockridge." He pronounced it carefully, as if checking the spelling against something. "Thank you for coming. Tea?"

There was tea. There was even milk, hoarded somewhere in this town of requisitions, and I understood that the milk was a message: *I can get things. Things you want.* I drank it and complimented it and hated myself for the compliment, because flattery given under duress is still a coin spent.

"You've been useful," Voss said. He said it the way a jeweler says *you've been appraised.* "Reliable routes. Good memory. Sergeant Hale tells me you can recite the filing order of the confiscated correspondence after two weeks."

"Sergeant Hale gives me too much credit. I just got tired of asking him where things went."

"Mm." He turned his cup a quarter-turn on its saucer, a small precise violence. "You understand what we do here. Most people think an army seizes paper to destroy it. Crude. Burning a record proves nothing except that someone feared it. We keep records, Miss Lockridge, because a kept record can be *corrected*. A burned one merely becomes legend, and legends grow teeth."

"I'm a driver, Captain."

"Yes," he agreed pleasantly. "Let's discuss that."

He rose, crossed to the shelving where the confiscated papers lived in their numbered boxes — I knew the order better than the clerks did, box forty-one through sixty-three, Marlowe's network alphabetized into evidence — and drew out a single folder. Thin. He laid it on the table between us without opening it, the way a card player sets down a hand he already knows wins.

"Sebastian Lockridge," he said. "Born Harrowgate, 1889. Father a railway clerk, mother deceased. One brother, Thomas, killed in '14. Educated at the parish school. Employed by Mrs. Marlowe's committee as a driver since March. Is any of that wrong?"

"Not a word."

"No," he said softly. "Not a word of it is *wrong*. That's rather the marvel, isn't it? Every fact checks. Every document exists. Your references answered their letters — I've read the replies. And yet." He opened the folder at last and turned it toward me. Inside was a photograph, grainy, of a churchyard in Harrowgate. Of a grave. "Thomas Lockridge's grave is well tended. Someone sends money for flowers. The parish clerk remembers the family fondly. But here is the thing that troubles my sleep, Miss Lockridge: the parish register lists the births of 1889. I've read it twice. There is a Sebastian born in June. There is no sister."

The room went very quiet. Outside, a lorry changed gear. Somewhere a typewriter clattered its idiot applause.

"The register could be incomplete," I said.

"It could be many things. Registers are like witnesses — imperfect, and improved by pressure." He closed the folder. "I am not the police, Miss Lockridge. The police would arrest you and produce nothing but a martyr, and martyrs are terribly inefficient. I am a historian. What interests me is not punishment. It is *accuracy*."

I looked at him then, really looked, past the courteous gray eyes and the immaculate cuffs, and understood that everything dangerous about him was true: he was meticulous, patient, sincere. He believed in the truth the way a tax collector believes in revenue. Absolutely, and with an invoice attached.

* * *

"What accuracy?" I asked. My voice came out steadier than I deserved.

"The accuracy you possess." He leaned back, folding his hands, a professor settling into the best seminar of his week. "You have been inside Marlowe's organization for months. You memorized her correspondence before my clerks ever touched it — don't look startled, Hale isn't a fool either, and drivers who recite filing orders are drivers who read. You know the names, the routes, the sums, the sympathizers in the telegraph office. You may even know things she never wrote down, because women like Marlowe trust their drivers with more than parcels." A thin pause. "And I believe you have been reconstructing her master ledger. From memory. Possibly with assistance."

My blood performed some complicated maneuver, dropping to my boots and then flooding back up behind my ears. Nell. Had they found Nell? Had the print shop been watched? I kept my face doing nothing, absolutely nothing, the way you hold a full glass across a crowded room.

"If such a reconstruction existed," I said, "it would be treasonous to admit."

"Treason is a word for losers' paperwork. Let me tell you what I'm actually writing, Miss Lockridge." He took from his case a sheaf of pages — his own manuscript, typed, the paragraphs marching down the pages in obedient ranks — and read aloud, in the voice of a man reading scripture he mostly agrees with:

"*In the spring of 1917, the War Department established a munitions depot at — *, and here follows the town, whose name will appear in the official record exactly once, spelled correctly, which is more than most towns get. *The transfer of population was effected with minimal disruption. Certain agitators, principally one E. Marlowe, circulated petitions of no legal standing; the matter was handled administratively. No significant dissent was recorded.*"

He lowered the pages.

"That last sentence cost me three drafts. *No significant dissent.* Do you see what it does? It doesn't deny your women existed. Denial invites investigation. It renders them statistically irrelevant. Fifty years hence, a researcher will find my book, and the parliamentary record, and the regimental histories, all agreeing, and she will conclude there was nothing to find. The cruelest erasure isn't the burning of names. It's the footnote that says *insignificant*."

"And mine would be significant, I suppose. In your version."

"In *a* version." He set the manuscript aside and regarded me with something I was unprepared for: respect. "Here is my offer, and I will make it only once, and I will not insult either of us by pretending it is generous. Surrender the reconstructed ledger. All of it. Names, copies, your notebook if you keep one — and you keep one, Miss Lockridge; I'd stake my commission on it. In exchange: immunity. Not the grudging sort. Written, signed, filed where the military police cannot lose it. Marlowe herself goes unmentioned in my account beyond the single administrative sentence — I cannot protect her from other departments, but I can make certain my book gives them nowhere to start. And—"

He paused, and this pause had been rehearsed. I could hear the rehearsal in it, the way you hear the second take in a gramophone record.

"—and your brother's death gets a paragraph. A good one. Thomas Lockridge, killed in the first autumn of the war, whose sister served faithfully through the requisition. Honest service, honorably recorded, in a permanent volume, under your name. Think about that. Under your *name*. Everything else you are is borrowed from a dead man's grave. I am offering to bury the fiction and print the woman. That, Miss Lockridge, is the only immortality on sale anywhere in this country, and I am the only shopkeeper offering it."

* * *

I want to be honest, because the book I eventually built was honest, brutally so, about everyone including me. So I will write down what I felt in that chair, with the tea going cold and the milk-sweetness turning faintly rancid on my tongue:

He almost had me.

Not with the immunity — I'd priced my neck long ago and found it cheap stock. Not even with the paragraph about Thomas, though that landed somewhere below my ribs and stayed there. No. What nearly had me was the sentence itself. *No significant dissent.* Because I had imagined, in my worst hours, exactly that future: researchers pulling down the official volumes, finding them unanimous, shrugging, moving on. Voss wasn't threatening to erase the women of Edith's network. He was describing how erasure actually works — politely, permanently, in good prose — and he was offering me the one thing I could not manufacture alone: a place inside the permanent record.

That is the temptation you should fear. Not the bribe that insults you. The bargain that respects you. He had done his homework on my vanity the way I'd done mine on his filing cabinets.

"You've thought about this," I said.

"Every version of this conversation. I told you — I'm thorough." He smiled for the first time, and it was not unkind, which made it worse. "You're wondering whether I'd keep my word. Fair. Historians are notorious liars, though usually only about causes. Yes, I would keep it. Every clause. I don't want your silence bought and broken; broken silences make bad footnotes. I want the record *settled*. Settled things stay settled."

"And the women in the ledger. The ones who signed petitions, lent money, hid pamphlets in flour sacks." I watched his eyes flicker — barely, a millimeter, but I'd spent months learning to read millimeters — at *flour sacks*, and I banked that flicker like stolen coal. "They become insignificant."

"They become *safe*. Insignificance is the best protection I know how to offer. Ask anyone who survived a war by being beneath mention." He stood, gathering his manuscript, and the audience was ending, and my heart was pounding out the question I hadn't dared ask: *what happens to Nell, what happened to Nell, why did you say possibly with assistance.* "You needn't answer today. Tomorrow, at this hour. Bring whatever you decide to bring. Or bring nothing, and I'll write around you, and we'll both live with our versions." At the door he stopped, half-turned. "One thing more, purely professional curiosity. The missing page — the informant in the depot command. Marlowe's ledger had a page cut out before confiscation. You wouldn't happen to know who removed it."

"Drivers don't read, Captain. Isn't that regulation?"

"Regulation," he agreed, "is a genre of fiction. Good evening, Miss Lockridge."

* * *

I walked back through the darkening streets with the town gasping into blackout, shutters banging, the searchlight on the water tower sweeping its slow idiot lighthouse arc, and I did my arithmetic.

He suspected. He had suspected long enough to dig up a churchyard, and he had let me drive his mail for weeks anyway, which meant either cruelty or cultivation, and with Voss it was always both. He knew about the reconstruction — or believed he knew — and had chosen recruitment over raid, which bought me time but not safety. Time, in my experience, is the one currency that always devalues overnight.

And he wanted the ledger. Wanted it badly enough to pay in the only coin I'd ever truly coveted: permanence. A paragraph under my own name. I turned the offer over and over as I walked, the way you probe a sore tooth. Immunity for the network. Silence for Edith. A grave in Harrowgate finally allowed to mean what it meant. And in exchange, the truth, owned — typeset in his ranks of obedient paragraphs, corrected, annotated, rendered harmless, every name in Nell's careful hand converted into evidence of insignificance. Preserved, yes. Preserved the way flies are preserved in amber: perfectly, forever, and entirely for the benefit of the amber.

At the corner by the shuttered bakery I stopped, because a figure was waiting in the lee of the doorway, hood up, and for one lurching instant I thought: *they've taken Nell and this is her ghost.*

Then the figure spoke, low and unmistakable, a voice I'd have known through six feet of earth.

"We need to talk," said Edith Marlowe, "and you look, Seb, like a woman who's just been offered the world at a discount."

She stepped out of the shadows, alive, presumed evacuated, wearing a stranger's coat — and every plan I'd been building since Voss's door closed behind me caught fire at once.
