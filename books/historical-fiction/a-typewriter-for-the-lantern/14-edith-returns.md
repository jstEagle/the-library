## 14. Edith Returns

The trouble with disguises, Nell had told me once, is that people wear them for the eyes and forget the voice. She said it about her father's customers — farmers who'd come into the shop in their Sunday coats expecting to be treated like aldermen — but I thought of it three weeks later, on a Tuesday night of thin rain, when a knock came at the cellar door of the Ashcombe print shop and a woman's voice asked, in an accent that belonged to no county in England but to every pulpit in it, whether this was where one might purchase pamphlets on the care of bees.

Nell looked at me. I looked at the door.

Nobody in that town kept bees. Nobody in that town had ever kept bees. The word was Edith Marlowe's — a signal from the old route days, meaning *I am here, I am watched, speak of weather* — and I had heard it exactly twice before, both times from Edith herself, standing in doorways with her hat at its usual defiant angle, delivering instructions she expected to be obeyed by Thursday.

I opened the door.

The woman on the step was not Edith Marlowe. That was my first thought, and my second, and then my third thought arrived late and corrected the other two: she was Edith Marlowe wearing a widow as one wears a coat two sizes wrong. Grey hair pinned under a grey scarf, grey coat, spectacles with plain glass in them, shoulders stooped into a curvature Edith's spine had never permitted. But the hands — the hands were folded in front of her the way she folded them when waiting for a train that was late through no fault of the railway's, and the chin above the scarf had the set of a woman who has been told *no* so many times that the word has stopped meaning anything.

"You're late," I said, because what else does one say to a ghost.

"The train was bombed outside Reading," she said. "Twice. Once by them and once, I'm told, by us. The confusion of modern war." And then, stepping past me into the cellar without waiting for invitation, because Edith Marlowe had never in her life waited for an invitation: "Is there tea? Don't answer. I can smell there isn't."

* * *

She sat down on the packing crate we used for type trays and accepted cold coffee instead, and while Nell lit the lantern and fussed with the flue, I performed the arithmetic I'd been refusing to perform since I opened the door. Edith had been evacuated — or had *appeared* to be evacuated — in October, on the last civilian transport out, waving from the window with the serene confidence of a general leaving the field in good order. The army's own records, which I had driven and copied and memorized, listed her as processed, catalogued, and relocated to a hostel in the north. I knew this because I had typed a fair copy of the entry myself, in Sergeant Hale's office, with Hale complaining the whole time about his pen.

Which meant either the records were wrong, or Edith had made them wrong, or Edith had made me make them wrong without knowing it. All three possibilities frightened me in different ways, and I set them aside the way one sets aside a bill one intends to deal with after Christmas.

"You've lost weight," I said.

"I've lost everything else; the weight seemed redundant." She set down the cup. "Now. Show me what you have."

So I did. I spread the notebook on the type bench — the second notebook now, the first having been burned page by page in Nell's stove when it grew too dangerous to keep — and showed her the reconstruction. Four months of nights pressed flat under lamplight: names recovered from memory of the ledger, addresses cross-checked against intercepted letters, dates reconciled against Nell's father's delivery books and the parish register Nell had lifted from the vestry under her coat. Two hundred and eleven names. Some entries certain, some probable, four marked with the small black dot that meant *unsure — verify or cut*. Beside each name, where I could reconstruct it, the record of what had happened: arrested, requisitioned-out, deported, vanished. The ledger the army had seized was inventory. This was testimony.

Edith read the way she did everything — fast, upright even seated, one finger moving down the column like a schoolmistress following a line of sinners. Nell and I stood in the lantern light and let her read, and I confess I felt something I hadn't allowed myself to feel in months, which was pride. Two hundred and eleven names. I wanted her to say something. I hated wanting her to say something; I had spent years teaching myself not to want Edith Marlowe's approval, on the sound principle that she dispensed it the way a stingy housewife dispenses sugar, and I noticed who was watching.

"This one," she said finally, tapping a line. "Mrs. Aldous. You've marked her unsure."

"She signed a petition in March. I saw the signature. But I never confirmed the address on Cordwell Street, and there were two Aldouses in the parish register, and if I put down the wrong one—"

"You'll put down the right one," Edith said. "Because you're going to publish all of it."

"We are going to publish—" Nell began.

"All of it," Edith repeated, "in full, with every name, every date, every method. Not a summary. Not an appeal. A complete record, printed and distributed widely enough that they cannot bury it, and framed as evidence — evidence demanding a public inquiry into the administration of the requisition. Names attached to sworn statements. That is how you force a trial. Not with anonymous pamphlets that any minister can dismiss as the work of hysterics. With a docket."

The lantern hissed. Somewhere above us the rain worked at the roof.

"Edith," I said carefully, "every name means every name. Including the ones that will hang people."

"Including," she said, "the informant."

There it was. We had known about the informant since December — the person inside the army's own filing system who had fed Edith's network copies of requisition orders and transport manifests, whose existence was recorded nowhere except on the missing page of the original ledger, the page I had gone back into the hall to find and found annotated in Captain Voss's neat, patient hand. We had built the entire reconstruction around protecting that identity. Every other name could be published because arrest was already done to those people; the worst had happened, or the worst was survivable. But the informant was alive, inside the wire, still working, and one line of type would end them against a wall.

"Whoever it is volunteered," Edith said, before either of us could speak. "I didn't recruit them. They came to me. They understood the risk better than anyone, because they watched the machinery eat people daily. They would tell you themselves, if I could ask them, that the record matters more than the life. That is why they went in."

"That's a lovely speech," Nell said. "Did they give it, or are you giving it for them?"

Edith turned on her the look that had reduced grown aldermen to apologizing for their own committees. Nell took it the way her father's press took a misfeed — loudly, and without stopping.

"I know how this sounds," Edith said, softer, which was worse. "I've spent twenty years being told I don't understand cost. I buried a husband and a son, Miss Ashcombe; I understand cost down to the farthing. But hear me. If we trim the record to protect ourselves, we produce exactly the document Voss wants us to produce — partial, deniable, dismissible. He wins not by burning our truth but by making our truth too timid to matter. A censored account confirms the official one. Only the whole thing convicts."

"And the whole thing kills one person," I said.

"One person," Edith agreed, "who chose the work knowing the price, and who would rather be named than be the reason the others were abandoned to footnotes."

And God help me, some part of me believed her. That was always the danger of Edith Marlowe: she was never lying. Every word she said was true, arranged in an order that made a lie. She had taught half the women in that county to stand up in public and risk gaol, and she had done it with precisely this voice, this certainty, this arithmetic of sacrifice conducted by someone who was always, somehow, not among the sacrificed. I loved her. I had crossed half of England for her, lied to soldiers for her, built a false self like a load-bearing wall around the space where she'd told me to stand. And I knew — I had known since the sergeant's bored pencil moved down the real ledger — that love of Edith was a thing you survived or you didn't.

"Say the name," I said.

"No," she said instantly.

"Then hear mine. If you order publication with the informant named, I need to know who it is, because I am going to warn them first. Whatever you decide, they get the hours between your decision and the presses that Nell's brother can give them. That's not negotiable. That's the toll."

"You'd compromise the whole timetable—"

"I'd compromise nothing. I'd spend one hour saving a life you're spending for rhetoric."

The silence that followed had texture. Nell looked at the floor. Edith looked at me over the plain-glass spectacles, and behind the glass her eyes did the thing they did in meetings when someone found the flaw in her resolution — a flicker, quickly governed, of respect that she would deny to her grave.

"You've become careful," she said. It was not a compliment. From her mouth, it was nearly an accusation.

"You taught me the routes," I said. "Careful is what keeps drivers alive."

"Careful is what keeps drivers employed," she said. "It isn't the same trade anymore, Seb."

* * *

We argued until two in the morning, which is to say Edith argued and Nell and I took turns holding the line, and the argument went around the same circuit like a horse in a mill: the docket versus the pamphlet, the trial versus the truth, the whole record versus the living informant. Edith wanted a public inquiry with counsel and subpoenas and newspapermen in the gallery; she wanted the machinery of the state turned on itself, formally, on the record, with verdicts. Nell and I wanted paper in ten thousand hands before Voss's history hardened into fact — anonymous, unattributable, unstoppable, no witness to subpoena, no author to arrest. Her way was a cathedral; ours was a fire. Her way required the enemy's courts to play fair. Ours required only ink and luck.

Somewhere past midnight she played her best card, and I should have seen it coming, because Edith always saved the card she knew would hurt the most.

"You wrote it," she said quietly. "All of it. You drove the letters, you read them, you carried the ledger, you walked into the hall and stole back the page. Your testimony is the spine of this document, Seb, and testimony is worthless anonymously. An anonymous accusation is a rumour. A signed deposition is evidence. If you won't sign it — if you hide behind the dead man's name again — then you are asking every woman on that list to take a risk you won't."

"That's not the same," Nell started, but I put a hand on her arm.

"It's exactly the same," I said. "That's what makes it a good argument. It's also wrong. My name isn't mine to give, Edith. It belongs to a dead man, and the moment I sign anything — anything at all — some clerk pulls his file and finds the discrepancies, and then they don't get me, they get the thread, and they pull the thread, and everyone I touched comes off the spool. You taught me that. Chapter and verse, 1913, the Harbury arrests. 'A network is only as strong as its most expendable member.' You called me that once. Expendable. You meant it kindly."

"I never—"

"You did. And you were right, and I've built my whole life on it. So no. No signature. No docket. No trial convened by the men who burned the town, because a trial requires them to lose gracefully and they have never once done anything gracefully in their lives." I was standing now; I don't remember standing. "You want justice with their fingerprints on the gavel. I want the truth loose in the world where no writ can reach it. Those aren't the same cause anymore, and I think they stopped being the same cause somewhere around the time you got on that train and left us holding everything."

The words landed. I saw them land. For one unguarded second Edith Marlowe looked her age — older than her age, older than the century — and then the governor came down over the machinery and she was smooth again.

"You think I left," she said. "You think I ran."

"I think the records say you were evacuated."

"The records," said Edith Marlowe, "say a great many things. Ask yourself sometime who taught me to write them."

She rose, straightened the borrowed stoop out of her spine as though shrugging off a wet coat, and stood in the lantern light looking down at the notebook with its 211 names and its four black dots.

"I'll give you three days," she said. "Not because you've persuaded me. Because I need them to reach the informant myself, and warn them, since apparently no one else in this room has the stomach for the truth in its entirety. After that, I go to London. I put the docket in the hands of a barrister who owes me, and I force the inquiry with or without your pamphlet. If your paper comes out first and whole, it strengthens my hand beyond measure. If it comes out trimmed—" she gathered her grey scarf, her grey dignity, her terrible patience "—then it weakens me, and I will say publicly that it does, and I will be sorry, and I will still say it."

At the door she paused. Rain, and the smell of wet coal, and somewhere across the darkened town the depot's searchlight swinging its idiot lighthouse arc.

"You were the best driver I ever had," she said, not turning around. "Do you know why? Everyone thinks it's the memory. It isn't. It's that you never once asked whether we should be doing it. You only ever asked how." She opened the door onto the rain. "When you start asking whether, Seb, you stop being useful and start being alone. Be careful which question you fall in love with."

Then she was gone up the steps into the dark, and the door closed, and Nell blew out the lantern, and we stood together in the black listening to footsteps fade that sounded, all the way to the corner, exactly like a woman with nothing left to disguise.

"Well," Nell said at last, in the dark. "Now we know where your stubbornness was apprenticed."

"Now we know," I said, "that in three days she puts a name in front of a firing squad to save a paragraph in a barrister's brief. And we know she'll win, too — she always wins — unless the paper is out first."

Nell struck a match to relight the lantern. Her hand wasn't steady, and I noticed, and pretended not to, the way she had been pretending not to notice things about me for four months.

"Two hundred and eleven names," she said. "Four dots. One of them we can fix tonight — the Aldous woman. The vestry book lists the Cordwell Street family separately."

"Then we fix it tonight," I said, and reached for the type trays, and that was when we both heard it: boots on the lane above, unhurried, plural, stopping directly over the cellar — and a knock, polite and devastating, at the street-level door of the Ashcombe print shop.
