## 15. Nell's Betrayal

There is a particular smell to a print shop at three in the morning: ink and oil and hot metal, and underneath it all the faint sweetness of paper that knows it is about to matter. I came to love that smell in those weeks, the way one comes to love the taste of a rope, I suppose — familiarity being no defense against the thing itself.

Nell worked the case while I set type by candlelight shielded behind a screen of flattened flour sacks. We had a system, Nell and I, refined over eleven nights of stolen hours. She read aloud from my notebook in a whisper pitched for confession booths; I set the letters into their sticks; every forty minutes we swept the floor of our own evidence and started again. Her father's press had been seized with everything else in the town — the army took presses the way magpies take anything that shines — but the type slugs he'd buried under the coal cellar in '14, when he'd printed something unflattering about the mayor, had survived two years of darkness and one year of Nell's silence about them.

"Again," she said. "The Hargreave entry."

I recited it from memory, because the notebook page was smudged and because by now the names lived in me like bones. "Margaret Hargreave, milliner, Church Lane. Dues paid quarterly from March 1913. Hosted the reading circle of the eleventh of January. Letters intercepted: four. Signed the petition against the depot requisition —"

"— which the petition says was *unanimous among the women of the parish*," Nell finished, "and which Captain Voss's history will say never existed." She said his name without spitting on it, which somehow made it worse. She had a way of handling Voss's name like something dead she'd found in the road and didn't want to touch twice.

We were, by that night, thirty-one pages deep. Thirty-one pages of names, dates, intercepted letters copied verbatim, Edith's routes, the ledger reconstructed out of my skull and Nell's grudge. It was slow work. That was the whole problem with truth, I was learning. Lies could be typeset by machine, run off by the thousand before breakfast. Truth you had to build by hand, letter by letter, in a coal cellar, by candlelight, while somewhere a boy was deciding what your life was worth.

But I'm ahead of myself. Let me do this properly, as Voss would say, with his maddening courtesy. Let me begin with the brother.

* * *

Tom Ashcombe was nineteen, and he had the misfortune to be nineteen in a year when nineteen-year-olds were being fed into France like coal into a furnace. He'd been called up in the spring, rejected — flat feet, or a heart murmur, or some other mercy — and then reclassified in the autumn when the depot made laborers suddenly more precious than soldiers. He hauled crates for the army now, twelve hours a day, for pay that bought less each week, and he came home to a sister who vanished every night into a cellar and wouldn't say why.

I'd met him perhaps six times. He had Nell's dark eyes and none of her architecture; where she was all corners and load-bearing walls, Tom was soft in the middle, a boy built for being liked. He liked me. That's the detail I keep snagging on, turning it over like a coin I can't spend. He used to bring me tea when I waited for Nell in the shop's back room, and once he'd said, shyly, that it was grand having the driver around, because the house had been so quiet since Da died.

He asked questions, of course. Where did Nell go. What did she carry in that canvas bag. Why did the candle stubs keep disappearing from the kitchen drawer. And Nell gave him nothing, because Nell was her father's daughter and understood that a secret shared with family was a secret shared with everyone the family loved, and Tom loved easily and widely and without discrimination.

What I know now — what I pieced together afterward, from the interrogation records Voss kept so beautifully, from the statements, from Tom's own face when they marched him past the shop window in custody — is that it began with a sergeant named Doyle who ran the crate-yard and had noticed Tom Ashcombe counting things. Tom counted everything; it was his one talent and his affliction. He counted the rifles in the depot, the sacks in the granary, the hours on his chit. Sergeant Doyle found this charming right up until the week Tom mentioned, in the aggrieved tone of a boy who has been cheated at cards, that the requisition lists posted on the hall door didn't add up. That goods marked as spoiled and destroyed were appearing instead in officers' parcels going south.

Doyle didn't care about the theft. Officers stealing from an army is weather; it happens everywhere and changes nothing. But Doyle cared about leverage, and a boy who had caught officers stealing was a boy who could be taught that his own survival depended on producing other useful facts. Facts such as: my sister goes out at night. Facts such as: there is a light under the coal cellar door. Facts such as: there's a woman, a driver, Sebastian Lockridge, who comes and goes, and I've seen the bag, and I think —

I don't think Tom knew what he was feeding. I have to believe that, though belief is cheap and I've spent too much of it already. I think he thought he was buying himself out of trouble with small change, and that the small change turned out to be his sister.

The statement he signed, which Voss later showed me with the tenderness of a man presenting a surgical photograph, reads in part: *"My sister Nellie has been printing papers in our late father's cellar with a woman calling herself Lockridge. They are copying names. I heard them say the names would hang people if the wrong ones saw them."*

*I heard them say.* We never said it. But Tom had stood at the top of the cellar stairs one night in early December — I know which night, the eleventh, because it was the night I set the name of Alice Pym, the informant inside the army, the name that cost me a trip through a blackout later, though I didn't know that yet — and he had heard enough fragments to understand that what we were building was dangerous, and that danger, in the economy of Sergeant Doyle, was currency.

They came on the seventeenth of December.

* * *

I wasn't there. That is the fact I will carry until they put me under whatever stone carries whichever name I die with: I was not there.

Voss's men had been watching the mail train schedules, and the seventeenth was the day I rode north with the army post to Harrowgate Junction, four hours each way, because the driver they kept had to drive when the driver was told. I argued with myself about it the whole way up. Leave the manuscript? Take it? Taking it meant explaining its absence if the house was searched; leaving it meant trusting a coal cellar to hold what a nation wanted burned. In the end I left it, wrapped in oilcloth inside the false bottom of Nell's father's old paper cabinet, because moving it was riskier than keeping it, because slow careful work was the whole point, because — because I was wrong. There it is. The whole strategy of those weeks rests on that word. I was careful, and care was exactly the wrong instrument, because they weren't hunting the manuscript. They were hunting us, and we sat still.

I got back to the town at twenty past five in the evening, in the blue-black hour when the lamps were lit and the snow squeaked underfoot. The first wrongness was the dog. Mrs. Fenwick's terrier, an animal of indiscriminate enthusiasm that had barked at me daily for a year, was silent. The second wrongness was the light: the print shop windows, which should have been dark, held a glow that flickered wrong for lamplight, and there were shadows crossing it that moved like men carrying things.

I stopped at the corner of Tanner's Row and did what I had trained myself to do, which is nothing. A woman standing alone on a street corner at dusk is invisible; a woman walking briskly toward a raided print shop is a volunteer. So I stood, and I watched, and I counted — Tom's habit infecting me — six soldiers at the door, two more at the alley mouth, a sergeant with a lantern going through the window display with a poker, breaking the glass cases of type specimens one after another, methodically, the way a man shells peas.

And then they brought Nell out.

She came down the steps between two privates with her hands tied in front of her with wire, and here is the thing I need you to understand about Nell Ashcombe, because history will get it wrong otherwise, history always gets it wrong: she walked out of her own print shop like a landlady showing difficult tenants the door. Chin up. Shoulders square. And as they reached the pavement she stopped, planted herself, and said something to the sergeant — I couldn't hear it over the wind, but I saw the effect, which was that he stepped back half a pace like a man who has touched a stove — and then she looked directly up the street, directly toward the corner where I stood, and found me in the dark the way you find a fixed star, and she shook her head.

Once. Small. *Don't.*

Then the lorry door slammed and the engine caught and the tail-lamps went away up the hill toward the hall, and I stood on that corner for I don't know how long, long enough that a constable spoke to me, long enough that the snow gathered on my shoulders like the beginnings of a shroud, doing arithmetic. The arithmetic went like this: they had taken Nell and not me, which meant either they didn't know about me, or they knew exactly and wanted me loose, the way you leave a mouse alive to find the hole. The arithmetic also went like this: the manuscript was still in the cellar, unless they had found it, and if they had found it they would not have needed to take Nell, because paper does not resist arrest. Which meant the manuscript was probably still there. Probably. That word again. My whole life had become the word *probably*, and every day it cost someone.

I went back at midnight, of course. You may judge me for it. I have judged myself for it every day since, and I've grown expert at the trial.

* * *

The cellar smelled of boot polish and trampled coal dust, and the paper cabinet stood open with its false bottom sprung, and for one full second I believed they had it. Then I saw the oilcloth, shoved sideways into the gap behind the water pipe, wrapped tight, and beside it, weighted down with a flat iron, a sheet of paper in Nell's hand, the letters pressed deep the way she pressed everything:

*They have Tom. Whatever they ask you, he told them only what he saw. Get it out. Don't come for me.*

Don't come for me. Four words, and I want you to notice what she assumed: that I would come for her, that coming for her was the default setting of anyone who loved her, and that it had to be countermanded in writing, in advance, because she knew me. Eleven nights of type-setting side by side and she knew me well enough to write my orders before I could disobey them.

I got the manuscript out under my coat, which fit poorly over it and made me walk like a pregnant scarecrow, and I carried it home through streets where the garrison was lighting extra lamps, and I sat with it on my knees in my rented room above the saddlery, and I did not cry, because crying takes time and I had learned by then to bill every minute. What I did instead was worse. I made a list. I am a list-maker; it is my disease. Item: they have Nell. Item: they have Tom. Item: they do not appear to have the manuscript. Item: they let me stand on a street corner and watch, and no one followed me home, and either I am invisible or I am bait. Item: Voss.

Because that was the shape of it, wasn't it. Six soldiers at a door, two at an alley, a sergeant with a poker — that was Doyle's operation, blunt and local. But the timing, the seventeenth, four hours when the driver was known to be away; the choice to take Nell and leave the shop unlocked overnight; the wire instead of rope, custody instead of charge — that was not Doyle. Doyle broke windows. Someone else arranged the afternoon the way a man arranges type in a stick, each piece chosen, each space accounted for. Somewhere in the requisitioned hall, a courteous captain with beautiful handwriting had set this scene the way you set a page: knowing exactly what he wanted the reader to feel.

He wanted me frightened. He wanted me guilty — and oh, he got his wish, guilt came free of charge, guilt arrived before the soldiers did, guilt had been sleeping in my chest for weeks wearing Tom Ashcombe's soft young face and asking for tea. And beneath the fear and the guilt, laid down like a trap beneath straw, he wanted me to do precisely what I was sitting there wanting to do: come forward. Bargain. Trade. Bring him the manuscript and ask for Nell, and hand the last true record of a hundred women into the hands of the man whose profession was making true records lie.

I put the list in the stove and watched it curl. Then I opened my notebook — the real one, the bone-deep one, the one I could burn and rewrite from memory anyway — and I added the day's entries in a hand steadier than I had any right to expect. *Seventeenth December. Print shop raided, 5 p.m. approx. Nell Ashcombe taken into custody. Thomas Ashcombe implicated, statement signed. Manuscript intact, pages 1–31. Location changed.* And then, because honesty is a discipline or it is nothing, I wrote: *Cause: Tom Ashcombe, under pressure. Fault: mine. I was slow, and slowness is a luxury purchased with other people's bodies.*

The candle guttered. Outside, the snow kept falling on the occupied town, softening every edge, laying its white page over everything, waiting to be written on.

And it was somewhere past two in the morning, staring at that line — *fault: mine* — that I heard, quite distinctly, three soft knocks on the saddlery stair below, spaced like a signal and not like a visitor, and a voice I had last heard giving orders on a platform seven weeks ago, low and even and unmistakable, saying my name through the door.

Edith Marlowe was not in the north, where the evacuation trains had taken her. Edith Marlowe was standing on my stairs in a borrowed coat, and she had heard about the raid, and she had come back — and I understood, in the moment before I opened the lock, that everything I had decided about the manuscript, about bargains, about what Nell's freedom was worth and who would pay for it, was about to be argued with by the one person alive who had never once lost an argument to me.
