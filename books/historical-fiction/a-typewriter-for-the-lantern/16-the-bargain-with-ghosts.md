## 16. The Bargain With Ghosts

I did not sleep. I want to say that plainly, because everything that follows happened in the particular fever of a sleepless night, when the mind stops being an instrument and becomes a room with too many doors.

Nell was in the cells on Cordwainer Street, or she would be by morning — Voss's men worked with bureaucratic patience rather than cruelty, and cruelty delayed was often mercy denied. Her brother had given them the print shop. I could not hate him for it; I had seen him at the market, a boy of perhaps nineteen with his collar too big for his neck, and I knew what two hours in a back room with a patient sergeant could do to anyone. The question was not whether Nell had been taken. The question was what she had been taken *for*.

And there was only one answer on the table.

Voss had made his offer three nights ago, in that courteous office smelling of pencil shavings and lamp oil: immunity for me, a flattering paragraph in his history, the reconstructed record surrendered intact into official hands where it would be preserved, catalogued, and buried so deep no spade would ever reach it again. He had presented it as generosity. It was, in its way. He understood something I was still learning — that the most effective way to destroy a truth is not to burn it but to acquire it.

Now he had Nell, and the offer acquired a second clause I hadn't negotiated: herself, released, uncharged, if I came in and brought everything. He hadn't said it yet. He didn't need to. A man like Voss never says the second half of a bargain aloud; he simply arranges circumstances until you say it for him.

So I sat in the safehouse above the shuttered bakery with my notebook open and my coat still on, weighing myself against a girl I'd known for eleven days.

That sounds monstrous when I put it that way, and it should. Let me be exact about the arithmetic, though, because I have found that people who claim they wouldn't hesitate are usually people who've never held anything worth hesitating over. If I gave Voss the record, every name in it became a charge sheet. Edith's network — the women who passed letters, the farmers who hid pamphlets in flour sacks, the clerk who looked away at the right moments — all of them converted from history into evidence, their courage reclassified as conspiracy. Nell would walk free into a town where everyone she loved was being processed through a requisitioned hall by bored clerks with efficient hands. She would be free the way a bird released from one cage into a larger one is free.

If I kept the record and let Voss do his worst to Nell, she might serve months, maybe years. She was twenty-three and her father's press had been seized before she was old enough to set type alone; the army would struggle to make her more than an accessory. Probably. *Probably* is a word that weighs different amounts at three in the morning than it does at noon.

There was a third option, of course, which was to go to Voss and trade myself without the record — confess to being the driver who read the letters, hand over nothing, take whatever came. But Voss was not a man who paid for half a manuscript, and I suspected he'd decline the purchase with genuine regret and proceed anyway. Sentimental gestures only work as currency with sentimental buyers.

I closed the notebook. I put on my cap. I went out into the dark, because there was one thing I needed to see before I decided anything, and it wasn't in my notebook or Voss's files or anyone's ledger.

It was on the requisition lists.

* * *

The lists were posted on the hoarding outside the old corn exchange, which the army had claimed in the first week and now used as a records annexe. They appeared each Monday, pasted up in long columns under the heading REQUISITION OF GOODS AND PERSONS — the phrase itself a small masterpiece of the occupier's grammar, persons filed between cattle and grain without anyone finding it remarkable enough to protest.

I had walked past them a hundred times. Drivers learn to stop seeing posted paper; it's part of the uniform of invisibility we wear. But tonight I stopped, because I had remembered something, and the remembering arrived the way bad news always does — late, unwelcome, and complete.

The army kept records of everything it took. That was the whole point of Voss, wasn't it? A war fought with such thorough paperwork deserved a thorough historian. And among the things it recorded, in those first chaotic weeks before the depot walls went up, were the deaths. Accidents during construction. A boiler burst at the quarry. Men conscripted for labour details who didn't come home. The army logged them all, because dead men generate paperwork like wet wood generates smoke.

My brother had died in this district. Not my brother — I had no brother, that was the architecture of the fiction, Sebastian Lockridge built on the bones of a boy who had actually existed, a real child born in 1889 in a village forty miles south, whose papers I had acquired through channels I prefer not to itemize even now. I had chosen him from a registry because the dates fit, because the family had scattered, because a dead man's name fits a living woman the way a borrowed coat fits — loosely, and forever with the smell of someone else in the collar.

But here is what I had never done in nine years of wearing him: I had never gone to look at his grave. His true grave. Whatever grave there was.

I stood in front of the lists with my lantern turned low and read down the columns, and the cold coming off the hoarding timbers seemed to enter through my eyes.

He was there. Third column, fourteenth line.

**LOCKRIDGE, T. — labour detail, quarry road — deceased 14 March 1917 — cause: fall, blasting operations — effects: one knife, one photograph (unidentified), wages unpaid, remitted to no known next of kin.**

Fourteenth line. Not a name to anyone. Not a grief to anyone. One knife, one photograph of someone the clerks couldn't identify — and God help me, I knew who was in that photograph, or I knew the shape of the possibility, some girl or mother or sweetheart he'd carried folded in a pocket through the quarry dust, and the army had it now, catalogued, boxed, possibly already in the same transport convoy as Edith's ledger, travelling to the capital with all the other confiscated lives.

*Remitted to no known next of kin.*

I was his next of kin. That was the entire legal substance of my existence. And I had never come to collect his wages.

I don't know how long I stood there. Long enough that a sentry crossed the yard once and thought better of bothering me — drivers have a look, a tiredness that reads as permission to be left alone. I pressed my palm flat against the hoarding as if I could feel the ink drying underneath.

Here is the thing about building yourself out of a dead man: you tell yourself the story that makes it bearable. You tell yourself he is forgotten, that no one mourns him, that you have rescued his name from oblivion and given it work to do — good work, dangerous work, work he might have approved of. You tell yourself the borrowing costs him nothing because he has nothing left to lose.

And then you find him on a list between a requisitioned horse and forty sacks of cement, and you understand that the story ran the other way. You didn't rescue his name. You spent it. Nine years of spending, and the account had finally come due, and the collector was standing in front of you in your own face.

Because that was the realization that dropped through me like a stone through thin ice, wasn't it — that Voss's clerks had his death on file. That somewhere in the records annexe behind me, in a folder I could probably have requested with a signature and a plausible reason, lay the full account of Thomas Lockridge's last morning on the quarry road. And that any clerk who pulled that folder and then looked at me — really looked, the way Voss looked at things — might notice what I had spent nine years making sure no one noticed.

A woman driving under a dead man's name is invisible until she isn't. And the man across the desk from me three nights ago had said, with that terrible gentleness of his, *You wear your identity well, Miss— forgive me, Mr. Lockridge.* I had taken it for courtesy. I had been taking a great many things for courtesy.

* * *

I walked after that. No destination, or one I didn't admit to. My feet took me along the quarry road, which was permitted madness since drivers use it constantly, and I stood where the lists said he fell — near the third marker post, where the cutting drops toward the creek — and I tried to feel something specific and failed, and then hated myself for failing, and then understood the failure was the honest thing.

I had not known him. Grief needs a person and I had only ever had a name, and the name had been mine so long it had worn smooth in my mouth like a river stone. What I felt was not mourning for Thomas Lockridge. What I felt was the vertigo of a woman discovering the ground floor of her own house is a coffin lid.

Nine years ago I had stood in a registry office and copied a dead boy's particulars into my own hand, practising the signature until the loops stopped trembling. I remembered telling myself: he doesn't need it anymore. I remember thinking it was almost a kindness. Names want to live, I told myself; I'm giving his a life.

Standing on the quarry road in the black hour before dawn, I finally asked the question I had avoided since the registry: what would he have wanted? And the terrible answer was that I had no idea, because I had never once thought of him as someone who could want. He was furniture in my escape. He was a door I walked through and locked behind me.

One knife. One photograph. Wages unpaid.

I thought about the photograph — unidentified, the clerk wrote, but photographs get identified eventually, in slow archives, by patient historians, by men exactly like Captain Voss. Somewhere in the capital a box would be opened someday and a face would look up out of it, and someone would ask: who was this to him? And the file would say Lockridge, T., no known next of kin, and the question would die there, unless —

Unless the true account reached print. Unless somewhere in the record I was building there was room for a line that said: the man whose name carried this testimony was himself carried, unburied and unmourned, by the machinery this book describes. Unless I could pay him back in the only currency left — witness.

It's a small thing, a name on a list. But I had learned from Edith that the whole war was names on lists, and who got to write them.

* * *

The decision, when it finally settled, did not arrive as a decision. It arrived the way dawn arrives — gradually, then all at once, and impossible to argue with.

I would not give Voss the record. I had known that since the hoarding, maybe since before; the hoarding only taught me why in words. Every name I surrendered bought Nell's freedom with thirty other women's chains, and Nell — I thought of her inking her father's slugs by candlelight, tongue between her teeth, furious about kerning at one in the morning — Nell would rather serve the sentence than be purchased that way. I knew it the way you know things about people you've set type beside.

But I could not leave her there either. Which meant the bargain had to change shape. Not her freedom for the record. Not my confession for her file. Something else, something I couldn't yet see the outline of — and to find it, I would need to go back to Voss, sit across that courteous desk, and negotiate with a man who collected truths the way other men collected stamps.

Before that, though, there was one errand. The records annexe opened at eight. Clerks processed requests until ten. And somewhere in that building, in a folder stamped with a date I now knew by heart, was the complete file of Thomas Lockridge — including, if the inventory habits I'd memorized in that hall held true, the photograph.

His face. Possibly the only likeness of him left in the world, sitting in an army box while I drove around inside his name.

I turned my collar up against the grey coming on. I had traded myself for a dead man once already, nine years ago, and called it survival. Perhaps this time the trade could run in the other direction. Perhaps there was still time to return something to the fourteenth line of the third column.

At eight o'clock, I would walk into the records annexe and request a dead man's papers in his own voice.

It was either the bravest or stupidest thing I had ever planned, and I genuinely could not tell which, which is how I knew I'd finally become the person the name had been waiting for.
