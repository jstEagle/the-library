## 17. Typesetting by Candlelight

The press smelled of burnt oil and old iron and, beneath it all, of Nell Ashcombe — machine grease and cheap violet soap, a smell I had come to associate with the only honest arguments of my life. Her father's shop had been wrecked with the particular thoroughness soldiers reserve for printing equipment: cases overturned, type scattered like spilled teeth, the big flatbed press tipped onto its side as though it might be made to confess something.

They had missed two things, because they had not known to look for them. The first was the trapdoor under the compositor's stone, where Ashcombe senior had hidden his stock of forbidden slugs during the last war — the ones for names he'd been ordered not to print. Nell had shown me that cache weeks ago, with the grim pride of a woman showing you a family curse. The second thing they had missed was me, crouched behind the paper store with my heart going like a telegraph key, while a corporal kicked over the last drawer and pronounced the shop dead.

Dead shops keep better secrets than living ones.

I righted what could be righted alone — which is to say, I failed at it, swore at it, and then sat down on the floor among the wreckage and laughed until it turned into something else entirely. Grief will do that if you let it stand up too long without support. Then I lit one candle, shielded it inside a biscuit tin with holes punched in the lid, and began to sort the type.

* * *

You cannot set a book by candlelight in any sensible way. Every compositor who ever lived would tell you so. You need steady light on the stick, light enough to read the nicks in the type, light enough to know your em from your en at a glance. What you can do by candlelight is make mistakes, beautifully and repeatedly, and so that is what I did, and then corrected, and did again.

The work had three parts, and each part had its own particular torment.

The first part was the ledger from memory: four hundred and eleven names. I had carried them for months, first in my notebook and then, after the notebook became too dangerous to update, in my skull, where I rehearsed them the way other people recite psalms — on walks, in queues, in the dark of the drivers' barracks while men snored around me. Four hundred and eleven names, with addresses where I knew them, occupations where they were relevant, dates of arrest or disappearance where those dates existed. I had written them out twice since the raid and checked both copies against each other, letter by letter, the way you'd check a ransom demand.

The second part was the letters. Forty-three intercepted letters, copied verbatim before I delivered them into army hands, because delivering them was my job and copying them was my penance. Mrs. Aldous writing to her sister about the depot requisitioning her hens and, in the same breath, asking whether the meetings were still happening. Ruth Calloway's last letter, posted nine days before she vanished, cheerful on the surface and underneath it running like a cracked jug. The schoolmistress's note that said only *tell E.M. the north road is watched now* — which I had delivered, God forgive me, straight into the hands of the man watching the north road.

That was the third part. That was the part I kept trying not to write, and kept writing anyway.

* * *

Here is what honesty costs, at two in the morning, by candlelight, in a wrecked shop: it costs you the comfortable version of yourself.

It would have been easy — it would have been so easy — to write the account as a clean story. Brave women organized. A brutal army erased them. A loyal driver preserved the truth. There. A pamphlet you could hand to a vicar without blushing.

But Edith Marlowe's methods were not clean, and I had watched them at close range for two years, and an account that left them out would be Voss's history with the flags changed. He wrote resistance out; I would not write it in as something it never was.

So I set the type that said: *Mrs. Marlowe used the confessional. She took information from women whose husbands drank, whose sons deserted, whose debts were public knowledge, and she held that information in trust — and occasionally, when she judged the cause required it, in leverage.* I set the passage about the Henderson woman, whose husband beat her, and whom Edith moved to her aunt's house in Leeds without asking the husband, and how the husband went to the authorities, and how Edith's network closed around the woman like water closing over a stone. Was it rescue? Yes. Was it also a demonstration, performed early, so every wavering member understood what the organization could do — for you, and to anyone who informed? Also yes. Both things. I put them both in the type.

And then there was me.

There is no way to typeset yourself flatteringly when you are working alone with a candle and a clear conscience, and I recommend the arrangement to anyone who finds their own memoirs getting warm. I set it plainly: *The driver who carried these papers read every letter entrusted to her. She did this without permission. She built a private record of names belonging to people who never consented to be recorded by her, and she did it partly from conviction and partly because she wanted, with an appetite she has never fully explained, to exist on paper somewhere.* I set the passage about my name — that Sebastian Lockridge was a dead man, that his papers had been his sister's inheritance and her armor, that every signature I had ever given this town was a fiction wearing my handwriting. I set the fact that I had volunteered to drive for the occupation, that I had smiled at sergeants, that I had memorized filing orders while women I knew by heart were being processed through those same files.

And I set the worst sentence last, after midnight, when the candle had burned low enough that I had to hold the composing stick close to my face:

*I do not know whether reading the letters saved anyone. I know it did not save Ruth Calloway, whose letter I copied on the ninth of March and delivered on the tenth, and who was taken on the eleventh. If my record is published, let it say that too.*

* * *

The errors were the enemy, more than the cold, more than the fear of discovery. A wrong name is worse than no name. Print *Aldous* where the ledger said *Aldis*, and some clerk in the capital finds the discrepancy and uses it to discredit everything; worse, print the wrong initial and some innocent cousin gets a knock at the door. So I built myself a ritual. Each name got set, then checked against my memory-copy, then checked against the second copy, then read aloud in a whisper — because the ear catches what the eye forgives — and only then locked into the chase.

Four hundred and eleven names. By the fourth night I could hear them in my sleep, a congregation murmuring in alphabetical order. On the fifth night I found I had transposed two letters in *Whitcombe* and had to break apart a full page, and I sat with my forehead against the cold stone of the compositor's frame and thought about Nell, wherever they were holding her, and whether she was being asked questions about names, and whether she was giving them mine.

She wasn't. I knew she wasn't. She'd had three days of questioning already and the soldiers hadn't come for me, which meant she was holding the line the way her father had held it in the last war, with stubbornness and bad tea and silence. Knowing it didn't stop me checking the door bolt eleven times a night.

The trapdoor slugs earned their hiding place. Ashcombe senior's forbidden stock included every letter of the alphabet in three faces, hoarded against the day someone needed to print what couldn't be printed — but the quantities were uneven, hoarded by accident rather than design. I ran short of lowercase e's twice and had to rob them from completed pages and reset, which is the compositor's equivalent of unpicking your own stitches. I learned to hate the letter e, which struck me, somewhere past the second week, as a joke the universe had arranged specifically for my benefit. A book about erasure, starved of its most common letter.

* * *

On the ninth night, Edith came.

I heard the knock before I saw anything — three soft raps, pause, two more — the signal she'd taught the network in the first winter, back when a knock was just a knock and not yet evidence. My hand froze over the case. Nobody living should have known that knock except me, Nell, and the army.

Then: "Sebastian. It's raining and I'm seventy-one years old. Open the door."

Edith Marlowe, presumed evacuated to her sister's in Harrogate, stood in the ruined doorway wrapped in a coat that belonged to somebody's grandmother, her hair greyer than I remembered and pinned up under a scarf, looking exactly like what she was — a respectable widow of no importance whatsoever, which had always been her most effective costume. She looked past me at the candle, the sorted cases, the locked pages drying under boards, and something crossed her face that I had seen only twice before: the look of a general reviewing troops she hadn't expected to survive.

"You're setting it all," she said. "Everything."

"Everything."

"The Henderson business. Your reading of the letters." It wasn't a question. She'd read the locked pages' worth in thirty seconds of looking at me, the way she always could.

"Everything," I said again. "If we leave out what we were ashamed of, Voss wins without printing a word. He'll only have to wait. Someone will find the flattering version and use it to prove the whole account is embroidery."

She came further in, dripping, and stood over the forme of type like a woman standing over a grave she'd dug herself. For a long moment she said nothing, and I watched her decide something, and I have wondered since whether what she decided was to forgive me or merely to conscript me more completely.

"My methods," she said finally, "were the only ones available. That is not the same as saying they were right. Put that in as well. Word for word."

"Those words?"

"Word for word. A confession that paraphrases itself is just a defense with better manners." She took off the wet coat and hung it, absurdly domestic, on a hook that had survived the raid, and then she rolled up her sleeves. "Now show me your system for checking names. You've been doing it alone and alone is how mistakes get made. From tonight you set and I verify, and we argue about every disagreement until one of us is certain."

"And if we're both uncertain?"

"Then the name goes in with a mark against it, and the mark says *uncertain*, because uncertainty is also the truth and Voss prints none." She pulled a stool to the candle. "Well, driver. You read my letters for two years. You may as well spend one night setting my sins in metal."

We worked until dawn. We argued about the spelling of *Aldis*. She remembered things about the network I had never known, and forgot things I had carried faithfully, and once she stopped altogether, staring at a name mid-stick — Ruth Calloway's — and had to leave the candle for five minutes and stand in the dark doorway breathing the rain-smell until she could come back and verify the date of the arrest.

At dawn she slept two hours on the paper-store floor with her coat for a blanket, and I set type above her head, and the strangest thing, the thing I could not have predicted, was how much less afraid I felt with her there. Two liars keeping one true book. It was, I think, the closest either of us came in that whole year to being honest.

* * *

By the twelfth night the account stood complete in the chases: sixty-four pages, four hundred and eleven names plus the marked uncertainties, forty-three letters verbatim, the methods, the confessions, mine and hers, the dates, the gaps — the gaps printed honestly as gaps, because pretending completeness is just another kind of forgery. All that remained was to print, and printing takes paper, ink, and hours on the flatbed, and the flatbed was on its side and half its rollers gone.

Nell would have fixed it in a night. I fixed it in three, badly, with Edith holding lamps and passing tools and offering opinions about machinery she did not understand, until the press stood upright and grudgingly agreed to turn.

We printed the first proof copy on the thirteenth night. I pulled the sheet and held it to the candle with shaking hands — not from fear, for once, but from the sheer strange weight of seeing four hundred and eleven people made permanent in ink, the vanished ones restored to the record in the only country that would have them, which is print.

Edith read the proof straight through without speaking. When she finished she set it down and said, "Now burn the plates' location out of your mind and get this off my conscience and out of this town," and told me about a printer in the south, a sympathizer, and a mail route that was searched carelessly because it was searched constantly.

"There's a clerk," she added. "Voss keeps a man named Ferro who checks the southern mails personally. He's thorough. You'll need the sheets sewn into something boring. Mail sacks are best — nobody interrogates a mail sack, they only weigh it."

"And if Ferro weighs it carefully?"

"Then you'll want to be very charming," Edith said, "or very quick."

I folded the proof copy into my coat, next to my heart, where I kept everything I couldn't afford to lose and most of what I should have thrown away years ago. Outside, the rain had stopped. Somewhere toward the depot a train whistled — the night mail, southbound, leaving in fifty minutes whether the truth was aboard it or not.

Fifty minutes. I blew out the candle and started packing the sacks.
