## 20. A Typewriter for the Lantern

The cellar belonged to a man named Hollis, though I doubt Hollis was any more his name than Sebastian Lockridge was mine. He had a printer's forearms and a bookkeeper's hands and the particular stillness of someone who had spent thirty years listening for footsteps on stairs that weren't supposed to be descended.

"You'll want to know why I'm doing this," he said, the first night, setting the lantern on an upturned crate so its light fell across the typewriter like a stage cue.

"Everyone wants me to ask that," I said. "It saves them from telling me."

He almost smiled. "It's not principle, if that's what you're hoping. Principle is expensive and I've never been able to afford it."

"Then what?"

"My sister's name is in your ledger." He said it flatly, the way you'd report weather or a debt. "Page eleven, if your memory is as good as they say it is. She organized the mill girls in the winter of '16. She's been gone two years now, and Captain Voss's history says—" he recited it without looking at anything, which meant he'd read it until the words wore grooves in him—"'no significant dissent was recorded in the district.'"

I didn't say anything. There was nothing to say that the lantern light wasn't already saying better, shaking slightly because the cellar floor was uneven and I hadn't thought to wedge the crate.

"So," Hollis said, "you'll forgive me if my motive is smaller than yours. I'm not writing history. I'm correcting a typo."

* * *

The manuscript came down those cellar stairs in pieces, sewn into the lining of my coat, rolled inside a hymnal, folded once into a false bottom of a biscuit tin that had once held actual biscuits back when biscuits were a thing this town still had. Three months of work: the ledger rebuilt from memory, the intercepted letters copied verbatim in Nell's hand before they took her, Edith's methods laid out with the unsparing clarity she herself would have demanded, and my own deceptions set down in black ink where anyone could read them—the letters read at night, the names memorized, the dead brother whose grave I had worn like a coat.

Hollis read the whole thing through twice before he touched the keys.

"It's ugly," he said the second time, not as criticism but as diagnosis.

"Yes."

"The part about you reading Edith's correspondence. The part about the page you stole for the informant. You could cut those. Nobody would know."

"I'd know," I said. "And worse, Voss would be right. He offered me a version of this story where everyone was brave and no one was complicated. If we print that version, we've just lost by other means."

He nodded slowly, and I understood that this was the test he'd been running—not of my courage but of my honesty, since courage without honesty just makes a faster kind of lie.

"All right," he said, and pulled the paper from its wrapper. "Let's make the correction."

* * *

You have never heard anything until you've heard a good typist working in a cellar under a curfew. The keys struck like small, disciplined gunshots, each one muffled by a felt pad Hollis had rigged beneath the platen, each one still loud enough that we both flinched for the first hour, and then stopped flinching, and then began listening past the sound entirely—to the street above, to boots passing, pausing, moving on. He typed the way I imagined soldiers dug trenches: steadily, without romance, because stopping was the only real danger.

I sat opposite him with the original pages and read aloud when his eyes tired, which was how we discovered that my voice shook on certain names and not others. My brother's name. Nell's. The informant whose page I had carried out of the filing room with Voss's annotations burning against my ribs like a coal. On those names the words came out of me strangely, half-swallowed, and Hollis learned to wait them out without looking up, the way you wait out weather.

It was on the fourth night—or the fifth; the nights had begun to run together like wet ink—that I asked him to stop.

He stopped mid-line, fingers hovering. "Error?"

"No." I looked at the page he'd finished, the last one, the colophon of our little illegal gospel, and saw my name sitting there in his careful type. *The account was assembled and carried by Sebastian Lockridge, driver.* "Strike that."

"Strike what?"

"Me. All of it. Every place I appear."

He turned in his chair and looked at me properly for the first time all week. In lantern light everyone looks like a portrait of someone else, and I watched him try to decide which portrait I was.

"That's forty references," he said. "You're in every chapter. You're the spine of the thing. Without you it reads like the ledger wrote itself."

"Good," I said. "Let it look like that."

"That's not how evidence works. Anonymous testimony weighs less. Any inquiry—"

"There will be an inquiry." I was surprised at how steady my voice was, given that everything behind it was not. "Someday. And when it comes, they'll trace every name in this account back through every hand that touched it. That's what inquiries do. They follow thread after thread until the threads are people. Edith's network survives only if the account can't be traced to a living person. And I am"—I smiled, and it felt strange on my face, like wearing someone else's hat—"I am extremely traceable. Papers, employment record, a requisition badge with a number on it. I'm the loosest thread in the entire garment."

"Then strike the network's names too," he said, too quickly. "Strike your sister's—" He caught himself. His sister. "Strike mine. Protect everyone."

"And leave Voss's history standing as the only complete record? A record that says no one resisted?" I shook my head. "No. The names stay. All of them. The women who signed, the men who hid them, the informant, Edith, even the ones who informed on us—Nell's brother goes in, with what was done to him to make him talk. Everything. That was always the price. Truth without mercy or omission, remember?"

"Who said that?"

"I did," I said. "To someone who isn't here anymore."

He was quiet a long moment. The lantern hissed. Somewhere above us a truck changed gears and went on toward the depot, indifferent, carrying whatever munitions the town had been emptied to make.

"And you?" he said finally. "You go in everything except your own name. You'll be in it as *the driver*. As a function. As a pair of hands on a wheel. Doesn't that—" he searched for the word and I watched him find it and decide whether to spend it— "doesn't that cost more than the others?"

I had three weeks to prepare an answer to that question, and I had prepared several, and they were all lies, and lying to Hollis seemed suddenly obscene in that little room where we were typing the only honest document either of us would ever touch.

"Yes," I said. "It costs exactly what it looks like it costs. I built myself out of paper, Hollis. Sebastian Lockridge is a fiction with excellent posture. And here's the joke of it: the ledger proved I existed. Page eleven or wherever—my assumed name, written down in Edith's hand, in the master list. It was the only proof anywhere on earth that I was real. When the army seized that ledger, they seized the only evidence of me. So you see, they've already erased me once. This second erasure is voluntary. It's the only thing in this whole affair I get to choose."

He didn't answer right away. Then he took the finished page, fed it back into the machine, and rolled it up until my name sat under the type bars like a man under a lamp.

"Last chance," he said.

"Strike it."

He did. Four keystrokes, spaced and deliberate, each one landing somewhere in my chest. *Sebastian Lockridge, driver* became *a driver* became, after he re-set the sentence, nothing at all—a gap in the line, a silence where a person used to be, grammatically seamless.

"There," he said softly. "Now nobody can be traced through you."

"Now nobody can be traced through me," I agreed. And found, to my genuine astonishment, that underneath the loss there was something that felt almost like relief—like setting down a suitcase I'd forgotten I was carrying, and standing up straighter without it, and realizing the weight had been the whole time the point of the exercise.

Authorship, I thought, watching him pull the corrected page free and lay it with the others. Everyone assumes it means putting your name on things. It doesn't. It means deciding what remains when your name is gone.

* * *

We worked nine nights in total. On the sixth, the air-raid siren went and we kept typing through it, because a raid meant the guards went below ground and the streets were ours for forty minutes. On the eighth, a key turned in the door above and we stood pressed against the cold brick with the lantern shuttered and the fresh pages held against our hearts like contraband infants, while someone—drunk, lost, or hunting, we never learned—wandered the shop overhead for eleven minutes by Hollis's pocket watch. Eleven minutes is longer than you think. I spent them composing my own obituary, which was fitting practice, given everything.

On the ninth night, the last sheet came out of the machine.

Hollis held it up to the lantern and read the final lines aloud, slowly, the way you'd read a verdict:

*"This account was compiled in defiance of the official record. Every name in it was verified by at least two sources or attested by direct witness. Where the compiler erred, the error stands uncorrected rather than concealed. No signature follows, because signatures are how they find you, and finding us must not become possible again. Let the work be the name."*

He lowered the page. "That last line isn't in your draft."

"No," I said. "I added it tonight. While you were sleeping off the eleventh minute."

"It's good."

"It's true, which is better."

There were four copies. We had planned six, but paper had grown scarce and honest, and four was what the world allowed. Hollis wrapped each one in oilcloth, sewed the seams himself with a sailmaker's stitch he claimed to have learned in a life I suspected he'd invented for the same reasons I'd invented mine, and laid them in a wooden box that had once, according to its stenciled ghost, contained detonators. I appreciated the humor. Humor was getting thin on the ground.

"One goes south on the mail train," he said. "One to a contact in the capital who owes me and will hate repaying. One stays buried under this floor until it's needed. And the fourth—"

"The fourth is yours," I said. "Your sister's copy."

His hands stopped over the box. For a moment the careful face failed him entirely, and I saw the man underneath, the one who had spent two years reading Voss's elegant lie and bleeding quietly onto it.

"She'll never read it," he managed.

"People said the same about the ledger," I said. "People said the same about every word in this cellar. The record holds, Hollis. That's the whole discovery of my life. You bury it, you burn it, you arrest the people who made it—and it holds. Paper outlives intention. It's the most stubborn material God ever invented."

He closed the box. Set his palms flat on the lid, as if taking an oath through wood.

"When this is over," he said, "when it's safe—will you tell me your name? Your real one?"

The lantern flame leaned in a draught from under the door and straightened itself again.

"No," I said gently. "Because if I told you, then there'd be one more person alive who knew it, and one more person who could be made to give it up. And besides—it's going in the notebook anyway. Someday. Written down where no one will ever file it. That's enough of a monument for a fiction like me."

* * *

I left the cellar before dawn, the way I'd come, up through the trapdoor into the print shop that smelled of ink and dust and Nell's absence, and out the back into a street the color of unwashed linen. The box rode against my hip under my coat, four copies weighing perhaps six pounds altogether, which seemed absurd—six pounds against a captain's typeset certainty, six pounds against an army.

At the corner I paused, as I always paused, and looked back at the dark building. Somewhere inside it, under floorboards, lay the account of a town that officially had never resisted, compiled by a woman who officially did not exist, typed by a man whose name was probably also a coat he wore.

The mail train left at seven-forty. I had a ticket in a pocket sewn into a coat that was, by now, more pockets than coat.

Six pounds, I thought, and started walking south before the fear could catch up with me, because I knew from long experience that it could walk very fast indeed—and somewhere behind me, faint through the morning fog, I could swear I heard the click of keys, though the machine was silent and Hollis asleep, and I chose to believe it was only the town itself, beginning, at last, to spell its own name.
