## 22. Signed, Nobody

The letter arrived on a Tuesday, which meant nothing to anyone but me. Tuesdays had been Edith's day for the eastern circuit — Harrowgate, then the two farmhouses past the mill, then back by dark with her gloves smelling of other people's coal smoke. Forty years of Tuesdays and I still flinch at the postmark.

It was addressed to *The driver who read the letters*, care of my landlady, Mrs. Petch, who has believed since 1949 that I am a retired schoolmistress named Agnes Wray and who considers this explanation entirely sufficient for my habit of burning correspondence in the grate. The envelope was cheap paper, the kind that goes grey at the folds, and the hand was young — a woman's, educated, with that slight overcorrection of someone taught penmanship by a mother rather than a school.

I sat on it for three days.

This is what age does that youth never warns you about: it does not make you braver or more careful, it simply slows you down enough that you can watch yourself being both. At thirty I would have torn it open on the doorstep. At fifty I would have waited until nightfall and read it by lamplight with the curtains drawn, out of pure theatrical instinct. At seventy-eight I made tea first, because my hands ache in cold weather and I have learned that fear and arthritis respond to the same treatment.

Then I opened it.

*Dear Madam,* it began, which was already a lie of politeness, because nobody writes *dear madam* to a stranger unless they are about to ask for something. And they were.

*I am writing a thesis on the Marlowe network. My supervisor says the pamphlet is a forgery, an agitator's invention, that no such ledger could have existed because no such organization could have been so thorough. He says women do not keep records like this. I believe he is wrong, but I cannot prove him wrong without a source.*

*The inquiry transcripts list one witness, unnamed, described only as "a driver attached to the Marlowe circuit." If you are she — if you ever were — I am not asking you to testify. I would not dare. I am asking only this: did the letters exist? Did you read them? Is there anyone left who could tell me I am not chasing a ghost?*

*You will not answer. Everyone tells me you will not answer, that the woman in the transcripts answered nothing for sixty years and is probably dead. But I am posting this anyway, because my grandmother once drove for the movement in Kent, and she used to say there was always one person who kept everything, and that person never got a paragraph in any book.*

*She was right about most things.*

*Yours faithfully,*
*R. Calloway*

I read it twice, then a third time with my spectacles pushed up onto my forehead, which is what I do when I want the words to arrive slightly blurred, the way truth often prefers to travel.

My supervisor says the pamphlet is a forgery.

Well. There's a sentence to grow old on. Sixty years ago Captain Voss published four hundred elegant pages establishing that the requisition of our town had proceeded "without significant dissent," and now a girl at some university is being told the rebuttal never happened either. We have been erased twice over, once by his history and once by doubt of ours, and between the two of them the record has been worn down to a rumor with good posture.

And yet.

And yet the letter exists. Someone remembered the driver. Someone passed down, through whatever channels grandmothers use for the things that matter, the fact that there had been one person who kept everything. Nell would have appreciated that — the survival of the detail over the document. She spent those candlelit months teaching me that typesetting was just memory made stubborn, and here was the proof arriving forty years late on cheap grey paper: the type wears away, but the story of the type gets told at kitchen tables, and kitchen tables outlast archives.

Mrs. Petch knocked to say the chimney man had come, and I put the letter inside *A History of English Gardens*, which is where I keep everything that frightens me, and went down to supervise a man poking at bricks while thinking about whether to answer.

*

I did not answer. Let me be clear about that from the outset, because the rest of what I did might look like answering to a less scrupulous narrator.

I did not answer because answering is a door, and I have spent eighty years learning that doors open from both sides. R. Calloway wanted confirmation; confirmation wants a witness; a witness wants a name; and a name, in my long experience, is the beginning of every trouble I have ever been in. Sebastian Lockridge had been dead since before the war ended in every way that paperwork cares about. Agnes Wray was a fiction with a pension. The girl who had driven Edith Marlowe's little Austin through mud and suspicion, reading other people's lives by lamplight and copying them into a notebook sewn into her coat lining — she had been deliberately, lovingly, painstakingly deleted from the account we risked everything to publish. That deletion had been the price. I had set the price myself. One does not haggle afterward.

But I will confess to something, since this chapter appears to be the last confession available to me: I wrote four drafts of a reply.

The first was dignified and useless — *Madam, you address a stranger.* The second was crueler than I intended and truer than I dared — an entire page on how the letters existed, how I read all nine hundred and forty-one of them, how I can still recite the opening of the one from Martha Quill begging the army clerk to return her confiscated deed because her husband couldn't read and thought the seizure notice was a bill. I burned that one quickly, before it could make me sentimental. The third draft offered to verify the pamphlet's authenticity through a bookseller in Zurich who owed me, which was cowardice dressed as logistics. The fourth said only: *Your grandmother was right.*

I kept the fourth draft for a week in the garden book. Then I burned it too, and felt the particular satisfaction of a person who has resisted temptation and the particular grief of a person who realizes resistance and loss are, at my age, the same gesture.

*

What I did instead was go to the notebook.

You should understand that the notebook has not been far from me since 1917. It has crossed two borders sewn into hat linings and one border in a tin of throat lozenges. It survived a flood in a boarding house in Antwerp and an enthusiastic customs officer in Lisbon who found it, leafed through it, and returned it with the observation that I took detailed notes on laundry prices. (I did. That was the point.) It is water-stained, mouse-nibbled along one corner, and held together now with the same black thread Nell Ashcombe used to sew printed sheets into mail sacks, because when I rebind it, I want my hands to remember.

I had not written in it since 1961. Before that, 1953. The entries had grown sparse as the names grew final — each decade taking its toll, each entry shorter, until lately they were just dates and initials, a private necrology in shorthand only I could read. *E.M., d. 1938. N.A., d. 1972, free.* Nell had died free, in a house full of grandchildren who knew only that Gran had once done time for something unfair, and that was a victory I intend to defend with my life, or what remains of it.

That evening I cleared the table, laid the notebook flat, uncapped the good pen — the one I bought with the first royalty statement that came to a dead man's name, because irony deserves decent instruments — and turned to the back pages where I had always kept the index of myself.

There was very little there. A few false names with their birthdates and habits, annotated like characters in a novel, which is what they were. *S. Lockridge — b. 1893, drives badly in rain, reads everything.* Below them, a gap. A deliberate gap, ruled off with a line, the way surveyors mark land they have decided not to build on.

I looked at that gap for a long time.

Here is the arithmetic of my life, as best I can render it: I gave up my name to save three hundred names, and the exchange rate held. Every one of those people lived, or at least none of them died because of me. The pamphlet went out, the inquiry eventually convened, the parliamentary committee read into the record the testimony of "the driver" and eleven others who surfaced like survivors after a shipwreck. Voss's history lost its authority the way ice loses a pond — gradually, then all at once, though never quite all the way. And I walked out of the story entirely, by choice, into a succession of rented rooms and assumed names, carrying the only complete copy of the truth in a notebook that no one would ever be permitted to find.

Was it worth it? The girl in Chapter One would have said yes instantly and been insufferable about it. The woman in the filing room, holding the missing page with Voss's annotations crawling up the margin like frost, would have said yes through her teeth. I am old enough now to give the honest answer, which is: I don't know, and the not-knowing is the inheritance. Certainty was Voss's disease. He knew exactly what had happened because he had decided it in advance. I kept records precisely because I refused to decide. The notebook was never a weapon. It was a refusal to let anyone else finish the sentence.

But a refusal is not a self. And somewhere around my sixtieth year I noticed that the gap at the back of the notebook had begun to feel less like protection and more like a wound that had healed over wrong.

*

So: Tuesday evening, the lamp turned up, the fire low, the letter from R. Calloway lying on the table like an accusation I had earned.

I did not write to her. I want that on the record, insofar as this record exists, which is to say nowhere, which is to say here.

I dipped the pen, and in the gap — the ruled-off space I had left empty for sixty years, through two wars and a dozen names and the slow erosion of everyone who had known the difference — I wrote one line.

Not in shorthand. Not in code. Not under a false attribution, the way I had signed away my authorship to a typewriter in a lantern-lit cellar while an editor struck my name out at my own insistence. Just the line, in my own hand, the hand that had copied nine hundred and forty-one letters and rebuilt a ledger from memory and forged a dead brother's signature so convincingly that an army paymaster once complimented its neatness.

I wrote my real name.

I won't tell you what it is. That is the whole point, and also — I admit — the old habit dying hard. Some things belong to the page and not the reader; even now, with the fire lit and no one living who could act on the knowledge, I felt the familiar vertigo of exposure and the unfamiliar warmth underneath it, like sun through a window on a winter afternoon. The name sat there on the paper, small and ordinary, belonging to a girl born in a town the army took, who drove for a woman with smoke-smelling gloves, who read every letter, who kept everything.

Nobody will read it. When I die, Mrs. Petch will find a modest estate, a garden book full of pressed flowers and one alarming manuscript, and instructions that the notebook go to a solicitor who will burn it unopened, as instructed, in the presence of another solicitor, because I have planned my exit with the thoroughness of the people I used to outrun. The name will burn with it. The restoration will be total and unwitnessed, which is the only kind I ever trusted.

But it will have been written. There is a difference — I learned this from Nell, setting type by candlelight — between a thing that never existed and a thing that existed once and was destroyed. The first is innocence. The second is evidence. I have chosen, at the end, to be evidence.

I closed the notebook. I put the letter from R. Calloway in the fire, watched it curl, and thought: *your grandmother was right, there was always one person who kept everything, and she kept herself last of all.*

Then, because I am still, at bottom, the girl who belongs everywhere and nowhere, I took out a fresh sheet of paper and began — not a reply, nothing so reckless — but a title page. *A Typewriter for the Lantern,* it said, and beneath it, *by Anonymous,* and beneath that, in pencil, lightly, so it could be erased by any sensible successor:

*A true account. Kept by the driver. Finished at last.*
