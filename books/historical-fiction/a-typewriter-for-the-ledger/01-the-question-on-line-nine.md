## 1. The Question on Line Nine

The harbor smelled of diesel and wet rope and the particular sweetness of rotting fish left too long in the sun, and Gideon Ishikawa thought, as he had thought every day for eleven years, that history was a thing that happened to other people, in other cities, at other times. He carried it now in a leather case chained to his wrist — four pounds of cipher books and sealed orders that weighed more than any luggage he owned — and he walked the length of Pier Nine with the unhurried gait of a man who had never once been late for anything.

The republic was dying around him. He could see it in the small details: the customs house windows boarded against riots that hadn't come yet, the flag over the harbor master's office hanging at half-mast though no one had ordered it lowered, the queue of refugees stretching from the gangway back through the fish market and out of sight, as if the whole city had been poured into a funnel. Overhead, gulls wheeled and screamed, indifferent to the fall of governments.

Gideon showed his papers at the first checkpoint — courier's warrant, diplomatic seal, photograph taken when his hair was still black. The clerk stamped them without looking up. At the second checkpoint, a lieutenant with chapped hands examined the chain on his wrist, tugged it once to test the lock, and waved him through. At the third checkpoint there was no one; the officer assigned to it had evidently decided to be elsewhere, which was its own kind of signature on the republic's death certificate.

* * *

Vera Lindqvist had been counting people since dawn.

She sat at a folding table beneath a canvas awning that flapped in the harbor wind like something trying to escape, with a census ledger open before her and a pencil worn down to a stub. Line one: a farmer with his wife and three children, all of them carrying more than they should. Line two: an old schoolteacher who insisted on being recorded with her full academic title, as if the title might survive where the country would not. Line three through line eight: names she wrote carefully, because names were the last thing anyone would let these people keep.

Her own name was not on any list. That was the arithmetic of the new regime: her people had been declared enemy nationals by decree on Tuesday, and by Wednesday the decrees had reached the docks, and by Thursday Vera — who had taken this census job because it was work, because it was indoors, because it came with a stamp that said she existed — had become a number instead of a counter. They had let her finish the morning's lines. There was a kind of courtesy in that, or a kind of cruelty; she hadn't decided which.

So she sat at the table she was no longer permitted to sit at, recording the last few families before the relief clerk arrived, and she watched the ship that would not carry her take on passengers, and she did her work. That was what Lindqvists did. Her brother Elias used to say it: *We do our work, and we keep our word, and the rest is weather.*

Elias was two years dead. The promise about him was six years old.

She saw Gideon Ishikawa before he saw her — of course she did; she had spent six years looking for him in every crowd, in every railway station, in every photograph of departing ships in the newspapers she refused to stop reading. He was thinner than she remembered, gray at the temples now, walking with that terrible composure of his, the leather case riding his hip like part of his body. He moved down the pier the way he had once moved through her mother's kitchen: politely, precisely, touching nothing, belonging nowhere.

He stopped at the foot of the gangway, where a naval officer checked manifests against faces, and while he waited, his eyes swept the dock — the habit of a careful man — and passed over her, and returned, and stopped.

Six years. He had gone gray. But she knew him the way you know a scar.

"Line nine," she said, when he came near enough, because the relief clerk still hadn't come and the table was hers until someone took it from her. "Name?"

He looked at the ledger, then at her face, and she watched the recognition arrive in stages — first the polite blankness of a man greeting a stranger, then the flicker of arithmetic behind his eyes, then the color leaving his face like water down a drain.

"Miss Lindqvist," he said quietly.

"You remember me."

"I remember you." His voice was level. It was always level. She had forgotten how much she hated that.

"Then sit down," she said, "and give me your name for the record. Everyone boarding goes through the census. That's the regulation this week." She turned the ledger toward him. Nine lines filled above the empty tenth. "It will only take a moment. I'm very good at my work."

"Vera." He said her name the way a man sets down something fragile. "You can't—"

"I can't what?" She kept her pencil moving, writing nothing, the lead whispering across the margin. "I can't ask questions? I ask questions all day. It's my profession. Name, age, nationality, destination." She looked up at him then, and let him see all of it — six years of it, the hospital corridor, the telegram she'd sent to his ministry that came back marked *no such addressee*, the letter she'd written after that which she'd never had the courage to post. "Or shall I skip to the question I've been saving? I've been saving it a long time. It keeps well."

Gideon stood very still. Behind him the queue shifted and murmured; somewhere a whistle blew; the ship's engines turned over with a sound like a sleeping animal changing position. He was a man who believed history happened elsewhere, and here it was, sitting at a folding table under a torn awning, holding a pencil stub, asking for his name.

"My brother," Vera said, "spent his last winter writing to you. Do you know what he called you? Not 'the attaché.' Not 'the courier.' He called you *the man who said yes*. Because you sat in our kitchen in March — do you remember March? — and you drank my mother's coffee and you said yes. You said, *I will see to it. Papers, passage, priority. You have my word.* You gave your word to a dying man, Mr. Ishikawa, and dying men have excellent memories, and so do their sisters."

"Vera—"

"Did you keep it?" Her voice didn't rise. That was the fury in her, that it never rose; it went down instead, deeper, quieter, colder, like water finding the crack in a foundation. "That's all. One question. Yes or no. I've waited six years to ask it, so please don't give me a speech. Just the word. Did you keep your word to my brother?"

The officer at the gangway called the next group forward. Somewhere down the pier a child began to cry, thin and persistent, and no one comforted it because everyone aboard was crying about something. Gideon's hand moved — without his willing it, she was almost sure — to the leather case chained to his wrist, and rested there, and she understood suddenly that whatever answer lived inside him lived in that case too, sealed and weighted and carried past every checkpoint in a dying country.

"The ship leaves at eighteen hundred," he said at last. "There are procedures. There are—"

"Yes or no."

He closed his eyes. When he opened them, he looked older than the gray had made him, and younger than his file photograph, and for one unguarded second she saw the man who had sat in her mother's kitchen promising everything, before the ministries got hold of him and taught him that promises were documents, and documents were classified, and classified things belonged to states rather than to people.

"It isn't a simple question," he said.

"It never is," Vera said. "That's how I know it matters."

She closed the ledger on line nine, unfinished, and folded her hands on top of it, and waited. The wind snapped the canvas overhead. Down the pier, the gangway groaned under the weight of the leaving.

And Gideon Ishikawa, courier of a collapsing republic, keeper of codes, a man who had crossed eleven years and half a continent believing himself invisible to history, opened his mouth to answer her —

— when a hand fell on his shoulder, and a voice behind him said, "Courier Ishikawa. Your seal is requested at the gangway. Immediately."
