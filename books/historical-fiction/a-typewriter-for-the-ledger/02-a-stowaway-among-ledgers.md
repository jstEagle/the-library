## 2. A Stowaway Among Ledgers

The officer at the gangway was young and had the particular politeness of a man reading from a list. He checked Gideon's seal against a wax impression in a leather folio, turned it twice under the lamp, and said, "Thank you, Courier Ishikawa. You may board."

Behind him, down the pier, Vera Lindqvist was being handed back her own papers.

Gideon saw it happen the way one sees weather arrive — distantly, helplessly, already inside it. A clerk in a gray armband slid the census ledger closed. A second man, broader, took her elbow with two fingers, not cruelly, the way one moves furniture that has been left in the wrong room. *Enemy national,* the armband said without saying it. *Barred. Next.* The line behind her did not part around her so much as absorb her, and then she was at the rail of the pier, alone among four hundred people, watching the ship that would not carry her.

She did not cry. That was the thing he remembered afterward — she did not cry, and she did not look away, and when his eyes found hers across forty meters of wet planking she looked back with the patience of someone who had expected exactly this and had asked her question anyway, on line nine, because some questions must be spoken aloud even when their answers are known.

*Did you keep your word to my brother?*

Elias Lindqvist had been twenty-six and coughing blood into a handkerchief he folded with geometric precision, in a hospital ward in a city that no longer existed as it had been named, and he had gripped Gideon's wrist with a strength that surprised them both and said: *My sister. When it comes apart — it will come apart — you will find her passage. Promise me. Not sympathy. Passage.*

And Gideon, who had never broken a seal, never missed a delivery, never once been late for a train, had said, "I promise," because in peacetime promises were light things, paper things, and paper was what he dealt in.

* * *

She came to him at the gangway as the last of the refugees went up. The deck above was loud with engines warming; below, the pier was quiet enough to hear the water slapping the pilings.

"You're boarding," she said.

"Yes."

"With the codes."

"That's my work."

"And your work," she said, "is worth a berth, and mine is worth a line in a ledger, crossed out." She held up her census card. Someone had stamped it. The ink was still wet enough to shine. "Eleven years ago my brother died believing you were a man who kept accounts honestly. I've spent eleven years believing he was a fool to trust a courier, and three days believing otherwise, and this afternoon I stopped believing anything except what's stamped here." She turned the card over, as if the reverse might hold better news. It didn't. "So. Answer me. Not the question I asked before. A simpler one. Are you going to walk up that gangway?"

The engines turned beneath them, patient, indifferent. Somewhere above, a bosun's whistle shrilled the ten-minute call.

"I don't have authority to override a deportation order," Gideon said. "No courier does. If I argued—"

"Then don't argue." She said it flatly, without heat, and that absence of heat frightened him more than shouting would have. "Don't argue, don't petition, don't file. Just tell me whether you are going to walk up that gangway knowing what you promised, or whether you are going to do something about it. Because those are the only two men aboard tonight, and I'd like to know which one is getting on the ship."

He looked at the gangway. He looked at the code case chained to his wrist — fourteen kilograms of cipher books and sealed orders that outweighed, officially, everything else about him.

"Ledger room," he heard himself say. "Deck C, aft. There's a hatch behind the manifest racks. The purser's boys finished inventory an hour ago; nobody goes in until we clear territorial waters. Can you climb a ladder quietly?"

Vera Lindqvist looked at him for a long moment, and something moved through her face like current under ice — not gratitude, not yet; too much anger for that. But she folded the stamped card into her coat pocket, carefully, the way she had folded her hands on the unfinished ledger.

"Elias said you kept accounts honestly," she said. "I'm going to hold you to the honest part."

* * *

The document room smelled of oilcloth and cold iron and, beneath that, the faint vanilla sweetness of aging paper — thousands upon thousands of pages, manifests and cargo declarations and passenger rosters, each sheet a small verdict rendered on some life Vera would never see. She sat wedged between a rack of sealed manifests and the hull plating, knees drawn up, in the dark, listening to the ship breathe.

Above her: footsteps, dozens, then fewer, then the deep vibration of the engines taking up their argument with the sea. The tilt of the deck as the harbor fell away. She had been on ships before — ferries, once, as a girl, holding Elias's hand while he pointed out gulls and named them wrong on purpose to make her laugh. This was different. This was a ship leaving without her name on any list, carrying her the way a smuggled letter carries itself: folded into darkness, addressed to no one.

Her fingers found the rack beside her. Paper edges, brass fittings, lead seals. She could read nothing in the dark, but she didn't need to. She knew what ledgers did. She had been a line in one all day — occupation, nationality, destination — and she knew how a column of figures could become a wall, how a clerk's neat hand could decide that a person was admissible, deportable, nonexistent. Four hundred people had walked past her desk at the dock census. Four hundred lines. She had written *refugee* beside names until the word lost its shape, and now she was the word herself: unregistered, unwritten, hidden among the very documents that had erased her.

It should have felt like defeat. What unsettled her was that it felt like clarity. All day she had processed the leaving; now she was simply another item of contraband, and there was a strange honesty in it. The state had declared her an enemy. Fine. Then let her be an enemy honestly, smuggled in the dark by a man keeping a promise made in peacetime — because the state that licensed that promise had also collapsed, and a promise, she thought in the dark, rocking with the swell, outlives its paperwork.

* * *

Gideon came down after midnight with bread and a tin cup of tea, navigating by shuttered lantern.

"You shouldn't have come," Vera whispered, though she took the bread. "Every visit is a risk."

"Every hour I don't come is worse." He sat on an overturned crate across from her, the lantern between them turning his face into planes and shadow. "Hale boards tomorrow."

"Hale?"

"Inspector Hale. Wireless order came through an hour after we cleared the lights. Every manifest audited. Every courier seal verified. They're hunting a traitor — someone in the ministry's service, they think." He laughed, short and airless. "They've sent a man to audit a dying state's paperwork, and the only dishonest entry on this entire ship is the one I wrote myself. I logged you as sealed cartography cases, Deck C aft. My first falsification in nineteen years of service. Do you know what the strange part is? My hand didn't shake."

Vera ate the bread slowly, watching him. "You could still turn me in. Say you found a stowaway. Your record survives."

"My record," Gideon said, "is a list of things I carried for people who are currently abandoning everyone on that pier." He looked at the racks around them, the thousands of sealed pages. "Do you know what's in these? I helped draft the evacuation priority schedules. Category A: ministries. Category B: assets. Category C—" he gestured vaguely upward, toward the deck where four hundred passengers slept, "—discretionary. I spent six years believing I was guarding history. I was guarding the filing system of a retreat." His voice dropped. "And then I opened a hatch and found the only honest thing aboard hiding between the lies."

"That's a terrible compliment," Vera said.

"It's the only kind I have left." He stood, snuffing the lantern. "Sleep if you can. I'll come at dawn."

His boots faded up the ladder. In the dark, Vera pressed her palm flat against the cold manifests beside her, all those sealed fates, and found, to her surprise, that she was no longer afraid of the dark — only of what would come looking through it.

* * *

The purser's boy found her on the third morning.

She heard him before she saw him — a hesitation of footsteps outside the hatch, the rattle of the handle, and then lamplight falling across the racks, and a face: very young, seventeen perhaps, with a purser's badge polished too bright and the expression of a boy who has rehearsed this moment and finds reality louder than rehearsal.

"You're not cartography cases," he whispered.

"No," Vera said.

"You're the stowaway. Everyone's saying Hale will search every compartment by Friday." He swallowed. "Courier Ishikawa signed for these racks. If Hale opens them and finds you—"

"He'll find me," Vera said. "Not him. I hid. He doesn't know I'm here." She lied smoothly, without deciding to, and hated how easily it came. "What's your name?"

"Tomas." He blinked. "Why?"

"Because, Tomas, you have a choice, and I'd rather you make it knowing you have one. Shut that hatch and you never saw anything. Open your mouth and you condemn both of us — and Inspector Hale will want to know why a purser's assistant waited three days to report it."

The boy stood in the doorway a long moment, lamplight trembling with his hand. Then he pulled the hatch shut, and through the iron she heard him say, half to himself, "Third shelf, fore side, there's a gap where the water-damaged manifests were pulped. It's deeper than it looks."

Footsteps retreated. Vera sat in the dark, heart hammering, understanding that she had just acquired a conspirator she hadn't asked for, in a conspiracy she couldn't control — and that somewhere above them, an inspector was already working his way down through the ship, deck by deck, seal by seal, toward the one compartment where the republic's paperwork had learned to lie.
