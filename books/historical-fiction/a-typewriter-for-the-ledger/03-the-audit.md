## 3. The Audit

The wireless came in on the third day out, when the sea had turned the color of unpolished pewter and the ship had begun to forget it was carrying anything but weather.

Gideon was in the document room when the purser's boy knocked twice — their signal now, invented in a moment of panic that had since hardened into ritual — and he climbed the ladder to the radio office to find the captain holding a flimsy yellow slip as though it were something dead that might still bite.

"Inspector Hale," the captain said. "Boarding by launch off the Azores. Wireless order from the Ministry. He's to audit every manifest, every courier seal, every seal's wax and thread." The captain did not look at Gideon when he said *courier seal*, which was its own kind of message. "There's a rumor of a traitor aboard."

"A rumor," Gideon said. His voice sounded like someone else's — a clerk's voice, a voice for filing things.

"They say a man who signed papers that shouldn't have been signed." The captain folded the slip along its crease, once, precisely, the way Gideon himself might have. "You understand I have no choice."

"Of course," Gideon said. "Neither do I."

He went back down the ladder slowly, one rung at a time, and stood outside the document room door for a full minute before opening it, because once he opened it he would have to speak, and everything he could possibly say was either a lie or an ending.

Vera was sitting on an upturned crate between two shelves of manifests, mending a seam of her coat by the light of a shaded lamp. She looked up. She had grown thin in three days; the room had been eating her by inches, feeding her biscuits and water through the purser's boy and taking her daylight in exchange. But her eyes were steady, and they were already reading him, the way they always read him, before his mouth caught up.

"What is it," she said. Not a question. She never wasted question marks on things she already knew.

"There's an inspector coming aboard," Gideon said. "Hale. He audits seals. He'll reach this room within a day, maybe less."

Vera set down the needle. She threaded it back through the wool with a small, deliberate violence, as if closing a wound. "Then you should decide what you're going to tell him."

"I've decided nothing."

"You've decided everything," she said quietly. "You decided it the night you pulled me off that gangway. Everything since has just been the interest accumulating on it." She stood. In the low room, among the gray boxes and the wax-sealed envelopes that held other people's fates filed alphabetically, she seemed to Gideon suddenly enormous — larger than the room, larger than the ship, the way grief is larger than the body that carries it. "Gideon. Listen to me. When he comes, I will walk out of this room myself. I will say I stowed away, that I tricked you, that you never knew. You are a courier with sealed orders. He cannot hang you for what a woman did behind your back."

"He can court-martial me for negligence."

"Negligence is survivable. Treason is not." She crossed the narrow space and took his hands, and her fingers were cold from four days without sun. "I watched them take my brother because no one would stand in front of him and say *he is mine to answer for*. I am not going to watch it happen to you."

"You promised him passage," Gideon said. "Your brother. And I promised it too. If I give you to Hale, then both our words die, and only the Ministry's survive. And the Ministry—" He stopped, because he had never finished a sentence like that aloud, and finishing it felt like stepping off a charted map into open ink.

"The Ministry is sinking," Vera said. "You're standing on it."

* * *

Inspector Hale came up the rope ladder at dawn in a gray coat too heavy for the latitude, carrying a leather case and a face like a closed ledger. He was younger than Gideon expected — forty, perhaps, with the smooth unhurried hands of a man who had never once needed to hurry and never once been wrong. He shook the captain's hand, accepted coffee he did not drink, and asked to see the courier seals first.

"First?" the purser murmured to Gideon in the corridor. "Not last?"

"Hale doesn't work deck by deck," Gideon said. "He works by weight. He goes where the paper is heaviest."

And so it began. Gideon stood witness — that was the official phrase, *the courier stands witness* — while Hale broke seal after seal with a small bone knife, checked thread against wax, wax against register, register against manifest, manifest against the great typed truth of the Ministry's ledgers. He worked in silence except for the small sounds of his craft: the crack of wax, the whisper of onionskin, the scratch of his own pencil, which never hesitated. All day the ship rocked beneath them and Hale did not appear to notice, as if he had made some private arrangement with the sea.

By evening they had reached B deck. By midnight, the purser came down the ladder to the document room with his face white as a manifest, and said, "Tomorrow morning. He's saved the document room for tomorrow morning. He wants the courier present at first light."

Vera heard it from her crate. She did not sleep. Gideon sat across from her with his code case on his knees — the black steel box, chained by regulation to his wrist in port and to his conscience everywhere else — and for a long time neither of them spoke, because the room was full of paper and paper is very good at listening.

"I falsified an entry today," Gideon said finally.

Vera lifted her head.

"In the transit register. Your presence — I logged the document room as sealed, occupant none, from departure." His hands were shaking. He looked at them with something close to fascination, the way a man looks at a machine of his own manufacture failing for the first time. "Twenty-two years keeping books. My father kept books before me. We Ishikawas do not falsify entries. It is the one commandment we have. The numbers are the last thing left when everything else rots."

"And how did it feel?"

"Like writing on water." He laughed, a small broken sound. "No. That's not true. It felt like breathing after holding my breath my whole life. That's the terrible part, Vera. That's the part I can't put back in the box."

She reached over and laid her palm flat on the code case, on the black steel above whatever slept inside. "What's in here? Truly. You've never said."

"Ciphers. Fleet movements. Evacuation accounts — which ministries paid which neutral banks for which seats on which ships." He said it flatly, like reading a manifest, and watched her understand it. "This case bought passage for men who abandoned your brother's name at the border. This case is the cargo of everything we're running from."

"Then you know what you have to do," Vera said, "and you're waiting for me to say it, so it will be my sin and not yours." Her voice did not rise. It never rose. It simply grew denser, like cold air. "I won't say it. Whatever you do, you'll do it as a free man or not at all."

* * *

Hale came at first light exactly, when the portholes of B deck were turning from black to the color of tarnished silver. He entered the document room with the purser, two armed mates, and his bone knife already in hand, and he walked the aisle of shelves slowly, trailing one gloved finger along the spines of the sealed boxes like a blind man reading.

He stopped at the crate.

It was a good hiding place. It had been a good hiding place for four days. But Hale did not search the way other men searched, casting about; he searched the way water searches, finding without looking the one low place in the floor. He set down his case. He knelt. He lifted the tarpaulin.

For a moment nobody moved. Vera sat blinking in the sudden lamplight, a woman emerging from four days of darkness into the faces of strangers, and Gideon thought, absurdly: *she looks like a census line come to life.* Line nine, given flesh.

"Name," Hale said.

"Lindqvist. Vera Lindqvist." Her voice was rough from disuse but level. "Enemy national, according to your ministry. Stowaway, according to yours."

"She is lying about one part," Gideon said.

Every head turned. He heard his own heartbeat, huge and slow, like the engines below.

"She didn't trick me," he said. "I knew her at the dock. She asked me about a promise I made to her dying brother — safe passage, sworn years ago, in peacetime, when such promises were still possible. I hid her. I falsified the transit register. The fault is mine entire, and I will sign any statement saying so."

"Gideon—" Vera began.

"No." He turned to her, and the whole room saw it happen, the shattering, though it made no sound. "You told me I'd do it free or not at all. So. Not at all." He raised his wrist. The chain of the code case glinted. "Inspector. Before you write anything — there is a matter of weight in this room heavier than any manifest, and I'd rather it burned than bargained."

Hale's eyes narrowed. "Courier, those seals—"

"These codes," Gideon said, working the key, "can purchase silence. They can purchase tribunals that go quietly. They can purchase a stowaway's disappearance at sea and a courier's clean record. I know their price. I helped carry it." The case clicked open. Inside lay the cipher books, the fleet keys, the evacuation accounts — the whole paper skeleton of a dying state, neat as scripture. He carried the case to the small iron stove in the corner, the one the clerks used in winter, and fed the pages into it one by one while the officers stood frozen, while Vera pressed her hand to her mouth, while the fire took the ciphers and turned the fleet's secrets into light.

The last page curled and went. The stove ticked. Somewhere above them, the sea went on being enormous and indifferent.

"Burn the evidence and confess in the same breath," Hale said at last, softly, almost with wonder. "That is either treason or the most honest act committed under this flag in a year. I genuinely don't know which." He took out his pencil. For the first time since boarding, it hesitated above the page. "Captain — convene the tribunal. Wardroom. Both of them. Let them argue it."

He looked up at Gideon, and something moved behind the closed-ledger face — not warmth, perhaps, but its distant cousin.

"You'll want to make it good," Hale said. "A promise outlives its state, does it? Prove it. No one has ever managed to convince a tribunal of anything but fear."

* * *

They were marched up through the waking ship, past sailors who pretended not to look and looked anyway, past the purser's boy who stood aside and, in passing, pressed something into Vera's cold hand — a biscuit, saved, wrapped in a page torn from a ledger. On the paper, in a boy's careful pencil, someone had written: *Line nine.*

The wardroom door opened ahead of them. Beyond it: the long table, the officers, the flag furled in the corner like a sleeping animal, and morning light coming hard across the water, showing them, for the first time in days, each other's faces clearly.

Vera squeezed Gideon's hand once, and let go.
