## 1. The Weight of Small Gears

The workshop on Tallow Row smelled of oil and brass shavings and, in winter, of the coal stove that Master Fenwick never allowed to burn past a dull orange, because heat made metal honest and honesty, he said, was a luxury a clockmaker could not always afford. Elodie Whitlock had been there seven years. She knew the room by its sounds before she knew it by its light: the tick of forty-odd movements hanging in their cases like patient hearts, the whisper of the lathe, the small dry cough of her master clearing his throat when something had gone wrong that he intended to blame on her.

She liked best the hour before dawn, when she arrived to find the day's work already laid out — a carriage clock with a broken pallet, a regulator whose pendulum had grown lazy, an anniversary piece someone's grandmother wanted to outlive them all — and no one watching. In that hour she was not the girl who swept the floor or carried the parcels or endured Fenwick's sighs. She was simply hands. Hands and eyes and the fine tremor of attention that passed through her fingertips into whatever mechanism lay open beneath them.

That morning it was a fusee chain from a wall clock belonging to a solicitor on Cordwell Street, and the chain had snapped at its thinnest link, as chains did, and the solicitor had demanded it be made whole again without any evidence of repair. No evidence. That was the phrase Fenwick used, tapping the order slip twice, as though the words were coins he was counting out.

Elodie understood what the solicitor actually wanted, which was not wholeness but continuity. He wanted to believe his clock had never faltered. He wanted the fiction of an object that had run faithfully since his grandfather's day, unbroken, unquestioned. So she sat at the bench with a loupe screwed into her eye socket and drew wire finer than a grass stem, coiling it, hardening it, cutting and filing until a new link existed that could not be told from its fifty brothers. When it was done she held the completed chain up to the gaslight and let it pour from one hand to the other like something living, and felt the particular satisfaction that was hers alone: the satisfaction of being nowhere in the thing she had made.

No one would know. No one ever knew. Her name appeared on no ticket, no invoice, no brass plate. Fenwick took the credit; the clock took the glory; the solicitor took home his seamless inheritance. And Elodie took the link, invisible, load-bearing, essential, and went on to sweep the floor.

*History belongs to other people*, her mother used to say, back when there was still a mother to say things. *It belongs to the ones who sign their names. The rest of us just keep the hours.* Elodie had believed this the way one believes in weather — not happily, but completely, and without imagining it might be otherwise. She repaired time for people who owned it. That was all.

* * *

Fenwick came down at half past nine, smelling of toast and grievance, carrying a letter in one hand the way another man might carry a dead rat.

"Verenchal," he said.

She looked up from the bench. "Where?"

"Up in the mountains. Past Marrow Gap, past the last place anyone sensible would put a town." He dropped the letter on her bench, deliberately, she thought, atop the clean rag she'd laid out. "The observatory up there has a master clock. Regulator of the first order, made by Vasseur — you'll have heard the name, even if you pretend otherwise."

She hadn't pretended otherwise; she'd simply never dared say the name aloud in this shop, where Fenwick spoke of Vasseur the way devout men speak of saints they resent. Marguerite Vasseur's father, the great Horace Vasseur, had built regulators for observatories across three countries before his death. His escapements were studied. His journals were quoted. Men wrote papers about his pallet shapes.

"The master clock's stalled," Fenwick went on. "Stopped dead, they say, three days ago, and nobody up there can coax it. The observatory director writes" — he squinted at the letter — "*that the instrument is of singular importance to the institution and to the town's forthcoming commemoration*." He sniffed. "Commemoration. A hundred years since the founding, apparently. They mean to have ceremonies."

"And you're sending me."

"I'm sending the person I can spare." He said it without cruelty, which was somehow worse than cruelty would have been; cruelty would at least have acknowledged she was worth the effort of inflicting. "You've stripped and rebuilt the Vasseur study movement in the front case twice. You know his hand better than anyone under this roof. You'll go up, you'll find what's seized, you'll free it or replace it, and you'll come back when the passes allow."

"When the passes allow," she repeated. It was nearly December.

"Winter comes early up there and stays late. You may be some weeks." He almost smiled. "Think of it as an education. You'll see how the other half keeps time — men with telescopes, timing stars instead of trains." He turned toward the stairs, then paused, and added, in the tone he reserved for instructions he expected her to ignore: "Do nothing clever, Elodie. Repair the clock. Touch nothing else. Sign nothing. You are my hands, not my name. Is that understood?"

It was understood. It was the only thing about her life that had ever been fully understood.

* * *

The train went as far as Marrow Gap, and after Marrow Gap there was only the coach: a closed carriage drawn by two horses whose breath came out like forge smoke, climbing switchbacks through country that grew more austere with every mile. The pines gave way to bare grey timber, the timber to scree, the scree to a sky so wide and so pale it seemed to press down on the road like glass. Elodie sat inside with her tool chest strapped beside her and watched her breath fog the little window, and tried to feel nothing in particular about leaving the city for the first time in nine years.

She was not, she told herself, being exiled. She was being sent. There was a difference, and the difference was that she would return. The city would be exactly as she left it: Fenwick grumbling over his ledgers, the customers complaining about cost, the clocks ticking their patient thousands upon thousands of beats, none of them aware of her, all of them dependent on her. She had made her peace with invisibility long ago. She had even, if she were honest, made a small religion of it. To be unseen was to be safe. To be safe was to be free, in the narrow way a fish is free inside its bowl.

Still. When the driver stopped at dusk to water the horses and stood stamping his boots in the blue snow-light, he offered her a flask and a remark.

"Going up to Verenchal, then? Observatory business?" He said the word *observatory* with a certain careful weight, the way one says the name of a rich uncle one does not entirely trust.

"Their clock's stopped," Elodie said.

"Hah." He drank, wiped his mouth. "Whole town's in a state over it, I hear. Big doings come New Year's Eve. Hundred years since old Ambrose Hale's grandfather and the others planted the flag, so to speak. They're writing a book about themselves." He grinned, showing few teeth. "Towns that write books about themselves generally leave things out. But don't mind me. I just drive."

He climbed back onto the box, and the coach lurched onward into the dark, and Elodie sat with those words rattling around among her gears. *Leave things out.* She thought of the solicitor's chain, the missing link remade so perfectly that the break itself was forgotten. Towns, perhaps, were mechanisms too. Perhaps somewhere in Verenchal there was a part that had snapped, and someone had paid to have it made whole again without evidence.

But that was fancy, and she was tired, and the road was climbing into clouds now, actual clouds, so that the coach seemed to move through wet wool lit dimly from within. She pulled her coat tighter and slept badly against the window, dreaming of chains that poured like water between hands that belonged to no one.

* * *

She woke to silence, and to cold, and to the coach standing still.

Not stopped — arrived. Through the frosted glass she could see lamps, a street descending in crooked tiers, buildings hunched against the mountainside like animals facing away from the wind. Above and behind the town, black against a sky salted with stars, stood a dome. The observatory. Even in darkness she could feel the clock up there, or rather feel its absence, the way one feels a missing tooth: the whole valley seemed to hang slightly out of joint, waiting for a beat that did not come.

The driver handed down her chest as though it were made of lead and grief combined. "Post office takes lodgers," he said. "Widow runs it. Quill, her name is. Reads everything, mind, but she won't cheat you." He tipped his hat. "Good luck with the clock. Mind the ice on the upper road."

And then he was gone, the sound of wheels fading down the valley road, taking with him the last easy route back to the world Elodie understood.

She stood a moment in the street with her breath streaming and her fingers going numb inside her gloves, and looked up at the dome, and felt — unexpectedly, unwelcomely — something that was not dread. Curiosity, maybe. Or the first faint pressure of a question forming, the way a mainspring coils tighter and tighter inside its barrel, storing force against some future release.

The post office windows glowed amber down the street. Behind their frost-ferned glass, she could see a figure moving — a broad, unhurried silhouette sorting letters by lamplight, each envelope held up and examined a moment longer than necessary, as if every one of them contained something worth knowing.

Elodie lifted her tool chest and walked toward the light.
