## 2. Bridge Out at Verenchal

The flood announced itself before Elodie ever saw it — a sound like a great loom working somewhere below the road, a low rhythmic thunder that she mistook at first for her own heart, hammering with the indignity of a woman traveling alone through country she did not know.

The carriage had climbed since dawn out of the river valley and up into the pines, the wheels complaining on frozen ruts, and now the trees opened onto a bend of road above the Veren gorge, where the water ran brown and swollen between walls of granite. The driver, a taciturn man named Pell who had spoken perhaps forty words in two days, reined in and stood up on his box to look ahead.

"Well," he said.

The bridge was still there. That was the first thing Elodie noticed, and the last thing that went right. It stood where the map promised it would stand, a covered span of gray timber crossing the gorge on stone piers, its roof furred with frost. But the river beneath it had risen to within an arm's reach of the deck, and it moved with a muscular, deliberate violence, carrying whole pine trunks sideways, spinning them like matchsticks when they struck the piers. The water was eating the bridge's foundations the way a child eats the last of a loaf — patiently, without malice, without hurry.

"It held this morning," Pell said. "It won't hold tonight."

"Then we should cross *now*."

He looked back at her over his shoulder, and there was something in his face that was not quite pity and not quite amusement. "Ma'am, I've driven this road eleven years. I don't cross a bridge that's chewing its own legs." He turned the carriage off the main track onto a side path that wound down toward a cluster of rooftops below. "We'll put you up in Verenchal till the water drops. Few days. Maybe a week."

Elodie sat very straight on the cold bench and thought about Master Fenwick's workshop in the city: the smell of oil and brass, the little stove that ticked as it cooled, her own bench by the north window where no one ever looked at her and nothing was ever expected of her except precision. She had been gone three days. The master clock at the observatory above Verenchal had stalled in November, and the town's letters — she knew because she had packed them herself — spoke of it as though the sun itself had stuttered. A town that lived by its observatory could not abide a clock that lied about time.

She had been chosen precisely because she was nobody. Master Fenwick could not be spared; his journeyman was sick; and so they had sent the apprentice who repaired escapements in silence and signed nothing, whose name appeared nowhere on the shop's invoices. *Invisibility,* Master Fenwick liked to say, tapping his own temple, *is a tool like any other. Keep yours sharp.* She had kept it sharp for nine years. She was twenty-six and she was excellent and she was nobody, and she had believed until this moment that those were compatible things.

The carriage descended into Verenchal.

* * *

It was a town built in tiers, like a cake no one had iced. The lower street followed the flooded river and was given over to mills and storehouses; the middle street held the shops and the church and a hotel with a sign reading THE VEREN HOUSE in gold leaf gone dull; and above everything, on a shoulder of bare rock, stood the observatory, its dome catching the last copper light of the afternoon so that it seemed to burn quietly against the gray sky. Between the tiers, stair-streets zigzagged up through the houses, each one named for some founding virtue — Providence Steps, Industry Steps, Concord Steps. Even the stairs were ambitious here.

Pell deposited her at the Veren House long enough for her to discover that the hotel's four rooms were already taken by surveyors come to inspect the bridge, and then delivered her, with the air of a man concluding a business arrangement, to a narrow house on Quill Lane hard by the post office.

"She takes lodgers," he said. "Ada Quill. Postmistress. Tell her Pell sent you and I'll settle accounts by return post."

The woman who opened the door was sixty-odd and built like a sparrow, all sharp bone and quick eye, with steel-gray hair pinned in a coil so severe it might have been load-bearing. She looked Elodie over from boot to bonnet with the unhurried thoroughness of a customs officer.

"Pell sent you."

"Yes, ma'am. Elodie Whitlock. Apprentice clockmaker, come to see to the observatory clock."

Something flickered behind Mrs. Quill's eyes — not hostility, exactly. More like a card being noted and tucked away. "Clockmaker," she repeated. "Well. The town will have opinions about you before you've been here a week, Miss Whitlock. You may as well know it going in. Room's upstairs, first door. Two dollars the week, meals included, and the privy is around back where the frost gets in, so mind your dignity after dark."

The room was small and clean and smelled of cedar and old paper. Through its single window Elodie could see, past the jumbled roofs, the observatory dome going dark as the light failed. Somewhere in that building, a machine she was meant to heal lay stopped, its hands frozen at whatever hour it had chosen to die.

She unpacked her tools with the ritual care of nine years' habit — each file in its slot, each jewel-bearing in its cotton bed — and told herself that a week in a strange town was merely a week, and that a week was made of hours, and hours were her native country.

* * *

Supper was taken at Mrs. Quill's table along with two other lodgers: a young man with ink-stained fingers who taught at the schoolhouse and answered to the name of Ostley, and a silent widow who took her plate to her room without a word. Mr. Ostley — Bram, though no one called him that yet — glanced at Elodie once, twice, and on the third glance said:

"You're the one come for the clock."

"I am."

"Hm." He buttered his bread with the concentration of a man defusing something. "They'll tell you the clock is old and tired. It isn't. Old clocks are honest about their age. That one—" He stopped himself, jaw tightening, and addressed the remainder of his supper instead.

Mrs. Quill poured tea without looking at either of them. "Mr. Ostley has opinions," she said mildly, "that the school board found excessive. Hence his present abundance of free time."

"That is not—" He set down the knife. "I asked why the centennial history doesn't mention anyone who lived here before 1850. That was the whole of my offense, Miss Whitlock. One question, asked in a faculty meeting, in a reasonable tone of voice." He laughed, but it was a thin sound. "Judge Hale was present. Judge Hale does not enjoy questions."

"Then he'd hate me," said Mrs. Quill placidly. "Questions are my trade. Every letter that comes through this post office passes under my stamp, and every letter tells you something the writer doesn't know he's telling." She looked at Elodie over the rim of her cup. "You'll want to write your workshop, tell them you're stranded. I can post it tomorrow if the roads hold. Or hold it. Either way, I'll read it first. Town charter gives me the right, and besides—" the sparrow-sharp eyes glinted— "it's how I keep the town honest. Or as near to honest as it cares to get."

Elodie felt the peculiar sensation of being seen through, like glass, like a watch case. In the city her letters passed through a hundred indifferent hands. Here, they would pass through one attentive pair of eyes.

"I'll write it tonight, then," she said, "if it's to be read anyway. No sense wasting your evening."

That earned her the ghost of a smile. "You may do," said Mrs. Quill. "Most folk take three days to work up to honesty. You might manage it in one."

* * *

The letter, when she wrote it by lamplight, came out shorter than she intended. *Stranded at Verenchal by the flood. Bridge down. Will proceed to observatory when possible. Send no parts yet.* She considered adding more and found she had nothing to add. Nine years in one workshop and this was the whole of what she had to report: delayed, proceeding, send nothing.

She fell asleep listening to the river, which even at this distance filled the town with its grinding, patient roar, and dreamed of gears the size of millwheels turning underwater.

* * *

Morning revealed Verenchal under a skin of ice-fog, and revealed something else besides: the way a small town looks at a stranger. It was not unfriendly. That was the unsettling part. The baker wished her good morning and meant it, and also watched her the whole while, weighing her. Two women outside the church paused their conversation as she passed — not abruptly, not guiltily, just a fractional hitch, like a clock losing half a beat — and resumed it only when she had gone by. At the foot of Observatory Steps, an old man sweeping snow straightened and touched his hat and said, "You'll be the clock lady," in a voice that made it sound less like a question than a filing.

"The clock lady," Elodie agreed.

"Aye. Been thirty years since anyone come up those steps to mend her." He went back to his sweeping. Then, without looking up: "Mind how you go. She's particular."

*Who* was particular — the clock or whoever waited at the top — he did not say, and Elodie did not ask. Invisibility, she reminded herself, climbing the frosted steps with her tool case knocking at her hip, is a tool. Keep it sharp. But the steps were steep and the fog was thick and the town watched her go with forty pairs of polite, unreadable eyes, and she felt the sharpness of her invisibility dulled by distance, worn thin, like a pivot turned too often in its jewel.

At the observatory door she paused and looked back. Below her, Verenchal arranged itself in its tiers, smoke rising from a hundred chimneys, the river roaring beyond it, the drowned bridge standing in the flood like a coffin lid nailed down over running water. It was, she thought, a handsome town. It was a town that had arranged itself very carefully, the way a shopkeeper arranges a window — everything displayed at its best angle, and nothing, anywhere, left open to view.

She noticed, as she turned to knock, that the observatory's cornerstone bore a date: 1849. And she noticed, filed away in the corner of her mind where she kept such things, that the old man with his broom had said *thirty years* — and that the cornerstone, by her arithmetic, was fifty years old, and that no one, apparently, mended anything here between the one date and the other.

The lock was stiff. She got out her picks, knelt in the snow, and let herself into the dark of the observatory, where the master clock hung silent as a stopped heart, waiting to tell her what it knew.
