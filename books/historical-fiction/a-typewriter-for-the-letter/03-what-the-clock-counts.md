## 3. What the Clock Counts

The master clock of the Verenchal Observatory stood in a stone chamber beneath the telescope floor, and it was, Elodie admitted on her first morning alone with it, the finest thing she had ever been permitted to touch.

It rose from the flagstones like a grandfather clock grown ambitious — nine feet of walnut case, brass-faced, its pendulum swinging with a patience that made her own heartbeat feel hurried. The escapement was a deadbeat design, rare enough that she had only ever seen one in engravings. The weights hung on cables worn to a silver shine. Somewhere in its chest, something had begun to hesitate: a tick that caught, then hurried, as though the clock were an old man remembering a word he'd misplaced.

She worked by lamplight because the chamber's single window faced north and gave nothing back in December. Her tools lay on a cloth in strict order, each in its place the way Master Fenwick had taught her — *a clockmaker's bench is a mirror, girl; if your mind is scattered, so is your layout.* She had been invisible in his workshop for eleven years. Invisible was not nothing. Invisible meant trusted. It meant that when the letter came from Verenchal asking for someone competent and discreet, Fenwick had looked straight through her at the wall and said, "Send Whitlock," as if dispatching a parcel.

She had told herself, on the long carriage ride, that this was what she wanted. To be sent for, used well, and forgotten. History belonged to other people — to judges and founders and men whose names went on plaques. Hers belonged to gears.

The hesitation, she found by noon, lived in the fourth wheel. A tooth had chipped, and worse, the arbor's bearing had been packed — inexplicably — with a waxy compound that had hardened over decades into something like amber. She scraped it free with a boxwood stick, working in slow strokes, and the wheel turned true again, and the clock above her exhaled into a rhythm so clean she could feel it in her teeth.

That should have been the day's whole story. But the wax bothered her.

Clockmakers did not pack bearings with wax. Wax attracted dust, held grit, slowed the train. Whoever had done it had wanted the wheel to run badly — wanted it just enough to make the clock stumble every few hours without ever quite stopping. And they had wanted it to stay that way for a very long time.

Elodie sat back on her stool and looked up into the case's dark interior, where the lamplight reached only partway and the rest was a brown-black country of shadows.

*Just enough to make it stumble.*

She thought of Ada Quill's kitchen, two nights past, where the postmistress had poured tea thick as syrup and talked around every subject in town the way water talks around stones. *Founding families,* Ada had said. *Hale, Merrick, Ashcom. Fifty years next year, they'll tell you, since the first survey.* And then she had looked at Elodie with those quick gray eyes and said nothing further, which from Ada Quill was a shout.

There was no reason — none — for a repairwoman to go looking inside a case beyond the fault she'd been paid to find. Fenwick would have said so plainly. *Fix the fault, Whitlock. Curiosity is how clocks get broken.*

But the wax had been deliberate. Deliberateness was a kind of signature, and signatures made her itch.

* * *

She came back after supper, when the observatory's night assistant had gone home to his wife and his supper and his ordinary life, and let herself into the chamber with the key the director had pressed upon her — *anything you need, Miss Whitlock, anything at all* — a generosity that now sat differently in her stomach.

The case opened at the back on two hidden pins, a trick as old as the trade. She had assumed the space behind them was empty ballast.

It was not empty.

A tin box, soldered shut, sat wedged between the weight channels, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark. It had been placed there deliberately, braced against the case's frame so the pendulum's swing would never touch it. Someone had built this hiding place the way she herself built things — carefully, with respect for the mechanism around it.

Her hands were steady. That surprised her. Inside, she felt the particular vertigo of standing at the top of a stair in the dark, knowing there was one more step than her foot expected.

She set the tin on the bench and worked the solder loose with a hot knife, breathing the smell of old metal and older paper. Inside, wrapped again in linen, lay a ledger — small, cheaply bound, the sort sold in any general store — and beneath it a folded sheet of instructions in a hand she did not recognize:

*To whoever services this machine. The maker Vasseur bound me to secrecy in his lifetime. He is dead these twenty years. I have kept his secret longer than my conscience can bear. These pages are true. I have checked them against the county records myself. Let them come to light when they must; I am too old a coward to be the one to carry them into daylight.*

No name. Just a date: 1887.

Elodie opened the ledger.

The first pages were ordinary — accounts, really. Payments recorded in a neat commercial hand, dated 1849: sums paid *to sundry persons of Kettle Creek, in consideration of quit-claim and removal.* Names followed. Whole families of them — Ostrander, Pell, Two-Rivers, Vance, Kettle itself — each with a figure beside it, most pitifully small, some marked *refused*, one marked simply *gone under* with a date in November of that year.

She knew the official story. Everyone in Verenchal could recite it in their sleep; Ada had recited it to her twice with the flatness of a woman reading a label. In 1849, the founding families — Hale, Merrick, Ashcom — had surveyed an empty valley and established the town on honest land. Empty. The word was even on the bronze plaque outside the courthouse, she'd walked past it her first morning: *HERE THE FIRST SETTLERS FOUND NO DWELLING BEFORE THEM.*

The ledger found dwellings before them. Dozens. A mill. A burying ground.

And then, near the back, in a different ink, a different hand — smaller, angrier — a page headed simply *WHAT WAS DONE:* three paragraphs in cramped script describing the spring flood of '49, how the upper settlement's dam had failed in a manner *not accidental*, how the water had taken the lower houses of Kettle Creek and drowned four souls, and how the survivors had been bought, threatened, or driven out within the year. The last line read: *They paid me to build the clock and keep my mouth shut about what I saw from the hill. I built the clock. Here is my mouth.*

Vasseur. Marguerite's father — though Elodie didn't know that yet; she knew only that the observatory's original clockmaker had been some foreigner whose name appeared on a small plate inside the movement, half polished away.

She closed the ledger and sat for a long time while the repaired clock counted above her, each second falling into the stone room like a coin into a well.

This was not her business. That was the whole architecture of her life: fix the fault, wind the weights, take the wage, leave no fingerprints. Other people's history was other people's weight to carry. If she carried this ledger to the director, she'd be meddling. If she carried it to Judge Hale — and Hale's name appeared in these pages more than once, a young man's signature witnessing the quit-claims — she'd be rewarded, perhaps, and complicit, certainly.

If she said nothing at all, she could finish the job and go home in the spring and be invisible again.

The lamp guttered. Outside, snow ticked against the high window. The clock ticked back, steady now, honest now, counting seconds over a buried lie the way it had for fifty years — faithfully, and without comment.

*Let them come to light when they must.*

She thought about that phrase. *When they must.* Not *when convenient*. Not *when safe*. When they *must* — as though truth were a debt accruing interest, and the ledger itself a note left in trust.

Elodie wrapped the pages back in their linen, but she did not reseal the tin. Instead she took the ledger to the bench, copied its headings onto a card in her own careful hand — names, dates, sums — and slid the copy into her tool apron, where it lay flat against her ribs like a second heartbeat. Then she returned the original to its hiding place among the weights, because taking it felt like theft, and leaving it felt like evidence, and she could not yet decide which she feared more.

She banked the lamp. At the door she paused and looked back at the great case, pendulum swinging, patient as ever.

"You knew," she said aloud, and heard how foolish it sounded, and said it anyway. "You've been counting all along."

* * *

Snow had come down hard while she worked, and the walk down to Ada Quill's took twice its usual time, boots punching through fresh drifts, lamplight from the houses smearing yellow in the flurry. Verenchal at night was a town of shuttered windows and woodsmoke, snug and silent and utterly certain of itself.

Ada was awake, of course — Elodie suspected the woman slept in snatches between mail coaches, like a lighthouse keeper — and had tea already steeping, as though she'd smelled intent on the wind.

"You were at the observatory late," Ada said, pouring.

"The clock needed me."

"Mm." Ada set down the pot and studied her over the rim of her cup, and Elodie understood that the postmistress read faces the way she read letters — every seal, every hesitation, everything written between the lines. "You've found something."

It was not a question, and lying to Ada Quill suddenly seemed as pointless as lying to a scale. Elodie put her hand over her apron pocket, over the copied card, and felt the paper's edge through the cloth.

"Mrs. Quill," she said slowly, "what do you know about a place called Kettle Creek?"

The teacup stopped halfway to Ada's mouth.

For a moment the kitchen was very quiet — only the stove ticking, the wind testing the shutters, the snow coming down on a town that believed its own plaque. Then Ada set the cup down with a small deliberate click, drew a chair close to the fire, and patted its seat.

"Sit down, Miss Whitlock," she said, "and mind you get comfortable. Because what I know would fill a book, and it seems to me you may be the one finally sent to write it."
