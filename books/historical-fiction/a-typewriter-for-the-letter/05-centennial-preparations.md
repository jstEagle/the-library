## 5. Centennial Preparations

The frost came down from the mountains the last week of November and settled over Verenchal like a judgment nobody had asked for. Elodie felt it first in her hands — the fine tremor that cold put into a clockmaker's fingers, the enemy of every delicate adjustment — and then in the town itself, which seemed to draw inward the way a mechanism does when a mainspring is run nearly to its end.

She had been at the observatory eleven days. The master clock was coming back to life by degrees: the escapement freed of its gummed oil, the pendulum's suspension re-seated, the striking train cleaned tooth by tooth with a brush no wider than an eyelash. Each morning she climbed the frozen track above town in the dark, her breath hanging around her like a veil, and each evening she descended with her ears still ringing faintly with the tick she could not yet let herself trust.

The clock was honest work. The town was not.

* * *

She understood it better now, having wintered three weeks among them. Verenchal was a town built on its own portrait — the way some people are, all chin and jaw arranged to flatter a face that isn't there anymore. In the general store, under a daguerreotype gone amber with age, the founding story hung framed in walnut: four families, one hard winter, a valley claimed by courage and consecrated by the surveyor's line. Hale, Vasseur, Merrick, Ostley. She'd noticed Bram flinch at his own surname on that plaque the first week, a small involuntary motion, like a man touching a burn scar through his coat.

Now there were new plaques everywhere. They appeared overnight, it seemed — lacquered boards nailed to the church door, the schoolhouse, the post office porch — announcing the Centennial of Founding, December 31st, 1899. One Hundred Years of Verenchal. All citizens invited.

"All citizens," Ada Quill said, reading the board outside her own post office with an expression Elodie could only describe as arithmetic. "That's generous of him. He used to say 'respectable citizens.' I suppose he needs the numbers for the photograph."

"Needs whose numbers?"

"Judge Hale's." Ada sorted the morning mail without looking up, though Elodie noticed her hand pause over a thick envelope bearing the state seal. "There's a book coming, girl. A history. Two hundred pages of gilt-edged certainty, written by a man from the capital who has been here four days and dined twice at the judge's table. It gets sealed into the state archive New Year's Eve, right after the unveiling, in a ceremony they'll photograph for the newspapers. Sealed — that's the word they use. Like a tomb."

Ada said it lightly, the way she said most sharp things, but her fingers had gone still on the envelope, and Elodie thought of the ledger hidden in the clock's case, the names and payments from 1849 that matched nothing in any founding story, and felt the cold come off the floorboards and into her spine.

"They're sealing it," Elodie said. "On purpose."

"A record is only as good as who's allowed to write it." Ada finally slit the state envelope with a bone-handled knife. Whatever was inside made her mouth go thin. She set it face-down on the counter, beneath the brass scale, and did not mention it again — but that night, after supper, she took down a tin from behind the flour jar and showed Elodie what was inside: letters, dozens of them, yellowed, tied in bundles with string. Names on the envelopes that Elodie had seen only once before, inked in a dead man's careful hand inside a clock case.

*Kettle Creek.*

"I've been keeping these thirty years," Ada said. "Half of them never delivered — addresses scattered to the wind when the settlement broke apart. Returned, undeliverable, forgotten. The other half came later, asking after kin, asking after land records, asking why their grandmothers' graves weren't on any map. You know what I sent back? 'No records found.' Because there weren't. Because someone made very sure there weren't." She retied the string with fingers that were not quite steady. "A postmistress is supposed to be neutral, Elodie. I have been neutral until my teeth ache. But a sealed book—" she tapped the state letter under the scale— "a sealed book doesn't get unsealed. Not ever. That's the whole point of sealing."

* * *

The summons came on the second of December, delivered by a boy in a good coat who would not meet her eyes.

Judge Ambrose Hale received her in his house on Courthouse Hill, in a room where the fire burned oak instead of pine and the smell of it alone told you more about Verenchal's hierarchy than any plaque. He was not what she expected — smaller than his reputation, silver-haired, with the mild, patient face of a man who had spent fifty years being agreed with. He offered tea. He spoke about the weather. Only after ten minutes of this gentle, unhurried courtesy did he come near anything that mattered.

"Miss Whitlock. Your master speaks highly of you. 'The best pair of hands I've trained in forty years,' his letter says — though I notice he keeps your name out of the trade journals." Hale smiled as if this were charming rather than damning. "Invisibility is a rare gift in a craftsman. Most want credit. You seem content to let the work speak."

Elodie said nothing. Her pride in her invisibility sat strangely in her chest now, like a coin discovered to be clipped.

"The observatory must be perfect by the thirty-first," Hale went on. "The clock especially. It will be heard across the valley at midnight — we're advertising it, you know. The last hour of the old century, struck by Verenchal's own master clock. There will be photographers. A correspondent from Denver. Perhaps even the governor." He turned his teacup a precise quarter-turn on its saucer. "I'm prepared to offer you the restoration contract in your own name. Full fee, triple your master's usual rate, and a letter of recommendation that would open any workshop in America. You could set up on your own account by spring. A woman with her own shop, Miss Whitlock, her own name over the door. That is not a small thing I'm offering."

It was not. Elodie's hands tightened in her lap. She had imagined that name over a door since she was twelve years old — imagined it the way other girls imagined weddings — and here it was, held out to her like a lamp.

"And in exchange," she said quietly.

Hale's smile deepened by a degree, warm as banked coals. "In exchange, the observatory remains what it has always been: a place of science. Stars, angles, instruments. Nothing else. Whatever oddments you may have encountered in the course of your repairs — old papers, obsolete mechanisms, the sentimental clutter of previous eras — such things are best left where found, or returned to the town's custody for proper disposal." He rose, crossed to the window, and looked down at the town glittering below in the early dark. "A century is a heavy thing to carry, Miss Whitlock. We carry it so that the next hundred years can travel light. That is not a crime. It is a kindness. Someday you'll understand that people cannot live beside a wound forever; sometimes the merciful thing is to close it cleanly and let the scar do the remembering."

"You mean forget it," Elodie said.

"I mean," Hale said, turning, and for just a moment the mildness dropped away and she saw the machinery underneath, oiled and patient and very old, "*seal* it. With the state's imprimatur, in the state's archive, where no clever apprentice with a screwdriver can reach it." Then the mildness returned, seamless as lacquer, and he shook her hand as though she had already accepted. "Think it over. Mr. Merrick will bring the contract papers to the post office tomorrow. Do give Mrs. Quill my regards."

* * *

She walked home the long way, past the schoolhouse where Bram Ostley's name had been scraped off the teacher's roster but not quite out of the paint, past the frozen creek that the maps called Verenchal Creek and that Ada, in her kitchen, in her flat postmistress's voice, still stubbornly called Kettle. Snow began to fall, fat and silent, erasing the road behind her step by step, and she thought: *this is how it's done. This is how it's always done. One winter, one signature, one sealed book at a time.*

At the post office, lamplight burned in the back room. Through the frosted window she saw the shapes of them — Ada bent over the sorting table, Bram hunched beside her with his long schoolteacher's hand moving across paper, and between them, under the lamp, the battered typewriter they had begun feeding with affidavits, with ledger transcriptions, with the undelivered dead-letters of Kettle Creek, key by clacking key, building a counter-record that had nowhere yet to live.

Elodie stood in the snow a long moment, the judge's contract a folded weight against her ribs — triple fee, her own name, her own shop, everything she had wanted since childhood, priced at exactly one silence.

Then she pushed open the door, and the bell above it rang, bright and clear as an escapement letting go.

Ada looked up. So did Bram. And it was Ada who read her face in one glance, the way she read every letter that passed through her hands, and said, without surprise, without mercy, and without hope:

"Well. What did the judge offer you?"

Before Elodie could answer, the outer door swung open again behind her, spilling snow and lamplight and the sound of wheels on frozen ruts — and a woman stepped in out of the dark, shaking the storm from a traveling cloak, with the Vasseur jaw and the Vasseur grey eyes and a leather case gripped in both hands like something rescued from a fire.

"I'm looking for whoever has been inside my father's clock," the woman said.

Her voice filled the little room the way a chime fills a tower.

"My name is Marguerite Vasseur. And I think you should hear what he was paid to hide."
