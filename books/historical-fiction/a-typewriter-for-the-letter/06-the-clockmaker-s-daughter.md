## 6. The Clockmaker's Daughter

The coach that brought Marguerite Vasseur to Verenchal smelled of wet wool and other people's secrets. She had ridden three days from Lyon — no, she corrected herself, watching the frost-furred pines slide past the window, three days from the boarding house in Geneva where she had spent nine years teaching young ladies to play Chopin with clean hands and clean consciences. Nine years since she had spoken her father's name aloud to anyone who might have cared to hear it.

Now the name was all she carried. That, and the ledger wrapped in oilcloth against her breastbone, riding beneath her coat like a second heart.

* * *

Verenchal announced itself first as a smell: woodsmoke and river rot and the particular metallic tang of a town built too close to its own water. The coach let her down at the edge of the flooded square, where sandbags were stacked like loaves of dark bread and men moved through the slush with the grim economy of people accustomed to losing ground each winter.

She asked at the livery for the observatory road and received directions delivered with a curiosity she could feel like a hand between her shoulder blades. A woman traveling alone, French accent frayed at the edges by decades of exile, asking after the clock tower — it would be all over the town by supplication's end, or supper's end; Marguerite could not yet tell which Verenchal ran on.

The observatory stood above the town on a shoulder of granite, white dome catching what little light the December sky allowed, so that it seemed to hold a small private moon. Her father had described it to her once, when she was small enough to sit on his workbench among the brass shavings. *A great eye, Margot, pointed up instead of down.* He had built the master clock inside it. He had built other things inside it too, if the letter was true.

She had not believed the letter. She had come anyway. Grief, she had learned, was not a respectable reason for anything, but it was a tireless one.

* * *

The postmistress met her at the foot of the observatory hill.

Marguerite saw her before she understood who she must be — a broad-shouldered woman in a man's storm coat, standing at the fork in the road as though she had been planted there, snow gathering on the brim of her hat. Watching the path up to the tower the way a woman watches a door she expects someone to come through.

"You'll be Madame Vasseur," the woman said. Not a question. "Ada Quill. Postmistress." She said the word *postmistress* the way other women said *widow* — an office assumed through loss and worn without apology. "We've been expecting you since Tuesday."

"Then you know more about my arrival than I do," Marguerite said. "I only decided Tuesday."

A flicker of something — approval? amusement? — crossed the older woman's face. "That's how it is here now. The weather decides most things. Judge Hale decides the rest." She tilted her head toward the tower. "You're here about the clock."

"I'm here about my father."

"The clock *is* your father, far as this town is concerned. Come along. There's tea at my house and three people upstairs pretending not to wait for you, and I'd rather we all stopped pretending before the kettle boils over."

* * *

The back room of the post office was warm and close and full of paper. Marguerite registered the details the way her father had taught her to register a mechanism — not judging, only observing, each part in relation to the whole. A battered typewriter on the table, its keys worn to the shape of use. Stacks of affidavits weighted under a brass lamp. A young man rising too quickly from his chair, knocking ink across a blotter — Ostley, the disgraced schoolteacher, his collar soft from washing and his eyes hungry and afraid. And at the window, silhouetted against the grey light, a slight figure in a work apron with her sleeves rolled, hands stained to the knuckle with clock oil.

Elodie Whitlock. The apprentice sent from the city. Ada Quill's letters had named her, and Marguerite had read those letters because reading other people's letters was the family trade now, apparently — her father had done it for Judge Hale's money, and the daughter did it for the truth.

"You've opened the case," Marguerite said. It came out as accusation. She had meant it as nothing at all.

"I've repaired the escapement," the girl said evenly. She did not flinch, which surprised Marguerite more than flinching would have. "And I found what your father left in it. The ledger pages sewn into the weight channel. We know about Kettle Creek."

"Do you." Marguerite unwound her scarf slowly, giving her cold fingers something honest to do. "And what is it you think you know?"

"That forty families were paid to vanish," Bram Ostley said, "and the payment was recorded in a book that never officially existed, and your father kept the true accounts hidden in the one machine in this valley nobody dared open but him."

"My father," Marguerite said, and heard her own voice go thin and sharp as wire, "was a coward."

The room went very still. Snow ticked against the window glass. Somewhere below them, the post office's mail slots clattered as Ada's assistant sorted the afternoon delivery — the ordinary heartbeat of a town that did not yet know what was being assembled above it.

"He took the money," she went on, because she had begun and grief would carry her through whether she willed it or not. "Do you understand what I'm telling you? You speak of him as though he were some martyr stitching truth into brass. He was paid. Handsomely. I found the deposit records after he died — I found them in his effects, in a drawer with my mother's letters, which tells you exactly how much shame he attached to it. None. Or enough that he couldn't bear to look at either, and so both went into the same drawer and stayed there thirty years while I played piano for girls who will marry judges' sons and never once ask whose valley they're standing in."

"And still he hid the evidence," Elodie Whitlock said quietly. "Inside the clock. Where it would outlast him."

Marguerite turned on her. "Yes. He hid it *and* he took the money. Both things are true, and everyone in this room wants only the half that's useful to you. That's what I came four hundred miles to say." She pulled the oilcloth bundle from inside her coat and set it on the table between the typewriter and the spilled ink. "So before you decide what my father was, you'll read what he wrote. All of it. Including the parts that make him monstrous. Including the parts that make your heroes monstrous too, if any of you can stomach finding out."

* * *

They read. The stove ticked. Outside, the light failed the way winter light fails — not setting so much as surrendering, grey becoming darker grey becoming blue-black, until Ada lit the lamp and their shadows swelled enormous on the plaster walls.

Henri Vasseur's ledger was a working document, not a confession, and that was somehow worse. Columns of payments dated autumn 1849. Names Marguerite did not recognize — Aldous Merrick, the Tavener brothers, a woman signed only as *R. Kettle, widow* — each entry annotated in her father's neat clockmaker's hand. Some annotations were arithmetic. Others were not.

*Paid in full per agreement. The widow wept. I counted the notes twice so as not to count her.*

*Hale insists the schoolhouse burn was accidental. I did not ask. Asking is a luxury of men with land.*

*Built the case double-walled as instructed. God forgive me, the mechanism sings better for the weight of it.*

Bram Ostley read that last line aloud and had to stop. Elodie Whitlock sat with both hands flat on the table, as though steadying herself against a tremor only she could feel. Ada Quill read with her lips moving slightly, the habit of a woman who had spent fifty years taking dictation from other people's lives.

"It's corroboration," Bram said at last, hoarse. "Original documents. His hand, his dates, his — " He gestured helplessly. "It's everything. With this and the pages in the clock, Hale can't bury us twice."

"No," Marguerite said. "He can't. But he doesn't need to." She watched their faces arrange themselves into question. "You're assuming he'll fight you with lawyers. Judges don't fight with lawyers when they can fight with fire. My father's annotations mention a schoolhouse burn that was *accidental*, Mr. Ostley. How accidental was yours?"

The schoolteacher looked as though she had struck him. Perhaps she had. That was rather the point of coming all this way — to strike people, gently, with the truth, and see what rang.

"Then we move faster," Elodie said. "The ceremony is New Year's Eve. Eleven days."

"Eleven days in which every word we write passes through the post office," Marguerite said, "under the nose of a judge who owns this valley, to correspondents we cannot vouch for, using a machine whose ribbons and typefaces can be traced by anyone who cares to look." She let that sit. "I am not your enemy. But I have spent nine years learning what happens to women who trust the wrong testimony, and I will not watch it happen twice in one family."

* * *

Later — much later, past midnight, when Ostley had gone home through the snow and Ada had banked the stove — Elodie Whitlock found her at the window.

"You hate him," the girl said. It wasn't quite a question either. These Verenchal people and their statements dressed as questions; the whole town seemed to conduct itself in interrogatives.

"I hate what he cost me," Marguerite said. "That's different. Hate is simple. Cost compounds."

"He hid the truth where time would keep it safe." Elodie spoke with the careful precision of someone handling a delicate train of gears. "He chose the one object in the valley guaranteed to be maintained forever, and then he made sure the maintenance contract went to strangers. To me. Maybe that was cowardice. Maybe it was the longest, slowest apology ever built."

Marguerite studied her — this girl with oil-stained hands and a spine like a mainspring, who had crossed a flood to sit alone with another family's sins.

"You sound like you've thought about it a great deal for someone who found the pages four days ago."

"I've been thinking about what clocks are *for*," Elodie said. "My whole life, I thought they were for keeping time. Now I think they're for keeping things."

Down in the square, a lantern moved — two figures, unhurried, pausing at the corner below the post office. One raised a hand, and the other handed him something small and pale, a folded paper passed between gloved fingers, and then both looked up, briefly, at the dark windows above the post office before walking on toward the fine houses on the hill.

Elodie saw them too. Marguerite felt the girl go still beside her, felt her breath change.

"Someone's watching the post office," Elodie whispered.

"Yes," Marguerite said. She had known since the livery yard, since the questions asked too casually, since the sense of being measured for a cage. She put her hand flat against the cold glass, over the fading lantern light. "Which means someone has already told them we're here. And I should very much like to know, Miss Whitlock, who walked into this room tonight carrying my father's name — and who walked out carrying theirs."

* * *

In the morning there would be a letter waiting on Ada Quill's desk, franked with the judge's own seal, offering Marguerite Vasseur passage home, a sum equal to her father's blood money, and — typed neatly at the bottom, in a typeface she would later match to a ribbon found in a drawer — *a reminder that daughters are not liable for fathers' debts, provided they decline to collect on them.*

By then, the draft on the typewriter would already be gone.
