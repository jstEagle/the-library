## 1. The Light That Answers No One

The wind came off the Pacific at eleven minutes past four in the afternoon, the way it always did in January, and Wren Sandoval stood at the gallery rail and let it shove her around like a drunk aunt at a wedding.

"Evening," she said to no one.

Six years of saying it to no one had worn the word smooth, the way the sea wore glass. She said it anyway. That was the trick nobody told you about grief — it didn't leave, it just got polished. You carried it until your hands learned the shape of it and stopped noticing the weight.

She went down the iron stairs, counting them out of habit rather than need — forty-one steps, forty-two if you took the landing wide, which she did, because the fourth tread from the top had a loose bolt she'd never fixed and never would. Fixing it would mean admitting someone might visit. No one visited.

The lamp room smelled of brass polish and kerosene and cold. The Fresnel lens sat in its cradle like a great glass beehive, all its prisms holding the afternoon's last gray light and giving nothing back. Wren wound the clockwork first — that was the order, always: clockwork before lamp, because a dark lens was an inconvenience but a lens that stopped turning was a lie. You'd be telling every ship on the water that Point Alder was a fixed light when it wasn't, and fixed lights got mistaken for other things. Stars. Distant houses. Hope.

She set her shoulder against the crank and turned. Sixteen full rotations until the governor weights reached the top of the case. The mechanism ticked beneath her hands, patient as a heartbeat.

"There you go," she said. "There you go."

The fog signal building next door held its diaphone like a sleeping animal. She checked the compressed-air gauges, bled a little water from the line, and wrote in the maintenance log with the stub pencil she kept tied to the desk by a string so it couldn't blow away or wander off, though there was nowhere for a pencil to wander at Point Alder except into the sea.

*Jan 3, 1934. Wind NW 35, gusting higher after dark. Barometer falling. Clockwork wound 4:20 p.m.*

She looked at *barometer falling* for a while. Then she underlined it, once, which was not protocol but was honest.

* * *

By six the storm had arrived in earnest, and by seven it owned everything.

Wren lit the lamp at sunset — kerosene vapor, mantles trimmed to a hair, the flame catching with that soft *whump* she never got tired of, even now, even alone, especially alone. The lens gathered it up and threw it seaward in a white wheel, one flash every ten seconds, and somewhere out in all that black violence the light went on answering questions no one had asked.

That was the part she liked. She'd figured that out around year three, sitting up with tea while a November gale tried to peel the roof off. A lighthouse was the only honest conversation left in the world. It said *here I am* over and over, and it didn't need anything back. It didn't need you to be cheerful. It didn't need you to explain yourself. It asked nothing of the dark except to keep being dark, which the dark was very good at.

Her husband Tom had understood that. Tom had understood most things about light and almost nothing about staying alive — killed in the logging accident three years before the light became hers, upriver at his brother-in-law's camp, crushed under a spar that everyone agreed, in the end, had been nobody's fault. Everyone agreed quickly. That was what she remembered most: how fast they agreed. How the company man wrote it down before Tom was even cold, and how the widow Sandoval had sat in the cookhouse with her hands folded and said nothing, because saying something meant a fight with men who owned the lawyers, and she was twenty-six and tired, and silence seemed survivable then.

It was survivable. That was the hell of it. Silence turned out to be the most survivable thing there was. You just had to give it everything else you owned.

Upstairs in the cottage attached to the tower, she put the kettle on and ate beans and bread standing at the counter, because sitting at the table felt like a performance for an audience of chairs. The stove ticked. The wind found the north face of the house and worried it, a long low note like a finger run along a wet glass rim.

On the table, where the mail boat had left it two days ago and where she had left it since, sat a letter.

She knew the handwriting without looking. Edith. Tom's sister, down in Eugene, who signed her letters *your sister, E.* as though repetition could make it true. Wren read them standing up, always, the way you'd take medicine.

She opened it tonight because the storm gave her nothing better to do and because putting it off another day would start to feel like a decision, and decisions were dangerous. Decisions were how you ended up doing things.

*Dear Wren,* Edith wrote. *I hope this finds you well. I will come straight to it, as you dislike it when I circle.*

*I spoke with Mr. Grahn at the Lighthouse Service office again. He says the Point Alder position is slated for automation review within two years, possibly sooner with the new appropriations. He also says — and he said this kindly, Wren, or as kindly as that man can say anything — that they have been carrying you. That a single keeper on a station like yours is against their own regulations and has been since Tom died. You have made yourself easy to forget, and men like Grahn do not forget favors forever.*

*There is a buyer. I know there is a buyer, because Mr. Grahn said so himself — some timber concern wanting the headland for a spur line. They would pay enough that you could buy a house in town. A real house, with neighbors. You are thirty-two years old. You have spent six winters talking to a machine. Come inland, Wren. Come to Eugene. There is a room here. There is church. There is me.*

*I know why you stay. I know you think you are keeping his light burning. But Tom is dead, my dear. He has been dead a long time, and the light does not know him from any other keeper, and the ships do not either. The light burns for whoever winds it. Please do not let it burn you instead.*

*Your sister, E.*

Wren read it twice. Then she folded it along its original creases, which were getting soft, and put it in the drawer with the others — eleven of them now, going back four years, each one a little more desperate, each one answered or unanswered according to some system Wren herself couldn't have explained.

The kettle screamed. She poured the tea and carried it up the forty-one steps to the watch room, because that was where nights happened.

* * *

At midnight the barometer had fallen another tenth and the wind had swung northwest and hardened, coming in long organized blows now instead of gusts, the kind of wind that had decided something. She logged it. *Wind NW 45–50. Sea confused, heavy swell setting NNE. Visibility poor, spray over gallery.*

The old logbooks stood on their shelf behind her, Tom's handwriting fading into hers somewhere in the middle of 1928, the year he'd taken this posting, the year they'd had eight months of talking to each other across this same watch room before the spar came down. His hand had been big and slanted and certain. Hers had started out trying to imitate it and given up sometime in 1930, settling into a small flat script that said as little as possible, which she supposed was honest of it.

She opened the current volume to check the previous entries and found herself reading backward instead, the way you'd reread old letters. *Dec 28. Wind SW 30, rain.* *Dec 29. Wind variable, fog.* *Dec 31. Wind calm. Fired two rockets at passing steamer showing no running lights; steamer acknowledged.* New Year's Eve, and she'd spent it signaling a stranger who never signaled back.

"Happy New Year," she said now, a week late, to the empty room.

The lens turned. Ten seconds. Ten seconds. Somewhere below, the sea worked the rocks like a jaw.

Here was the thing Edith didn't understand, the thing Wren couldn't have written back even if she'd wanted to, which she didn't: it wasn't Tom keeping her here. Or not only Tom. It was that Point Alder was the last place on earth where she still had a job to do, and a job was a reason, and a reason — however thin, however worn — was the only thing standing between Wren Sandoval and the open question of what she was for. Inland there were neighbors and church and a room in Edith's house, and every one of those things required her to be somebody, to have opinions and answers and a face she was willing to show at breakfast. Here she was required only to keep the light. The light did not ask her to be anyone. After Tom, and after what she hadn't said about Tom, being no one had begun to feel like the safest available identity. Like weather. Like something that happened to you rather than something you chose.

And underneath that — under the habit and the beans and the winding and the log — lived the quiet terror Edith had accidentally named without knowing it: a life with nothing left to lose was not a peaceful life. It was a life balanced on the edge of a question. What would it take? What would finally make this count as enough loss? She was thirty-two years old and she had already paid everything, and some animal part of her knew that the world did not issue receipts. The world could always ask again.

The wind slammed the gallery door above her. She climbed to check it, leaned out into the stinging dark with one hand on the rail, and looked north along the black blur of the coast, out of pure habit, the way you check a lock you know is locked.

Nothing. Rain and spume and the faint phosphorescent churn where the swell hit the reef half a mile out. The usual emptiness.

But the barometer was still falling, and falling hard now, faster than she'd logged in two winters, and there was a quality to the sound of the surf — a deeper register under the wind, a long heavy drumbeat — that she knew the way she knew her own pulse. Three days of it, maybe more, coming up from the southwest. A gale that would close the mail boat, empty the sky, and leave Point Alder alone with itself.

Wren went back inside, latched the door, and wrote it down in the small flat script that said as little as possible:

*Jan 3, 11:40 p.m. Barometer falling rapidly. Expect severe weather of some duration. All secure.*

Then she sat with her cooling tea and watched the light go round, one flash every ten seconds, patient as a heartbeat, asking nothing of the dark except that it keep being dark —

— and three days later, when the storm had spent itself and the sea lay heaving and littered like a street after a parade, she would walk down to the north bluff and find that the dark had sent something back.
