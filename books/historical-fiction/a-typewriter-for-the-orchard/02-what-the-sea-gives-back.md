## 2. What the Sea Gives Back

The storm spent itself sometime before dawn, and when Wren climbed the tower at first light the air had that scoured, ringing quality it only got after three days of violence—the whole coast scrubbed down to its bones.

She logged it the way she always did: *Storm abating 0400. Barometer rising. Seas heavy but diminishing.* Her handwriting in the logbook was neat, unremarkable, a hand that had learned not to draw attention to itself. Six years of entries and every one of them true and none of them saying anything at all.

The lamp had held. That was the small victory of every storm now—she'd rebuilt the ventilator damper herself two winters back after the salt corrosion nearly choked the flame out during a November gale, and since then the light had burned through everything the Pacific threw at it. She checked the clockwork, wound it through its full turn, listened to the weights descend with their familiar patient groan. Machinery didn't need conversation. That was most of why she'd stayed.

"Three days," she said aloud to the lens, because the silence in the tower got thick enough to push against, and her voice broke it up like a gull breaking flat water. "You're welcome."

The Fresnel answered with its usual indifference, throwing rainbows across the ironwork as the sun came through it sideways.

She went down the ninety-one steps and made coffee she wouldn't taste and ate bread she toasted too long because attention was still somewhere up in the lamp room. Through the kitchen window the sea lay flattened and brown-green, heaving with what it had taken in—driftwood stacked along the tideline like kindling someone had abandoned mid-chore, a shattered dory hull turned inside out on the rocks below the point, kelp in ropes thicker than her arm wound into the fence wire forty feet above the high-water mark. The storm had thrown the ocean's whole attic onto her beach.

It was the kelp line that pulled her outside. Not curiosity exactly—she'd stopped believing in her own curiosity years ago—but something more practical. After a blow like this you walked your ground. You looked for what needed hauling off before it rotted, for damage to the boathouse door, for anything of yours the sea might have borrowed. It wasn't optimism. It was maintenance.

She buttoned her husband's oilskin coat—it had fit him loose across the shoulders and fit her like a tent and she had never once considered replacing it—and stepped out into the washed light.

* * *

The north bluff path was churned to soup. She picked her way down it anyway, boots sliding, one hand on the basalt face where the lichen grew in gray continents. From up top the beach below the bluff looked like a battlefield after the surgeons had been through: timber, foam, dead fish silvered on their sides, half a whaleboat with *CARMEN B.* still legible on the stern in flaking paint.

She saw him from thirty yards and knew right away what he was, and made herself keep walking anyway.

A man, half-buried in a windrow of bull kelp near the base of the bluff, facedown, one arm thrown above his head as if reaching for something just past his fingers. She'd found drowned men before—in six years you found things; the sea used this stretch of coast as a place to put things down—and the protocol was simple and terrible. Mark the spot. Note the time. Send word down-coast at the first opportunity. Touch nothing but what you must.

She walked up to him because walking away without looking would haunt her longer than looking would.

He was young-ish. Thirty, maybe a little more, though the kelp had pressed his features flat and the cold had gone to work on his coloring, so it was hard to say. Dark hair matted with sand. A good coat, ruined now—a wool coat, city-made, the kind of thing a man wore to an office, which meant the sea had taken him from somewhere he'd never belonged. His shoes were leather and split at the seams. No hat. No pack.

His chest moved.

It was so slight she thought at first she'd imagined it, willed it, the way you will a candle flame to catch. But then she saw the kelp over his ribs rise and fall—not once but twice, slow, the rhythm of a body running on whatever fuel it had left—and Wren Sandoval stood on her own beach in the wreck-light of a finished storm and understood that her life, which had been arranged so carefully around the principle of nothing happening, was about to have something happen to it.

"No," she said. Out loud. To him, or to the morning, or to whoever kept the ledger of such things. "No, absolutely not."

The tide was coming back in. That settled it, or told her it had already settled, or gave her the excuse she needed not to examine the decision she'd apparently made somewhere between seeing his chest move and opening her mouth. The water would reach him within the hour, and the water was in no condition to be merciful.

She knelt beside him in the kelp. He smelled of brine and diesel and under it something faintly chemical, lamp oil maybe, and his lips were the color of the slate rocks. She put two fingers under his jaw and found a pulse—thready, quick, stubborn.

"Hey." She said it louder than she meant to. "Hey. Can you hear me?"

Nothing. His head lolled against the wet stone.

There was no way she could carry him up the bluff path. She was strong in the particular way lighthouse keeping makes you strong—forearms, back, the long muscles from hauling fuel and cranking winches—but he was a grown man and the path was forty feet of vertical mud. She looked around instead, cataloging, the way she cataloged everything: there was the old supply sled, half-buried in driftwood up the beach, runners still sound. There was the block and tackle rigged at the bluff top for hoisting crates. It could be done. It would take all morning and both her shoulders and probably some language she hadn't used since her husband died, but it could be done.

First she rolled him carefully onto his side, drainage position, the way the Coast Guard manual said, and that was when she felt it—hard and angular against his chest, wrapped inside the coat and strapped there with a cord tied in a knot she didn't recognize. An oilskin packet. Flat, rectangular, about the size of a hymnal. Sealed at the edges with wax that had gone soft in the seawater but held.

Her hands stopped.

She was not a fool. She'd read the papers these last two years same as anyone—the ferry sinking, the senator lost with eleven others off the Coos Bay run, the coroner's tidy verdict, the timber men's black-bordered tributes full of words like *irreplaceable* and *statesman*. She'd read about the inquiries that found nothing and the reward notices that described men no one had ever seen. She knew what it looked like when powerful people wanted a story closed, and here on her beach was a man the sea had declined to close it on, clutching a packet like a man clutches a Bible at his own drowning.

Whatever this was, it wasn't hers. Every instinct she owned, every instinct she'd sharpened for six years on this rock, told her the same thing: leave him for the tide, note the time, send word down-coast. Let the county take the body and the packet and the questions, all of it, everything that reached toward the world where history happened to other people. She had paid, once, terribly, for getting involved in other people's stories. She had learned the tuition by heart.

But the tide was coming in, and he was breathing, and she had spent six years telling herself that the person who once failed to act was someone else, some earlier version, some woman who no longer existed.

Wren stood up. Looked at the bluff. Looked at the sled.

"All right," she said, to nobody, in the voice she used for the generator when it balked in cold weather. "All right, damn it."

* * *

It took until well past noon.

She hauled the sled free of the driftwood, dragged him onto it—by then he'd groaned once, deep and involuntary, the sound of a body objecting to being moved, which she took as encouraging—and hauled the whole arrangement a quarter mile down the beach to the foot of the old hoist. The block and tackle had rusted but the pin held. She rigged a sling under his arms, went up the switchback twice to test the anchor log, and then cranked him up the cliff face one groaning revolution at a time, watching him swing against the rock like a landed fish, certain at least four separate times that the rope would part and deliver him back to the sea's custody and end the whole argument.

The rope held. She took that personally, as if it were judging her.

Getting him to the cottage was easier in the mechanical sense and harder in every other sense, because each yard closer to her door was a yard further from the life she'd built, which had walls exactly as wide as this property and not one inch wider. By the time she got him through the cottage door and onto the daybed by the stove, her arms were shaking and her hair had come loose and she was breathing hard enough to fog every window in the room.

She lit the stove properly. Got his coat off—it fought her, wet wool and that corded packet, which she finally unbuckled with fingers that wanted very much not to—and stripped him down far enough to get blankets around him. His skin was cold as the rocks he'd lain on. She rubbed warmth back into his arms and legs with the grim efficiency of a woman churning butter, not letting herself think of the last time she'd tended a man's body, not letting the muscle memory of that arrive.

He shivered. Then he shivered harder. Then, an hour in, the shivering turned to fever, and his skin went hot as a stove door, and he began to talk.

Not words, mostly. Fragments. Numbers, sometimes. Once, clearly, twice, like a man rehearsing: *"Eleven days. Eleven."* And once, in a voice so different from the rest—quieter, almost formal, the voice of a different man entirely—he said: *"Tell them I kept my word. Tell them August kept—"*

And then nothing, just the fever breathing.

Wren sat in the chair across from the daybed with her coffee gone cold in her hands and looked at this stranger filling up the room she had kept empty on purpose for six years.

The cottage felt wrong with him in it. That was the plain truth of it. She'd arranged this place the way you arrange a sentence you've rewritten a hundred times—every object load-bearing, every silence deliberate. One chair at the table because one chair was honest. His boots by the door, still, because taking them to the shed would have been a second funeral and she hadn't the strength for it even now. The rooms were tuned to the frequency of one woman, and this man's ragged breathing had knocked the whole instrument out of true within twenty minutes of crossing the threshold.

She should send for Marta Reyes. The supply boat came Friday, two days out, and Marta could carry word to the county, and the county would come take him away, and the packet would go into an evidence drawer in some courthouse, and in a month the drawer would be quietly emptied by someone whose name she'd never know, and the story would close the way stories closed for men like the ones in the newspapers. She knew how it went. She'd watched it go before, from a distance she'd chosen and hated choosing.

Or.

Or she could wait and see what the fever left behind. What he said when he could say it. What was in the packet that a drowning man had strapped to his heart with a knot she didn't recognize.

"You're trouble," she told him. His face had gotten some color back, or the firelight was flattering him. Sand still gritted the creases at his eyes. "That's all you are. Trouble the sea delivered to the one address on this coast with nobody home."

The kettle ticked. Outside, the gulls were working the tideline, screaming over the storm's leavings. In the corner, the barometer needle climbed another notch, steady as a promise.

Wren got up, put water on for broth she'd make him drink if he ever woke, and told herself—with the firmness of a woman bolting a door behind her—that she wasn't deciding anything yet. She was just waiting for the weather. That was all. Anyone would.

Under the daybed, where she'd stowed his coat while it dried, the oilskin packet sat in the shadows with its wax seals gone dull, and every few minutes her eyes found it again whether she sent them or not.
