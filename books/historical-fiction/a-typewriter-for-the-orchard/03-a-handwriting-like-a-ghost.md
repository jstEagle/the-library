## 3. A Handwriting Like a Ghost

The first thing was the sound. Not the sea—though the sea was there, a long gray animal breathing somewhere beyond a wall—but a ticking, patient and mechanical, like something counting him.

Elias opened his eyes to a ceiling of pale boards and a crack running through them like a river on a map he didn't know. His mouth tasted of salt and iron. His ribs announced themselves when he breathed, one by one, a ledger of damage. He was under blankets that smelled of cedar and lamp oil, in a bed too narrow for a man his height, wearing a flannel shirt buttoned wrong.

He did not remember being undressed.

That fact moved through him colder than the water had. Someone had taken off his wet clothes while he was dead to the world, had rolled him onto his side—there was the ache in his hip where they'd pressed—and had left him here to be counted by whatever was ticking. He turned his head, slow, the way you turn your head in a room where you've been sleeping with money under the mattress.

A kitchen. A stove gone gray. A table with a single chair. A wall of shelves holding jars, coils of rope, and books—logbooks, dozens of them, spines cracked, dates inked along their edges going back years. And on the desk beside the window, a typewriter under an oilcloth cover, and beside it a clockwork mechanism in a glass case, brass and gleaming, turning its small gears with that patient tick.

*Counting him.* He almost laughed. It wasn't counting him. It was counting everything but him. That's what clocks in empty houses do.

Where was the packet.

His hand went to his chest before his mind caught up—nothing there, just borrowed flannel—and the panic came up his throat like seawater, fast and total, and then he saw it: the oilskin bundle, still knotted, sitting on the corner of the desk in plain view like a loaf of bread.

She hadn't opened it. Or she had, and put it back, and was playing a longer game. Both possibilities required the same next move.

He swung his legs off the bed and the floor tilted and he rode it, jaw locked, waiting for the room to settle like a boat coming about. Three steps to the desk. Four. He lost count. The packet was heavier than it looked; paper always was, once you knew what it carried. Two years of carrying it and his hands still did that thing—closed around it, knuckles whitening—as if it might bolt.

He heard the floorboard behind him and turned with the bundle pressed to his chest like a man caught stealing his own heart.

She stood in the doorway with an armload of split wood, rain streaming off her hood, and she looked at him the way you look at weather: noting it, not surprised by it.

"You shouldn't be up," she said.

"I need—"

"Floorboard," she said. "Third from the hearth, there's a gap at the edge. The nail's loose. I noticed this morning." She nodded at the desk. "I didn't look inside. It isn't mine."

Then she walked past him, knelt at the stove, and began feeding it wood as though a half-drowned stranger clutching a sealed bundle were simply another item on the day's list, between the fire and the kettle.

Elias stood there with his heart slamming and thought: *either she's telling the truth or she's very good.* In his experience the second kind was rarer and more dangerous, and both kinds eventually wanted something.

He crouched at the hearth. Third board. The gap was there, honest as she'd said. He worked the packet into the dark under the house's skin and pressed the board back until it sat flush, and only then did he realize how much the bending had cost him—he had to put a hand on the floor to keep from listing sideways.

"Bed," she said, without turning around. "There's broth on the stove when you can hold a spoon."

He went back to the bed because his body outvoted him, and because a keeper who found your hiding place and told you about it was either safe or patient, and either way he'd learn more from the ceiling than from standing.

From the bed he watched her. That was the work now. Everything else—fever, ribs, the eleven days burning down like a fuse—was weather around the work.

She was thirty-something, maybe, tall in a way that seemed accidental, as if she'd grown to fill the space no one else would take. Dark hair cut blunt at the jaw, practical, unadmired. She moved through her own kitchen without looking at anything, which meant she'd lived alone long enough for the objects to know her. There was no ring. There was a photograph on the shelf above the stove—a young man in front of a truck, squinting into sun—and she never glanced at it, which is its own kind of looking. You don't avoid a face unless you know exactly where it hangs.

"Sandoval," he said.

Her hand paused on the kettle handle. Just a pause, half a heartbeat, then water into a cup.

"You said my name in your sleep," she said. "Twice. Along with some other things I'd rather you hadn't."

"What other things."

"Doesn't matter." She set the cup down hard enough to slosh. "You want to tell me why a drowned man knows my name?"

Because I read it, he thought. Because three weeks ago, in a rented room in Astoria with rain coming sideways, I sat cross-legged on a bad mattress with a shipping manifest spread across my knees, tracing cargo routes backward through two years of forged tonnage, and your name surfaced out of the columns like a body out of deep water. *W. Sandoval, Point Alder Light, receiving agent of record, kerosene deliveries, quarterly.* Your name on the paperwork of the light that stands over the northern approach—the same approaches the syndicate's barges used, running unmolested, running *lit*, on nights when every honest vessel was warned off by weather. Somebody had kept that light burning for them, or somebody had been too grief-struck to notice what passed beneath it, and I could not tell which, and I lay on that mattress until dawn deciding whether W. Sandoval was a witness, an accomplice, or a widow who'd stopped reading the world.

"I don't," he said. "Know it. I dreamed it."

"Liar," she said, without heat, almost with respect, and handed him the cup.

* * *

The fever took him after that, in waves, the way storms work a coast—come in hard, back off, come in harder. Between waves he surfaced into fragments:

Her hands on his forehead, cool, impersonal, a nurse's efficiency with nothing tender in it. "Drink."

Him saying, into the dark of the room, to no one: "August, the tide tables are wrong, someone's altered the—" and her voice from far away, flat: "Who's August?"

The smell of lamp oil and cedar and, underneath, faintly, apples—or maybe that was memory, his grandmother's orchard in Hood River, gone now like everything else that predated the letters.

And once, worst of all, waking clear-eyed at three in the morning to find her asleep in the chair across the room, upright, arms crossed, chin dropped to her chest—not watching over him, he understood, but guarding the floorboard. Guarding it the way you guard a door in a house where you live alone: not expecting trouble, but knowing the door's weight by heart.

He lay in the dark doing arithmetic.

Eleven days. He had told himself eleven days before the sweep reached this stretch of coast, and even if that number was generous, generosity wasn't a plan. He could not walk to Coos Bay. He could barely walk to the privy. Every hour the fever stayed, Corben's men moved south along the highway, buying silence town by town, and somewhere behind them came the men who didn't buy—who burned.

Which left the woman in the chair.

Wren Sandoval. Witness, accomplice, or widow. He needed her to be the first, could survive the second, and could do nothing about the third except lie in her bed and let her decide for him.

Trust, he reminded himself, wasn't a feeling. Feelings got people buried. Trust was a calculation performed with incomplete figures, made quickly, committed to fully, and audited afterward at whatever cost the audit demanded. He'd trusted August Hale with his life twice—once on the ferry deck in the rain, once every day since, carrying paper that could hang him in any county in Oregon. Both times the calculation held. He could run the numbers on a lighthouse keeper too.

Assets: she'd dragged him off the rocks instead of letting the sea finish its work. She'd shown him the loose floorboard instead of keeping it as leverage. She hadn't touched the packet, or claimed she hadn't, and liars who lied small were usually lying big, but—no. He corrected himself. Liars who lied small were usually lying *well*, and well was worse.

Liabilities: her name was on manifests connected to Hale route cargo. Her brother-in-law—there had been a name in the file, Sandoval-adjacent, a logger, dead in 1930, accident ruled his own fault, and Elias had read that ruling twice because rulings like that were the syndicate's signature, neat as a knot. If she knew, she was a threat they'd already neutralized once. If she didn't know, she was a fuse waiting for a match, and fuses didn't get to choose what they lit.

Unknowns: nearly everything, including why a woman with nothing apparently binding her to this rock had stayed six years on it, winding clockwork and logging weather for an audience of gulls.

In the morning the fever broke for good, or mostly, and he woke to find her at the desk writing in the current logbook, her pen moving in quick sure strokes.

"Point Alder Light," she said, without looking up. "Keeper's daily record. Weather, wind, lamp hours, vessels sighted." She finished the line, blotted it, and turned in the chair to face him for the first time properly, and her eyes were the gray of the water past the point, and they missed nothing. "It also records strangers. So. Before I write you down—I'd like to know what to call you."

"Elias," he said. Then, because the audit required it, because the calculation demanded one more figure: "Vane."

Something crossed her face. Not recognition—something before recognition, the shadow of a shape seen through fog. Her gaze went to the floorboard by the hearth, then to the window, then to him, and when she spoke her voice had gone quiet in a way that made the ticking clock seem loud.

"There's a name I need to say," she said, "and you're going to tell me why it's written all over whatever's under my floor."

She pulled a sheet of paper from the logbook's back pages—old stationery, cream-colored, kept for letters home—and laid it on the blanket over his legs. On it, in looping, elegant, unmistakable script, were four words.

*Ask the ferry. A.H.*

Elias felt the room tilt again, and this time it wasn't the fever.

"That handwriting," Wren Sandoval said, and her voice finally shook, just once, "belonged to a dead man. I watched Oregon bury him in 1932."

* * *

He should have denied it. He knew that even as he lay there silent, watching her face move through disbelief toward something harder. Every survival instinct he'd sharpened over two years said: shrug, feign fever-dreams, give her nothing. The packet stayed hidden. The secret stayed cheap.

But she had said *I watched Oregon bury him*, and underneath the words was a crack, and through the crack he could see it—the same vertigo that had lived in his own chest since the night he climbed out of the Umpqua shallows and understood that the world's ledger had been falsified and only he held the true copy. She had lost someone and been handed a false accounting. He knew that arithmetic by heart.

"The man who wrote that," Elias said slowly, "is alive. He is seventy miles north of here as of last Tuesday, moving south ahead of me. And the reason you've never heard of it is that eleven men have died making sure you wouldn't."

She didn't step back. He gave her credit for that. She set the pen down with great care, squared it against the logbook's edge as if order could hold the morning together, and said:

"Start at the beginning. And don't stop until I believe you."

So he started—Hale, the ferry, the oilskin, the eleven days—and as he talked he watched her hands, because hands confessed what faces managed, and her hands had gone still on the table, absolutely still, the stillness of a woman hearing a door open onto a room she'd boarded shut years ago.

Outside, the storm had spent itself, and the light through the salt-fogged window came down thin and clean, catching the dust above the floorboard where two years of testimony slept three feet from a woman whose name appeared in its margins.

He had done the calculation. Now he waited to see if the sum would hold—or bury them both.
