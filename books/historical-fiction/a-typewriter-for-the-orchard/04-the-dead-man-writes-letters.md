## 4. The Dead Man Writes Letters

The fever broke its rhythm on the second night, and that was worse.

For two days it had climbed and fallen like a tide chart, predictable as anything else in Wren Sandoval's life. She'd learned the pattern the way she'd learned the lamp's mechanisms—by watching, by logging, by refusing to be surprised. Hot at dusk. Worse past midnight. Sweat through dawn. She'd stripped the man to his shirt, wrapped him in her own spare blankets, and sat in the straight-backed chair by the stove with a basin of seawater and a cloth, doing the only thing she knew how to do for another human being, which was to keep a record and apply what the record recommended.

But tonight the rhythm broke. His temperature spiked past midnight and kept climbing, and he began to talk.

Not words, at first. Sounds—the wet, urgent muttering of a man arguing with someone who wasn't there. Then fragments. *Not the manifest. Not the manifest.* And once, clearly, terribly: *He signed it himself. I watched him sign it.*

Wren wrung out the cloth and laid it across his forehead and did not let herself wonder who had signed what. That was the discipline of the lighthouse: you attended to what was in front of you. The lamp. The weather. The body in your bed with salt still crusted in the seams of his ears. Everything else was the sea's business, and the sea kept its own counsel.

She had dragged him up from the north bluff two days ago, half-buried in kelp like something the storm had decided to give back after considering keeping it. He'd weighed less than a drowned man should have—less than Tom had, when they'd finally brought him home—and she had made herself not think about that either. She'd hauled him up the switchback path in stages, breathing through her teeth, and told herself the whole time that she was doing it because leaving a man to die on her beach would make her no better than the gulls, and not because six years of silence had built up in her like pressure and needed somewhere to go.

His name, when he could produce one, was Elias Vane. It sounded invented, the way names sounded when men were running from something. She hadn't asked from what. Asking was how you became part of a story, and Wren had retired from stories.

Now she sat with the cloth and the basin while he burned, and outside the wind came off the Pacific in long gray shoves against the tower, and the lamp above them turned and turned, answering nobody, the way it always did.

* * *

Near three in the morning, the fever peaked, and the packet came out.

He'd been clutching it when she pulled him from the kelp—an oilskin-wrapped bundle the size of a hymnal, tied with waxed cord, which he had gripped so hard in the water that his fingers had to be pried loose one by one. She'd put it on the high shelf of the cupboard, above the flour, meaning nothing by it except that it was dry there and out of the way. But sometime in the first night he had found the strength of a drowning man to get up, cross the room, and move it under the floorboard beside the bed—the loose one, third from the wall, which every keeper who ever lived here had used for the same purpose. She'd noticed the scuff mark in the morning and said nothing. A man who hid things under floorboards was a man who would fight you for them, fever or no fever.

Tonight, though, delirium undid him. He worked the board up with fingers that couldn't find purchase, hauled the packet into bed with him, and began working at the cord with his teeth.

"Here." Wren took it from him before he shredded the oilskin. "You'll choke on the string."

He looked at her with eyes gone bright and unfocused, and for a moment he seemed about to argue—she could see the calculation swimming up through the fever, the arithmetic of a man deciding whether even a hallucination was safe—but then whatever he saw in her face satisfied some test she didn't know she was taking, and his hands dropped.

"Just—" he said. "Don't read them."

"I don't read other people's mail."

"You will want to," he said, with terrible clarity, and then the fever took him back under, and he was gone again into his argument with the absent dead.

Wren set the packet on the table, retied the cord where he'd gnawed it loose, and stood looking at it.

She did not read other people's mail. This was not a virtue; it was scar tissue. There had been a period, after Tom died, when the letters people sent her had felt like stones thrown through windows—*so sorry, such a fine man, if there's anything you need*—and she had stopped opening them for almost a year. Her sister-in-law Ada's letters came now twice a year, patient as invoices, urging her to sell the light and come inland, and Wren read those because Ada had earned the right to be read. Everyone else's correspondence could drown itself.

So she turned away from the packet. She banked the fire. She changed the cloth on his forehead. She logged the barometer, which was climbing, which meant the sea would lie flat for a few days and then start collecting debts again.

And then, because the oilskin had been gnawed and one corner had come unfolded in her handling, and because the lamp of her attention swung back to it the way the great lens swung back to everything eventually—

She saw the handwriting.

Just the top letter of the stack, just the salutation, just four words in ink gone brownish with age or sea air: *To whoever finds this.*

But it was the loops that stopped her heart.

A hand like that announces itself. The capitals tall and narrow, leaning forward like men walking into wind. The lowercase letters joined in long, confident ligatures, the descenders swept deep and finished with a small upward flick, as though the pen could not quite bring itself to end a thought without a flourish of hope. It was an old-fashioned hand, a schoolmaster's hand, the hand of a man who had been taught penmanship as a moral discipline and never entirely abandoned the belief.

Wren knew that hand.

She had known it across a kitchen table in Coos Bay in the spring of 1924, ten years ago and a hundred miles south, when August Hale had sat with a young girl of fourteen and a steel-nibbed pen and taught her the Palmer method the way other men taught fly-casting—with patience, with jokes, with an inexhaustible belief that beauty was a skill like any other and could be installed in anyone willing to practice. Her sister Ruth had wanted beautiful handwriting the way other girls wanted curls. And Hale—then a state senator, then a family friend, then simply a large warm presence in their small world who noticed that Ruth practiced her letters on butcher paper—had spent three Sunday afternoons that spring drilling her in the loops and joins until Ruth's writing bloomed into something almost identical to his own, because that was how Palmer worked: you copied the master until you became a copy.

*Ruth,* Wren thought, and the name went through her like cold water through a hull.

Ruth, who had married into the logging camps. Ruth, whose husband Dell had died under a fallen spar in 1931 while a company doctor wrote *worker error* on a form he'd filled out before the body cooled. Ruth, who had moved inland after that and written fewer letters each year until now she wrote none, and Wren understood why and had never once made her say it.

Ruth, whose handwriting Wren still received occasionally, and which still carried, in every loop and join, the ghost of August Hale's Sunday afternoons.

Wren picked up the top letter with both hands, the way you pick up something hot.

*To whoever finds this.* The date beneath it was from March of 1932. Two years ago. More than two years.

August Hale had died in September of 1932. Everyone on the coast knew it; it had been in every paper from Astoria to Eureka. The ferry *Corvina*, outbound from Coquille River bar in a fog that ate sound itself, gone down with eleven souls aboard, among them State Senator August Hale, returning from Portland where he had been, the papers said, preparing testimony before a legislative committee on timber practices. They'd found one life preserver and part of a deck chair. They'd found a shoe. The coroner's report ran nine pages and concluded, with the thoroughness of a document paid for in advance, that all aboard were lost.

Wren had read that report. Everyone had. There had been something in it—a smell of varnish over rot—that she had recognized without naming, the same smell that hung around the paper that cleared Dell's death. But she'd been six years into her apprenticeship in silence by then, and she had filed the feeling away where she filed everything, under *not my business*, and the feeling had stayed down there in the dark and grown.

Now she held a letter dated five months before the ferry sank, in a dead man's handwriting, beginning *To whoever finds this*, and the fixed past—the past she had built her whole architecture of survival on, the past that said *what happened happened and cannot be revisited*—came loose in her chest like a mooring line in a squall.

"No," she said aloud, to the room, to the sleeping man, to the sea.

But the letter was still in her hands.

* * *

She should have put it back. She knew that the way she knew the draft on the north door. She put it back—or meant to. What actually happened was that she read the first paragraph standing up, and the second sitting down, and by the third she had drawn the chair close to the stove and turned the page with the care of a woman defusing something.

It was not a confession. It was colder than that. It was an inventory.

Hale wrote the way he'd taught penmanship—methodically, believing that beauty and truth were both matters of correct procedure. He named the syndicate: the Aldercrest Timber Combine, and behind it three men whose names meant nothing to Wren and therefore everything, because powerful men are only ever anonymous to the people they own. He described, in numbered paragraphs, the arrangement by which the Combine had bought the state lands' appraisal outright, the amounts, the dates, the intermediary banks. He described the bought coroner—not by name but by office and by date, and Wren felt the floor tilt under her, because the date matched Dell's inquest to the month and the county, and Hale had written, in his looping hopeful hand: *the same physician has certified eleven "accidents" in Halloway district since 1928. I have copies of his fee schedule.*

Fee schedule. Like lamps and oil.

And then the last paragraph of the first letter, the one that made Wren look up at the stranger burning in her bed and see him, really see him, for the first time:

*I write these because paper survives men. They know I know. If this reaches anyone with the courage to read it aloud, the ferry may become necessary to them. If it does—if I am lost—these letters are the ferry's answer. I ask whoever keeps them to keep them moving. South. To people. Paper survives men, but only if someone carries it.*

Dated March 1932. Six months before the *Corvina* went down carrying him.

Wren sat very still and listened to the wind and felt history step through her own front door and stand dripping on her floorboards, asking nothing, demanding everything.

Because here was the vertigo of it, the thing that unmade six careful years: she had organized her entire existence around the proposition that the past was settled. That Dell was dead because spar fall, that the *Corvina* was dead because fog, that grief was simply the interest you paid on facts already booked. You could survive anything if it was finished. She had survived it because it was finished.

And now a man lay in her bed with the *Corvina*'s death warrant in an oilskin packet, and the handwriting was alive under her fingers, and the loops leaned forward into wind like men walking, and somewhere south of here a physician had a fee schedule, and Ruth's beautiful Palmer-method letters—all those years ago, all that hope installed in a girl's hand—suddenly looked like evidence too.

The man in the bed stirred. His eyes opened—not fever-bright now, but clear, and fastened on her with a focus that had been waiting behind the delirium the whole time.

"I told you not to read them," Elias Vane said. His voice was gravel and rust. But he didn't reach for the letter. He watched her instead, watching her face, reading it the way she read weather.

"That's August Hale's handwriting," Wren said.

"Yes."

"August Hale is dead."

"Yes," Elias Vane said again. And then, because the fever had burned away whatever caution a man in his position ought to have left: "Is he?"

Outside, the lamp turned. Somewhere under the floorboards, the past lay coiled and waiting, and Wren Sandoval—who had spent six years learning that history happened to other people—felt it turn its attention, patient as tide, toward her.

The barometer was still climbing. Three fair days, maybe four.

Whatever came next would come fast.
