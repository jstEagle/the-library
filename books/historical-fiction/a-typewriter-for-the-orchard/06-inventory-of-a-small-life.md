## 6. Inventory of a Small Life

The list began, as all honest lists do, with lies.

*One lamp, Fresnel lens, fourth order. One clockwork mechanism, eight-day. One set of oilskins. One fog bell.*

Wren wrote it out on the back of a supply manifest, sitting at the kitchen table while Elias slept in the room that had been hers and Tom's for four years and had been nobody's for two. The pen scratched. The stove ticked. Outside, the gulls were working the tideline where the storm had left its offerings—kelp in ropes, a broken dory rib, half a crab pot from some boat she'd never know.

She was cataloging her life so she could leave it, and the strange thing—the thing that made her stop once with the pen hovering—was how short it came out. Six years of keeping Point Alder, and everything she owned fit on one page of a supply manifest with room left over for a grocery order.

*One house, government-owned.* She crossed that off. It wasn't hers to sell or pack. *One husband, deceased.* She didn't write that down at all. Some inventory didn't go on paper.

The letter from Ada was still pinned to the wall by the door, third time around. *Sell the light, Wren. Come inland. There's a room here and the winters are shorter.* Ada wrote every few months, patient as tide tables, and every few months Wren had written back the same answer dressed in different weather: *the light needs me.* It wasn't entirely a lie. The light did need someone. But she'd let herself believe the reverse too—that she needed the light, that without it she would be nothing but a woman shaped like a hole—and standing now in the middle of her own kitchen making lists, she understood that this was the lie she'd been living inside of. You can't be needed by a machine. A machine doesn't know you're there. Six years she had spoken aloud to no one, and the light had never once answered, because light doesn't answer. Light just burns, whether you watch it or not, whether anyone does.

She had already abandoned this life. She was only now catching up to the fact.

Elias slept badly, the way drowning men sleep—like the body hadn't been told yet that it survived. Twice in the night she'd heard him say a name. Once it sounded like *August*. Once like *no*. She didn't ask. There would be time for asking on the road south, eleven days of road, if she actually went, and she was going; she had known it since she read the last letter and saw the ferry's name written in handwriting she'd have known blindfolded, by feel, by the particular way August Hale crossed his t's like a man signing a confession.

So: the inventory.

She went through the cottage room by room with the manifest and a pencil, and it was worse than sad, it was embarrassing, like undressing in front of a stranger. In the bedroom: two wool blankets, one quilt Tom's mother made, clothes enough for one woman and no occasions. In the kitchen: pans, a pressure lamp, six jars of preserves she'd put up last September when putting things up still felt like a reason to get up. In the front room: Tom's chair, which she sat in exactly never. His pipe on the shelf above the fireplace, cleaned out the week after the accident and never touched since, kept not as a relic but as an excuse—a thing she could dust around instead of dusting out the whole past.

And the logbooks. Fourteen of them, cloth-bound, her husband's square carpenter hand filling page after page with weather and lamp-hours and the small daily liturgy of the light. Those went into the oilskin packet's category: things that were records of a life. She wrapped them in sailcloth and set them by the door, because she was not leaving them behind, and because somewhere in the arithmetic of the last three days they had stopped being Tom's and started being useful. Hale's letters would ride south inside those bindings, hidden in plain sight among fourteen years of fog reports. No one robs a dead keeper's logbooks. No one even looks at them. That was the whole trick of invisibility—she'd had six years of practice—and for the first time it felt less like a wound and more like a skill.

What she owed was harder to count. She owed Ada an answer that wasn't weather. She owed Marta Reyes the truth, or enough of it, because Marta's boat was due Thursday and Thursday was tomorrow and the truck couldn't drive itself to Coos Bay. She owed Elias nothing, strictly—he'd said so himself, hoarse and proud, *you don't owe me a thing*—but debt wasn't always strict. Some debts you incur by witnessing. She had read what the syndicate did to that ferry, to forty-one people who trusted the schedule and the water, and she had read what they'd done after, buying the coroner like a fence rail, and something in her chest that had been packed away like winter blankets had come open and would not close again.

Tom had died in a logging accident ruled his own fault. She knew now, from the letters, how such rulings got made, whose money smoothed the paperwork. For two years she had believed the verdict because believing it was easier than believing the world worked that way. The letters had cost her the world's innocence and given her back, oddly, a piece of her own.

By afternoon she had the truck loaded in the lean-to: the logbooks, a crate of food, the oilskins, Elias's one set of dry clothes, the packet itself sewn flat against the underside of the logbook wrap where even she forgot it was there. She banked the stove. She filled the lamp reservoirs to their marks and wound the clockwork full—eight days of rotation it could give, though the light would sit unlit, dark glass turning in an empty tower like a headstone revolving slowly on its own grave.

That was the part that stopped her. Not leaving the house. Leaving the light dark.

Six years, through two hundred storms and one ferry sinking she'd watched the flares of from this very gallery, and never once had Point Alder gone unwatched. Boats knew it. The chart knew it. Somewhere tonight some fisherman would pass the headland expecting the flash—two seconds on, five off, the rhythm she could recite like a heartbeat—and see only black rock, and he would think: *storm damage, maybe,* and give the point a wider berth, and live. Probably live. The odds said probably.

She stood in the lantern room with her hand flat on the cold lens and told herself the truth, which was that the light would survive eight days of darkness better than forty-one more people would survive the syndicate learning about the letters. Courage as arithmetic. She was getting good at arithmetic lately.

Still, she took the log pencil and wrote the entry herself, dated today, in the book she'd keep:

*Point Alder discontinued — keeper's absence. Resuming upon return. God forgive the gap.*

Then she closed the logbook, and the closing sounded final as a coffin lid, and also—she noticed this with genuine surprise—like a door.

* * *

Marta Reyes came early.

Wren heard the diesel before dawn, that familiar laboring knock of the *Petrel*'s engine coming around the north reef, and stood in the yard with her breath smoking, watching the boat feel its way in on the last of the ebb. Early. Marta was never early. Marta ran her supply runs the way God ran the seasons—reliably, impersonally, on schedule—and an early boat meant a reason, and reasons on this coast were usually bad ones.

She tied off at the little landing below the cottage and came up the path with two crates on her shoulder and her eyes already working, quick and brown and missing nothing, the eyes of a woman who had run cargo up this coast through Prohibition and knew exactly what a changed shoreline looked like. Forty-five years old, forearms like dock line, a face that gave away nothing except when she decided to give away everything.

"You're packed." Marta set the crates down. "Boat's barely tied and you're packed."

"I'm going south for a while."

"Since when do you go anywhere?"

"Since now."

Marta looked at her a long moment. Then past her, toward the cottage, where the door stood open a hand's width and the smell of woodsmoke carried, and where—Wren saw it happen, saw it in slow motion—a figure moved across the window. A man's silhouette. Tall, thin, moving wrong, favoring one side.

Marta's eyes came back to Wren and settled there and stayed.

"That's a man," Marta said.

"It is."

"In the lighthouse. Where there has been no man for two years."

"Yes."

The wind came off the water and pushed between them, smelling of diesel and kelp. Down at the landing the *Petrel* knocked gently against the pilings. Wren found, to her own astonishment, that she wasn't afraid. She'd spent six years perfecting invisibility, and here was the first person in six years looking straight at her, and instead of dread she felt something almost like relief—the relief of a held breath finally let out.

"He came out of the sea," Wren said. "Three days ago, after the storm. Half drowned. He's carrying letters, Marta. Letters in August Hale's handwriting. And before you say the senator's dead—I've seen the writing. You remember the writing. Your Tom—" she caught herself, corrected, "—my Tom bought supplies off the *Meridian* for six years, and Hale chartered her twice, and I know you saw him sign the manifests."

Something moved behind Marta's face then, fast, like a fish under ice.

"Hale's been dead two years," Marta said.

"So the coroner says. So the newspaper says. The same coroner who signed off on my husband's accident, as it turns out, for the second time in his career being paid by the same timber company." Wren watched that land. "There are men sweeping this coast right now looking for these letters. They'll reach here soon, if they haven't started asking already. So I need to know what you'll tell them."

Marta was quiet long enough for the gulls to argue twice over something on the tideline.

"What will I tell them," she repeated. Not a question. A woman buying time, weighing coin.

"I can't pay you," Wren said. "I know what they pay. I know what silence costs on this coast and who sets the price. I'm asking anyway."

"The syndicate pays well," Marta said, and her voice was flat, careful, giving away nothing again. "Paid me to carry freight for six years. Paid your Tom's crew, too, though I expect he never took it. There isn't a boat running this water that hasn't touched their money somewhere. That's not a confession. That's just the coast."

"I know."

"But." Marta picked up one of her crates, shifted it, put it down again. "Your Tom Sandoval pulled my brother's skiff off the bar in '29 when the whole channel was white water and nobody else would go. Carried Juan up the bluff on his back with a busted arm of his own. Juan died two winters back and never got to repay it, so I suppose the debt's mine now." She looked out at the gray water. "I hate owing debts to dead men. They collect at the worst times."

"Marta—"

"I delivered to the north shore landing Tuesday," Marta said, louder, addressing the water and the gulls and any listening ear the coast cared to provide. "Saw nothing. Nobody home at Point Alder, light's dark, storm damage likely. That's my report, if anyone asks, and men like Corben will ask, because Corben asks everyone now." Her eyes cut back to Wren, sharp. "For now. I'll lie for now. But understand what you're carrying south, girl. Those men burned a ferry with forty-one souls aboard and bought the paperwork before the water settled. When they find out what you've got—and they will find out—they won't send deputies. They'll send the kind of quiet that doesn't get investigated."

"I know."

"Do you?" Marta stepped closer, and her voice dropped to almost nothing. "Because I've seen what happens to people who help. Ollie Brandt, Newport, spring of thirty-three. Helped a man nobody would name. Found in his own skiff, drowned, in four inches of calm water. The paper called it drink." She let that sit. "For now, I saw nothing. But the *for now* has a price tag, and the price goes up every day you're gone. So go fast, and go far, and don't send word back here—not to me, not to anyone. Nothing that touches Point Alder comes back to Point Alder. You hear me?"

"I hear you."

Marta nodded once, picked up both crates, and carried them into the cottage as if she owned it, because Marta Reyes had spent forty-five years on this coast and owned every inch of it she walked on. At the door she paused.

"Truck's loaded already, I saw." She glanced back, one eyebrow up. "You want me to look at the fuel line before you take that rig over the coast range? Because that fuel line's been weeping since fall, and I won't have Tom's widow dying of a five-cent hose clamp."

It was such a small, ordinary kindness—such a human thing amid all the arithmetic of ferries and fire—that Wren felt her throat close unexpectedly.

"Yes," she managed. "Please."

Marta worked on the truck for twenty minutes in the gray morning, sleeves rolled, cursing the hose clamp in Spanish and English both, and when she finished she wiped her hands on a rag and stood looking at the loaded bed—at the sailcloth-wrapped bundle of logbooks, at the crates, at the small complete wreckage of a woman's life packed for transport—and she didn't say anything about it, which was its own kind of mercy.

At the landing she paused with one boot on the gunwale.

"Wren." First time she'd used the name all morning. "Whatever he was to you—Hale, or the letters, or the man sleeping in your house—you're doing what your Tom would've done. I want that on record somewhere, in case the record gets burned." She swung aboard. "Light's dark. Coast'll notice. Go."

The *Petrel* backed off the landing and turned north, and Wren stood in the yard until the sound of the engine faded under the gulls and the surf, holding the empty feeling of a place already becoming memory.

Behind her, the cottage door opened. Elias stood leaning on the frame, gray-faced, upright by will alone, watching the water where the boat had been.

"Does she keep quiet?"

"For now."

"They always say *for now.*" He said it without bitterness, just the flatness of a man quoting scripture. He looked at her then, really looked—at the packed truck, at the dark tower, at her face—and something in his expression shifted, some weight redistributing. "You left the light dark."

"Eight days' wind in the clockwork. It'll turn either way."

"No. I mean—" He stopped. Started again, slower. "Six years. You never left it. I read your husband's logs, the ones you showed me. Two hundred storms and the man writes *lamp burning* every single night, and then you write it after him, and now you've written *discontinued* in your own hand." He shook his head slightly. "I've spent two years asking people to risk everything for these letters, Mrs. Sandoval, and most of them said no, and the ones who said yes are dead or paid. And you—" he gestured at the truck, the tower, the whole small inventoried life, "—you just made a list, packed a truck, and put out your own light."

"Somebody should have put it out sooner," Wren said. "It wasn't answering anybody."

Elias looked at her a moment longer, and whatever he saw made him nod slowly, like a man accepting terms.

"Eleven days," he said. "Coos Bay."

Wren took one last look at the tower—the dark lens, the gulls wheeling the gallery rail, the rock that had held her shape for six years the way a mold holds wax—and got in the truck beside the logbooks and the letters and the man the sea gave back.

The engine caught on the second try. The road south ran gray and wet through the salal and the stumps, and Point Alder shrank in the mirror until it was just a smudge of black against a smudge of gray, and then not even that.

They were nine miles along, the coast dropping away below them, when Elias stiffened in the seat beside her.

"Stop the truck."

"What—"

"Back there. On the road behind us." His voice had gone very quiet, the voice of a man who had learned the hard way what a car in a mirror meant. "Don't turn around. Just look. There's a black sedan, half a mile back, that's been taking every curve when we do."

Wren watched the mirror.

The sedan took the next curve too.
