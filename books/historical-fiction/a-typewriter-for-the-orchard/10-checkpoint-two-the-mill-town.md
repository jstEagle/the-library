## 10. Checkpoint Two: The Mill Town

*Elias*

The town announced itself by smell before it came into view—sulfur and hot sawdust, the sweet rot of the log ponds—long before the gray shoulder of Halloway Mill rose out of the drizzle like a crouched animal.

Elias had been through company towns before. He'd carried August Hale's briefcase into half a dozen of them in the years when the senator still believed he could fight the syndicate from inside its own house. But he had never seen one from the passenger side of a fish truck, and never with a reward notice bearing his own face folded in his coat pocket.

"Rules," he said, for the third time since dawn. Wren drove with both hands on the wheel, her jaw set at an angle that meant she was listening but would not admit it. "We're clerks. I talk. You don't. If anyone asks, you're my brother's widow and you don't like towns."

"I am your brother's widow," Wren said.

"Then it shouldn't be hard."

She didn't smile. She hadn't smiled since Halloway's water tower came into view, with HALLOWAY MILL — A GOOD PLACE TO WORK painted across it in letters that had faded to the color of old bone. Below the slogan, someone had hung a banner of newer canvas: SAFETY WEEK — PRIZES DAILY. The exclamation point on PRIZES had dripped in the rain and run down the canvas like a tear.

The truck rolled past the first row of company houses—all identical, all painted the same exhausted green—and Elias counted what he saw without wanting to. No private vehicles parked outside the houses except two Ford sedans that gleamed despite the weather. No storefronts on the main street, only a commissary, a clinic, a church, and an office with COMPANY PAYROLL stenciled on the window. Every building sat where the company wanted it. Every road led back to the mill.

"There," Wren said quietly.

The checkpoint wasn't even pretending to be anything else this time. At the bridge over the millrace, two men in slickers stood behind a sawhorse barrier, and beside them a third man sat under an awning at a card table, dry, unhurried, a ledger open before him. Not a guard's table. A bookkeeper's table. That was worse. Guards stopped you because they suspected something. Bookkeepers stopped everyone, as a matter of course, because everything here belonged to somebody else already.

Elias got out of the truck. His leg had stiffened overnight in the boarding-house bed, and he made himself walk evenly, making the limp small enough to read as fatigue rather than injury. He carried the fish buyer's ledger under his arm—the same prop that had passed them through the toll bridge—and kept his eyes down in the manner of a man who found paperwork tedious.

"Morning." The man at the table didn't look up. "Name."

"Orrin Teague. Clerk to Halloran Fish Buyers, Astoria." He set the ledger down. "Picking up a salmon consignment from the cold shed, then south."

The man turned pages. Rain drummed on the awning. Somewhere behind the barrier, the millrace roared through its sluice, white and constant, and Elias thought absurdly that it sounded like applause for no one.

"Halloran's got three trucks registered out of Astoria," the man said. "White panel, green stake bed, and a flatbed with a cracked windshield. Which are you?"

"The stake bed." Elias said it without hesitation, because he had memorized the answer two days ago in Marta Reyes's wheelhouse while she'd quizzed him with a shotgun across her knees and something close to enjoyment on her face.

The man looked up then, finally, and Elias gave him the look he had practiced: the flat, faintly resentful stare of a clerk whose time was being wasted. Under the table, his hand rested on the ledger, and beneath the ledger's cardboard cover, wrapped in oilcloth against his ribs, were forty-one letters written in a dead man's hand.

"Open the box," the man said, nodding at the truck bed.

Elias opened the box. It held ice, sawdust, and eleven dressed salmon that Marta had procured from God knew where, along with the words *you owe me now, Vane, and I collect in directions, not dollars.* The man poked the fish with a pencil, found nothing, and lost interest.

"Company notice," he added, sliding a printed sheet across the table. "Read it before you go into town. There's been some trouble with agitators. Safety Week or not, we don't want troublemakers buying our fish."

The sheet was a reward notice. Elias's own description stared up at him from the second paragraph—*approximately thirty-five years, medium build, may travel with a woman*—and below it, in smaller type, the phrase that made his stomach drop: *last seen northbound on the coast road in company of an unidentified female.*

Northbound. Someone had gotten the direction wrong, or someone had lied about it, and either way the error was sitting between him and freedom like a coin on a rail.

"Troublemakers," Elias repeated, and laughed the way a tired clerk laughs. "In Halloway? What would they agitate about? The hymnals?"

It was meant as a joke. The man at the table did not laugh. Neither did the two men in slickers.

"You best get your fish," the man said.

Elias walked back to the truck slowly, folding the notice into his pocket, and climbed in beside Wren. She pulled away across the bridge without speaking until the checkpoint was out of the mirror.

"They know we're a pair now," she said.

"They've known since the toll bridge. That notice is two days old." He watched the mill grow larger ahead of them, its stacks bleeding smoke into the low sky. "What they don't know is which way we went. Corben thinks north. As long as we stay south of this town faster than word can travel south of it—"

"Word travels fast in a town where every telephone belongs to the company."

"Yes," Elias said. "So we buy our fish, we keep moving, and we do not sleep here. Whatever happens."

He believed it when he said it. He would remember, later, that he had believed it, and understand that believing a thing in Halloway Mill was the surest way to watch it come apart.

* * *

*Wren*

Wren had grown up inland, among farms that belonged to nobody in particular—to families, to banks, to the weather—but she had never stood inside a place that belonged to one thing so completely. Halloway Mill owned the houses and the commissary and the church. It owned the schoolhouse, she learned from a boy hauling ice, and paid the teacher directly, so that no parent ever had to be asked for money and therefore no parent ever had leverage. It owned the water system, the electric line, the one road out, and—this was the detail that lodged in her like a splinter—it owned the hymnals.

She saw them herself, an hour later, in the First Church of Halloway Mill, which stood at the end of Main Street with a bell tower that had never held a bell, because the company had decided a whistle served better for calling men to work and calling them to prayer alike. Inside, the pews were new pine and the hymnals were stacked in the entryway, each one stamped on the spine in gold leaf: PROPERTY OF HALLOWAY TIMBER CO. Even the songs, she thought, were checked out.

They had gone to the church for cover, which had seemed clever in the rain. The plan was simple: the fish consignment wouldn't be ready until the afternoon shift change, and a church was the one building in a company town where a man could sit still for an hour without drawing a second look. So they sat, Wren near the front and Elias toward the back, not together, and she pretended to read scripture while actually reading the room—the framed photograph of the mill owner above the altar, hung there as naturally as a cross; the donation ledger chained to a post, listing pledges by employee number rather than name.

She was beginning to understand why the syndicate had chosen this coast. You didn't need violence if you owned every exit. Violence was what happened elsewhere, in places with doors.

The door opened. Cold air, rain smell, and a man in a millworker's coat—forty-ish, big through the shoulders, hands scarred in the particular pattern of a gang saw operator. He shook off the wet, took a hymnal from the stack, and slid into the pew across the aisle from Wren. Then he took out a pencil stub and a folded paper and began, of all things, to write.

She should have looked away. She told herself to look away. But the paper was a pay stub—the company's standard form, she'd seen a dozen by now—and he was writing figures in the margin, checking them against the printed numbers, his lips moving slightly, and after a moment he stopped, frowned, and turned to look straight at Elias.

Not at her. At Elias. At the ledger Elias had left open on his knee while he rubbed his bad leg, and at the columns of figures in it—columns Wren knew were copied, in part, from another document entirely, because Elias used the fish ledger as practice, keeping his hand in, keeping Hale's handwriting warm the way a pianist keeps scales.

The millworker stared at those loops and flourishes the way a man stares at a ghost standing in a field he plows every day.

Then he closed his pay stub, put it away, opened the hymnal, and sang—not loudly, but unmistakably—a few bars of "Abide with Me," alone, in an empty church, watching Elias the entire time.

Wren's heart went very quiet. She had learned, in six years of nights alone with a lamp, to hear danger before she could name it, and this was danger: not the shout of discovery but the hush of decision. This man knew something. And in a town where the company owned the church, the question of what a man did with what he knew had only ever had one answer.

She crossed the aisle. It was against every rule they'd set, and she did it anyway, because she had spent one life being silent in rooms where silence cost other people everything, and she was done with it.

"That's a fine hand," she said pleasantly, nodding at the hymnal, at nothing. "My husband had writing like that. Kept books for a shipping office."

The millworker looked up at her. Up close she could see he hadn't slept much lately, and that his coat was mended at both elbows with the same careful stitch, and that he was more frightened than angry—which meant he was more dangerous than either.

"Ma'am," he said. "Your husband keep books around here?"

"No. Back east. Years ago." She let a pause stretch just long enough. "You recognize the hand?"

"I cut lumber for twenty-two years," he said, "and before the crash I ran the tally floor. Every pay ledger, every timber cruise report, every letter the company wrote that needed to look honest—that hand signed half of 'em. Senator Hale's secretary had a fist exactly like that." He glanced toward Elias, then back. "Course, the senator's dead. Ferry sinking. Everybody knows that."

"Everybody does," Wren agreed.

The millworker turned his hymnal a page he did not read. When he spoke again his voice had dropped to almost nothing, pitched under the sound of the rain on the roof.

"Name's Alder Brice. My brother worked the tally floor too, till March of '33. Company said he fell off the trestle drunk. My brother never drank a day in his life—he had a Bible verse taped inside his lunch pail." His jaw worked once. "I asked questions. I got moved to night shift. Then my wife got moved—transferred to the Coos Bay commissary, ninety miles off, where I couldn't ask anybody anything. That's how it works here, ma'am. They don't break your legs. They move your wife."

Wren thought of her sister-in-law's letters. *Sell the light, Wren. Come inland.* She thought of how easy it had been, all these years, to believe that silence was safety, when really it was just a debt accruing interest somewhere out of sight.

"My husband died in a logging accident," she heard herself say. "Ruled his own fault. He hadn't touched a drink in ten years either."

Brice looked at her for a long moment, and something passed between them that had no name in either of their vocabularies yet—recognition, maybe, of two people who had both been handed a lie and told it was a coffin lid.

"Your friend in the back," Brice said quietly. "If that's who it looks like, there's men in this town who'd burn the church down around him and call it Safety Week. Deputy Corben wired ahead yesterday. There's a list at the office, and my foreman reads names off it every morning like scripture." He paused. "You need to be gone by four o'clock. Shift change. After that, the yard fills with men who count heads for a living."

"And before four o'clock?" Wren asked.

Brice was quiet. Outside, the mill whistle blew the hour—one long note that vibrated in the new pine pews—and down the street, men began walking toward the gates in a slow gray river, all of them moving the direction the company pointed.

"Before four o'clock," he said at last, "a man has to decide what kind of coward he's going to be. The kind that keeps his family, or the kind that can look at his brother's lunch pail." He closed the hymnal and stood, and Wren saw that his hands were shaking and that he was holding them steady anyway, which told her more about courage than any sermon in this owned church ever could. "Cold shed opens at three. Your fish'll be ready. Don't take the south road out of the yard—take the millrace path, past the trestle. There's a gate the company locked in '31 and forgot to chain. It comes out on the county road a mile past the checkpoint."

"Why are you telling us this?"

Brice picked up the hymnal to return it to the stack, and his thumb brushed the gold stamp on the spine—PROPERTY OF HALLOWAY TIMBER CO.—the way a man might touch a scar.

"Because last month I found out they're not just moving wives anymore," he said. "And because if your friend's handwriting is what I think it is, then somebody finally wrote down what happened to my brother. And I'd like to still be alive when it gets read out loud."

He left. The door swung shut behind him, and the rain filled the silence, and Wren stood alone in the aisle of a church that belonged to the men who had killed her husband's truth, feeling—for the first time since Point Alder—not afraid, but furious, which was a much more useful thing to be.

Behind her, Elias had risen from his pew. He was watching her with an expression she couldn't read, and when she reached him he spoke low and fast.

"We leave now. Before three. Brice is right about the shift change, and he's wrong if he thinks he's safe—we just made him a witness, and witnesses have a shelf life in this town."

"He's known since the moment he saw your hand," Wren said. "He chose this himself."

"People choose wrong under fear. We should—"

A knock sounded at the church door. Three knocks, even, unhurried. Through the fogged glass, Wren could see the shape of a man in a company coat, and behind him, at the edge of the steps, two more shapes waiting in the rain.

Elias's hand closed around the oilcloth packet under his coat.

"Commissary closes at noon," the man outside called through the door, in the pleasant carrying voice of someone performing helpfulness for an audience. "Church too, friend. Company rules—no gatherings outside service hours. You'll want to step along to the office and sign the visitor book."

Wren looked at Elias. Elias looked at the back door of the church—the one that opened, if Brice had told the truth about the geography of this place, onto the millrace path and the forgotten gate and the mile of county road between them and the rest of their lives.

The whistle blew again. Somewhere beyond the stacks, the day shift began to pour toward the gates, four hundred strong, right on schedule, exactly where the company wanted everyone to be.
