## 11. What Belonging Costs

The millworker's name was Oskar Lind, and his house smelled of bread and kerosene and forty years of sawdust worked into the grain of the wood itself. He'd gotten them in through the back gate before the second whistle, past a dog that didn't bark because it was too old to care, and up three steps into a kitchen where a woman stood at the stove without turning around, as if she'd been expecting strangers all her life and was only mildly disappointed it had taken this long.

"This is my wife, Ingrid," Oskar said. "She won't ask your names. She's better at this than I am."

"I don't want to know them," Ingrid agreed, still not turning. "Names are what get people hanged." She nodded at the table. "Sit. Eat. You look like two people who haven't eaten since Halloway, which means you haven't eaten since Halloway."

Wren sat. The bread was dense and dark and warm in the middle, and she made the mistake of closing her eyes when she bit into it, because for one unguarded second it tasted like a kitchen that wasn't hers, like Sunday mornings in a house with more than one person in it, like a life she had walked away from six years ago and told herself she hadn't missed.

Elias sat across from her, hunched over his plate like a man eating in a train station—efficient, watchful, already halfway out the door. His ribs were still taped from the fall on the bad road, and every time he reached for the water pitcher he moved like the motion cost him something he was keeping count of.

"The loft over the barn," Ingrid said. "There's blankets. The dog sleeps under it, so if anyone comes up the drive you'll hear him complaining before you hear anything else." She finally turned, wiping her hands on an apron gone gray from washing, and looked at Wren directly for the first time. She had a broad, plain face and eyes the color of dishwater, and there was nothing soft in them at all. "You'll be gone by first light. Whatever it is you're carrying—and I know you're carrying something, because nobody travels through company country with a face like hers"—a jerk of her chin toward Wren—"for the scenery—you carry it out of here tomorrow and you never say my husband helped you."

"We understand," Elias said.

"I'm not talking to you," Ingrid said.

* * *

They went up to the loft after the lamps went down. Through the gap between the boards Wren could see the mill town laid out below: the rows of company houses, identical as teeth; the water tower with HALLOWAY LUMBER painted on its flank in letters visible from the ridge, visible, she suspected, from anywhere the company wanted them visible; the single road in and the single road out, both of them ending at gates with men on either side.

Every window in town glowed the same weak yellow. Every chimney smoked the same thin smoke. It should have looked peaceful. Instead it looked like a ledger, each house a line item, entered and owned.

"They're the same age as me, probably," she said quietly. "Oskar and Ingrid. Maybe younger."

"Older," Elias said. "Company towns age people fast."

"You knew him. Before. You said he kept Hale's pay ledgers."

"For eleven years. August used to say Oskar could tell you what a man earned down to the penny just by looking at his boots." Elias shifted against the wall, favoring the ribs. "He wrote to us twice after the ferry sank. Both times asking whether it was true. Both times we couldn't answer, because answering meant killing him. So we let him believe it, and he's spent two years believing it, and tonight he opened his door anyway because a stranger looked at him the way—" He stopped.

"The way what?"

"The way someone looks when they've got nowhere else." He said it flatly, without self-pity, which somehow made it worse.

Wren lay back on the blankets and stared at the rafters, where swallows' nests from some previous summer clung like old arguments no one had finished. Below them, through the floor, she could hear the Lind household settling: a door, low voices, a child coughing once and being soothed. There were children here. She hadn't expected children.

"Do you know what they pay him?" Elias asked. "Oskar. Do you know?"

"No."

"Thirty-one dollars a month, minus twelve for the house, minus scrip deductions for the company store, which charges thirty percent over cash prices because there's nowhere else to buy. He nets less than a deckhand. And he keeps perfect books, because keeping perfect books is the one power they let him have, and he does it beautifully, and every figure he enters is another brick in the wall around his own life." Elias's voice had gone quiet and bitter. "That's what belonging costs in a place like this. Not money. Agreement. You agree, or you're out, and out here 'out' is a word with weather attached."

Wren thought about the hymnals in the company church, stamped inside the cover. Even God did business with Halloway Lumber here.

"And yet he helped us," she said.

"Yes."

"Why would he do that? With children in the house?"

Elias was quiet long enough that she turned to look at him. In the dark his face was mostly shadow and the pale line of a healing cut along his jaw.

"Because somebody has to stop agreeing eventually," he said. "And most people can't afford to be first. We made him second. That's a gift, in its way. Terrible, but a gift."

* * *

She woke before dawn out of six years of habit and lay listening to the house breathe. Then she heard footsteps on the ladder rungs, careful ones, and Elias came up into the loft's dimness fully dressed, moving stiffly, with the oilskin packet already strapped flat against his chest beneath his coat.

"We need to talk before they're up," he said. "I've been thinking."

"That sounds ominous."

"We split here." He said it quickly, like pulling a tooth. "Two people traveling together are one description. One person is half of one. Corben's notice describes a man my height with a rib injury and a woman who looks like a lighthouse keeper—which, incidentally, is exactly what you look like, you might try a hat. If we separate at the north fork, I take the river route with half the letters and you take the coast road with the other half. Either one of us makes it, the case survives. Neither of us makes it, well." He shrugged, and winced doing it. "Then it was always going to end that way."

"No."

"Wren—"

"No." She sat up, and the word came out of her with more force than she'd intended, enough that she lowered her voice and started again. "No. Think about what you're actually proposing. You want to divide the testimony—the whole point of it, the thing that only works whole—into two halves, each of which proves nothing, each of which can be dismissed as forgery or madness, so that whichever one of us dies, the other one carries a dead weight south and gets caught with it anyway. That isn't strategy. That's you trying to die alone in a way that feels like math."

His jaw tightened. "It is math. Two targets are harder than one."

"One target that knows how to shoot back is harder than two tired people limping in opposite directions through syndicate country." She stood, and the loft was low enough that she had to stoop, which put them nearly eye to eye. "You've been alone with this for two years. I've watched how you move. You check exits before you check faces. You eat like the food might be the last thing anyone ever gives you. And every plan you make has a version of it where you're the one who doesn't come out."

"That's called contingency—"

"That's called a man who decided a long time ago that he's already dead and everything since is bookkeeping." Her voice shook, and she hated that it shook, and kept going anyway. "I know that man. I lived with a gentler version of him for years, and then I buried him, and then I became him. I know exactly how much of your thinking is survival and how much of it is grief wearing survival's clothes. And I am telling you—I am telling you, Elias Vane—that splitting up is not the plan that saves the letters. It's the plan that lets you stop needing anybody. Again."

The silence after that had weight. Somewhere below, the dog grumbled in its sleep.

"You don't know what it costs," Elias said finally, very quietly. "Everyone who's helped me. Tomás in Reedsport, who hid me in a fish hold for nine days—they found out what he'd done and now he sells bait in Newport and won't meet anyone's eyes. The schoolteacher in Winchester. The widow who ran the ferry landing at Scott Creek. I could give you a list, Wren. A list with dates. Two years long. And every name on it is a person who looked at me the way you're looking at me right now and decided I was worth the price, and paid it." His hands had curled into fists at his sides. "You think I'm trying not to need you? I'm trying not to need you *the way they needed me*. Because the way this ends, if it ends badly, is with your name on my list, and I have to sleep somewhere, and I can't sleep next to that list anymore."

"Then stop making lists," Wren said. "Start making plans. They're different things."

"They're really not—"

"They are. A list is names you've lost. A plan is people you keep." She heard herself say it and realized she wasn't talking about him anymore, hadn't been for several sentences, and that the argument had walked her straight into a room in herself she'd kept locked since the accident, since the coroner's report, since the funeral where she'd stood silent while men in suits explained that Silas Sandoval had died of his own carelessness and everyone in the church had agreed, because agreeing was cheaper, because agreeing was what belonging cost.

"Elias." She sat back down on the cot, suddenly not trusting her legs. "Can I tell you why I said no? The real reason?"

He didn't answer, but he came and sat across from her, close enough that their knees almost touched, and waited.

"When Silas died, his crew knew the rigging on that spar line was rotten. Knew it. The foreman had written it up twice and been told maintenance was over budget. And at the inquest, every one of those men—good men, men who'd eaten at our table—said the load shifted. Said Silas hooked it wrong. Because the company man asked them to, and the company signed their checks, and their wives were standing behind them in the gallery, and what were they going to do?" Her hands had found each other in her lap and were holding on. "And I sat there and listened to them bury my husband under a lie, and I said nothing, because saying something meant leaving. Meant losing the light, the cottage, the whole small world I'd built inside the grief. So I stayed silent, and they let me stay silent, and I told myself for six years that silence was just what a woman alone on a headland did." She looked up at him. "I exiled myself, Elias. Nobody banished me. I did it. And every year the island got smaller and the mainland got scarier until silence felt like safety instead of what it was, which is surrender with good posture."

Elias's fists had loosened. He was watching her with an expression she hadn't seen on him before—not calculation, not assessment. Just attention, whole and undivided, like she was a page worth reading slowly.

"So when you say split up," she went on, "I hear the same arithmetic the company taught all of us. Divide the risk. Protect yourself first. Trust no one far enough that losing them costs nothing." She shook her head. "I did that math for six years and it balanced perfectly and it bought me nothing but a lighthouse full of my own voice. I'm done balancing. We go together, we arrive together, and if Corben's men take us, they take the whole case in one piece, which is worth ten times more than your two halves of nothing. And—" her voice caught, steadied— "and I am not adding your name to any list of mine. I've got one name on it already and it's one too many."

For a long moment Elias didn't move. Then he reached up and pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes, hard, the way a man does when he's holding something back that would embarrass them both, and when he took the hand away his face had changed—something set down, something unbuckled.

"Hale used to argue like that," he said. "Not the words. The refusal to let the numbers do the deciding." He laughed, short and rough. "I hated it. Took me four years to learn he was usually right, and then he went and faked his own death, so apparently I learned it for nothing."

"Not for nothing. For me."

"God help me," he said, "yes. For you."

Above them the rafters creaked as the house began its morning. Down in the yard the old dog barked twice at nothing in particular, and a rooster took it as a personal insult and answered, and somewhere a door opened and closed on the smell of bread beginning again.

* * *

Ingrid fed them eggs and strong coffee and asked nothing, but as they gathered their things by the back door she took Wren's arm—not gently, not roughly, just firmly, the grip of a woman transferring information through her hands.

"He kept ledgers for that company eleven years," she said, low, nodding toward where Oskar stood in the yard seeing Elias out the gate. "Eleven years, and last night he slept better than he has since the ferry went down. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

Wren looked at her. "You're telling me it was worth it."

"I'm telling you obedience is a debt that never closes. Somebody finally handed that man a receipt." Ingrid released her arm and stepped back, and her dishwater eyes were bright and absolutely dry. "Now go. North fork, before the shift change. And if anyone asks, we never saw you—but I'll know, and I'll light a candle in the company church anyway, just to spite them."

They went out into the gray early light. The road north climbed away from the town of identical windows, and at the top of the rise Wren looked back once—at the mill with its smoke, the water tower with its painted name, the small lit kitchen where a man was sleeping free for the first time in years—and beside her Elias said nothing at all, which was itself new, and which she found she liked.

Then, on the far side of the rise, where the road bent down toward the river, they both saw it at the same moment: fresh tire tracks in the mud, deep-treaded, turning off toward the north fork.

Toward the route they'd just chosen.

Toward wherever Marta Reyes's boat had come in yesterday, and whatever Marta Reyes had told them.
