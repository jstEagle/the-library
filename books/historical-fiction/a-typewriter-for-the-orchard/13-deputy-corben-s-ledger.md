## 13. Deputy Corben's Ledger

The telegraph came in at 6:40 in the morning, before the coffee was done, and Deputy Amos Corben read it twice standing at the counter of the Halloway exchange because the operator was watching him and he didn't want to look like a man who needed to read things twice.

HALLOWAY MILL TO COOS COUNTY SHERIFF'S OFFICE. RE: CIRCULAR NO. 4. FEMALE TRAVELER MATCHING DESCRIPTION OBSERVED AT MILL STORE YESTERDAY P.M. ACCOMPANIED BY MALE ANSWERING NOTICE. DID NOT DETAIN. AWAITING INSTRUCTIONS.

*Did not detain.* Corben set the flimsy paper down on the counter and looked out the window at the wet street of Coos Bay, where the gulls were working the fish waste behind the cannery and a dray horse stood hipshot in the rain like it had given up on the whole arrangement of legs.

They had her in Halloway yesterday afternoon. She'd walked through a company town with a dead man's letters in her bag, signed the store ledger — he'd bet his pension she'd signed it, that was the kind of woman she was, the kind who wrote her name down where it could be found — and the mill guard had watched her go and then sat on the information overnight like a man sitting on an egg, waiting to see if it would hatch into trouble.

Corben folded the telegram into his breast pocket, took his coffee without sugar from the girl at the diner next door, and walked back to the office to do what he did best, which was paperwork.

* * *

The ledger lived in the bottom drawer of his desk, under the blotter calendar and the box of .38 rounds he kept meaning to shoot more of. It wasn't an official book. Officially, Corben kept the county log, the warrant returns, the mileage vouchers, the monthly report of persons arrested, fined, warned, or released — all of which went to Sheriff Dunmore, who signed them without reading them, which was the entire point of Sheriff Dunmore.

The other ledger was his own. A school composition book, five cents at the drugstore, ruled pages, his blocky carpenter's handwriting marching down each line. He'd started it four years ago, the winter after the bank called the note on the house on Elm Street, and Ruth had cried in the kitchen not because they might lose the house but because she thought he blamed her for the doctor bills, and Corben had sat at this same desk until two in the morning figuring out what a man's word actually cost per month.

Here is what it cost. Here is what it paid.

He opened the book now and turned past the early entries, the ones he still couldn't look at straight on. *October '31 — Alder Creek mill dispute. Told organizers permit required for meeting hall. No such permit exists. Paid $40.* That one had put three men out of work and one of them, a Swede named Lindqvist, had gone down to California and never come back, and his wife had come to the office twice asking if there was any word, and both times Corben had told her no, ma'am, nothing yet, and both times it had been true in the way that mattered least.

*March '32 — Ferry sinking. Statement drafted re: pilot error. Signed by surviving crew, three names furnished. Paid $200.* He hadn't written the ferry sinking up that way at first. At first the entry said only *statement re: ferry,* as though language itself had tried to look away. Later, older and past pretending, he'd gone back through the book and made himself specific, because vagueness was a luxury and he'd decided he wasn't entitled to it anymore. If you were going to sell something you ought to at least write down what you sold.

That was the thing nobody understood about men like him. They pictured a bad man as a man who enjoyed it. But the money was never enjoyment. The money was the mortgage, and the doctor in Portland who had fixed Ruth's heart valve, or slowed it down anyway, and the shoes for Danny that he grew out of every eight months like a crop coming in. The money was small and regular and it arrived in plain envelopes, and each envelope bought exactly one small survivable thing, and none of the things, taken singly, would keep a man awake. It was only the sum of them that had a face. The sum of them looked like Lindqvist's wife in the doorway, holding her hat in both hands.

Corben turned to the current page.

*Marta Reyes, supply boat *Petrel*. Reported male stranger at Point Alder light, injured, keeper Wren Sandoval harboring. Reported southbound departure via coast road.* And beneath it, in smaller writing, the part that wasn't for the syndicate's eyes: *Which I don't believe.*

Because here was the shape of Marta's lie, and Corben had spent twenty years learning to read lies the way a sawyer reads grain. She had come to him eager — too eager, first off the boat with the story already built, which meant rehearsed. And the story had a hole in it the size of a truck. Southbound coast road, she'd said, alone, the man staying behind sick. But the Sandoval woman hadn't come to town for supplies in six years — Corben had checked, and the checking had cost him half a day and a favor at the mercantile — and a lighthouse keeper does not leave her light on the same week a drowned man washes up below her bluff unless the two facts are the same fact. Marta had given him a direction instead of a destination. She'd pointed him south with one hand while the other hand passed them something — gas, maybe, or food, or just silence — and then she'd gone home to count her envelope money and hate herself at her own kitchen table, if she had any sense, and Corben thought she probably did.

And now Halloway. The woman and the man together, walking north-to-south through company country, and the mill guard too scared or too well-paid to stop them, and the telegraph arriving a full day late because somebody at the mill had let it sit.

Corben did the arithmetic he was paid to do. From Halloway, southbound, on foot or by whatever wheels they could beg, there was exactly one crossing left between them and the open line to San Francisco: the river ferry at Tenmile. The bridge upstream was washed out since the January freshet — he'd filed the report himself, another piece of paper, everything was pieces of paper — so the ferry was not one option among several. It was the option. A funnel with a tollbooth at the bottom of it.

All he had to do was be there when they arrived.

He got out a fresh sheet and began the wire to the man he reported to on the second channel, the one whose name appeared nowhere in any ledger, official or otherwise. Mr. Grieves. Even the name sounded like a bill.

*TO GRIEVES. SUBJECTS LOCATED HALLOWAY YESTERDAY P.M., PROCEEDING SOUTH. INTERCEPT PLANNED TENMILE FERRY CROSSING. REQUIRE AUTHORITY TO DETAIN AND SEARCH. CORBEN.*

He read it over. Then he added, because precision was the only integrity left to him: *FEMALE IS KEEPER'S WIDOW, LOCAL, KNOWN TO ME. WILL HANDLE ACCORDINGLY.*

He didn't know why he wrote that last sentence. He told himself it was operational — tell the principal what he needs to know to plan around. But he knew, in the place where a man keeps the account he doesn't show anyone, that it was a flag planted. *Known to me.* As if knowing a person were a kind of claim. As if six years of watching Wren Sandoval buy flour and lamp oil in this town, nodding to her on the plank sidewalk while she looked through him the way she looked through everyone, amounted to something a man could spend later.

Ruth would say it did amount to something. Ruth believed people were owed their neighbors. Ruth had sent pies to the Lindqvist house for a year after the Swede left, and Corben had eaten his slices in silence and never told her why the pie tasted like ash.

* * *

At noon he drove out to Tenmile to look at the ground.

It was eleven miles of bad road along the slough, water standing in the cutbanks, alders leaning over the gravel like tired men at a bar. The ferry landing sat where the river narrowed, a wooden ramp, a cable strung across the current, the flat itself moored on the far side with its chain rattling in the wind. The ferryman, Osk, lived in a shack above the landing and ran the boat when there was traffic, which in February was a Model T a day if the weather held.

Corben stood on the ramp and made his inventory the way he'd once made inventories framing houses, before the framing business died and the badge became the only trade left. Sight lines: good, open water both ways, nothing approaching unseen except downriver, and downriver was Elias Vane's road if he was the kind to circle — and the notice photograph said he was. Cover: the ferry office, a shed really, with a stove, a desk, a window facing the ramp. Communications: the telephone line ran to the landing, one wire, easy to cut, and Corben noted that too, noted that a man expecting trouble should expect his own line to go quiet.

He could hold this crossing alone. He'd held worse with less. But holding it wasn't the hard part and he knew it, standing there in the rain with the river going by brown and patient, carrying everything toward the sea the way rivers do, without opinion.

The hard part was the woman.

He'd been composing the conversation for two days now, the way he composed all of them, and the composition always came down to the same architecture: give her a door and make sure she sees it. *Mrs. Sandoval. Nobody's accusing you of anything. You're a widow keeping a light; you've got no stake in dead senators. Hand over the papers, sign a statement saying the man forced them on you, and drive home tonight.* Every word true-shaped, every word a lie. That was the craft of it. Enforcement wasn't violence, whatever the novels said. Enforcement was paperwork with a gun nearby in case the paperwork failed. You didn't beat a confession out of a person; you built them a form to sign, and you made the form comfortable, and you waited, because nearly everyone, given a door marked EXIT and enough hours in a cold room, will take it.

He was good at doors. That was the honest summary of his career. Twenty years of building exits for people and charging the syndicate for the lumber.

Except.

Except that he'd read the reward circular again that morning, Circular No. 4, and noticed what he'd noticed the first time and filed away unexamined: the description of the man matched Elias Vane down to the scar on the left forearm, but the description of the *papers* was vague. *Documents pertaining to commercial matters.* Not testimony. Not evidence. Commercial matters. Which meant whoever wrote the circular either didn't know what Vane carried or didn't want the searchers to know. And a man who pays deputies out of envelopes doesn't stay vague about anything worth two hundred dollars unless the vagueness is worth more than the money.

Corben had turned that over all afternoon, driving back along the slough with the wipers slapping, and the conclusion kept arriving in the same unwelcome shape: the syndicate was afraid of these papers in a way that exceeded their value as evidence. You burn evidence to destroy it. You stay vague about evidence because you're afraid of what happens when too many people learn it exists — because existence itself is the danger. Papers that could topple August Hale's murderers weren't just papers. They were a fuse, and fuses don't care whose hands carry them.

And if that was true, then the question underneath his whole tidy ledger rose up again, the one he'd been stepping over for four years the way you step over a loose board: what happens to the man who holds the door shut when the building finally comes down?

Grieves would say: nothing happens, Amos. We pay you, we thank you, we move along. Grieves had a voice like a warm bath and had never once raised it. But Grieves also had a habit, which Corben had catalogued without meaning to, of retiring the people who knew too much — not violently, nothing so crude, just reassigned, forgotten, their envelopes quietly discontinued, their usefulness converted into irrelevance. Lindqvist's wife had gotten a letter from the mill, once. Corben had seen the mill's letterhead on enough envelopes to know the family resemblance.

* * *

Back at the office, the evening mail brought him two things.

The first was a bill from the Portland clinic, $14.50, Ruth's follow-up, due on the first. He set it in the tray with the others. The tray was the real ledger, when it came down to it. The composition book just recorded what the tray demanded.

The second was a plain envelope, no return address, postmarked Marshfield. Inside, a single sheet, unsigned:

*THE WOMAN MATTERS LESS THAN YOU THINK. THE MAN MATTERS MORE THAN YOU KNOW. IF THE PAPERS REACH PORTLAND, EVERY NAME IN YOUR BOOK BECOMES EVIDENCE. CONSIDER WHAT THAT MEANS FOR THE MORTGAGE.*

Corben read it three times. Someone inside the machine was talking to him directly now, which meant someone inside the machine was nervous, which meant the fuse was shorter than he'd calculated. And the message itself was a masterpiece of the genre — half warning, half threat, zero instructions — designed so that whatever Corben did next, it could be read afterward as obedience.

He sat with it a long time. Outside, the rain moved in off the bay and took the streetlights apart into long smears, and somewhere down the block the diner girl was locking up, keys jingling, ordinary as bread.

Then he opened the composition book to the current page and, beneath the entry about Marta Reyes, he wrote a new line. Not for Grieves. Not for the syndicate. For the version of himself that might someday have to answer for all of it, if answering ever became survivable.

*Feb. 9 — Subjects expected Tenmile ferry within 3 days. Have advised principal of intercept. Note: papers reportedly concern ferry sinking of March '32. Note: I signed the statement of March '32. Note:*

He stopped. The pen hovered. There was a fourth note, the true one, and he could not yet afford it, and he knew the exact price to the cent.

He closed the book, put it back in the drawer under the ammunition, and reached for the telegraph blank instead. His hand was steady. That was the worst part, how steady it stayed.

*TO GRIEVES. ARRANGEMENTS PROCEEDING. WILL REPORT AT CROSSING. CORBEN.*

He paid the night operator out of his own pocket to send it immediately, and walked home through the rain to a house he did not fully own, where his wife had left a plate covered with a cloth, and ate standing up, and did not sleep, and in the morning began packing the car for Tenmile — the rifle he hoped not to use, the blank statement forms, the handcuffs, and, wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of the kit where Ruth wouldn't see it, the composition book.

Because a careful man brings his ledger everywhere. And Amos Corben had been a careful man for twenty years, and the road south was already washing out behind the weather, and somewhere ahead of him a woman who kept a light was counting the miles to his ferry.
