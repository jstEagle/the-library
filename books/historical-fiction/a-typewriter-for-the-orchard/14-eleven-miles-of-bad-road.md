## 14. Eleven Miles of Bad Road

The road died a quarter mile past the last Halloway shack.

They heard it before they saw it—the way the truck's wheels went from gravel-song to that soft sucking sound, and then Marta Reyes's cousin, who'd lent them the truck and his silence, stood on the brake and swore with the fluency of a man who had driven this grade for thirty years. Ahead, the road simply wasn't. A whole section of it had let go in the night's rain, sliding down the hillside in a brown tongue, taking culvert, fence post, and forty feet of asphalt into the alder brush below. Water still moved through the wound, patient as a saw.

"Well," Elias said.

"Well," Wren said.

The cousin looked at the slide, then at them, then at the sky, which was doing its gray nothing that meant more rain by dark. "Coquille River ferry's eleven miles south if you cut over the ridge," he said. "Road comes back at the bottom. You can walk it by nightfall if the weather holds, and if it doesn't hold you'll wish it hadn't." He dug in his jacket and produced a canvas satchel—hard bread, dried salmon, a jar of honey gone sugary at the edges—and pressed it on Wren before she could refuse. "Ruthie says to tell you the boy sleeps better now," he added, not looking at her. "Since you-all come through."

Wren took the satchel. There was nothing to say to that that wouldn't cost them both, so she said, "Tell her thank you," and the cousin nodded like a man closing a ledger, and turned the truck around, and was gone.

Elias was already testing the edge of the slide with one boot, finding where the mud held. He'd gotten his strength back faster than she expected—two years of running did that, apparently, built a body out of habit—but he still moved like a man listening for something behind him. Which, Wren thought, he always was.

"We could go back through Halloway," he said. "Wait for a crew to clear it."

"A crew won't clear it this week. And Corben will have a telegraph from the mill by noon."

"Then we walk."

They walked.

* * *

The logged-off hills were a country Wren knew the way you know a scar. She'd grown up in country like this before her father moved them to the coast—hills skinned bare, stumps standing in their thousands like teeth pulled but not replaced, slash piles rotting into black lace between them. No cover taller than a man's shoulder for miles. The rain had stopped an hour out of Halloway and now the sun came down hard and white through the gaps in the cloud, lighting everything equally: the stumps, the slide scars, the two of them picking their way along a skid road that hadn't seen a log truck since the crash.

Elias counted under his breath. He'd been counting since the top of the ridge, and Wren finally asked him what.

"Mileposts," he said. "In my head. The old man used to do it on the ferry runs—said a journey you can number is a journey you can finish." He caught himself on the name and went on before it could open between them. "Eight miles to the river. We're losing light faster than we're losing ground."

Behind them, faint, came a sound that stopped them both: an engine, low and far, working up the grade they'd left.

Elias didn't turn. That was the thing Wren had learned about him in nine days of roads—he never turned to look at what might be following, because a man who turns has already told the world he's afraid. Instead he took her elbow, gently, the way you'd steady a lamp, and angled them off the skid road toward a fold in the hill where the slash grew thick.

"There's a cut maybe two hundred yards down-slope," he murmured. "Old haul road. If we drop into it we're invisible from above and visible from below, so we move fast until it bends."

"You've walked this country before."

"I've walked every country between here and San Francisco, some part of it running." He almost smiled. It didn't reach anywhere near his eyes. "Two years makes a map of you."

They dropped into the cut. The engine behind them climbed, crested, and faded north, and neither of them spoke until it was fully gone, and then they didn't speak anyway, because there was nothing to say that the hills weren't already saying: *you are small, and the country is large, and the men behind you have trucks.*

* * *

It happened at mile five and a half.

Later, Wren would take the moment apart the way she took apart the clockwork in the lighthouse, piece by piece, looking for where it had gone wrong, and the honest answer would always be: nowhere. Nothing went wrong. The world simply did what the world does on a steep slope after three days of rain.

They were crossing a shoulder of hillside where fire had taken the timber years ago and the reborn alder stood thin and whip-straight, holding a mat of ferns on a slope of forty degrees. Elias went first, testing, the oilskin packet slung across his chest under his coat the way he wore it always, sleeping and waking, like a second heart. Wren watched his feet, chose her own, put her weight down—

And the hillside shrugged.

Just a shrug. A yard of slope letting go, wet earth and fern roots sliding, nothing dramatic, nothing you'd even call dangerous if you were standing on flat ground watching it. But Elias was mid-step when it moved, and his downhill foot found nothing, and he did the only thing a man carrying something precious does when he falls: he turned the fall away from the packet. He twisted. He threw himself uphill onto his bad side, onto rock he couldn't see under the ferns, and the sound he made when he landed was short and deep, a door slamming somewhere inside him.

Wren was down the slope to him before she remembered deciding to be.

He was trying to get up. That was the worst of it—that even flat on his back with his face gone the color of the fog, he was trying to get up, one hand already clawing at his coat front to check the packet, checking the letters before his own ribs. "It's fine," he was saying, to her, to the packet, to God, "it's fine, it's dry, it's—"

"Hold still."

"—we can still make the bend before—"

"*Hold still.*"

She knelt in the wet ferns and put her hands on him the way she'd put her hands on the storm-drowned man on the beach ten days ago, and felt what she'd been afraid of feeling: the left side of his chest moving wrong. Not broken-broken, maybe. But the kind of wrong that turns walking into arithmetic, each step a problem with a painful answer.

"It's cracked," he said. Not asking. He knew his body the way she knew her light. "Not through. Cracked. I've had—" He stopped. Laughed once, breathlessly, without any humor in it at all. "I've had worse. In '33, outside Astoria. I walked four days on this exact rib."

"And how is the rib now?"

He considered. "Retired."

Wren sat back on her heels. Above them the white sun was going amber, which meant the light was going, which meant the eleven miles were becoming twelve and thirteen and fourteen, because darkness in logged-off country doesn't slow you—it stops you. Behind them, north, the road they'd abandoned ran empty and silent, and somehow that silence was worse than engines. Somewhere in it, or behind it, or ahead of them on the river road, Amos Corben was being careful.

"Elias." She said his name the way you test ice. "Can you stand?"

He tried. She helped. He got most of the way up, got his feet under him, took one step, and the step cost him visibly—a flinch he strangled before it finished—and Wren watched him begin, right then, in the failing light, to do the math she'd already done. Him walking eleven miles on that rib. Him slowing them to a crawl. Him, if it came to running.

"No," she said.

"Wren—"

"Give me the packet."

"That's not—" He actually laughed again, and this time there was something underneath it, raw. "That's not how this works. Two years I've carried these. Every person who ever touched them besides me is either bought or buried, and I am not adding your name to—"

"I kept a lighthouse alone for six years." She heard her own voice come out flat and strange, a voice she barely recognized, a voice from before the grief wore smooth. "I've carried things heavier than paper up stairs in a hundred-mile wind. Give me the packet and give me your arm, and we'll argue about whose fault it all is when we can sit down."

For a long moment he just looked at her, and the light came down amber through the whips of alder and lay across his face, and she thought: *he's deciding whether to protect me from the truth or trust me with it.* She'd watched men make that calculation her whole life—her father, her husband, the coroner, the whole polite machinery of the world that decided for women what they could carry—and she stood in the ferns and let him look, and did not soften, and did not look away.

He unpinned the strap. Slung the oilskin packet across her chest, adjusting it with hands that were very careful not to tremble, and settled its weight against her sternum like he was hanging a lantern.

"There," he said quietly. "Now you know what it weighs."

It weighed almost nothing. That was the terrible thing. Two years of blood and burials and burned bridges, and it weighed less than a loaf of bread. She put her hand flat against it anyway.

"Now give me your arm," she said, "and count us down the hill."

* * *

Mile seven. Mile eight. They invented a rhythm without discussing it—his good arm hooked through hers, his weight arriving in small honest increments on every third step, her body learning his limp the way her hands had once learned the clockwork's temper. He counted the miles under his breath. She matched her breathing to his counting. Neither of them mentioned the engine sounds they half-heard, twice, far off on the river road below, because mentioning them wouldn't change whether they were real.

The sun went. The dark came up out of the valleys first, pooling, then rose to meet them. The temperature dropped the way coastal dark drops, straight through cloth. Wren got the canvas satchel open one-handed while Elias leaned on a stump, and fed him bread and honey, and he ate like a man remembering food is a thing bodies need, and then she ate, and the honey had gone sugary and crystalline and tasted, absurdly, like her wedding day, like honey cake on a table in a house that had burned down in more ways than one.

"My sister-in-law sent letters for six years," Wren said, to the dark, because the dark was easier than his face. "After Daniel died. Asking me to sell the light. Come inland. Be with family." She turned the honey jar in her hands. "And every year I wrote back the same letter in different words. *Next year.* Because answering honestly would've meant saying out loud that I'd chosen to stop living, and I wasn't ready to say it, and also I was afraid if I said it, someone would come and make me stop."

Elias said nothing. He was good at that—she'd learned it on the road, learned that his silences weren't absence but attention, a whole architecture of listening.

"Nine days ago a dead senator washed up on my beach," she went on, "and I dragged him indoors because leaving him seemed like one silence too many. That's all it was. Not courage. Arithmetic." She laughed, short. "I keep doing arithmetic."

"What's the sum?" he asked. Quiet. Real question.

Wren looked south, where the dark hid the river and the ferry and the checkpoint and the man with the careful ledger, and then she looked at Elias Vane, sitting against a stump in ruined clothes with a cracked rib and two years of graves behind his eyes, holding his arm against his chest like a man protecting something that wasn't there anymore because he'd given it to her.

"I don't know yet," she said. "But it's not zero. For the first time in six years, it isn't zero."

Something shifted in his face—not a smile, nothing so resolved as that. More like a door in a house long shut, opening onto a room with light in it. He reached over, slowly, the way you reach toward a bird, and tucked a strand of wet hair off her cheek, and let his hand rest there a moment against the cold of her jaw, and neither of them named it, because naming it would have made it a thing with a future attached, and futures were exactly what both of them had stopped believing they were owed.

Instead Elias said, "Mile eight," and she said, "Then we're nearly out of numbers," and they got up together, his weight coming home to her shoulder like something returning to harbor, and they walked on down through the dark toward the water.

They came out of the trees at half past nine and saw the river below them, black and broad, and on the far bank a single yellow window burning—the ferry landing, the crossing, the last door between the letters and the truth. And beside the landing, unmistakable even at this distance in the lamplight, stood a government motorcar, and a tall figure beside it, hat brim low, hands folded, waiting with the patience of a man who has checked his watch and found the hour agreeable.

"Oh no," Elias breathed. "Oh, no. That's—"

"I know who it is," Wren said.

She put her hand flat against the packet over her heart, and began to count.
