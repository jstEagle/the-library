## 15. Checkpoint Three: The River Ferry

The Umpqua showed itself to us an hour before we reached it, a gray gleam through the alders like a coin at the bottom of a pocket. Wren had been counting landmarks the whole way down—Marta's truck left in the second-growth above Halloway, the burned-out trestle, the fence post with three tin cans rattling on it that Elias said marked the last mile—and now she counted the river too, because counting was what kept her from thinking.

Eleven days since Point Alder. Nine since they'd left the light dark behind them. Two nights walking since the road washed out and the hills took their boots apart. Her feet were a rumor of pain inside swollen skin, and every joint she owned had filed a complaint she was declining to hear.

"There," Elias said.

He said it quietly, but everything he said now came out quiet, as if loudness were something he'd used up years ago. He crouched in the salal at the edge of the cut bank, and Wren knelt beside him, and together they looked down at the crossing.

The river ferry sat mid-channel, a flat iron barge riding a cable strung bank to bank, its diesel engine coughing smoke into the wet air. On the near shore stood a toll shed with a new coat of paint—the syndicate's paint, that careful institutional green—and in front of the shed, under an umbrella he didn't need because the rain had thinned to a hanging mist, stood Deputy Corben.

Wren knew him from Marta's descriptions and from the reward notice they'd read nailed to a fencepost outside Halloway: *Deputy R. Corben, Coos County, acting agent of record.* She'd expected a brute. What she saw instead was a tired man of fifty-odd in a rain slicker gone gray at the shoulders, hands folded over his belt buckle, watching the handful of travelers queue for the morning crossing the way a man watches water rise around his foundations.

"He's not here by accident," Elias murmured.

"No."

"He knows this is the last door. If we're coming south at all, we come through him." Elias's jaw worked. He hadn't slept more than two hours at a stretch since the hills; the fall he'd taken protecting the packet had opened his elbow to the bone, and Wren had stitched it by firelight with sail twine and no novocaine, and he had not made a sound, which frightened her more than screaming would have. "I can go downstream. There's a bar two miles below where a man might wade if the tide's right. I cross, I come back up the far bank, and you walk past Corben clean, because without me there's nothing about you to stop."

"And when he searches you on the far bank?"

"I'll be a drowned man climbing out of a river. Nobody searches a corpse for letters."

"Nobody," Wren said, "except the man who's been hunting one for nine days."

Elias didn't answer that. The ferry groaned against its cable, taking on a load of loggers and one milk truck. The engine coughed again. Somewhere a gull made its idiot complaint, and the mist came down fine as flour sifting through the alders, and Wren understood with a strange calm that they were going to do this now, today, because tomorrow the syndicate would finish sweeping the coast and there would be no crossing at all, only checkpoints and telegraph lines and men like Corben standing under umbrellas at every door between here and Portland.

She looked at the packet. It lay in the bottom of her canvas satchel, wrapped in oilskin, forty-one pages in a dead man's handwriting—or a live man's handwriting wearing a dead man's name—and it weighed nothing. That was the terrible thing. It weighed less than the bread she'd bought in Halloway. It could burn in three seconds. It had already cost six people their lives, or so Elias believed, counting the ones who'd helped him and been bought or buried, and it weighed nothing at all.

*Courage is arithmetic,* her husband used to say, back when he was alive and she'd thought he meant it about the sea. You count what you're willing to lose, and then you count whether what you stand to keep is worth more, and then you step off anyway, because the sea doesn't care about your sums. She'd thought she understood him. She'd spent six years learning she hadn't.

"Wren." Elias touched her sleeve. "Say it back to me."

"The logbooks go in the basket with my other things. The letters stay home in the bindings. I'm a widow hauling her household south to her sister-in-law in Bandon because the light's being decommissioned and I can't afford the coast anymore. I'm sad. I'm tired. I talk about weather. I don't look at the deputy's face any longer than politeness requires." She recited it flatly, like a lighthouse log. *Wind southwest, moderate. Visibility poor. All well.*

"And if he takes you?"

"Then I've lost a day and my temper, and you've lost nothing, because the letters are in the bindings and the bindings are—" she lifted the satchel, felt the weight shift, the solid rectangular presence of three cloth-bound volumes she'd carried from Point Alder like relics—"in plain sight."

That had been her idea, in the end. Not Elias's. He'd wanted to sew the pages into his coat lining, into the heels of his boots, into every clever hidden place a hunted man could think of, and Wren had listened to him enumerate them and finally said, "Every hiding place is a confession. A man hiding something looks like a man hiding something." And then she'd shown him the logbooks.

Thomas Sandoval had kept three of them, besides the official light station logs—private ones, dense with tide tables and bird counts and, in the margins, the small furious observations of a man who watched the timber companies strip the hills and write it all down as progress. Wren had rebound them herself, years ago, after his death, with new spine boards and fresh linen tape, working by lamplight because her hands needed occupation her heart couldn't provide. Between the spine board and the sewing tapes of each volume, there was a channel. Not wide. Wide enough.

Forty-one pages, folded to the width of a pencil, sleeping inside the spines of a dead keeper's private books. Anyone who opened those books would find exactly what they expected: weather, birds, grief. The truth of August Hale bound into the machinery of Thomas Sandoval's mourning, and neither ghost the wiser.

"It's the best hiding place I've ever seen," Elias had told her that night in the millworker's attic, and he'd looked at her with an expression she was still deciding how to survive, "because it isn't hiding. It's inheritance. Nobody steals a widow's dead husband's logbooks."

Nobody, she hoped, except a widow.

* * *

They parted at the alder bend, where a fisherman's path dropped toward the shore road and the mist swallowed everything past twenty yards. No ceremony. They'd used up ceremony somewhere in the logged-off hills, on a night when she'd carried him half a mile with his arm over her shoulders and they had both stopped pretending, silently and mutually, that this was only about the mission. What remained between them now didn't have a name yet, and neither of them tried to give it one, because names were things you said to people you might not see again, and saying it would make that true.

"Two miles below, if the tide serves," Elias said. "If I'm not at the far landing by four o'clock—"

"You'll be there."

"If I'm not, you go on to Portland alone. You know the contacts. You know the order of the letters. You don't wait for me, Wren. Promise me you don't wait."

She looked at him—at the stitched elbow, the beard gone gray at the chin though he wasn't old, the eyes that had spent two years becoming very good at expecting the worst—and she lied to him, gently, the way you lie to a man standing at a door you may never see him walk back through.

"I promise," she said.

He held her gaze a moment longer, knowing, letting her keep the lie, and then he turned and went down the path and the mist took him the way the sea once had, and Wren stood alone in the alders with her satchel and her arithmetic.

She did the sums one last time.

What she was willing to lose: her freedom, possibly—a cell, a trial, maybe worse if Corben answered to harder men than himself. Her life, if it came to that, though she intended to make the price of it obscene. The logbooks, if someone pried. Elias, though she refused to enter him on the loss column; some entries you simply decline to make, and dare the ledger to object.

What she stood to keep: the truth, delivered. Thomas's name cleared of the lie that had killed him as surely as the snag in the river had—*operator error*, the coroner had ruled, *keeper negligence during storm conditions*, when every keeper on the coast knew the company had ordered the fog signal shut down to save coal money. Her sister-in-law's silence avenged. Her own silence, most of all, ended.

And the number came out smaller than her fear. That was the thing nobody told you about courage—that it wasn't the absence of fear but a sum that fear lost. She shouldered the satchel and walked down toward the ferry landing, and her legs shook, and she let them, because shaking legs still carry you.

* * *

The queue was short. Three loggers with bedrolls, a woman with a crate of chickens, a traveling dentist whose leather bag smelled of ether even in the open air. Wren joined the line with her head at the angle widows wear—down, patient, apologetic for occupying space—and studied the ground and let the others' voices wash over her.

"—closed the north camp Tuesday, just walked in and posted it—"

"—Corben himself, they say he turned back a state man last week—"

"Hush, you fool, he's ten feet away—"

The mist beaded on her collar. Ahead, the toll shed's green paint glistened. The ferry engine coughed its way through another crossing, bringing the morning load back, and the cable sang a low note in the wind that Wren felt in her back teeth, familiar as a mooring line humming.

Then it was her turn.

"Name and business." Corben's voice was mild. That was the first shock—not a bark but a ledger clerk's voice, unhurried, faintly apologetic, a voice that had said these words ten thousand times and expected to say them ten thousand more.

"Sandoval. Mrs. Thomas Sandoval." She let the name sit there. It was a true name, and true names were the best camouflage; she'd learned that from Elias. "Keeper's widow, Point Alder light. I'm going south to my husband's sister in Bandon. The station's being decommissioned in the spring and I've got nowhere to winter on the coast."

Something moved behind Corben's eyes—not suspicion exactly. Recognition of a category. *Widow, coast, decommissioned.* He'd processed a hundred of her in the last two years; the syndicate's sweep had emptied whole stretches of shoreline, and the displaced had flowed south like water finding its level, and each one had stood where she stood with the same tired story.

"Point Alder." He wrote it in a ledger that lay open on the shed's counter, protected from the damp by a sheet of oilcloth. His handwriting was small and neat. Evil as paperwork, Elias had said once, describing men like this, and Wren had hated him a little for how precisely he understood it. "That's a long walk from Point Alder, Mrs. Sandoval."

"Trucks most of the way. Walked the last bit. The roads are washed out past Halloway."

"They are that." He glanced up. For the first time he actually looked at her—not the category but the woman—and Wren gave him what she had rehearsed in the dark of a millworker's attic: the face of a woman who has cried herself out years ago and now runs on habit and duty, competent, closed, unremarkable. Grief worn smooth. She had six years of practice at that face. It was the one honest disguise she owned.

"Toll's a dollar. Search is a formality—bag on the table, please."

She set the satchel on the counter. Her hands did not shake. She noted this with distant interest, the way she noted the barometer falling.

Corben went through it with a workman's thoroughness and a bored man's speed. Bread, gone hard. A tin of tea. A change of clothes rolled tight. A woman's knife, small, which he examined and returned without comment. A coil of good line. And then the logbooks.

He lifted the top one and turned it over in his hands, and Wren watched him and thought: *count.* The ferry was loading. The loggers grumbled behind her. The mist hung. Somewhere downstream, if the tide served and the bar held, a man was wading into a cold river carrying nothing at all.

"Keeper's journals?" Corben asked.

"My husband's. Private ones. Weather, mostly." She heard her own voice say it, steady, almost indifferent, and marveled at the stranger producing it. "I couldn't leave them. Silly, probably. They're worth nothing to anyone but me."

Corben opened the first volume. His thumb moved through the pages—tide tables, bird counts, the marginalia of a dead man's indignation—and Wren stood in the rain-smelling morning and waited for the world to split open along a spine board.

But he was reading it as what it appeared to be, because it *was* what it appeared to be, that was the genius and the horror of it, the pages were real, Thomas's real grief and real fury, and a man flipping through a dead keeper's weather notes found exactly the sorrow he expected and nothing else. Corben's expression softened by one degree. She watched it happen. She watched a tired man hold another tired man's posthumous complaints about fog signals and coal rations, and she saw him decide, with the small mercy of the overworked, not to care.

"Fine writing," he said, and closed the book.

"Yes," Wren said. "It was."

He stacked the logbooks, returned them to the satchel, and pushed it across the counter. Then his hand paused on the ledger, pen hovering.

"Point Alder," he said again, slowly, as if consulting something internal rather than the page. "You wouldn't have seen anything unusual up that way, I suppose. Strangers. Wash-ups. We've had a notice out since autumn—a man wanted in connection with federal matters. Medium build, dark hair, scar through the left eyebrow. Travels light. Might be calling himself any number of names."

"Nothing but weather," Wren said. "Weather and gulls."

Corben nodded. He stamped her ledger line, slid the satchel back, and stepped aside with a gesture that was almost courteous. "Mind the gap on the barge. The boarding plank's been loose since October and the company keeps promising."

"Thank you, Deputy."

She picked up the satchel. It weighed nothing. Forty-one pages slept in the bindings, and the bindings slept in the bag, and the bag hung from her hand, and none of it weighed anything, and she walked toward the boarding plank feeling the extraordinary lightness of a woman who has counted everything she owns and found the total bearable.

Behind her, Corben called the next traveler forward. The dentist. The chickens. The loggers, grumbling about the loose plank.

The ferry took her out onto the gray water, the cable singing overhead, the far bank resolving slowly out of the mist—alder and fir and the mud-flat shine of the lower river, and no figure on the landing, not yet, because the tide wouldn't turn for hours and Elias was two miles downstream doing his own arithmetic with a cold river.

Wren stood at the rail with her dead husband's logbooks and watched the shore she was betraying slide away, and allowed herself, for exactly the length of the crossing, to feel what she felt.

* * *

The far landing was a plank platform on pilings, a shack, a sign: *UMPQUA FERRY — SOUTH BANK.* The ferryman took her dollar's worth of respect and no more. The loggers dispersed up the south road. The chicken woman argued with the ferryman about a return fare. The dentist lit a pipe and coughed.

Wren sat on her satchel at the end of the platform and waited, and the waiting was worse than the search had been, because searching had at least been motion. The mist thickened. Four o'clock approached. The river ran brown and patient, carrying its cargo of rain toward an ocean that kept everything given to it.

At ten past four, a heron lifted off the flats below the landing—startled, she thought, by nothing, by shadow—and Wren rose to her feet with her heart going like a lamp in wind.

Because there, upstream along the far bank, moving through the alders with the slow deliberate gait of a man whose stitches have held and whose luck has not yet run out, came a figure. Dark hair plastered flat. Medium build. Scar through the left eyebrow, visible even at fifty yards, even in the murk.

And behind him, up the road, a horse.

Not Corben's. Someone else's—a patrol rider, company-green slicker, and he had seen the figure too. Wren heard the shout come thin across the water, heard it repeat, and saw the man in the alders break from cover and run for the landing with the terrible practical speed of a man who has been running for two years and is very good at it.

Toward her. Toward the ferry platform. Toward the one place on the river where the letters were already safe in a widow's bag—and where the man who had carried them through fire was about to be taken on open ground, in full view, unless someone did something.

The ferryman was looking too, shading his eyes, reaching for the shotgun he kept racked inside the shack.

Wren counted—one breath, two—and stepped forward into the open, lifting her voice across the water with everything a lighthouse keeper's lungs had left in them.

"Here! *Here!* Over here—he's hurt—I need help!"

It wasn't even a lie. He was hurt. She just hadn't said by whom, or how badly, or that the help he needed was a woman with a satchel and a plan assembling itself out of wreckage at the speed of desperation—because down the south road, kicking up mist, came a second rider, and a third, and the afternoon had just become a question with a very short fuse.

The first rider reined in at the head of the landing, thirty yards away, and put his hand on something under his slicker, and called out in a voice made for carrying:

"Ma'am! Step away from that man!"
