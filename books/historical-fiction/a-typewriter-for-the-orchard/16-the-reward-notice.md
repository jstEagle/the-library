## 16. The Reward Notice

Deputy Corben had been standing in the rain at the River Ferry landing for two hours and forty minutes, and he had reached the stage of a stakeout where a man begins to have opinions about his own boots.

They were leaking. They had been leaking since Halloway. He'd bought them from the company store because they were three dollars cheaper than the ones in Coos Bay, and now he understood what the three dollars bought: a slow cold seep through the left heel that turned his whole foot into an argument he couldn't win.

He shifted his weight and looked down the gray line of the river.

The Umpqua ran wide and brown here after the storms, carrying whole trees sideways like matchsticks, and the ferry — the *Alder Queen*, flat-decked, chain-driven, older than the county — crossed on the half hour whether there was anything to carry or not. On the far bank stood the third checkpoint. His checkpoint. The syndicate's money had built it out of a tollbooth and two hired men and a sign reading VEHICLE INSPECTION — OREGON STATE AUTHORITY, which was a lie so comfortable it had practically become true.

Corben had a telegraph in his coat pocket. It had come up from Halloway the night before, relayed through three offices to keep it off any single wire, and it said, in the clipped grammar of men who didn't want to be quoted: *WOMAN AND MAN MATCHING NOTICE BOARDED SOUTHBOUND FROM HALLOWAY DISTRICT. TRAVELING IN FISH BUYER'S TRUCK. CONFIRM.*

Below it, folded twice, was Marta Reyes's statement, taken at the Point Alder landing nine days ago. She'd said she saw no one at the light. Then, forty minutes later, over coffee she hadn't touched, she'd said — offhand, looking out the window — that if a man were fool enough to come down the coast road this time of year, he'd be a fool enough to take the river ferry, because the coast route south of Tenmile was washed out and everyone knew it.

She hadn't seen anyone. But she knew which way they'd go.

That was Marta all over. That was half this coast, if he was honest. Nobody lied outright anymore; lying outright cost you your soul and usually your job. People just told you the shape of a thing and let you do the arithmetic yourself, so that whatever came of it was your own doing.

Corben had gotten very good at other people's arithmetic.

He heard the chain take up its slack across the water, the groan of the drive wheel, and the *Alder Queen* came wallowing out of the rain toward the north landing, riding low with a truck and four passengers and a smell of wet canvas and diesel.

He straightened. His foot screamed. He ignored it.

* * *

He watched her come down the ramp last.

The truck went first — a fish buyer's rig, mud to the windows, driven by a man who kept his cap brim low and his answers short, and who passed inspection with nothing more remarkable than a bad cough. Two loggers followed, arguing about wages. And then the woman.

She was maybe thirty-five, tall, with the kind of plainness that took a second look before it turned into something else — a face like weather, he thought, like a coastline you could read if you knew how. She carried a canvas satchel over one shoulder and a stack of leather-bound books strapped together with twine, held against her chest with both arms, the way a woman carries a Bible into church or a baby out of a fire.

Her papers were in order. He checked them twice because checking them twice was his job, and because the reward notice pinned inside the booth behind him — WOMAN APPROXIMATELY 35 YEARS, LIGHT BROWN HAIR, MAY BE TRAVELING WITH MALE COMPANION, CONSIDERED ARMED WITH INFORMATION — described a woman who would have exactly those papers, in exactly that condition, because whoever had made them was good.

"You're crossing alone?" he asked.

"I am."

"Coming from?"

"The north. You can see where I'm coming from, deputy. It's written on my boots."

It was. Mud to the knee. He almost smiled. "Purpose of travel?"

"My husband's papers. I'm taking them to my sister in Marshfield." She lifted the books slightly. "Estate matters."

He looked at the books then. He'd been looking past them, the way you learn to look past things in this work — past wedding rings, past children's drawings in truck windows, past the thousand small human shields people carry — but something about them caught him. They were ship's logbooks. He knew ship's logbooks the way he knew ledgers, which was to say intimately and without affection. Heavy board covers, cloth spines, brass corners gone green.

Except these corners weren't green. They were new. Recently rebound — recently enough that the fresh leather hadn't darkened yet, that the paste still showed pale at the seams, and that under the new binding, where the cover met the spine, he could see the ghost of older tooling. Stamped letters, mostly hidden by the rebind.

He couldn't read them. Not quite. Just the curve of one letter, a capital, ornate in a way that official stationery never was.

"Husband's name?" he asked.

"Sandoval. Tomas Sandoval. He kept the light at Point Alder." She said it steadily. Too steadily, maybe. Or maybe that was just grief, six years old and worn smooth as sea glass. He knew that texture too.

"Sandoval," Corben said. "The keeper's wife."

"That's right."

There was a notice for a woman matching her description, traveling south with a man matching another notice, carrying documents belonging to a dead senator. There was a keeper's widow from Point Alder standing in front of him with rebinding done within the month — done, he'd bet the mortgage on it, in the last eleven days — on books that had presumably sat on a shelf for six years waiting for someone to care about their covers.

And there was a promotion letter in his desk drawer in Coos Bay, unsigned, contingent, promising him the marshal's post and a salary that would retire the mortgage on the house Ruthie cried about at night because the roof leaked over the girls' room.

Corben looked at the river. A log the size of a schoolhouse went by, turning slowly, patient as everything else on this water.

"Step into the office a moment, Mrs. Sandoval," he said. "Just routine. The ferry's not due back across for twenty minutes anyway."

* * *

The office was a shed really, with a stove that smoked and a desk that had belonged to the toll era and a window fogged with breath and river damp. He hung her satchel on the hook by the door without asking — she let him, which told him something, or she didn't fight it hard enough to make a scene, which told him the same thing differently — and set the books on the desk between them like a card game about to begin.

"Mind if I look at these?"

"They're my husband's logs."

"They're rebound," he said. "Recently."

"People rebind old books."

"People rebind old books when the contents matter more than the cover." He turned one over in his hands. The tooling ghost again. That capital letter, curving like a signature. He'd seen that lettering before, years ago, on the flyleaves of ledgers in the Hale Timber Company office in North Bend, back when he was a young deputy doing inventory work nobody important bothered to supervise. Back when August Hale was alive and signing things.

August Hale, who had drowned with thirty-one other people when the *Cape Arago* went down in 1932. Whose death certificate Corben himself had filed a copy of, because the county coroner's office was shorthanded and paperwork flowed downhill.

Whose handwriting, according to a certain kind of rumor that traveled the coast in whispers and got quieter every month as the whisperers stopped whispering, had been appearing in letters. Letters that made claims. Letters that named names.

Corben set the book down.

"Mrs. Sandoval," he said, and heard his own voice change register — drop out of the official range and into something lower, more careful, almost gentle, and hated it a little even as he did it, "I'm going to tell you what I think is in these books. And then I'm going to make you an offer. And you should understand that the offer is real, because I don't enjoy this part and I only want to do it once."

She didn't answer. She watched him the way lighthouse people probably watch weather — not afraid, exactly. Calculating.

"I think there are letters in those bindings. I think they're in August Hale's hand. I think they say what happened to the *Cape Arago*, and why, and whose order it was, and how much it cost, and who got paid. I think a man has been carrying them down this coast since the winter of '32, and I think every person who helped him is either rich now or in the ground, and I think you picked up that burden somewhere around Halloway because you're the kind of woman who picks up burdens." He paused. "I think you're the most dangerous thing to come through this checkpoint since the syndicate built it, and I think you know it."

The stove ticked. Outside, the chain groaned as the ferry began loading for the return crossing, and neither of them moved.

"And if you're wrong?" she said finally.

"Then I've insulted a widow and wasted twenty minutes." He opened his desk drawer. Took out the telegram, unfolded it, laid it beside the books where she could read it upside down. Took out the reward notice — the internal one, the one that wasn't posted, the one with the figure at the bottom that had made him sit down when he first read it. Five hundred dollars. Plus consideration for permanent appointment.

He didn't slide it toward her. That was the trick of it, he'd decided. You didn't slide temptation across a table; that made people grab it or throw it. You just let it lie there in plain sight, in your own handwriting, and let them see that you were holding it loosely. That it was available.

"Here's the offer," he said. "You give me the letters. You walk out of this office free. No detention, no report, no name in anybody's file. As far as the record shows, a keeper's widow crossed the river with her husband's estate papers and a deputy found nothing worth noting. You go to Marshfield. You bury what needs burying. You live."

"And you?"

"And I turn the letters in. I get the appointment. Everybody eats." He leaned back. "That's the deal, Mrs. Sandoval. And I'll tell you the truth about it, since nobody else on this river will: it's a good deal for me and a bad deal for the truth, and I've lived with worse arithmetic than that for six years. So has everyone you've passed between here and Point Alder. Marta Reyes. The toll man at Tenmile. Half of Halloway. This coast runs on deals like this the way that ferry runs on that chain. You can hate it. I do, some mornings. But hating it doesn't stop the wheel."

Something moved behind her eyes at Marta's name — a flinch, quickly governed — and he noted it and filed it and felt the small familiar sickness of a man who has just confirmed a betrayal by watching it land.

"You'd be giving up the story," Wren Sandoval said quietly. "Not just the letters. The story. The one where somebody finally says it out loud in a room with a flag on the wall."

"I'd be keeping my house," Corben said. "I've got two girls. Eight and five. Their mother works the cannery line when they'll take her, which is fewer months every year. You want to know what the syndicate owns on this coast? It doesn't own the mills first. It owns the pay envelopes, and the pay envelopes own everything else. I've got a mortgage with a bank that Hale Timber built, and a roof that leaks, and I am so tired, Mrs. Sandoval. I am tired in a way sleep doesn't touch." He heard his voice go rough and made himself stop. "So. The letters. And you walk."

* * *

She looked at the books for a long time.

He watched her hands — they were strong hands, rope-callused, a working woman's hands — resting on the twine binding, and he thought: she's counting. Whatever she keeps in there, she's weighing it the way you weigh cargo before a crossing. What it costs. What it's worth. Whether the water's too high.

And he thought, with a clarity that arrived like cold water: *she's going to refuse.* He could see it forming in her the way you see a squall forming over open water, still far off but already certain, and something in him — some part he'd been feeding scraps to for six years, the part that had joined the sheriff's office at nineteen because he'd believed a badge meant something — that part leaned forward and wanted her to refuse, and hated itself for wanting it, because if she refused, then he had to arrest her, and if he arrested her, then the men in North Bend would send someone else to handle the questioning, someone who wouldn't offer tea first, and this tired woman with her dead husband's books would go into a room he wouldn't be allowed to follow her into.

"Before you answer," he said, and his voice surprised him by being quiet, "you should know that if you say no, I don't get to be the one who decides what happens next. That's the part they don't put in the recruitment poster. A deputy thinks he's the law out here, and then one day he finds out he's just a hinge. The door swings whether I like it or not."

"Then swing it," Wren Sandoval said.

She untied the twine.

For one heartbeat — long enough for Corben to understand, later, that he had misread everything — he thought she was surrendering. Her hands moved to the top book's binding, found the seam of the fresh leather, and began, deliberately, to peel it back.

And underneath, where the cover gaped open along the spine, he saw paper. Dozens of pages. Folded once, pressed flat, sewn into the hollow of the rebinding like a wasp's nest inside a wall — Hale's unmistakable loops marching down the visible margin, dense and damning, page after page after page, far more than one copy, far more than one life's worth of risk.

She wasn't opening it to hand it over.

She was showing him what he'd be taking. Showing him the size of it. Showing him that burning it or burying it wouldn't kill the story, only delay it — because a woman who would rebind her dead husband's logbooks into a smuggler's false cover was a woman who had already made copies in her head, or allies on the road, or both.

"There are eleven days of testimony in these bindings, Deputy," she said, "and every name in them is a name you already know, because they sign your paycheck's paycheck. You can take them to North Bend and collect your marshal's post. And in a year, maybe two, one of those names will decide you're a loose end — because that's what happens to hinges, Mr. Corben. Doors wear them out." She pushed the book gently across the desk toward him. Toward *him*. An offering. A dare. "Or there's a hearing coming. In Portland. Federal men. And when it comes, they're going to need one honest witness from this coast who isn't already bought — and I don't mean honest like pure. I mean honest like tired. Honest like a man who knows exactly what the deal costs, because he's been paying it."

Outside, the ferry bell rang the half hour. The ramp came down onto the planking with a knock that sounded, in the small smoky office, very much like a door closing.

Corben looked at the book. At the letters. At the woman who had just offered him, in one motion, both halves of his life: the promotion and the exit.

His hand moved toward the book —

— and stopped, because through the fogged window, out on the landing, a man had just stepped out of the tree line at the edge of the lot. Soaked to the ribs, limping badly, one arm cradled against his chest, moving with the particular desperate calm of someone who has walked eleven miles of bad road and has decided to stop running.

Wren Sandoval saw him too. Corben watched her face break open — just for a second, just a crack, before she sealed it — and he understood, with the sick clarity of a man watching his own arithmetic collapse, that the companion from the notice had not circled downstream to escape.

He'd come back for her.

The man from the water raised his empty hands, stepped into the open, and said, clearly, in a voice pitched to carry into the office:

"Deputy. You're looking for me. Let the woman cross."
