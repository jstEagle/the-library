## 17. Burn It or Be Burned

The ferry office smelled of cold stove iron and old tobacco, and the lock on the door was the kind that clicked rather than slammed—a small courtesy, as if Corben believed a polite prison was somehow less of one.

Wren stood at the center of the room and took inventory.

It was an old habit, inventorying. Six years at Point Alder had taught her that survival began with knowing exactly what you had: three gallons of lamp oil, half a cord of split spruce, one spare mantle, four cans of beans gone soft at the seams. You counted because counting kept panic from doing your arithmetic for you.

So she counted now.

One desk, scarred oak, bolted to the floor. One stove, black and banked low, a kettle of water ticking on its plate. One window, fogged with river damp, looking out on the ferry landing where Corben's men moved crates with the unhurried confidence of people who owned everything they touched. One chair. One door, locked.

And in her hands, wrapped in oilcloth against her chest like a second heart, the logbooks of Thomas Sandoval.

She set them on the desk and unwrapped the binding slowly. Beneath the canvas cover of each volume, sewn into the spine where no clerk would ever think to look, the letters lay flat and patient—two years of August Hale's handwriting, carried down a coast full of men paid to make sure it never arrived. Eleven letters. She'd counted them so many times the number had worn smooth, like a stone in her pocket. Nine remained. Two had been left behind in Halloway Mill, tucked into the hymnal rack of a church that sang from company-owned paper, entrusted to a millworker named Odette Brand who had looked at Wren across her own kitchen table and said, *I was obedient for thirty years. I can be disobedient once.*

Nine letters. One stove.

Wren crouched before the stove and opened its grate. The coals beneath were gray-rimmed but alive; Corben kept a warm office, a man who liked his small comforts. She fed in two sticks of kindling from the basket by the wall and watched them catch, then closed the grate and stood, wiping her hands on her coat as though she'd done nothing more remarkable than light a lamp.

Her hands were steady. That surprised her. All her life her hands had trembled at the wrong moments—at her sister's funeral, at the coroner's hearing where men in good suits explained that her brother-in-law's death on the logging line had been his own carelessness, his own fault, nothing anyone could have prevented, certainly not the frayed cable everyone on the crew knew about and no one reported. Her hands had shaken then. Now, when it mattered most, they were still.

Maybe that was what happened when you finally stopped being afraid of the wrong thing.

* * *

Corben came back within the hour.

He unlocked the door himself, which told her there were no other guards close enough to trust with the key—or that he trusted no one else with it. He was carrying two cups of coffee, and he set one on the desk near her hand without ceremony, without asking, the way a man does when kindness has become a tool he uses without thinking about whether it's honest.

"Mrs. Sandoval." He settled into the chair behind the desk—not behind it, exactly; he sat sideways, angled toward her, a man performing informality. "I want to say again that nobody here means you any harm."

"You've locked me in a room."

"A room with coffee." He almost smiled. It was a tired face, she thought. Not a cruel one. There was a mortgage somewhere behind those eyes, a sick mother or a son in college or just the plain grinding weight of being forty-five years old and owned by other men—and he had made his peace with paying that weight off in installments of small betrayals. She'd seen his kind before, at the coroner's hearing, at every hearing where the truth was priced out of reach. They weren't monsters. That was the whole trouble with them. Monsters you could hate cleanly.

"Deputy Corben," she said. "Do you know what's in these books?"

"I know what I'm told is in them."

"And what are you told?"

He turned his cup a slow quarter-turn on the desk. "That a dead man's papers are stolen property. That returning them is worth a promotion I've been passed over for twice." He said it flatly, without shame, and she understood that this was part of his method too—to show her his price, to make the transaction feel clean. "You walk out of here tonight. The books stay with me. Nobody follows you. By morning you're a woman who lost some luggage on a river crossing, and by next month nobody remembers your name."

"You'd be surprised how hard I worked to earn that," Wren said. "Being forgotten."

Something flickered across his face—recognition, maybe, the look of a man meeting someone whose loneliness rhymed with his own. He covered it with a sip of coffee.

"It's a good offer, Mrs. Sandoval. Better than you'll get from whoever sent you. Those people—" he nodded vaguely southward, toward Portland, toward money, "—they don't leave loose ends. You've read enough newspapers to know how this goes."

"I have read the newspapers," Wren agreed. "I've also read the letters."

His eyes came up sharply.

"Yes," she said. "I've read them. Every page. So let me tell you what you're buying with your promotion." She laid her hand flat on the top logbook. "In here is a sworn account of who ordered the ferry *Maiden of Coos* scuttled in November of 1932. In here is the name of the man who paid the coroner in Tillamook County eleven hundred dollars to write *accidental drowning* over what was murder. In here are copies of telegrams between August Hale and a United States marshal, telegrams the syndicate never found, describing the shipment schedule of cut timber taken from land Hale held title to—land they faked his death to take. And in here—" she tapped the bottom book, the oldest, its canvas cover soft as cloth from handling, "—is a list. Fourteen names. Men who helped Hale hide the truth, and what was done to each of them. Three drowned. One burned. One went into an asylum in Salem and doesn't speak anymore. Two simply vanished, the way men do when a company owns the sheriff and the newspaper and the ferry crossing."

She watched him hear it. Watched the tired arithmetic move behind his eyes—the promotion on one side of the scale, and on the other side fourteen names, and his own name not yet among them but the space reserved for it, blank, waiting.

"They'll say it's forgery," Corben said finally. But his voice had gone quieter.

"Some of it will be proven. Enough of it." She stood, and the movement made him stand too, and for a moment they faced each other across the desk like two people on opposite shores of something. "You asked me to trade you the truth for my freedom. I'm going to offer you a different trade."

"I'm listening."

"The stove is lit."

He glanced at it. The kettle ticked. The fire behind the grate breathed its small orange breath, and she saw the moment he understood—not fully, not yet, but the shape of it moving toward him like weather.

"Those books are sewn through the spines," Wren said. "Canvas and thread and ninety-year-old glue. Do you know how fast old glue lets go near heat? A page comes free in under a minute. A letter in five. I've been sitting alone in this room for an hour, Deputy. I've had time to practice."

"That would destroy evidence—"

"Evidence of murder. Evidence that would clear a dead man's name and hang the living ones." She lifted the top logbook and held it, not theatrically, not with any flourish—just held it, the way you hold something you have already decided about. "Here is my trade. You unlock that door, you put me on the next crossing south, and you report that the woman and her husband's logbooks passed your checkpoint clean. You keep your job. You keep your mortgage paid. And in a month, when the hearings start—and they will start, Deputy, because there are nine letters and I only need one to survive—you testify honestly about everything you know. Not heroically. Honestly. The way a tired man does when he finally decides he'd like to sleep."

"And if I refuse?"

Wren walked to the stove.

She didn't hurry. Hurrying would have made it drama, and drama was a thing performed for an audience, and she was done performing. She knelt, opened the grate, and slid the first logbook halfway into the firebox, holding it by the binding so that only the lower corner met the heat. The canvas began to brown. The smell of scorching glue rose thin and sharp, like a struck match remembered.

"Wren." Corben's voice cracked on the name, just slightly. "Stop. Think about what—"

"I spent six years thinking." The corner of the book glowed at its edge, a small red seam opening along the canvas. "My husband died keeping a light for strangers, and I thought my grief was private property, something to be stored and dusted and never spent. My brother-in-law died under a frayed cable and I sat in a hearing room and said nothing because silence felt safe, and I have learned, Deputy Corben, that silence is never safe. Silence is just a slower way of burning." She fed the book another inch into the firebox. The first page curled. "So no. I won't stop thinking. I'm thinking very clearly right now. I am thinking that you have perhaps thirty seconds to decide which half of this transaction you'd rather lose—the evidence, or the deal."

* * *

What she did not tell him—what she kept in the same locked room where she kept everything, all these years—was that her hands were not entirely steady after all. That some part of her was screaming at the cost of it, at the idea of nine letters becoming eight, eight becoming seven, history going up in smoke one page at a time because she'd run out of better options.

But that was the trick of it, she realized, kneeling at the stove while a deputy's career balanced on her wrist. Power had never once asked her permission. It had simply arranged the world around her—men who owned the rain, the roads, the ferries, the words printed under photographs of drowned senators—and expected her to live inside the arrangement quietly. Well. If power could arrange things, so could she. If they had taught her anything, all these years of being overlooked, it was this: nobody guards the invisible woman. Nobody counts her pages. Nobody imagines that the quiet keeper of a lighthouse on a storm-battered coast has learned, better than any of them, exactly what fire is and how to feed it.

The first letter caught. Hale's handwriting—those unmistakable loops her sister had learned at his elbow, decades ago, back when the senator was merely a decent man with beautiful penmanship—curled and blackened and went up the flue in sparks.

Wren did not flinch. She counted instead. Eight.

"Three seconds, Deputy."

"For God's sake—" Corben crossed the room in two strides, and she braced herself for his hand on her arm, for the whole balance of it tipping the other way—but he reached past her, wrenched open the grate, and dragged the burning logbook out onto the iron plate, stamping the flames with the flat of his palm wrapped in his coat sleeve. He swore, low and continuous, a man cursing not at her but at the shape of his own life. When the smoking ruin of the cover lay still, he straightened, cradling his scorched hand, and looked at her with something that was not quite anger and not quite awe.

"You'd have done it," he said. "You'd have burned all of it."

"Every page." She rose from the hearth, ash on her knees, and met his eyes. "Now we do this my way. Door unlocked. Crossing south. And your testimony in a month, or I mail what remains of these letters to every newspaper on the coast with your name in the covering note—both names, yours and mine, keeper and deputy, the two people who tried to get the truth to Portland and got stopped at a river checkpoint by men working for the syndicate. Think how that reads, Corben. Think whose side of that story gets printed."

He stared at her for a long moment. Outside the fogged window, the ferry landing lights burned yellow through the river mist, and somewhere a bell buoy clanged its slow, patient count.

Then Corben laughed—one dry, exhausted note, the laugh of a man watching his own ledger catch fire and finding, to his astonishment, that he didn't entirely mind. "Six years tending a light," he said, shaking his head, reaching past her for the door key hanging on its nail. "They should have checked what kind of woman keeps a light."

He fitted the key to the lock.

And it was then—just as the tumblers gave, just as the door swung inward on the yellow lamplight of the landing—that Wren saw, beyond his shoulder, out on the dark water below the dock, a figure rise dripping from the river rocks. Broad-shouldered, unsteady, unmistakable. A man who was supposed to be dead, standing in the open where every lantern on the landing could find him, lifting his arms deliberately, calling out in a voice pitched to carry:

"My name is Elias Vane—and I'm the man you're hunting!"
