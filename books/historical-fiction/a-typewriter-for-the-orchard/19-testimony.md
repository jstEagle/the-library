## 19. Testimony

The federal courthouse in Portland smelled of floor wax and old radiators, and Wren Sandoval decided within the first five minutes that it was the most frightening room she had ever stood in — worse than the ferry office with Corben's kind voice and his terrible arithmetic, worse than the storm that had given her Elias.

At least the storm had been honest about wanting to kill her.

The hearing room was long and gray-walled, built for men in wool suits to say important things quietly into microphones that looked like brass flowers. Rows of folding chairs faced a raised bench where three examiners sat behind a rail of dark oak, taking notes in hands that never hurried. A stenographer sat at the front with a machine that clicked like a typewriter learning to swim. Along one wall, windows gave onto a wet Portland street where traffic moved without any knowledge at all of what was being decided above it.

That was the thing about history, Wren thought. It happened in rooms like this, and the street outside never once looked up.

Elias sat beside her in a borrowed suit that fit him badly across the shoulders. Two weeks of rest had put flesh back on him, but he still held himself the way a man holds himself when he has spent two years being a rumor — slightly forward on the chair, weight on the balls of his feet, ready to be told to run. His hands lay flat on his knees. The oilskin packet, or what remained of it, was already in evidence, tagged and photographed and carried off by a clerk who treated it with less reverence than he'd have shown a lunch order.

Half the letters had survived the stove. Wren had counted them a hundred times since: thirty-one pages pulled smoking from Corben's grate by a federal marshal who'd arrived four hours too late to matter and just in time to matter completely. Thirty-one pages out of what Elias swore had been ninety. The rest were ash in a stove on the Umpqua River ferry, and no one would ever know what those dead pages had said, and that loss sat in Wren's chest like a swallowed stone.

Thirty-one pages were enough. That was the miracle nobody in this room seemed to recognize as a miracle. They were enough because August Hale had written like a man building a wall — each letter load-bearing, each fact set against the next fact so that even a partial wall still stood.

"Mrs. Sandoval." The examiner in the middle — a narrow man named Ostrander with spectacles that magnified his eyes to something owl-like and gentle — said her name the way you'd knock on a door. "You've been sworn. Do you understand you may decline to answer anything that might incriminate you?"

"I understand it," Wren said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "I don't expect to use it."

A rustle went through the benches behind her. She didn't turn to look at it. She'd spent six years talking aloud to an empty gallery at Point Alder; a room full of strangers was nothing. She'd logged fog for six years. She could log this.

Ostrander consulted a folder. "For the record, state your occupation."

"Lighthouse keeper. Point Alder station, Curry County." A pause she couldn't help. "Formerly."

"And how did you come into possession of the documents marked Exhibits One through Eleven?"

"A man washed up below my north bluff after the January storm." She heard herself say it flatly, the way she'd have entered it in a logbook. *Man sighted in kelp line, breathing. Taken indoors.* "He was carrying them."

"The man known as Elias Vane."

"That's the name he uses."

"It is not the name on his birth registration."

"No," Wren said. "I imagine it isn't."

She could feel Elias beside her, not moving, not looking at her, holding very still in the way of a man letting someone else take fire for him. She kept her eyes on Ostrander.

"Describe the condition of the documents when you first saw them."

"Wrapped in oilskin. Dry. He'd protected them better than he'd protected himself." She let that sit. "They were letters. In the handwriting of Senator August Hale."

"You knew the senator's handwriting."

Wren thought about how to answer that honestly. There was no way to answer it briefly.

"When I was fourteen," she said, "August Hale came through our town on the campaign circuit, and my sister wanted to be a secretary more than she wanted to breathe. He stayed for dinner and spent the evening teaching her penmanship at our kitchen table. I watched. I copied every loop he made." She folded her hands. "Six years later I was filing shipping manifests for a cannery, and half of them carried his signature. I saw that handwriting four hundred times. It has a particular way of crossing its T's — low, like it's in a hurry to get somewhere. Dead men don't write letters, Mr. Ostrander. But if they did, they'd cross their T's exactly like that."

Someone in the gallery laughed — one short breath of it, quickly strangled. Ostrander didn't smile.

"Exhibit Four," he said. "Read the third paragraph, please."

The marshal brought her the letter. The paper had been flattened under glass, but the creases from two years of travel were still visible in it, roads drawn in the paper itself. Wren read aloud, slowly, because she understood now that slowness was a form of weight:

*"On the night of October 9th, 1932, I was taken from the *Cascadia Queen* by boat before she cleared the bar, against my will and with my knowledge of why. The men who took me were employed by the Aldercrest Timber Company. I name them: R. J. Calloway, vice president; Thomas Merrick, head of security; and the county coroner, Dr. Emil Foss, who was paid nine hundred dollars on October 11th to certify my death by drowning. The ferry was scuttled with eleven souls aboard. Their deaths are mine to carry. This document exists so that they do not go unwitnessed."*

The stenographer's machine clattered through it. Nobody spoke. Somewhere behind Wren, a man coughed once and then made himself very quiet, the way people do when they realize the room has become a place where coughing might be remembered.

"Thank you," Ostrander said. "No further questions from the panel at this time. Mr. Vane — or shall we proceed under that name?"

"Elias is fine," Elias said. "It's the only one I've earned lately."

* * *

Elias had rehearsed nothing. That was the decision he'd made on the train north, watching the rain run sideways down the window while Wren slept against his shoulder with her mouth open like a tired child, and he had stuck to it: he would not rehearse. Rehearsed testimony sounded like rehearsal, and the whole architecture of the syndicate's defense was that everything was fabrication, everything was forgery, everything was a drowned clerk's fantasy. The truth had to come out of him crooked and unpolished or it wouldn't come out at all.

So he sat in the witness chair and let Ostrander walk him backward through two years, and he answered in the plain words he had, and when his voice shook he let it shake.

He told them about the night of the ninth. About being Hale's secretary for six years and knowing, by the end of the last one, that something had gone rotten in the timber accounts — that the Aldercrest syndicate was borrowing against stumpage that didn't exist, that the senator's investigation was going to land on them like a felled tree, and that someone in the senator's own office had been selling his schedule to Calloway's men for months.

He told them about the launch that came alongside the *Cascadia Queen* in the dark, and the two men who put a coat over Hale's head and walked him down as if he were drunk, and the hand on Elias's own collar, and the moment — he had never told anyone this, not even Wren, not entirely — when the man holding him had simply let go, turned away, and pretended Elias wasn't there. Someone had been paid to lose him. He had never learned who, and he had stopped, mostly, trying to deserve the mercy.

"I swam for the beach at Cape Arago," he said. "The ferry went down an hour later with the night watchman aboard and ten passengers who'd bought tickets that afternoon. Dr. Foss certified the senator drowned. There wasn't a body. There didn't need to be. The senator was alive and knew it, and a living senator who surfaces is worth more to these men as leverage than as a corpse, so they kept him. First at a lodge on the Deschutes. Then, when he started writing letters, somewhere he couldn't leave."

"How did he write letters if he was held?" Ostrander asked.

"Badly guarded men get careless," Elias said. "And paper is small. He wrote them over eighteen months and passed them out through laundry boys, hunters, one deputy sheriff who lasted four days before he sold his copy back to Calloway and then disappeared into a job in Idaho. I collected them. I copied the critical ones. I moved them town to town along the coast because the coast is the only place I know, and everyone who held them longer than a week was bought or buried." He looked at his hands. "There were nine of them. Helpers. I can give you names, and I'd like the record to show I'm giving them, because four of them have graves and five of them have mortgages and all of them deserve to be more than footnotes."

Ostrander nodded to the stenographer. "Names will be dictated."

Then Ostrander asked the question, the one Elias had known was coming since Portland, the one he'd watched Wren brace for on the train without either of them saying it aloud:

"Mr. Vane. If the letters are genuine, the man who wrote them is alive. Where is Senator August Hale?"

The hearing room went so quiet Elias could hear the radiator ticking.

"He'll tell you himself," Elias said. "He insisted on coming. He's been in a federal safehouse outside Salem for nine days. I'd ask you to remember, when he walks in, that the last time most of you saw him he was fifty-one years old, and that two years in a locked room ages a man faster than twenty free ones. Whatever you see — don't mistake it for weakness, and don't mistake it for a trick. It's just what they did to him."

Ostrander removed his spectacles, polished them on his tie, and put them back on. "Marshal," he said, "bring him in."

* * *

Wren had prepared herself for a ghost. What came through the door was worse and better than a ghost — it was a man.

August Hale came down the aisle between the benches at the pace of someone who had learned to measure floors by how far it hurt to cross them. He was thin in the way wood gets thin when termites have been at it a while, hollow through the cheeks, white to the ears where there had certainly been color two years ago. But the suit fit him, someone had shaved him with kindness, and he walked unassisted, and his eyes — Wren recognized the eyes immediately, the same quick, impatient eyes that had bent over her sister's penmanship exercises and said *no, no, the tail goes under, ambition lives under the line* — those eyes went around the room once, cataloging it, and Wren understood that whatever else they had broken in him, they had not broken the habit of taking inventory.

He reached the witness chair. He sat. The clerk administered the oath, and Hale raised his right hand, and his voice when he swore was rusty but level.

"State your name for the record."

"August Reed Hale." He paused. "Formerly, according to the State of Oregon and the County of Coos, deceased."

The laugh that went through the gallery this time was bigger and no one tried to stop it, and Ostrander let it live for three seconds before he rapped the bench with a knuckle.

"Senator. For the benefit of the panel: confirm your identity."

"Gladly. Ask me anything a stranger wouldn't know."

Ostrander smiled for the first time all morning. "What did you eat on the night of October eighth, 1932, aboard the *Cascadia Queen*?"

"Halibut, badly cooked, which I complained about to the steward, whose name was Ostrowski, and whom I tipped anyway, because bad halibut was not his fault. My secretary ate none of it. He was seasick in the head, which I teased him about cruelly, and which saved his life, though I didn't learn that part until later." Hale glanced toward Elias, and something passed between the two men that had no language in it at all. "Next question. You'll want to verify me thoroughly. Take your time. I've waited two years to be difficult."

They verified him for forty minutes. Childhood neighbors, college roommates, a vote he'd cast in 1918, the combination to a Senate desk drawer, the middle name of his first wife. He answered everything, some of it with dry humor, none of it with hesitation. And through all of it Wren watched the back row of the gallery, where the lawyers sat.

Because the lawyers had come. Of course they had come. Three of them, gray suits, gray faces, representing interests the subpoenas had dragged blinking into the light: Aldercrest Timber, the estate of R. J. Calloway — dead of a self-inflicted gunshot in November, the newspapers had been very delicate about it — and the law firm of Hobb & Prentiss, which had handled the syndicate's paper for twenty years. They sat with their files squared and their pens aligned, and they had spent the morning wearing the expressions of men watching weather they had confidently predicted fail to arrive.

When Hale finished verifying himself, the senior counsel for Aldercrest rose — a heavy man named Prentiss himself, silver-haired, famous for never raising his voice — and said, "The panel will forgive me. But this entire proceeding rests on documents of contested provenance, produced by a man living under an assumed name and a witness with no standing in these matters. Handwriting can be forged. Memory can be coached. With respect, we intend to challenge—"

"Exhibit Seven," Hale said.

Prentiss stopped.

"Exhibit Seven is a letter dated March of 1933," Hale continued, in the tone of a man reading a grocery list. "In it I describe a meeting in the Aldercrest offices in which your firm's junior partner, Mr. Dellwood, drafted the trust instruments used to move the ferry insurance proceeds into a shell company. I describe the wallpaper of the room — green stripes, water stain in the northeast corner — the brand of cigar on the table, and the exact figure discussed. Nine hundred thousand dollars. I was in that room because they wanted me to sign, and I refused, and that refusal is the reason I spent nineteen months in a farmhouse outside Drain with barred windows." He looked directly at Prentiss, and his ruined voice found a register it hadn't had all day. "Your junior partner has already been interviewed by the marshals, counselor. He talked for six hours. Would you like to hear what he says about your instructions to him, or would you prefer to withdraw and let your client answer for itself?"

Prentiss sat down. It was not dramatic. That was the terrible thing, Wren thought. He simply gathered his pen, squared his file, and sat down, and something in the set of his shoulders admitted everything, the way a man's shoulders admit defeat before his mouth does.

After that it moved fast, the way ice goes when it finally decides to go. The second examiner read into the record the affidavit of the former junior partner. The third read a telegram log from the Halloway Mill office, subpoenaed that week, showing payments to Deputy Corben — payments Corben himself, in a signed statement delivered from Coos Bay, had itemized voluntarily, date by date, mortgage payment by mortgage payment, in handwriting that got smaller and harder as it went, as though writing it down cost him physically and he was trying to spend as little of himself as possible. Marta Reyes had come in the day before, Wren knew; she'd testified an hour and walked out onto the courthouse steps and lit a cigarette with shaking hands and said, "Your husband lent me my boat, Wren. It was time the loan came due."

One by one, the names went into the record. Calloway, dead and beyond reach. Merrick, in custody in Eugene, talking. Dr. Foss, the coroner, surrendered his license that morning by letter and had not been seen since, and there were warrants. The shell companies, the insurance fraud, the eleven dead on the *Cascadia Queen* — eleven names read aloud by a clerk in a monotone, and Wren found she was gripping Elias's hand hard enough to hurt them both, and that he was gripping back, and neither of them let go.

By four o'clock it was over, adjourned sine die pending grand jury action, and the gray lawyers left first, fast, files clutched, scattering down the marble corridor like birds flushed out of a hedgerow — startled, yes, but also, Wren thought, finally doing the thing birds do, which is going where the wind tells them.

Hale stood at the front of the emptying room, leaning on the rail, looking older than the hour warranted and lighter than any man of his age had a right to look. When Elias and Wren reached him he took both of Wren's hands in his two cold ones.

"Mrs. Sandoval." His eyes crinkled. "You crossed your T's exactly right, reading that letter. Low and in a hurry. My God, you're the penmanship girl's sister. How is Ruth?"

Wren opened her mouth. Closed it. There was no way to say *my sister died in '29* quickly, and no way to say it slowly here, and Hale saw all of that happen on her face in about a second and a half, and his grip tightened, and he said, softly, "Ah. I'm sorry. Another debt for the ledger."

"There's a lot in that ledger," Elias said.

"There is," Hale agreed. He straightened, and looked at the two of them together — the drowned clerk and the lighthouse keeper, salt still somehow in both of them despite the suits and the train and the city — and something almost mischievous moved through the ruin of his face. "Which brings me to a matter I've been saving. There's a typewriter."

Wren blinked. "A what?"

"My typewriter. The Underwood from my Salem office. They seized everything when they declared me dead — sold the contents of the office at auction like a dead man's socks. Everything except the papers, which conveniently vanished into Aldercrest's custody, and the machine, which apparently nobody wanted." He smiled, and for a moment he was the man at the kitchen table again, correcting a girl's loops. "It's in a storage auction lot downtown, going on the block Saturday morning. I'd buy it myself, but a dead man bidding on his own typewriter would raise questions I've only just finished answering." He looked pointedly at Elias. "Some things ought to be written on the machine that wrote the first draft. Don't you think?"

Elias looked at Wren. Wren looked out the tall window at the wet street, where the traffic went on without knowing, and thought about a dark lighthouse on a storm-battered bluff, and a lamp that needed relighting, and a house that had held one keeper for six years and might, if she were brave in an entirely new direction, hold two.

"Saturday," she said. "We'll be there."

Hale nodded once, satisfied, and turned to go — and then stopped, and looked back at them with the expression of a man adding a final line to a letter he'd thought was finished.

"One more thing," he said. "The grand jury convenes Monday. And the first witness they've subpoenaed — the one whose statement broke Prentiss open downstairs an hour ago — has asked, formally, that a certain lighthouse keeper be present when he testifies." He tucked his stick under his arm. "Seems the man from the water believes he owes you a drowning. He intends to start repaying it in public."
