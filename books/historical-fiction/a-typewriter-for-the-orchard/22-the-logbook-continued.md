## 22. The Logbook, Continued

The storm came back on the anniversary of the ferry sinking, which Wren Sandoval chose not to believe was a coincidence and also chose not to say aloud.

It arrived the way the big ones always did at Point Alder—not all at once but in installments, like a bill collector with patience. First the swell, long and green and wrong, rolling in from a place past the horizon where something was gathering its strength. Then the barometer falling so fast Marta Reyes called it "a cliff with weather attached" over the radio, laughing in that way she laughed when she meant *batten down*. Then the wind, out of the southeast, carrying salt so heavy it frosted the windows on the landward side of the cottage.

Wren had been waiting for it since September. She'd cleaned the lens twice, re-waxed every gasket, oiled the clockwork until the rotation mechanism ran so smooth it made almost no sound at all—a whisper instead of a groan, the light turning like a thing thinking rather than a thing laboring. She'd laid in lamp oil enough for three weeks of fog, split wood enough for two, and baked bread enough for four days because Elias baked when he was nervous and the storm made him nervous even now, even after everything.

"You're doing the thing," he'd said that morning, watching her check the oil reservoir for the fourth time.

"I'm doing my job."

"You're doing the thing where you do your job like the job is going to leave you." He'd said it gently. He'd earned the right to say things gently; it had taken him most of a year to learn how. "The light held through the last one. It'll hold through this one."

"The last one wasn't a December blow."

"No," he agreed, and didn't argue further, because we both knew what I wasn't saying: the last great storm to hit Point Alder had taken the *Corvidae* off the rocks south of the light, and two years before that, another December blow had drowned thirty-one people on a ferry whose sinking had not been an accident at all. The sea kept its own ledger. We were only ever borrowing against it.

Now it was night, and the storm had arrived in full, and I sat in the lantern room with the wind trying to take the roof apart above me while the beam turned beneath me, steady as a heartbeat, answering no one and everyone.

I had learned, in one year, to love the sound of it. Not the storm—the storm I tolerated, respected, kept accounts with—but the mechanism. The clockwork's whisper. The lamp's white hiss. The slow metronome sweep of glass across water, glass across water, the oldest sentence in the world saying the same true thing over and over: *here. here. here.*

A year ago I would have told you that sentence was addressed to ships. A year ago I believed most true things were addressed elsewhere—to other people, other lives, other histories. I had spent six years at Point Alder believing history happened in Portland hearing rooms and Halloway Mill offices and the cabins of ferries, and that my job was simply to keep a small machine running at the edge of it, uninvolved, untouched, safe.

The hearing room in Portland had cured me of that particular comfort. I had sat in the third row while a ruined man with a dead man's name read testimony into the record in a voice like paper tearing, and I had understood—finally, fully—that there was no edge of history. There was only the middle of it, and you were always standing somewhere in the middle, and the only question was whether you would be one of the people who wrote it down or one of the people it was written about.

I wrote it down now. Every night, without fail, in a hand I had finally made peace with.

*

At eleven forty the main lamp flickered.

Not much. A stutter, half a second, the kind of thing you could miss if you were dozing or reading or letting yourself believe the night was ordinary. But I had spent six years learning this light the way you learn a face, and a half-second stutter from the mantle was the same as a stranger clearing their throat in a dark room.

I was up the stairs before the thought finished forming.

The lantern room was cold in a way it shouldn't have been. The draft guard on the seaward port had worked loose—one screw backed out a quarter turn, just enough—and December was pushing its fingers through the gap, worrying the flame. I put my thumb and forefinger on the screw and turned it home, then checked the other three, then stood a moment with my palm flat against the glass while the wind leaned on the whole structure like a drunk leaning on a friend, enormous and familiar and asking to be held up.

"Hold," I told the light, the way I told it every bad night, and it did. The beam swung out, found the water, came home, went out again. Somewhere in that black churn, if God was kind, someone on a boat saw it pass and corrected their heading by two degrees and lived.

That was the whole arithmetic of the work. Two degrees. One life. You never learned their names. For six years that had felt like enough—like the purest form of duty, anonymous and unfalsifiable. Then I had learned names. I had learned them the hard way, in oilskin packets and federal subpoenas and a man coughing seawater onto my kitchen floor, and I had discovered that knowing names didn't corrupt the work. It anchored it. The beam was still anonymous to the ships. It just wasn't anonymous to me anymore.

Elias appeared on the stair below me, blanket around his shoulders, hair flattened on one side, twenty-six years old going on ninety.

"Flicker?" he said.

"Quarter second. Loose port screw. Fixed."

He looked at the lamp, at the glass, at the sea hammering itself flat against the headland below. "You want me to stay up with you?"

"You were asleep."

"I was pretending." He came up the last steps and settled himself on the watch bench, the old one, the one with the burn mark from a keeper who'd died in 1911 and whose initials some bored hand had scratched into the seat. He patted the space beside him. "Sit. Ten minutes. The mechanism can survive ten minutes of you sitting down."

"It survived six years of me sitting down."

"And look how that turned out." He said it lightly, but neither of us laughed, because we both knew exactly how it had turned out: with a woman alone in a tower, keeping perfect records of a false world. With letters in the floorboards. With a sister-in-law's penmanship lessons and a brother-in-law's death certificate and thirty-one names on a ferry manifest, all of it stacked up behind my silence like water behind a dam.

I sat down. Outside, the storm threw a fistful of kelp against the gallery rail hard enough to hear it slap.

"Marta came by yesterday," Elias said. "While you were in town."

"She didn't come up?"

"She left a crate of oranges and a note." He produced it from the pocket of his robe. Marta's handwriting looked like it was being chased. *"Storm coming. Corben sent a telegram from Roseburg—says the grand jury seated in February wants statements from 'all coastal witnesses.' That means you. That means me. Also the oranges are going soft, eat them first."*

I read it twice. "February."

"He says they'll subpoena us formally after the holidays. Hale's lawyers want us deposed before the syndicate's appeal." Elias took the note back and folded it along its worn creases—he'd been carrying it around, I realized, working up to telling me. "Wren. It means going to Portland again. Sitting in rooms again. Saying it all again."

"I know what a deposition is. I gave testimony in a federal hearing. I burned half a letter in front of a deputy to make a point about my willingness to do it again."

"I'm not worried about you." He looked out at the dark. "I'm worried about the light. Eleven days minimum, maybe more. We'd have to arrange a temporary keeper through the district office, and they'll ask questions about why both keepers need to travel, and—" He stopped. Started again. "I keep waiting for it to be over. The testifying. The looking over shoulders. And it keeps not being over. It keeps being the next deposition, the next appeal, the next telegram."

"That's because you helped pull a nail out of a wall and expected the building to fall down," I said. "Buildings don't fall down. They get torn down, board by board, in hearing rooms, by tired people giving statements." I took his hand. His fingers were cold; mine were colder; between us we approximated warmth. "August told us this in the hospital in Coos Bay. Remember? He said the truth isn't a firework, it's a crop. You plant it, you wait, you fight the weeds, and eventually—"

"—eventually you harvest," Elias finished. "Yes. I remember. I was there. I've read his letters so many times I dream in his handwriting." He exhaled. "All right. February. Portland. We'll go together."

"We'll go together," I agreed, "and we'll come home together, and the light will be here, and Marta will check on it twice, and the district man they send will complain about the damp the entire time."

"You've already thought this through."

"I thought it through in September, when I saw the barometer trend and the docket news in the same week." I squeezed his hand. "Elias. I plan things now. It's the strangest side effect of almost dying at a river checkpoint—I find I can't stop making arrangements."

He smiled at that, the real smile, the one that started crooked and ended honest. Then he yawned hugely, apologized to the lamp, and went back down to bed, and I stayed in the lantern room alone with the turning light and the shouting sea, and it was fine. It was better than fine. Alone had once been my native country, then my exile, and now it was just a room I visited sometimes, with good light and a view of everything I'd chosen.

*

Toward morning the storm peaked.

I don't have language for the worst hour of a December blow at Point Alder that wouldn't sound like exaggeration, so I'll give you the facts instead. Wind gauge maximum: eighty-nine knots before the needle pegged and stopped being useful. Visibility: the length of my arm. The sea came over the north bluff—not spray, *water*, whole moving tons of it—and hit the tower's base hard enough that I felt it through the floor, through the bench, through my teeth. Somewhere down the coast a fog signal at Cape Arago went silent mid-blast, and I prayed briefly and professionally for whoever was responsible for it.

And through all of it, the light turned.

Glass across water. Glass across water. The clockwork whispered. The mantle burned white. Twice more the draft tested the ports and twice more found them sealed, because in October I had replaced every original screw with brass ones from a machinist in Coos Bay who owed August Hale a favor and charged accordingly, which is to say nothing, which is to say that debts of honor, unlike debts of money, appreciate over time.

At 4:17 the wind began to fall. At 5:30 the rain thinned to a hiss. At 6:12, gray light came up out of the southeast, and I stepped out onto the gallery into air scrubbed so clean it felt newly manufactured, and I looked down at the rocks below the north bluff—the same rocks, the same bluff—and saw the sea giving back nothing at all. No wreckage. No kelp-wrapped man. Just water, settling itself after a tantrum, and gulls already working the foam line.

The light had held. Not barely. Not miraculously. It had held the way it was built to hold, because a woman had maintained it, and because a woman before her had built it well, and because maintenance plus good construction plus attention is what holding actually consists of. Peace, I had learned, was not granted either. Same recipe.

*

Elias was still asleep when I came down.

This was unusual—he rose with the lamp the way sailors rise with bells—but the storm had kept him half awake until nearly three, and so I let him sleep, and I did what I had done every dawn for a year and would do every dawn for as many years as I was given: I opened the logbook.

The official log lived in the district office now, a clean copy mailed monthly. This book was the other one—the real one—the one I'd started the night after we got back from Portland, the one with the plain canvas cover that had once belonged to Tom, my husband, and had held, in its earlier life, the careful false entries of a woman recording a life she wasn't living. Weather fair, routine noted, nothing to report, nothing to report, nothing to report. Six years of a heartbeat written by someone refusing to feel her own pulse.

I turned past all of that. Past the first entry in the new hand—*Relit the light. Chose to.*—and down to the fresh page, and I dipped the pen, and I wrote:

*December 14th, 1935. Point Alder Light, Station log (private).*

*Great storm of the season passed overnight. Peak winds 89+ knots (gauge pegged), seas over north bluff, visibility nil from midnight to 0430. Lamp flickered twice—loose seaward port, screw backed a quarter turn; secured and upgraded to brass fittings in October, no further trouble. Clockwork true throughout. No vessels lost within sight of the station. No signals answered or unanswered.*

*I am entering this line beside a sleeping man, which the regulations would not approve of and which I therefore consider the finest entry of my career.*

Then I stopped, pen lifted, and looked at the sentence I hadn't planned to write but which had arrived anyway, the way true sentences do, and decided to let it stand. Because it was the record, and the record was finally telling the truth: not just about weather and lamps and screws, but about the keeper herself—who was no longer keeping grief, no longer keeping silence, no longer keeping anything except the light, and the man upstairs, and the account of her own life, entered daily, signed, witnessed.

*Weather: moderating. Barometer rising. Lamp burning. All hands accounted for.*

*—Wren Sandoval, Keeper, and Elias Vane, Keeper's husband (he doesn't know about the word yet).*

I blotted it, closed the book, and set the pen down.

Above me the light turned on into the brightening day, unnecessary now and burning anyway, because that was the agreement—we kept it lit through daylight hours in winter, a small extravagance the district grumbled about, because you never knew. Because *here*, said steadily enough, long enough, to enough people, stops being a warning and becomes a promise.

Upstairs, the floorboards creaked. Elias was awake.

"Wren?" His voice, sleep-roughened, drifting down the stairwell. "Is the coffee on?"

And under his voice, faint but unmistakable, carried up from the beach far below—the way sounds carry after a storm, the whole coast rinsed and resonant—a sound I had heard once before on this shore and never expected to hear again:

Someone calling up from the rocks. A voice, thin with distance and salt, hailing the lighthouse.
