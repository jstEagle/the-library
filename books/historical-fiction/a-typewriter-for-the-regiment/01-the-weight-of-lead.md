## 1. The Weight of Lead

My father taught me to measure the world in things that could be lifted with two hands.

A chase — the iron frame that held a page together — two hands, if your back was honest. A forme of set type, locked tight and ready for the press: one hand, if you were careful, and careful was the whole religion of the trade. A single piece of type, a letter cut in lead and antimony and tin, small enough to balance on a child's fingertip and heavy enough to outlast every hand that ever touched it.

That was the arithmetic I grew up on. Not crowns and hellers, not acres or years. Weight. The weight of what a person could carry, and the weight of what a person could leave behind.

On the morning this story properly begins — though stories, like type, are always begun long before anyone notices — I was standing at the type case with my sleeves rolled to the elbow, cutting a capital V, because it was my name and because the V's were running low and because there is a particular satisfaction in replenishing your own letter before any other.

The casting machine was my father's design, cobbled from a coffee roaster, a bicycle wheel, and what he swore was a piece of a church organ, though he told that story differently each time and I had stopped believing the organ entirely. Molten metal went in at the top; a finished sort dropped out at the bottom, still warm, smelling faintly of smoke and pennies. You had to dress the matrix just so. You had to trust the mold. My father said a caster was like a mother: you did the work once and then spent the rest of your life wondering how the child turned out.

"Quentin." His voice came from the composing stone at the window, where the light was best and where he had sat, more or less, since before I was born. "How many V's does a man need?"

"A man needs as many as his daughter's name requires."

"That's vanity."

"That's inventory."

I heard him laugh, which was a good sound, a short dry cough of a laugh like a press taking its first impression of the day. Then I heard him try to stand, and I heard the little catch — the pause, the breath drawn through the teeth — that had been living in his movements all winter like a mouse in the wall. You stop hearing it after a while. That's the danger of it. You stop hearing the thing that means everything.

He made it upright. He always made it upright. Aldo Vasquez had been making himself upright for fifty-eight years and did not intend to be defeated by a chair.

"Bring me the ledger," he said.

And there it was. The sentence we had been circling since autumn, the way you circle a tooth that needs pulling.

* * *

Every town has a book it pretends not to keep. In Vellmark the pretending was thin, because the book itself was enormous — a ledger the size of a paving slab, bound in green leather gone black at the corners, kept in the magistrate's office under a key that three different people claimed to hold and none of them would admit to losing. It held every family in the valley. Names, births, deaths, marriages, debts owed to the mill, debts owed by the mill, who had paid their river-tax and who had paid the tax collector instead, which was not the same thing and everyone knew it. Forty years of Vellmark, written in the hands of four different clerks, one of whom had clearly been paid by the word and another of whom had been paid, judging by his entries, almost nothing at all.

The council had finally voted — after eleven years of argument, which for the council was brisk action — to have the whole thing reset. Printed fresh. Clean columns, clean names, a record a person could read without a lantern and a grudge.

And the only print shop in Vellmark was ours.

"Two thousand names," my father said, when I brought the ledger over and set it down on the stone with a thump that rattled the quoin keys. He opened it the way you'd open a door you weren't sure you wanted behind. "Give or take the dead."

"The dead get printed too?"

"The dead get printed *especially*. A ledger that forgets its dead isn't a ledger, it's a rumor." He ran his thumb down a column, and I watched his thumb, and I tried not to watch his thumb, and failed at both. There was a tremor in it now. Small. A bird's heartbeat of a tremor. But type doesn't forgive tremors. Type is the most unforgiving material God ever let men melt down. "Four hundred pages. Sixty thousand sorts if we're thorough, and we're Vasquezes, so we'll be thorough. A year's work. Maybe two."

"We could hire—"

"Hire whom? Old Brenner sets type like he's punishing it. And the Kessler boys can't tell an em from an en, which is forgivable in a man but not in a printer." He closed the ledger gently, the way he touched everything he meant to keep. "It's us, Quentin. It's you."

There it was again, underneath the sentence about the ledger. The other sentence. The one neither of us would say straight, because saying it straight would make it true, and we were both, in our separate ways, devoted to the truth and terrified of it.

His hands were failing. Not dramatically — no falling compositing sticks, no spilled cases of type, nothing a doctor could put a name to and therefore nothing real, according to my father, who believed that anything without a Latin name was merely bad manners. But his letters had begun to lean. A compositor's page is supposed to stand like a well-drilled regiment, and lately his pages stood like a regiment the morning after a lost battle. He saw it. I saw him see it. Neither of us said a word, because in our family words were the last thing you spent, after you'd exhausted silence, work, and soup.

"I'll start with the S's," I said. "Surnames first. Alphabetical order builds character."

"Alphabetical order builds *boredom*. Character is what stops you from dropping the case halfway through the S's." He looked out the window at the street, where the morning was doing its ordinary business — the fish cart, the school bell, Mrs. Halloran beating a rug with the fury of a woman avenging something the rug had done in a previous life. "You know what's in this book, don't you?"

"Names."

"Debts. Grudges. Marriages somebody regretted by Tuesday. Every secret in Vellmark wearing a number." He tapped the cover twice, softly, like knocking on a neighbor's door. "People think a ledger is boring. A ledger is the most dangerous book in any town. It's the only one everybody's in whether they like it or not."

I was nineteen that winter, and I knew everything, which is to say I knew exactly enough to be wrong about the important things. I thought the ledger was a job. Two thousand names, sixty thousand sorts, a year of honest work. I thought danger was something that happened in novels, and that the worst thing waiting for me in that green book was the discovery of how many people in Vellmark owed my father money for wedding announcements.

I cut my V's. I sorted my case. I ate lunch standing up, as printers do, because sitting is for people whose work can be interrupted.

* * *

In the afternoon my father taught me — for perhaps the fortieth time, because teaching, in his practice, was less instruction than ritual — how to judge a piece of type by ear.

"Flick it," he said, holding out a sort between finger and thumb, "against the case rail. Listen."

I flicked. It rang.

"Good metal. Hear that? A cracked sort sounds dull, like a coin in a beggar's cup. A good sort rings like it's proud of itself." He passed it to me and I turned it over. An H. His initials, if you took the A from elsewhere. "Your grandfather cast that in 1871. Still true. Still square. Still prints a clean impression. Everything else in this shop will die — the press, the roof, me, eventually even you, though you show no signs of hurrying — but the type goes on. Somebody a hundred years from now will set a wedding announcement with that H and never know whose hands made it."

"Whose hands made it?" I asked, because I knew my part in the ritual.

"Hands that are tired," he said, "and glad of it."

Then he sat down too fast, in the way that wasn't sitting but surrendering to gravity's paperwork, and pretended he had meant to sit, and I pretended to believe him, and between the two of us we maintained a fiction so old and well-set that it might as well have been locked in a chase.

* * *

That evening, after the lamps were lit and the presses covered, I stayed at the stone alone. This was my favorite hour and I would have denied it under oath. The shop at night smelled of ink and coal oil and cold iron, and the silence had texture, like felt. I pulled the census ledger toward me across the stone — it lived in the shop now, until the job was done; the council had agreed to that much, mostly because nobody else wanted to lift it — and opened it to the first page of surnames, and began to plan.

A printer plans a big job the way a general plans a war, except the printer has to actually win. I counted pages. I estimated sorts. I made a list of the letters we'd need to cast in quantity — E foremost, because E is the tyrant of the alphabet and demands tribute in every language — and I got as far as the second column of the second page before I saw it.

Not anything remarkable. That's the thing worth writing down, if anyone writes this down: it was nothing remarkable. Just a name in a list of names, set down by Clerk Number Three, the one with the stingy hand, in faded brown ink:

*Ilves, Tomas. b. 1893.*

I knew the name the way you know a scar. Not by looking — by owning it.

Tomas Ilves. Who had lived two doors down until he was seventeen. Who had taught me my letters at the age of six, kneeling beside me at this very stone — this exact stone, I could have put my palm on the spot — moving pieces of broken type around like storybook characters. *This is Q. Q is a difficult letter. Like you.* Who had shown me how the inverted n became a u, how p became d, how the same handful of lead could be turned and flipped into every word there was, so that the alphabet seemed suddenly not a wall but a deck of cards, and I remember thinking, with the earth-shaking clarity available only to six-year-olds and drunks, *I will do this forever.*

Who had left for the capital nine years ago to be a clerk, or a journalist, or a troublemaker — the accounts differed by household — and who had promised, on the last afternoon, standing in this shop with snow melting off his boots, that he would write.

He had written once. A postcard. A picture of a bridge and four words: *The bridge is fine.* As messages went it was honest, and useless, and very much him.

"Tomas Ilves," I said aloud, to the empty shop, and the name sounded strange spoken, the way a word sounds strange when you've only ever seen it set.

I looked at the entry a while longer. Brown ink. Stingy clerk. Nothing marked against it — no debt, no note, no asterisk of scandal. Just a boy from Vellmark, born 1893, gone to the capital, unaccounted for by the greatest accounting machine in the valley.

I dipped a pencil and made a small mark in the margin — a printer's mark, a private one, meaning *check this sort before locking the forme.* Meaning nothing. Meaning, though I didn't know it yet, everything.

Then I blew out the lamp, because lead keeps better in the dark, and because my father said a shop left burning past midnight invited either fire or thoughts, and we'd had enough of both.

Outside, the river moved under its first skin of ice, patient as a creditor. Inside, in the dark, sixty thousand letters waited to be born, and among them — though I did not yet know how many, and would soon count them the way you count soldiers — waited every Vellmark name there was, including one that belonged to a boy who had taught me that the alphabet was a deck of cards.

I locked the door. I pocketed the key. And somewhere downstream, though no one in town could have said how they knew, the army was already reading its maps.
