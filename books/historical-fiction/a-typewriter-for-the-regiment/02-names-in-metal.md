## 2. Names in Metal

The census ledger arrived on a Tuesday, which Quentin Vasquez would later think was fitting, because Tuesdays were the days nothing ever went right in the print shop and everyone had simply agreed not to mention it.

It came in a canvas satchel carried by the town clerk himself, a round man named Herr Fenn whose spectacles fogged whenever he climbed the shop's three steps, so that he always arrived at the counter looking like a man emerging from his own breath.

"Master Vasquez," he said, though he was looking at Quentin, because her father was at the window doing his morning inventory of the street — a ritual that had lately become less about the street and more about proving he could still stand unassisted for a full minute. "The magistrate wants the new ledger set before the equinox. Every family. Every holding. Every debt of record."

"Every debt," her father said without turning from the window. "How many pages is that?"

"Four hundred and six." Herr Fenn set the satchel down with the reverence of a man handling either scripture or explosives, and with the census of Vellmark it had never been entirely clear which.

"Four hundred and six." Her father finally turned. His hands were in his apron pockets, which was how he hid the tremor now, the way other men hid cigarettes or love letters. "Quentin. You'll want to start sorting the case today. We'll need every em we own and then some."

Quentin was already unlacing the satchel. Inside lay the old ledger, its leather spine cracked like river ice in March, and beside it the magistrate's fresh instructions in a hand so precise it looked printed already, which she supposed was the highest compliment a clerk could pay a printer.

"Six weeks," Herr Fenn said. "He said to tell you there's no hurry, and then he said it again in a way that meant there was an enormous hurry."

"They always do," Quentin said, and Herr Fenn laughed, relieved, the way people did when she said things like that — as if she were joking, when really she was just telling the truth in a tone that let everyone pretend otherwise. It was a skill her father called *the second trade*, and he claimed it was worth more than type-cutting, though he'd never once offered to put it on the sign.

The bell over the door jangled as the clerk left, and the shop settled into itself: the smell of ink and solvent and the particular metallic sweetness of lead, the morning light coming through the north windows in long dusty bars, the presses standing quiet as sleeping cattle.

Her father lowered himself onto the stool by the stone composing table — lowering being the operative word; it took him four movements and a sound like a ship changing its mind. "Start with the A's," he said. "And mind your leading. The magistrate counts lines the way misers count coins."

"I know how to set a page, Papa."

"You know how to set a page the way I know how to dance. Enthusiastically, and with complaints from the audience."

She grinned despite herself and went to the type cases.

* * *

Setting a census ledger was unlike any other job the shop took. A broadsheet wanted speed. A wedding invitation wanted beauty. But a ledger wanted *truth*, or at least the town's agreed-upon version of it, set clean and even, line after line after line, until the reader forgot they were reading metal and paper and believed instead that they were reading the town itself.

Name. Trade. Holding. Debt. Name. Trade. Holding. Debt.

Quentin worked the cases by feel more than sight, her fingers walking the bays the way her feet knew the lanes of Vellmark. Lowercase here, figures there, the small caps for surnames up and to the left where her father insisted they lived because, he said, respectability should always be slightly out of reach.

*Aaltonen, Marta. Widow. Holding: house and garden plot, Mill Lane. Debt: none.*

*Alver, Joss. Cooper. Holding: workshop and yard, Bridge Street. Debt: fourteen crowns, Vellmark Lending Office.*

She set them into the stick one character at a time, each piece of lead clicking against the last with a small sound like teeth. By midmorning her hands had found their rhythm and her mind had gone to that pleasant empty place printers go, where the work does itself and the worker merely supervises.

Which is why she didn't notice the name until it was already half-set in the stick.

*Ilves.*

Her thumb stopped on the capital I. She looked down at the galley, at the lines she'd been setting without reading, the way you can walk a road you've walked ten thousand times and arrive without having seen anything.

*Ilves, Ansa. Widow. Holding: house and garden plot, Tanner's Row. Debt: none.*

And beneath it, in the column for dependents:

*Ilves, Tomas. Son. Residence: the capital. Occupation: clerk.*

*Clerk.* The word sat there in metal, small and smug and wrong, the way a stranger's hat looks on a familiar peg.

Quentin stood very still. Somewhere behind her the stove ticked. Outside, someone was arguing with a horse, unhappily, at length.

Tomas Ilves. Who had taught her the alphabet at nine years old by cutting letters out of potato halves because the shop's damaged type was off-limits to children. Who had shown her that a B was just two Ds stacked and pretending. Who had left for the capital seven years ago with a cardboard suitcase and a coat too thin for the journey, promising — laughing, actually, laughing with his whole crooked face — that he'd write so often her father would complain about the postage.

Seven years. Not one letter.

She looked at the word *clerk* and felt something in her chest pull tight, the way wet rope pulls when it dries. It wasn't anger, exactly. Anger would have been simpler. It was more like the ache you get in a tooth you'd forgotten existed — the body reminding you that something had been broken all along and you'd simply gotten good at chewing on the other side.

"Tomas Ilves," she said aloud, to no one.

"Friend of yours?" her father asked, without looking up from the forme he was locking, his hands steadier at the press than anywhere else on earth, as if the metal lent him its calm.

"He taught me my letters."

"Mm." Her father turned a quoin key a quarter-turn. "The potato boy."

"The potato boy," she confirmed.

"He never wrote?"

"Not once."

Her father considered this with the gravity he gave to everything, including weather and bread. "Then either he's dead, or he's rich, or he's ashamed. In my experience those are the only three reasons a man stops writing home, and two of them are curable."

"Papa."

"I'm only saying. Dead men don't buy stamps, but shame and wealth both can be talked out of." He locked the forme and lifted it — a two-handed lift, careful, the tremor visible now and neither of them pretending otherwise — and set it on the stone. "Set his mother's line true and move on. The ledger doesn't care who taught you what. That's the whole trouble with ledgers."

But Quentin didn't move on. Not yet. She stood there holding the stick with Tomas's name in it, feeling the small cold weight of the letters, and thought about promises.

* * *

They had made it on the mill bridge, the summer they were eleven, the summer the river ran low and green and full of the reflected gossip of the whole town. Tomas had been cutting letters into a plank with his pocketknife — he was forever carving letters into things; fences, tree stumps, once, memorably, the flank of a very patient pig — and Quentin had been correcting his spelling, because she was that kind of child and he loved her for it, or at least tolerated it with unusual grace.

"When I'm grown," he'd said, not looking up from the plank, "I'll have real type. Cast type, from the capital, the kind with the serifs like little feet. And I'll give it to you."

"You can't give me type. Type costs money."

"I'll have money."

"You'll have debts. Everyone has debts. It's going in the ledger."

He'd laughed at that, and then he'd gone serious in the way he could, suddenly, like a cloud crossing the sun, and he'd held out the plank with the letters ILVES cut rough and backwards into the wood.

"Then promise me this instead. Whatever happens, wherever I end up — you cut my name true. Not fancy. Not flattering. True. If I'm a clerk, write clerk. If I'm a fool, write fool. Promise me the truth, Quen, because everyone else in this town will promise me kindness, and kindness lies."

She had promised. Eleven-year-olds promise easily; it costs nothing and the interest doesn't come due for years. She'd pressed her thumb into the wet wood shavings and sworn it on the river, which was, her grandmother maintained, the only oath in Vellmark that actually bound anyone, because the river remembered.

Now she stood at the composing stone, twenty-three years old, holding his name in lead, and the river was frozen solid outside the window, and somewhere in the capital a man who carved letters into pigs had apparently become a *clerk*, and hadn't written, and hadn't come home, and his mother on Tanner's Row had stopped speaking to anyone except the woman who sold her milk, and even then only to say *yes* and *no* and *thank you*, and sometimes not even the thank you.

Quentin set the line true. *Clerk.* She justified it, spaced it, slid it into the galley with all the others. The truth, delivered exactly as promised, seven years late and useless as a shoe for the wrong foot.

"There," she said quietly. "Kept my word."

The word *clerk* gleamed up at her, indifferent.

* * *

She worked through the afternoon. The B's gave way to the C's, the C's to the D's, and the shop filled with the small industry sounds she loved better than any music: the click of the stick, the whisper of the paper proofs, her father humming something tuneless and old while he cleaned the press rollers with a cloth gone stiff as bark.

It was near dusk, the light going amber and the stove needing feeding, when she noticed the thing that would matter later — though of course she didn't know it would matter later; she thought it was nothing, a clerical quirk, the sort of thing Herr Fenn would explain away with a cough.

Some entries were different. Not wrong, exactly. Nothing you could point to. But certain names in the ledger had been written in the clerk's hand with a strange carelessness — a blot where there shouldn't be a blot, a margin note scraped half-away, a date corrected twice. And in three places, in the oldest pages, whole entries seemed to have been pasted over, little paper patches the color of weak tea, as if someone had decided history needed mending.

"Herr Fenn's been busy," she said, tilting one of the patched pages toward the lamp.

Her father came to look. He read the way he did everything, slowly and completely, and when he finished his mouth did the thing it did instead of frowning, which was to become very slightly more rectangular.

"Patches," he said.

"Patches."

"On a census."

"People spill things, Papa."

"People spill soup." He touched the edge of one patch with one careful finger, the way he'd touch a piece of type with a flaw in its face. "This isn't spilled. This is *decided*." He straightened up, and for a moment the tremor in his hands seemed to travel all the way up into his voice. "Ask Fenn about it tomorrow. Politely. Printers ask politely. That's how we've kept our teeth for two hundred years."

"I'll ask politely."

"And Quentin—" He paused at the door to the back room, silhouetted against the lamplight there, an old man-shaped hole in the evening. "Set what's under the patches exactly as it is. Patches and all. A ledger is a record, not a portrait. We print what's there, not what's pretty."

"Yes, Papa."

He went through, and she heard him moving around back there, the familiar small noises of a man putting tools away for the night, each tool in its place, each place known by heart.

Quentin banked the stove and stood a moment in the darkening shop, surrounded by the town in lead. Four hundred and six pages of Vellmark, every debt and holding and secret, waiting to be made permanent. Somewhere in the D's a patched-over entry slept under its tea-colored bandage. Somewhere in the I's a boy named Tomas was a clerk and not a letter-writer, and his mother was a widow with no debts, which the ledger counted as solvency and Quentin suspected was something much closer to ruin.

She blew out the lamp. On her way to the door she paused at the window, out of habit, and looked down at the street.

There were lanterns moving at the far end of Bridge Street. More than usual. Moving in step.

She watched them for a moment, told herself it was the watch, told herself it was nothing, told herself it was Tuesday and Tuesdays were allowed one strangeness apiece.

The lanterns kept coming.
