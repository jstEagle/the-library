## 3. The Colonel's Warrant

The lanterns turned out to be soldiers.

Quentin learned this the way she learned most things — by standing at a window too long and drawing conclusions. By full light they had stopped pretending to be anything other than what they were: two columns of grey coats coming across the bridge, boots striking the planks in unison, a sound like a very large clock deciding to keep time in her street. The river mist hadn't burned off yet, so the column appeared out of it piece by piece — first bayonets, then shoulders, then faces, young and mostly bored, the way men look when they have marched since before dawn for a town that has nothing worth stealing but paper.

She counted forty, then gave up counting because her father was calling her name from below in the voice he used when something was burning or when breakfast was ready, and in this house those voices were identical.

"Quentin! If the kettle's on, pour; if it isn't, lie about it."

"It's on," she said, which was true, and went down.

Her father was already at his stool by the window, which was where he conducted all business now that his hands had opinions of their own. He'd tied his apron crooked. She fixed it without asking, the way you straighten a picture frame, and he let her, and neither of them mentioned it, because that was their whole arrangement regarding his hands: never mention them.

"Soldiers," he said.

"I saw."

"In Vellmark." He said it the way another man might say *frost in June*. "Last regiment through here was my father's time, and all they did was get lost and ask after the ferry."

"These don't look lost."

"No," her father said, and drank his tea, and watched the grey coats fill Bridge Street like water filling a ditch. "These look like they know exactly where they're going."

Quentin stood at the sink with her hands in the washwater and felt the morning tilt, very slightly, off its axis. It was Tuesday. She had a forme of wedding invitations to lock up by noon. Mrs. Halloran wanted three hundred funeral bills printed for a man who was not yet dead but everyone agreed was close. The census ledger sat half-set on the stone, six weeks of her evenings in it, every name in the town standing on its feet in lead like a congregation waiting for service.

It did not occur to her — not once, not even as a joke — that any of these things had anything to do with soldiers.

This is the mercy of ordinary mornings. They let you finish your tea.

* * *

They came to the print shop at nine o'clock precisely, which Quentin would later think about often, because only clerks and printers respect an exact hour, and these men respected theirs.

There were four of them at the door, and one officer, and a carpenter with a hammer and a board, and a boy of perhaps fifteen carrying a bucket of paste. The officer was not the colonel. The officer was a lieutenant with the particular freshness of someone who had been told exactly what to do and intended to do it in order.

"By requisition of the Fourth Regiment," the lieutenant read from a paper, in the flat voice of a man reading a paper, "these premises are taken into the use of His Majesty's Army for the duration. All work in progress is suspended. All materials are held subject to inventory. The proprietor will present himself and his records for inspection."

He finished and looked up, and seemed almost embarrassed, the way a man is embarrassed delivering news he hasn't examined.

"My records," said Quentin's father.

"The town ledger," said the lieutenant. "And any stock of type, paper, ink."

"This is a print shop," said her father mildly. "Everything here is type, paper, or ink. You've requisitioned air."

The lieutenant blinked. Behind him one of the soldiers made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled in its cradle.

"Sir—"

"I'm not *sir*, I'm a printer. That's my daughter, she's also a printer. We are both extremely busy printing a town's worth of wedding invitations, which, I'll note, the army has no use for, unless your colonel intends to marry Vellmark."

"Dad," Quentin said quietly.

But the carpenter was already nailing the board across the door frame, and the sound of it — four hammer blows, unhurried, competent — did more than the lieutenant's whole speech. Her father looked at the board going up over his own door and stopped talking, and Quentin watched him stop, watched the sentence die in him somewhere behind the ribs, and understood that something had ended that she didn't yet have a word for.

"The ledger," she said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "Where is it being taken?"

"Nowhere," the lieutenant said. "It stays. The colonel wants the lists built here. He says—" the lieutenant consulted his paper again, grateful for it, "—he says the town's own press will serve the town best. Under supervision."

Under supervision. The words went down into her like a stone into a well, taking their time, making their small cold splash at the bottom.

* * *

Colonel Brandt arrived at eleven.

She would remember that too — that he let the sergeant major and the boards and the inventories go first, the machinery of occupation, and came afterward himself, alone except for an adjutant, at an hour that suggested he had spent the morning letting the town grow accustomed to the idea of him.

He was not what she expected, which annoyed her immediately. She had expected bulk, or bluster, or at minimum a mustache with ambitions. Brandt was narrow and neat and perhaps fifty, with spectacles and the kind of face that belongs to a schoolmaster or a coroner. He took off his gloves finger by finger before he spoke.

"Vasquez," he said, reading the shop's painted window. "You cut your own type?"

"Some of it," her father said. "Most of it, lately, is my daughter's hand."

Brandt turned and looked at Quentin properly for the first time. His gaze went, without hurry, to her fingers — ink-stained, nicked, one thumbnail permanently blackened from the composing stick — and then back up to her face, and she had the distinct unpleasant sensation of being read, thoroughly, by a man who read everything.

"You set the census ledger," he said. Not a question.

"Six weeks of evenings," her father answered for her, because fathers do. "Every family in Vellmark, spelled correctly, which is more than the last clerk managed."

"Spelled correctly," Brandt repeated. Something moved at the corner of his mouth that was not quite a smile. "Do you know what I need, Miss Vasquez?"

"Proclamations," she said. "I've seen the paste buckets."

"Proclamations. Yes. And lists." He walked along the stone where the ledger forme stood under its paper dust jacket, and paused beside it the way a man pauses beside a horse he is considering buying. "A town is a difficult thing to govern, Miss Vasquez. Too many people in it who believe they know each other. Loyalty becomes — vague. Neighborly. Worthless." He touched the edge of the dust jacket, not lifting it. "A list is honest. A list says yes or no. Your metal here is very good at yes and no."

"It's good at whatever's set in it," Quentin said.

"Exactly." And now he did smile, briefly, and it was the worst thing she had seen all day, because it was patient. "Which is why I requisitioned a press instead of a clerk. Clerks write. Printers fix. I am told your father can make a correction disappear so completely that God himself couldn't find the erratum."

Her father said nothing. Quentin said nothing. Outside, a sentry shifted his weight on the boards, and the bridge creaked under cart traffic that had doubled since dawn.

"We will begin tomorrow," Brandt said, replacing his gloves. "The proclamation first. Then the ledger. My adjutant will provide the copy. You will find me reasonable, Miss Vasquez. I want the same thing you want — a clean page, honestly set." He put on his hat. "The difference between us is only in what we call clean."

* * *

That night they ate supper in the kitchen with the shutters closed, which they had never done before, and neither remarked on it, which was its own remark.

"They'll take the house lists," her father said at last. "Names, debts, who owes whom. It's all in there. Half of Vellmark's secrets are sitting on my stone in twelve-point."

"I know."

"And we'll set the army's lists from it. Loyal and not loyal." He turned his cup slowly on its saucer, not drinking. "Your grandfather set type for a magistrate who hanged three men. He used to say the lead remembers. Said you could feel it in the forme, what the letters had been made to say."

"Do you feel it?"

He considered the question seriously, the way he considered everything, including jokes, including weather, including his own dying hands.

"I feel six weeks of your evenings," he said. "And I feel that tomorrow we open our door to men who nail doors shut, and we print what they tell us, and we do it beautifully, because we are printers, and that is the trap of being good at something." He looked up at her. "Your mother used to say I could ruin any fine moment with a proverb. This appears to be inherited."

Quentin laughed despite herself, and then the laugh ran out, and the quiet came back and sat down between them like a third guest.

Upstairs, later, she lay awake listening to the sentry's boots on the boards outside, back and forth, back and forth, keeping time. Below her, through the floor, she could feel the shop — she swore she could feel it, the way you feel a sleeping animal in the next room. The stone, the chases, the formes, the half-built ledger with its congregation of names standing in the dark, every person she had known since childhood arranged in rows of lead.

Somewhere in those rows was Tomas Ilves. She had set his name herself, weeks ago, carefully, the *lves* fitted tight because it always had been. She thought of it lying there in the dark among the others, small and hard and true, and she thought of the colonel's clean page, and his yes and no, and his patient smile.

The boots went back and forth. Back and forth.

In the morning, she decided, she would pull a proof of the ledger before the army touched it. Just to have it. Just to know what the truth had looked like, in her own hands, while her hands were still allowed to hold it.

Downstairs, in the locked dark of the shop, the forme waited under its paper jacket, patient as lead, holding every name in Vellmark — and one name among them that the army had not yet learned to read.
