## 4. What the Army Cannot Read

The soldiers arrived at the print shop on a Tuesday, which Quentin thought was fitting, because Tuesdays were when her father had always said the world's worst ideas were set into type.

They did not know how to work the press. This became apparent within the first hour and was, in its way, the only comfort of that whole gray season.

The requisition order had been nailed to the door with four nails — an extravagance, since one would have held it fine against a door that nobody would dare open anyway. Inside, the shop smelled wrong: boot leather and pipe smoke where there should have been ink and solvent. A corporal named Doss had been left in charge, a broad young man with a face like a closed ledger and the manner of someone who had been told he was in charge and believed it about sixty percent.

"Proclamations," Corporal Doss said, slapping a sheaf of papers onto the stone sink where her father washed his rollers. "The colonel wants five hundred by Friday."

Quentin looked at the papers. She looked at the press. She looked at the six soldiers standing around it the way men stand around a horse they have been assured is dangerous.

"Who's setting the type?" she asked.

Doss frowned. "Setting?"

"Type doesn't grow on the press, Corporal. Someone has to compose it. Letter by letter." She picked up a stick, held it out to him. "Backwards, mind you. And mirrored. And you'll want to know which letters are which, because half of them look alike until you've cut them yourself for ten years."

There was a silence in which six soldiers discovered a sudden fascination with the floor.

"I can read," said one of them, defensively, from the back.

"Wonderful," said Quentin. "Can you read *this*?" She pulled a case drawer and let him look at the field of little metal faces, all of them reversed, all of them sorted not alphabetically but by frequency, by the printer's own logic, a private geography learned over years. The soldier leaned in, went slightly pale, and withdrew.

By noon, Quentin was setting the proclamation herself while the soldiers watched, and by Friday the town walls bore five hundred copies of a notice about curfew hours, printed crisp and clean, and Corporal Doss had begun calling her "the compositor" in a tone that was almost respectful.

Her father called it something else, later, in the dark of their rooms above the shop.

"You're working for them," he said. Not accusing. Just laying the type out flat, so they could both read it.

"I'm keeping the presses alive," Quentin said. "And I'm keeping *us* alive. They could have shot us and dragged the shop's contents to the square. Instead they need me."

"They need you," her father agreed. He turned his bad hand over, watching the fingers tremble. "That's a rope with two ends, my girl. Mind which end you're holding."

* * *

The ledger sat in the corner of the composing room under a canvas sheet, and every day Quentin found a reason to walk past it.

The army had plans for it. That much was clear from the beginning. Colonel Brandt himself had come once more after the first day — he came often, actually, always alone, always courteous, always with his gloves on — and stood before the ledger cases the way other men stood before altars.

"The census type," he'd said to her, on his third visit. "Your father cut new sorts for it this year, I understand. Every name in Vellmark, reset in fresh metal."

"That's right."

"And who else could sort those cases? Who else knows your father's arrangement?"

"No one," Quentin said, and heard how it sounded, and hated how true it was.

Brandt smiled. He had a smile like a well-locked drawer — nothing in it visible, but you knew things were kept there. "Good," he said. "Indispensability is a form of safety, Fräulein Vasquez. For everyone concerned."

She thought about that sentence for three days afterward, turning it over the way she'd turn a doubtful letterform to the light.

What the lists would decide became clear slowly, in pieces, the way a forme is locked up piece by piece until the whole picture stands. Sergeant Mirele — a thin, careful clerk with ink-stained cuffs and the apologetic posture of a man permanently requesting pardon for existing — came twice a week to pull proofs of the loyalty lists as they were compiled. Quentin was required to assist. Required, officially, to hand him paper and hold the tympan.

Unofficially, she read everything upside down, which was, after all, how printers read the world.

She learned that the lists had columns. Not just names — dispositions. *Retained. Requisitioned. Cleared.* And beside certain names, small pencil marks whose meaning she pieced together over weeks: a house on Tanner's Row marked *R*, and two weeks later the Havel family's furniture carried out into the snow; the widow Brenner marked *C*, and her shop shuttered with army seals by morning.

*Cleared.* Such a clean word for it. Somebody at a desk had decided that a word meaning *emptied* should also mean *safe*, and the word had obeyed, because words do what type tells them.

"You're frowning at the proof," Mirele said one afternoon, catching her.

"I'm checking the spacing," Quentin said. "It's crooked."

It wasn't crooked. But he accepted it, because he wanted to accept it, and she filed his face away among the things she was learning: that some soldiers looked at the lists like men reading a menu, and some looked at them like men reading a diagnosis, and Mirele was the second kind.

* * *

The dangerous part — she understood this even as she sank deeper into it — was that being useful felt good.

Not the collaboration. Never that. But the *work* itself, the old sweet mechanics of it: the soldiers brought her jobs and she solved them, and solving them meant the shop stayed hers in every way that mattered except the deed. She fixed their crooked lockups. She showed Corporal Doss how to pack the platen so the impression wouldn't bite through cheap army paper. She re-inked their rollers when they ruined one by leaving it to dry overnight, and lectured them about it, and Doss took the lecture, because what else could he do?

"You know," Doss said one evening, watching her distribute type with both hands moving like water finding its level, "in the village where I'm from, my uncle was a wheelwright. People used to come to him the way they come to you. Like you're a well or something."

"What happened to your uncle?"

"He's fine. Wheelwrights aren't political." Doss said it lightly, but then he seemed to hear it, and glanced at her, and didn't say anything else for a while.

That was the thing about the occupation, Quentin was discovering. It wasn't all boots and barked orders. Mostly it was this: ordinary men doing ordinary tasks around you, apologizing with their shoulders, wanting — visibly, pathetically wanting — for you to be all right with them. Wanting the wheelwright's niece to keep fixing their rollers so they could go on pretending this was a job and not a theft.

And the terrible temptation was to be all right with them. To be useful and warm and indispensable, and to let the lists compile themselves in the corner under the canvas, and to sleep at night telling herself she was only the hands, only the trade, only a girl keeping her father's shop running.

Her father saw it happening. Of course he did. He sat upstairs most days now, his hands too unsteady for the case, listening to the army's business conducted below him in his own shop, and in the evenings he asked questions that weren't really questions.

"Doss seems fond of you," he said one night.

"He's a boy. He misses his uncle."

"Mm. And the colonel?"

"He comes to admire the type."

"Does he." Her father turned his cup slowly on its saucer. "In my experience, Quentin, men who come to admire the type are usually wondering whether it can be made to say something different than it says."

She kept her face still. She was getting better at that. It was, she realized, another kind of typesetting — composing your own expression, locking it up tight, making sure nothing showed through but what you intended.

"Well," she said. "Then it's lucky the type says exactly what it says."

Her father looked at her for a long moment across the lamplight, and she could not tell — genuinely could not tell — whether he believed her, and that frightened her more than anything Colonel Brandt had ever done.

* * *

The ledger's sorting was scheduled to begin in earnest after the new year, Mirele told her, when the compilation clerks arrived from the capital. Until then the cases sat under canvas, and the army's attention stayed on proclamations: curfew notices, requisition schedules, a long dull decree about grain quotas that took her two full days to set and gave her, in compensation, nearly two full days of near-privacy at the composing stone.

It was during the grain quota job that she allowed herself, for the first time, to lift the canvas.

Just a corner. Just to check — she told herself — that the type hadn't been disturbed, that damp hadn't gotten at the sorts, that the cases were sound. A printer's diligence. Nothing more.

But her fingers found the I-case without her permission, and her fingers knew her father's arrangement the way her tongue knew the shape of her own name, and before she had fully decided anything she had set four letters in a spare stick and held them up:

*I-L-V-E-S*

Small. Bright. New metal, her father's cutting, the S with its slightly heavy spur that she'd teased him about all spring. Tomas Ilves, set in lead, waiting in the census cases like a coin someone had dropped behind a wall.

She put the stick down. She covered the cases. She stood there breathing in the smell of ink and cold iron and trying to feel like a person who had merely checked on her inventory.

He had taught her the alphabet. That was the whole of it, and the whole of everything. She'd been six, and he'd been eight, and he'd sat backwards on the chair by the composing stone — he was always sitting backwards on chairs, a boy built like a question mark — and he'd held up the sorts one at a time and said, *this one's Q, like you, see, it's got a tail, it never goes anywhere without its tail.* And she had laughed, and he had shown her how the letters fit together into words, and she had thought, with the absolute conviction available only to children, that this was the best thing anyone would ever do for her.

He'd gone to the capital at seventeen. He'd promised to write. He hadn't written. Four years of nothing, and now here was his name in fresh metal, in a ledger the army had stolen, bound for whatever column the army chose to put it in.

*Retained. Requisitioned. Cleared.*

She thought of the Havel family's furniture in the snow.

"Compositor!" Doss's voice from the front room. "Colonel's here!"

Quentin dropped the canvas straight. By the time Brandt's footsteps reached the composing room she was at the stone, setting grain quotas, the picture of a woman with no thoughts beyond em-quads and justification.

Brandt stopped beside her and regarded the stick of type with genuine, unhurried interest.

"Fine work," he said. "You justify beautifully, Fräulein. Most compositors rush the spacing." He paused. "Patience with the spaces between things. It's rarer than you'd think. In men, especially."

"Thank you, Colonel."

"The compilation clerks arrive in January," he said, conversationally, examining the ledger cases without touching them. "They'll want the census type distributed properly. Sorted to their scheme, not your father's. You'll supervise, of course."

"Of course," said Quentin.

"It will be delicate work." He smiled his locked-drawer smile. "Names are such small things, and yet people make such fuss over them. One letter wrong, and a man becomes someone else entirely. Isn't that so?"

"Yes, Colonel," said Quentin. "One letter wrong changes everything."

He nodded, satisfied, and left, and she stood at the stone with her heart going like a press run, understanding two things at once and unable to decide which was worse: that the colonel trusted her completely —

— or that he might not trust her at all, and was simply waiting, patient as winter, to see what the type would say.
