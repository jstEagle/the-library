## 5. A Proof in the Snow

The army slept badly in Vellmark.

Quentin learned this the way she learned most useful things: by listening. The sentries on the corner of Binder's Row changed watch at two and again at four, and between those hours they grew careless, stamping their feet and complaining about the cold in a dialect from three provinces east. At four they were sharpest. At half past two, when the wind came down off the hills and made even soldiers wish for their mothers, they huddled under the awning of the chandler's shop and thought about nothing but their own noses.

It was at half past two that Quentin went to work.

She had not planned it, exactly. Planning was for people who had a choice, and she had discovered over the past week that she did not — that some question had lodged itself under her ribs the day Colonel Brandt smiled his locked-drawer smile and told her names were small things, and the only cure was an answer. The loyalty lists sat in their galley trays like teeth in a jaw, set by her own hands, sorted by Sergeant Mirele's careful pencil marks, and she could recite perhaps a third of them from memory by now, which frightened her more than she liked to admit. She knew which families would eat next winter and which would be turned into the road. She knew it the way you know the contents of someone else's letter after you've set it in type — completely, unwillingly, forever.

But knowing was not proof.

Her father said that once about the census ledger, back when the world still belonged to printers. "A man will swear on his mother's grave he paid his tax," he'd said, wiping ink from his fingers with a rag that had been mostly rag since before Quentin was born. "Show him the counterfoil and he'll swear his mother never had a grave. Proof is the only thing that outlives a lie."

So: proof.

* * *

Getting downstairs without waking the soldiers billeted in the front room was a matter of craft, and Quentin was, if nothing else, a craftsman. There were seven steps that creaked and six that didn't, a fact she had mapped as diligently as any surveyor, and she descended them in the dark with the particular tenderness of a woman walking through her own house like a burglar. In the shop proper, the army had left its mark everywhere — crates of proclamation paper stacked against the type cabinets, a rifle leaning against the imposing stone where no rifle had any business being, the smell of boot polish and cold iron where there should have been oil of cloves and warm lead.

She lit no lamp. She didn't need one; her hands knew the shop the way her father's hands knew the alphabet, and besides, the moon through the frost-furred window gave enough grey light to work by if she kept to the edges of things.

The forme holding the loyalty list stood locked in its chase near the press. She lifted it onto the stone, felt its weight settle — forty pounds or so of other people's fates — and stood a moment with her palms flat against the cold type-high surface, listening to the house breathe.

Above her, a floorboard shifted. Her father, turning in his sleep. Or not asleep. With him lately she could no longer tell, and that was its own small grief she folded away to be examined later, or never.

She inked the brayer lightly — too much ink and the proof would take longer to dry, and every minute in this shop after midnight was a minute borrowed at interest — and rolled a coat across the type. Then she pulled the press lever with both hands, easing it rather than pulling it, because a full pull would make the old iron groan like a church organ, and Vellmark had heard quite enough organs this winter.

The impression came up soft and grey. A proof. Not an edition, not ten thousand copies marching down the river to decide everything — just this one ghostly page, this single whispered copy of what the army intended to say about the people of Vellmark.

She carried it to the window, held it to the moonlight, and read.

* * *

The list ran in columns, and Quentin had set most of those columns herself, so her eye moved down them the way a shepherd moves through a familiar flock: Ackermann, loyal. Brandt's own clerks had added the marginal marks in red pencil — Mirele's neat hand transcribing the colonel's decisions — and beside each name either a small tick or a small cross, and the crosses, Quentin noticed, clustered along certain streets the way blight clusters in an orchard. The riverside lanes. The tanners' quarter. The streets where people had been heard saying things, presumably, though nobody would ever say what things, because the whole apparatus of the list depended on nobody ever asking.

Ackermann, tick. Bacher, tick, with a note: *debtor to regiment sutler — reassess*. Czerny, cross.

And then, third column, halfway down:

**ILVES, TOMAS. ENEMY OF THE STATE. PROPERTY FORFEIT.**

Quentin read it twice. Three times. As if the letters might rearrange themselves under attention, the way letters sometimes did in dreams, the way she had spent half her life making them rearrange themselves on purpose. But type does not dream. Type says what it is made to say, and this type said *enemy*, and beneath it, in the property schedule appended to the list, it said:

*Ilves, widow of — house and lot, Binder's Row, seized to the Crown.*

The house on Binder's Row. The blue door that Tomas had painted himself at fourteen, badly, so that it looked like the sky after rain. The kitchen where Mrs. Ilves had fed two children plum dumplings and taught them both to whistle through their thumbs. Marked with a red cross in a column of a list that would go down the river when the ice broke, and after that the paper would do what paper does, which is whatever the strongest hand tells it.

Quentin stood very still.

Here is the thing about a promise made in peacetime: it keeps beautifully. It sits in a drawer of the heart with the other pressed flowers and dried ribbons, and it costs nothing, and it smells faintly of summers that are never coming back. Quentin had been nine years old, kneeling on the floor of this very shop among the scattered sorts, when Tomas Ilves — twelve, solemn, missing a front tooth — arranged the letters of her name on the stone and said, *This is you. Q-U-E-N. See? You're in here.* And she had looked at the little metal pieces and understood for the first time that she existed in a form that could be picked up, and set, and printed, and kept, and she had loved him for it in the uncomplicated way of children, the way you love the person who shows you your own face.

*I'm going to the capital,* he'd said the summer he left. *I'll write. I'll send my address and you'll set it in type and print it, and I'll always know where to find you.* And she had promised — laughing, because promises made while laughing don't count, everyone knows that — *I'll cut your name true. However they spell it in the capital, however fancy they make it, I'll keep the true one here.*

He had never written. Six years of silence. And the promise had gone into the drawer with the pressed flowers, and she had almost — not forgotten, never forgotten, but *shelved* it, the way you shelve a book you mean to read someday.

Now the book had opened itself, and the words inside were in red pencil.

*Property forfeit.*

Mrs. Ilves lived alone in that house since Tomas's father died. She was seventy if she was a day, and deaf in one ear, and she still called Quentin *my little printer* in a voice like a kettle coming to boil. The army had not yet come for her — the list hadn't gone downstream, nothing was final until the ice broke, that was the terrible arithmetic of it all — but the list was the future, typed and locked in a chase, and futures set in type have a way of printing themselves.

Quentin's hands were shaking. She noticed this with a kind of detached professional interest, the way she'd notice a loose quoit in the press. Her father had taught her that shaking hands were the body's way of telling you something your mind hadn't finished agreeing to.

"All right," she said aloud, very quietly, to the moonlit shop, to the type, to the boy who had shown her her own name in metal and then vanished into the capital like a sort dropped back in the case. "All right."

It was not a plan. It was barely a sentence. But resolve has to start somewhere, and in the Vasquez family it traditionally started with talking to machinery.

* * *

She hid the proof where her father hid the good brandy, which is to say inside the hollowed-out copy of *A Treatise on Hydraulics* that nobody had opened since 1888, and she cleaned the press and returned the chase to its place, and she climbed the seven safe stairs and lay in bed staring at the ceiling until the window went from black to iron-grey.

At breakfast her father ate his porridge in the slow deliberate way that had become his habit since his hands began failing him, as if eating were itself a kind of typesetting requiring patience and precision. He did not look at her. He said, conversationally, to the middle distance:

"Press sounded heavy last night."

Quentin's spoon stopped halfway to her mouth. "You dreamed it."

"Mm." He took another bite. "Dreamed it pulled easy, though. Someone eased the lever." He looked at her then, and his eyes — her eyes, set in an older face — were perfectly mild. "Good technique. Whoever it was."

"The wind," said Quentin.

"Of course," said her father. "The wind. It's been very interested in the press lately." He pushed his bowl aside and flexed his right hand, watching the fingers tremble with the expression of a man reviewing unsatisfactory apprentice work. "You know," he said, still conversational, "in thirty years I've learned there are only two reasons anyone pulls a proof at night. To learn something. Or to change something."

"And if it's both?" asked Quentin, before she could stop herself.

Her father considered this. Outside, a patrol went by on Binder's Row, boots crunching the frozen ruts, and they both listened to it pass with the new attentiveness that occupation teaches.

"Then," he said, "that person had better be very good with their hands." He gathered his bowl and his bread and stood, steadying himself on the table, and paused beside her chair long enough to say, quietly, in the tone he used for corrections that were not really corrections: "The trick with a lie isn't making it look true. Anyone can do that. The trick is making it look *boring*. Nobody magnifies boredom." And he shuffled off toward the shop, leaving Quentin sitting in the wreckage of the assumption that she had any secrets left in that house.

* * *

She went to Binder's Row that afternoon, carrying a loaf of black bread and a jar of her mother's — her late mother's — plum preserve, because in occupied towns you cannot visit anyone empty-handed without the whole street composing theories by suppertime.

Mrs. Ilves answered the door on the second knock, the first having gone unheard by the deaf ear. She was a broad woman gone narrow with age, with white hair pinned up in a style that had been fashionable during the previous century's war, and when she saw who it was her face performed the complicated maneuver it always did — delight layered over something older and sadder, like paint over woodworm.

"My little printer," she announced, loud, because loud was how she spoke to everyone including herself. "Come in, come in, before the street decides we're conspiring."

"Are we?" asked Quentin, stepping inside.

"We're drinking tea," said Mrs. Ilves. "If the tea turns out to be conspiracy, that's between me and the tea."

The kitchen was exactly as Quentin remembered it from childhood — the same blue-painted chairs, the same dented kettle, the same calendar from a feed merchant dated 1904 because Mrs. Ilves saw no reason to update a calendar that was still technically correct about the months. They sat. They drank tea. Quentin talked about the weather and the price of tallow and the scandalous behavior of the miller's dog, and Mrs. Ilves answered in her carrying voice, and both of them maintained the fiction that this was a social call.

It was Quentin who finally put down her cup and said, carefully: "Have you had word from Tomas? Any word at all?"

The kettle ticked as it cooled. Mrs. Ilves looked at her hands, which were folded on the table like two sleeping birds.

"No," she said. Then, quieter than Quentin had ever heard her: "Six years. A mother can live on one letter a year. I've had none to live on." She raised her chin, defiant, daring Quentin to pity her. "He's busy. Important men in the capital are busy. His father was the same — weeks silent when he was courting me, and then a whole paragraph at once."

Quentin thought of the red pencil. *Enemy of the state. Property forfeit.* She thought of the blue door, painted badly by a fourteen-year-old's hands, and of the list locked in its chase downstairs, waiting for the ice to break so it could become real.

"Mrs. Ilves," she said, "if anything — if the army should ask anything of you, anything about Tomas, anything at all—"

"What will they ask?" The old woman's eyes, milky-rimmed but sharp behind the cataract fog, fixed on her suddenly. "Little printer. What will they ask?"

"I don't know," said Quentin, which was true, and also the safest thing she had said all day.

Mrs. Ilves studied her for a long moment. Then she rose, went to the dresser, and moved the good teapot aside, and behind it, in a gap in the wood that Quentin realized must have been pried open many times, she took out a bundle of papers tied with string. She held them against her chest without offering them.

"He sent these before he stopped sending anything," she said. "Three letters, all at once, like his father. The rest—" her voice caught and recovered, a rope slipping and catching on a cleat. "The rest I keep for him. When he comes back, he'll want them."

"May I see one?" Quentin asked. "Just one. Please."

Something passed over the old woman's face — suspicion, hope, the exhaustion of a person who has guarded a thing alone too long. She untied the string with fingers that shook worse than Quentin's had at the press, and selected the topmost letter, and handed it over as if surrendering a hostage.

The handwriting was Tomas's, grown-up now, slanted and hurried. The date was five years old. Quentin read the opening lines standing in the blue-door kitchen while the kettle ticked and Mrs. Ilves watched her with the whole of her remaining heart in her face.

*Dear Mama — forgive the delay and the poor paper. Work continues. I have seen things here I cannot write yet but will tell you when I am home, and soon, I think, soon. Keep the letters somewhere safe. If I am slow in writing again it is not forgetfulness. Tell Quentin—*

The letter stopped there in Quentin's reading, because Mrs. Ilves reached out and took it gently from her hands, retied the string, and put the bundle back behind the teapot, and said, in her ordinary carrying voice, "More tea?"

"Tell Quentin what?" Quentin asked.

"I never finished reading it myself," said Mrs. Ilves, pouring. "I stop at his name. An old woman is entitled." She handed the cup over, and her hand was steady now, and her eyes were not. "Drink your tea, little printer. And mind the wind. They say it's been very interested in presses lately."

Quentin walked home through the blue hour with the snow squeaking under her boots, and by the time she reached Binder's Row's far corner she was no longer thinking of the promise as a flower in a drawer. Flowers were for keeping. This was something else now — a debt, entered in a ledger she couldn't close, marked due on the day the ice broke.

Downriver, under the moonrise, the river lay black and solid and patient, holding its breath exactly as she was.

She had until the thaw.

She had one name to keep true, and one house to keep standing, and one letter that ended mid-sentence with her own name in it — and somewhere in the capital, a boy who had seen things he could not write yet, and a state that had already written its answer in red pencil.

Quentin Vasquez let herself into the print shop, hung up her coat, and stood a long moment in the dark among the cases of type, listening to the river's silence.

Then she lit the lamp.
