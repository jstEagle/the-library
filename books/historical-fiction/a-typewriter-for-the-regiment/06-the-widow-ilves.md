## 6. The Widow Ilves

The Widow Ilves lived at the end of Cordwainer Lane, in a house the color of an old bruise, and she had not spoken a word since the soldiers came.

That was the story, anyway, and Vellmark told stories the way my father set type — carefully, with attention to spacing, so that every word sat where it could be read at a glance. Marta Ilves had gone silent the morning the requisition order went up on the courthouse door. She had stood in the square with her market basket on her arm, reading the colonel's proclamation through her spectacles, and then she had turned around and walked home, and she had not said a word to anyone since. Eleven days of silence. The baker counted. The baker counted everything.

I told myself I was going for ordinary reasons. I had eggs to spare — three of them, which in Vellmark in the first week of occupation was practically a dowry — and it was the kind of neighborly errand that drew no attention. A girl with eggs. Nobody arrests a girl with eggs. I had learned to think this way now, to wrap every movement of my body in a layer of dullness, and I was almost proud of how good I had gotten at it, and then I was ashamed of being proud, and by the time I reached Cordwainer Lane I had argued myself into and out of cowardice twice.

The truth was simpler. Tomas Ilves's name was in the ledger I was setting, and it was marked, and I had not been able to cut the letters of it without my hands shaking.

*Ilves, Tomas. Son of Marta. Enemy of the state. Property of the household subject to seizure.*

I had set a hundred enemy names by then. The army's clerk read them out in his flat capital voice and I sorted type and I did not flinch, because flinching is a luxury for people whose names are not on lists. But Tomas's name — Tomas, who had taught me the alphabet by drawing letters in the river mud with a stick, who had told me the letter Q was a proud letter, "a letter with its tail up, like you" — his name went into the forme like a nail going into a coffin, and I had stood there afterward looking at the reversed metal, at *ILVES* spelled backward and wrong the way all type is wrong until it's pressed into paper, and I had thought: somewhere in this town his mother is sitting in a bruise-colored house, and she doesn't know yet, or maybe she does, and either way she is alone with it.

So: eggs. And underneath the eggs, something I couldn't have named yet.

* * *

Her garden was the only one on the lane that had been seen to since the soldiers came. Everyone else had let the frost take what it wanted. But the Widow Ilves had been out with her shears, and the dead stalks stood in tidy rows like a regiment of their own, and the gate was freshly oiled and closed with a click so soft it was nearly an apology.

I knocked. I waited. I knocked again, and from inside came a sound I recognized from my father's shop — the particular scrape of a heavy thing being moved across a floor, deliberate, unhurried, the sound of someone who wants you to know they heard you and are deciding about you.

Then the door opened, and there she was.

Marta Ilves had been a big woman once, broad as a door, the kind of woman who could carry a sack of flour on one hip and a complaining child on the other and still have an arm free to point out your moral failings. The occupation had gone through her like a fire through a house. What was left was smaller, and stooped, and her hands — her wonderful, terrible hands, hands that had boxed my ears when I was nine for saying a swear word I hadn't even understood — those hands were wrapped around a walking stick as if the stick were the last honest thing in Vellmark.

She looked at the eggs. She looked at me. She did not say anything, because she did not say anything, but her eyebrows performed an entire conversation.

"Good morning, Widow Ilves," I said, in the bright, empty voice I used with soldiers. "I had eggs to spare. From the Kesslers. You know how it is, their hen overproduces, and Father says —"

She stepped aside and let me in, which was her way of telling me she knew exactly how it was, and that she had outlived two husbands and one empire and was not going to be managed by a girl with eggs.

* * *

The inside of the house was cold in the way houses get when the person keeping them has stopped believing in the future. But it was clean. That was the first thing I noticed, the thing I kept coming back to all afternoon: the floors were swept, the dishes were done, the mantel was dusted, and on the mantel, in a place of honor, stood a photograph of Tomas at eighteen, in the coat he'd left for the capital in, looking at the camera with the half-smile of a boy who knows something you don't.

I knew that half-smile. I had spent a good portion of my childhood trying to find out what he knew. I never had.

She gestured me into a chair and went to the stove, and I watched her make tea without a single wasted motion, and I understood that her silence was not emptiness. It was the opposite. It was so full it had nowhere to go. Some people's grief is a flood and some people's is a freeze, and Marta Ilves had frozen solid, and everything in her — the rage, the fear, the love, all of it — was in there, under the ice, moving like the river moves in January. You could hear it if you listened.

I set the eggs on the table. She looked at them.

"Three," I said, stupidly, as if the number were the important part.

She sat down across from me. She poured the tea. And then, because I have never in my life been able to leave a silence alone — my father says it's my worst trait and also the reason I'm a printer, because a printer's whole job is refusing to let blank paper stay blank — I said:

"You've seen the proclamation."

Her eyes came up. They were Tomas's eyes exactly, gray with a green thread in them, and they held mine with a patience that was worse than anger.

"About the lists," I went on, and my voice, that bright empty voice, finally cracked and let something real through. "The loyalty lists. They're printing them at our shop. I set the type. I —" I stopped. I had rehearsed a dozen versions of this conversation on the walk over and every one of them had ended with me saying something wise and comforting, and now that I was here I had nothing, because there was nothing. "I wanted you to hear it from me. From someone. Not from the baker."

The Widow Ilves looked at me for a long moment. Then she put down her cup, rose with the help of her stick, crossed the room, and did something I will remember until I die.

She took the photograph of Tomas off the mantel, turned it around, and opened the back of the frame.

* * *

Letters. A fat bundle of them, tied with butcher's twine, and beneath the twine the paper had gone soft at the edges from being handled. She carried them back to the table the way you carry a sleeping child, and she set them down in front of me, and she untied the string with fingers that shook only a little, and she opened the top letter to a marked page and turned it toward me.

She had never stopped reading them, I understood. She had only stopped speaking. Those were not the same thing at all. I had made the mistake everyone in town had made — I had confused silence with surrender, and here was this woman who had simply closed her mouth and opened everything else.

The letter was in Tomas's hand. I knew his hand the way you know a voice — he had a slanted, impatient script, letters leaning forward like they were in a hurry to get somewhere, and I had spent years of copybooks trying to make my own letters lean like that and being told by the schoolmistress that I slanted, Quentin, you slant, and I had taken it as a compliment.

*Dearest Mama* — it began, and then a paragraph of the small domestic things he knew she lived on, his health, his lodging, the price of boots in the capital — and then, halfway down the second page:

*— and the machine is sent ahead of me, so don't let them tell you the freight is lost. It went down with the Herrmann consignment weeks ago. A typewriter, Mama, a real one, and not for me — I'm sending it to Vasquez, to old Vasquez's shop, because there are things that need to be written that shouldn't be in my hand, and a machine writes in no one's hand at all. Tell them to keep it in the cellar, the dry corner, under the floorboards by the coal chute. Don't let them sell it. Don't let them open it. And don't ask me why, not in a letter. When I come home at midwinter I'll show you what it's for.*

Midwinter. The letter was dated two years past. He had not come home at midwinter. He had not come home at all, and the letters had stopped eight months ago, and the bundle in the widow's hands was the whole of what she had.

I looked up at her. "There's a machine," I said. "In our cellar."

She nodded. One nod. And then — this is the part I keep turning over, the part I can't make sit still — the corner of her mouth moved. Not a smile. The ghost of one, the place where a smile had been before the occupation, a small bitter twitch that said: *well, girl? You wanted the truth. There it is. It's been under your feet the whole time.*

"Under the floorboards," I said. "By the coal chute. We keep the — we store the misprints there. I've walked over it a hundred times."

She reached across the table and turned the letters toward me again, and tapped one line with her stick-hard finger. *Don't let them open it.* And then she tapped it again, harder, and her eyes were not bitter anymore. They were burning. Whatever had frozen in her was cracking, right there at the table, and what was underneath was not grief. Grief was on top. Underneath was something with teeth.

"You knew," I said slowly. "He wrote to you. All this time, you knew what he — you knew there was something in my father's cellar, and you never said."

The widow drew herself up. Even stooped, even shrunken, she managed to look down at me, and her silence, for the first time, felt like a choice rather than a wound. She picked up the letters, retied the twine with two efficient pulls, and held the bundle against her chest.

And then she did speak. One word. Her first in eleven days, rasped out like a hinge that hadn't been oiled, so quiet I had to lean in to catch it:

"*Print.*"

* * *

I don't remember walking home. I remember the eggs — she made me take two back, pressing them into my hands with a look that permitted no argument, because even frozen, even grieving, the Widow Ilves was not going to be in anyone's debt for a whole three eggs — and I remember the river, gray and locked, and the soldiers at the corner by the chandlery who didn't look at me at all, because a girl carrying eggs is the most invisible thing in an occupied town, and I remember thinking, over and over, the way you worry a loose tooth:

*Don't let them open it. Don't let them open it.*

I had been walking over it for two years.

Our cellar is not large. It holds the coal, the misprint barrels, my father's collection of broken chases that he swears he'll mend, and the particular smell every print shop cellar has, of lead and lamp oil and cold stone. I had gone down a thousand times for a stick of type, a rag, a bottle of ink. I knew every crate down there by its stenciled markings. HERRMANN CONSIGNMENT — I knew that one. It sat in the dry corner by the coal chute, exactly where a letter had said it would, and for two years I had taken it for what it appeared to be: freight, misdelivered, forgotten, one of the dozen anonymous boxes a print shop accumulates the way a church accumulates dust.

My father was asleep in the back room when I came in. I stood in the doorway and looked at him a long moment — at his ruined hands folded on his blanket, at his face gone sharp and translucent the way faces do at the end — and I thought: *he knows. He has to know. Tomas wrote to him too, or the widow told him, or he opened the crate himself two years ago and made his own decision to say nothing.* My father, who had taught me that a printer's first duty is to the record. My father, who sat in that cellar every evening with his pipe.

I took the lamp off its hook and went down the stairs into the cold.

The coal chute's draft stirred the flame as I knelt in the dry corner. The crate was right where the letter said, its boards silvered with age, its stencil half worn away. There was no lock. There had never needed to be one. The lock was the fact that nobody in an army-occupied town looks twice at a box of old freight in a printer's cellar — and the deeper lock, the one that had held for two years, was whatever my father had decided, alone, in the dark, with his failing hands, about what was worth keeping and what it would cost.

I got the crowbar from under the stairs. I set the edge to the first board, and I paused — one breath, the lamp flame steady, the river ice groaning somewhere far off under the town like a thing turning over in its sleep — and I thought about Tomas's half-smile, the boy who always knew something I didn't.

I pried up the board.
