## 8. Apprentice to No One

The first thing you learn about forgery, Quentin discovered on her third night of it, is that it is mostly patience and only a little crime.

She had imagined something more dramatic — masks, dark lanterns, a getaway through the coal chute. Instead there was a candle turned low behind the paper screen in the composing room, a stick of type, and her own hands, which had never once trembled at a jobbing case in eleven years and now shook like a wet dog every time she lifted a letter that did not belong to her.

The ledger's plates lay locked in their chases under the floorboard her father had loosened with a chisel two nights before, saying nothing, humming an old river song while he worked, as though he were fixing a squeaky hinge rather than committing treason. The army believed the plates were stored in the army's own strongbox upstairs, because Quentin had shown Sergeant Dorn the strongbox herself, with a flourish, and let him admire the lock. Dorn admired locks the way other men admired horses. He had patted it twice and left satisfied.

Under the floorboard, the town of Vellmark waited in metal.

Quentin knelt on the cold boards with the forme open before her and read the names by candlelight like scripture. *Aaltonen, Marta — loyal. Aaltonen, Pietr — loyal, debt noted. Brandvold, Ester — suspect, harbor sympathies.* On and on, the whole town sorted into two columns by men who had been in Vellmark for nine days and could not have found the church without asking a child.

She had started small. That was the discipline of it — her father's voice in her head, though he hadn't said a word: *Never begin a job with the hard part. Begin with the part that teaches your hands.* So she had not touched Tomas Ilves. Not yet. She had begun with old Widow Kask, whose name appeared as *Kask, Anneli — suspect,* for no reason Quentin could find except that the widow had once shouted at a requisition sergeant about her hens, and shouting was apparently a capital offense against the state's feelings.

Quentin cut a new *s* — no, she didn't cut; she *sorted*, she *reset*; cutting was for characters that didn't exist yet, and the alphabet, thank God, already existed. She reset the entry. *Kask, Anneli — loyal.* Two letters moved, one word changed. It took her forty minutes because she checked each piece against the surrounding type six times, holding it to the candle, comparing serifs, weighing it in her fingers like a jeweler.

Then she pulled a proof onto scrap paper, looked at it, and felt something she had no name for. It wasn't guilt. She decided firmly it was not guilt. It was more like the feeling of stepping off a ferry onto moving water — the ground agreeing to hold you after all.

She set the plate back, replaced the board, scattered sawdust over the scuff marks, and went up to bed, where she lay awake listening to her father cough in the next room and thinking: *I am a criminal now.* And then, because she was her father's daughter: *I am a very slow criminal.*

* * *

By the fifth night she had learned the first great secret of forgery, which was that the state's own typesetting was sloppy.

This delighted her in a way she felt slightly ashamed of. The loyalty lists had been composed by army clerks working from handwritten muster sheets, and the clerks were soldiers first and spellers second. They set *harbor* as *harbor* one time and *harbor* another. Their spacing wandered like a drunk walking home. Their em-quads were a scandal. Which meant that if Quentin made her alterations too *clean* — too even, too correct — they would stand out like a Sunday coat at a funeral. To forge the army, she had to forge the army's mistakes.

"Set it ugly," she whispered to herself, spacing out *suspect* so it sagged in the middle the way the clerks' work did. "Ugly is invisible."

Her father appeared in the doorway. She froze with a piece of type in her hand, absurdly, as if stillness would make her invisible, which works for herons and not for people.

He looked at her. She looked at him. The candle between them did its little flickering dance, the only honest witness in the room.

"There's tea," he said.

"Thank you."

"It's on the stove."

"I know where the stove is, Papa."

"Mm." He stood there a moment longer, in his nightshirt, his big ruined hands hanging at his sides — hands that had set forty years of other people's truths and now couldn't hold a composing stick for ten minutes without the ache waking him at night. His eyes went to the floorboard. Then to her face. Then back to the floorboard, with the mild expression of a man noting that the weather had turned.

"The third board from the wall creaks," he said. "If anyone comes down, step on the second instead. Second holds quiet."

And he went back up to bed, leaving the tea, leaving everything unsaid, which was the Vasquez family's native language.

Quentin sat down on the stairs and laughed until she cried a little, quietly, into her sleeve. He knew. Of course he knew. He had probably known since the night she'd asked him, with elaborate casualness, whether he thought the army counted its type. He had answered, equally casually, that armies count bullets, because counting letters requires reading them — and had gone back to his newspaper wearing the faint smile of a man who has just handed his daughter a loaded gun and watched her walk out the door with it.

* * *

The near-discovery came on the ninth night, and afterward Quentin could never decide whether it had lasted four minutes or four years.

She was deep in the work — three names altered now, plus the widow Kask, all of them small, all of them deniable, none of them Tomas — when boots came down the alley outside. Not passing boots. *Stopping* boots. The kind of boots that stop because they have heard something, or think they have, or are bored and want to hear something.

Quentin pinched out the candle so fast she burned her thumb and stood in absolute blackness, one hand flat on the forme, listening to her own heart conduct an unseemly parade.

The side door — the one she'd oiled, the one she'd *oiled*, she remembered oiling it with a smugness that now seemed cosmically punished — rattled.

"Printer!" A voice, young, muffled by the door. "Printer Vasquez!"

Her father's cough answered before she could. It floated down the stairwell, theatrical in its timing, and then his footsteps, slow, shuffling, the footsteps of a dying man who had been asleep and resented it. Quentin pressed herself into the shadow behind the paper stock, the forme at her back, and understood with total clarity that if the soldier came down those stairs she was going to have to do something with the crowbar, and she did not want to think about what.

The door opened. Lamplight spilled down the top of the stairwell.

"Proclamation run at dawn," the sentry said. "Colonel's orders. Two thousand copies. The press is to be inked and ready."

"At dawn," her father said. "Yes. I'll want my daughter for the feed board. She's the quick one."

"She here?"

"Asleep, like any sensible person at this hour. Unlike some." A pause, delicately rude. "Was there anything else? At this hour?"

The lamplight lingered. Quentin watched dust turn in it and thought about the forme six feet behind her, the altered plates, the proof sheet she had not yet burned sitting in the ash bucket where any lamp thrust downward would find it white as a confession.

"No," the sentry said finally. "Dawn."

"Dawn," her father agreed, and closed the door, and shot the bolt, and stood at the top of the stairs breathing hard — not from the climb, she realized, but from the same parade her heart had been conducting.

They looked at each other across the dark.

"He'll be back at dawn," Quentin whispered.

"Yes."

"So I can't finish tonight."

"No."

"Papa—"

"Second board," he said, "holds quiet. And the ash bucket wants emptying before you sleep, because ash from a fire nobody lit is a funny thing to explain to a man with a lamp." He paused on the stairs. "Also the tea's cold by now. It's always cold by now. You'd think after nine nights you'd drink it when it's hot."

He went up. Quentin emptied the ash bucket out the window into the snow, where it made a gray star on the white, evidence for exactly as long as it took the next snowfall to forgive her. Then she sat on the cold stairs and drank cold tea in the dark and thought about what had just happened, which was nothing, which was everything: her father had lied to a soldier of the occupation with the smoothness of a man ordering bread, and neither of them had said one word about why.

* * *

The signal system invented itself over the following week, the way systems do in families — not designed but grown, like moss.

It began with the tea. Her father took to leaving the teapot on the stove at night, and Quentin took to finding it, and one morning she noticed that when the pot sat on the *left* burner it meant *all clear, work tonight,* and when it sat on the right — well, the first time it sat on the right, she spent the evening upstairs doing mending with her mother's old thimble and feeling like a girl kept in from the river, until near midnight she heard why: boots below, lamplight, the sentry making his rounds early, checking on the press that belonged to the army now. Her father sat up with him for an hour discussing the colonel's opinions about ink coverage. In the morning the pot was back on the left, and beside it, folded under the tin lid, a slip of paper in her father's upright, unhurried hand:

*Right burner means company. Left means the coast is a coast. Do not get clever about other signals. Cleverness is how printers die.*

She wrote back — because of course she did, she was her father's daughter and obedience in that house had always been a negotiation — and slipped the note under his door:

*Noted. Also your em-spacing on yesterday's proclamation drifted wide after the third paragraph. If you're going to print their lies, Papa, at least set them tight.*

The note came back the next morning with one line added beneath hers, in the same hand:

*Tight lies last longer than loose ones. Ask any government. — Also, the widow Kask reads nothing anymore but her hymnal. Whatever you did or didn't do, her hens came back Tuesday. Strange weather we're having.*

Strange weather indeed. That was the whole of their conspiracy: a teapot, a few notes, and a shared vocabulary of things not said. They never spoke of the ledger. They never spoke of Tomas Ilves, though once, sorting type together in daylight for an army handbill, her father's fingers found the *I-l-v-e-s* ligature box in the case — the letters they'd cast special years ago because Ilves was a name with too many of the awkward ones — and rested there a moment, on the little wooden compartment, the way you'd rest a hand on a grave or a cradle.

"He had good hands," her father said. "That boy. Quick at the case. Never once put an *e* where an *o* went."

"You never let me forget it either."

"Because you did it *twice*." But he said it softly, and his fingers stayed on the compartment a moment longer, and then he took up his stick and went back to setting the colonel's proclamation about grain requisitions, in tight, beautiful, damning type.

* * *

On the twelfth night, Quentin finally opened the chase that held the *I* entries.

She had promised herself she wouldn't rush it. She had rehearsed it in her head like a wedding. And still, when she lifted the page-plate and found the line — *Ilves, Tomas — enemy of the state, property forfeit* — set there in the army clerk's drunken, wandering spacing, her breath went out of her like she'd been struck in the stomach with a padded mail sack.

Enemy of the state. Property forfeit. The boy who taught her the alphabet by cutting letters from potato prints because she was five and impatient and the shop's type was too precious to let children touch. *Q is the fancy one,* he'd said, pressing a potato stamped with a Q into her palm. *It's just an O that decided to be somebody.* She had carried that sentence around for fifteen years like a coin in her pocket and never once told him what it was worth.

Property forfeit. The house on Tanner's Row where his mother sat silent among her son's hidden letters. Forfeit to whom? To the colonel's quartermaster, presumably. To some lieutenant who needed lodging. The state, having declared Tomas Ilves an enemy, proposed to sell his childhood out from under the woman who kept his letters in a flour tin.

Quentin sat back on her heels and let the anger finish arriving. It arrived completely.

Then she got to work, because anger is fuel and she had been raised not to waste fuel.

The problem was elegant and horrible. She could not simply remove the line — a missing entry would show, the way a pulled tooth shows. She could not overwrite it heavily — the army's own clerks might re-pull proofs and compare. What she could do — what her father's notes had been circling for days without saying — was make the entry *move*. Recompose it, in the army's own ugly style, with the name respaced, the designation softened, the forfeiture clause transposed into the conditional grammar the clerks used for cases *pending review*. Pending review. The three most beautiful words in the language. A case pending review shipped downstream as paperwork, not verdict — a question mark instead of a death warrant, and questions could be reopened, argued, survived.

She worked until her eyes burned. She pulled the proof. Held it to the candle. Set it back. Pulled it again. Compared the serifs of her *p-e-n-d-i-n-g* against the clerk's *f-o-r-f-e-i-t* until she could see both in her sleep, and did, that night, unpleasantly.

Near dawn she climbed the stairs and found her father awake, propped on his pillows, watching the window go gray.

"Well?" he said. Only that.

"Tight," she said. "Tight as a government."

He nodded slowly, and something crossed his face that was pride and grief in equal measure, the exact mixture, she would think later, of every inheritance ever passed. "Then tomorrow," he said, "I'll teach you the magnifying-glass trick. Because the colonel owns a magnifying glass, my girl, and a man who carries one to dinner is a man who looks closely at things."

Outside, the river ice groaned in the dark — a long, settling sound, like the world clearing its throat before it spoke.

Quentin stood at the window and listened, and for the first time it sounded less like winter holding on, and more like a countdown beginning.
