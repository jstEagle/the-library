## 9. The Printer's Hands

Aurelio Vasquez had spent fifty-one years setting other people's truths in metal, and he had come to a conclusion about it that he intended to take to his grave — or as near to the grave as the winter allowed, which was close enough now that he could smell the lilies before anyone had thought to buy any.

The conclusion was this: nobody owns their own story. Not the magistrate with his ledgers, not the colonel with his lists, not even the widow who swears she was married on a Tuesday when every church record says Thursday. A town's truth is like water. It belongs to whoever holds the bucket.

For most of his life, Aurelio had held the bucket for Vellmark and been paid modestly for the privilege.

He woke before dawn, as he always did, though dawn itself had become an increasingly theoretical proposition. The window of the room above the shop showed only grey. His hands were the first thing he consulted each morning — you learned to read them the way other men read weather. Today they said: two hours of good work, perhaps three, then the shaking would come up from the wrists like a debt collector.

Two hours. Well. He had taught apprentices to cut a serviceable 'e' in less.

He dressed carefully. This took longer than it used to. There had been a time when he could dress in the dark, one-handed, while chewing bread, because Quentin's mother had still been asleep beside him and he'd have sooner died than wake her. He died a little differently now, he supposed. More gradually. With more time to chew the bread.

The stairs down to the shop were fourteen. He counted them out of habit and out of something else he didn't have a word for. A man counts stairs the way he counts remaining teeth: not because he'll forget, but because the counting is its own small defiance.

Quentin was already at the stone. Of course she was. He could hear her before he saw her — the particular whisper of a graver doing finish-work, that sound like a secret being told very politely to a piece of lead. She had her back to him, hair pinned up with what appeared to be one of her mother's old combs, and she was hunched over the type in the posture he recognized from mirrors: shoulders curved around the work like a wall built against interruption.

She hadn't slept. He could tell by the lamp oil level and the way she flinched slightly when he cleared his throat — the flinch of a person caught somewhere they weren't supposed to be at an hour that didn't officially exist.

"Good morning," he said, in the tone that meant *I know exactly what you've been doing and we will discuss it at the proper moment.*

"Morning, Papa." She turned. Her face attempted innocence and got about halfway there, which for Quentin was a personal best. "You're up early."

"I'm up when my hands say so. Your mother used to say I'd argue with God himself if God gave me a delivery date." He lowered himself into the chair by the stove — lowering had become the operative verb; descending suggested control — and held his palms toward the warmth. "Show me."

She hesitated. Then she brought the galley over and set it in his lap, the way you'd hand someone a sleeping infant, and Aurelio looked at the night's work.

She had altered four names. Four. He read the plates the way he read everything, with his fingertips first and his eyes second, and what his fingers found made him close his eyes briefly — not grief exactly, and not fear, but the strange vertigo of watching your child walk out onto ice you yourself had warned her was thin, and discovering she'd brought nails and hammer along to build on it anyway.

"You cut the serifs back," he said. "On the substituted letters."

"So they'd sit lighter in the line."

"Mm." He turned the galley toward the lamplight. "And here — the 'a' in Aldermann. You matched the punch, but you didn't match the wear."

She frowned. "Wear?"

"There it is." He tapped the plate gently. "Sit down, Quentin. We're going to talk about how a lie grows old."

* * *

Here is what fifty-one years teaches a printer, and what no manual will tell you because no printer worth his salt has ever written a manual:

Every letter lies about its age the moment it's born. Fresh-cut type is a newborn wearing its father's coat. It takes months of pressing before a character develops the small honest damages of work — the softened corners where the paper has kissed it ten thousand times, the hairline bruise across a serif where somebody dropped the stick, the faint asymmetry that comes from a cutter's mood on a Tuesday in March. A page set entirely in new type looks wrong to a trained eye the way a man's hands look wrong without calluses. Nobody can say precisely why. Everybody feels it.

"The colonel," Aurelio said, "has a magnifying glass and a suspicious soul, which is the worst possible combination in a man, because suspicion gives the glass somewhere to go. If he examines your ledger plates and finds characters that are too perfect — too young — he won't know what he's looking at. But he'll feel it. And a man like Brandt trusts his feelings more than his eyes. That's what makes him dangerous."

"So I age the letters."

"So you *hurt* them," Aurelio corrected, and enjoyed the way she sat back slightly. "Carefully. In specific places. You're not damaging type, Quentin. You're giving it a biography."

He made her fetch the old case — the veteran type, the letters that had printed the parish records and the tax notices and eleven years of auction bills — and he laid a worn 'e' beside her fresh-cut one under the lamp.

"Look. Here." He pointed with a bodkin. "This corner has gone soft. Why?"

"Pressure. Ten thousand impressions—"

"Twelve thousand, if you must be accurate; I counted once during a slow Lent. And here—" the bodkin moved to a hairline nick along the stem, "—this came the winter of '04, when I dropped the composing stick reaching for the coffee. I swore for a full minute, in front of the mayor's wife. She pretended not to hear. That is also part of the craft, incidentally — knowing which of your customers are pretending not to hear things. Half of Vellmark's secrets live in this shop and none of them were ever told to me directly."

"Papa."

"Yes, yes. Biography." He handed her the graver. "Now. Take your new 'e' and give it twelve thousand impressions' worth of history. Soften the corner. One scratch, small, somewhere a scratch could plausibly happen. And then — this is the trick entire — stop. The amateur forger ages everything evenly, because he thinks wear is uniform. It isn't. Wear is *accident*, and accidents are local. One letter in twenty should look older than its neighbors. One letter in fifty should look almost new, as if it were a replacement — because every honest old page has one or two, from the days when a compositor broke a letter and swapped in fresh stock rather than reset the line. Perfection is the fingerprint of guilt. Imperfection, distributed carelessly, is the fingerprint of honesty."

Quentin worked. He watched her hands.

They were good hands. That was the thing that undid him a little, sitting there in the grey light with his own hands trembling in his lap like birds deciding whether to stay. They were his hands, is the thing — the same long second finger, the same callus on the right thumb from the burnisher, the same habit of tucking the little finger under when concentrating — but they were better than his had been at her age, because he had been a merely talented boy learning a trade, and she was a gifted girl learning a crime, and crime, whatever the clergy said, sharpens a student wonderfully.

"Slower," he murmured. "A scratch made in fear shows fear. Make it like you're bored."

"I'm not bored."

"You will be. Boredom is ninety percent of this trade and all of its honesty. Any fool can print beautifully when he cares. The craft is printing beautifully when you don't."

* * *

They worked through the morning in the rhythm they'd fallen into since the soldiers came — which was to say, the rhythm of people speaking plainly only in metaphor. The army had ordered proclamations for afternoon delivery; the presses needed to be dressed; Sergeant Mirele would arrive at two o'clock sharp to collect the forms, stand in the doorway with his cap in his hands like a schoolboy at a headmaster's door, and leave without having looked at anything, which was its own kind of message, though neither Vasquez said so aloud.

But between jobs, while Quentin washed the rollers, Aurelio sat in his chair and let his memory do what his hands could no longer do, which was work.

He thought about his own father, who had kept this same shop and died at this same desk, and who had taught him nothing whatsoever about forgery, because in his father's day the question had never come up. His father's generation believed in records the way farmers believed in rain: as a force of nature, indifferent but reliable. Set the name true, his father said, and God would mind the rest. Aurelio had set forty thousand names true in his life. Weddings, births, debts, deaths, the price of herring, the outcome of elections nobody had voted in. He had been Vellmark's memory for half a century, and he had told himself that made him neutral, the way a mirror is neutral.

It had taken an occupying army and his daughter's sleepless eyes to teach him that a mirror that refuses to lie is not neutral. It is loyal — to whoever stands in front of it.

"Tomas Ilves," he said suddenly, because his mouth had begun doing that thing it did now, saying the quiet parts aloud without consulting him.

Quentin went still over the wash sink. Water dripped. Somewhere outside, boots passed in the frozen street, unhurried, the boots of men who owned the morning.

"He taught you the alphabet," Aurelio continued. "I remember. You were five and I was too busy to notice my daughter couldn't read, which is the sort of failure a man doesn't forgive himself until he's dying and has the leisure. He sat you on that crate by the door — the herring crate — and wrote letters backwards on the fogged glass so you could read them from inside. Backwards. Because he figured out before either of us that you'd learn faster if the letters were a puzzle."

"He cut his name into that crate," Quentin said quietly. "I found it last month, under the paint. T. ILVES, and underneath, in tiny letters, Q.V., apprentice."

Aurelio smiled. It hurt his face to smile now, or perhaps it hurt everything to everything, at this stage the geography of pain had become debatable. "Then the debt runs both directions. Good. Debts that run both directions are the only kind that hold a town together."

"Papa, about the manuscript in the cellar—"

"No." He raised one trembling hand — deliberately trembling it further, for effect, an old showman's trick — "Not today. Today you learn one thing properly. Tomorrow, if my heart cooperates, we discuss what to do with a truth too large for a single plate."

* * *

The afternoon proclamations went off without incident, which in occupied Vellmark meant without incident visible above the surface. Mirele collected his forms, blushed at nothing in particular, and departed. A lieutenant signed the delivery chit with a flourish that suggested he believed himself to be signing a treaty. Quentin burned the wastepaper in the stove, all except one bundle, which went into the pile bound for the magistrate's clerk, and Aurelio watched her do it and said nothing, because a teacher's final duty is to stop teaching at the correct moment and simply bear witness.

By evening his hands had retired for the day entirely. He sat in the chair by the press with a blanket over his knees like a man at a seaside he'd never visited, and called instructions across the shop in the manner of a ship's captain who has become the mast.

"Your 'r' is drifting leftward. You always drift leftward when you're tired. Your grandfather drifted rightward. There's a moral in that, probably, but I'm too comfortable to find it."

"It's straight," Quentin said.

"It's *nearly* straight, which is worse. Nearly-straight is the signature of a liar working hard. Truly crooked is honest. Truly straight is honest. Nearly anything is where the devil lives." He paused, then added, more gently: "Also you haven't eaten since yesterday. Bring the bread over and eat while you work. Your mother ate standing up her whole life and she lived to eighty-one, or would have, if the fever hadn't disagreed."

Quentin fetched the bread. She sat on the floor beside his chair, cross-legged like the child on the herring crate, and worked on a plate balanced on her knees, and ate, and the shop smelled of ink and woodsmoke and rye, and Aurelio Vasquez looked at the ceiling beams he had known longer than his own marriage and felt the peculiar lightness that comes near the end of things — not peace exactly, but the sensation of a forme being locked up tight, every piece of type in its place, quoins snug, ready at last for the press.

"Quentin."

"Hm?"

"When I'm done — and we needn't discuss when, the river will announce it better than I can — the shop is yours. Not because I say so. Because you can carry it. There's a difference, and most inheritances fail on it. Men pass down property to children who can't lift it, and the property crushes them. You, my girl, have been lifting this place since you were six. I'm just making the paperwork honest."

She didn't answer for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice was steady, which cost her, and he knew it cost her, and loved her the more for spending it. "You're not done yet. You still owe me the rest of the magnifying-glass trick."

"Do I?" He closed his eyes, savoring the setup. "And what makes you think I didn't teach you all of it already?"

"Because you saved the hardest part for last your whole life. Mama said so."

"Your mother," said Aurelio Vasquez, "talked too much." But he was smiling, and she was smiling, and outside the window the river ice gave a long groan — deeper than the night before, closer, the sound of a giant turning over in bed — and both of them stopped, and listened, and understood without saying it that the winter was keeping its own ledger, and its entries were running short.

"One more lesson tomorrow," he said. "The last one. About locking a forme so tight that even the truth can't fall out of it."

He heard her breath catch, just slightly — the sound of a woman realizing her father has known the whole plan all along, and has been improving upon it in silence.

The ice groaned again, and began, very faintly, to tick.
