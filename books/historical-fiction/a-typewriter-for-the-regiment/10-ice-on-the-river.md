## 10. Ice on the River

The river froze on a Tuesday, all at once, the way a lamp goes out.

Quentin heard it from her bed in the loft above the print shop — a long groan that traveled up through the floorboards and into the frame of the house itself, as though the building had been asked a question it couldn't answer. She lay still and listened. The sound came again, deeper this time, a grinding complaint that went on for nearly a minute, and then stopped, and then there was nothing at all. No current. No slap of water against the pilings of the ferry dock. Just silence, thick as felt.

By morning the Vellmark was a road.

She stood at the shop window with her breath fogging the glass and watched men walk out onto the ice where no man had any business walking — soldiers first, testing it with bayonets, then teamsters with sledges, then children, because children will always go where they are told not to. The river that had carried every barge, every shipment, every order and counter-order for two hundred years now held its cargo still, like a clerk who has put down his pen mid-sentence and simply walked away.

"Solid," her father said behind her. He said it the way other men pronounced a diagnosis. "Down to the gravel, I'd wager. My grandfather used to say the Vellmark doesn't freeze, it *quits*."

"It quits," Quentin agreed.

"And with it," her father said, "the army's post."

He was right, and she had known he would be, and she had been dreading being right since the first hard frost in November. The requisition order nailed to their door had named the print shop a facility of the occupying regiment; the ledger's type sat locked in its cases under Sergeant Mirele's indifferent guard; and every week a courier had gone downstream with proofs of the loyalty lists to be ratified at division headquarters before the final plates shipped. Now the couriers were stuck too. The whole machinery of the occupation — its proclamations, its decrees, its lists of who would keep a roof over his head come spring — sat frozen on the Vellmark like a fly in amber.

Which meant, Quentin understood, standing there with her hands growing warm against the cold glass, that she had time.

And which meant, three seconds later, that she had a deadline.

Both thoughts arrived together, the way they always did, twins that could not be separated. Time was not a gift. Time was a rope with a knot at the end of it, and the knot was coming whether she pulled or not.

* * *

Colonel Brandt announced it four days later, in the town square, in weather so cold the words seemed to crackle coming off his breath.

Quentin was there because Mirele had told her to be there — "he'll want the printer present, it's an official announcement, there'll be a broadside" — and so she stood at the edge of the crowd with her collar up and her mittens hiding the ink stains that never quite left her knuckles, and listened.

"The river," Brandt said, "has closed." He was a tall man who wore his courtesy like a dress uniform: correct, pressed, and never once comfortable. "All shipments are suspended until the ice-break. This is an inconvenience. It is not a delay." He let that sit in the frozen air while the crowd shifted and breathed steam. "When the ice breaks — and it will break, gentlemen, the Vellmark has never once failed to break — everything goes downstream at once. Every list. Every record. Every decision this regiment has made on your behalf." A thin smile. "You may consider the winter, then, a period of review. If anyone believes an error has entered our records, the winter is the season in which errors may be brought forward for correction."

He said it kindly. That was the terrible thing about Brandt; he said everything kindly. It was the kindness of a man reading aloud from a document that condemns you, taking care with the pronunciation.

Quentin walked home through the snow doing arithmetic she did not want to do.

The ice-break came late February some years, March others. Her father remembered a year it held until April and old men loved telling that story, but old men also loved telling stories about winters worse than this one, and this one was already bad enough. Call it six weeks. Perhaps eight if the gods were generous, and the gods had shown no particular interest in Vellmark's affairs lately.

Six weeks to finish altering a census ledger plate by plate, name by name, without discovery, in a shop guarded by soldiers, while the colonel hunted a forger he knew existed with the patience of a man who owned the calendar.

Her father was sorting type when she came in — badly, one-handed, his right hand curled in his lap like a sleeping bird — and he looked up at her face and read it the way he read everything, instantly and without mercy.

"He announced the ice-break."

"You were there?"

"I don't need to be there. I can hear a deadline the way you hear a train." He set a piece of type down, missed the slot, tried again. "How many names left?"

"Thirty-one on the enemy list. Eleven marked for seizure. And the Ilves entry is the worst — it's cross-referenced in three places, and each reference has to lie identically or the whole thing falls apart."

"So thirty-one names, eleven houses, one boy, six weeks." He nodded slowly. "In my apprenticeship we reset a hymnal in nine days. Four hundred pages. But a hymnal doesn't have a colonel checking the kerning."

"No," Quentin said. "It just has God."

Her father laughed — a real laugh, surprising them both — and then coughed, and the laugh cost him something, and Quentin pretended not to see what it cost because pretending not to see was the only mercy either of them had left to trade.

* * *

That night she worked.

The army slept badly in Vellmark — sentries stamped their feet on the corner by the chandlery, changed posts at two and at five, and between those hours the streets belonged to the snow and to whoever moved through it quietly enough. Quentin had learned the sentry's rhythm the way she'd once learned the alphabet: patiently, letter by letter, until it made words. Two o'clock change. Forty minutes of true dark after the new man settled. Then the long stretch.

She descended to the shop at half past two, lit the lamp down to its lowest wick, and unlocked the case that held the ledger plates.

The metal was cold enough to bite. She blew on her fingers, flexed them, and began.

Tomas Ilves. Enemy of the state. Property forfeit.

She had cut his name true once, years ago, when she was eleven and he was fourteen and the promise had been made over a composing stick in exactly this room. *Always cut my name true,* he'd said, laughing, teaching her the difference between an I and a J, and she had said yes the way children say yes to promises about the future — lightly, certain the future would be kind enough not to collect.

Now she collected on it herself, in reverse, letter by letter, in lead.

The alteration was delicate work. Not deletion — deletion left gaps, and gaps invited magnifying glasses. Substitution. An entry re-dated. A cross-reference turned to point at a different parish record, one that existed, one that could survive an audit. Her father called it *making a lie with load-bearing walls*, and he had taught her the trick of it in the weeks since the raid: never forge a clean page, forge a dirty one, a page with a smudge and a correction already in it, because the state trusts documents that look used.

Tonight it was the third cross-reference, the one in the debt register, and her hands were steady and her mind was elsewhere — counting weeks, counting names, counting the forty minutes of dark she had left — when the floorboard above her head creaked.

She killed the lamp so fast the flame barely had time to object.

Footsteps. Slow ones. Her father's cane-and-shuffle gait, unmistakable, moving across the loft above. He crossed to the head of the stairs, paused there a long moment — she could feel him listening the way she was listening — and then knocked twice on the stair rail. Pause. One knock more.

Their signal. Invented a month ago without a word of discussion, the way conspirators become conspirators: sentry on the move, come up now.

She banked the plate back in its case, locked it, climbed the stairs into the cold loft where her father stood wrapped in two blankets like a monument to stubbornness.

"Sentry," he whispered. "Changed early. Walked past the window twice."

"Twice?"

"Twice. Counting steps, maybe. Or looking in." His eyes found hers in the dark. "Quentin. Six weeks is not six weeks. Six weeks is however many nights the sentries stay stupid. You cannot spend them all."

"I know."

"Do you? Because your mother spent her whole life saying *I know* and then doing precisely what she knew better than to do, and I loved her for it, but I buried her for it too." He lowered himself onto his stool by the cold stove, breathing carefully. "So tell me the plan. Not the hope. The plan."

Quentin sat on the floor beside him, the way she had as a girl, and told him the truth: that she didn't have a plan yet, only a count. Thirty-one names. Eleven houses. One boy. And a river that would open like a mouth in six weeks and swallow every lie the army had written unless she got the truth underneath it first.

Her father listened all the way through, which was his gift, and then said, "Then we stop working nights alone. Nights alone are how forgers get caught. We work in daylight, under their noses, inside the work they ordered. You're printing their proclamations anyway. Their paper passes through your hands by the hundredweight." He tapped the arm of the stool with one crooked finger. "Hide the river in the rain, girl. Nobody searches a flood."

* * *

The winter tightened.

It tightened around the town — bread queues starting before dawn, the garrison burning furniture from the seized houses for heat, including, Quentin noted with a cold fury she filed away for later, a chair from the Ilves parlor — and it tightened around her own small conspiracy, which now had three members, one of whom wore a uniform.

Mirele came on Thursdays, officially, to collect the week's proofs. Unofficially he came to stand near the type cases and talk about nothing in a voice pitched to carry, while his eyes did the actual conversation. This Thursday his eyes said: *they've doubled the patrols on the warehouse district.* His mouth said: "The colonel compliments the clarity of last week's proclamation. He asks whether the same face is used throughout."

"The same face," Quentin said. "Type doesn't have moods."

"Colonel Brandt believes everything has moods. Rivers. Type. Towns." Mirele squared the proof stack against the bench, unnecessarily, twice. "He said a strange thing this week. He said the ice is the most honest officer in the regiment. It reports for duty every year and it never lies about the date."

"What date does it report?"

"Late February. Early March." Mirele finally looked at her directly, one second, no more. "He has ordered the barges provisioned by the first of February. Everything loaded. Everything ready. So that when the river opens, the lists ship within the hour."

Within the hour. Quentin kept her face pleasant and her hands busy and her heart, somewhere beneath the apron, very quiet. Within the hour meant there would be no final night, no desperate loading, no margin at all. Whatever she intended to do with the true ledger would have to be finished, hidden, and positioned before the ice cracked — because afterward there would be only minutes, and minutes belonged to the army.

"That will be efficient," she said.

"Yes," Mirele said. "He is very proud of it."

He gathered his proofs and left, and Quentin stood in the empty shop among the smells of ink and oil and cold iron, and did the arithmetic again, because arithmetic was the only prayer she trusted.

Six weeks of river. Thirty-one names. Eleven houses. One crate in the cellar holding a dead-seeming typewriter, a traitor's manuscript, and a town's stolen memory. One father running out of seasons. One sergeant whose sister's name now lived on the safe list because Quentin had put it there, which meant Mirele was no longer a soldier who suspected her; he was a conspirator who owed her, and both of those men would hang from the same beam if the wrong drawer opened.

Time, she thought, was not passing. Time was *patrolling*. It walked past the window twice. It counted steps. It waited for her to make the mistake that hope always makes, which is to believe there is more of it than there is.

* * *

On Sunday the town gathered at the riverbank, because there was nothing else to do on Sundays and because watching a frozen river is a form of worship available even to the occupied.

Quentin went with her father, who insisted on walking the whole way rather than be pushed, and who stood at the rail of the ferry dock breathing steam and glaring at the ice as though it owed him money.

They were not the only ones. Half of Vellmark had turned out, and so had the army: out on the white plain of the river, a squad of soldiers was measuring the ice with augers, driving stakes, laying out a road of brushwood and planks from the warehouse district clear across to the far shore. Preparing the crossing route. Provisioning the barges that waited, tarped and patient, at the winter dock.

"They're building a highway," her father murmured. "On top of the river. On top of the thing that's supposed to save us."

Below the dock, under the shelf of ice, Quentin could see the water itself — black, swift, unhurried, going about its business in the dark underneath all that white certainty. It struck her suddenly that the river hadn't quit at all. It was down there doing exactly what it had always done, carrying everything toward the sea, patient as type in a case, waiting for the one warm wind that would let it rise.

The ice looked solid. The ice always looks solid. That was the whole art of it — the same art she practiced nightly at the case, a surface that bears weight, a lie with load-bearing walls.

A child ran out onto the marked road and was shouted back by soldiers, laughing. Somewhere a dog barked at the augers. Colonel Brandt stood at the far end of the dock speaking with two officers, and for a moment his gaze crossed the crowd and rested — courteously, briefly, the way a man rests his hand on a locked drawer — on the print shop's daughter and her failing father.

Then he returned to his maps, and Quentin turned for home, and behind them all the river lay silent under its white lid, keeping every secret, holding every schedule, counting down in the dark.

Six weeks. Maybe five.

She had a ledger to finish, and winter had just begun to keep score.
