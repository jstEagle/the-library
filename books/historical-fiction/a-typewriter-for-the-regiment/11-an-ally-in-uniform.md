## 11. An Ally in Uniform

The trouble with forging a government ledger, Quentin discovered, was that the government kept coming to watch you work.

Not on purpose. That was the maddening part. The soldiers of the occupation treated the print shop like a well they had found — something you went to when you were thirsty and thought nothing of otherwise. They came for proclamations, for ration notices, for the little cards that told a butcher which side of the street he was permitted to stand on. They leaned their rifles against her father's type cases and asked her to hurry. They complained about the cold. One of them, a boy with chilblains on his ears, had fallen asleep for a full quarter hour in the corner by the stove while Quentin sat not six feet away with an enemy's name half-erased under her thumb.

She had developed a theory about it, which she kept to herself: an army is a machine for making men brave about enormous things and careless about small ones. No sentry would abandon his post at the dock. Any one of them would leave his post at her composing stone to chase a cat.

Still, she had counted on carelessness. She had not counted on Sergeant Mirele.

* * *

He arrived on a Tuesday, in the blue hour between the lamplighter's rounds and full dark, and he did not bang on the door the way soldiers banged on doors. He knocked. Twice. Politely. Quentin, who was alone downstairs because her father had gone up to bed early — "the lead," he said, "has gotten heavy lately" — stood very still behind the composing stone and considered the back stairs, the cellar hatch, and the chimney, in that order.

"Print shop," said the voice outside, carefully neutral. "It's Sergeant Mirele. I've a requisition slip."

That was plausible. Requisition slips came at all hours; the army's appetite for paper had no bedtime. Quentin wiped her hands, which was a habit now rather than any use, since the telltale smudge of ink could be explained by a hundred innocent things, and opened the door.

Sergeant Mirele was younger than his uniform. That was the first thing she noticed. The second was that he held his cap in his hands rather than wearing it, as though he were calling on a schoolmistress. The third thing she noticed was that his eyes had already moved past her shoulder to the composing stone, and that they had stopped there, and that he had seen what was on it.

She knew he'd seen it because of what his face did — which was nothing. A man who hadn't seen anything lets his face do whatever it likes. A man who has seen something and decided not to have seen it goes very carefully blank, the way a householder does when the tax assessor walks past his window.

Quentin's heart performed a maneuver she felt in her teeth.

"The requisition," she said, stepping out onto the step and pulling the door mostly shut behind her, "can wait until morning."

"It can't, actually." He held out the slip. His hand was steady. His voice was pitched low, beneath the hearing of the sentry across the street, who was stamping his feet and blowing on them and interested in nothing but his own misery. "Two hundred copies by dawn. Colonel Brandt wants them posted before the officers' inspection." A pause. The kind of pause that costs a man something to hold open. "And I need to come inside to count the stock. Regulations."

"Regulations," Quentin repeated.

"Yes." He looked at her then, straight on, and she saw that underneath the blankness there was something raw and awake, like a man standing on ice he has just heard crack. "Regulations say I count your type stock and log it. So you'll want everything on the stone to be... logged properly."

The sentence sat between them in the cold air, doing its strange double work.

"Sergeant," Quentin said slowly, "if there's something you mean to say, the street's listening too."

His jaw tightened. He glanced left, right. The sentry stamped. Somewhere a shutter banged. Then Mirele said, very quietly, "My sister's name is Anka Mirele. She lives on Tanner's Row, third house past the tannery, with our mother. She's seventeen. She once wrote a letter to a cousin in the capital saying the colonel's boots made him walk like a duck." He swallowed. "It was a funny letter. It was a *stupid* letter. And it got read, because letters get read now, and there's a mark next to her name in a file I have to carry in my own satchel."

Quentin did not move. Down the street, the river groaned under its ice, a long settling sound, like a giant turning over in bed.

"I'm telling you this," Mirele went on, "because ten minutes ago I walked into this doorway intending to arrest you. That was my intention. I rehearsed it. I had the words ready." His laugh was brief and had no humor in it at all. "And then I saw the stone, and I saw what you'd done to the entry for the Kessler brothers — I deliver the lists, Fräulein Vasquez, I know every name on them — and I stood there holding my own arrest in my mouth like a hot coal, and I thought: this woman is the only person in Vellmark who can do what needs doing. And what needs doing is my sister."

The street was quiet. The stove inside ticked. Quentin became aware that she was gripping the door frame hard enough to whiten her knuckles, and made herself let go.

"You understand," she said, "what you're asking."

"No." He shook his head once, honestly. "I understand pieces of it. I understand that if I report you, they take the shop, and they take you, and my sister's mark stays where it is, and I go on carrying files for men who decide with a pencil whether people eat. I understand that if you refuse me, I ought to report you anyway, and we both know I won't, because I've apparently been a bad soldier for some time without noticing." He drew a breath. "What I don't understand is how you sleep."

"Badly," said Quentin. "In shifts."

For the first time his face cracked — not quite a smile, but the place where a smile would later build its house. "That's honest."

"Honesty's the only currency I have left, Sergeant. Everything else in this shop is counterfeit."

* * *

She let him in. Not all the way — never all the way — but into the front room, with the lamp turned low, while she pulled the two hundred copies' worth of type for the inspection notice with the mechanical ease of eleven years' practice, her hands working and her mind running along entirely different tracks.

Because here was the arithmetic she could not escape: a clerk who carried the files was worth more than a whole crate of type. He knew where the marks lived, which pages traveled downstream first, which names Brandt had underlined in his own hand. But a clerk who carried the files could also carry her to the courtyard wall, and no amount of cleverness with lead would help her then. Trust, her father liked to say, is a forme you lock up blind — you don't know what's loose inside it until you pull the proof.

She pulled the proof, so to speak. She watched him while she worked.

Mirele stood where she'd put him, hands clasped behind his back like a museum visitor, and he did not touch anything, and he did not look toward the cellar hatch even though the cellar was the obvious place, and he did not ask a single question about the Kessler entry or the stone or the faint ghost of a corrected line she could still read in the locked plate if she tilted it to the lamp. Instead he talked, quietly, about stock counts. He gave her numbers. He wrote them in his logbook in a neat clerk's hand, and she understood, with a slow spreading warmth that frightened her more than fear did, that he was inventing figures to protect her inventory from future requisitions — padding the losses, hiding the surpluses — and that he had begun conspiring before either of them had agreed to conspire.

"You're good at this," she said.

"At counting?" He glanced up. "I'm good at making things add up. It's most of what an army is, when you get under the uniforms. Things that are supposed to add up." He closed the logbook. Then, after a moment, he added, in a voice stripped of the careful neutrality: "I joined for the boots, if you want the truth. And the regular meals. My father died of a cough the winter I was twelve, and I swore I'd never again watch a winter win." He looked at his cap in his hands. "I didn't know I'd be handing winters out to other people."

The press clock — her grandfather's, older than the occupation, older than the war scare, possibly older than sin — ticked through a silence.

"Anka Mirele," Quentin said at last. "Tanner's Row. What's the mark?"

"Amber. 'Under observation.'" His mouth twisted. "It means she can't get flour on Tuesdays. It means if the lists ship as they stand, she ships to the resettlement column in the second wave. It doesn't mean anyone thinks she's dangerous. It means nobody bothered to think about her at all." He met Quentin's eyes. "You've seen the plates. You know how a mark gets made. Somebody's tired hand, somebody's grudge, somebody's cousin who wanted the house. It's not malice, mostly. It's *sloppiness*. Malice I could almost respect. This—" he gestured at the whole dim shop, the town beyond it, the frozen river holding its cargo of fates, "—this is sloppiness with artillery."

Quentin looked down at the forme she was locking up — two hundred clean copies of a notice announcing that the officers' inspection would proceed as scheduled, that all was orderly, that all was well. Her father's trade in miniature: setting other people's truths in metal so they'd hold still long enough to believe.

"If I do this," she said, "you're not buying a favor. You're buying a partner. Partners see each other's work. Partners get caught together. There's no version of this, Sergeant, where you stay clean."

"I stopped expecting to stay clean around the time I started carrying the files." He said it simply, and it landed heavier than any oath would have. "I just want to stop being furniture."

She should have said no. Some sensible Quentin, some prudent daughter of a prudent trade, should have weighed the risk of a second pair of hands against the risk of one pair failing from exhaustion — and the truth was she *did* weigh it, standing there in the lamplight with the inspection notice locked in its chase, and the scale tipped anyway, and not because of strategy. Because she was tired. Because her father was dying by inches upstairs and she had been carrying the whole conspiracy in her chest like swallowed glass, and here was a young man with ink-stained fingers offering to carry a corner of it, and relief — stupid, reckless, glorious relief — went through her like the first swallow of brandy.

"All right," she said.

He exhaled like a man surfacing.

"But we do it properly." She crossed to the type case and ran her finger along the compartments, thinking aloud now, the plan assembling itself the way a page assembled — piece by piece, upside down, true only when you knew how to read it. "I don't touch Anka's entry in the master plate. Too visible; Brandt reviews the master himself, and a changed name in his own ledger is a shout, not a whisper. Instead—" she tapped a shallow drawer near the bottom, the one for duplicates, the army's own redundancy turned traitor, "—the duplicate plates get recut. The copies are what travel. The copies are what the clerks downstream actually sort by. Your sister's amber mark stays amber in the book Brandt reads and comes out clean in every list that matters."

Mirele stared at her. "That's—"

"Crooked as a river bend, yes. My father taught me. He says the state taught him the rest."

"And the risk?"

"The risk is that someone compares the master to a copy. Which happens twice a year on average, when a clerk upstream gets ambitious." She shrugged. "So we make sure no clerk upstream gets ambitious. Which brings me to you."

"Me?"

"You carry the files, Sergeant. From tonight, you carry them *carefully*. If a comparison order comes down — and one will, eventually, Brandt's too careful not to try it — I need to know days before it reaches this shop. Not hours. Days." She held out her hand, palm up, over the composing stone, in the old printer's gesture: *here is the surface, lay it down true.* "Can you do that?"

He looked at her hand. He looked at the stone, at the ghost of the corrected Kessler line, at the cellar hatch he still hadn't glanced at, bless him, not once.

"I've lied to my commanding officer exactly zero times in four years," he said. "I've wanted to maybe nine hundred times." He set his hand in hers, and his grip was a soldier's grip, dry and firm and slightly cold from the street. "Starting tomorrow, I'll try to keep the number of lies equal to the number of times it matters."

"One more thing." Quentin withdrew her hand and busied it with the forme, lifting it into the press bed, because if she kept looking at him she was going to grin like a fool and grinning was not operational security. "If you're coming here at night, you come with requisition slips. Every visit logged, every visit dull. You'll complain about the cold. You'll complain about the coffee — complain loudly, the sentries love a fellow complainer. And you will never, ever knock politely again. Polite knocks are memorable."

"Dully memorable," Mirele agreed gravely. "I'll practice being tedious on the walk home."

He gathered his cap, his logbook, his two hundred copies' worth of justification, and paused at the door with his hand on the latch.

"Fräulein Vasquez. Why did you say yes? You don't know me. By every sane measure you should have shown me a locked door and a boring evening."

Quentin considered several answers — the strategic ones, the flattering ones, the lie about having weighed it all coolly — and discarded them, because a man who had just promised to become a liar deserved at least one truth from his co-conspirator.

"Because you saw the stone," she said, "and your face went blank instead of hungry. Hungry men report people. Blank men are deciding something." She smiled, tired and real. "Also because I've been alone in this since the ice came, and alone is a luxury I can't afford anymore. Go home, Sergeant. Walk past Tanner's Row. Look at the third house past the tannery, and remember why you're doing this, because in about three weeks you'll be lying to Colonel Brandt's face and you'll need a reason bigger than fear."

He nodded once — the nod of a man accepting a commission — and let himself out, and his footsteps faded down the frozen street, unhurried, tediously ordinary, a clerk going home.

* * *

Quentin stood in the empty shop for a long moment, listening to the stove tick and the river groan, feeling the shape of what had just happened settle over her like fresh snowfall: soft, weightless, and hiding the fact that the ground had completely changed.

Then she went to the duplicate drawer and pulled it open, and there they were — the copy plates, rows of them, each one a little tin mirror of the master ledger, waiting dumbly for the lists that would decide everything. She found the section for the riverside wards, ran her thumb down the columns, and stopped.

*Tanner's Row.* Third house past the tannery. *Mirele, A.*

One letter. That was all it would take — one letter cut in soft lead, an *a* becoming something safer, a small surgery performed by lamplight. She'd done harder things in the last month. She'd done harder things *tonight*.

But as she lifted the plate to the lamp, tilting it so the light ran along the rows of names like water over stones, another line caught her eye — three entries below Anka Mirele's, in a ward she had altered weeks ago and not dared revisit since. The entry for the widow Ilves.

Someone else had marked it. Recently. In a hand that was not the census hand, not the army hand, not any hand Quentin knew — a thin, slanted, deliberate script, pressed deep enough into the plate to leave a burr she could feel with her fingernail.

And beside the widow's name, where the amber mark should have been, there was no mark at all.

There was a question mark.
