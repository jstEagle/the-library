## 12. The Proclamation Job

The order came down on a Tuesday, which felt like a small cruelty in itself. Tuesdays were ink-ordering days. Tuesdays had always been ink-ordering days, and there was something obscene about the army choosing one to demand ten thousand copies of a man's death.

Sergeant Mirele brought the requisition to the shop at half past eight, and he would not look at Quentin while he read it aloud. She noticed that. She had begun, in the weeks since the river froze and their conspiracy of two became a conspiracy of two-and-a-half, to keep a private catalogue of the things Sergeant Mirele could not bring himself to do: he could not meet her eyes when he carried orders; he could not say the word *decree* without his jaw tightening as though the word itself were a stone he'd been made to swallow; and he could not, under any circumstances, accept her father's offer of coffee, though he clearly wanted it, because wanting it would have meant sitting down, and sitting down would have meant being a man in a kitchen instead of a soldier in a requisitioned print shop.

"Ten thousand copies," he read. "Single sheet. The text is enclosed." He set the paper on the composing stone with the delicacy of a man laying down something that might go off.

Quentin picked it up and read it twice. The first time for the sense of it. The second time because she could not believe the sense of it.

They meant to shoot a man named Aldric Voss on Saturday morning in the market square, and they wanted the whole town to have fair warning, nicely printed, in a legible face, with proper margins. Ten thousand copies for a town of four thousand souls, which meant the surplus was not for Vellmark at all. It was for the towns downstream. It was a message that would ride the ice when it broke, folded into every satchel and saddlebag, telling the whole river valley what happened to men who did what Voss had done — whatever it was Voss had done. The decree did not say. Decrees, Quentin was learning, never said. They named the crime the way you'd name weather. *Acts against the security of the occupation.* Rain expected. Frost by morning. A firing squad before church bells.

"He's a fishmonger," her father said from his chair by the stove. He said it the way other men reported the time.

"He's a fishmonger," Quentin agreed.

"I sold him paper for his stall wrappings for eleven years." Her father's hands were folded in his lap now, most days, because folding them was easier than watching them tremble. "He haggled over the price every single time and paid a copper too much anyway, because I think haggling was the part he enjoyed."

"Well," Quentin said, and heard how flat her own voice came out, "then he'll be pleased to know he's getting a very good typeface."

The joke landed in the room like a dropped tray. Nobody laughed. But nobody flinched either, and in the Vasquez household that was its own kind of grace. Her father looked at her for a long moment — she could feel the look without turning around, the way you feel sun through a window — and then he said, "You'll want to lock up the forme from last night before you start," which was his way of saying everything, and they both pretended he hadn't said anything at all.

* * *

She set the decree herself. That was the arrangement, had been since the autumn: the soldiers supplied the orders, but no soldier's hands touched the type. Colonel Brandt had discovered early what she suspected he had known all along, that an army can seize a press but cannot operate one, any more than it can seize a violin and expect music. So Quentin composed, locked, inked, and pulled, and the soldiers stood around looking martial among the furniture of a trade they'd never learn, and everyone pretended this was normal, the way everyone pretends a funeral is normal right up until the moment the dirt goes in.

It took her most of Wednesday to set the decree. She worked slowly, which was unlike her, and she knew why she worked slowly, and she hated knowing. Every character went into the stick with a small argument in her head. *This is the trade,* said one voice, the sensible one, her father's voice. *A printer prints. What comes off the press is the world's business, not yours.* And another voice — younger, and it sounded maddeningly like Tomas Ilves at eleven years old, chalk dust on his fingers, teaching her the alphabet backwards because forwards was too easy — said nothing at all, just held up the letter *V*, for Voss, for Vasquez, for all the names that started somewhere and got cut off.

By Thursday morning the forme was locked and she had run the first proof, and it was good work. That was the bitterest part. She looked at the proof sheet — the heavy black headline, the date, the place, the hour, six o'clock, market square — and it was *good*. Clean margins. Even impression. Not a letter out of true. Somewhere in the capital there was probably an officer who would hold this sheet up to the light and think, *now there is a printer who respects her work*, and he would be correct, and that correctness would feel like a knife going in.

Her father came to stand beside her while she studied it.

"You're doing it wrong," he said.

Quentin's heart performed a maneuver it had not been authorized to perform. "What?"

"The impression. You've got the platen pressure even across the whole sheet." He tapped the proof with one knuckle. "For a broadsheet that hangs on a wall in all weathers, you want the center a hair heavier than the edges. The edges fade first out there in the rain and wind, and a fading notice gets taken down and replaced, and you'll be printing this thing until midsummer." He paused. "Also, your ink's a touch thin. Thin ink washes out. You want this to survive being read by every soul in Vellmark, don't you? You want it legible for months."

She stared at him. Then she understood, and understanding arrived with a feeling she couldn't immediately name — something adjacent to laughter, something adjacent to grief. Her father, who could barely hold a coffee cup, had just instructed her in how to make the execution decree as permanent and unignorable as possible, and he had done it in the voice he used for teaching her kerning, and underneath the instruction ran a current only she could read: *if the town must read this, let them read it so well they cannot pretend they didn't. Let it be undeniable. Let it indict.*

"That's very sound craft advice," she said.

"It's what your grandfather taught me." He shuffled back toward his chair. "He printed a tax notice once, in 'sixty-eight, for a magistrate everybody hated. He made it beautiful. Said if a man's going to be robbed, he should at least be robbed in a handsome font, so he remembers who to be angry at."

Quentin turned back to the press and adjusted the platen. Center a hair heavier. She mixed fresh ink, thicker, black as tar.

Complicity, she was discovering, tasted like this: like doing excellent work on a thing you despised, and having your father coach you through making it worse, or better, or whatever the word was when the excellence itself became a kind of witness. The decree would be perfect. Perfect enough that no one could say they hadn't seen it. Perfect enough that whoever read it would know exactly whose hands had made it — because there was only one pair of hands in Vellmark that could.

Let them know, she thought. Let them all know.

* * *

Friday was the long pull. Ten thousand sheets is not a metaphorical number; it is ten thousand times feeding the press, ten thousand times cranking, ten thousand small decisions about whether this sheet or that one was clean enough to count. Mirele was assigned to supervise, which meant he spent eleven hours standing beside her in a silence so carefully maintained it was practically architectural. Around noon he began stacking finished copies, and around three he began, very quietly, sorting them into bundles.

The bundles were the thing. Army procedure — and Brandt ran a tidy army, tidy as a typeset page — required the notices distributed in counted lots: five hundred for the town criers and wall-pasters, and the rest packed for downstream, sealed and addressed. One lot, though, went local and official: fifty copies bound in wastepaper stock, misprints and spoiled pulls, destined for the magistrate's office, where clerks filed such things the way magpies collect buttons — not because anyone wanted them, but because a record was a record.

The magistrate's clerk was a narrow, patient man named Oskar Pell who had kept his post through the occupation by being so relentlessly, transparently boring that the army had concluded he was harmless, which was the single greatest act of camouflage in the history of Vellmark. What the army had not concluded — what only Quentin and her father and now Mirele knew — was that Pell's filing room had become the town's attic. Everything the occupation produced passed across his desk, and everything Pell touched got a second copy, folded into ledgers nobody asked to see.

And Pell had received pages before. Three weeks ago, in a bundle of spoiled proclamation proofs, Quentin had slipped the first leaves of Tomas Ilves's manuscript — the pages naming officers, describing payoffs, listing dates. She had wrapped them in wastepaper and trusted them to a cart. She had not slept properly since.

Now there were more pages. She had copied them out by hand over two nights, working in the cellar by lamplight below the sleeping house, copying Tomas's cramped furious script into her own careful hand, because the originals could never leave the cellar and her copy could never be traced to the crate if it was found. Nine new pages. Names. Dates. A description of a night in the capital that Quentin had read so many times she could recite it with her eyes closed, and wished desperately she couldn't.

The question was the bundle. Fifty copies of waste paper, bound with twine, loaded onto a cart in front of a corporal who checked bundles by weight, not content. There was room inside a bundle for nine thin pages. There was also room for discovery, and discovery meant not her arrest — she had made her peace with arithmetic like that weeks ago — but the unraveling of everything: the altered plates still hidden in the forme of tomorrow's proclamation, the ledger, Mirele's sister's name on the safe list, all of it, undone because she'd gotten greedy with a wastepaper bundle.

At six o'clock, with forty-three acceptable sheets remaining to pull, Mirele spoke for the first time in hours.

"The spoil rate's high today," he said.

Quentin kept cranking. "Is it?"

"Mm." He examined the stack. "Corporal Dren counts bundles by weight. Standard practice." A pause the length of a heartbeat. "Standard practice means a bundle runs light if the spoils are thin stock. You might want to pad it."

She almost smiled, and stopped herself just in time, because the corporal by the door was watching her hands, and a woman smiling at nothing while printing a death warrant was the sort of detail a careful colonel's men remembered. "I'll use the older misfeeds," she said. "Thicker stock. From the tax proclamations in January."

"Sensible," said Mirele, and went back to counting, and that was the whole conversation, and it was the most intimate conversation Quentin had had in a month.

* * *

The bundling happened after dark, by lamp, because the press run had to finish before the curfew bell and the army wanted the cart loaded tonight, ready to roll at dawn. Two soldiers stood watch at the door. Her father sat by the stove, ostensibly asleep, his breathing pitched — she could tell by the rhythm — at exactly the register of a man listening hard.

Quentin built the bundle in layers, like a cake, like a lie. Wastepaper at the bottom, thick and honest. Then the nine pages of Tomas's manuscript, folded once, wrapped in a single spoiled proof of the execution decree itself — she had chosen that wrapping deliberately, with a cold clarity that surprised her. If the bundle were ever opened and searched, the searcher's eye would land on a death notice and recoil, and move on. Grief and horror made excellent padding. She hated herself a little for knowing that. She wrote the knowledge down nowhere, but she kept it, the way she kept everything.

Then more wastepaper on top, the twine, the knot, the weight. She hefted it. Lighter than the others, maybe, but the older misfeed stock was dense, water-marked, heavy in the hand. It would pass. It would pass. She said it to herself the way her father said *measure twice*.

Mirele carried the bundle to the cart himself. She watched him do it through the window — watched him set it down among the sealed lots, watched him straighten, salute the corporal, turn back toward the shop with his face arranged into the particular blankness of a man carrying exactly what he appears to be carrying and nothing else.

When he came back inside, stamping snow off his boots, he said, quietly, to the room in general, "Dawn delivery. Pell signs for it himself."

"Does he," said Quentin's father, from his chair, eyes closed, sounding entirely asleep.

"He's very thorough about signatures," Mirele said. "Always has been."

* * *

She did not sleep. She had not really expected to.

At two in the morning she sat in the dark kitchen with her hands wrapped around a cooling cup of chicory, doing sums she didn't want to do. Tomorrow at dawn, the cart. Tomorrow at six, Aldric Voss, fishmonger, haggler, overpayer of paper bills, would be stood against a wall in the market square, and the notice explaining why would be pasted on every wall in town in a typeface she had cut herself, in ink she had mixed thick so it would last, with the center of the impression a hair heavier so the edges wouldn't fade — and the town would read it, all of it, unmistakably, and remember.

And somewhere in the middle of all that machinery of death, folded inside spoiled paper, nine pages would travel four streets to a boring man's desk, and Tomas's voice — the boy who taught her the alphabet, the man the ledger called enemy — would take one more step out of the cellar and into the world.

It wasn't enough. She knew it wasn't enough. Nine pages in a wastepaper bundle against ten thousand copies of a death decree — the arithmetic was insulting. But her father had taught her something else about type, years ago, back when her hands were too small for the composing stick: that a single character, set true, in the right line, in the right place, can overturn a sentence. That whole columns of lies had fallen because one letter was where it shouldn't be. You didn't beat a press run with a bigger press run. You beat it with the truth, set carefully, delivered to the one reader who mattered.

The chicory had gone cold. She drank it anyway.

Through the window she could see the market square, white under the lamps, and the wooden barrier the soldiers had erected in front of the fishmonger's stall, already built, two days early, patient as type in a case, waiting for Saturday to lock it into place.

Quentin washed her cup, dried it, and set it back on the shelf in its exact spot, because order was the discipline that held everything else together. Then she went down to the cellar, lifted the lamp, and unlocked the forme of tomorrow's proclamation — the army's next order, the loyalty oath renewal, due to print Monday — and checked, one last time, that the true ledger's plates lay bolted flat beneath it, hidden under the state's own words like a knife under a pillow.

Monday, she thought. First the dawn cart. Then Saturday's terrible bell. Then Monday.

One letter at a time.
