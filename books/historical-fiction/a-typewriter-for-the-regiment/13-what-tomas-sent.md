## 13. What Tomas Sent

The manuscript pages came out of the wastepaper bundle the way a splinter comes out of a finger: slowly, with a small sound only you could hear.

Quentin had bound them into the bundle three nights ago, folded into eight, hidden between spoiled proclamations and misprinted orders, and she had spent every hour since then imagining Sergeant Mirele's clerk hands unfolding them, reading them, carrying them to Colonel Brandt on a silver tray. She had imagined the knock on the print shop door at dawn. She had imagined the rope, because her imagination in those days ran to rope the way other people's ran to weather.

Instead the pages came back to her on Thursday, tucked inside a returned proof of the loyalty list, wrapped in brown paper and marked, in Mirele's careful clerk's hand: *Wrong paper stock. Reprint.* And beneath it, so small she almost missed it: *Read them first this time.*

She waited until her father was asleep — or pretending to be asleep, which in his condition amounted to the same courtesy — and she took the pages to the stove and read them by its light while the snow ticked against the window like a compositor tapping sorts into place.

The handwriting was Tomas Ilves's. Of course it was. He had written her name on the slate behind her father's shop when they were seven years old, V-A-S-Q-U-E-Z, the Z last, like a flourish, and he had said, *You'll want your own letter someday, Quen. Everyone should own one letter at least.* The handwriting had grown up without him. It was narrower now. Hurried. But the Z still landed like a flourish.

*My dear Q,* the first page began. *If you are reading this, then you found my crate, which means you are still cutting type, which means some things hold. I have been writing this for two years and sending it nowhere, because there is no one to send it to who isn't already bought or already dead. But you were neither, last I heard, and I have decided to believe you are neither now.*

*You will want to know why I left Vellmark without saying goodbye. You deserve the truth more than anyone alive. Here it is, as plain as I can set it.*

Quentin turned the page.

*In my third year at the capital, I worked as a copying clerk in the Office of Records — the same office that keeps the master ledgers for every river town from here to the sea. I copied names, debts, births, deaths. It is dull work and I was good at it, and being good at dull work teaches you things that exciting work never does. It teaches you what the state bothers to write down.*

*In the autumn of my second year, I was sent to help reconcile the Vellmark census against an older ledger — the original one, from nineteen years ago, the year of the flood winter. You were four years old. You don't remember it. Nobody talks about it, which should have been my first warning.*

*I found eleven entries altered.*

Quentin stopped reading. The stove ticked. Outside, a patrol went by, boots crunching in a rhythm too regular to be human, four men marching to a count someone else kept for them.

Eleven entries. She thought of the forme downstairs, the plates her father had locked together at dawn two weeks ago, the true ledger bolted beneath the army's lies. Eleven entries in the old record. She had assumed the army had doctored them for its lists. She had assumed the rot began with Brandt.

She turned the page.

*Not smudged, not lost, not worn — altered. Cut out and reset. Someone had gone into the master ledger with type and replaced eleven households' worth of names. Whole families, gone from the record. Not erased, mind you. Erasure would leave a hole, and holes get noticed. They were *replaced*. New names set where old ones had been, in matching type, on matching paper. Somebody wanted the record to look untouched, which means somebody understood printing. Which means somebody understood that a town's memory is made of metal before it is ever made of stone.*

*I did what any fool does. I asked my supervisor about it. Politely. A single question, asked politely, in the capital, in the year of our Lord 1909. Within a week I had been reassigned twice and promoted once, which is how they move you when they intend to lose you. Within a month I was given a travel warrant to the northern garrison — "for archival verification," the papers said. I went, because refusing would have been its own answer.*

*I never reached the garrison.*

The fire shifted. Quentin fed it a stick and read on, hunched forward, the way she'd once hunched over her father's shoulder watching him justify a line of type, letter by letter, until the whole line held.

*The road north passes through Kessel Hollow. You won't know the name. That is rather the point. In Kessel Hollow, in the winter of '09, something happened that eleven families were cut out of a ledger to conceal. I arrived three days after it. I saw what remained. I will not write it down in detail, because I have learned that the state reads everything, and I refuse to hand them a second copy of their own work. But I saw it. I counted what I could not stop counting for months afterward. And I understood, standing in that hollow with my breath freezing, why the ledger mattered more than the deed itself.*

*A massacre needs a witness to become a crime. Take away the record and it becomes weather. It becomes a bad winter. It becomes nobody's fault, because officially nobody was there. The men who did it were never stationed anywhere. The village was never listed as existing past a certain date. The ledger doesn't just forget them — it makes them into people who never needed remembering.*

*That is what the census is, Q. I know you think of it as your father's great work, the town set in metal, every family true. It is that. It is also the state's alibi. Whoever holds the type holds the truth, and whoever holds the truth can spend it like money.*

*I fled that night. I have been running since — not from what I saw, but from the fact that I am the only living person who knows both what happened and what was done to the record afterward. A man in that position has two choices. He can disappear, in which case he proves their method works. Or he can come home, in which case he leads them straight to everyone he loves.*

*So I did neither. I wrote instead. And I am sending this to the one person I trust to understand what it means — because you were the girl who taught herself letters off gravestones, and because you promised me, when we were children and promises cost nothing, that you would always cut my name true.*

*I am asking you to keep a larger promise now. Not mine alone. Keep all of them true. Every name they cut. Every name they will cut. The ice breaks in spring, and whatever goes down that river in the army's crates will be the version of Vellmark the world receives. Make sure it isn't theirs.*

*Your friend, always,
Tomas Ilves*

*P.S. — There is a second packet beneath the manuscript in the crate. Cast type. My own cutting. If you're reading this, you've probably already found it and sworn at me for the state of my kerning. I taught myself in exile with borrowed tools; be gentle.*

Quentin sat back. Her eyes were wet and she resented them for it, briefly, practically — wet eyes blurred type, and type deserved better.

"Eleven entries," she said aloud, to the stove, because saying it aloud made it a fact in the world rather than a splinter in her head. "Nineteen years ago."

Her father's voice came down through the floorboards, thin but clear. "I heard that."

She climbed the stairs. He was propped up in bed with the blanket over his knees like a forme locked in its chase, and his eyes were open and steady.

"You knew," she said.

"I knew the flood winter took people." He said it slowly, the way he set a difficult word, testing each letter. "Whole houses on the low street, gone by morning. Water and cold. That's what we were told. Your mother helped bury what they let us bury." His hands moved on the blanket, the tremor in them quieter at night, as if the shaking too got tired. "I never checked the old ledger against anything. Why would I? Who checks the church clock against God?"

"The army checks," Quentin said. "The army checks everything, and writes down what it wants."

He was quiet a long moment. Then: "Tomas's pages say that?"

"You read over my shoulder through a floor?"

"I read *you*, girl. I've been setting you in type for twenty-three years. I know when a line's been changed." He patted the blanket beside him. She sat. "Tell me the rest."

She told him. The hollow. The counting. The alibi. Her father listened the way he read proofs — completely, without interrupting, hunting errors. When she finished he looked at the ceiling for a while.

"Nineteen years ago," he said finally, "a man came through Vellmark selling surveying services to the council. Wanted copies of everything. Census, debts, land titles. Your grandfather — no. Before your time. Anyway, the council gave him what he asked, proud to be noticed by the capital." His mouth twisted. "I always wondered why a man surveys a town he never surveys."

"They weren't taking records," Quentin said. "They were checking their edits."

"Mm." He closed his eyes. When he opened them again he was looking at her with an expression she couldn't set — part pride, part grief, part something older than either. "Then you know what you're holding. It's not forgery anymore. It's testimony."

"It's treason," she said. "By their law."

"By their law, so was teaching your mother to read." He said it mildly, and it landed like a hammer on a planer. "Go to bed, Quen. Tomorrow you set type. Tomorrow you set it *true*."

* * *

The next morning she went down to the cellar and opened Tomas's crate all the way for the first time.

Beneath the typewriter and the manuscript pages lay a second layer, wrapped in oilcloth: type. Not army type, not foundry stock. Hand-cut sorts, hundreds of them, each one slightly imperfect, each one unmistakably *his* — she recognized the tilt of his serifs the way you recognize a friend's walk from a street away. He had cast an entire alphabet in exile, letter by letter, the way she had once taught herself off gravestones. Two people, twenty years apart, learning letters from whatever the world left lying around.

And among the sorts, wrapped separately, a dozen matrices she didn't recognize at first — until she saw they matched the altered entries' style. Old official type. Where had he gotten—

No. He hadn't gotten it. He'd *made* it. Copied it. Which meant somewhere in two years of hiding, Tomas Ilves had learned to cut faces that could pass for the state's own, and had sent them ahead of him to the one place he trusted more than himself.

"He planned this," she murmured. "Before the colonel ever marched in. He planned—"

He hadn't known about the occupation. He couldn't have. But he had known the ledger would matter. He had known that someday someone would come to rewrite Vellmark, and he had armed the print shop the way other men arm a house — quietly, years in advance, and told no one.

She laid the matrices out on the composing stone in the lamplight and felt something turn over in her chest, large and slow, like the ice on the river shifting in its sleep.

It wasn't one name anymore. It had never been going to be one name. The promise she'd made on a summer afternoon, cutting T-O-M-A-S-I-L-V-E-S into lead while he watched and pretended not to watch, had grown teeth and roots and a purpose while she wasn't looking. She was going to set the town back into its own record — all of it, the eleven households, the hollow, the flood winter, every name the state had swapped out for a lie that fit the space.

And she was going to need help doing it, because eleven households' worth of alteration, in matching type, undetectable under a magnifying glass, before the ice broke — that was not one girl's night work. That was a press run.

She was still staring at the matrices, planning, when she heard the front door above open, and boots — army boots, unhurried — cross the shop floor, and her father's voice, pitched carefully casual:

"Colonel. To what do we owe—" a pause "—the honor isn't quite the word."

"No," said Colonel Brandt's voice, pleasant as fresh ink. "It rarely is. Is your daughter in? I find I have questions about typefaces. And I do so enjoy talking to experts."

Quentin stood very still in the cellar, one hand flat on Tomas's matrices, listening to the boots cross the floor directly overhead.
