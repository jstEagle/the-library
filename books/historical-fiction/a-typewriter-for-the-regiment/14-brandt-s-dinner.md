## 14. Brandt's Dinner

The invitation came on cream-colored paper, delivered by a corporal with frost in his mustache and an expression of a man carrying something he'd rather not touch.

*Colonel A. Brandt requests the pleasure of Miss Quentin Vasquez's company at dinner, Thursday evening, seven o'clock, at the requisitioned house on Alder Street.*

Requisitioned. Even the house had been stolen politely.

My father read it twice, holding it close to the lamp because his eyes were going the way of his hands. Then he set it down and laughed — a dry, clicking laugh, like type falling into a galley.

"Well," he said. "Nobody's asked me to dinner since your mother died. And now they ask *you*. I'd be insulted if I weren't so relieved."

"Papa—"

"Quentin." He took my wrist, careful as a man handling a wet proof. "You'll go. You'll eat whatever he serves you, you'll praise the soup even if it's glue, and you will not say one word more than the situation requires. Do you understand? A printer's best tool is silence. We just happen to keep ours in drawers."

I understood. What I didn't understand — what I spent all of Thursday afternoon failing to understand while I sorted someone else's mail into alphabetical columns for Sergeant Mirele's filing cabinet — was *why*. Colonels did not invite printers to dinner. Colonels summoned them, shouted at them, requisitioned their fathers' presses. They did not send cream-colored paper with the word *pleasure* spelled out in full.

Unless, of course, the colonel wanted something. Or wanted to look at something. Or wanted to watch something squirm.

Mirele stamped a folder shut beside me and murmured, without looking up, "He had the cook sent over from the garrison this morning. There was a discussion about fish."

"A discussion about fish."

"The colonel believes river trout demonstrates respect. The sergeant-major believes it demonstrates extravagance. It went on some time."

"And which side are you on?"

"I am," Mirele said, stamping another folder with great precision, "on nobody's side, Miss Vasquez. That is my entire strategy for surviving this war, and so far it is working beautifully."

There was no war. Not officially. That was the joke of the whole winter — soldiers in a town at peace, lists of loyalists in a town that had never been disloyal to anyone but its own creditors. But I let him have his strategy. I had one too, and mine involved a locked forme under tomorrow's proclamation and a name I recut every night until my fingers knew Tomas Ilves's letters better than my own.

* * *

The house on Alder Street had belonged to the Halloran family, who manufactured rope, and the army had taken it in November with a receipt that would have made a highwayman blush. Now it smelled of lamp oil and roast meat, and a portrait of old Halloran hung in the hall watching everything with the offended dignity of a man whose own walls were being used against him.

Colonel Brandt received me in the parlor. He was smaller than I remembered from the day he nailed the warrant to our door — or perhaps distance had inflated him, the way print inflates a name. Up close he was a neat, gray man with neat, gray hands and eyes that did all his talking for him.

"Miss Vasquez." He shook my hand. His grip was precise, calibrated — the handshake of a man who had measured ten thousand handshakes and found most of them wanting. "Thank you for coming. Sit, please. May I offer you wine? It's from the Halloran cellar, so technically we're drinking the property of a man who owes the regiment money, but I find that simplifies the conscience."

I sat. I accepted the wine. I praised nothing yet, saving my praise like a compositor saves spaces — knowing exactly where each one goes.

"You're wondering why I've invited you," he said, settling across from me with his glass.

"It had crossed my mind, Colonel."

"Good. I distrust guests who aren't wondering anything. Curiosity is the only honest appetite." He smiled. It was a good smile, well-machined, and it reached his eyes without disturbing them, the way water reaches a stone without moving it. "I'll tell you plainly: I asked you here because your work is remarkable."

"My work, Colonel?"

"Your type-cutting. Don't play modest; it doesn't suit you, and we haven't time for it anyway." He leaned forward. "When I first saw the proclamations coming out of your shop, I assumed a machine had cut the type. Some new imported device. Then I looked closer." He turned his glass slowly by the stem. "A machine cuts evenly. Yours doesn't. Your serifs have opinions. Your letter *e* tilts a half-degree to the left when you're tired and stands up straight when you're not — don't look alarmed, I've had six months of your proclamations to study. You sign every piece of work you do, Miss Vasquez. Most artists would kill for a signature as honest as yours."

Somewhere behind my ribs, a small cold bell began to ring.

"I hadn't thought of it that way," I said.

"No. Printers never do. They think of type as language. I think of it as evidence." He said it lightly, the way a man says something terrible while passing the bread. "Every document tells you who made it, if you know how to listen. Handwriting, ink, pressure, habit. The state spends a fortune teaching men like me to read documents the way priests read scripture — looking past the words to the soul beneath them."

The corporal announced dinner. Brandt rose and offered his arm as though this were Vienna and I were somebody. In the dining room, under old Halloran's glowering portrait, there was indeed trout, and candles, and a table set for two with a precision that suggested the sergeant-major had lost the argument about extravagance.

We ate. He talked — about the capital, about typography, about a bookbinder in Vellmark he'd once known ("a criminal, incidentally, but excellent with leather"), about the ice on the river, which he mentioned three times with the casualness of a man checking a pocket watch. Each mention landed in me like a coin dropped down a well: *the ice-break*, *when the river opens*, *the first barge of spring*. He wasn't threatening me. He was simply reminding us both of the clock.

Then, over the cheese, with the candlelight doing its flattering work, he set down his knife and fork, aligned them parallel with the table edge, and said:

"Now. I'm going to tell you something, Miss Vasquez, because I believe you deserve honesty from me, and because I'm curious what you'll do with it."

I waited. The trout sat in my stomach like a stone with opinions.

"There is a forgery in this town," Colonel Brandt said.

Outside, the wind worried at the shutters. Old Halloran glowered. I lifted my wine glass and found my hand steady, which surprised me, because inside I was already running — through the shop, down the cellar stairs, to the crate, to the plates, counting what might already be gone.

"A forgery," I said. My voice came out interested. Mildly interested. The voice of a woman hearing gossip about strangers.

"In the loyalty lists. One name — perhaps more — altered after compilation. Someone has been setting metal into the record." He picked up his glass, considered it. "Do you know how difficult that is? To alter a compiled ledger? The plates are locked. The press is guarded. And yet someone has done it, cleanly, professionally, with type that matches the original almost perfectly." He looked up at me. "Almost."

"Have you found who?" I asked, and marveled at myself. *Have you found who.* As though I might help him look.

"No." He smiled again, and this time the smile was different — slower, warmer, and infinitely worse. "That's rather the point of tonight. I can't find it. I've searched the obvious places. The shop by daylight, the presses, the cases, the cellars of four houses on this street. Nothing. Which leaves me two possibilities, and I find both fascinating." He held up one finger. "Either the forgery was done somewhere I haven't thought to look—" a second finger "—or it was done somewhere I *have* looked, and done so well that I looked straight through it."

"That sounds frustrating, Colonel."

"Frustrating?" He laughed — genuinely, warmly, a man delighted with his own predicament. "Miss Vasquez, it is the most interesting thing that has happened to me all winter. Do you know what most people are? Transparent. I ask a question, they answer it; I look at a face, I read it like a broadside. But whoever forged those plates—" he shook his head with real admiration "—that person is a craftsman. And craftsmen are the only people left in this country worth talking to."

He refilled my glass himself, waving off the corporal.

"So here is what I want from you," he said. "Nothing, tonight. Eat your dinner. Drink my illegal wine. Go home and sleep well." He settled back, the picture of a reasonable man concluding a pleasant evening. "But I wanted you to know that the hunt exists. Because I've noticed something about craftsmen, Miss Vasquez. They cannot bear bad work. When I finally find the forgery, whoever made it will want to inspect my inspection — will want to see how close I came. And I suspect she won't be able to resist being in the room when I do."

The word *she* fell onto the table between us and lay there, gleaming.

I looked at it. I looked at him. And I heard myself say, lightly, "You think the forger is a woman?"

"I think," said Colonel Brandt, "that in this town, the only hands capable of that work belong to either a dead master printer or his daughter. And the dead don't set type." He raised his glass to me. "To craftsmanship."

* * *

He walked me to the door himself. At the threshold he paused, breath fogging in the cold, and said, almost gently:

"One more thing, purely professional. Your father taught you the trick of the matched re-cast? Where new type is ground down and inked to match the wear of the old, so no line betrays the insertion?" He watched my face with the patience of a man fishing through ice. "It's very rare knowledge. I've only ever met two printers who knew it. One of them died last month." A pause, delicate as a knife laid on silk. "The other cut my proclamation headings personally, not three days ago. Beautiful work. The *e* stood perfectly straight."

"Good night, Colonel," I said.

"Good night, Miss Vasquez. Mind the ice on the steps. Spring is coming, and everything frozen is going to move."

I walked home through streets gone blue with evening, past proclamations I had printed myself curling off the walls, past sentries who nodded to me because I was useful, useful, indispensable — and I understood at last what the dinner had been. Not a trap. Traps snap shut. This had been something colder and more patient: a man showing me the length of his reach, the quality of his attention, the fact that he could wait. He hadn't accused me because accusation ends a hunt, and Brandt did not want it ended. He wanted to draw it out. He was courting the chase the way I courted a difficult page — slowly, respectfully, certain of the ending.

At the shop my father was awake, sitting up in his chair by the banked stove, and he read my face before I got my coat off.

"He knows," my father said. Not a question.

"He knows there's a forgery. He doesn't know where. He thinks—" I stopped. My voice did something embarrassing. "Papa, he told me my *e* stands straight when I'm not tired. He's been reading my type like a diary."

My father was quiet a long moment. The stove ticked. Somewhere upstairs the wind found a loose shingle and worried it.

"Then we make your *e* lie," he said at last.

And I realized, standing there with Brandt's courtesy still clinging to my coat like snow, that my father had known exactly what I'd been doing since the first night — and that the trick he'd promised to teach me, the one that survives a magnifying glass, had better be learned by Friday.

Because the ice was groaning on the river, and in the morning, when I opened the shop, there was a note nailed to the door in the colonel's neat gray handwriting:

*Miss Vasquez — the regiment requires five hundred copies of the new list by week's end. I trust the type will stand up straight. — A.B.*

He was going to put the final loyalty plates in my own hands.

He wanted to see what my *e* would do.
