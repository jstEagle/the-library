## 15. Mirele's Choice

The summons came at seven in the morning, delivered by a corporal who would not look him in the eye.

"Sergeant Mirele. The Colonel requests your presence at nine."

*Requests.* In the army, as in everything else, the gentlest words carried the heaviest loads. Mirele had been in the regiment long enough to know that when Brandt *requested*, men came back changed — quieter, or not themselves anymore, or in one memorable case not present anymore at all.

He ate breakfast anyway. He'd learned that much from his mother: no man ever made a better decision hungry than fed. The porridge tasted like wet paper, but he finished it, and he buttoned his tunic straight, and he polished the buckle of his belt until it shone, because if this was going to be the worst morning of his career it might as well be conducted by a man whose boots reflected light.

At half past eight he stopped by the records room on the pretext of checking an inventory. His sister's file sat where it always sat, third drawer, second folder from the left: *Mirele, Anja — sympathies under review.* One page. One sentence, really, written by some clerk in the capital who had never met her, reporting that she'd said something careless about the requisition at a market stall in Doren. Something careless. Anja, who cried at the death of other people's cats. Anja, who once walked four kilometers in the rain to return a neighbor's lost hen.

One page stood between her and the seizure list.

He closed the drawer and went upstairs to lie to a colonel.

* * *

Brandt's office in the commandeered magistrate's building had been arranged with a care that unsettled people. Most officers who took over a town's courthouse threw their maps over the judge's bench and called it headquarters. Not Brandt. He kept the bench clear, dusted it even, and sat instead at a small writing table near the window like a man who wanted everyone to remember the law was still theoretically in the room.

"Sit down, Sergeant." He poured coffee himself, two cups, and slid one across. It was good coffee. That was the thing nobody warned you about Brandt — the courtesy never lapsed, which meant you could never use discourtesy as evidence of danger. "You've been with the regiment six years?"

"Seven in the spring, sir."

"Seven. And in seven years, how many times have you been disciplined?"

"None, sir."

"How many times late to duty? Drunk? Insolent?"

"None, sir."

"Mm." Brandt drank his coffee and looked out the window at the frozen river, white and silent as a sheet of unmarked paper. "I'll tell you what I admire about you, Sergeant. You are precise. You keep the files the way a jeweler keeps stones. Nothing misplaced, nothing smudged. When I ask for a record, I receive a record." He turned back. "So help me understand something."

Here it comes, thought Mirele, and discovered that his heart was a fist knocking politely on a door that would not open.

"Three proofs of the loyalty list were pulled last month," Brandt said. "Pulled, printed, and vanished. Not destroyed — vanished. Someone took them out of this building. My inventory says they left through the print shop. The print shop is staffed by two people: a dying printer and his daughter. And one soldier assigned to oversee the work." He let that settle like sediment in water. "You."

"Yes, sir."

"You signed the log showing the proofs accounted for. Then the count came up short. Then the count came up short again." Brandt's voice stayed level, almost kind. "Now. A man of your precision does not lose three sheets of paper twice. Either you're incompetent — and I have read your file, Sergeant, and you are not incompetent — or someone is taking them past you, and you are letting them go. Which is it?"

There was a version of the truth he could tell. He'd built it during breakfast, brick by brick: *the girl miscounted, sir; she's grieving her father; I didn't want to bring charges against a printer we need.* It was even mostly true. It was the kind of truth soldiers told every day in every occupied town from here to the capital, and it would cost him a reprimand and her a warning, and it would keep Anja's name off a list, and it would change nothing, save nothing, mean nothing.

Or there was the door behind his ribs.

"The proofs passed me, sir," Mirele heard himself say.

The silence afterward had texture. It had weight. Somewhere downstairs a press thumped like a slow heartbeat.

"Go on."

"I don't know who took them. I suspect I do, but I won't say without proof, because you taught us at Doren that suspicion isn't evidence and an officer who acts on suspicion makes widows." He was surprised at how steady his own voice sounded, as though it belonged to a braver man wearing his uniform. "What I know is this: I counted them wrong, or I looked away, and either way the fault is mine. If you want a court-martial, I'll stand for it. But I'll stand for my fault, not hers — theirs — not anyone else's."

"*Hers,*" Brandt repeated softly. He set down his cup. "Interesting slip, Sergeant."

"It's a slip, sir."

"Is it." Brandt rose and walked to the window, hands clasped behind him, watching the ice. For a long moment Mirele honestly believed he was going to arrest him. He found, to his own astonishment, that he felt less afraid than *clean*. Whatever happened next, he had not handed them the Vasquez girl to be convenient. He had not traded a stranger for his sister.

"You know what I think, Sergeant?" Brandt said without turning around. "I think somewhere between Doren and Vellmark, a very orderly young man began to notice that order can serve anything. A butcher's scale serves the butcher just as well as the customer." He turned. "I'm not going to court-martial you. I'm going to do something worse. I'm going to trust you. Your oversight of the print shop continues. And I will know, within the week, whether my trust was well placed. Because if another proof goes missing — if so much as a single sheet walks out of that building — I will stop looking for papers and start looking for people. Do you understand the difference?"

"I do, sir."

"And Sergeant—" Brandt smiled then, and it was the smile that frightened everyone, because it reached all the way to his eyes, and his eyes were patient as winter. "Whatever you're protecting, whatever name you swallowed in this room this morning — I hope it's worth it. Protection is a debt like any other. It comes due."

Mirele saluted, turned, and walked out on legs that only began shaking on the stairs.

* * *

He did not go back to the barracks. He walked to the river and stood on the bank among the fish-houses until his hands stopped trembling, and then he laughed — actually laughed, alone in the cold like a madman — because he had lied to Colonel Brandt's face and lived, and because the lie had been *true*, which was the strangest kind of lie there was.

Then he thought about Anja, and stopped laughing.

Because here was the arithmetic Brandt hadn't done yet, but would. A clerk who lies once is compromised forever. Every day Mirele stayed silent deepened the debt; every proof that walked out of the print shop now walked out *through him*, over his signature, into his grave. Sooner or later Brandt would collect. And when he collected, he would want a name, and Mirele would have exactly one piece of property worth trading.

His sister's file.

He could burn it tonight. Nobody audited the sympathy-review drawer; he was the man who kept the files the way a jeweler keeps stones, and no thief steals from his own vault. One match, third drawer, second folder from the left, and Anja became a ghost in the records — no paper, no case, no list. The state could not seize a woman it could not prove existed.

And the moment he struck that match, he would be exactly what Brandt suspected: a man protecting something. A conspirator. The word landed in his chest and sat there, heavy and warm at once.

His father had been a customs officer in Doren for thirty years. Mirele remembered being eleven years old and finding a merchant's bribe — real money, more money than his father made in a season — sitting on the kitchen table, returned, wrapped in its original brown paper with a note: *Not for sale.* His father had caught him staring and said, not unkindly, "It's cheaper to keep your hands clean than to buy them back, boy. Remember the price." Mirele had nodded solemnly and gone back to his tin soldiers, and for twenty years he had kept his hands clean at essentially no cost whatsoever.

It turned out the price only arrived later. It arrived all at once, on a frozen riverbank, in the shape of a matchbox.

* * *

The records room at midnight was lit by one lamp and heated by nothing, which suited him; cold kept a man honest, or at least quick. He unlocked the third drawer. The folder was thin. He'd known it would be thin — one careless sentence, one market stall, one sister who loved hens and hated cruelty — but seeing it, actually seeing how little paper it took to endanger a life, made something in him go quiet and hard.

*Sympathies under review.*

Under review by whom? By clerks. By men like him. By drawers and folders and the slow grinding bureaucracy that Brandt served so beautifully, the machine that needed no villains, only accurate files.

"Not for sale," he said aloud, to the empty room, in his father's voice.

He fed the corner of the page to the lamp flame and held it until the fire climbed his fingers' distance, then dropped it into the iron stove and watched it curl. It burned fast, the way guilty things do, or maybe the way innocent things do — he couldn't decide, and decided it didn't matter. Ash settled among the ash of a hundred ordinary documents, indistinguishable. The first truly invisible act of his career, committed against the institution he'd served perfectly for seven years.

Anja was safe. Or safer. Safe-ish, which in Vellmark that winter counted as a fortune.

He was relocking the drawer when the door opened behind him and he nearly died of fright on the spot.

It was Corporal Denn, the night clerk, yawning, carrying a ledger. "Oh — sorry, Sergeant. Didn't know anyone was in here. Cold as a witch's tit in this room." He shuffled to the far shelf, oblivious, pulled his volume, and paused. "Say, you hear about the ice? Captain says it'll break inside a fortnight. Whole barge load going downstream at once, lock, stock, and ledgers." He grinned. "Big day for the paperwork trade."

"Aye," Mirele managed. "Big day."

Denn left. Mirele stood in the dark a while longer, listening to his own heartbeat come down.

A fortnight. Everything moved at the ice-break — the lists, the seizures, the barge, whatever the Vasquez girl was doing at night that he had chosen, irrevocably, not to see. He had burned his bridge and his sister's file in the same hour, and now there was nothing to do but walk forward on the ash.

He blew out the lamp and went to find her. There were things a jeweler of files ought to tell the forger before the river woke up — starting with the fact that Brandt knew, and was waiting, and that the fortnight they thought they had was a fortnight Brandt intended to spend hunting.

The snow squeaked under his boots all the way across the yard, loud as a confession.
