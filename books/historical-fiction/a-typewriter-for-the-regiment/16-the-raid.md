## 16. The Raid

They came at the hour when the lamps burn lowest and the will to argue burns with them — an hour past midnight, boots on frost, twelve men and a lantern that swung like a small captive sun.

Quentin heard them before she saw them. She had learned the grammar of boots by now: sentries shuffled, patrols marched, but a raid arrived with a particular rhythm, quick and uneven, the sound of men who had been told they would find something and were eager to be proved right.

"Father," she whispered across the dark shop. "They're here."

Her father did not open his eyes. He had been sleeping in the chair beside the press for three weeks now, too weak for the stairs, too stubborn for a bed, wrapped in the great coat he refused to admit was warmer than any blanket because admitting so would mean the cold had beaten him at something. Now he opened his eyes all the same, and in the grey light from the window Quentin watched him do the arithmetic she was doing — the forme locked in the press, the plates beneath the army's own type, the proofs still drying under the loose floorboard by the sink — and arrive at the same terrible sum.

"How long to print off a page?" he asked. His voice was gravel poured slow.

"A minute. Less."

"Then we are rich," he said, "in minutes."

The knocking came anyway, though the door had never once been locked since the requisition order went up. That was the army's way: they knocked on doors they already owned, the way a man tips a hat to a horse he has bought.

* * *

Sergeant Mirele stood at the threshold looking like a man who had swallowed a whistle and could not decide whether to blow it. Behind him crowded six soldiers with crowbars and two with nothing but their hands, which somehow looked worse.

"Printer Vasquez." Mirele's voice had been sanded down to regulation smoothness. "By order of Colonel Brandt, these premises are to be searched for stolen regimental property."

"Stolen," Quentin repeated. "You mean the proclamations you ordered? The ones you paid for out of the regimental fund, receipt signed?"

A flicker crossed Mirele's face — the ghost of a smile, quickly buried alive. "The colonel believes certain *plates* may have been altered. Counterfeit work. He wants every case opened, every drawer, every—"

"Every floorboard lifted?" Quentin said sweetly. "Please. Mind the sink drain. It bites."

It was not brave. She knew it was not brave even as she said it, because the fear in her chest had gone past trembling into something cold and clear, the way water goes just before it decides to be ice. But her father had taught her that a printer's first weapon was tone — that ink obeys confidence, and so, mostly, do men.

Mirele stepped aside and the soldiers came in like weather.

* * *

What followed had a horrible choreography to it, the same in every occupied town, she supposed, the same as it had been in every century since men first decided that other men's belongings were inventory. Cases upended, quoins scattered, brass letters skittering across the boards like startled beetles. A soldier pried open the paper cabinet and stood ankle-deep in ten thousand sheets of blank stock, looking personally betrayed by the quantity.

"Careful with the galleys," Quentin said, following them. "Drop one and you'll spend your whole enlistment picking up em quads off this floor. They roll. They're very committed to it."

Nobody laughed. One young soldier — he could not have seen twenty winters — glanced at her with something like apology in his face before plunging his arm elbow-deep into the fourteen-point case and stirring it like soup.

And through all of it, the press ran.

Her father had hauled himself upright in the chair, then into the standing frame of the machine itself, both hands flat on the bed, and when the first soldier reached for the lever, Quentin's father said, without raising his voice:

"You'll want to let me finish that sheet."

The soldier blinked. "Sir?"

"That forme is the colonel's next proclamation. Locked, composed, ready to run. You stop me mid-pull and the type springs, and then you can explain to Colonel Brandt why his order for four hundred copies arrives late, misprinted, or not at all." He drew the lever, and the platen came down with its great soft iron sigh, and the shop filled with the smell of ink and the small thunder of the press saying what it always said: *still here, still here.*

He printed. He printed while they tore the shop apart around him. He printed with his breath coming short and his knuckles white on the lever, pulling each sheet, laying each against the drying rack, pulling again — four hundred copies of an announcement regulating the price of firewood, of all the dignified documents in the world — and the rhythm of it became a kind of music the raiders could not argue with. It is very difficult to ransack a room in which an old man is industriously doing exactly what your colonel commanded. Twice a soldier stopped near him, uncertain; twice the old man simply held up one finger — *wait* — the universal gesture of every craftsman on earth, meaning *you may destroy my country but not my register*, and the soldier waited.

Quentin understood then what her father was doing, and her throat closed around it. He was not printing proclamations. He was daring them. Every pull of the lever said: *this shop is not empty. This shop is working. Touch it and you are touching the colonel's own orders.* He had made himself into a shield made of paperwork, which was, he would have told her, the only kind of shield an honest town ever gets.

* * *

They found things. Of course they found things; a print shop is a museum of suspicious objects. A soldier emerged from the cellar holding the box of Tomas Ilves's cast type aloft like a relic, and Quentin's heart performed a maneuver she felt in her teeth.

"What's this?"

"Private stock," she said. "Cast by a journeyman years ago. He left it in storage." Which was true, every word, and true words are the best mortar for a wall of lies. "Unless the regiment has begun arresting alphabet molds."

The soldier turned the box over. Ordinary type. Nothing incriminating about ordinary type — that was the beauty of lead; it confessed nothing. He set it down, disappointed, and moved on to the ink barrels, where he discovered that ink barrels contain ink, and reported this finding with great seriousness.

Mirele, meanwhile, conducted his search like a man reading aloud in a language he pretended not to know. His lantern passed over the drying rack — over the four hundred firewood proclamations, each one innocent, each one printed by a dying man as a fortification — and paused, one heartbeat too long, on the loose floorboard by the sink.

Quentin began composing her confession. She got as far as *your honor, it seemed prudent at the time* before Mirele stepped directly onto the board, stamped twice — testing it, testing *her*, she realized, watching her face in the lantern light — and found it solid as the earth.

"The cellar again," he barked to the soldiers, buying her the moment, costing himself something visible. "Take the barrels out. Check behind the stairs."

*Check behind the stairs.* As if anyone in the history of hiding had ever chosen behind the stairs. She could have kissed him, and stored the impulse away as one more debt the war kept adding to her account.

* * *

By the third hour, the shop looked like a dictionary that had been argued with. Type everywhere. Furniture scattered. The soldiers' initial zeal had curdled into the sullenness of men who had been promised contraband and delivered only dust and splinters and the smell of lampblack.

"It's not here," the young one said finally, wiping his hands on his trousers and immediately regretting it.

"It's not here," Mirele agreed quietly.

But the tall sergeant — Kessel, the one who enjoyed his work, whose face Quentin had memorized the way one memorizes a stain — was not finished. He drifted toward the press, toward her father, and stopped there with the particular patience of a man who saves his cruelty for last, the way some people save dessert.

"All night at the machine, old man. In your condition." He smiled. "Dedication. Or perhaps there's something in that forme you're fond of. Something you'd rather we didn't unlock."

The shop went quiet enough to hear the river muttering under its ice.

Quentin's father laid down the sheet he was holding. He did it slowly, the way he did everything now, and looked up at Kessel with eyes that had spent fifty years judging the straightness of lines and found most of them wanting.

"Son," he said, "do you know what a lock-up is?"

Kessel's smile thinned. "No."

"It's when you wedge type tight between furniture and quoins until it cannot move, cannot shift, cannot lie. It takes a strong hand and a true eye, and if either fails, the forme spills and the job is ruined." He rested one hand on the iron frame, gently, the way you'd touch a shoulder. "This forme was locked by my hand. If you open it, you'll ruin the colonel's proclamation, and I'll tell him — truthfully — that a soldier who couldn't read a job ticket broke it. He'll believe me. Old printers are believed. It's the one perk of the trade."

He said it without heat, without theater, a plain statement of fact delivered in the voice of a man describing weather, and Quentin saw Kessel weigh it — the trouble of explaining a ruined order against the pleasure of breaking an old man's work — and saw the scales tip the way they always tip for bullies, toward whatever requires no courage.

Kessel spat on the floor, called it clean, and gave the order to withdraw.

* * *

They left the way weather leaves: noisily, leaving wreckage, taking only the ink barrels, which would be missed by no one, the good ink having moved to the cellar two nights prior under a tarp of onion sacks that had offended every nose in the building.

For a long time after the boots faded, nobody spoke. The stove ticked. Somewhere upstairs a shutter knocked against its frame, keeping its own count.

Then her father sat down — not into the chair but *downward*, in stages, like a building deciding — and Quentin caught him under the arms and lowered him the rest of the way, kneeling on the floor among the scattered brass letters, holding a man who weighed less than a full case of twelve-point.

"Papa."

"I'm well." He was smiling. There was ink on his chin, black as a comma. "Did you see their faces? When I wouldn't stop pulling?" He laughed, and the laugh cost him, and he paid it gladly. "Fifty years, Quentin. Fifty years they walked past this shop and never once looked in. Tonight they tore it apart because they were afraid of it." He pressed one ink-black finger against her wrist, a signature, a seal. "That's the whole trade, girl. That's everything I know. Make them afraid of the record. Everything else is just spelling."

She wanted to argue with him — about resting, about the doctor, about the word *condition* that Mirele had used and that had landed in her chest like a dropped quoins-weight — but his eyes had already gone to the press, to the locked forme sitting in its bed with its secret cargo wedged tight between the army's own proclamation and the frisket, hidden in the one place in Vellmark no soldier would ever dare open: inside the colonel's own work.

"Leave it locked," he murmured. His voice was going soft at the edges, fraying like old type. "Leave it locked until morning. And Quentin — tomorrow, before the run — come sit with me. There's a trick I haven't shown you. About making a lie hold its line." His eyelids sank. "About making it survive a glass."

"Papa, sleep now."

"Counting the quoins," he said, in the voice of a man descending a staircase into somewhere warmer. "One. Two. All present..."

She sat on the floor among the wreckage with her father breathing shallow against her shoulder, the forged plates bolted safe beneath four hundred copies of the firewood decree, and outside the window the sky was turning the color of wet newsprint, which meant dawn, which meant tomorrow was already arriving, asking for its forme.
