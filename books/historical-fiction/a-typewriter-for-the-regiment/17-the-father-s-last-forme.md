## 17. The Father's Last Forme

My father had always said a printer could tell the hour by her hands. At two in the morning mine told me it was too late to be awake and too early to give up, and that my fingers smelled of lead and lamp oil and the particular sourness of fear sweat, which no soap in Vellmark would ever fully lift.

He was working.

That was the thing I could not get past, the thing I kept circling like a moth at a window: he was *working*. Three days past the point where the doctor — when we could still get the doctor, before the occupation made even illness a matter of permits — had shaken his head and spoken softly to me in the doorway while my father pretended not to listen and sharpened a knife he would never again need. Three days past, and Aldo Vasquez was at the stone composing stick in hand, setting type by feel because his eyes had begun to fail him in the way that rivers fail in January, freezing from the edges inward.

"You should be lying down," I said, for perhaps the hundredth time.

"And you should be asleep," he answered, "and neither of us has ever been good at taking sensible advice, which is the only thing I regret passing down to you."

He was building a forme. Not tomorrow's proclamation — tonight's work was something else, something he kept angled away from me whenever I came near the table, which was a feat of stubbornness in a man who needed both hands on his cane to cross the room. He'd shoo me off like a hen with opinions.

"Hand me the quoins," he said now.

I handed them. His fingers closed over the steel wedges, and I watched him tighten them into the chase, quarter turns, patient as tide. His hands — God, his hands. They had been beautiful once, in the way that tools are beautiful, knuckles like hinges, fingertips flattened and silvered by forty years of lead. Now they trembled unless he gave them a job, and so he gave them jobs without end. He had been dying the way he lived: employed.

"Papa," I said, "what are you locking up?"

"The truth," he said, "with any luck."

* * *

I want to set down what happened next properly, because in the days after I kept remembering it wrong — remembering it as calm, as orderly, as if we were two printers finishing an ordinary job against an ordinary deadline. It was not orderly. Grief had been sitting in the shop all week like an unpaid customer, and it chose that hour to finally name its business.

It began well enough. He asked for furniture, the wooden strips that pad out a forme, and I cut them to his measurements. He asked about spacing and I answered. He quizzed me the way he used to quiz me when I was nine, sitting on a crate with my feet swinging: *What leads a line better than brass? What do you call the blank that gives the words their room?* And I answered, and he grunted approval, and for a few minutes it was 1903 again and the worst thing in the world was a dropped capital B.

Then he stopped mid-turn of a quoin, and his breath went somewhere and took a long time coming back, and I was around the table before I knew I'd moved.

"Sit," I said. "Papa. Sit down."

"I am sitting," he said, which was true; he had sunk onto the stool without appearing to decide to. His face had gone the color of uninked paper. "Quentin. Listen to me. The forme is finished. Do not open it."

"What?"

"Do not unlock it. Do not proof it. Do not correct it." He gripped my wrist, and for a man three days past the doctor's verdict his grip was a scandal. "Tomorrow the army wants their proclamation. Two thousand copies. The decree about curfew and confiscation, the one Mirele brought over this evening. You will print it exactly as ordered — every lie of it, every cold word — and underneath, bolted into the same forme where no one thinks to look, is the ledger. The true ledger. The real census, the original entries, the names they mean to rewrite."

I stared at him. The lamp hissed. Somewhere outside a sentry coughed, the sound carrying across the frozen street like a shot in miniature.

"You've hidden the ledger plates *inside* the proclamation forme?"

"Locked in. Quoined tight beneath the text block, face down against the packing." A ghost of his old grin moved through his face like a fish under ice. "They will march in here tomorrow morning, stand over your shoulder, read the first pull upside down if they're clever enough to read at all, and see nothing but their own orders. And then you will run two thousand copies of their decree with the whole town's record printed on the reverse side of every sheet — or rather, you won't. You'll pull a few, bundle them, and let the rest come off the press when the time is right. But the forme stays locked. The forme is your alibi and your arsenal in one. Let them search the shop. Let them turn over every drawer. The truth will be standing in plain sight, dressed in the colonel's own uniform."

"Papa—"

"I have spent my life," he said, and paused to breathe, and I waited, because you learn to wait, "setting other people's truths. Other people's lies wearing truth's clothing. Every debt in that census ledger, every bribe recorded as a fee, every name misspelled just enough to make a man nobody — I set it, Quentin. I set what they paid me to set and told myself the press is only a press, the hands are only hands. Well." He looked down at those hands, spread open on his knees like two tired birds. "These are not only hands. And neither are yours."

The tears came then, without permission, hot and stupid, and I wiped them with the heel of my hand the way he'd taught me to clean ink from a roller — briskly, thoroughly, no sentiment allowed near the machinery.

"Don't you dare die before explaining the packing trick," I managed.

"There is no packing trick. There is only doing the work correctly, which frightens them more than any trick ever could." He closed his eyes. Opened them. "One more thing. When I'm gone — not if, Quentin, when, don't make that face — you will grieve afterward. On schedule. Like a professional. Tonight you will sit with me and you will tell me about the plan for the river, because I want to hear it, and because keeping you talking keeps me here, and I am a vain man who intends to stay until the last page is pulled."

* * *

So I told him. About the crate in the cellar — Tomas's type, Tomas's manuscript, the machine that had crossed half a country to reach a print shop it never reached, arriving instead into the middle of a war of paper. About Mirele, and the burned file, and the terrible fragile arithmetic of trusting a soldier. About the sledge under tarpaulin behind the coal shed. About the ice, groaning some nights like old floorboards, holding, holding.

He listened with his eyes shut and his hands folded, correcting me twice — once on the weight distribution of a loaded sledge ("lead goes low, girl, always low, same as in a forme") and once on my estimate of the army's barge capacity, which he considered optimistic in a manner he found personally offensive.

"And Brandt?" he asked. "The courteous one?"

"He'll be watching the river on the last night. He knows something leaves this town that isn't on his manifest. He doesn't know what."

"He knows a forgery exists," my father said. "He told you so at dinner, with the fish course. That man hunts the way winter hunts — slowly, everywhere, politely." He was quiet a moment. "You'll beat him anyway. You know why?"

"Because I'm better at the trade?"

"Because he thinks the record is a weapon." My father opened his eyes and looked at me, and in the lamplight his gaze was clear as it had been in years, all the fog burnt off, a man seeing straight down to the bottom of himself. "You know better. The record is a *witness*. Weapons you can seize. A witness who has already spoken — that, you cannot undo. Ten thousand townspeople reading their own names off a wall in the melting snow. He can shoot the printer. He cannot shoot the reading."

* * *

Dawn came the way it does in late winter, grey and grudging, seeping under the door rather than entering it. My father had made me lie down on the cot in the back room around four, and I had obeyed because he asked it as a printer asks a thing — as an instruction in a job ticket — and because I think we both understood it was the last instruction he would ever issue.

I woke to silence of the wrong kind.

You must understand: the print shop was never silent, even asleep. It breathed. Lead settles. Wood contracts in the cold with small ticking sounds, like a slow clock. My father snored in a rhythm I had known all my life, a rhythm I had complained of, mocked, imitated at dinner parties to general delight.

The shop was simply quiet. Quiet as a forme after locking, everything held, everything still.

He was at the composing stone, upright, one hand resting flat upon the locked chase as though taking its pulse, his head bowed forward as if in concentration — as if he had merely paused between lines and meant to continue in a moment. I knelt beside him and stayed there a long while, long enough for the light to change, long enough for the sentry outside to finish his round twice. I did not pray. I did the only offices our family kept. I straightened his collar. I brushed the lead dust from his sleeve. And I said the words we say over finished work, the ones he'd said a thousand times over a completed form, tapping the locked chase with two fingers:

*"Well and truly set."*

Then I stood up, and I did not fall apart, because there would be time for falling apart later — on schedule, like a professional — and because the army would arrive within the hour wanting their proclamation, and my father had left me the most terrible, perfect inheritance imaginable: two thousand sheets of official lies with the town's true memory bolted beneath them, waiting for the day I chose to let it speak.

* * *

Sergeant Mirele arrived at seven with the escort detail and the requisition copy, stamping snow off his boots, and stopped dead when he saw me standing composed at the press in a black armband I had cut from mourning ribbon that hadn't been touched since my mother died.

"Printer Vasquez," he said quietly. "I'm sorry."

"He finished the forme last night," I said. "Locked and ready. Your proclamation will come off clean."

Mirele glanced at the chase on the bed of the press, at the soldiers idling by the door picking wax from their ears, and something passed across his face — quick, controlled, gone — like a man recognizing a co-conspirator's handwriting in an official document.

"We'll need to inspect—"

"You'll need to *read* it," I corrected him, loud enough for the escorts, "to confirm the text matches your order. First proof is pulling now."

I cranked the press. The bed slid home. The impression came up crisp and black and obedient, and I peeled the sheet and handed it to the sergeant, proclamation-side out, curfew and confiscation and the colonel's signature block all present and correct.

Mirele read it. Nodded. And as he turned it toward the light — just for a heartbeat, just for me — his thumb rested, deliberately, on the edge of the sheet nearest the packing, where beneath the army's tidy lies the true ledger lay locked face-down, patient as spring under ice.

"Text conforms to order," he said formally. "Proceed with the run."

And I thought: *Papa, you old fox, you've done it.* The truth was in the machine. All that remained was to choose the moment to let it speak —

—and that was when the door banged open and a mud-spattered trooper from the river watch shoved past the escorts, saluting wildly, shouting the news down the length of the shop:

"The ice! Sergeant — the ice is breaking! The colonel wants every crate on the barge by nightfall!"
