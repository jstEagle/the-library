## 18. Before the Ice Breaks

The last night of winter came in on a wind that smelled like iron and thawing mud, and Quentin Vasquez spent it lying to her own hands.

"You're steady," she told them. "You are the steadiest pair of hands in Vellmark. You cut a lowercase *g* last month so fine that Sergeant Mirele mistook it for type from the capital foundry, and you will not shake now, not tonight, not when it matters."

Her hands ignored her entirely. They shook like a drunkard's hymnal.

She stood at the cellar door of the print shop with her coat buttoned wrong — two buttons off, which she had been too distracted to notice and which Mirele would notice within four seconds of seeing her, because that was the kind of man he'd turned out to be: the sort who caught a mis-set line at ten paces but couldn't lie to his colonel without sweating through a uniform collar. She could hear him upstairs now, keeping the sentry at the front door occupied with a conversation about requisition forms. He was doing his dutiful-clerk voice, the one that made boredom contagious. She'd once watched him use it for twenty minutes on a corporal who had come to complain about ink rations and left having volunteered for latrine duty just to escape.

Through the floorboards, faint as a heartbeat: "—and of course the column for widows must be cross-referenced against the grain allotment, otherwise you get the situation we had in Kesselmark, which I am *sure* you've read about—"

The sentry made a sound like a man being slowly buried in paperwork.

Quentin smiled. Then she stopped smiling, because smiling was a luxury and tonight was not a night with room in it.

She opened the cellar door and went down into the dark she knew better than her own bedroom.

* * *

The crate waited where they'd built it over eleven nights, plank by plank, each one carried down separately so no soldier would see a whole box moving through the shop. It was the size of a coffin, which was a comparison Quentin refused to make aloud even in the privacy of her own skull, and it weighed what a town's memory weighs, which is more than you'd think and less than you'd fear.

Inside, packed in wool shavings and sawdust: the true ledger, every page of it, reset in her father's plates with Tomas Ilves's name spelled true and forty-one other names un-doctored, restored to their honest spelling after months of her working by candle stub while an army slept above her head. Beneath the ledger, wrapped in oilcloth: the manuscript, Tomas's pages, water-stained and furious, the account of what happened at Kesselmark that the census entries had been doctored to bury. And around both, cushioning them like eggs in a market basket, the type itself — her father's life's work in lead, thousands upon thousands of characters, each one cut or cast or mended by the hands that were now too weak to hold a composing stick.

She'd said goodbye to the type three nights ago, quietly, running her thumb along the shoulder of a capital *Q* — her letter, the one she'd always claimed, the one with the little tail her father added so she'd know it anywhere in a galley of strangers. He'd caught her doing it. He hadn't said anything. He'd just looked at her across the shop with that expression he'd worn since October, the one that said *I know everything, I have known everything since before you did, now go be careful* — and then he'd coughed for a long time, and she'd held his cup of barley tea, and neither of them had said a word about any of it, which was how the Vasquez family said the important things.

"Right," she whispered to the crate. "You're going to behave yourself. You're going to ride quiet. And if anyone opens you before the magistrate does, you're a crate of scrap lead bound for the army's melting pots, do you understand? Nothing in here but punctuation."

The crate said nothing, being a crate. It was the only thing in Vellmark she could count on to keep silent, so she appreciated it all the more.

She got her shoulder under its edge and lifted, and her back informed her that it had opinions about this plan, and she lifted anyway, walking it up the stairs one tread at a time, pausing on each step to listen. Above her, Mirele was still talking. "—which brings us to annex B, sub-column three, and I want you to pay particular attention to the hyphenation protocols—"

The sentry groaned.

Quentin brought the crate up into the dark of the shop, set it beside the back door, and stood there breathing, listening to the house settle and the river mutter to itself outside.

* * *

The river had been talking for three days.

That was the thing nobody warned you about an ice-break — it wasn't silence and then catastrophe. It was a long argument. All day the ice along the banks had been cracking and sighing, booming like a door slammed in a far-off room, and the townspeople had gone about their business pretending not to listen, the way you pretend not to hear your parents arguing downstairs. Everyone in Vellmark knew what the sound meant. The army knew too. Colonel Brandt's men had been down at the docks since morning, loading the barge by lantern light, crates and crates of them, and Brandt himself had walked the quay at dusk with his hands clasped behind his back, looking at the ice the way a man looks at a clock.

Tomorrow — tomorrow, if the warm wind held — the ice would go out, and with it the barge, and with the barge every list, every file, every carefully sorted name, shipped downstream to the capital before the town could read its own record.

Unless tonight went exactly right.

Mirele appeared at the back door at half past midnight, exactly on schedule, and closed it behind him with the exaggerated care of a man who has recently learned that doors make noise. In the dark he was mostly a uniform and a nervous smell of lamp oil.

"Corporal Dett is thoroughly educated on hyphenation," he whispered. "He may never volunteer for sentry duty again. I may have destroyed a career."

"One can only hope." She turned. "Button."

"What?"

"My coat. I've done it up wrong. Two off."

There was a pause, and then his hands, careful as a compositor's, undid the two buttons and redid them properly, and if his fingers weren't entirely steady either, she was polite enough not to mention it, because hers hadn't been steady since supper.

"Sledge is at the tannery fence," he said. "Old Hallik lent it. He didn't ask why. He said—" Mirele's voice did something complicated. "He said his wife's name is in the ledger. The true one. He said he'd have pulled it himself if his knees let him."

"Hallik," said Quentin, "has been pulling things for this town since before either of us was born. Sledge runners waxed?"

"Waxed. Greased. He also gave us his dog, briefly, to walk ahead and bark at anything that moves."

"I don't want anything barking."

"He insisted. The dog's name is Turnip. Turnip has opinions about soldiers." Mirele paused. "Turnip is currently tied to my bedframe chewing a boot, because we agreed that a dog with opinions is a liability until we're past the quay."

"Good. One less mouth." But her heart had squeezed at it anyway — old Hallik with his ruined knees sending his sledge and his dog, the widow Ilves pressing a heel of black bread into her pocket at the door that evening without a word, without needing a word, the mute fierce solidarity of a woman whose son's name had been cut false and would by morning be cut true. The whole town wasn't in on it. But pieces of it were, quietly, the way a forme holds together: each piece locked tight, bearing weight, trusting the others not to move.

"Ready?" Mirele asked.

"No," said Quentin honestly. "But the ice isn't waiting for me to feel ready, so."

They got the crate out the back door and into the alley, and here was the terrible mathematics of the night: the print shop sat six streets from the tannery, and those six streets crossed open ground within sight of the quay, and the moon — traitor, informer, collaborator with colonels everywhere — was three-quarters full and showing off.

They moved the way her father had taught her to move through a crowded shop: fast between stations, still at each station. Ten steps, freeze. Ten steps, freeze. The crate rode between them on a hand-barrow, and every joint of the barrow creaked like it was composing a confession, and twice Quentin was certain she heard boots on the quay — a quarter mile off, maybe less — and twice they stood pressed into a doorway with the crate between them like a third conspirator holding its breath, and the boots passed, and the ice boomed somewhere downriver, long and rolling, like the world clearing its throat.

On Fetter Lane, halfway there, Mirele spoke without turning his head. "When this is done. Tomorrow. If it goes badly—"

"It won't."

"If it goes badly, I want you to know I'd burn the files again. All of them. I lied to Colonel Brandt's face and it turns out it doesn't hurt at all. That's the part that frightens me. Not the lying. How easy it was."

"Mirele." She waited until he looked at her. In the moonlight he looked about nineteen and exhausted and braver than any man she'd ever met, including her father, which was saying something. "You didn't become a bad soldier tonight. You became a good clerk. There's a difference. A good clerk keeps the record straight. That's all any of this is. We're just clerks."

"With a sledge."

"With a sledge. Now shut up and pull."

He almost laughed. She felt it more than heard it, a small earthquake of relief passing between them, and then they moved again.

* * *

At the tannery fence, Turnip was absent (chewing his boot, guarding nothing, bless him), the sledge waited with its runners gleaming, and Old Hallik himself was there, which had not been the plan, sitting on an upturned bucket in the dark like a gargoyle with a pipe.

"You're late," Hallik said.

"We're early," said Quentin. "It's not dawn yet."

"Dawn's a state of mind." He knocked out his pipe against the bucket, stood, and took the near end of the crate before either of them could object, grunting it onto the sledge with a strength that had clearly outlived his knees' cooperation. "Widow Ilves says you're to take the south lane past the church. Her cousin saw two sentries moved to the mill race this afternoon. They're watching the water, not the streets."

"They're watching for a boat," said Mirele. "Brandt thinks whoever's altering the lists will try to run them downriver themselves."

"Brandt," said Quentin, "thinks a great many tidy things." She lashed the crate to the sledge frame with rope, checking each knot twice, then a third time, because knots were the one place where thoroughness was free. "He thinks the forgery is a plate he can seize. He keeps searching for a piece of metal. He can't search for a pair of hands."

Hallik snorted smoke. "Hands leave. Hands get caught."

"Then we won't get caught."

They hauled the sledge out onto the packed snow of the lane, and it ran smooth as a proof coming off a well-inked press, and for a few hundred yards the night was almost kind. The cold had teeth but the wind had dropped. Somewhere a church bell counted one-thirty with the weary honesty of a bell that has no stake in anybody's conspiracy. Quentin pulled with her head down and her breath tearing white ribbons behind her, and she thought — absurdly, in the middle of everything — about breakfast. About how tomorrow morning, whatever happened, there would be a morning, and someone would put a kettle on. Her father used to say that the mark of a real printer was thinking about the next job while the current one was still wet. Well. This was the next job, and the one after it was breakfast, and she intended to survive to eat it.

They reached the corner by the churchyard, and that was where the night stopped being kind.

Because the church sat on the low rise above the quay, and from the churchyard wall you could see the whole frozen sweep of the river — and the barge, riding high and empty at its moorings, ringed with lanterns, and the figures moving between them, and among the figures, unmistakable even at distance by the way he stood, hands clasped, utterly still, a man watching ice the way other men watch fires:

Colonel Brandt.

"He hasn't slept," Mirele breathed. "Look at him. He's been down there for hours. He knows something's happening tonight. He doesn't know what."

"He knows a forgery exists," Quentin murmured. "He told me so over soup, with his little courteous smile. He just can't find it. So now he stands at the one door the record has to pass through." Her chest went tight. "We can't take the riverside lane. We'll be silhouetted against the ice."

"The mill road, then. Longer. Through the tannery yard—"

"—smells like a crime against God—"

"—but it bends away from the water until the last hundred yards."

They stood a moment in the churchyard shadow, three breathers and a sledge full of treason, while below them the lanterns of the army made a broken necklace on the ice, and the river boomed again — louder now, closer, a crack that ran the length of the channel like a zipper opening, followed by a groan that Quentin felt in her back teeth.

And then, faintly, carried up the hill on the cold air, they heard singing.

A soldier on the quay, some bored sentry, was singing a marching song, tuneless and half-hearted, and another voice told him to shut up, and the first voice sang louder, and Quentin closed her eyes for half a second and thought: *they're just boys.* Most of them were just boys, tired and cold, guarding a ledger they couldn't read, for a colonel who'd taught them the names on it mattered and never told them why.

"All right," she said. "Mill road. Quiet feet."

They swung wide, down through the tannery yard — the stench rose up and embraced them like a relative — past the vats and the stretching frames, the sledge runners hissing on the snow, until the lane bent north and the quay lights slid out of sight and there was nothing ahead of them but the dark mouth of the street that led down to the magistrate's court.

The ruined court. The delivery point. The place where, come daylight, she meant to put this crate into the returning magistrate's hands and dare the world to argue.

Twenty yards from the corner, Turnip's replacement arrived: a cat. A huge grey torn, one of the print shop's semi-employed mousers, materialized out of a doorway directly in their path and sat down, tail curled, regarding them with the flat judgment of his entire species.

"Not now," Quentin whispered.

The cat blinked slowly.

Behind them, downriver, the ice gave a sound none of them had heard yet that winter — not a boom, not a groan, but a long tearing shriek, like the sky ripping along a seam, and every dog in Vellmark began to bark at once, and Hallik's bucket clattered somewhere behind, and Mirele grabbed the sledge rope with both hands and looked at her with wide eyes.

"That's early," he whispered. "That's hours early. Quentin, that's the *main sheet* going—"

The cat, offended by the noise, flowed off into the dark.

Quentin put her shoulder to the sledge, felt the crate shift and settle against her hip like a sleeping child, and thought of her father's last lesson, the trick that survived a magnifying glass: *the secret isn't in the letters, girl. It's in the spacing. Leave the truth enough room to breathe and no one sees it's there.*

The river was screaming. The army was watching the water. And the truth was breathing quietly on a sledge behind her, wrapped in wool and sawdust, patient as lead.

"Pull," she said.

They pulled.

The sledge runners hissed. The lane tilted down toward the court and the dark. And behind them, at their backs the whole way, the ice broke its long silence at last — and began, with a sound like ten thousand presses starting at once, to come apart.
