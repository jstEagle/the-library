## 21. The Magistrate's Bench

The courthouse had been a courthouse the way a drowned man is, for a while, still shaped like one.

Quentin stood in the doorway with the sledge rope still burning a line across her shoulder and looked at what the winter had made of it. The roof over the east wing had gone in during February, when the army had used the hall to stable horses and then stopped bothering to feed them properly. Snow had come through the broken slates and melted and frozen and melted again until the floor wore a skin of ice that cracked under her boots like bad type. The magistrate's bench — old Alderman Kessel's bench, her grandfather's contemporary, oak black as printer's ink — stood at the far end with its front panel pried off and burned, presumably, because someone cold had wanted it more than the law did. What remained was a scarred box of wood on a dais, and behind it a wall where the town seal had been chiseled out and left as a pale ghost in the shape of nothing.

They had taken everything they could carry and ruined most of what they couldn't. But they had not taken the bench itself. Quentin found she was absurdly moved by this. You could burn a panel, you could chisel a seal, you could stable your horses in the room where Vellmark had settled its debts for ninety years — but the bench was too heavy to lift with two hands, and so it stayed, and staying, it waited.

She went back out and took hold of the crate with Mirele, and together they walked it up the aisle between the fallen plaster and the horse-apples and the frozen puddles, setting it down at the foot of the dais with a thump that echoed in the hollow of the building like a full stop.

"That's it," Mirele said. He said it the way he said most things now, carefully, as if each word were type he'd set himself and didn't quite trust. "Ledger. Type. The manuscript pages. All of it."

"All of it," Quentin agreed, though her eyes went to the crate's lid as if the words inside might have wandered in the night. They hadn't. She had checked eleven times since dawn, which Mirele had counted and mercifully not mentioned.

The magistrate was expected within the hour. Word had come down from the ferry crossing at midmorning: Magistrate Oren Halvorsen, reinstated by whatever distant authority did such things now, traveling with two clerks and an escort of soldiers who were technically no longer Brandt's but had not yet been told what else to be. The withdrawal order had been signed four days ago. The barge had gone downstream empty of lists, carrying instead Brandt's resignation of the town and, rumor said, a stomach complaint that half the regiment shared and no one mourned.

Vellmark was sweeping its streets. That was the thing Quentin kept noticing, walking through town these last days — brooms. Every doorway had someone at it, pushing the gray drift-slush and the trampled mud and the army's leavings toward the gutters, as if the town could be made clean by Friday, as if eight months of occupation could be swept like sawdust. Old Frau Beckel had been out at first light sweeping the steps of a house the army had never even entered. When Quentin asked her why, Frau Beckel had said, "Because it wants doing," and gone on sweeping, and Quentin had understood that this was the whole answer and a better one than most.

The crate sat at the foot of the ruined bench and Quentin sat down on it, because her legs had opinions of their own, and Mirele leaned against a pillar and took off his cap and turned it in his hands.

"You'll stay?" she asked him. She'd asked twice already. She intended to keep asking until the answer bored them both.

"Until the ledger's read into record." He turned the cap. "Then I don't know. There's a hearing. About the occupation. They'll want clerks who can write down what happened without—" he paused, hunting for the word, "—without improving it."

"You could apprentice," Quentin said. "To me."

Mirele looked at her. Under the grime and the army pallor he was younger than his uniform had ever let him seem. "You're taking apprentices now."

"I'm taking one apprentice. Possibly. If he can learn to distribute type without dropping it, which I doubt, because I've watched him handle paper."

"I carried your sledge three miles."

"And dropped two proofs in the snow, which I had to dry over a candle like a mother hen." She let him see the smile, though. Let him see it plainly, because there had been too many months of hiding things from people's faces, and she was out of the habit of showing hers, and meant to get back into it. "Come on. Say yes. My father always said the trade needs more left-handed men; something about the quoins."

"I'm not left-handed."

"He also said a good lie about the trade needs more left-handed men would bring in the ones who wanted to believe it. Consider yourself recruited."

Mirele laughed — actually laughed, a short startled sound, as if the noise had escaped custody — and put his cap back on, and that was how the doors opened.

* * *

Magistrate Oren Halvorsen came into his own courthouse like a man returning to a house after a long illness, touching things.

He was smaller than Quentin remembered, and grayer, and he wore his reinstated office the way some men wear a coat borrowed for a funeral — correctly, and with a constant faint sense of not owning it. His two clerks hung back at the door with their cases, taking in the ruin, the ice, the pillar with the bayonet gouges. Halvorsen himself walked the length of the aisle slowly, one hand trailing along a pew-back, and when he reached the dais he stood before his mutilated bench for a long moment and put his palm flat on the scarred wood.

"Well," he said, to the bench, or the town, or possibly to the chisel-marks. "Well."

Then he turned and saw Quentin, and the crate, and his face did something complicated.

"Miss Vasquez." His voice was careful. "I am given to understand you requested this meeting. In writing. On a printed form." He held up the paper — her summons, run off the shop's press two nights ago, set by her own hands in a face she'd cut herself, because if she was going to summon a magistrate she was going to do it beautifully. "Which is, I must say, the finest piece of official typography I have seen from this district in twenty years. Including my own correspondence."

"Thank you, sir."

"It also threatened me with prosecution for obstruction should I fail to appear." His eyebrow rose a fraction. "On what authority, Miss Vasquez?"

"On the authority of the record, sir." She stepped aside from the crate. "Which is in here. And which has been waiting longer than I have."

He looked at the crate a long time. Clerks shifted at the door. Somewhere outside, a crow remarked on the morning to another crow, at length.

"The census ledger of Vellmark," Quentin said, and heard her own voice go formal without her deciding it, falling into the cadence of proclamations, the language of the forme. "Reset in metal. Carried through an occupied winter. Delivered to the court of this district, together with certain documents pertaining to the conduct of Colonel E. Brandt and officers of the garrison, and certain plates bearing alterations made under requisition authority, which alterations are hereby tendered as evidence of forgery by the occupying command." She breathed. "And one more item, sir. Which isn't evidence. It's just true, and it's been waiting nine years."

Halvorsen looked at her for a moment over the top of his spectacles. Then he climbed the two steps to his ruined bench — there was no other way up; the stairs were splintered — and sat, and the sitting had weight to it, the way a man sits when he means to be a magistrate again whether or not the furniture cooperates.

"Open it," he said.

* * *

She opened it the way she'd opened it a hundred times in her head: lid off, straw back, the oilcloth folded corner by corner. The ledger came out first, bound in its winter-worn boards, every page a plate of lead and antimony and tin, every name standing in relief like something carved to survive a flood. Halvorsen took it in both hands — Quentin noted, with a craftsman's involuntary approval, that he lifted it correctly, from beneath — and began to turn the pages.

The clerks crept forward. The ice on the windows let through a light the color of skimmed milk, and it fell across the open ledger and the names shone.

"Vasquez, Aldous," Halvorsen read quietly. "Printer. Assessment: loyal. Note: hands failing, autumn of the year." He glanced up. "You set your father's entry yourself."

"Somebody had to, and he was dying at the time."

Something moved through the magistrate's face — not quite a smile; a recognition, perhaps, of what kind of witness he had been sent to receive. He read on. Beckel. Sorrel. Ilves.

He stopped at Ilves.

Quentin had known he would stop there. She had set that name four times over the winter — once true, once forged, once true again, and once, on the final night, in the forme locked beneath the army's own proclamation, where it had ridden through the town pasted to every wall while the ice broke. Her fingers ached now just seeing the page turn to it.

"Tomas Ilves," Halvorsen read. "Declared enemy of the state by order of Colonel Brandt, requisition authority. Household marked for seizure." He looked up. "This is the forgery?"

"This is the truth, sir. The forgery was what the army shipped downstream — or thought it did." She let that sit. "What the colonel doesn't yet know is that the barge went out carrying my corrections, and the corrections are the only version that survived the ice-break. Every proclamation posted in Vellmark on the morning of the thaw had the true page showing through underneath. Half the town has read it aloud by now. The other half memorized it."

One of the clerks made a small sound, like a man swallowing a whistle.

"And the manuscript," Halvorsen said.

Quentin lifted out the bundle — Tomas's pages, water-stained at one corner where the snow had gotten at them in February, wrapped now in clean oilcloth. "The account of Captain Ferren's action at the mill crossing. Written by an eyewitness. Named, signed, and dated." Her throat tightened around the next part, and she made herself say it anyway, plainly, because the whole point of type was that it didn't flinch. "He wrote it before he ran. He hid it with his mother's letters. It's been in my cellar since before the army came."

Halvorsen untied the oilcloth with the deliberation of a man unwrapping something that might be either dynamite or scripture, read the first page, and went very still. He read the second. At the bottom of the second page there was a signature, and Quentin watched the magistrate's thumb come to rest beside it, gently, the way you'd touch a bruise to learn how deep it went.

"Signed," Halvorsen said softly. "Named. Dated." He closed the bundle. "Do you know what this is, Miss Vasquez? Do you know what you've carried through a winter?"

"Yes, sir. Heavy."

That surprised a sound out of him — half laugh, half something older — and he pressed his lips together and became a magistrate again by visible effort.

"Then the court acknowledges receipt." He beckoned the elder clerk forward. "Enter it all. Ledger, plates, manuscript. Chain of custody from—" he paused, pen hovering, "—from whom, precisely? Who delivers this?"

"I do," Quentin said. "Quentin Vasquez, printer. On behalf of the town of Vellmark, whose memory this is." She heard the words leave her and felt, absurdly, that her father had written them, that he had been writing them all winter in the formes she thought were only proclamations. "And there's a witness statement besides, sir. Mine. I'll give it under oath whenever you're ready. I altered those plates myself, and I'd do it again before lunch."

The clerk's pen scratched. The crow outside had found an ally. And that was when the doors opened again.

* * *

Quentin heard the footsteps before she turned — unhurried, slightly uneven, the step of a man who had walked a long way on roads of varying quality — and she knew them. Not from the capital years. From before. From a boy crossing the print shop floor in stocking feet so as not to scuff the boards, reciting the alphabet backward because forward was too easy, while she cut her first letter and her father pretended not to watch.

Tomas Ilves stood in the doorway of the ruined courthouse with snow in the seams of his coat and a travel bag over one shoulder and nine years of distance on his face, and behind him, filling the door as if afraid to come further, Sergeant Anselm Mirele, who had apparently sprinted from the ferry landing and lost the race to exactly the right person.

Quentin did not move.

Later she would try to describe it — to Mirele, to no one — and find there weren't words set for it. It was not joy arriving all at once. It was more like pulling a proof: first the impression coming up pale, then darker, the image surfacing out of blankness, and her standing there watching her own history take ink. A boy at the case, teaching her letters. A promise made over a tray of loose type: *I'll always cut your name true.* A name in a stolen ledger. A cellar. A winter. And now this — the proof pulled, the image complete, Tomas Ilves standing alive in the doorway because he had followed his own manuscript home.

"You got my pages," he said. His voice was rougher than she remembered, and lower, and it shook on *pages* in a way he tried to hide and failed.

"Every one. Water damage on folio seven. Your handwriting deteriorates under emotional pressure, incidentally — folio twelve is barely legible."

"I was describing a massacre."

"Well, it's no excuse." And then her voice went entirely, and she crossed the aisle — over the plaster, through the puddle, past the clerk with his raised pen — and Tomas met her halfway, and they held on to each other the way you hold on to a doorframe in a ship's swell. Not romantic. Nothing in it that the town gossips could later embroider, though she knew they would anyway, because Vellmark would embroider a laundry list. Just two people who had paid a debt across nine years and a war, gripping arms, checking that the other was solid, that the type hadn't scattered.

"You're thin," she said into his shoulder.

"You're ink-stained."

"I'm a printer."

"You're a *forger*," he said, and there was awe in it, and grief, and something that might have been guilt beginning to surface, and she stepped back and stopped it with a look.

"Don't," she said. "Whatever speech you rehearsed on the road — the one where you apologize for the winter we had — fold it and distribute it. I didn't do it for gratitude. I did it because I promised, and because the ledger was wrong, and because somebody in this town had hands that could fix it." She looked him up and down. "Also because your mother would have haunted me. Has anyone told her yet?"

"She doesn't—" He stopped. Started again. "She hasn't spoken since autumn. The neighbors said."

"I know. I've been sitting with her Tuesdays." Quentin felt her eyes sting and blinked it down with a professional's discipline. "Come tell her yourself. Today. Before you bathe, before you eat, before you do anything official. Some debts you pay in order."

Behind them, Halvorsen had risen from his bench, and the courtroom had gone quiet in the particular way rooms go quiet when a document has been recognized. The magistrate was looking from Tomas to the manuscript in his own hands and back again, and Quentin watched him arrive, visibly, at the arithmetic of it: the eyewitness, delivered; the record, restored; the town, reading.

"Mister Ilves," Halvorsen said. "You are Tomas Ilves. Of Vellmark. Son of Marta Ilves."

"I am, sir."

"Nine years absent. Presumed—" the magistrate chose the word with tongs, "—unaccounted for."

"Yes, sir."

"You are aware that the census of this town declares you an enemy of the state."

"I wrote a book saying otherwise," Tomas said. "It appears to have arrived ahead of me."

Halvorsen held up the manuscript. "It did. This court acknowledges the receipt of testimony bearing your signature, and takes notice that the declaration against you was entered under requisition authority subsequently withdrawn." He set the pages down on the scarred bench, squarely, deliberately, in the middle, where a seal used to be. "As of this hour, Mister Ilves, the record of this town says you are owed an apology by persons considerably above my station. I can't compel their remorse. I can enter the correction." He picked up the ledger's newest plate — the one Quentin had cut, the one that had shown through ten thousand proclamations — and read it aloud for the record:

"Ilves, Tomas. Printer's apprentice. Witness. Loyal."

The words stood in the milk-colored light. Quentin had set them at three in the morning in February with her heart going like a platen, and she had wondered then if any living voice would ever read them aloud, and here was a magistrate doing it, in a ruined courtroom, while a crow heckled through the broken roof, and it was — it was enough. It was the whole job pulled and dried and squared, and she stood there with ink under her nails and her father's trick of steadying her breathing, and let it be enough.

Tomas's hand found her shoulder. Squeezed once. Said what his voice couldn't yet.

* * *

The clerks worked through the afternoon by candlelight, because the army had stripped the courthouse wiring for copper and the days were still short. Quentin stayed to supervise the handling of her type — not because she distrusted Halvorsen, but because she distrusted everyone's thumbs — and Mirele stood post at the door in his uniform, which drew looks from every citizen who passed, and returned each look with the steady, unimpressive dignity of a man who had burned a file and meant to spend the rest of his life earning it back in small installments.

Near dark, Halvorsen dismissed the clerks and came down from the bench, and stood with Quentin and Tomas among the wreckage, and said, quietly, without ceremony:

"There will be a tribunal. Brandt, and several others. The manuscript will be central. So will you, Miss Vasquez — the plates, the chain of custody, your testimony. It will not be gentle. Men facing the rope become inventive about who helped them."

"I know," Quentin said. "I've been expecting the invitation since November."

"They may come for the shop. For your father's name."

"My father's name," Quentin said, "is on every honest page in that ledger, and every dishonest page has mine. Let them read whichever they like. We set them both true."

Halvorsen studied her for a moment, then nodded, once, the nod of one craftsman to another. "The court stands adjourned," he said, to the empty benches, to the crows, to the town going home in the blue dusk with its lamps being lit one by one. "God help us all, it stands."

Outside, the river was talking. It had been talking for days — that long groaning dialogue under the ice, the sound of a held breath finally being let out an inch at a time — and tonight it sounded looser, wetter, closer to giving way altogether. Tomas stood on the courthouse steps beside her, bag still on his shoulder, looking down the street toward the lamplit sweep of the town and, beyond it, the black shine of the water.

"Mother first," he said.

"Mother first." Quentin pulled her collar up. "And tomorrow you'll come to the shop. There's something you should see. Father locked a forme the morning he died — the proclamation forme, the one the whole town pulled from the walls. It's still bolted. I haven't opened it."

Tomas glanced at her. "Why not?"

Quentin looked out at the river, at the ice going gray and rotten along the banks, at the spring that had spent all winter being promised and was now, at last, collecting its debt.

"Because whoever unlocks it," she said, "should be holding the keys to the shop. And I haven't decided yet whose name goes over the door."

She started down the steps, and the ice on the river groaned like a press taking the pull of its life, and somewhere behind them in the dark courthouse a clerk's candle went on burning over the true names, late into the night.
