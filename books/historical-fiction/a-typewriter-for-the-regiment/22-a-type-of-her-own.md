## 22. A Type of Her Own

The ice went out of the Vellmark River on a Tuesday, with a sound like God clearing his throat, and by Thursday morning the swallows were back under the eaves of Vasquez & Son.

Quentin stood in the doorway with a pot of black paint and a brush, looking up at the sign her grandfather had hung before she was born. *Vasquez & Son.* Her father had never once complained about it, not even after it became clear that his son was going to be a daughter and that this daughter was going to have ink in her blood instead of milk. He had simply started teaching her, as if the sign had always meant her, as if the whole town had been waiting fifty years for a girl to walk through that door and pick up the graver.

But signs, like type, could be recast. That was rather the point of the trade.

She climbed the ladder — Mirele held its feet, having insisted, having been refused twice, having insisted again on the grounds that if she fell and broke her neck he would be left alone in Vellmark with no one who understood kerning — and painted over *Son* with slow, careful strokes. Then, beneath it, in letters she had drawn herself the night before at the kitchen table, tongue between her teeth like a child learning to write:

*Q. Vasquez, Printer.*

"Not *Vasquez's Daughter*," Mirele observed from below. "Not *Late Vasquez*."

"Dead men don't set type," Quentin said. "I checked the trade journals."

She came down the ladder, wiped her hands on her apron, and looked at what she'd done. It was a small thing. A painted board. But her father had taught her that all the big things were small things first: a single character cut true, a single name spelled right, a single page pulled clean off a press. You did not inherit an empire. You inherited a chase, a forme, a name — and then you lifted them, two hands at a time, for as long as your back allowed, and you handed them down heavier than you found them.

Inside, the shop smelled the way it always had: lead and oil and the particular sweetness of the old paper stock in the back room, which her father had hoarded against all economic sense because "good paper keeps its promises." The press stood where it had always stood, scrubbed and oiled, its iron still carrying, if you knew where to look, the faint scars of the winter — the scratch where a soldier's bayonet had slipped, the burn where someone — Mirele had never confessed, but Quentin had her suspicions — had spilled a lamp trying to look busy during the raid.

And in the corner, where it had sat since the thaw, the crate.

They had not opened it together yet. There had been too much else: the magistrate's return, the depositions, the long gray weeks of testimony in which Quentin had said the same sentences so many times they wore smooth as river stones. The army had withdrawn in disgrace and paperwork. Colonel Brandt had answered for the massacre in the capital, or would, eventually, in a courtroom where everyone spoke quietly and nothing was printed until the record was approved — a process Tomas described with such contempt that Quentin had offered to print him a pamphlet just to watch him smile.

He had smiled. Then he'd packed his bag.

That was three weeks ago now. He'd stood in this very doorway, hat in hand, looking exactly like the boy who had once taught her the alphabet by cutting letters into a birch plank with a stolen knife. *You'll come to the capital,* he'd said. *When you testify. I'll show you the city.* And she'd said she would, and she'd meant it, but they both knew the truth underneath: that some debts are paid by parting, that gratitude is not a rope to be tied with, and that what they had survived together was a promise kept, not a future begun. He had kissed her hand like a foreign count, which made them both laugh, and then he'd walked down to the barge with his manuscript under his arm — the real one now, printed properly, five hundred copies, bound in gray boards — and the town had come out to watch him go, and Widow Ilves had waved her handkerchief until the bend in the river took him.

Widow Ilves had started speaking again in April. First words out of her mouth, delivered to Quentin over a plate of seed cake: "Your father owed me six crowns for binding work from 1898. I'll take it in pamphlets." She came by every week now to check the shop's accounts, uninvited and indispensable, and had appointed herself, without ceremony, the treasurer of whatever it was Quentin was building.

The bell above the door rang. Mirele came in from the street carrying a box of brass fittings and wearing the expression of a man perpetually astonished to be happy.

"The magistrate sends his regards," he reported, setting the box down. "And also a request. And also — this is important — a complaint about the last invoice."

"What's the request?"

"He wants five hundred copies of the corrected census ledger. Bound. For the courthouse, the school, and — his words — 'every household that lost something.'"

Mirele said it lightly, the way he'd learned to say heavy things that spring. Quentin nodded and wrote it in the daybook. Five hundred copies. Her father had printed ten thousand execution decrees under orders; she would print five hundred books of names under none at all. Somewhere her father was laughing, or would have been, in the place where printers go when the last forme is locked.

"And the complaint?"

"He says our margins are crooked."

"Our margins are *perfect*. His eyes are eighty years old."

"He says the same thing about his spectacles."

* * *

The new type had taken her all winter, in the evenings, by lamplight, when the forging was done and the fear had burned down to embers and there was finally room in her hands for play.

Her father had shown her the trick of making a lie survive a magnifying glass — the slight irregularities, the honest wear, the imperfections that read as truth. But what nobody had ever shown her, because nobody had ever needed to, was how to make a letter that was entirely her own. Every piece of type in that shop had been cut to somebody else's pattern: her father's, her grandfather's, the foundries' in the capital, designs older than the town itself. A printer inherited her alphabet the way she inherited her nose. Nobody thought to question it.

Quentin questioned it.

She had started with the lowercase e, because it seemed friendly. Hers had a slightly open eye and a crossbar that tilted, very faintly, like a person raising one eyebrow. Then the a, rounder than tradition, stubborn about it. The g she gave a tail that curled upward at the end, defiant, and when she showed it to Mirele he studied it for a long moment and said, "That's you," and she had pretended not to be pleased and had been pleased for a week.

By March she had a full face of type — roman and italic, though the italic was really just the roman leaning, which Mirele said was cheating and which Quentin said was *efficiency* — cast in her own molds, from her own patterns, in lead mixed with a little of her father's old metal melted down from the worn sorts of his most-used cases. There was something in that, she thought. The old letters hadn't died. They'd gone into the alloy. Every word she set now carried a little of him in its body.

On the morning after the sign was painted, she locked the first forme of the corrected history.

It was a modest page. Title, date, and then the opening lines, which she had rewritten eleven times and which now read:

*This book contains the true census of the town of Vellmark, as recorded in the year 1911, restored to its proper form by the hands of its people. Where the state's list lied, this list tells the truth. Where the state struck names, this book returns them. No entry herein has been altered except to make it accurate, which is the only alteration worth making.*

Below that, the names began. All of them. The loyalists and the so-called enemies, side by side, in alphabetical order, as if the distinction had never existed — because, the book argued quietly, on the page where it mattered, it never had. The Ilves family appeared in their proper house with their proper holdings and no seizure mark, the line struck through in the army's copy now struck through in turn, rendered illegible by a printer's careful blade and reset clean.

Tomas Ilves. She set his name herself, in her own type, and pulled a proof of just that line, and kept it. Not sentiment, she told herself. Quality control.

She was lying, and the proof knew it, and she put it in her apron pocket anyway.

* * *

Mirele turned out to be a better apprentice than either of them expected, chiefly because he approached printing the way he had once approached military paperwork: with terrified precision and a private conviction that any error might get someone shot. This made him slow. It also made him excellent. Within a month he could lock a chase that passed even Widow Ilves's inspection, and Widow Ilves's inspection had been known to make grown magistrates weep.

"You're pressing too hard on the *th*," she told him one afternoon, standing behind him at the stone with her arms folded. "The ligature isn't a battle, Sergeant. It's a conversation. Let the letters agree."

"Yes, ma'am."

"And stop calling me ma'am. I'm your employer's neighbor and moral overseer."

"Yes, ma'am."

Quentin, at the composing stick across the room, hid her smile in the case. She had hired Mirele formally in May — a contract, signed, witnessed by the magistrate, with wages written down — and the whole town had found it hilarious and touching in equal measure: the sergeant who had once guarded the shop now sweeping its floors, learning its mysteries, being scolded by an eighty-year-old widow over his kerning. Mirele himself did not find it funny at all. He found it, he confessed once, late, over the wash-up, the only penance he could imagine that didn't feel like running away.

"I burned my sister's file," he'd said, staring into the solvent. "One page. One little lie, told well, to a colonel's face. And afterward I couldn't sleep, and I couldn't eat, and I kept thinking: this is what it costs, this is the price of one page, how does anyone carry more?" He'd looked up at her. "Then I watched you carry a whole ledger. So. Teach me to carry things."

She had raised his wages the next morning without comment, and he had understood, and that was the whole conversation, and it had been enough. Some apprenticeships were about type. Most were about weight.

Now he broke the news about the capital while they were distributing a forme together, passing sorts back into their cases in the old rhythm, his hands finally sure.

"The tribunal summons arrived. They want you in August. All the witnesses." He sorted an *n*, an *o*. "You could refuse. The magistrate would support it. After everything—"

"After everything," Quentin said, "they're going to want the record examined by the person who made it. By someone who can tell them what a forged counter looks like. By someone whose hands they can watch." She slotted a final *t* home. "Besides. Someone has to keep an eye on Tomas. He'll let them publish his testimony with crooked margins otherwise."

"You hate the capital."

"I've never seen the capital."

"That's what hating it means, in my experience."

She laughed, and the shop took the sound and kept it, the way good rooms do — the way hers had kept her father's laugh for forty years and now, apparently, intended to keep hers. She caught herself listening for it. Catching herself, catching it. Inheritance, it turned out, was mostly this: discovering that the dead had left instructions everywhere, and that the instructions were all the same instruction. *Keep the record. Keep the record. Keep the record.*

* * *

In June she went down to the cellar one last time before the summer heat, to see whether the damp had gotten into anything, and stood for a while among the crates and the ghosts.

The cellar was just a cellar now. Tomas's crate had been emptied months ago — the typewriter cleaned and oiled and given to the school, where it clattered all day under the enthusiastic abuse of children who would grow up believing that machines for making words were ordinary things, that towns could keep their own records, that names cut in metal were promises and promises could be kept. His manuscript lived upstairs now, in the shop, in a locked drawer beside the original ledger plates, which Quentin had not melted down. Everyone assumed she would. Everyone was wrong.

"You keep the evidence of what you survived," she'd explained to the magistrate. "Otherwise someday someone says you invented it."

He had looked at her for a long moment and then written something in his notebook, and she had decided not to ask what.

But the cellar walls still held the marks of that winter — the candle smoke, the scrape where the crate had been dragged — and standing there in the cool dark, Quentin let herself feel the whole of it at once, the way you can only do after the danger is truly past: the terror of the sentry's boots overhead; her father sitting straight-backed at the press through the raid, printing the army's lies with the truth bolted beneath, daring them with his dying body to stop the work they themselves had ordered; the groan of the ice; the paste-brush freezing in her fingers as she hung the pages on the courthouse wall while Brandt's men watched the wrong riverbank.

A town's memory, hidden in a cellar. A town's memory, walking out of it in a crate on a sledge. A town's memory, pasted to the walls in melting snow, read aloud by lamplight by people who had been listed for disappearance and who stood in the cold reading their own names like psalms.

She had been afraid her whole life of inheriting a trade with no future. Standing in the cellar, she understood at last that she'd had it backwards. The trade had no future without inheritance — without someone willing to lift what could be lifted with two hands and carry it through the winter. The future wasn't a thing the century owed her. It was a thing she set, letter by letter, herself.

She blew out the candle and went upstairs to work.

* * *

On Midsummer's Eve, the shop held its first open house. It was Widow Ilves's idea, which meant it was not optional.

They hung paper lanterns — Mirele on the ladder again, refusing help on principle now, having learned ladders the hard way — and set the press running on the newest toy: a handbill announcing, in Quentin's own type, in her own slightly eyebrow-raising *e*:

*Q. VASQUEZ, PRINTER — BOOKS, BROADSHEETS, LEDGERS SET TRUE. NO JOB TOO SMALL. NO NAME TOO DIFFICULT.*

Every visitor got a souvenir printed on the spot: their own name, set by Mirele or by Quentin, pressed onto a card while they watched. Children queued up twice and thrice under false identities, delighted by crime. Old Marta Kessler, whose name the army had misspelled on its loyalty list in a way that had nearly gotten her house seized, requested hers three times in large type and nailed one above her door, one inside her door, and, reportedly, one to the underside of her table "where the government won't think to look."

Toward dusk, the magistrate himself came, moving slowly, leaning on a cane, and stood a long time before the framed proof hanging by the press: the frontispiece of the corrected history, with its eleven-times-rewritten opening lines.

"You know," he said, "when you brought that crate into my court, I thought the remarkable thing was the courage of it. Sneaking it past an army. Now I think the remarkable thing is quieter." He tapped the glass over the lines. "*No entry altered except to make it accurate.* Do you know how many officials in my long career could have signed those words? I can count them on no hands at all."

"It's just good practice," Quentin said. "My father used to say—"

"I know what your father used to say. Half the town quotes him now. The other half misquotes him, and we'll need a second edition to correct them, so don't go anywhere."

Later, when the lanterns were low and the crowd had thinned to the loyal and the tired, Mirele found her at the composing stone, already setting tomorrow's job — a wedding announcement, of all the blessed ordinary things, for the cooper's daughter.

"There's a letter," he said, holding it out. "Capital postmark."

She set down the composing stick. Her heart performed a maneuver she chose not to dignify with analysis. The envelope was thick — manuscript-thick — and the handwriting on it was the same handwriting that had once carved the alphabet into a birch plank, grown up now, steadier, but recognizably the same boy who had taught her that letters were small machines for keeping people alive across distances.

She opened it at the stone, under the lamp, with Mirele pretending very hard to sort type four feet away.

*Tomas* had testified in July, it said. The hearings had gone long; the record was enormous; the officers named in his manuscript were answering questions in rooms with bad lighting, which was, he supposed, poetic justice of a kind. The tribunal wished to publish the full proceedings — all of it, the massacre, the doctored ledger entries, the whole machinery of the lie — as a book. An official book, this time, with the state's own seal on it, admitting its own record.

Which meant, he wrote, that they needed a printer. Not a capital printer. Those were compromised, or timid, or both. They needed a printer who had already proven she could set the truth in metal and make it survive a magnifying glass. They needed, the tribunal had resolved after some argument, *the printer of Vellmark* — and he, Tomas, had informed them that she was extremely busy and would require persuading, and that he was enclosing his best attempt at persuasion, namely: terms, deadlines, and the observation that the corrected history deserved a companion volume and that no one on earth should be allowed to set it but her.

There was a postscript, in smaller writing, the ink of a man adding something after deciding not to say it in the body:

*P.S. — You once promised you'd always cut my name true. Consider what you might do with a whole nation's.*

Quentin read it twice. Then she set the letter down on the stone, next to the wedding announcement with its hopeful names and its crooked-free margins, and looked around her shop — the press, the cases, her own type gleaming faintly in the lamplight, the apprentice sorting sorts with the concentration of a man defusing ordnance, the widow's account books squared on the shelf, the framed proof on the wall, the whole small kingdom of paper and lead that she had inherited by carrying it.

"Mirele," she said.

"Ma'am."

"How do you feel about national commissions?"

He looked up. He understood immediately — he always did — and she watched the arithmetic run behind his eyes: the deadline, the volume, the travel, the risk of working at the scale of governments, the sheer unreasonable size of the job.

"We'll need more lead," he said at last.

"We'll need more everything." She picked up her composing stick and weighed it in her hand, two-handed weight in one palm, light as a promise. "Fortunately," Quentin Vasquez said, "we know how to cast."

And outside, the river ran high and free toward the sea, carrying nothing but spring.
