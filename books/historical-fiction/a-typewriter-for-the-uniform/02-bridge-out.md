## 2. Bridge Out

The snow began at dusk on the third day, and by morning it had erased the road.

Oscar stood at the window of the Gasthaus Adler and watched it come down in fat, deliberate flakes, each one taking its time, as if the sky had all winter to spend and meant to spend it here. The mountains across the valley had gone soft-edged, indistinct, a watercolor of themselves. Somewhere beneath that white was the road south. Somewhere beneath that white, in a week or a month or however long the weather allowed, was the border Oscar was paid to forget existed.

They dressed without hurry. Ten years of courier work had taught them that hurry was a smell, and dogs and clerks and suspicious men in railway stations could all smell it. They buttoned the coat with the sewn lining and felt the dispatches press flat against their ribs like a second heartbeat. Eleven pages. Eleven names that were not names, eleven sets of coordinates that were not coordinates, written in a cipher so dull it looked like a grocer's inventory. The banality was the point. No one dies for a grocery list.

Downstairs, the Adler's common room smelled of woodsmoke and boiled ham and the sour breath of the stove, which had been lit since October and never quite forgiven. Frau Haller, the landlady, was wiping tables that were already clean.

"You'll want to hear this," she said, without looking up. "They've closed the bridge."

Oscar kept their face in the ordinary register. "Closed."

"Closed. Since before dawn. The avalanche took the road on the south side — you could hear it yesterday evening if you were listening, a sound like the mountain clearing its throat. Two men came down from the tollhouse to say the engineers won't attempt the cut until the thaw. March, maybe April. The captain at the checkpoint says the town is closed in and closed out. No one crosses. No one leaves." She wrung her cloth over the basin. "You'll be wanting a room for the winter, then. I'll knock a bit off the weekly rate, since it's a long stay."

"Very kind," Oscar said.

"Is it?" Frau Haller looked at them then, with the frank, unembarrassed curiosity of a woman who had fed travelers for thirty years and missed nothing worth missing. "You don't seem the sort to be caught by weather. You seem the sort who knows the timetables."

"The timetables lied," Oscar said, and it was true enough, and true things were always the safest lies.

They went out into it anyway. This was the discipline: when the world changed, walk into the change and see it with your own eyes, because secondhand news was where panic lived. The streets of Vellacorte were muffled, the snow absorbing sound the way wool absorbs blood. The river ran gray-green and fast beneath its crust of new ice, and the bridge — the bridge was a black iron spine across it, and at its southern end, small in the falling white, soldiers were stacking sandbags with the unhurried industry of men building something they intended to keep.

Oscar stood at the rail of the embankment for a long time. The cold found the gap between their collar and their jaw and settled there.

Eleven pages against their ribs. A town sealed like a jar. A major's checkpoint at one end of the bridge and a wall of collapsed mountain at the other, and between them perhaps four hundred souls, one bakery whose bread was famous two valleys over, and a winter that had only just begun to show them what it intended.

*Answer less than asked,* the mentor's voice said, from ten years and a grave away. *Never more.*

Oscar turned up their collar and went to learn the town.

* * *

Above the bakery on the Kirchgasse, Ilse Brandt watched the same snowfall through a window she kept deliberately dirty.

There was a craft to a dirty window. A clean one invited eyes; a dirty one was furniture. She had applied the grime in layers over two years, thin washes of soot and tallow, until the glass gave back the street in a brown murmur — the shapes of carts, the hunch of passers-by, the black iron spine of the bridge — without ever surrendering a detail. From the street, the room behind it was a rumor. Let it stay one.

She sat at her worktable with her hands in her lap because her hands, this morning, were not to be trusted.

They had begun in the autumn. A fine tremor, first in the left, arriving in the pauses between keystrokes, so that the *e* of a name would land a hair late and land a hair light, and the ribbon would print it gray where its neighbors stood black. A trained eye would catch it. A trained eye was exactly the kind of eye her work existed to defeat. So she had begun soaking the offending keys' impressions in a vinegar bath to dull the type, and slowing her rhythm to disguise the hesitation, and telling herself it was the cold, it was the cold, the stove was temperamental, anyone's hands would complain in this cold.

But this morning, holding her hands still in her lap, she could feel it running under the skin like water under ice. Both hands now. And a new thing: a tightness across the chest when she climbed the stairs, and a cough that came at night and left a taste of iron.

*You have until the thaw,* she told herself, because the thaw was the number her mind returned to the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. The bridge would open at the thaw. The papers on her desk would go where they were going. And after that — she did not finish the sentence. She was a woman who finished sentences for a living, forged endings onto other people's unfinished lives, and she could not finish her own.

She looked at the ledgers. Three of them, bound in gray board, unmarked on their spines, and inside them the whole quiet population of her decade: the names she had built and the names she had buried, the birthdates invented, the parishes invented to hold the birthdates, the priest's signatures practiced until a bishop could not have told the difference. Two hundred and some lives, each one begun at her typewriter with a keystroke that had to sound — had to *be* — indistinguishable from the truth.

The snow kept falling. Through the dirty window the bridge was going white, the sandbags going white, the soldiers becoming suggestions.

*Closed for the season,* the tollhouse men had said in the bakery below, and she had heard it through the floor while pretending to count change. *No one crosses. No one leaves.*

No one leaves.

She thought of the papers stacked in the drawer to her left — the Weisz family, four of them, promised for the new year. The father was already across the border in a safe house with a borrowed name; the mother and two girls were waiting in a village to the north, in a house that would be searched before the thaw, everyone knew the pattern, the searches came with the cold weather like the coughs. The papers had to reach them by February. There was no February road. There was no road at all.

Ilse put her hands flat on the table to stop their trembling and found that the table itself seemed to tremble instead, or the room, or the town — the whole white jar of Vellacorte with its four hundred souls and its single black spine of a bridge, sealed at both ends, filling up with snow and with time.

A bell rang below. The bakery door. Someone had come in out of the storm, and she heard Frau Brandl's voice go careful and flat, the voice she used for strangers.

Ilse rose, and the stairs complained under her the way they always did, and she went down to sell bread and to look, through the doorway's honest curtain of steam, at whoever the winter had just delivered to her street.

* * *

They met, that morning, without meeting.

Oscar passed the bakery on the Kirchgasse because it was on the way to nowhere in particular, and nowhere in particular was where a stranded courier learned a town. The smell reached the middle of the street — yeast and woodsmoke and the caramel edge of the morning's first loaves — and beneath it, fainter, the oil-and-ribbon smell of a machine kept working somewhere above. Oscar's nose had been trained on that smell in three countries. It was the smell of secrets being manufactured at speed.

In the bakery window, a woman with gray-streaked hair and a typist's stoop was wrapping a loaf for a soldier, her movements precise, her face arranged into a pleasant blankness Oscar recognized the way one recognizes a fellow speaker of a language everyone else believes is dead.

The woman looked up. For half a second their eyes met through the glass — hers reading, Oscar understood later, not a face but a coat, a walk, a set of shoulders that carried weight sewn flat against the ribs — and then she looked down at her loaf and was nobody again.

Oscar walked on. Behind them the snow kept falling on the bridge, on the sandbags, on the soldiers, on the town that had closed around them like a hand. Eleven pages beat against their ribs. Above the bakery, in a room with a dirty window, a woman sat back down at a machine and struck a key, and the small iron sound of it — *tak* — was swallowed whole by the snow, and heard by no one at all.

Except that it was heard. Sound travels strangely in a town under snow. It went down through the floorboards of the workshop into the rafters of the bakery, and from the rafters it went into the ears of the two soldiers who had been billeted that very morning in the room behind the ovens — the advance men of a signals unit whose major, they said, was arriving on the evening coach with a crate of his own, a man, they said, who collected machines.

The coach, they said, was due at six.

And the snow, they said, was expected to last all week.
