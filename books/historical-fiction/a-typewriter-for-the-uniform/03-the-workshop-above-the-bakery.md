## 3. The Workshop Above the Bakery

The cold found Oscar before the morning did. It came up through the floorboards of the Pension Adler like a patient animal, nosing at the edges of blankets, and by dawn it had won. Oscar dressed in the dark, buttoning the courier's coat over three shirts, and stood a while at the window watching Vellacorte assemble itself out of the snow.

The town was smaller in daylight than it had been at night, when lamplight had given it depth and mystery. Now it was simply a fistful of grey buildings hunched against the mountainside, chimneys smoking straight up into windless air, the river below running green-black between shelves of ice. The bridge — the famous bridge, the only road south until spring — hung above the water on its stone legs, wearing a shawl of frost. A wooden barricade stood at its near end, and two figures in field-grey leaned against it with the boneless patience of men who had nowhere to be for four months.

*Closed for the season.* The innkeeper had said it twice last night, once as fact and once as apology, as if repetition might soften it. Oscar had nodded and asked for another log.

Now, standing at the window with breath fogging the glass, Oscar performed the small arithmetic of survival. Dispatches sewn into coat lining: still there, still flat against the ribs, still silent. Bridge: closed one hundred and forty days, give or take. Major arriving tonight: unknown quantity. Options: none that ended well.

There remained only the problem of warmth, and warmth, unlike everything else, was a problem that could be solved by walking.

* * *

The bakery on the Kirchgasse opened early because bread waits for no one's politics. Oscar followed the smell down the lane through falling snow — woodsmoke and yeast and something sweeter beneath, burnt sugar maybe, or memory — and pushed open a door heavy enough to be a church's.

Inside, heat struck like a hand. The ovens ran along the back wall, iron mouths glowing orange, and a broad woman with flour to her elbows was sliding loaves in and out with the economy of long practice. She looked up, took in the coat, the travel-stained boots, the face of someone who hadn't slept properly in weeks, and said nothing, because in border towns you learned not to ask. She sold Oscar a loaf still warm enough to hurt through the paper, and when Oscar lingered, palms open toward the oven's radiant breath, she jerked her chin toward a staircase in the corner.

"Warmer upstairs," she said. "My sister keeps rooms. She doesn't like being interrupted, but she likes company less than she hates the cold." A pause; flour drifted from her hands like slow snow. "Knock twice."

Oscar climbed.

The stairs were narrow and smelled of paper and machine oil, an odd scent for a room above a bakery, where one expected sugar and yeast. At the top: a door of dark wood, unmarked. Oscar knocked twice, as instructed, and heard — after a moment — a chair scrape, a drawer close with a soft decisive click, footsteps.

The door opened on a woman perhaps fifty, thin as a winter branch, with grey hair pinned severely and eyes the colour of the river below the ice. She wore a man's cardigan over a high-collared blouse and she looked at Oscar the way a customs officer looks at luggage: thoroughly, without apology, cataloguing.

"You're not from here," she said.

"No one is from here," Oscar answered, "in winter."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth. Not quite a smile; more the place where a smile would go, if she permitted such things. "You want work done," she said. It was not quite a question, and that was the trap, though Oscar did not yet know it was a trap.

"I was told—" Oscar began, meaning to say *I was told there were warm rooms*, but the sentence died, and the silence filled itself with other meanings, and Ilse Brandt — though Oscar did not yet know her name — read the silence exactly wrong.

"Come in, then," she said, stepping aside. "Quickly. The cold gets in behind you."

* * *

The workshop was a single long room under the eaves, and it was warm — warm the way only rooms with secrets are warm, sealed and banked and carefully heated. A stove ticked in the corner. Along one wall stood shelves, and the shelves held paper: reams of it, cream and blue and buff, watermarked and plain, stacked with the tenderness other people reserved for linen or children.

And there were documents. Oscar saw them before understanding them, the way one sees a snake before knowing it is a snake. Passports, half-finished, their owners' faces not yet pasted in. Baptismal certificates with dates of birth going back fifty years, ink aged artificially in a tray by the window — Oscar could see the tea-staining apparatus, could see the little brush beside it with worn bristles. Marriage records. Travel permits. A whole civilisation of alternate lives, hanging from clips like laundry.

On the table in the centre of the room sat a typewriter.

It was a battered thing, black enamel chipped to bare metal at the corners, and even from across the room Oscar — who had spent ten years noticing machines, because machines noticed things about people — could see that something was wrong with it. The keys were one alphabet; the typeface slugs visible through the ribbon guide were another. Someone had rebuilt it deliberately, key by key, so that what a typist's fingers remembered was not what the page received. A machine that lied fluently.

Ilse Brandt watched Oscar look at all of it. And Oscar, whose entire adult life was built on the principle of answering less than asked and never more, understood in a single vertiginous instant what was happening: she thought they were a customer. She had drawn the obvious conclusion from the knock, the stranger, the hesitation, the coat that concealed things. In her mind, Oscar had climbed these stairs seeking a new name.

She was wrong. But the room was warm. And the door had closed.

"I should explain my prices," she said briskly, moving to the stove to feed it a coal, "before anything else, because people always want to haggle afterward, and I don't haggle. Lives are not sausages." She spoke with her back turned, in the tone of a woman who had delivered this speech many times. "A full set — passport, baptismal record, internal permit — is expensive. Half the cost is paper, a quarter is time, and the last quarter is the part nobody thinks about, which is making sure your name has never existed anywhere except where I say it has. Anyone can forge a document. What matters is forging the absence of a document."

Oscar said nothing. Silence, ten years of it, rose automatically, and for the first time in those ten years the silence felt different in the mouth — felt less like armour and more like agreement.

She turned, misreading it again, and softened. "You're frightened. They usually are." She pulled out a chair for Oscar, the gesture unexpectedly gentle. "Sit. Tell me who you need to be."

* * *

And here was the moment — Oscar would return to it often in the weeks that followed, turning it over like a stone found in a pocket — here was the moment when a single true sentence would have set everything right. *I'm sorry. I'm a courier. I came for the warmth. I've mistaken nothing and I'll leave now.*

Ten years ago, Oscar would have said it without thinking. Ten years ago, before the mentor with his catechism — *answer less than asked, never more; every word is a door and doors can be forced* — before the family that no longer existed except as names on a document that was itself a forgery. Ten years of practice had made the silence automatic, and automatic things do not ask permission.

So Oscar sat. And let her believe it.

Because the room was warm, said one voice. Because the major arrives at six and a customer of the forger is invisible in a way a mere lodger is not, said another voice, colder and more honest. Because if she believes you belong here, you belong here, and belonging is the rarest currency in a town full of soldiers.

Ilse Brandt pulled a ledger toward her — a plain school exercise book, unremarkable, the kind a child might use — and opened it to a blank page. Her hands, Oscar noticed, trembled slightly as she smoothed the paper, and she pressed them flat against the desk to still them, and pretended she had not.

"Name?" she asked, pencil ready.

And Oscar, who had abandoned their own name years ago in a railway station in another country, who had been a dozen neutral nobodies since, heard themselves say the first false thing aloud:

"Thornbury."

It was, at least, half true. It had been a grandmother's name, before. Names are like coats; you wear what keeps you alive.

Ilse wrote it down. Her pencil moved in a small, beautiful, old-fashioned hand. "First name?"

"Oscar."

"And you need papers saying—"

"Whatever papers get a person south," Oscar said, "in spring."

Her pencil stopped. She looked up, and for a moment the customs-officer gaze returned, sharper than before, probing. "South," she repeated. "In spring. When the bridge opens anyway." She closed the ledger slowly. "You don't need papers for spring. You need papers for the waiting."

The stove ticked. Snow ticked against the window. Somewhere below them, faintly, the bakery woman called out to someone in the street, and the ordinary sound passed through the floorboards like a reminder that the world still had ordinary hours in it.

"Yes," Oscar said quietly. "For the waiting."

Ilse studied them a moment longer. Whatever she saw — and she saw a great deal; Oscar was beginning to understand that this frail, severe woman missed very little — she chose to fold it away rather than unfold it. She had her own silences; you could see them, worn smooth as the keys of the lying typewriter.

"The waiting costs extra," she said dryly, and almost smiled.

Then the light changed.

It came through the frosted window as a shadow first — two shadows, actually, cast long and wavering across the workshop floor by something happening in the street below — accompanied by the crunch of many boots in snow, and the low carrying voice of authority giving instructions it expected to be obeyed. Ilse rose in one motion, and her whole body had changed: the severity gone rigid, the hands suddenly steady, steadied by fear the way a mast is steadied by wind.

She crossed to the window and looked down into the Kirchgasse, and Oscar watched her face do something complicated and terrible.

"They're measuring," she said. "They have a surveyor's tape. They're measuring the building."

Below, in the falling snow, the two soldiers from the night before were walking the perimeter of the bakery, unreeling yellow tape behind them, while a third man — an officer, greatcoat, gloves, unhurried — stood looking up at the facade with the mild appraising interest of a man considering a purchase.

He looked, for a long moment, directly at the workshop window.

Ilse stepped back from the glass too late. Down in the street, the officer smiled — a small, courteous, terrible smile — and made a note in a little book he carried.

"He collects machines," Ilse said softly. Her hand went to the battered typewriter on the table and rested there, the way one rests a hand on a sleeping child one expects to be taken away. "His men told the baker. Typewriters. He collects them." Her fingers tightened on the chipped black enamel. "He cannot have this one. This one has saved more lives than any doctor in the province."

Outside, the coach from the valley ground into the square below, hours early, hauling its cargo through the snow — and among the crates being unloaded into the bakery's cellar entrance, one was long, flat, and shaped unmistakably like a coffin for a machine.
