## 4. Names Not Their Own

She named her price over bread and cold coffee, as though it were nothing more than the cost of a stamp.

"You'll learn the machine," Ilse said. "Every evening. Two hours, maybe three. And when my hands go—and they will go, Oscar, don't look at me like a man being asked to believe in weather—you take the work where I leave it."

"I'm a courier. I carry things. I don't make them."

"Everything carried was made by someone." She pushed the plate of dark bread toward them across the scarred table, and in the lamplight her fingers trembled—a fine, quick shiver, like the surface of water when a fish passes beneath. She saw Oscar see it, and folded her hands flat against the wood as if pressing down something that wanted to rise. "You crossed a border with dispatches sewn into your coat. You stood in front of that sergeant at the checkpoint and gave him four true words and eleven false ones and he never knew the difference. Don't tell me you can't learn a trade that is only lying with your hands instead of your mouth."

The bakery below had gone quiet hours ago; the ovens ticked as they cooled, a language of small metallic complaints. Through the frosted window the snow kept falling with the patience of something that intended to outlast them all.

Oscar looked at the typewriter on its table beneath the hanging lamp—the battered one whose keys wore letters that did not match what they struck, the machine that had produced baptismal records for children who had never been christened and travel papers for people who would otherwise have been traveled to camps. Ten years of silence sat in their chest like swallowed stone. Silence had rules. Silence said: deliver, do not ask. Carry, do not open. The moment you learn a name, you owe it something.

And she was asking them to learn every name.

"If I refuse?" they said.

"Then in three weeks there will be no one in Vellacorte who can produce a document that survives inspection, and every person in those ledgers will be exactly who the ledgers say they are." She said it without drama, the way a doctor reports a temperature. That was the worst of it—the absence of appeal. She was not begging. She was arithmetic. "I'm not asking you to save them. I'm asking you to keep the door open until spring does."

Oscar heard themselves say yes before they had decided to. Perhaps that was how it always worked—perhaps courage was only the word people used afterward for the thing they'd agreed to before understanding it.

* * *

The first lesson was ink.

Ilse unscrewed a small brown bottle and poured a measure into the machine's reservoir, and the smell rose up sharp and medicinal, cutting through the warm bread-scent that had soaked into the workshop's walls. She showed Oscar how the ribbon fed, how the spools turned, how a document aged properly under a damp cloth and a warm iron so the paper took on the soft yellow of years it had never lived.

"Paper has a memory," she said. "Fresh paper remembers the mill. You have to teach it to remember other things—a drawer, a pocket, a church registry shelf. Hold it near steam but don't touch the steam. Patience. Forgers are ruined by hurry more than by skill."

Her hands shook when she threaded the ribbon. It took her three tries, and she narrated each failure in a flat, uninflected voice that told Oscar this trembling was new enough to still be shameful and old enough to be terrifying. On the fourth try the ribbon caught, and neither of them remarked on it, and the not-remarking felt like the first truly intimate thing that had passed between them.

"Now the keys." She laid Oscar's index finger on the T. "Watch. This key says T. What does it strike?"

Oscar pressed. A clean F appeared on the scrap paper.

"F," Ilse said. "Every key lies about itself. The whole machine is built on the principle that what a thing appears to be and what a thing produces are two different facts. You learn the map once—" she tapped her temple "—and after that your eyes stop believing the keys and start trusting your memory. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

"That the machine is a courier," Oscar said slowly. "It carries one message on its face and another underneath."

Something moved through her face then—not quite a smile, but the place where a smile would live if it ever came back to stay. "Yes," she said. "Exactly that. Good. Now type me your name."

"My name isn't—"

"The name on the paper in your pocket. The one that got you across the border. Type that."

Oscar typed it, hunting the false keys letter by letter, slow as a child. THOMAS WEHLER emerged crooked and pale on the page, and Ilse studied it the way a jeweler studies a stone she suspects of being glass.

"Wehler," she read aloud. "Thomas Wehler. Is he real?"

"He's real enough. There was a Thomas Wehler. He died in 'thirty-one, age nineteen, and his papers didn't die with him because his mother understood that grief is not the only thing the living inherit."

"And where were you born, Thomas Wehler?"

"In a town I've never seen. In a house I've never stood in. My birthday is a Tuesday in March that belonged to a dead boy." Oscar heard their own voice go dry and strange. They had never spoken these mechanics aloud. Ten years of carrying other identities, and the identities had been luggage—sealed, unexamined. Saying the seams out loud made the coat feel suddenly thin. "He's a good name. He's kept me alive twice."

Ilse nodded slowly. Then she opened the ledger—the fat one, bound in grey cardboard, the one Oscar had glimpsed and looked away from in the first days—and turned to a page near the middle.

"Then you'll understand these," she said. "Because I don't fill pages with names I invented. I fill them with names that were taken away."

* * *

The lessons became the architecture of the evenings.

Each night, after the bakery slept, Oscar climbed the stairs and sat at the machine while Ilse worked at the long bench behind them, cutting stencil letters or mixing inks, correcting Oscar's errors with a tap of one trembling finger on their shoulder. And each night she taught one name from the ledger—not as a lesson in forgery, but as a lesson in why.

"Marta Keller," she said on the third night. "Age thirty-four. Schoolteacher from Freiburg. Her crime was a signature—she marked a student absent on a day the girl was actually at a union meeting. They wanted the girl. Marta wouldn't give her. So now Marta needs to become Frau Brenner of Basel, a widow, and she needs to become her so completely that even her own handwriting forgets Marta." She slid a practice sheet across the desk. "Type her new name fifty times. If you're going to give someone a name, you should know what it costs."

On the fifth night: "The Aldermanns. Father, mother, two boys. Jewish, which on paper we cannot fix, so on paper we must erase—new surname, new parish record, new everything. The younger boy is seven. When they come to collect the papers, he'll ask me why the man in the photograph isn't his father. What do I tell him, Oscar? What does a seven-year-old understand about necessary lies?"

"They understand being hidden," Oscar said quietly. "Children hide all the time. It's the mothers who need the lie explained."

Ilse stopped working. For a long moment the only sound was the cooling ovens below and the wind testing the window frame. Then she said, "That's a thing someone taught you," and Oscar did not answer, and she did not press, and the silence between them changed quality—it stopped being a wall and became a room they were both standing in.

By the eighth night Oscar could type a full identity at speed, eyes closed, trusting memory over the lying keys. By the tenth, Ilse let them produce a complete document unassisted—a passport for a man named Josef Haller, a butcher, a widower, a fiction assembled from a dead man's dates and a living man's photograph—and she examined it for a long time before setting it down.

"It would pass," she said. "In Bern it would pass. In Berlin—" she paused "—in Berlin it would pass if the inspector was tired, and half of them are tired, and the other half are paid." Her voice caught slightly on the last word. Oscar watched her right hand, resting on the bench, tremble so hard the fingers blurred. She closed it into a fist. "You're ready for the harder part."

"Which is?"

"Deciding who goes first." She pulled the ledger toward her. "There are forty-one names in this book. Some can wait for spring. Some cannot wait for anything. Every week I have ranked them—urgency, risk, route—and every week the ranking changes because the war outside changes faster than ink dries. When my hands fail completely, that judgment becomes yours. Not the typing. The typing anyone can learn." She looked up, and her eyes were bright with something that was not sentiment—it was fury, banked and precise. "The judgment is the trade. Everything else is craft."

* * *

On the twelfth night she could no longer hide it.

They were working on the Aldermann boys' papers when her hand spasmed mid-stroke and the pen tore through the baptismal certificate, gouging a dark line across the name of a child who did not yet exist. She stared at the ruin. Then she picked up the paper, very carefully, and began to tear it into strips, and her hands shook so badly that the tearing took three times longer than it should have, and somewhere in the middle of it she stopped and put both palms flat on the table and bowed her head, and did not cry, which was somehow worse.

"The doctor in Bern says Parkinson's," she said to the table. "Or something wearing Parkinson's clothes. Six months ago I dropped a teacup. Three months ago I couldn't thread a needle. Last week I signed a death certificate"—she lifted her head, and her mouth twisted—"a forged death certificate, for a woman who is alive and eating dinner tonight in Zurich—with the wrong year in it. 1934. I wrote 1934, Oscar. The inspector caught it. He let it pass, God knows why, maybe pity, maybe laziness, but I can't build a life on an inspector's pity."

Oscar said nothing. There was nothing to say that wasn't either a lie or a cruelty.

"So now you know the timetable," she went on, recovering her flatness piece by piece, like a woman reassembling a shattered window pane. "Weeks, not months, for fine work. The machine doesn't forgive shaking hands. Paper remembers everything, including hesitation."

She swept the torn certificate into the scrap basket. Then she straightened, and fixed Oscar with a look that was almost stern.

"One more thing, and then we sleep. The ledgers—the names, the routes, the contacts, everything—are the most dangerous objects in this town. More dangerous than your dispatches. Your dispatches condemn an army. These condemn forty-one families. You understand? Whatever happens—whatever comes through that door downstairs—if the choice ever arrives between the work and the book, the book burns. Promise me."

"The book burns," Oscar repeated. The words tasted like ash already, like a thing rehearsed.

"Good." She began covering the machine, drawing the canvas hood over it with exaggerated care, steadying her wrist with her left hand. "Now go down before the baker wakes and wonders why his upstairs lodger smells of ink."

Oscar was at the stair door, hand on the latch, when she spoke again—quietly, not looking up from the covered machine.

"Tomorrow, when you make your delivery past the major's office—he's requisitioned the ground floor, did you hear? Signals post. His men moved telegraph equipment in this afternoon." She finally raised her eyes. "From tomorrow on, we work directly above the ears of the German army. So whatever you learned tonight, learn it twice as well. And whatever you feel—" her gaze held theirs, steady despite the tremor in her hands, steady despite everything "—whatever you feel, keep it off your face. He collects typewriters, they say. Let's pray he never develops a taste for what they write."

Below, faint through the floorboards, came the first thin clatter of a telegraph key beginning its night watch—tapping out its coded lies to the world, one floor beneath the machine that wrote the truth under other names.
