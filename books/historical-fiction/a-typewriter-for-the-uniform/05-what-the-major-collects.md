## 5. What the Major Collects

The soldiers came on a Tuesday, in the grey hour when the bakery's ovens had been lit but the bread was still only a promise, and they came without violence, which was somehow worse.

Oscar heard them from the workshop above — the crunch of boots in packed snow, the muffled authority of voices — and stood at the frosted window with a half-forged baptismal record curling in their ink-stained fingers. Below, four men in field-grey arranged themselves around the bakery door with the unconscious choreography of men who had done this many times and would do it many more. A fifth emerged from the staff car idling at the kerb: tall, silver at the temples, wearing his major's insignia the way other men wore reading glasses — as though it were merely an instrument for seeing more clearly.

He did not kick in the door. He knocked.

Oscar watched old Marta Voss open it, flour to her elbows, and watched the major remove his glove before extending his hand. They spoke for perhaps three minutes. Oscar could not hear the words through the double glass, but they could read the shape of them: the major gesturing upward once, briefly, toward the floors above; Marta's chin lifting; the major's small, patient smile; and then the transaction concluded, because that was what it was — a requisition conducted with the manners of a man buying lace.

By evening there was a stenciled notice pasted beside the bakery door, and by the following morning there were wires strung along the eaves like strange winter bunting, and a mast bolted to the roof two feet from the workshop's chimney, so that smoke now rose past the fluttering cloth of an antenna and the whole building breathed occupation with every loaf.

*Signals post*, the notice said. *Civilian premises commandeered under authority of the District Command. Cooperation is expected. Compensation will be arranged.*

The last line was the most frightening thing Oscar had read all winter, and they had spent the season reading lists of names that meant death.

* * *

"They took the front room," Ilse said. She had not gone down to look; she had sent Oscar, and Oscar had returned with the report she already seemed to possess. She sat at her workbench with her hands folded, as if holding them still required attention now, as if the tremor were something she could starve out of them through discipline. "The room above the ovens. The warmest room in the building."

"You knew he would come here."

"I knew someone would, eventually." She said it without drama, the way one reports weather. "A border town with one working telegraph office and a bakery with thick walls and a chimney that draws well? If I were running a signals post, I would take it too." Her mouth did something that was almost a smile. "I chose this building for the same reasons he did."

That was the thing about Ilse Brandt that unsettled Oscar more than any soldier could: she thought like them. Not like a fugitive — like a professional. Ten years of silence had taught Oscar to think in exits and alibis, but Ilse thought in *systems*. She had built her trade the way an engineer builds a bridge, and now the enemy had simply walked onto the span and declared it military property.

"The dispatches," Oscar said quietly. "I have to take them past him. Every day. To the drop at the chandler's."

"I know."

"He'll see me. Daily. He'll learn my face the way a man learns the face of his milk delivery."

"Yes," Ilse said. "So we must make sure the face he learns is worth nothing. Boring. A courier who is only a courier." She turned on her stool to face them fully, and in the lamplight Oscar could see how the illness had carved her — the cheekbones standing out like ridgelines, the skin gone translucent over the temples. "You will be polite. You will be slightly stupid. You will complain about your feet. Men like him collect information the way you collect impressions — passively, constantly, involuntarily. So give him nothing sharp. Give him wool."

* * *

The first delivery was three days after the requisition, and Oscar rehearsed it in their head the way they had once rehearsed nothing at all, because for ten years the whole self had been rehearsal.

The major had converted the bakery's front room into an office that was neat as a hymnal. Wires ran along the skirting boards to a receiver on a trestle table; a map of the valley hung on the wall with pins in it like a specimen board; and against the far wall, on a shelf he had evidently built himself — the sawdust was still visible in the joints — sat typewriters.

Seven of them. Eight. Oscar counted without meaning to, the way one counts exits. Uprights mostly, their carriage arms raised like the necks of grazing animals, each one tagged with a paper label in a precise hand. Confiscated. Collected. Arranged by make, or by date of seizure, or by some private taxonomy Oscar could not begin to guess at.

"Good morning." The major looked up from a signal log with the unhurried attention of a man who had expected them. "You're the courier. Thornbury."

"Oscar Thornbury, sir. Papers run between Vellacorte and the valley offices. Weekly round, though the bridge—" They let it trail off into the shrug everyone in town had perfected, the shrug that said *the mountain does what the mountain does*.

"Closed, yes. Which makes you, temporarily, a courier without a route." He said it mildly, almost kindly, and Oscar felt the sentence go through them like a needle finding the gap between ribs. He rose, crossed to the shelf, and rested two fingers on one of the machines — a black upright with a cracked spacebar. "Do you type?"

"A little, sir. Two fingers. Slowly."

"Mm." He said it the way a fisherman says *mm* when the line twitches. Then, pleasantly: "This one belonged to the schoolmaster in Tarnfeld. He taught thirty years. When his unit came through, he asked if he might keep it, and my adjutant said no, and now it sits on my shelf and teaches no one anything." He touched another machine, further along. "This one came from a newspaper office in the lowlands. This one from a lawyer who was also a man we wanted. The typewriter stayed. The man did not." He said it without pride or menace, the way a collector describes provenance. "People imagine we confiscate machines because of what's written on them. But a machine remembers everything it has ever written. Every document has a fingerprint. The rhythm, the wear on the keys, the alignment of a bent letter. I could show you two pages typed by the same hands twenty years apart, and I would know." His eyes came up, mild and blue and bottomless. "It's why I collect them. Machines are honest. People are not."

"No, sir," Oscar said, and complained about their feet, and signed the register with a deliberately clumsy hand, and walked out past the smell of baking bread and field telephone insulation into the cold, where they stood for a full minute in the snow letting their heartbeat come back down to something a doctor wouldn't remark upon.

* * *

Above, Ilse listened to the report with her eyes closed, and when Oscar described the shelf of machines her face went very still.

"How many?"

"Eight. Nine. Tagged."

She was quiet for a long moment. Through the floorboards came the faint, arrhythmic ticking of the receiver below, and beneath that, deeper, the oven's heat rising through the joists like a pulse. The whole building had become an ear. Oscar realized they had begun to walk differently in the workshop — softly, on the balls of the feet — and could not remember deciding to.

"One of them," Ilse said finally, "will be mine."

"What?"

"Someday. Perhaps not today." She opened her eyes. "He collects confiscated typewriters, and I keep a machine whose keys don't match its typeface, in a room directly above his office. Do you understand what that means? It means that if he ever hears it — one page, one paragraph — he won't need to find me. The sound alone will convict me. Every typewriter sings differently, and he has trained himself to know the songs."

Oscar felt the vertigo arrive properly then, the way Ilse must have felt it watching the snowfall from this window while the bridge closed. The building was no longer a hiding place. It was a bell jar, and they lived inside it with the instrument that would ring them out.

"So we don't type," Oscar said. "We work at night, when his receivers are quiet—"

"We work less. We work slower. We choose which names matter." Ilse pushed herself upright using the bench edge, and her hand slipped once, caught itself. Neither of them acknowledged it. "And you will keep taking those dispatches past his desk every week, wearing your wool face, until the thaw."

"And if he asks again about typing?"

"Then you will say two fingers, slowly, and you will mean it less every time, because soon it will be true." Something flickered across her face — not quite humor, not quite grief. "That's the trap of learning this trade, Oscar. You get better at lying by telling truths that stop being yours."

Below them, faintly, the receiver ticked. And then — both of them froze — beneath that, unmistakable, the clatter of keys: the major himself, typing, late into the evening, patient as snowfall.

Ilse tilted her head, listening the way a musician listens to a rival.

"That's an Adler," she murmured. "Model seven. The E sticks." And then, catching Oscar's expression: "Don't look at me like that. We are going to be living above this man until spring. I intend to know everything about him that he intends to know about us."

* * *

The days found their shape after that, and the shape was narrow.

Mornings: Oscar descended past the major's window with the weekly pouch held loosely, unimportantly, signing the register in their worsening counterfeit scrawl. The major always greeted them. Always courteously. Once he offered coffee, and Oscar drank it and remarked on the cold and mentioned — wool, only wool — that the chandler's shop smelled of tar and gave them headaches. The major wrote something down. Oscar never learned what.

Afternoons: the workshop, where Ilse drilled them on spacing and pressure while keeping her own hands wrapped in a shawl as though the cold were the culprit. Where they learned to lift the carriage without the squeak, to type in socks on the rug so the floorboards wouldn't carry the song downward.

Evenings: the listening. Both of them, without agreement, had developed the habit — heads bent over their separate tasks, ears tuned to the sounds rising through the joists. Footsteps crossing to the map. The scrape of the trestle table. The Adler's sticking E, sticking, sticking, faithful as a heartbeat.

On the ninth day, the footsteps stopped beneath them at a point they had never stopped before — directly under the workshop door — and remained there for the length of forty heartbeats. Then moved on.

Neither spoke until the boots had faded entirely.

"He knows the room isn't empty," Oscar whispered.

"Yes," Ilse said. Her hands, over the ledger, had gone perfectly still. "But knowing and proving are different countries, and I have spent eleven years forging passports between them." She drew breath, and it caught, and this time she could not disguise it. "Help me to bed. And Oscar — tomorrow, when you sign his register —"

She didn't finish. She didn't have to. Tomorrow the major would ask something new; he collected questions the way he collected machines, patiently, one shelf at a time.

What Oscar could not yet know — what would only arrive with the knocking, much later, at midnight — was that the major's collection had room in it for people too.
