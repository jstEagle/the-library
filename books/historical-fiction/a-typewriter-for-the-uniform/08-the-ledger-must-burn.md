## 8. The Ledger Must Burn

Ilse's fever came with the morning, the way the snow came — steadily, without malice, without any intention of stopping.

Oscar found her at half past six, still in her chair before the typewriter, one hand resting on the carriage as though she had only paused between words. Her breath made a thin whistling sound, and when Oscar touched her shoulder she was burning through the wool of her sweater. Her eyes opened halfway.

"The Wexler boy," she said. "His papers need the second stamp. The ink—"

"Later."

"There is no later." She tried to rise; her legs would not agree to it, and Oscar caught her under the arms and felt how little of her there was now, how the winter had eaten down to the frame. "There is never any later in this trade. There is only before they knock and after."

They got her to bed in the narrow room behind the workshop, among the smells of camphor and machine oil, and she fought them for every step of it — not out of fear, Oscar thought, but out of the simple outrage of a body that had been given orders all its life and did not know how to disobey them. At last she lay still, her gray hair spread on the pillow like frost on a windowpane, and her hands, those hands that could make a stranger into a citizen in forty minutes flat, lay open at her sides, trembling faintly even in sleep.

The family upstairs was quiet. Marta Wexler had heard everything through the floor — Ilse's weight against Oscar's arm, the scrape of the chair — and when Oscar climbed the ladder to the attic room she read the news in their face before a word was spoken. She said nothing. She only took little Josef by the hand and drew him close, and looked at Oscar with an expression that asked nothing and forgave everything, which was somehow worse than either.

Four lives. Oscar counted them again, the way one counts money in a dark room, by touch alone. Marta. Josef. Old Aaron Wexler, who coughed all night and apologized for it each time. And Ilse, downstairs, burning.

And beneath the floorboards, Major Reiner's machines ticked and hummed.

* * *

At eight o'clock sharp Oscar carried the dispatches down the stairs, wearing the face of nobody.

The signals office had transformed the bakery below. Flour sacks were stacked along one wall; the great brick oven stood cold, its iron door shut, as if the building itself had been told to hold its breath. Wires ran along the ceiling in neat black lines. Three operators sat at three tables with three confiscated typewriters — Oscar recognized the Erika from the stationer's shop, and the Adler that had belonged to the notary across the square — their keys clattering in unison like rain on a tin roof.

Major Reiner's door was open. It was always open. That was his method: he kept nothing hidden, because hiding was for people with something to lose.

"Fräulein Thornbury." He did not look up from the signal log. His pen moved in small, precise strokes. "You're punctual. I admire punctuality. In a town where nothing else arrives on schedule anymore." He signed the receipt for the dispatches and held it out, and only then raised his eyes. They were pale eyes, patient, the eyes of a man who had learned long ago that watching was more profitable than acting. "You look tired. The cold, I expect. Or the altitude. Vellacorte is hard on visitors."

"I'm used to it, Herr Major."

"No one is used to it." He smiled — a courteous, terrible smile. "That's what makes it useful. Sit, please. One moment." He rang a small bell, and a soldier brought coffee, real coffee, dark and smelling of another country entirely. He set the cup before Oscar like a man laying a card face up. "I have a proposal. Not official. Merely a conversation between two practical people stranded in an impractical town."

Oscar did not touch the cup.

"The bridge is closed," Reiner said. "It will be closed until April, perhaps May. Avalanches have taken out two spans above the gorge. The engineers say the ice must go first. Meanwhile the road south is a rumor and the passes are graves." He folded his hands. "But I am authorized to requisition transport. A sledge party goes over the high trail next week, weather permitting. Military business. It is dangerous, but it is possible, and possibility is a currency I deal in."

He let that sit in the warm air between them.

"You are a courier," he went on mildly. "A neutral courier, with documents in good order — I checked them myself, you'll be flattered to know, twice. Your contract ends when your dispatches are delivered. You are stranded here through no fault of your own, in a town that is, frankly, beneath you." He paused. "I can put you over the mountain. Alive, warm, delivered. All I ask in return is something you don't even own."

"And what is that?"

"A typewriter." He said it lightly, almost playfully. "An old machine, upstairs. And everything with it. Papers, records, whatever the previous tenant left behind. She is dying — everyone in this town knows it, they simply haven't said so to her face, a courtesy I'm prepared to extend — and dead women cannot own property. The material would pass to the Reich regardless. This way it passes cleanly, quietly, and you pass over the bridge of ice before anyone freezes." He leaned back. "Consider it house-clearing. Consider it mercy, if you like. I find that people can bear anything if they're permitted to call it mercy."

The operators' keys clattered on. Somewhere in the walls the wires hummed.

"You think she keeps lists," Oscar said.

"I know she keeps lists. Forgers always do. Sentimental creatures — they want proof they existed, proof they mattered. Baptismal dates, parish names, mothers' maiden names, all in her careful little hand or her careful little typeface." He picked up his pen again. "Every name on those pages is a name I can check. Most will be nothing. But somewhere in that ledger there will be one true thing, one thread, and I am very good with threads." He glanced up. "You've seen the ledgers. Don't trouble yourself denying it — you carried coal up those stairs four times this week, and no one carries coal four times in a week to a woman who is dying alone."

The room was very warm. Oscar realized their hands had gone cold anyway.

"You'd hang them," they said. "Every name. You'd hang them alphabetically."

"Hang is such a blunt word." He wrote something in the log. "Some will be questioned. Some will be released. The arithmetic is not mine to promise. I only offer the exchange: one machine and its paper, for one courier on a sledge. You have until Friday. After that the weather decides, and the weather has no conscience at all." He looked up once more, and for a moment — just a moment — something almost human moved behind the pale eyes. "You strike me as a person who has survived by keeping silent. I respect that. Silence is a discipline. But silence, Fräulein Thornbury, can also be a form of agreement. Think about which kind yours would be."

* * *

Oscar climbed the stairs slowly, one hand on the rail, the other pressing the receipt flat against their chest as though it might stop the heart from hammering loud enough to be heard below.

In the workshop the light was the color of dishwater. The typewriter sat where it always sat, on the table by the window, its mismatched keys gleaming dully — the battered old Remington whose typeface belonged to a machine that had been melted down years ago in some other town, some other life. Ilse's whole grammar of survival lived in that table. The ledgers lay beside it, wrapped in oilcloth, exactly where she'd shown Oscar weeks ago with the first lesson: *If I die mid-word, burn these first. Burn them before you grieve.*

Oscar unwrapped them now, and sat, and read.

Not all of them. No one could read all of them — eleven years of names, hundreds of names, each entry dated, each with its small notation in Ilse's shorthand: *paid in eggs*, *paid in silence*, *paid nothing, sister of a friend*, *do not trust the priest*, *good man, bad lungs*. A girl from Trieste who became a widow from Graz. A teacher who became a bookkeeper. Whole families folded into new identities like letters into envelopes, sealed, sent south, lost to history in the kindest possible way.

And there, dated three nights ago, in fresh entries: *Wexler, M., born 1899, Kovno — now Vogel, Marta, born 1902, Bregenz.* *Wexler, J., age 8.* *Wexler, A., age 61.* The ink still smelled faintly of Ilse's special mixture, vinegar and iron.

Everyone she had ever helped. Everyone who had ever trusted her. Every debt of gratitude and every secret, catalogued in one place, waiting — if the major got his wish — to become an index of arrests.

Oscar sat with the ledger open on their knees for a long time. The light failed by degrees. Downstairs the machines clattered; above, the family murmured in Yiddish over their supper; behind the wall, Ilse breathed her thin whistle of breath, and once called out in her sleep, a word in German, a word that sounded like *wait.*

Burn them. The instruction was clear enough. Burn them and the past died with the paper — no arrests, no threads, no index. But also: no record that any of it had happened. No proof that Ilse Brandt had existed and mattered. No names for the saved to someday find each other by. And the ledgers were the only map of her trade — the techniques, the mixtures, the stamps, the whole inheritance — that could be passed to whoever came after. Burn them and the work burned too.

Keep them, and every page was a rope with a neck in it.

And Reiner's voice, courteous as ever, hung in the cold air of the stairwell: *one machine and its paper, for one courier on a sledge.* Passage. Warmth. April instead of May. Survival, gift-wrapped, priced at everyone else's life. He had even offered the mercy of calling it mercy.

Oscar thought of ten years of practiced silence. Answer less than asked. Carry, deliver, vanish. Nobody's face, nobody's name, nobody's grave to answer for. It had worked. It had kept them alive through checkpoints and winters and the slow erosion of everything soft. And now the same silence, the same competence, the same nobody, was being offered a promotion: become somebody's informer, and live.

The stove in the corner had gone cold. Oscar fed it a stick of wood and watched the flame take, orange light crawling up the ledger's oilcloth, and understood suddenly, with terrible clarity, why Ilse had taught them the typewriter at all. Not because her hands were failing — though they were. Because someone had to be standing here, on this night, holding this choice, and she had known it would come, and she had chosen to spend her last weeks making sure the person holding it would not be a stranger.

The floorboard creaked. Ilse stood in the doorway wrapped in her blanket, swaying, fever-bright, one hand braced against the frame.

"You've been sitting in the dark," she said. "That's how they get you. The dark makes the choices look symmetrical." She crossed the room in three unsteady steps and looked down at the open ledger, and her face did not change at all. "He spoke to you today. Below. I heard the chairs."

"He offered me the bridge. For the machine and everything with it."

"Mm." She lowered herself into her chair, into the shape her body had worn into it over eleven years. "And what did you say?"

"Nothing yet. He gave me until Friday."

"Friday." She laughed — a dry rustle, quickly spent. "He thinks in deadlines. Soldiers do. Deadlines make men feel powerful over things they don't control." Her trembling hand came to rest on the ledger's cover, light as a bird landing. "Listen to me, courier. These names are not my property. I only borrowed them. Each one belongs to whoever is living under it now — the girl from Trieste, the teacher, the family upstairs. My job was never to keep the list. My job was to keep the *people*, and the list was only ever the shadow they cast." She looked up, and her eyes were clear despite the fever, clear and pitiless toward herself. "So the question isn't whether to burn it. Of course it burns. The question is what we do first. What we carry out of the fire. What the next forger needs, and what Reiner must never see."

Outside, the wind rose off the glacier and shook the shutters, and somewhere below a soldier laughed at something, and the machines went on clattering their endless indifferent rain.

"How long," Oscar asked quietly, "before he comes up those stairs?"

Ilse turned her head toward the window, toward the falling snow, and smiled.

"Sooner than Friday," she said.
