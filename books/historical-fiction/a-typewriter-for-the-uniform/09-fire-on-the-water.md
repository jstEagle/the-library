## 9. Fire on the Water

The oven had been lit since four that morning, because bread did not care what the century was doing.

Oscar came down the back stairs with the ledgers strapped against their chest like a child carried out of a burning house — which was, in every way that mattered, exactly what they were. Three volumes bound in grey board, swollen with use, their spines cracked into soft furrows where Ilse's hands had opened them ten thousand times. Every name she had ever made lived in those pages. Every name was a door, and behind every door a body breathing, and Oscar could feel them all pressing outward through the cardboard, warm as loaves.

Below the stairs, behind the partition wall Reiner's men had built in November, a wireless crackled its morning numbers. The major kept early hours. He liked to say that a signals post and a bakery were cousins — both of them in the business of turning raw material into something the state could digest.

Oscar stood at the oven door and let the heat press against their face until their eyes stung. The coals inside were the color of the inside of a mouth. Through the small iron window they could see the fire lying on its side like something sleeping, patient, indifferent to what it was asked to eat.

*Everything with it.* That was how Reiner had phrased it yesterday, standing in the doorway with snow melting on his shoulders, courteous as a man offering a seat on a train. *The typewriter and everything with it. In exchange, passage over the bridge. I can arrange that, even now. Even in February.*

He had not said *or else*. He had never once needed to say or else. The word lived in the pauses between his sentences, the way the river lived under the ice.

Oscar unstrapped the first ledger.

Their hands were steady. That surprised them most of all — after ten years of being nobody, of answering less than asked and never more, of folding themselves flat as a document between other documents, their hands should have been shaking. Instead they felt almost light, almost clean, the way one feels on the far side of a decision that cannot be taken back.

They opened the oven door. Heat rolled out and took the breath from them for a moment, and in that moment they heard, faintly, through two floors of old timber, the sound of Ilse's typewriter beginning above.

She was working. She should have been dead already, by any honest measure of her lungs, and instead she was working, each keystroke arriving through the floorboards like a slow heartbeat, like someone tapping a code through a wall: *I am here. I am here. I am still here.*

Oscar fed the first ledger to the fire.

It went reluctantly, the way all true things go. The grey cover charred brown, then black, then curled back from the pages beneath, and the pages themselves — onionskin, cheap, chosen by Ilse precisely because they burned well — caught in a rush of orange that lit the oven window like a lamp. Names bloomed and vanished. A baptismal record for a boy in Trieste. A marriage certificate for two women who had never legally existed together anywhere on earth. A passport for a man whose photograph showed him smiling, though he had been dead three years when the paper was made, because his widow needed the pension and the pension needed the husband.

Oscar watched each one go and spoke none of their names aloud, because speaking them would have taken too long, and there were two more books.

* * *

Above, in the workshop, Ilse Brandt sat at her desk with a blanket over her knees and the window open an inch, because she wanted to feel the cold while she worked. It kept her honest. Warmth made her drowsy, and drowsiness made mistakes, and there could be no mistakes in this last name.

The paper in the platen read: *MARGARETE VOSS, born 14 March 1901, Graz.*

Margarete Voss was the mother of the family hiding in the loft above the flour store. She had come to Ilse in October with her husband and two children and a face like a woman reading her own obituary, and Ilse had made her new papers over four nights, and Margarete had wept and kissed her hands, and Ilse had said, *don't thank me, thank the machine,* which was a joke, except that it wasn't.

Now the family would need to move tonight — Oscar had told her that morning, before dawn, in the dark of the stairwell, their voice low and stripped of everything but fact: *Reiner knows. Or he will know by tomorrow. The ledgers burn today; you go tonight, up the goat path to the ice caves, and you don't stop.* And Ilse had looked at this person she had taught to type, this courier who had arrived in her town as a blank page and was leaving as something written, and she had understood at once what the burning meant and what it cost, and she had said only: *Then let me finish Margarete's correction. The date is wrong. If the date is wrong, the whole document is wrong, and a wrong document is worse than none.*

Her hands trembled so badly now that she had to steady the right one with the left, guiding it like a parent guiding a child's pencil. The tremor she had hidden for a year — hidden from customers, hidden from soldiers, hidden from herself — had become impossible to hide, and she had stopped trying. There was no one left to hide it from who mattered.

The keys struck. *G-r-a-z.* The little hammer arms leapt and fell, leapt and fell, and the typeface sang its small metal music, and Ilse bent close to check each letter, her breath coming in shallow pulls, her lips moving with the letters like a girl learning to read.

Downstairs, the second ledger caught fire.

* * *

The alarm was Oscar's own design, and they were not proud of it, and they were proud of it completely.

A bakery chimney that flares suddenly black with the smoke of burning glue and binding-board, on a windless morning, in a town where the major's men had been bored since December — that was a bell that rang itself. Within minutes there would be shouting in the street, boots on the frozen cobbles, men sent running to see whether the bakery was aflame, whether the signals post was threatened, whether the major's precious confiscated typewriters were in danger. And while every eye and every boot ran toward the smoke, four people with false names would slip out the loft window onto the goat path, and the mountain would take them the way the mountain takes everything, silently, without paperwork.

Oscar fed the third ledger to the flames and stood watching it, and that was when the floor shuddered with boots — not many, not yet, just the baker's boy sent up to shout that the chimney was smoking black, that Herr Major wanted to know what was burning, and Oscar called down through the door, in the flat voice they had worn for ten years:

*Old ledgers. Nothing worth keeping.*

And heard, even as they said it, how the sentence had changed weight. For ten years those words had been armor. Now they were ash in their mouth, because nothing worth keeping was a lie, and Oscar Thornbury had decided, somewhere between the first ledger and the third, to stop lying about what mattered — even to a baker's boy, even to the empty air.

* * *

Major Reiner found them in the bakery yard, standing in the falling warmth of the chimney smoke like a figure in a bad painting.

He had come out in his greatcoat without a hat, and the cold had gone pink at his ears, and he looked up at the black column rising from the stack with an expression of genuine scholarly distress, the look of a collector watching a moth get into a drawer.

"The ledgers," he said. Not a question. He had seen the smoke and done the arithmetic; he was, whatever else he was, never slow. "I offered you a bridge, and you gave me a bonfire."

Oscar said nothing. The old reflex rose in them like water filling a lung — say less than asked, never more — and they let it rise, and they let it pass through them, and they did not obey it.

"You understand what you've done," Reiner continued, softly, stepping closer. His breath smoked. Somewhere behind him, his men were still clustered at the front of the building, staring up at the chimney, waiting for orders that he had not yet given because he was curious, incurably curious, about this courier who had sat across from him all winter wearing silence like a uniform. "Those pages were records. Records are history. You've burned history."

"No," Oscar said.

The word came out of them strange and enormous, their own true voice, unused at full volume for so long that it sounded like a stranger's. Reiner's head tilted slightly, the way a bird tilts.

"I burned evidence," Oscar said. "The history is in people. Four people walked out of this building twenty minutes ago, and you didn't see them leave, because your men were watching a chimney." They heard their voice steady itself, gather, grow. "My name is Oscar Thornbury. I carried dispatches for the republic. I learned the trade from Ilse Brandt, upstairs, and every name in those books was a living person, and I knew some of them, and I will remember all of them, and you—" the word cracked, and they let it crack— "you will write down nothing about any of them, because there is nothing left to write."

The yard was very quiet. The smoke rose straight up into the white sky. Somewhere above them, faintly, impossibly, the typewriter was still going — Ilse, finishing, always finishing — and both of them heard it, and both of them knew what it meant, and neither of them said it.

Reiner studied them for a long moment. Then he took off his glove, finger by finger, and held out his bare hand toward the heat of the chimney, as if warming himself at a hearth.

"A courier," he said at last, "who has decided to be a witness. Do you know what happens to witnesses?"

"They're remembered," Oscar said. "That's the whole point of them."

For a moment — one moment, no longer — something moved behind the major's eyes that was not anger. It might have been envy. It might have been grief for a version of himself that had died quietly, decades ago, in some other winter, in some other town with a river in it.

Then his hand dropped, and the moment closed like water over a stone, and he turned toward his men and began giving orders in a voice like a filing cabinet, and Oscar turned and went back inside, up the stairs, taking them two at a time, because the typing had stopped.

The workshop was warm and smelled of ink and coal oil. Ilse sat at her desk with her head tipped back against the chair, her eyes half open, her trembling hands folded in her lap like finished work. On the platen, the page stood finished to its last letter: *MARGARETE VOSS, born 14 March 1901, Graz.* The date was correct. The document was perfect.

Oscar knelt beside her and took her cold hands, and she smiled without opening her eyes, and said, in a voice like the last of the fire settling:

"Read it back to me. I want to hear it in your voice. I want to know it will keep."

And outside, far off across the valley, the ice on the river groaned — a long, deep, cracking sound, like something vast beginning, at last, to let go.
