## 10. One Unfinished Machine

The ice let go on a Tuesday.

Oscar heard it from the workshop window before dawn — not the groaning of the last weeks, which had sounded like grief, but a clean report, like a rifle fired under the river's skin, and then another, and then a long rushing exhale as the water beneath found its voice again. By noon the river ran brown and muscular through the gorge, carrying whole trees southward, branches raised like arms. The bridge at Vellacorte stood above it, dripping, its stone piers scoured white to the waterline, and by evening there were men on it with shovels and lanterns, clearing the season's snow from the planks, calling to one another in voices that had forgotten they could carry.

Spring came the way Ilse had said it would: all at once, without apology. Water ran in the gutters. The bakery below smelled of yeast again instead of ash. And in the workshop, where Oscar had lived for five months among ledgers and ribbons and the ghost of a dying woman's hands, the light through the north window had turned from grey to gold, and lay across the empty desk like something left deliberately behind.

Oscar packed the way she had taught them. Nothing hurried; nothing hidden unless hiding was the point. The dispatches went first, sewn back into a new coat lining with stitches so fine they might have been factory work — she would have approved of that, would have held the seam up to the lamp and said *good, good, no one looks for what looks like nothing*. The false bottom of her trunk gave up three blank passports already stamped with seals that did not exist, each one perfect down to the watermark, each one waiting for a name that had not yet been chosen for it. These Oscar wrapped in oilcloth and buried, after long thought, in the flour bin of the bakery below, between two sacks marked with the miller's ordinary blue chalk.

Not destroyed. Not carried either. Left where the next pair of hands would find them — because there would be a next pair of hands. There were always hands reaching toward Vellacorte from the wrong side of some border or other, and now there was a room above a bakery with a north-facing window and a stove that heated evenly, and someone, someday, would climb those stairs the way Oscar once had, seeking warmth, and find the trade waiting like an inheritance nobody had willed.

The ledgers were gone. That debt was paid, and the interest had been Ilse's life, and Oscar did not regret it — that was the strange thing, the thing they turned over night after night like a coin worn smooth. They did not regret it. Somewhere beyond the mountains, people whose names existed nowhere now except in memory and flesh were still breathing, still eating breakfast, still arguing over small things, and Major Reiner sat in his requisitioned office in the valley town with a collection of confiscated typewriters and no ledger, no list, no thread to pull. He had searched the ashes of the bakery oven himself. Oscar had watched him do it, standing in the doorway with snow melting off their boots, and Reiner had looked up once, and whatever he had seen in their face had made him close his little brass magnifying glass and say only: "You are leaving soon, I think."

"I am."

"The bridge opens this week." He had dusted his fingers on his handkerchief, fastidiously. "I will not require your papers. I find I know your name already — or enough of one." A pause, thin as paper. "Travel safely, courier."

It was the closest he ever came to admitting what he knew, and the closest Oscar ever came to answering. Some silences, Ilse had taught them in the end, are not surrender. Some are a wall you build around the living.

* * *

On the last morning, Oscar did the thing they had been avoiding for eleven days.

They sat down at the typewriter.

It stood on the worktable where she had left it — battered, iron-ribbed, its keys worn to silver ovals, its typeface still stubbornly mismatched, an *e* that printed high and an *r* that slanted like a man leaning out of wind. Her machine. The one whose flaws had made every document that came off it traceable to this room and therefore worthless for forgery and priceless for truth, though it had taken Oscar most of the winter to understand the difference. She had used it for letters. For practice. For the names, sometimes, when a document needed to feel human rather than official — because machines, she said, have accents too, and the accent of this one was honesty.

Oscar rolled in a single sheet of good paper. Their hands, which had learned the keys through a winter of drills — *the quick brown fox*, over and over, until the tremor in their own fingers stilled — hovered and then began.

*Vellacorte. Spring.*

The words came slowly, two-fingered, imperfect. They wrote about the bridge opening. About the river running brown and loud. About a workshop above a bakery where papers could be made for those who needed them, and a flour bin, and a north-facing window with good light, and a stove that heated evenly. They did not write her name. Names, on paper, were dangerous; that lesson they kept. But they wrote: *She was ill and she worked anyway. She taught me everything I know about lying, which is to say, everything I know about mercy.* And then, because it seemed important that whoever came next understand the terms of the inheritance:

*The price is silence in public and speech in private. You will learn to tell these apart. When you cannot bear it, type. This machine does not lie well, and neither, eventually, will you.*

They signed it with nothing. They left it in the typewriter, sheet seated straight, carriage returned, exactly as she would have left a finished page — and beside the machine they set the small tin box with her spare ribbons, her good penknife, and the brush she had cleaned the type-slugs with every Sunday. Then they took the machine itself.

That had not been a decision so much as a slow capitulation, argued over for days. It was heavy. It was identifiable. It was, in any rational accounting, a liability strapped to the back of a courier crossing a frontier. But she had died with her hands on it, mid-word — the last name unfinished, the carriage stopped between letters — and Oscar had understood finally that the machine was not a tool. It was a witness. Witnesses should be carried out of burning buildings.

It fit into the old courier's frame-pack with the dispatches, iron against spine, and when Oscar stood and shrugged the weight on, it settled there as if it had always meant to be carried.

* * *

The bridge at midday was a line of carts and mules and returning families, and the soldiers at the southern end checked papers with the bored efficiency of men watching winter die behind them. Oscar joined the queue with their collar up and their heart doing its old work — steady, steady, the metronome ten years of silence had taught it — and when the soldier reached them, took the proffered passport, and glanced from the photograph to the face and back, the moment stretched the way such moments do, elastic and endless.

"Purpose of travel?"

"Courier," Oscar said. "Trade goods and correspondence."

The soldier stamped the page. The queue moved. The bridge planks rang hollow underfoot, and below them the river tore past with the trees in its arms, going south, going anywhere, and Oscar walked with the typewriter pressing its cold ribs into their back and did not look back at the town. Looking back was for people who could afford to be remembered.

The pass road climbed. The avalanches of December had long since been cut away, leaving walls of dirty snow taller than a cart, and the air smelled of stone and running water and, faintly, impossibly, of pine sap beginning to move. Twice Oscar stopped to shift the pack's weight, and twice, standing in that enormous quiet, they spoke aloud — just a word or two, testing — *"Ilse,"* said once to the mountains, and the mountains kept it, and it cost nothing and it gave everything, and they walked on lighter.

* * *

The border town beyond the pass was called Steinbach, and it was small and wet and smelled of coal smoke, and the clerk who processed travelers sat in a wooden kiosk at the rail depot behind a window of cloudy glass. He was an old man with ink on his fingers and spectacles pushed up into thin hair, and he had the manner of someone who had asked the same questions for forty years and expected the same evasions.

"Name?" he said, pencil ready, not looking up.

And here it was. The moment the whole winter had bent toward. Ten years of aliases lay stacked behind Oscar's teeth like firewood — Thornbury, Marchand, the name on the passport in their pocket, the name sewn into the lining, the name they'd worn at checkpoints and in queues and across three borders and one frozen river. Each one had saved their life. Each one had cost a piece of the person wearing it. The clerk waited. The pencil waited. Outside, the train breathed steam into the cold like something waking.

Oscar set the pack down. Felt the typewriter's weight come to rest on the platform stones.

Their true name — the one from before, the family name, spoken last in a kitchen that no longer existed, written last in a hand that had trembled with fear instead of age — rose in them like water finding a crack. They let it rise. They let it out.

They said it aloud, clear, in a voice that carried past the glass, past the kiosk, into the ordinary afternoon.

The clerk looked up for the first time. He blinked once, wrote the name down letter by letter, sounding it out under his breath, and said, "Purpose of visit?"

"Passing through," Oscar said. "Carrying a friend's work home."

The clerk stamped the register. The train doors opened. And Oscar Thornbury — the whole name, the real one, the first and the last — lifted the typewriter onto their shoulder and stepped aboard, while somewhere behind them, high over the pass, the snow on the peaks held the day's light like a page holding fresh ink, waiting for whatever name would be written next.
