## 1. The Clock That Lost a Minute

The summons came on a Tuesday, which I took as a good omen. Tuesdays are honest days — nothing expected of them, nothing owed. The note was delivered by a boy who couldn't have been more than twelve, who looked at me the way people look at clocks: as though I might be useful but probably weren't.

*Maison Verhoeven & Fils* had no fils. It had old Verhoeven, dead three years and still listed on the shopfront because changing letters costs money, and it had me, Theo — just Theo, one syllable, easy to say, easy to forget. The customers called me *der Junge*. The boy from the shop. It fit me like a coat cut for someone else, which is to say: it kept off the rain, and nobody asked why I wore it.

The note said the observatory's master clock had lost a minute. Not stopped — losing. A minute a day, drifting, and in a city where armistice negotiators were gathering like crows on a fence, a minute was not a small thing. A minute could move a signing. A minute could move a war. They wanted it fixed before Thursday, quietly, by someone discreet.

I read *discreet* twice. In my experience, when a man of importance writes *discreet*, he means: *send us someone whose name we will not need to remember.*

So I packed my tools and went up the hill to the observatory, and I flattened my vowels the way you flatten dough — with pressure, with patience, until every rough edge of Lagos was rolled thin enough to pass for somewhere else. Somewhere neutral. Somewhere that didn't make gendarmes look twice at papers. I had been doing this so long that the accent came off my tongue like a glove; the trouble was remembering there was still a hand underneath.

* * *

The observatory smelled of brass and cold stone and, faintly, of men who had been drinking coffee since dawn and would drink it until midnight. The master clock lived in its own chamber, a great regulator with a pendulum nine feet long, hung in a case like a cathedral window. It was beautiful the way honest things are beautiful — no ornament, all function, every tooth of every wheel cut to do exactly one job forever.

It was also lying about the time.

I opened the case and put my ear to the movement first. You learn to listen before you touch; a clock tells you what hurts if you let it speak. There it was — a hesitation on the fourth beat of the escape wheel, small as a held breath. Something catching. Something worn.

"Lost a minute," said the caretaker, hovering behind me, a nervous man with ink-stained cuffs. "A minute a day. The delegation uses this time, you understand. Everything is scheduled against it."

I understood. I said only, "Yes, sir," in the flattest voice I owned, and knelt down among the gears.

Here is what I know about invisibility, having practiced it professionally since I was fourteen: it is work. People think hiding is idleness, standing still while the world passes over you. But it is the hardest labor there is. Every sentence is measured for accent before it leaves your mouth. Every glance is calibrated — long enough to seem polite, short enough to invite nothing. You become a clock yourself, wound tight, keeping perfect time, showing only the face you've been given.

I worked. The fault turned out to be an impulse jewel worn at its edge, letting the pendulum lose a sliver of arc on each swing — invisible unless you knew to look, accumulating into theft over days. A tiny dishonesty, compounding. I have known men built the same way.

While I worked, I became aware that I was being watched.

Not by the caretaker, who had retreated to his desk to worry at papers. From the public gallery above — a railed walkway where visitors could look down at the great pendulum swinging in its well. A man stood there, alone, in a coat too heavy for the season, holding a leather satchel against his chest the way you'd hold a child, or a wound.

He was gaunt the way bread is stale — hollowed out, the shape still there but the substance gone somewhere else. He didn't fidget, didn't shift his weight, didn't do any of the things bored visitors do. He stood perfectly still and watched my hands move among the gears with an attention that made the back of my neck prickle.

I gave him nothing. Eyes down, tools steady, the Junge absorbed in his work. But some part of me — the part that never quite sleeps, the part that reads a room the way other men read weather — kept him in view the way you keep a loose dog in view.

He stayed twenty minutes. Then thirty. The coffee smell faded; the caretaker left; the light through the tall windows went from white to amber. And still he watched.

* * *

Near five o'clock, I finished the adjustment and set the clock to be tested against my own regulator. We waited together, the man above and I below, for the hour.

At five, the master clock struck — and stuttered.

Only once. Only slightly. A hitch in the strike train, the bell catching between the second stroke and the third, a half-second of silence where silence should not be. To anyone else it would be nothing, a flaw so small it barely registered. I noted it for later correction.

But the man in the gallery flinched.

Not a startle — I want to be precise about this, because the difference matters. A startle is surprise. This was older than surprise. His whole body locked, shoulders up, chin tucked, satchel crushed to his ribs, and for one full second he was somewhere else entirely — somewhere with mud and noise and things falling from the sky — and then he hauled himself back into the observatory, back into his coat, back into stillness, and pretended, with magnificent discipline, that nothing had happened.

I know that performance. I have given it myself, in smaller rooms, for smaller audiences. The art of being unremarkable while your blood screams. Watching him, something cracked in me — some seam I'd stitched years ago and never examined. Because I recognized him, you see. Not his face; I'd never seen his face before that afternoon. I recognized the work. The daily, grinding, meticulous work of pretending not to tremble.

And here was the thing that troubled me most: he was watching *me*. Not the clock. Me. With the particular hunger of a man looking for something specific in a stranger — courage, maybe, or weakness, or just another person who understood what it cost to stand still.

The bell settled. Five strokes, complete. The clock resumed its lie, one minute adrift, and I climbed down from the movement and began packing my tools slowly, giving him time, giving myself time, not knowing time for what.

When I finally looked up at the gallery, he was gone from his place — and then he wasn't gone at all but coming around the stairwell toward me, coat dragging, satchel clutched tighter now, his eyes fixed on mine with the desperate directness of a drowning man choosing whom to grab.

"You're the clockmaker," he said. His voice was low, scraped thin, accented from somewhere east of everywhere. "They told me — they said a clockmaker comes Thursdays. I waited two days." He glanced at the empty chamber, the closed door, the caretaker's distant pen scratching. Then, closer, almost a whisper: "I need to show you something. It's about a dead man. He writes to me every month."

My hands, still packing tools, went very quiet inside their gloves of habit.

I should have said no. Any sensible man says no. Sensible men survive wars by knowing which doors to leave shut, and I had spent four years becoming the most sensible man in any room — the Junge, der Junge, just Theo, invisible, intact.

But he was opening the satchel already, and underneath the oilcloth I could see paper. Letters. Bundles of them, tied with string, soft-worn at the edges from handling, from reading, from being carried against the chest of a man who flinched at bells.

And my hands — traitorous, honest hands that had spent their life fixing things — reached toward the bundle before my head had cast a single vote.
