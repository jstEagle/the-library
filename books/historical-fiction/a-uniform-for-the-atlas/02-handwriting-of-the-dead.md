## 2. Handwriting of the Dead

A clockmaker learns to read people the way he reads escapements — by the small irregularities, the hesitation between beats. The gaunt man in the gallery had been ticking wrong since I arrived.

I told myself it was none of my business. My business was the master clock, its escapement worn to a whisper, its pendulum losing a minute a day as though even time itself had grown tired of this city and wanted to slip away quietly before anyone noticed. I worked with my head down, the way my father taught me, the way a man survives in a place where his name is a question and his accent an answer he'd rather not give.

But the man came down the gallery stairs anyway, boots careful on each step, like a soldier who has learned that floors talk. He waited until I had the movement half-disassembled on the bench, gears laid out in their anatomical order, before he spoke.

"You fix it?"

"I'm fixing it." I did not look up. Looking up invites conversation; conversation invites questions; questions invite a man to say *Theo* and hear *Theodore?* and then the whole careful architecture of my days comes down like a dropped movement.

"It struck eleven twice," he said. "Last night. Eleven, then again, like it changed its mind."

"The striking train slips when the mainspring runs low. It's not haunted."

"Nothing here is haunted," he said, "because nothing here is allowed to exist."

That got me. Not the words themselves — border cities breed men who talk like pamphlets — but the way he said them, flat, exhausted, a man who had stopped expecting to be believed. I looked up after all.

He was younger than his posture suggested. Thirty, perhaps, with the kind of thinness that comes from rationing rather than fashion, and eyes that kept moving to the door behind him every few seconds, counting it, reassuring himself it was still there. He held a leather satchel against his chest the way other men hold children or Bibles.

"My name is Emil," he said. Then, as if remembering the rules of this country: "I'm nobody. A clerk. I have business with no one."

"In this city," I said, "that's the most convincing introduction I've heard all week."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. The memory of one, kept in storage.

"I saw you watching me yesterday," he said. "In the gallery. You looked away too fast. People who are hiding something always look away too fast." He said it without accusation, almost with tenderness, the way you'd point out a shared limp. "You're not from here either."

I turned back to the clock. "I'm from here."

"No," he said gently. "You're *from* somewhere else, and you're *here*, and those are different things. I know the difference. I've been both."

The pendulum ticked behind us, patient, indifferent. I wiped my hands on the rag though they were already clean, because hands need something to do when the truth gets close.

"What do you want?" I asked.

Instead of answering, he opened the satchel.

* * *

There were nine letters. He laid them out on my workbench among the gear wheels and the screwdrivers, and I want to say I understood immediately what they were, but the truth is slower and stranger than that. First I noticed only the paper — good paper, army-issue, the kind that isn't supposed to leave the camps. Then the handwriting, which was what undid me.

It was beautiful. Round, confident, the letters of a schoolmaster's son, each *J* looped like a drawn breath. And across the top of the first page, a date: three weeks ago.

"Jakob Brandt," Emil said. "My brother. Second Infantry. Reported killed at Verdun." His voice did something complicated on the word *killed*, splitting into two registers, grief and fury braided together so tightly I couldn't tell which was holding up which. "Two years ago. We buried an empty box. My mother sewed his name into her coat lining so she could carry him around."

"And these—"

"Came by way of a nurse, who got them from a chaplain, who will deny everything if asked, which is why I am telling no one but you." He tapped the page. "Read it. Read where he says he is."

I read.

*They tell us the war is ending and that we should be grateful, and we are, God knows, but gratitude is cold comfort when your own government has written you off as mud. There is a camp here they call Lager Nord, though it has no name on any map, and there are four hundred of us whom the lists have eaten. When the delegations come to sign their peace, they will sign over our heads and call it clean. Tell them. Tell anyone who still has ears. I was Jakob Brandt and I am Jakob Brandt still, whatever the ink says.*

The observatory was very quiet. Even the pendulum seemed to be listening.

"There is no such camp," I said. "The negotiators published a list. Every facility accounted for. I've seen it posted outside the delegation offices."

"Yes," Emil said. "I wrote the fair copy of it myself."

I looked at him then, really looked, and understood the shape of the thing: a clerk, a nobody, a man whose job was to make lies legible, who had spent months copying official falsehoods in his own neat hand until his brother's handwriting arrived like a stone through a window.

"They'll erase him," he said. "When the armistice is signed, the record closes. Whatever is on the paper becomes true forever. Four hundred men become four hundred absences, and in fifty years some historian will write *no irregular detentions were recorded*, and it will be *true*, because the paper says so." His hand flattened on the letters, protective. "Paper is how they kill people the second time."

I knew about names on paper. I knew about a clerk in Lagos writing *Okafor* as *Ochaffour* on a shipping manifest, and a mission schoolteacher who decided *Theo* needed a *dore* appended for respectability, and an immigration officer in this very city squinting at me and writing down whatever suited his pen that morning. I had spent twenty-six years being miswritten, and I had learned to live inside the errors the way a snail lives in its shell — cramped, but mine, and better than exposure.

But Jakob Brandt was not living inside an error. Jakob Brandt had been deleted. There is a difference between a name spelled wrong and a name struck through, and I felt that difference land in my chest like a dropped weight.

"Why me?" I asked. "Why bring this to a clockmaker?"

"Because you're invisible," Emil said simply. "Because you carry tools through checkpoints every week and no one looks at you. Because you're not one of theirs and not one of ours and that makes you the only free agent in this building." He paused. "And because when you looked at those letters just now, you didn't ask if they were forged. You asked why I chose you. Forgers get questioned first. Believers ask second."

He had me there. I hadn't doubted the handwriting for a moment. Some instinct older than reason recognized it — the hand of a man insisting on himself against all evidence.

"If I'm caught," I said, "carrying correspondence out of a camp the government denies exists—"

"You won't be caught."

"Everyone says that."

"Everyone believes it, too," Emil said. "That's the tragedy of everyone."

The pendulum swung. Somewhere below us, the city went about its business, negotiators arriving in black cars, gendarmes checking papers at the corners, the great machinery of peace grinding toward Thursday's signing. Three days. I thought of the striking train slipping, eleven striking twice, a clock that couldn't keep its own story straight. I thought of my mother, whose name no document in this hemisphere had ever rendered correctly, and how she used to say that a person's name is the one possession that can't be confiscated — only surrendered.

Emil was watching me with those door-counting eyes, and I understood that he had already decided about me, had decided before he came down the stairs, and that my consent was a formality he was enduring out of politeness.

And here is the thing I am not proud of and will never unsay: my hands agreed before I did. While my head was still listing dangers like items on an invoice, my hands had already gathered the letters off the bench, already folded them flat, already reached for the thin brass sheet I use to line carriage clocks — measuring the pages against it with a craftsman's eye, seeing exactly how they would lie against metal, wrapped in oilcloth, sewn into the belly of a disassembled movement where no gendarme pats a clockmaker's pockets because clockmakers are furniture, furniture with legs.

"Thursday," I said. "The signing. Everything will be watched."

"Everything except you," Emil said.

I took the letters. All nine. They weighed almost nothing. They weighed more than the master clock entire.

"I'll need until tomorrow night to build the housing," I said. "Where do I find you?"

"You don't," he said. "I find you. Safer that way." He closed the satchel, and for one moment his composure slipped and I saw underneath it a boy from a small town who had lost his brother twice — once to the war, once to the paperwork — and could not bear a third loss. "Thank you," he said. "What do I call you? Your real name, I mean. Not the one on your work permit."

No one had asked me that in years. The question went through me like a key through a lock I'd rusted shut.

"Theo," I said. "Just Theo. For now."

"For now," he repeated, and almost smiled for real this time. "That's more hope than most of us can afford."

He left the way he'd come, quiet on the stairs, and I stood alone in the dome room with nine dead men's letters hidden in my toolbox and the pendulum swinging its steady count toward Thursday.

Then, from the gallery above, a sound: the door at the top of the stairs, easing shut a half-second too slowly. Someone had been listening.

And across the city, faint as a heartbeat, the observatory bell began to strike twelve — and stumbled, and struck thirteen.
