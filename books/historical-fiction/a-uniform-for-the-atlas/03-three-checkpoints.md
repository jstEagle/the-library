## 3. Three Checkpoints

The tick of the master clock followed me home that night, or perhaps I carried it. Either way, I lay in my room above Herr Brandt's shop with the letters sewn into my coat lining and counted seconds until sleep gave up on me entirely.

*Tick.* Thursday, nine o'clock, the signing.

*Tick.* Three checkpoints between me and it.

I had done the sewing myself, which was its own small confession. An apprentice clockmaker learns to work thread through brass and canvas with a patience that would shame a surgeon, and by lamplight I had unpicked the hem of my tool roll and laid the letters flat against the clock parts they would travel with — nine sheets of paper pressed between a mainspring and its housing, invisible to any hand that did not already know to look. Emil had wept watching me do it, quietly, the way men weep when they have run out of louder options.

"They'll weigh your toolbox," he'd said. "They weigh everything."

"Then let them," I said, with more courage than I felt. "Clock parts are heavy. That's their whole character."

* * *

The first checkpoint was at the river bridge, Wednesday morning, and it belonged to the gendarmes — grey coats, grey faces, grey coffee steaming from a cart like the city itself was exhaling.

*Tick.*

I joined the queue of tradesmen with my toolbox swinging from my right hand and my face arranged into the expression I had spent eleven years perfecting: pleasant, vacant, slightly bored. The face of a man whose thoughts never rise above gears and wages. It is remarkable what a man can hide behind boredom. It is the great camouflage of the century.

The gendarme waved forward a fishmonger, then a woman with a pram he inspected with more interest than I liked — because if he opened every basket, mine would not survive the arithmetic of his suspicion.

*Tick.*

"You." He pointed at me with two fingers. "Papers."

I handed them over. Théodore Renard, horloger's apprentice, born — so the papers said — in Besançon, which was a detail I had paid a forger three weeks' wages to invent because Besançon makes clocks and nobody questions a clock town's sons. My accent sat where I had trained it, flattened and local, a bird that had learned to sing only one song.

"Purpose?"

"Repair. Observatory. The master clock." I let a craftsman's grievance creep into my voice. "It lost a minute last month. You'd think the end of the world."

Something flickered behind his eyes — the reflexive sympathy of one man for another's employer troubles — and he gestured me through with a jerk of his chin. The toolbox passed under his gaze unexamined, heavy and dull and utterly beneath notice.

*Tick.* One down. My hands did not shake until I was twenty paces past the bridge, and then they shook beautifully, and I let them, alone, where no one could charge them with anything.

* * *

The second checkpoint was worse precisely because it wore no uniform. The delegation had established an office in the Hôtel Métropole's east wing, and the corridor outside the signing hall had been given over to clerks — pale, precise men with ink-stained fingers who examined every delivery against a manifest as though the fate of Europe depended on the correct quantity of stationery.

Which, in fairness, it might have.

I had engineered my own invitation, which is to say I had written a letter to the delegation's secretariat complaining that the hall's regulator clock ran slow and offering my services gratis, in the name of civic pride. Civic pride, I have found, opens doors that money cannot. A clerk named Vogel — young, bespectacled, ambitious in the way of men who alphabetize things voluntarily — met me in the corridor.

"Theo Renard?" He checked his list. "You're expected. But I must inspect the tools."

"Of course." I set the toolbox on his desk and opened it, and here is the truth about fear: it sharpens you. I lifted each piece out with a craftsman's unhurried hands — the mainspring last, cradled in its housing with the letters sewn flat against the canvas wrap — and narrated as I went, because a talking man seems like a man with nothing to hide. "Escapement. Balance wheel. This is the pendulum suspension — you can see the wear, here, this is why she loses time—"

"Yes, yes." Vogel was already looking past me, at the manifest, at the next crisis of stationery. "Fine. Go up. Don't touch anything but the clock."

"I never touch anything but clocks," I said, and meant it more deeply than he could know.

*Tick.*

Upstairs, in the signing hall itself — empty now except for the long green table and the flags waiting to be unfurled like stage curtains — I installed the mainspring in the regulator's housing, letters and all, where they would sit in plain sight among the machinery of the room where the record would be sealed. Then I took them out again. Because a plan made at midnight in terror is not the same plan you trust at noon in daylight, and I decided, standing in that hushed hall with dust motes turning in the light like slow snow, that the letters should go into a human hand before the ink dried on history. Machinery can be dismantled. Paper can be confiscated. A man who has read something cannot unread it.

I re-sewed them into my coat instead. And I went looking for the junior delegate whose name Emil had whispered to me like a prayer: Moreau. Young. Honest. Unimportant enough to be ignored, which in diplomacy is the same as being safe.

*Tick.*

* * *

The third checkpoint was my own front door, in a sense — the observatory guard, Thursday evening, posted at the foot of the tower stairs because the delegations were arriving within the hour and nothing moved in that building without a soldier counting it.

And there, across the observatory square, I saw Emil.

He was supposed to be gone. We had agreed — agreed firmly, with the full weight of my cowardice behind the agreement — that he would stay away, that if anything went wrong he would be nowhere near me. But there he stood by the iron fence, gaunt and hatless in the cold, watching the tower with the fixed devotion of a man watching a grave that might still breathe.

A guard noticed him. Of course a guard noticed him; a man who stands still in a moving crowd is a signal fire. Two soldiers detached from the checkpoint and started toward him, and Emil saw them coming, and Emil looked directly at me.

*Tick.*

I understood then, with a clarity that felt like cold water poured down the spine, what he intended to do. There was no time to signal him, no time to be noble on his behalf. He had been a prisoner for two years; he knew exactly how much attention a body could buy.

"Here!" Emil shouted, loud enough to crack the evening air, and he ran — not away, but at the guards, arms wide, shouting something in German that I couldn't parse and didn't need to. Every eye in the square swung toward him. Every rifle. The guard at the tower stairs turned, pulled by the gravity of the commotion, and for four full seconds the stairwell stood unwatched.

Four seconds. I have repaired escapements that measure finer.

I went up those stairs with my heart hammering out the clock's rhythm for it — *tick, tick, tick* — past the dome room, along the gallery, to the narrow window above the square where I stopped, because I am not made of stone, and looked down.

They had Emil against the fence. He wasn't struggling anymore. He was smiling — a terrible, cracked, triumphant smile — and his eyes came up, searching the windows, and found mine, and he nodded once, small and certain, a man confirming a delivery.

Then they took him around the corner, out of sight, and the square resumed its business of endings.

*Tick.*

I want to say I stood there and grieved properly. I didn't. Grief is a luxury with a schedule attached, and somewhere below me, cars were arriving, delegates stepping out into the cold with their documents and their reputations, and inside my coat lining, against my ribs, nine letters from a dead man waited to be delivered.

Terror is a strange fuel. It burns clean, once you commit to it. I buttoned my coat over the paper hearts of nine unwritten men, descended the stairs past the returning guard — who glanced at me, saw only a tradesman, and looked away — and walked, quickly, steadily, toward the signing hall, where history was about to put on its best handwriting.

And I found, to my astonishment, that I was no longer pretending to belong there.
