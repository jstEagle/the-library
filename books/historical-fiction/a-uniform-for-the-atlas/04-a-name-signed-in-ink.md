## 4. A Name Signed in Ink

The hall smelled of beeswax and fear, which is to say it smelled of diplomacy. I had entered it carrying a toolbox, because no one in the history of checkpoints has ever stopped a man with a toolbox. They look at the leather case and see a profession. They do not see a satchel's worth of resurrection sewn into the lining between an escapement and a spare mainspring.

I had learned that trick from my father, who used to say that the best hiding place in the world is inside something people believe they understand.

The delegates were assembling along the long table like chess pieces being arranged by a hand that had already decided the game. Junior men at the ends, senior men toward the middle, and at the center — I will not name him; the record names him enough — the negotiator whose reputation required that Jakob Brandt remain dead. He was laughing at something. That is the detail I keep returning to. Two years of a brother erased, a camp denied, a mother presumably still setting one fewer place at the table, and this man laughed, and the room laughed with him, because rooms always do.

My hands were steady. I want to report that they were not, that they trembled like Emil's had when the clock struck, but no — my hands were steady, and this frightened me more than trembling would have. Steady hands meant some part of me had already accepted what was about to happen and priced it accordingly.

*Tick,* said the observatory clock above us, its voice carried down through the floor. It had lost only seconds now. I had given it back most of its minutes that morning. There is a lesson in that, if you want one: even a thing declared broken can be wound true again, provided someone is willing to open the case.

*Tick.*

The junior delegate sat third from the end. I had studied him through two afternoons of pretending to service wall regulators in the antechambers — a thin young man with ink-stained cuffs who took notes nobody senior ever read. The unread are useful. I have been unread all my life.

I crossed the hall under the honest pretext of checking a timepiece mounted near the delegation's table, knelt, opened my toolbox, and while ostensibly doing nothing at all to the clock, slipped the bundle of letters beneath the folder of papers at the young man's elbow. Sewn canvas, clock-oil stains, unremarkable. Then I stood, and made the mistake of hesitating half a second too long, because a man who has spent years practicing invisibility knows exactly how long he can stand in a room before the room begins to notice him, and I had exceeded my allowance by precisely that half second.

"You there." A voice from the table's center. Not the negotiator — an aide, gray-faced, important in the way of men whose importance depends on other people's secrets staying folded. "What were you doing?"

"Servicing the chronometer, sir."

"I didn't ask for servicing."

"No one ever does," I said, "until the clock has already failed them."

It was, I admit, a flippant answer for a man holding his life together with clock oil. But something had come loose in me on the stairs, where Emil had shouted his own name into three rifles so that the shouting would cover the sound of my running feet. Once a man gives away his name like that — spends it, the way you'd spend the last coin in your pocket — it seems cowardly to hoard yours.

The aide rose. The young delegate, meanwhile, had looked down, had seen the corner of canvas where it protruded past his folder, had touched it, had gone very still. He knew handwriting when he saw it. Clerks always know handwriting; it is their whole religion.

"What's your name?" the aide demanded. And here is the thing I had rehearsed a hundred times in a hundred cities: the false name, the flattened vowels, *Theo Marchand, horologist, local born*, the whole costume I wore so long I had forgotten it was a costume. My mouth opened to say it.

What came out instead was:

"Theodore Okafor."

The vowels arrived from Lagos before I could stop them, round and full, my grandmother's vowels, my father's vowels, every vowel I had spent seven years sanding flat now suddenly standing up straight in a foreign hall and refusing to apologize.

"Okafor," the aide repeated, frowning. "Of where?"

"Of Lagos," I said. "Colony and Protectorate of Southern Nigeria. Apprentice clockmaker, formerly of the firm Hentschel & Sons, currently of nowhere in particular, and bearer of testimony." I heard myself say *testimony*, a word I had never once spoken aloud, and found it fit my mouth better than any word I'd used in years. "In the matter of Corporal Jakob Brandt, listed dead since 1916, and presently alive in a place your records insist does not exist."

The hall went quiet in the particular way halls go quiet when everyone pretends they didn't hear and everyone heard.

The aide reached for the canvas. The young delegate — bless his ink-stained heart, may his unread notes someday be read — did not stop him, but neither did he move the letters out of reach, and in that small refusal lay the whole hinge of the morning. The aide unfolded the topmost page. I watched him read his own institution's handwriting declaring a dead man alive, and watched the color leave his face in stages, like a tide going out.

"This is irregular," he said finally, which is what powerful men say instead of *this is true*.

"Yes," I agreed. "So am I. Irregularity appears to be the medium we're working in."

They took my statement sitting down, which I hadn't expected; I had expected to be taken standing up, against a wall, perhaps by the collar. Instead the young delegate produced paper, and the gray aide dictated questions in a voice gone careful and flat, and somewhere behind them the senior negotiator — the laughing man — had stopped laughing and begun, quietly, to confer with the man beside him, and the conferring had the specific quality of damage control. You cannot unwitness a thing once it is in the record. I know. I spent a decade trying to be unwitnessed myself.

When they asked me to sign, I wrote:

**Theodore Adeyemi Okafor, Lagos**

and beneath it, *witness and bearer*. My hand shook then. Only then. Seven years of a borrowed name, and it took signing my own to make me tremble — as though the body understood before the mind that something was being returned, the way a pawned watch comes home heavier than you remembered, weighted with everything it cost to redeem it.

Jakob Brandt's file was reopened that afternoon. I know because the young delegate told me, pressing the information into my hand along with a receipt for the letters as though I were a laundry. "Amended," he said. "Officially amended." As if the paper had been a suit taken in at the waist. Perhaps, for governments, it is.

Emil did not get a receipt. Emil got a cell, or worse — I asked, twice, and received answers shaped like corridors leading nowhere. Grief does not resolve neatly into relief. They arrive together, like sisters who dislike each other, and share a room in your chest for the rest of your life, and you learn to move carefully between them.

* * *

That evening I climbed back to the observatory gallery, where the master clock ticked in the dark with its recovered voice, steady as a heartbeat that had decided to go on. I opened the case one last time — not because anything needed repair, but because I wanted to put my hands on something honest.

The pendulum swung. Eleven fifty-three, and gaining nothing, losing nothing.

"Okafor," I said aloud to the empty dome, testing it the way you test a repaired escapement — listening for the catch, the stutter, the lie. It came out clean. It came out mine.

Below me, the city's church bells began to mark the hour, and far off — or perhaps not far off at all, perhaps only in the next cell, the next camp, the next miswritten file — someone else's name waited to be spoken by whoever was brave enough, or foolish enough, or steady-handed enough to speak it.

I took off my coat, rolled up my sleeves, and got to work.
