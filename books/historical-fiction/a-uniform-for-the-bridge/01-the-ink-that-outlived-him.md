## 1. The Ink That Outlived Him

There is a particular silence that follows a death in the house. Not the absence of sound—Marseille never stops making noise, not even for its dead—but a different quality of quiet, as though the walls themselves have stopped listening for one voice and don't yet know what to do with their attention.

I had lived with that silence for eleven weeks when I sat down at Master Aubert's desk to finish his atlas.

The candle was tallow, cheap, and it guttered every time the wind came off the harbor through the shutters that didn't quite close. I could have fixed the shutters. I could have done a great many things differently over the past eleven weeks, but grief is not an efficient housekeeper, and neither was I. Instead I trimmed the wick, uncorked the gall-ink I'd ground that afternoon from oak galls and iron sulfate—the way he'd taught me, the way his own master had taught him, all the way back to some Flemish monk who probably also complained about his candle—and I set the first clean sheet of rag paper on the blotter.

Then, as I did every night, I listened.

Footsteps on the Rue de la Loge, three doors down, unhurried. A drunk singing something in Provençal about a fisherman's wife who deserved better. The creak of the chandler's sign swinging. Nothing that sounded like boots in formation. Nothing that sounded like the *flic-flac* of a sabre scabbard against a thigh, which is a sound you learn to dread very quickly once you know someone has died for paper.

Paper. Eleven weeks ago, a man died because of paper. Not even good paper—he'd been killed, if you can call being pushed into the harbor with a lead weight tied to your belt a death with dignity, for forty-one sheets of drawn coastline. They fished him out two days later near the Mole, so bloated the fishmongers wouldn't sell what the sea returned to us. We buried what we could recognize under the floor of the workshop, beneath the flagstone where the cold always pooled, because a grave in the churchyard would have been a signpost: *Here lies the mapmaker; dig here for maps.*

So. The atlas.

You should understand what it was, or rather what it was becoming, because I didn't fully understand it myself for a long time, and I resent being made to seem more knowing than I was. It was a survey of the fortifications of Marseille and its approaches—the seawall, the batteries, the depths of the channels where a hull might run aground or slip through, depending on whose hands held the chart. Master Aubert had been commissioned by the Crown. He had been paid by the Crown. And then, somewhere in the last months of his life, he had begun to understand that the Crown intended to use his careful measurements not to defend the harbor but to clear it—that the "marshland" along the eastern seawall, the quarter where four hundred families kept their homes and their goats and their grudges, was slated for demolition, and that his beautiful precise atlas was the instrument of the razing.

He had been rewriting it when they came for him. Or he had finished rewriting it, and they came for him anyway. I still don't know which. There are nights I work through both possibilities like a knot in thread, pulling each end, and the knot only tightens.

What I knew was this: the atlas in my keeping was now a lie told in the Crown's favor unless I finished what he'd started. And finishing it was treason. Not the romantic kind—not the kind with ballads. The kind that ends at the gallows outside the Porte d'Aix with your body left hanging as a lesson to other apprentices.

I dipped the pen. I began to ink the coastline.

* * *

His hand and mine were close enough to fool most people, which was fortunate, since fooling people was now the entire project of my life. He'd trained me six years to draw as he drew—the long confident strokes for headlands, the stippled hatching for shallows, the little anchors and soundings rendered with a patience I had never once possessed naturally and had to counterfeit nightly. My own inclination was toward haste. Elodie, he used to say, you draw like a woman fleeing a fire. I told him that was exactly the ambition, since everything worth drawing seemed lately to be on fire.

But coastlines cannot be hurried. A coastline is a promise the land makes to the sea and breaks continuously, and if you rush it, the reader will feel the lie in the line. So I worked the pen down the curve of the Anse des Auffes, adding the sounding marks he'd left in pencil—*seventeen feet*, *twenty-three at low water*—converting his ghost-gray arithmetic into permanent black. Somewhere in the city there were men who wanted these numbers. Men who would pay for them, or kill for them, or both, in whatever order proved convenient.

The trick, I had decided, was to be boring. Dangerous things are sought by men who imagine danger has a shape—locked chests, hidden compartments, letters written in cipher. Nobody searches the boring. So the atlas lived in pieces. Three plates rolled inside the hollow leg of the workbench. Four more pressed flat between the boards of the pressing frame under a stack of genuinely dull commissions—a survey of a widow's garden boundary in Saint-Marcel that I hoped no one would ever want to read, including me. The rest, the ones I'd already corrected, slept beneath the loose floorboard behind the drying rack, wrapped in oilcloth, under a layer of dust I had cultivated with real dedication. Dust, I have found, is a craft like any other. You must be patient with it. You must resist the tidy instinct.

The floorboard was the best hiding place and therefore, logically, the first place anyone would look. I knew this. I hid them there anyway, on the theory that a searcher who finds nothing in the obvious place often assumes the obvious place was the only place, and gives up with the smugness of a man who believes himself thorough. Whether this theory would survive contact with an actual officer of the Crown remained untested, and I prayed nightly it would stay that way.

Eleven weeks, and no knock at the door. But prayer, I've learned, is just worry wearing nicer clothes.

* * *

Somewhere past midnight—I measured time then by candles, three fingers of wax meaning an hour of his life spent posthumously—I reached the section that stopped my pen.

Plate thirty-four. The seawall.

Master Aubert's original survey of the eastern seawall quarter was a masterpiece of damning precision. Every house plotted, every foundation depth recorded, every well and cesspit and goat-shed noted in his small upright hand. And in the margins, in the shorthand we used for private notes—the same shorthand I was using now to annotate his corrections—his findings. *Foundations sound to twelve feet. No marsh. No subsidence. Ground firm as the Rock itself.* Over and over. *No marsh here.* The Crown's engineers had declared the quarter built on unstable reclaimed marsh, unfit for habitation, dangerous to the harbor works—a finding that required the demolition of four hundred homes and happened to free up waterfront land that certain men of influence had long coveted. His survey said otherwise. His survey called their report a fiction. And his corrections to the atlas turned the whole document from a weapon aimed at the quarter into a shield laid over it.

That was the treason, then. Not the drawing. Anyone may draw a coastline. The treason was the truth, written plainly enough for a councilman to read.

I thought about the widow Couteau next door, who brought me soup I pretended not to need. Her sister lived in the seawall quarter, above her son's rope-walk. I thought about the boys who dove for coins off the Mole, whose mothers washed linen in the quarter's communal fountain. I thought about how a map is never really about land. It's about who gets to keep standing on it.

My master had understood this too late to save himself, or perhaps not too late at all—perhaps dying was simply the price he'd calculated and paid, the way one pays for vellum. I resented him for it. I loved him for it. Grief, I have come to believe, is mostly those two feelings refusing to share a room and shouting through the wall.

I inked his marginal note into the plate: *Ground firm.* Two words. Small enough to fit under a thumbnail. Heavy enough to hang a man.

* * *

The third candle was drowning in its own puddle when I heard the thing that ended the night's work.

Not boots. Worse: the particular rhythm of a patrol pausing. The singing drunk had gone quiet. In the Rue de la Loge, a lantern's glow slid across my shutter-crack, hesitated, moved on. Then voices—two of them, low, the clipped vowels of soldiers speaking French instead of Provençal, which meant they were not locals and wanted everyone to know it.

I blew out the candle. This is a skill; do not laugh. Blowing out a candle silently, without the wet hiss of spittle on the wick, takes practice, and I had practiced eleven weeks' worth.

In the dark, I folded the night's plate along its prepared creases and slid it up my sleeve, against my forearm, where the oilcloth lining I'd sewn into every sleeve that autumn kept the ink from touching skin. If they knocked—and they would not knock, soldiers do not knock, they arrive—I would be a tired apprentice surprised at her late needlework. I had a half-finished shift on the table by the window for exactly this purpose. I had even pricked my thumb on occasion, to make the story bleed true.

The voices stopped directly below my window.

"—says the old man had an apprentice," one said. "Girl."

"The old man's dead." This voice was younger, bored. "Dead men don't pay wages. Girl's probably sold his instruments by now and gone back to whatever village squeezed her out."

"Captain wants the workshop looked at regardless. Thursday, maybe. After the customs house rotation."

"Thursday," the young one agreed, with the enthusiasm of a man agreeing to weather.

Their lantern swung away. Boots on stone, fading toward the Vieux-Port, taking the light with them. The drunk resumed his song, off-key and undeterred, and I discovered I was gripping the windowsill hard enough to leave marks in the wood, and that I had not breathed in what felt like a year.

Thursday. Three days.

I stood in the dark and took inventory the way he'd taught me to take inventory of a chart: what is certain, what is probable, what is merely hoped. Certain: they were coming to search. Probable: a routine look, bored men, a captain covering himself with paperwork. Merely hoped: that the dust on my floorboards was thick enough, that the pressing-frame commissions were dull enough, that boredom would win.

And underneath all of it, certain as bedrock: the atlas could not be found here. Not Thursday, not ever. Which meant it had to move—all forty-one plates, plus his notes, plus the corrected margins, the whole lethal weight of truth—through a city crawling with the men who'd killed him, past checkpoints I could count on one hand and would soon learn to count in nightmares. The customs house. The arsenal gate. The harbor watch. Three gates between this room and safety, wherever safety had gotten to.

I waited until the moon cleared the chimney pots and gave me gray light to work by. Then I knelt, pried up the floorboard behind the drying rack, and lifted out the oilcloth bundle. It was heavier than paper should be. Paper is light; what's written on it isn't.

"Three days," I whispered to the bundle, or to him, I'm not sure the distinction mattered anymore. "You couldn't have left instructions? A list of allies? A single honest friend?"

The bundle said nothing. Dead masters are terrible conversationalists. But as I rewrapped it, my fingers found the edge of something tucked between plates thirty-nine and forty that hadn't been there—or that I hadn't noticed—in eleven weeks of handling: a second sheet, smaller, folded twice, sealed with a blob of plain brown wax and no mark at all, addressed in his hand not to me, not to any name at all, but to a single word:

*For the child.*

I sat back on my heels in the moonlight, holding it, feeling the silence of the house lean in around me.

We had no child. We had never had a child. There was no child in my master's life that I knew of—

Which, I was beginning to understand about my master, meant almost nothing at all.
