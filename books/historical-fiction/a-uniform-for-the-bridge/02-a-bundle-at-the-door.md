## 2. A Bundle at the Door

There are three sounds in this world I have learned to distrust above all others: the knock of a tax collector, the creak of my own floorboards when someone else is standing on them, and a baby crying.

The sound at my door that morning was none of these. It was worse. It was silence — the particular, deliberate silence of someone who has knocked and then decided they would rather not be there when the door opened.

I had been awake since the fourth bell, which was nothing unusual. Grief keeps its own hours and does not consult yours. Master Aurélien Delacroix had been dead eleven weeks, three days, and — if you counted the way I did, which was obsessively — some number of hours I refused to calculate because it made me feel like an accountant of sorrow. In those eleven weeks I had learned to do his work with his pens, his ink, his habits. I inked his coastlines by candlelight and slept, badly, in his chair. The atlas lived beneath the third floorboard from the hearth, wrapped in oilcloth, and I lived above it like a woman sleeping on top of a powder keg and calling it a mattress.

So when I heard that silence on my step, every hair on my arms stood up and took inventory.

I did not open the door immediately. This is not cowardice; this is Marseille. In this city, opening your door too quickly is how you end up owing money to people whose names you never learn. I pressed my eye to the gap beside the shutter instead.

The lane outside was empty. October fog off the harbor had come up the hill the way it always did, softening the rooftops until the whole quarter looked like one of the master's unfinished sketches — all suggestion, no commitment. A gull sat on the opposite gutter with the smug air of a witness who will testify for whoever pays.

And on my doorstep: a bundle.

Not a bundle of rags. Not a bundle of firewood. A bundle that breathed.

I want to tell you that I flung open the door and swept the child into my arms, that some dormant maternal instinct roared awake like a banked fire. I want to tell you that, but D. M. Marchetti taught me — no, forgive me, life taught me — that we are mostly made of smaller, meaner stuff, and heroism is just cowardice that arrived late. What I actually did was stand there with my hand on the latch and think, very clearly and very coldly: *No.*

*No*, because I was a twenty-three-year-old apprentice mapmaker with a dead master, a forbidden atlas under my floorboards, and a face that three separate soldiers had already tried to memorize. *No*, because children were loud, and loud drew eyes, and eyes were the one thing my carefully closed life could not afford. *No*, because I had spent eleven weeks perfecting the art of being nobody, and nobody does not answer doors at dawn for bundles that breathe.

Then the bundle sneezed, sat up, and looked at me.

He was perhaps six years old, small for it, with a shock of dark hair that had clearly lost several arguments with a comb and won them all anyway. His coat was good wool once, now outgrown and salt-stained. He clutched something against his chest with both hands, the way children clutch things — not holding an object so much as defending a border. And he looked at me with the flat, unblinking patience of a child who has been waiting long enough to stop expecting anything, which is among the most terrible expressions a human face can make.

"You're the map lady," he said.

"I'm not the map lady," I said, because denial is free and I have always been frugal.

"The fish man said the map lady lives here. With the dead man." He said *dead man* the way children say such things — without ceremony, without the elaborate cushioning adults wrap around the word. "Are you dead too?"

"Not yet. It's on my list."

He considered this. He seemed to find it acceptable. Then he held out the thing he'd been clutching, and my careful, closed, eleven-weeks-perfect life cracked straight down the middle like a bad plate.

It was paper. Folded twice, soft at the creases from handling, spotted with what I prayed was rainwater. And even before I unfolded it — even before my eyes did their treacherous, professional work — some animal part of me knew. You spend enough years bent over another person's drawings and you learn their hand the way you learn a voice through a wall. The weight of the line. The way the hatching leaned eastward like grass in a wind that only existed in the master's head.

I unfolded it.

It was a drawing of the seawall. Not the seawall as any tourist or tax assessor would draw it — not a pretty rampart with little waves doing decorative duty beneath it. This was the seawall as the master had drawn it in the last months of his life: annotated, coded, dense with symbols I half-recognized and wholly could not read. Numbers ran along the base of the wall in a cipher I knew existed because I had found fragments of its key burned in the grate after they took him. There was a section of the drawing — the stretch below the Rue des Salins, where the poorest families stacked themselves against the stone like barnacles — that was rendered in a heavier hand, circled, marked with a symbol like an anchor crossed with a cross.

My mouth went dry. My hands, traitors to the last, stayed perfectly steady, because eleven weeks of hiding dangerous paper teaches you that your hands are not your own anymore.

"Where," I said, and had to start again. "Where did you get this?"

"A man gave it to me." The boy shrugged, a whole philosophy in one gesture. "He said give it to the map lady. He said she'd know."

"What man? What did he look like?"

"He had a boat voice."

"A *boat voice*."

"Loud. Like he's always shouting over water." The boy demonstrated, which involved no change in volume whatsoever, six-year-olds having exactly one volume and it being this one. "And then the fish man gave me bread and said walk up the hill and don't stop. So I walked up the hill and didn't stop." He said it with the modest pride of a soldier reporting a completed campaign. Then, because he was six and honesty is a disease children haven't yet learned to manage: "I stopped twice. For the cats."

"Naturally."

"There were four."

"An acceptable casualty rate."

He almost smiled. It was a small, crooked thing, gone as fast as it came, and I felt it land somewhere under my ribs with more force than any drawing had ever managed.

I looked past him down the lane. Fog, gulls, the wet cobbles shining like the scales of some enormous sleeping fish. Whoever had delivered him — the man with the boat voice, the fish man, whichever ghosts had conspired to leave a child and a death warrant on my step — they were gone, and they had gone quietly, which meant they had gone deliberately.

That was when I saw the note. It had been tucked into the boy's collar, and it had worked itself loose during his feline detours and lay on my doorstep, weighted under a stone. I picked it up. One line, in a hand I did not know, written quickly:

*Forget the face. Keep the paper. Trust no uniform.*

Well. I thought. That's three instructions, and I am already failing at the first, because I was looking at his face right then — the chapped cheeks, the eyelashes still fog-damp, the stubborn chin — and I knew with a certainty that felt like a bruise forming that forgetting it was going to be impossible.

*Keep the paper.* The paper was the final plate. Or part of it. Or the key to it. The piece the master had died rather than surrender, the hole in my atlas shaped exactly like this ragged rectangle of cheap stock. Eleven weeks I had told myself the atlas was nearly complete, that the missing plate was a wound that would scar over, that I could finish the work around the absence the way a river finishes itself around a fallen tree. And now the absence was sitting on my doorstep, sneezing, counting cats.

*Trust no uniform.* Easy. I hadn't trusted a uniform since the night two of them carried the master out of his own study and left me with his blood drying into the grain of the drafting table, a stain I still covered with a cloth each morning like a widow covering a mirror.

"Can I come in?" the boy asked. "It's cold, and the fish man said you wouldn't be scary, but you're a little bit scary."

"I'm not scary. I'm busy."

"It's the same face."

I opened my mouth to deliver some further devastating riposte and discovered I had nothing. Six years old, one morning in Marseille, and he had already found the seam in me that all my stitching couldn't close.

Here is what I should have done. I should have taken the drawing, closed the door, and spent the day finding out who the fish man was and which parish had a claim on the boy. I should have treated him as a delivery, a problem with a return address. Every prudent instinct I owned stood in a row inside me like soldiers — *trust no uniform*, indeed — and voted no.

Instead I stepped back and held the door wide.

"Come in. Wipe your feet. Touch nothing, especially nothing that looks important, because everything looks important, because everything is."

He wiped his feet with the thoroughness of someone who had been shouted at about it before, by someone whose voice I suddenly ached to hear. He came into the workshop and stopped, and I watched him take it in — the maps pinned everywhere like the room had grown feathers, the smell of ink and salt and old wood, the drafting table with its draped stain — and I watched something in his shoulders come down half an inch, some tension he'd carried up the hill finally setting down its load.

"My papa drew," he said. "Not like this. Little ships. On scraps."

"Little ships are harder than they look," I said. "Everyone thinks they're easy. They lie about it."

"Papa said the sea is just a big map that moves."

I turned away fast, busying myself with the stove, because there are only so many blows a heart can parry before breakfast. The fire needed feeding anyway. Everything needed feeding. That was the trouble with opening a door — things came in, and things came in hungry.

His name, extracted over bread and the end of yesterday's cheese, was Tomas. He did not know his surname, or claimed not to, or had been instructed not to, and watching him eat I understood that whatever else this child was, he had been coached. Children who have been coached hold their bread differently. They chew and listen at the same time. They answer questions with questions. *Why do you live alone? Why is the table covered? Is the dead man under the floor?*

"No," I said to that last one, more sharply than I intended, and his eyes went wide, and I softened it with the truth, or a slice of it: "The dead man is in the churchyard of Saint-Victor, under a stone that says far less about him than he deserved."

"Did you love him?"

"He was my master. I loved the work. It's safer."

"That's sad," said Tomas, with the terrible precision of the young, and ate his crust.

I let him. I sat across from this stranger-child in my master's kitchen and let him call my life sad, because the alternative was arguing with him, and I suspected he would win.

Through the window, the fog was lifting off the harbor, and I could see the seawall itself in the distance, that gray spine of stone with its barnacle-families clinging on, the ones the Crown had begun calling *structurally unsound* in documents written by men who had never touched the stone. The drawing lay on the table between us, face down. I had turned it face down an hour ago. Neither of us had mentioned it since. We had a treaty, the boy and I, unspoken and already binding: the paper was the reason we were both here, and the paper could wait until the bread was done.

But treaties expire.

When the plate was clean and the boy was yawning in a way that suggested the walk up the hill had cost him more than he'd admit, I turned the drawing over and spread it flat, and I put my finger on the anchor-crossed-with-a-cross, and I asked him, gently, the question I had been circling all morning like a gull over a fish market:

"Tomas. The man with the boat voice. Did he say anything else? Anything at all?"

Tomas yawned again, rubbed his eyes with the heels of his hands, and said, in the tone of a child producing a forgotten errand at the worst possible hour:

"He said the map lady would know it isn't finished. He said she'd know what's missing." He frowned, working to keep the words exact, because someone had made him practice them. "He said, *tell her the rest is where the bones sleep.*"

The fire popped. Outside, the gull screamed once and went silent, as if even it understood that something had just changed hands.

Because I did know. God help me, I knew exactly where the bones slept. Saint-Victor's crypt was full of them, centuries of them, and the master had walked me through that crypt a hundred times on his endless, suspicious walks, tapping the walls, counting niches, humming — and I had thought it was grief, or piety, or the ordinary madness of old mapmakers.

It had been a filing system.

I looked at the boy who had carried the missing piece of a dead man's life's work up a foggy hill on his own two feet, stopping twice for cats, and I felt my carefully closed life — the quiet one, the invisible one, the one built plank by plank over eleven weeks of pretending to be nobody — crack open along its whole length like ice in spring.

"Finish your bread," I said, my voice steadier than I had any right to expect. "We're going to church."
