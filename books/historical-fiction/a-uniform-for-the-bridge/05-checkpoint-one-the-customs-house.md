## 5. Checkpoint One: The Customs House

There is a particular kind of silence that comes from a man who has been bored for eleven years and has stopped pretending otherwise. I had met it before—my master used to say the Intendance employed two kinds of clerks: those who feared their superiors and those who had outlived all feeling for them—and on the morning I walked the plates of a dead man's atlas into the Customs House at the Vieux-Port, I was counting on the second kind.

His name, according to the little brass plate he never polished, was Monsieur Aubert.

I knew this because my master had known it, and my master had known everything about everyone whose signature could slow down a delivery. *Charm the clerk, not the soldier*, he told me once, when I was seventeen and foolish enough to think smuggling meant pistols and dark alleys. *The clerk is paid to look. The soldier is paid to see. Never give either one a reason to do more than his wage requires.*

I have thought about that advice every day since he died. I thought about it as I wrapped the plates in oilcloth at three in the morning, with Tomas sleeping in the widow Couteau's spare cot across the landing and my hands shaking so badly I had to press them flat against the table like a woman calming a startled bird. I thought about it as I chose my dress—the drab grey one, the one that makes people's eyes slide off me like rain off slate. And I thought about it, most especially, when I saw the soldiers at the customs gate and understood that no amount of drab grey was going to matter if the wrong pair of eyes happened to be paying attention.

But first, the clerk.

* * *

The trick, if you can call it a trick, is in the vellum.

A mapmaker's shop receives deliveries. That is its whole nature; paper comes in, paper goes out, and nobody blinks unless the paper going out is going somewhere it shouldn't. So three nights earlier I had written myself an order—in my master's hand, which after nine years of apprenticeship I could produce better than my own—commissioning forty sheets of fine calfskin vellum from the supplier on the rue de la Loge. The order was genuine in every particular except the date, which I had moved forward by a week, and its purpose, which had nothing to do with vellum at all.

The plates themselves were thin. Thinner than vellum, barely thicker than good rag paper, and cut to dimensions that sat comfortably within the bounds of a standard sheet. I had spent an entire evening with a razor and a ruler, trimming the edges of the seawall survey and the two harbor approaches until they would lie flat against forty innocent sheets of calfskin like pressed flowers between the pages of a Bible. Then oilcloth, then string, then the supplier's own wrapping paper—I had bought a genuine roll from him, paid genuinely, chatted genuinely about the weather—and finally the wax seal, pressed with a matrix I had carved myself in the shape of the supplier's mark. Forgery, my master always said, is ninety percent honest labor done by someone else.

Which is how I came to be standing at the customs gate on a bright, cold Tuesday morning, holding a package of vellum that weighed perhaps half what real vellum should weigh, praying to a God I only spoke to under duress that Monsieur Aubert was having one of his days.

He was. God, it turns out, does answer prayers, though rarely in the form you'd choose.

Monsieur Aubert sat behind a desk too large for him, in a room too dim for reading, with the expression of a man who had been asked to count grains of rice for a living and had long ago stopped caring whether the count was right. He was maybe fifty-five, with the soft hands of someone who hadn't lifted anything heavier than a stamp since the Regency, and when I set the package on his desk he looked at it the way a man looks at a bill he intends to dispute.

"Delivery," I said. "For the Delacroix workshop, quai du Lazaret. Vellum, forty sheets."

"Vellum." He said the word as though I'd brought him a dead cat. He pulled the manifest toward himself without touching the package, ran one finger down the column, and sighed—a long, theatrical sigh, the sigh of a man who wanted me to understand precisely how much suffering my existence was causing him.

I gave him what he wanted.

"I know," I said, leaning in slightly, lowering my voice into the register women use for shared grievances. "Forty sheets, and half of them will have flaws you could drive a cart through. But try telling the master that. He inspects every sheet like it's going to hang in Versailles, and if there's so much as a vein in the skin—" I rolled my eyes. "You've dealt with mapmakers before, monsieur?"

Something flickered behind the boredom. Recognition, maybe. The small warm light of a man being asked about his expertise by a pretty woman who seemed to mean it.

"The ones on the quay," he said. "Madmen, the lot of them."

"Madmen," I agreed solemnly. "My father's cousin worked the trade. Died with ink on his fingers and a grudge against the Dutch." This was not true. My master was not my father's cousin, was not dead of any grudge, and the Dutch were blameless. But grief makes liars of us all, and I have made peace with the fact that I now lied fluently, cheerfully, and mostly about things that didn't matter.

Monsieur Aubert almost smiled. It was a terrible thing to witness, like watching a door open in a wall where no door had been for years.

He stamped the manifest. He did not weigh the package. He did not ask me to open it, did not glance at the wrapping, did not perform even the token inspection that his post existed to perform. He waved me through with a hand already reaching for the next document, already retreating back into his eleven-year silence.

*Triumph*, I thought, walking toward the gate with the package tucked against my hip like a sleeping child. *That's how it's done, old man. Charm the clerk.*

And then I made my mistake, which was to feel triumphant.

* * *

Because the soldier was standing at the gate. Of course he was. There is always a soldier at the gate; that is the entire arrangement of the world.

He was young—younger than me, probably, which put him somewhere south of twenty-five—with the raw, unformed face of a provincial boy who had been issued a uniform and a musket and very little else in the way of purpose. He wasn't checking papers. Papers weren't his work. His work was to stand, and to watch, and to report anything worth reporting to whatever sergeant had drawn the short straw of supervising him.

And he watched me.

Not the way men usually watch me—not the market-stall appraisal, not the quick inventory of a woman alone on a dock. This was different. This was the flat, patient attention of a man who had nothing else to do and a memory that hadn't yet been dulled by routine. His eyes went to my face, then to the package, then to my face again, and something in the sequence of it—the double-take, the slight narrowing—made the skin between my shoulders prickle.

I did what my master taught me. I looked through him. Not past him, not away from him—through him, the way you look through a window at weather. Invisible people are made by other people's eyes, he said, and the trick is to make your own eyes agree. So I let my gaze pass over the soldier without pausing, without acknowledging, without giving him the one thing that would make him real to me and me real to him.

It almost worked.

I was three steps past the gate—three steps from the open street, from the noise of the fish market, from safety—when he said, "Citizen."

Not "mademoiselle." Not "miss." *Citizen.* The word of a man reading from a script of duty, and my stomach dropped through the cobblestones.

I turned. Slowly. Confused, but not frightened—confusion is safe, fear is confession. "Yes?"

"You dropped this." He held up a glove. My left glove, which had slipped from under my arm where I'd tucked it while adjusting the package string. Grey kid leather, unremarkable, worth perhaps six sous.

Except that inside the cuff, embroidered in thread the same color as the leather—because I had embroidered it, because vanity survives even grief—was a single letter. *D*. For Delacroix.

"Thank you," I said, and took it from him, and our fingers did not touch, and his eyes stayed on my face for exactly one heartbeat longer than courtesy required. One heartbeat. I counted it. I counted everything in those few seconds—the number of steps to the corner, the position of the sun, the exact shade of boredom returning to his face as I turned away and became, once more, furniture.

Then I walked. Not fast—fast is flight and flight is guilt—but steadily, through the fish market, past the stalls reeking of brine and blood, until the customs house was behind me and the package was against my hip and my heart was hammering so hard I could feel it in my teeth.

* * *

I did not go straight home. Home, in that hour, was a trap; anyone following me needed to lose me, and losing a tail in Marseille is an art my master drilled into me with the patience of a man teaching calligraphy. I bought onions I didn't need. I argued about their price. I stood in front of a fabric stall and considered, with great seriousness, a bolt of blue wool I had no intention of buying. I doubled back through the covered passage behind the Hôtel de Ville, emerged onto a different street going a different direction, and only then, satisfied, let myself take the long way along the quay to the workshop.

Tomas was sitting on the step.

This was not the plan. The plan, meticulously negotiated with the widow Couteau over bread and suspicious silence, was that the boy would stay indoors, away from windows, away from the street, away from the small constellation of neighbors who noticed things. Instead he was on the step, knees drawn up, clutching the toy boat Baptiste had whittled for him from a scrap of pine, watching the street with the fixed, unblinking vigilance of a cat guarding territory it has no right to believe is its own.

"You're supposed to be inside," I said.

He shrugged. It was a comprehensive shrug, the shrug of a child who has learned that adults' rules are weather—they happen to you, and complaining changes nothing.

I crouched in front of him. Up close he smelled of wood smoke and the widow's lavender water, and there was a smudge of something—soot, maybe, or ash—on his chin. "Did anyone come by? Anyone at all?"

Another shrug. Then, after a moment, in that small scraped voice of his: "A man looked at the house."

My chest went tight. "What man? What did he look like?"

"A soldier." He drew a shape in the air with one finger—tall, apparently, and hat-shaped. "He walked. Then he walked back."

Walking, then walking back. Pacing. Soldiers pace when they're waiting for orders; soldiers also pace when they're deciding whether a house is worth reporting. I looked up the street, empty now in both directions, ordinary as bread, and felt the morning's triumph curdle into something colder.

"Inside," I said, and he went, and I followed, and I locked the door behind us with a key I suddenly wished were larger.

* * *

The plates came out of the vellum wrapping with my heart in my throat, though there was no rational reason for it—if the package had been compromised, I'd have known at the gate, in irons, not here in my own workroom with the afternoon light coming gold through the east window. But hands remember what minds refuse to admit, and mine shook as I unwrapped the oilcloth, shook as I checked each plate against my mental inventory, shook until I laid them all out on the drafting table and saw them whole: the harbor approaches, the battery positions, and the seawall survey, the one that had killed my master, lying there flat and quiet and more dangerous than any blade in Marseille.

They had passed. That was the truth of the morning, and I tried to hold it. Checkpoint one, passed. The plates were moving. In three days, Baptiste would take them by water past the arsenal gate, and then the harbor watch, and then—

And then what? I still didn't fully know. A council. A hearing. Some room where men in better coats than mine would look at these lines and decide whether the families along the seawall kept their homes or lost them. I was a courier carrying a verdict I would never be allowed to read.

I sat down at the table and looked at the plates and thought about the soldier at the gate.

Here is what I could not stop turning over, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth: he had seen my face. Not glanced at it—seen it. Had marked it, the way a clerk marks a ledger, one entry among hundreds but entered nonetheless. And my face was attached to a name, or soon would be, because the Delacroix workshop received deliveries and deliveries required signatures and signatures left trails, and trails, given time and a reason to follow them, led to doors.

To this door.

My master died because of these plates. I had always known that in the abstract way you know the sea is dangerous—respectfully, from a distance, in fair weather. But sitting there in the gold light with the survey spread out before me, I felt the knowledge turn concrete. Somewhere in this city there were men who had decided that this paper must not exist, and those men had soldiers, and those soldiers had eyes, and one pair of those eyes had spent four seconds learning my face at a customs gate on a Tuesday morning.

Four seconds. It costs nothing, to notice a person. It costs everything, later.

"Tomas," I said.

He was on the floor under the window, pushing the toy boat across the boards, making a low engine-noise in his throat that was either a ship or a dying goose. He looked up.

"Do you know how to be quiet?" I asked. "Not just now. For a long time. Quiet like a game. Like hiding."

He considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "I'm good at hiding," he said. "Nobody found me at Madame's. For two days."

Two days. The words landed somewhere below my ribs and stayed there. Two days, a six-year-old had kept himself unfound in a city full of men looking, and I had nearly undone it at a checkpoint by dropping a glove with my name stitched inside it like an invitation.

"Well," I said, and my voice came out steadier than I deserved. "Then we'll practice. Both of us."

He nodded once, a soldier accepting terms, and went back to his boat.

I looked at the plates a moment longer. Then I took up the oilcloth and began, carefully, to wrap them again—for tomorrow there would be another errand, another package, another pair of eyes to slip past—and as I worked, the image returned to me unbidden: the young soldier's face at the gate, patient and blank, filing me away.

Somewhere across the city, in some barracks or guardroom, a report was possibly being written. *A woman, grey dress, brown hair, delivering vellum for the Delacroix workshop, quai du Lazaret.* Nothing in it. Everything in it.

I finished the wrapping and tied the string, and my hands, at last, were steady.
