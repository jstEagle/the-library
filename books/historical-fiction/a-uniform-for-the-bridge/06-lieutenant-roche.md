## 6. Lieutenant Roche

I have been an officer of His Majesty's engineers for six years, and in that time I have learned that the most dangerous objects in any garrison are not the cannon but the papers. Cannon announce themselves. Papers sit quietly in their drawers until someone dies for them.

The order came down on a Tuesday, which I have always thought was fitting—Tuesdays being, in my experience, the day on which inconvenient truths prefer to arrive. Colonel Vasseur summoned me to his office above the arsenal, where he kept a window that looked out on nothing in particular and a decanter that looked out on everything. He did not offer me a glass. That was my first warning. The second was that he had locked his door, and the third was that the map spread across his desk was wrong.

"You will find it," he said. "The final survey. Delacroix completed it before—" He made a small gesture with two fingers, the gesture men make when they mean *died* but would rather not sully the word by saying it. "Before his accident."

"His fall," I said. "From the ramparts at Saint-Nicolas. At night. Alone. In dry weather."

"The stairs were wet," Vasseur said.

"In dry weather," I said again, because I was young enough then to believe that repetition was a kind of courage.

He looked at me over the rim of his spectacles with the patience of a man who has buried several lieutenants' careers already this year. "Roche. The survey of the seawall quarter is a matter of state security. The Crown requires a complete set of fortification drawings, and one plate is unaccounted for. You will locate it. You will return it to me. And you will not," he added, rolling the map closed with unnecessary force, "ask questions about stairs."

I saluted. I remember the salute distinctly, because it was the last one I gave him without something sour underneath it.

* * *

Here is what they do not teach you at the academy: cartography is a moral profession.

They teach you projection and scale, the mathematics of latitude, how to hold a pen so your hand does not tremble at the crucial curve of a headland. They do not teach you that every line you draw is a decision about who lives where, whose house is real and whose is an error to be corrected, whose ground is solid and whose has been declared, by men who never stood on it, to be marsh.

I knew Julien Delacroix.

Not well—that must be said plainly, because later events will tempt people to exaggerate—but I knew him. He came to the academy in Toulon when I was a second-year cadet, a guest lecturer with salt in his beard and ink permanently stained into the creases of his knuckles, as though the maps had gotten into his blood and refused to come out. He taught a fortnight's course on coastal surveying, and I sat in the third row and copied his every stroke into my notebook like a monk transcribing scripture.

He had a way of drawing coastlines that no other hand could imitate. Where the rest of us produced lines, Delacroix produced *opinions*. A harbor in his hand was not merely a shape cut into the land; it was an argument, each promontory asserting itself, each depth-sounding placed with the confidence of a man who had personally argued with the sea and won. He dotted his shallows in a particular rhythm—three close, one apart, three close again—and he drew his fortifications with a double-stroked bastion line that flared slightly at the salient angles, like a man squaring his shoulders before delivering bad news.

I spent two years after the academy trying to draw like him. I failed. Everyone failed. His hand was his own, the way a voice is one's own.

So you will understand why, four days into my investigation, sitting in the customs archive at the Vieux-Port with a lantern and a growing sense of unease, I stopped breathing when I opened a folio of recovered drafts and saw those shoulders squared back at me from the page.

* * *

The folio was catalogued as *miscellaneous maritime*, which is bureaucratic French for *we found this and could not be bothered*. Inside were working sketches, tide tables in Delacroix's crabbed leftward slant, and—this was what stopped my heart—a partial draft of the seawall survey itself. Not the missing plate. A preliminary study, rougher, earlier. But unmistakably his.

Three dots, one apart. Three dots again. Bastion lines flaring at the angles like squared shoulders.

And beneath the seawall, where the official Crown survey—the one upon which the demolition order rested—showed soft marsh unsuitable for habitation, Delacroix's preliminary showed bedrock. Solid stone, sound footing, annotated in his tight hand: *fond rocheux à 2 toises — vérifié trois fois.* Rock bottom at two fathoms, verified three times.

The Crown's survey said the houses along the seawall stood on mud that would not bear their weight. Delacroix said they stood on stone.

Both surveys claimed to describe the same ground. Only one of them could be telling the truth, and I had a very good idea which one, because I had met the men who produced the Crown's version, and I had watched them produce it from a comfortable chair in an office whose windows overlooked, as I have mentioned, nothing in particular.

I sat there for a long while. The lantern guttered. Somewhere above me, the customs clerks went home in twos and threes, laughing about wine or women or whatever customs clerks laugh about. And I sat in the archive with a dead man's handwriting under my fingers and understood, with the slow cold certainty of ice forming, that Julien Delacroix had not fallen down wet stairs. He had drawn something true, and truth, in Marseille in the year of our Lord 1747, is a substance handled only with gloves.

* * *

Let me be honest about my first instinct, because a man who lies in his own head is beyond saving. My first instinct was to put the folio back, report that the trail had gone cold, and request a transfer somewhere the air smelled less of trouble. Toulon, perhaps. Grenoble. Anywhere the paperwork was boring and the corpses stayed metaphorical.

I am twenty-eight years old. I have a widowed mother in Aix who writes to me twice a month about her health in terms usually reserved for military dispatches. I have a career that is progressing at exactly the speed careers progress for men without family money or powerful friends—which is to say, slowly, and entirely dependent on the goodwill of superiors like Colonel Vasseur. Every rational instrument I possess pointed in the same direction: put the paper down, Lieutenant. Walk away. Wet stairs. Terrible tragedy. So sorry.

I put the folio down. I picked up my hat. I got as far as the door.

Then I turned around and took the preliminary study with me, folding it inside my coat like a thief, which is precisely what I had become—an officer stealing evidence from his own garrison's archive—and walked out into the evening feeling the paper against my ribs like a second heartbeat.

My mother would say the Delacroix in me finally woke up. My mother says many things. She is rarely wrong, which is insufferable of her.

* * *

The next morning I began the part of the work I dreaded most: canvassing the master's neighborhood. Standard procedure. Someone in the quarter near Saint-Victor had inherited his papers, or knew who had. An apprentice, most likely—Delacroix had taken students, I remembered that much from the gossip that followed his death. Find the apprentice, find the atlas, close the file, keep the career.

It should have been simple. It was not simple, for reasons I only partly understood at the time.

The street where he had kept his workshop was narrow and leaned toward the sea like an old woman eavesdropping. I went door to door in my uniform, and I noticed something that a civilian would not have noticed and that troubled me more than anything else that week: the doors were not merely shut. They were *uninhabited*. Faces appeared at windows and vanished. Conversations died mid-word as I passed, not with the guilty silence of people hiding something, but with the practiced silence of people who had hidden things before and gotten good at it.

A fishwife told me she remembered no mapmaker. A chandler told me the workshop had been empty for years. A child of perhaps ten looked at my coat, looked at my face, and ran—not away, but *alongside* me for half a street, shouting something up a stairwell in Provençal too fast for me to follow, before vanishing into the maze.

They were protecting someone. That much was clear. What was not clear—what gnawed at me through the afternoon and into a supper of bread and bad cheese eaten standing at my window—was why. Communities do not wrap themselves around a dead man's apprentice out of habit. Either the girl had done something to earn it, or they all knew something about the stairs that I was only beginning to suspect.

There was also the matter of the soldier.

One of my corporals, making routine inquiries at the customs house on my orders, reported that a young woman had presented herself two days prior with a delivery of vellum, and that she had charmed the duty clerk into waving her through without inspection. Ordinary enough. But the corporal, who had a memory for faces that would have made him rich in a better trade, described her to me with unusual precision: small, dark-haired, quick-eyed, ink stains on her fingers, and a manner—his word—of *someone counting exits*.

An apprentice couriering vellum through a checkpoint. With ink on her hands. Counting exits.

I thanked him and dismissed him and then stood at my window for a long time watching the harbor lights come up along the mole, thinking about a dead man's coastline and a living girl's ink-stained fingers, and about the particular quality of silence on a street that knows how to keep its mouth shut.

* * *

That night I did a thing I am still not sure how to name. I laid the stolen preliminary study on my desk beside the official Crown survey—the one in the locked drawer Vasseur trusted me with—and compared them line by line, sounding by sounding, as Delacroix had taught us to compare charts: not looking for differences, but asking of each difference, *who benefits?*

Every discrepancy answered the same question. Where the Crown's chart showed shoaling, Delacroix showed depth—and depth meant the naval anchorage could expand, which meant the seawall quarter's warehouses and homes were suddenly valuable, which meant the demolition order was not engineering. It was acquisition wearing engineering's coat. The falsified survey had been commissioned, paid for, and signed by men whose names I knew, and its purpose was to convert thirty families' homes into Crown property at the cost of a little ink and one inconvenient mapmaker.

I poured myself the last of a bottle I had been saving for a promotion celebration that now seemed unlikely ever to occur, and I drank it not celebrating, and I asked myself the question that has followed me since like my own shadow:

What is a uniform?

At the academy they gave us an answer. The uniform is the King's trust made visible; wear it and your word carries his weight; the cloth stands between chaos and order, and you stand inside the cloth. I believed it at nineteen. I believed it at twenty-four, when I pinned on the lieutenant's bars. I had even, until Tuesday, believed it at twenty-eight.

But a uniform is also a thing that can be worn by liars. Vasseur wore one. The signatories of the false survey wore them. Somewhere in this city, if my suspicions held, were men wearing the same cloth I wore, who had arranged a mapmaker's death on wet stairs in dry weather, and the uniform had covered them as smoothly as it covered me.

If the cloth protects the liar as readily as the honest man, then what exactly am I loyal to? The King, yes—in the abstract, the way one is loyal to the tide. But the King does not read his own surveys. The King sees what the Vasseurs choose to show him. And so the uniform, which I had imagined as a bridge between the throne and the truth, was in practice a wall with the truth on the far side of it, and me standing on the wrong side, holding a stolen piece of paper against my ribs like a second heartbeat.

I thought about Delacroix in the lecture hall at Toulon, tapping a chart with his stained forefinger. *Gentlemen,* he had said, *a map is a promise. When you draw a coastline, you are promising every sailor who reads it that the land is where you say it is. Break that promise and people drown. There is no error so small that it cannot kill.*

He had been talking about sailors then. He had not known—or perhaps he had; perhaps that was why he verified his soundings three times—that the same promise extended to families on a seawall, to children in narrow streets, to apprentices carrying vellum past checkpoints with their eyes counting exits.

* * *

In the morning I wrote my first report to Vasseur. I wrote it carefully, in the language we officers use when we mean to say nothing: inquiries proceeding, archives examined, certain lines of investigation promising, no result yet fit to communicate. Every word of it true. Every sentence of it hollow. I sealed it, sent it, and then sat looking at my own hands, wondering when exactly I had learned to write that way, and horrified to discover how fluently it came.

Duty, I had always believed, was a straight road. You walk it, you reach honor at the far end. But I was beginning to understand that the road forks in places the academy's maps do not show, and that the fork I faced was this: obey the man who sent me to find the truth, or serve the truth he intended to bury. Both paths called themselves duty. Only one of them deserved the name.

Somewhere in the lanes below Saint-Victor, an apprentice with ink-stained fingers was moving through the city counting exits, carrying whatever remained of a dead man's promise. And somewhere above her, in offices with windows overlooking nothing, men were waiting for me to bring it to them.

I took out a fresh sheet of paper. Not a report. Notes—private ones, coded in the shorthand we cadets had used to pass remarks behind the lecturers' backs, which no one at the arsenal could read. I wrote down the discrepancies. I wrote down the names of the survey's signatories. I wrote, at the bottom, a single sentence I did not let myself cross out:

*The plate exists. Find it before they do.*

Then I put on my uniform—it felt heavier that morning, though the tailor swore nothing had changed—and went down into the city to hunt a ghost's apprentice, praying silently, for the first time since I was a boy, that she would prove harder to find than my orders required.

She would be. I was almost certain of it already. And some traitorous part of me, the part that had copied Delacroix's strokes into a notebook ten years ago hoping to catch his genius like a fever, hoped she would stay lost until it was too late—for my career, for my orders, for everything except the truth.

I just did not yet know how late *too late* would turn out to be. Nor did I know that the answer was already moving through the harbor in a bundle of vellum, three checkpoints ahead of me and gaining.
