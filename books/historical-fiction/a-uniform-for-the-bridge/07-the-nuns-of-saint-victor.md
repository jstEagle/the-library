## 7. The Nuns of Saint-Victor

There is a particular kind of arithmetic that grief teaches you, and it is not the arithmetic of maps. It is this: one child plus one dangerous atlas equals nowhere in Marseille safe enough to sleep.

I had run the sum three times by morning, and each time it came out worse.

Tomas slept on my cot, curled around the toy boat he refused to relinquish even unconscious, his fingers hooked through its rigging like a drowning man who had made peace with the rope. He had eaten half a loaf of yesterday's bread and asked no questions about where we were going, which told me either that he was very tired or that six-year-olds have already learned that questions are a luxury adults revoke without notice.

I could not keep him here. That was the plain truth of it. My attic room above the dead master's shop was a coffin with good light, and I meant that as no figure of speech — if the men who killed Guillaume Delacroix came looking for the survey he died protecting, they would come to the shop first, and I would rather not have a child in arm's reach when they kicked the door. The atlas under the floorboards was my burden. Tomas had carried enough of it already, tucked inside a toy boat across half the city.

Which left Saint-Victor.

* * *

The abbey of Saint-Victor sits above the harbor like an old woman leaning over a railing to watch the ships, and it has watched everything for fifteen centuries — raids, plagues, sieges, and the smaller daily disasters of fishermen's wives — so I reasoned that one more secret would hardly strain its memory. The nuns there had a reputation, which is to say they had none at all, which in Marseille is the highest recommendation available.

Mother Anselme received me in the parlor off the cloister, a cold stone room smelling of beeswax and dried lavender, and she did so only after making me wait forty minutes on a bench clearly designed for penitents with bad knees. I understood the tactic. I respected it. I also hated her a little for it, which I suspect was the intent.

She was small, Mother Anselme. Small the way a keel is small — most of her below the waterline. Her hands were folded on the table between us, and they were the hands of a woman who had scrubbed floors for sixty years and would scrub them for sixty more, and God help anyone who mistook them for gentle.

"Mademoiselle Delacroix." She said my name the way one reads a bill of lading. "You wish the abbey to take charge of a child."

"Temporarily. A week. Perhaps two."

"You wish us to feed him, house him, and ask nothing."

"I wish you to do exactly what you always do, Mother, which I am given to understand is considerably more than what you admit to doing."

One eyebrow moved. It was the most dramatic event of the interview so far, and she let it stand alone for a long moment before speaking again.

"The boy. His name?"

"Tomas."

"His family?"

"That," I said, "is complicated."

"Everything is complicated." She unfolded her hands, which I recognized, from watching my master negotiate with port officials, as the gesture that precedes either a bargain or a dismissal. "Complicated is the word people use when they want something without paying for it. The Church has fed complicated children for a thousand years, mademoiselle, but it does not do so blind. Tell me why he matters, and I will tell you whether we can help."

"He doesn't matter," I said. "That's the point. He's nobody. An orphan left on a doorstep."

"Whose doorstep?"

"Mine."

"Ah." She said it softly, terribly, the way a surgeon says *ah* over a wound you'd hoped she wouldn't notice. "And orphans are customarily left on doorsteps chosen at random, are they? With notes? With drawings clutched in their fists?" She leaned forward. "Child, I am seventy-one years old. I buried my husband in the plague year and took vows at thirty because grief needed somewhere to live. Do not bring me your nobody from nowhere. Bring me the truth, or take the boy back down the hill and pray the harbor watch is feeling charitable."

I should have walked out. Any sensible smuggler walks out when the price goes up mid-negotiation. But Tomas was asleep on my cot with salt-crusted cheeks and a boat full of treason, and sensible had stopped being available to me sometime around the night a man I loved like a father was pulled from the harbor with his fingers broken.

So I paid.

* * *

I will not record here everything I told her. Some confessions belong to the person who hears them, and Mother Anselme kept hers behind eyes like shuttered windows. But I told her the shape of it: that Guillaume Delacroix had been my master for eleven years; that he had spent the last two of them secretly surveying the seawall quarter; that the Crown wanted that survey destroyed along with the homes it described; that he had been found in the water in March with his hands broken and his ledgers gone; that I had spent four months finishing what he started; and that a child had appeared at my door carrying the one plate missing from the atlas, drawn in a code I half-recognized and could not yet read.

I told her all of this in the flat voice I use for cargo manifests, and she listened without interrupting, which is rarer than honesty.

When I finished, she was quiet for the length of three bells somewhere below us in the crypts, and then she said:

"And you believe the boy is connected to your master."

"I don't know how. There's a resemblance around the eyes. And the code — the drawing is in his hand, or someone trained in his hand. Someone who knew him well enough to learn the cipher he never taught me."

"Someone who feared the same men he feared."

"Yes."

"And you cannot protect him alone."

It was not a question, but I answered it anyway, because that was the toll. "No. I can hide paper. I've hidden paper since I was twelve years old — rolled in vellum, sewn into hems, painted over with landscapes. Paper doesn't cry in the night. Paper doesn't need feeding. Paper doesn't look up at you with his mother's— " I stopped. "With someone's eyes, and ask if he can stay forever."

Mother Anselme studied me for a long time. Then she rose, went to the window, and looked down at the harbor, and I realized she was giving me privacy to compose my face, which was the single kindest thing anyone in authority had done for me in years.

"There were fugitives here during the last war," she said, still facing the window. "Huguenots, mostly, moving north. We hid them in the crypt among the bones, because no soldier searches a crypt twice — the first search frightens him and the second bores him. My predecessor, Mother Hyacinthe, ran it for six years and never lost a soul." She turned back. "You thought you were coming to beg a favor from strangers. You were not. You were coming home to work other women already finished."

"I didn't know."

"No one knows. That is the entire discipline of it." She sat back down and folded her scrubbing hands. "Here is my price, mademoiselle, and it is not the confession, which you gave freely once you understood the bill. My price is this: you will come to him. Not send letters — letters burn. Come yourself, every few days, openly, as a cartographer selling charts to the abbey, which we do purchase, being fond of maps of holy places. Let the boy see your face often enough that you remain real to him. A child who is hidden forgets he exists unless someone remembers him out loud."

"That's all? Confession and visits?"

"That is everything, and it is more than you think." She stood, and the audience was visibly over. "You believe you are leaving him with me. You are not. You are entrusting him to a conspiracy older than the Crown that wants your atlas, and conspiracies keep their own accounts. Now — go and fetch him. And mademoiselle."

I paused at the door.

"The man who broke your master's fingers. When you find him—" she said *when*, not *if*, and I felt it land somewhere under my ribs, "—do not become him. Vengeance is a map with one road on it, and every road ends at the same place. I have seen it. Draw yourself something better."

* * *

Tomas woke badly, the way children wake who have learned that waking means the world has changed again while they slept. His hand went to the boat first, then to my sleeve, and only after confirming both did he permit himself to be fully conscious.

"We're going up the hill," I told him. "To a church with old ladies in black. They have soup."

"Is it good soup?"

"Tomas, I have eaten that soup. It is the soup against which all other soups will be measured and found wanting."

He considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "Will you stay too?"

"A few days. Then I have to work."

"Work on the picture?"

My stomach performed a maneuver the harbor pilots would have admired. "What picture?"

"The secret one." He said it the way children say things, which is to say loudly, in a public street, at a volume calculated to reach every informer within a quarter mile. I steered him into an alley with a hand on his shoulder and crouched to his level.

"Tomas. Listen. The picture is like the boat. Very precious, very secret, and we don't talk about it outside. Not with anyone. Not even nice old ladies." I held his eyes until he nodded. "Especially not with soldiers. If a soldier asks you anything, you're my nephew and you're bored and you hate me. Can you hate me convincingly?"

A slow, mischievous light came into those too-familiar eyes. "Yes."

"Show me."

"You're *mean*," he announced, at astonishing volume, folding his arms, "and your bread is stale and I *hate* your face."

"Perfect. Never change."

We climbed the hill toward Saint-Victor in the late gold light, the harbor falling away beneath us, the whole glittering treacherous city spread out like a chart I was no longer permitted to trust. At the gate, Mother Anselme waited with the expression of a woman who has already decided everything and is merely permitting events to catch up.

She looked at Tomas. Tomas looked at her. Something passed between them that excluded me entirely — some treaty ratified in the ancient currency of children and old women.

"He'll do," she said.

Then she took his hand, and he went with her without a backward glance, and I discovered that being relieved and bereaved simultaneously is a sensation for which the language offers no adequate term.

* * *

I told myself, walking back down the hill alone, that the arrangement was temporary, practical, reversible. A week. Two. Until I could decode the plate and move the atlas.

But at the bottom of the steps I stopped, because a soldier stood at the corner of the Rue Sainte talking to the customs clerk from Checkpoint One — the bored clerk whose flattery had bought my passage two days past — and beside the clerk, arms crossed, stood a young officer in a blue uniform coat, studying the foot traffic with the patient thoroughness of a man reading a document line by line.

He looked up the hill. Toward the abbey. Toward me.

I became intensely interested in a fruit stall and bought figs I did not want from a vendor I overpaid, and when I glanced back the officer was still looking uphill, and he was not looking at the abbey.

He was looking at the space where I had been standing.

Lieutenant Roche, though I did not yet know his name, had just added a second face to whatever list he kept behind his academy-trained eyes, and the soup at Saint-Victor would have to be very good indeed, because the month ahead suddenly tasted of iron.
