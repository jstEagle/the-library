## 8. The Demolition Order

The drums began before dawn.

I knew that sound the way I knew the creak of my master's floorboards, the way I knew the particular sigh a quill makes when it dies mid-stroke. Marseille has many languages—Provençal in the fish market, Italian on the docks, the clipped Latin of the priests—but the language of drums is universal, and it never means anything good.

I was at my table with the atlas open before me, inking the last of the Sainte-Marie headland, when the first drumbeat rolled up from the Vieux-Port like a stone dropped into a well. The ink pooled where I'd stopped. A blot, right over the little church I had drawn so carefully. Later I would decide it looked like a wound. At the time I only thought, *You've ruined the plate*, because grief is a coward and hides behind small complaints.

Tomas was still asleep in the widow Couteau's rooms below—I had not yet worked out how to say his name without my voice doing something embarrassing—and so I went down alone into the street to hear what the Crown wanted at an hour when even the gulls hadn't finished their arguments.

The square by the customs house was filling with people pulled out of their doors like teeth from a jaw. Fishwives still in their aprons. Stevedores with rope burns on their palms. Old men who had come to stand, as old men do, precisely where they could see best and be seen least. And there in front of the customs house, on a platform that had been assembled overnight with a speed the city had never once extended to anything useful, stood a captain of the royal engineers with a paper in his hand and a face like a man reading someone else's eulogy.

He read it badly. That is the thing I remember most—the badness of it. He stumbled twice over "in perpetuity," which I suppose no one enjoys saying.

"By order of His Most Christian Majesty," he announced, "the seawall quarter, from the Gate of the Arsenal to the salt warehouses inclusive, having been surveyed and found unsound upon ground unfit for habitation, shall be cleared of all structures and all persons within thirty days' time."

Thirty days.

I did the arithmetic the way one does arithmetic in nightmares—quickly, correctly, and with a growing certainty that the numbers were lying to you. Thirty days. Four Sundays. Nine hundred or so souls along the seawall, if you counted the children who were counted and the ones who weren't. Baptiste's room above the chandlery. The widow Couteau's two rooms and her garden of herbs grown in barrels because the soil was salt-killed. The bakery. The net-mender's shed. Every door I could draw from memory with my eyes shut.

*Surveyed,* he said. *Found unsound.* I felt the words land on me like hands. There had been a survey. My master had made it. It said the opposite. And it had gotten him killed for the trouble, drowned in four feet of harbor water with his ledger weighted in his coat—a detail the coroner had called misadventure, because coroners are paid to know nothing.

Around me the crowd made the sound crowds make when catastrophe arrives wearing official livery: not screaming, not yet. A low murmur, the sound of several hundred people simultaneously realizing that the map they lived on had been redrawn without them.

A woman near me—I think she was a laundress, arms red to the elbow—said it first, quietly, to no one: "Unsound. My mother was born in that house. Is my mother unsound?"

Nobody laughed. It wasn't a joke. It was a diagnosis, and everyone within earshot understood that we had just been declared a disease.

* * *

I do not remember walking home. I remember arriving, and I remember Tomas awake and sitting on the floor with his toy boat, and I remember the drums still going, moving through the quarters of the city like a search party, announcing the same sentence to every square until the whole city had heard it and could begin the private work of disbelieving it.

"Elodie." He held up the boat. "They woke me up."

"I know."

"Why?"

Because a king three hundred leagues away had looked at your home on a piece of paper and seen not homes but an obstruction, I did not say. Because paper is the most dangerous substance in the world and ink is its ammunition, and I am sitting on enough of both to hang us all.

"There's going to be work done on the harbor," I said, which was true in the sense that a flood is going to involve water.

"The loud kind?"

"Yes."

He considered this with the gravity of a six-year-old evaluating weather. "Can we watch?"

"No."

"Can Baptiste watch?"

"Baptiste lives where the noise will be."

That stopped him. Children understand displacement faster than adults do—they have less furniture of habit to hide behind. He looked down at the boat in his lap, then at me, and asked the question with the terrible directness of the very young:

"Will our house fall down?"

"No," I said, and discovered I meant it as a promise rather than a prediction, which frightened me more than any drumbeat. "No house of ours is falling down. Come here. Help me with something."

What I handed him was a rag and the job of cleaning my brushes, which he does badly and loves completely. What I actually gave him was something to hold while the world rearranged itself. It is the oldest trick in the caretaker's book, and I learned it from watching a dead man do it for me.

While his small hands worked, I opened the atlas to the final plate—the one that had come to me clutched in this same child's fists, coded in my master's shorthand, the survey of the seawall quarter showing bedrock under every foundation the Crown now claimed was marsh. Solid ground. Stone under Baptiste's room. Stone under the widow's herb barrels. Stone under the bakery where Tomas had eaten his first bread since his mother stopped feeding him.

The king's engineers had surveyed the same ground and found swamp. Both surveys could not be true. One set of men had lied, and the other set of men had died for noticing.

Thirty days. Somewhere in this city was the man who had signed the falsified report, drinking his morning wine in a uniform I would learn to hate by its cut alone. And somewhere in this city—no, not somewhere; beneath these exact floorboards—was the document that could prove him a murderer and save nine hundred people, if I could find a way to move it through three military checkpoints into the hands of anyone with the power and the spine to read it aloud.

I closed the atlas. Underneath the floorboards it went, into the dark between the joists, next to the memory of the man who had hidden it there first.

* * *

Baptiste heard the proclamation from the top of a bollard, which was where he heard most things worth hearing, being a man of forty years who had never once accepted information secondhand if climbing could get it firsthand.

"They read it pretty," he told the chandlery crowd afterward, perched on a barrel with the gravity of a pulpit. "Nice voice. Good lungs. Said 'unsound' like it was a flavor. Then the drums went off and half the soldiers looked embarrassed, which tells you what you need to know about soldiers—they're not stupid, they're just arranged."

"What do we do?" This from young Marius, whose family kept the bakery and whose flour-dusted hands were shaking around a loaf he'd forgotten he was holding.

"Do?" Baptiste spat thoughtfully off the barrel. "First we do nothing, because panic is free labor for whoever wants your house. Then—" and here he lowered his voice, though the whole street leaned in anyway, "—then somebody finds out who wrote the report that says we live on marsh. Because I've slept on that ground my whole life, friends, and let me tell you about the marsh. The marsh doesn't exist. I've hauled stone across that quarter in carts that would sink to the axles if there were so much as honest mud under it. Somebody drew us a lie, and lies can be drawn back."

He didn't say the word *survey*. He didn't need to. Half the harbor knew the story of the mapmaker who drowned with his ledger in his coat, and the other half knew better than to say it aloud. But eyes met eyes along the street, and a silence passed among them like contraband.

That evening Baptiste came to my door. He stood in my doorway holding his cap, a big careful man trying to make himself smaller, and said, without preamble: "Mademoiselle Delacroix. Your master was my friend. Not close—we didn't drink together, he didn't drink—but I carried his cases off the fishing boats for eleven years and he always paid me in drawings. Little ones. Coastlines. I've got a wall of them." He swallowed. "The boy's been fed. Widow Couteau's got him. I'm not here about the boy."

"No?"

"I'm here about the paper." His eyes went past my shoulder, to the floor, with the accuracy of a man who had guessed exactly where a secret sleeps. "There's a copy of that survey somewhere. There has to be. Men like him don't die without insurance."

"And if there were such a paper," I said carefully, "what would you want with it?"

"A copy. Just the part that shows the ground under my building. Proof my home stands on rock, not marsh—that's all I'd ask. In exchange—" he straightened, and the stevedore fell away and something older and harder stood in his place, something that had moved cargo past customs officers for twenty years without once paying duty, "—I can move anything you need to move through this harbor. Past the arsenal gate, past the watch boats, past men with rifles and short tempers. I know which inspectors eat late. I know which boats get boarded and which get waved through. I know the night the tide covers the third step of the north stair and the sentry goes under cover to smoke."

I looked at this man I had exchanged nods with for years and understood, with a strange sinking relief, that I had never been invisible at all. Neither had he. We had simply agreed, the whole seawall quarter of us, to perform invisibility together, the way a congregation performs silence.

"You'd risk that," I said, "for one page."

"For one page," Baptiste said, "I'd risk the other twenty-nine days too. Because mademoiselle, I've been thinking about what happens after the drums stop. They clear the quarter. They build their grand fortifications. And ten years from now some clerk writes down that the seawall quarter was empty marsh, and nobody left alive to say otherwise. Paper's the only witness that doesn't die." He put his cap on. "Think on it. I'll come back tomorrow. Bring me a yes or bring me nothing, but either way—" he glanced down at the floorboards, and smiled for the first time, "—put a rug over that. Even the widow knows."

* * *

Roche heard the proclamation too, from the parade ground, standing at attention while the engineers' captain read and the drums spoke and three hundred soldiers pretended this was routine.

He was thinking about handwriting.

It was a small thing, a professional tic—he'd been praised for it at the academy, the instructor who could identify a forged requisition by the way a man crossed his sevens. And the engineer's report posted that afternoon beside the proclamation, the official survey justifying the demolition, bore a signature that sat wrong on the page. Too much flourish. A man signing his own name doesn't decorate it; he's written it ten thousand times and grown bored of it. Only a man performing someone else's name adds the flourishes.

And beneath the signature, in the body of the survey, the descriptive notes—the little annotations about soil and soundings—were in a hand Roche knew. Knew intimately. Had studied under, once, in a classroom smelling of ink and chalk, learning coastal cartography from a brilliant, difficult, unbending old man whose sevens were crossed with a single savage stroke.

Delacroix's notes. Delacroix's method. Copied, altered, inverted—and signed by someone else.

Lieutenant Roche stood at attention in the autumn sun while the drums rolled, and felt the ground shift under him, which was fitting, since the entire affair was allegedly about ground. His orders were clear. Find the missing original survey. Find whoever possessed it. The demolition would proceed; his conscience was not a consideration listed anywhere in the articles of war.

But he had known the old man. Had eaten dinner at his table once, awkwardly, while the master explained why a coastline drawn from ambition instead of observation was a form of murder with a delay built in.

*A form of murder with a delay built in.*

The drums stopped. The captain dismissed them. Roche turned toward his office, where the falsified report waited on his desk for his endorsement, and walked slowly, a man already halfway to treason and discovering, to his considerable surprise, that the road was comfortable.

* * *

That night I lit one candle and took the atlas out from under the floorboards one more time—not to work, but to look. To look at the seawall quarter as my master had drawn it: not as parcels and structures and obstructions, the way the king's men saw it, but the way he always drew inhabited places. Every roof drawn slightly warm at the edges, as if the buildings themselves were keeping something alive inside them. Every door marked. Every window. He had mapped a neighborhood the way you map a heartbeat.

Thirty days.

Somewhere out there, drums were still rolling through the last quarters of the city, telling the rest of Marseille what had been decided about us. And here in my room, a boy slept in the widow's bed below with a toy boat under his arm, on solid stone that three governments' worth of paper insisted was marsh.

Tomorrow Baptiste would come for his answer, and I would give it. Tomorrow I would begin learning the geography of checkpoints, tides, and lies.

Tonight I sharpened a pen, and started making copies.
