## 9. Dockworkers' Ledger

There is a particular arithmetic a stevedore learns before he learns his letters, and it goes like this: a barrel of salt cod weighs two hundred pounds, a man's back can carry one hundred fifty of those pounds for six hours, and the difference between what a man can carry and what he must carry is paid for in years. I have been paying that difference since I was eleven years old, when my father's back gave out at the Quai de la Joliette and the harbor took me up the way the sea takes a dropped coin—without ceremony, without giving it back.

My name is Baptiste Ferrand. I am forty-one years old, I have hands like cured leather, and until three nights ago my ledger was simple: loads carried, wages earned, wine owed to Marthe at the Cabanon, wine drunk instead. Then a woman with ink-stained fingers and a boy who wouldn't look anyone in the eye came down to the docks asking after me by name, and the ledger got complicated.

* * *

You have to understand something about the seawall quarter before you understand why I did what I did. The Crown's surveyors—and I use that word the way you'd use a curse in church—have declared our homes stand on marshland. Sinking ground. Ground too soft to bear the weight of the new fortifications they mean to build. That's what the proclamation says, anyway, read out loud at the customs house by a clerk with a voice like wet paper: *unstable terrain, unsuitable foundations, demolition within the month.*

I have lived on that ground for forty-one years. My father lived on it for fifty. The house on the Rue des Salins has stood through three storms that flattened warehouses on "solid" ground up the hill. If that's marsh, then Marseille is built on clouds and we're all walking on air.

But here's the thing about paper: paper doesn't care what your knees know. Paper says marsh, and marsh it is, unless somebody puts different paper against it. And the only different paper in all of Provence—the only survey of that ground done honestly, done stone by stone and sounding by sounding—belonged to a dead mapmaker whose death nobody talked about above a whisper, and to an apprentice woman who was either the bravest fool in the city or the most dangerous.

So when she came looking for me, I didn't ask how she knew my name. I asked her upstairs to the loft over the chandlery where the dockmen keep their accounts, and I put wine in front of her because a bargain goes better with wine, even a bargain between strangers.

She set the boy on a crate. He held a toy boat against his chest like a shield and watched me with eyes too old for his face. I've seen that look before—in men coming off a bad voyage, in women waiting for husbands who won't come home. It's the look of someone who has learned that adults are weather.

"You're Baptiste Ferrand," she said. Not a question.

"I'm him."

"They say you know every route through this harbor that isn't written on any chart."

"I know some routes." I poured myself a cup too. "The ones written on charts are the ones they watch."

"And the ones not written?"

"Those are the ones that cost."

She didn't flinch. I'll give her that. Most people flinch when you name a price before you've named the goods. She just looked at me with those ink-stained fingers curled around her cup, and she said, "What would it cost?"

And here is where I should tell you what I'd already decided, sitting in that loft three nights earlier, when the demolition proclamation went up and old Yvette from the Rue des Salins came to the harbor crying so hard she could barely say the words. I had decided that if the mapmaker's ghost sent anyone to me, I would trade. But I hadn't known what I wanted until she asked, and when she asked, it came out of me like blood from a reopened cut:

"A copy. Of the survey. The true one—the soundings, the borings, the depth of the bedrock under the Rue des Salins. A copy I can hold in my hand and put in front of whatever council will listen. That's my price. Not coin. Paper."

* * *

She stared at me. The boy stared at me. Even the candle flame seemed to lean in.

"Most men," she said slowly, "would ask for money."

"Money spends. Paper proves." I leaned forward. "Mademoiselle—or madame, or whatever you are—they've declared my street is marsh. Marsh means no compensation worth having, because you don't pay much to clear a swamp. But if that ground is rock—if the survey shows what my father's bones already know—then it's not clearance, it's expropriation, and expropriation pays, or it can be fought in the courts, or it can be shouted about in the council chambers until somebody with a wig gets embarrassed enough to listen. I need the truth in ink. You need routes past three checkpoints. It seems to me God arranged this meeting, though He's never struck me as organized before."

Something moved across her face—not quite a smile, but the place where a smile had been living recently and might come back. "You understand what you're asking me to copy? What happens to people caught with that paper?"

"I understand that the last man who owned it is dead," I said, "and I understand that dead men's truths are heavier than live men's lies, and somebody has to carry them. I've carried two hundred pounds of salt cod up a gangplank in a mistral. I can carry paper."

"It's not the weight," she said quietly. "It's what it does to you after. My master—" She stopped. Started again. "The man who made that survey died for it. And not quickly."

The loft went very still. Outside, the harbor made its usual noises—rigging knocking, water slapping pilings, a gull arguing with God about something—but inside, nothing moved except the boy's thumb, rubbing the little wooden hull of his boat.

"I'm sorry," I said, and meant it.

"So am I." She set down her cup. "Every day. But sorry doesn't finish an atlas. So. You'll take us through?"

"Three checkpoints." I held up fingers, calloused as rope-burn. "The customs house, the arsenal gate, the harbor watch. Each one worse than the last, because each one closer to the water, and the water is where the real soldiers sit—the ones who shoot first and fill out forms after. But there are ways. There's always a way; the harbor is full of holes, it's just that most holes have eyes in them now. Give me two days. Two days, and I'll have a path mapped that no chart in Versailles knows exists."

"And the copies?"

"I'll have them ready when you come back. As many as you can carry." I looked at the boy then, and I don't know why—I'm not a man given to children, they're loud and sticky and they ask questions—but I said to him, "You like boats, petit?"

He nodded, small, careful.

"That one yours?"

Another nod.

"My father made me one once. Carved it from a broken oar. I sailed it off the end of the pier and it sank in four feet of water and I cried like the end of the world." I shrugged. "Turns out the world kept going. First useful thing the harbor ever taught me."

The boy almost smiled. Almost. It was like watching a door open one inch in a wall you'd thought was solid.

* * *

Two days. I spent them the way I spend everything—on my back, mostly, hauling cargo, but with my ears working double. That's the other thing a stevedore learns: the harbor talks. Every winch creak, every bored guard's complaint, every clerk's cough carries along the quays like news along a washing line. By the second evening I knew which checkpoint had doubled its watches, which sergeant drank, which lieutenant had been seen asking questions about a dead mapmaker with a face like a man chewing on his own conscience.

That last one interested me. Lieutenant Roche. Young. Academy manners. The kind of officer who salutes cleanly and looks slightly ill doing it. I'd seen him at the arsenal gate twice, watching the traffic go through with eyes that counted things they weren't supposed to count. A dangerous man, or a useful one. Sometimes the same thing.

I also spent the evenings making copies. Well—not making them myself; my handwriting looks like a spider drowned in the inkwell. No, I paid old Séverin, the retired harbor pilot who drinks at the Cabanon and once copied charts for thirty years before his eyes went soft at the edges. His eyes are soft but his hands remember. For the price of good wine and the pleasure of conspiracy—which, for a man of seventy, is worth more than wine—he sat in my room copying the pages Elodie Delacroix lent me, page by page, while I stood watch at the window feeling like a man holding a lit fuse.

And I'll tell you what I saw in those pages, because it matters. Soundings taken at forty points along the seawall. Borings showing limestone bedrock at depths that would make a cathedral architect weep with envy. Notes in a fine, precise hand—*strata firm, bearing excellent, no subsidence observed in 40 years of record*—each line a small hammer blow against the lie the Crown had bought. The man hadn't just surveyed the ground. He'd documented it the way you document a crime scene. Because that's what it was. A crime scene, drawn in beautiful ink, waiting years for someone to read it aloud.

Séverin copied five sets. Five. One for me, one for the widow Couteau—who, it turns out, has been hiding more than her late husband's good silver these twenty years, and who received her copy with a nod like a queen accepting tribute—one for the nuns, one for the priest at Saint-Laurent, and one wrapped in oilcloth and buried under the third flagstone of my floor, because a stevedore trusts the ground under his house more than any institution above it.

* * *

She came back on the third night, alone this time, moving along the shadowed side of the chandlery like she'd been born to it. Maybe she had. Grief teaches stealth faster than any smuggler.

"You have them?" she asked.

I put the packet in her hands. Watched her thumb the edges of the pages, check the count, check the hand. Careful. This was a woman who checked everything now; the world had taught her the cost of assuming.

"Five copies," I said. "One's yours to do with as you please. The rest are planted around the city like seeds. Burn my quarter down, and four copies bloom in places that will embarrass the Crown for a decade."

"You realize," she said, tucking the packet into her coat, "that what you've just described is treason."

"What I've just described," I said, "is a ledger. They keep ledgers; why shouldn't we? Theirs says marsh, demolish, forget. Ours says rock, remain, remember. When the accounts come due, I want to be holding the honest column." I shrugged. "Besides, treason requires a crown worth betraying. I'm loyal to the harbor. Always have been. The harbor feeds me, the harbor buries my father, and the harbor doesn't lie about what's under its streets."

She laughed then—a short, surprised sound, like she'd startled herself with it. "You're not what I expected, Baptiste Ferrand."

"Nobody ever is. That's the whole trouble with expectations." I jerked my chin toward the dark mouth of the alley behind the chandlery. "Now. Your route. Listen close, because I'll only say it once, and then it lives in your head or nowhere."

I walked her through it, step by step, the way my father taught me the harbor's veins: the coal chute behind the ropewalk that comes out inside the customs house yard itself—past the first checkpoint without touching it, if the chute's grate hasn't been relocked since Tuesday, and I'd know by tomorrow if it had. The fish carts that cross the arsenal gate at dawn, so thick with scales and stink that no soldier searches deeper than the top layer—and a toy boat, packed among the mackerel, is just another piece of the catch. And for the harbor watch, the fishing boat *Sainte-Anne*, old Guillaume's tub, whose holds have moved more untaxed brandy than the entire French navy has moved honor.

"Three checkpoints," I finished. "Three holes. But hear me, mademoiselle—" and I took her arm, gently, because some things you say holding on. "Holes close. Guards get promoted, grates get locked, boats get seized. Whatever night you choose, choose soon, and choose knowing that if it goes wrong, I was never part of it. I'm a stevedore. I carry things. What those things are, I forget the moment they leave my hands."

"Understood." She looked at me for a long moment in the dark. "Why?" she said finally. "You have a life here. A safe one. Why risk it for strangers?"

I thought about my answer before I gave it, because she deserved a true one.

"When I was eleven," I said, "my father couldn't work anymore, and nobody helped us. Nobody. Neighbors crossed the street. The parish gave us bread and a sermon about patience. I swore then that I'd never be one of the crossing-the-street kind." I let go of her arm. "You're not strangers, mademoiselle. You're the seawall quarter. You're Yvette's kitchen smoke and the widow Couteau's shutters and that boy's little boat. The Crown drew a line on a map and called us marsh. Fine. Then we're marsh people, and marsh people stick together, because that's the one thing about us that's true no matter what their surveys say."

She didn't say anything for a moment. Then she pressed the packet flat against her chest, the way the boy presses his toy boat, and I understood that she and the child were carrying the same thing in different shapes.

"Dawn after next," she said. "The customs house first."

"Dawn after next," I agreed. "I'll be at the ropewalk before light, working the coal chute. If the grate's locked, look for chalk on the third bollard—a circle means wait, a cross means the harbor route only."

"And if there's no mark at all?"

I smiled, and it wasn't a happy smile. "Then run, mademoiselle. Run, and burn nothing, and trust the copies."

She slipped into the dark, and I stood a while in the cold coming off the water, listening to the harbor talk its endless talk.

* * *

Here's the last entry in the ledger for tonight, and I make it reluctantly, the way you write down a debt you were hoping to die before owing.

This morning, a clerk from the intendant's office came to the quay with fresh orders. The demolition crews mobilize within the week. Within the week. Not the month the proclamation promised—the week. Somebody upstairs is nervous. Somebody upstairs knows there's paper loose in this city that could undo them, and they mean to bury the evidence under rubble before it can speak.

And this evening, Lieutenant Roche walked the length of the Quai de la Joliette alone, no escort, stopping at the seawall quarter, standing a long time in front of the houses on the Rue des Salins. My street. Looking at the walls, the foundations, the ground itself, like a man reading a document written in stone.

A dangerous man, or a useful one.

Dawn after next, we find out which.
