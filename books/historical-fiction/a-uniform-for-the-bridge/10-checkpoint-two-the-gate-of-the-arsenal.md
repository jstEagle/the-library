## 10. Checkpoint Two: The Gate of the Arsenal

There is a particular quality to fear that arrives on schedule. It is worse than the kind that ambushes you. The ambush at least has the decency to be over quickly; the scheduled fear gives you three days to rehearse your own ruin.

I had spent those three days rehearsing.

*You are a mapmaker's apprentice delivering vellum to the naval engineers.* That was the story, and it was even true, which is the best kind of lie—the kind built on a foundation of fact so the whole structure holds weight when someone leans on it. Baptiste's cousin had procured me a genuine delivery chit from a genuine stationer on the Rue Caisserie, and I had dressed in my master's old coat, which hung off my shoulders like grief itself, and I had practiced saying *the engineers requested six skins, calfskin, unbleached* until the words came out smooth as varnish.

The plates themselves rode in Tomas's toy boat.

This was Baptiste's idea, and I will confess I thought it madness when he first proposed it. A toy boat—a little lugger carved from pine, with a cotton sail and a hull deep enough, as it turned out, to hold four sheets of paper rolled tight as wicks. "No soldier in Marseille will search a child's toy," Baptiste said, spitting into the harbor for punctuation. "They'll pat your skirts, they'll open your parcel, they'll make you recite your grandmother's name. But a boy holding a boat? They'll ruffle his hair and wave him through. Children are invisible, mademoiselle. You of all people should know."

He meant it kindly. He meant: *you have lived unseen for two years; use it.* But the words landed strangely, because I had spent two years believing invisibility was my armor, and here was a stevedore telling me it was also a cage—that the reason no one had ever caught me was the same reason no one had ever *seen* me, and that I had mistaken one for the other.

Tomas, for his part, took to the role with an actor's instinct I found slightly alarming in a six-year-old. He carried the boat everywhere. He slept with it. He invented a name for it—*L'Étoile*, the star—and told anyone who would listen about how his papa had given it to him before going to sea, which was not true, but which had the ring of truth, and I learned something watching him lie: that the best falsehoods are the ones the liar half-believes himself.

"Are we going through the gate today?" he asked me that morning, swinging the boat by its rigging.

"We are."

"And the men with the guns will look at us?"

"They might."

"Will they take L'Étoile?"

I crouched down to his level. This was the part I had not rehearsed—how to explain to a child that the thing he loved most might be taken apart by strangers' hands, and that if that happened, he must not cry, must not clutch, must not do any of the thousand natural things a six-year-old does when frightened.

"Tomas," I said. "Listen carefully. If a soldier wants to look inside the boat, you let him. You smile. You say, *it's only my toy, monsieur.* Can you do that?"

"What if he breaks it?"

"He won't." I said it with more certainty than I felt. "And even if he does—" I touched his chin, made him look at me. "Even if he does, we can build another. But what's inside it, we cannot build again. Do you understand? Whatever happens, whatever they say, however frightened you feel—you keep your eyes soft and your mouth quiet, and you think about the star."

He nodded. Then, because he was six and not a saint: "Can I have a pastry after?"

"After the gate, yes."

"*Two* pastries."

"One and a half."

"Baptiste said two."

"Baptiste," I said, standing, "negotiates like a stevedore. I negotiate like a mapmaker. One and a half."

He grinned, and I felt that treacherous warmth in my chest that I had been trying to evict since the night he arrived on my doorstep. It kept coming back. It had apparently signed a lease.

* * *

The Gate of the Arsenal stands where the old moat used to run, a squat arch of honey-colored stone with the royal arms carved above it and two sentry boxes flanking it like a pair of bad teeth. Beyond it lies the arsenal proper—the ropewalks, the powder stores, the long sheds where they build the galleys that row out each morning to remind everyone whose sea this is. Civilians pass through on business, but everything that passes is looked at, weighed, and written down, because the Crown's great talent is not building things but cataloguing them.

We joined the queue at mid-morning, when the sun had burned off the mist and the line was a mixture of carters, fishwives, and clerks with document cases held against their chests like breastplates. I had chosen the hour deliberately—not too early, when soldiers are fresh and suspicious, not too late, when they are bored enough to be thorough out of spite. Mid-morning, they are merely tired, and tired men want the queue gone.

The cart ahead of us was being searched. I watched a sergeant pull apart a load of onions with the delicacy of a man defusing a bomb, and I watched the carter stand there sweating, and I thought: *this is what it looks like when they find nothing. Remember this. Nothing looks like this.*

Then it was our turn.

The soldier who waved us forward was young—younger than me, probably, with the raw pink face of a boy from the mountains who had never seen the sea before the Crown posted him beside it. His musket was slung with the careless comfort of habit. He looked at me, then at Tomas, then at the delivery chit I held out.

"Purpose?"

"Delivery of vellum to the naval engineers' office. Six skins, calfskin." I produced the parcel from under my arm—the decoy, real vellum, expensive enough to be convincing, wrapped in oilcloth. "The stationer sent me because his man is ill."

The boy soldier squinted at the chit. I watched him read it at the speed of a man sounding out his letters, and I felt the first cold drop of doubt. Too young. Too careful. The bored clerk at the customs house had been easy; this one was still at the age where checking things thoroughly feels like virtue rather than tedium.

"The child is yours?"

"My brother's son. I mind him while my brother's at sea." The lie came out smooth. Smoothness, I have learned, is not the absence of fear but the mastery of it.

He looked at Tomas. Tomas looked back with enormous brown eyes and hugged L'Étoile to his chest, and I thought, *good, perfect, be adorable,* and then the soldier did the thing I had been dreading since dawn.

He held out his hand. "Let's see the toy."

Everything in me went very still and very loud at once. Somewhere behind my ribs, my heart began running calculations it had no business making: distance to the water, weight of the parcel, whether I could scream convincingly, whether screaming would help or simply summon more soldiers. None of the calculations came out in my favor. They never do. That is the arithmetic of being small against the state.

"It's only a toy, monsieur," Tomas said, exactly as rehearsed, and held up the boat.

The soldier took it. He turned it over in his hands. He ran a thumb along the hull—and I saw his thumb catch on the seam, the tiny wooden plug Baptiste had fitted to hide the opening, and I saw him notice it, and I saw the question form in his face like weather coming in over the islands.

"This opens?"

"It's where I keep the star," Tomas said.

I could have kissed him or fainted; both seemed rude. The soldier frowned, worked at the plug with his thumbnail, and I stood there in my dead master's coat doing the only useful thing left to me, which was nothing, absolutely nothing, while a boy from the mountains pried open the vessel carrying the death warrant of every family on the seawall.

The plug gave. He tilted the hull. And—

"Ho there! Vasseur!"

The shout came from inside the gate. A lieutenant strode toward us across the cobbles, and I knew him before I knew him—before my mind finished assembling the recognition, my body had already gone cold, because I had seen this face before, at the customs house, three days ago, standing at the edge of the crowd with the stillness of a man who watches rather than waits.

Lieutenant Roche. The officer who had noted our faces at Checkpoint One. The officer whose entire assignment, if Baptiste's sources were right, was finding exactly what was currently sitting in a mountain boy's hands.

"You're wanted at the powder store," Roche said to the soldier. "Now. I'll finish here."

"But sir, I've just—"

"Now, Vasseur. The count doesn't balance and Captain Berthier is asking whose name is on the ledger."

Vasseur went pale in the way of soldiers suddenly mentioned in proximity to ledgers, thrust the boat back into Tomas's arms without re-plugging it—I heard the loose roll of paper shift inside, a sound like a whisper, a sound I would hear in my sleep for weeks—and trotted off toward the powder store.

Roche turned to us.

Up close he was younger than his bearing suggested, with ink-stained fingers and the kind of tired eyes that come from reading too much and sleeping too little. He picked up my delivery chit. He read it—actually read it, at full speed, which told me his academy masters had been worth their salt—and then he looked at the manifest clipped beneath it, the stationer's list of goods.

"Six skins, calfskin, unbleached," he read aloud. "One dozen goose quills, cut. One pound iron-gall ink." He glanced up at me. "Heavy order."

"The engineers draw large," I said. My voice did not shake. I have never been prouder of anything.

"Mm." He studied the manifest a moment longer. Then he said, in a tone of mild bureaucratic regret: "This lists seven skins."

My blood stopped. "I beg your pardon?"

"Seven." He tapped the paper. "See—the figure's smudged, but it's a seven. Your chit says six. There's a discrepancy." He sighed, pulled a pencil from his coat, and struck through the number with two quick strokes. "Stationer's error. I'll correct it to six to match your chit. Can't have the arsenal ledgers arguing with themselves; Berthier would have the whole office flogged." He initialed the correction, handed everything back, and stepped aside. "Pass through. Mind the child doesn't run near the ropewalk."

And that was all. That was all. We walked under the arch, between the bad teeth of the sentry boxes, into the arsenal, and no one stopped us, and the plates rolled softly in their hiding place against Tomas's hip, and the sun shone on the cobbles as though nothing whatsoever had occurred.

* * *

It was not until we were three streets beyond the gate, in the shadow of the cathedral, that I let myself stop walking. Tomas immediately demanded his pastry. I bought him two—one and a half having been forgotten in the general collapse of my negotiating principles—and sat him on a step with them, and then I stood there in the fish-market stink trying to understand what had happened to me.

Because here is the thing I could not stop turning over, the stone in my shoe, the splinter under the nail:

He lied.

Not bent the truth. Not overlooked. *Lied*—deliberately, fluently, with a pencil stroke and an initial, the way a man lies who has decided in advance to lie and has chosen the method carefully. Seven skins. There was no seven. I had written that manifest myself, or rather Baptiste's cousin had, and I had checked it eleven times. There was no discrepancy. There was no smudge. Lieutenant Roche had invented an error out of clean air and then corrected it, and in doing so had given himself a reason to touch the papers, close the question, and move us along before anyone else could look.

Why?

Three possibilities presented themselves, and I examined each like a specimen.

First: he had not recognized me, and his lie was mere laziness—he couldn't be bothered with a search and manufactured a shortcut. Possible. But a lazy man does not invent a specific false number; a lazy man waves you through and forgets you. The pencil work had been deliberate. Deliberate is not lazy.

Second: he had recognized me, remembered the customs house, and was playing a longer game—letting us pass so he could follow, catalogue our contacts, and take us all at once with the net cast wide. This possibility I liked least, because it fit the facts best. Officers who hesitate are often officers gathering evidence. Mercy and strategy wear the same face at checkpoints.

Third—and this was the one that kept me awake that night, staring at the ceiling of my master's old room while Tomas slept in the bed that had been mine: third, he had recognized something else entirely. Not my face. The hand. My master's hand, on the coastlines, on the survey notes, on anything he'd ever seen from the atlas—for Roche had been at the academy, Baptiste had learned that much, had studied under men who traded in maps, and a man who loves maps knows hands the way priests know voices.

If he had recognized the master's work—if he had understood, in that moment at the gate, exactly what was rolling loose inside a child's toy—then he had not merely let us pass. He had chosen a side. Quietly, with a pencil, in front of a subordinate, he had committed a small treason, and he had done it for reasons I could not see and therefore could not trust.

Gratitude, I discovered, is an uncomfortable emotion when you cannot verify its object. It sits in the chest like swallowed smoke. I owed this man my freedom, possibly my life, certainly the atlas—and I did not know whether he was my ally or my hunter, whether the pencil stroke had been mercy or bait. Suspicion and thankfulness fought in me like cats in a sack, and neither would win, and I began to suspect they never would.

"Tomas," I said. He looked up, sugar-glazed. "The man at the gate. The tall one who took the soldier away. Have you seen him before? Think carefully."

Tomas considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "He came to the widow's door," he said. "Before. When I lived with Mama. He talked to her on the stairs." He licked jam off his thumb. "She cried after he left. But she smiled when she saw him coming. She always smiled when she saw him coming."

The pastry went dry in my own mouth.

He had known her. The daughter—the woman who had left a child on my doorstep with a note that said *forget the face*—had known Lieutenant Roche. Had smiled at him. Had cried after he left. Which meant the officer who had just saved her son at the gate of the arsenal was not a stranger performing an inexplicable kindness.

He was a man settling a debt—or collecting one.

I looked back toward the arsenal, at the honey-colored arch now distant in the haze, and I understood that the game had changed shape entirely. I had thought I was smuggling paper past the Crown. I was beginning to suspect I was a piece moving on a board whose other players I had never agreed to meet.

"Finish your pastry," I told Tomas, and took his hand, and walked him home the long way, watching every shadow, including—especially—the ones that fell from uniforms.
