## 11. The Master's Letters

There is a particular kind of silence that lives in a dead man's desk. Not the ordinary quiet of an empty room—rooms are never truly empty in Marseille, what with the gulls arguing over the rooftops and the fishwives conducting their negotiations at a volume that could strip barnacles—but the specific, held-breath silence of paper that has been waiting for someone to read it.

I had been avoiding that desk for eleven months.

Not out of laziness. Out of cowardice, if I'm honest, though I prefer the word *prudence*, which is what cowards call cowardice when they can afford better tailoring. Master Guillaume Delacroix—no relation, whatever the neighbors assumed from the shared surname; he took me on when I was fourteen and let the mistake stand because he found it funny—had died with his affairs arranged like one of his own maps: everything in its place, nothing labeled where a stranger might find it.

The atlas lived under my floorboards now, three-quarters finished, the coastlines inked in his hand and the interior details waiting for mine. The final plate—the seawall survey, the one that had gotten him killed, the one a six-year-old had carried to my door folded inside his coat like a love letter from a ghost—lay beside it, matched to his notes at last. I knew what the two documents together could do. I knew whose houses stood or fell depending on which version of the harbor's foundations reached the Crown.

What I did not know, until tonight, was why the boy had been sent to *me*.

* * *

It started, as most of my troubles do, with Tomas being too quiet.

Children have two silences. There is the silence of a child absorbed in something—a beetle, a stolen sugar lump, the fascinating question of how much water a boot can hold before the owner notices. And there is the silence of a child who has learned that being noticed is dangerous. I recognized the second kind because I had worn it myself for years, the way you wear a coat cut for someone else: functional, but never quite warm.

Tomas was wearing it this evening, sitting on the floor by the window with his toy boat, running his thumb along the little hull the way other children thumb the edge of a blanket.

"You've broken it," I said. "Whatever it is, you've broken it, and you're deciding whether to confess."

"I haven't broken anything."

"Then you're planning to."

He looked up at me with his mother's—or whoever's—exact expression of patient exasperation, and said, "The box under your bed rattles when you take it out. At night. When you think I'm asleep."

I am, I will note for the record, a professional. I keep secrets the way surgeons keep knives: clean, sharp, and out of sight of children. And yet here was a six-year-old informing me that my floorboards were, acoustically speaking, a disaster.

"It rattles," I agreed, because lying to him seemed suddenly both pointless and unkind. "It has papers in it. Important ones."

"From your master? The one who's dead?"

"From my master. The one who's dead." I sat down across from him, joints protesting. Thirty-one years old and creaking like a wharf ladder. "Why do you ask?"

He shrugged, which is the universal childhood answer meaning *I asked because asking was the whole point*. But then he added, quieter, "My mama had a box that rattled. Before."

The word *before* sat between us like a third person. Before the doorstep. Before the note that said *forget the face*. Before whatever had happened to make a woman fold her son into a stranger's life the way you fold a letter into quarters—small enough to slip through a crack.

"Tomas," I said carefully. "Do you remember anything about the woman who brought you here? Anything at all?"

"She smelled of rosemary," he said immediately, as though he'd been holding the answer ready for weeks. "And she cried in the boat but not after. She told me to be brave until the door opened, and then I didn't have to be anymore." He went back to his boat. "I'm still being brave, though. In case."

In case of what, he didn't say. I didn't ask. Some questions you ask when you can bear the answer, and I could not yet bear that one.

But after he slept—I had given him my bed and taken the pallet myself, a reversal of every sensible arrangement, since he weighed perhaps forty pounds and I weighed considerably more than the pallet appreciated—I did not go to the floorboards. I went to the desk.

Because rosemary, you see. Master Delacroix kept rosemary in a pot on his windowsill, and when I'd once asked why, he'd said, "For memory," in the tone of a man making a joke he alone understood. I had thought it sentiment. I had thought a great many things about that man, and I was beginning to suspect I had thought them all wrong.

* * *

The correspondence was not hidden. That was the first surprise. It lay in the second drawer of the desk, beneath a false bottom so shallow it was less concealment than courtesy—as if he had wanted it found eventually, by someone patient enough to look twice. A bundle of letters tied with green ribbon, maybe thirty of them, all addressed in a hand I knew intimately because I had spent a year imitating it: small, precise, the letters standing upright like soldiers who had been told about the importance of posture.

All of them addressed to a *Mademoiselle Céleste Delacroix*.

Delacroix.

I sat down rather hard on the stool. Outside, a gull screamed something obscene about another gull's mother, and the harbor bells counted the hour, and I sat in my master's chair reading his private correspondence by candlelight like the worst sort of spy, which is to say the sort who discovers she has been a stranger in her own house.

The earliest letters were dated nineteen years back. He wrote to her the way he drew coastlines—with obsessive care and no wasted line. I learned that Céleste had been born in Toulon. That her mother—his wife, a name I had never heard him speak, Marguerite—had died when the girl was four. That he had sent her away to an aunt in Aix *"because a harbor is no place to raise a daughter, and a secret is worse than a harbor."*

A secret. He had written it plainly, in ink, in a drawer. The man who had taught me that the safest document is the one that never exists had kept thirty letters documenting his own greatest one.

*"You ask why you cannot visit,"* ran one from fifteen years past. *"I will tell you a truth your aunt would rather soften: I draw maps for men who would burn the villages those maps describe. One day I will draw a map they should not have, and on that day, my darling, you must be nowhere near me. Distance is the only inheritance I can give you that no one can confiscate."*

I read that sentence three times. Then I read it aloud, quietly, just to hear how it sounded in the air, and it sounded like a prophecy being fulfilled in slow motion over two decades.

The letters continued through the years—birthdays, small triumphs, his complaints about my predecessor apprentices ("the boy has the soul of a customs clerk"), and then, abruptly, seven years ago, the letters changed. They grew shorter. Warier. The handwriting itself tightened, as though the pen were pressing hard enough to leave marks on the wood beneath.

*"They have asked me to survey the seawall quarter,"* he wrote. *"I have told them the marsh claims are exaggerated. They did not enjoy hearing it. Céleste, if anything happens to me, there is a plate missing from my atlas. Do you understand me? There is a plate MISSING. Find Baptiste at the Quai des Charbons—he knows. Trust no uniform, trust the widow two doors down, and above all—"*

The sentence stopped. Not faded, not trailed off. Stopped, mid-clause, the way a man stops when he hears boots on his stairs.

The next letter was dated eight months later and was in a different hand entirely—Céleste's, answering. I read it with my heart going like a drum in a procession.

*"Papa. They tell me you drowned. They tell everyone drowned men drowned. I do not believe the sea took you; I believe men did, and I intend to find the plate you wrote of. I have a son now. His name is Tomas. He has your hands—you would say so, you would be insufferable about it. If I find what you hid, I will finish what you began. Keep the rosemary alive. —C."*

I set the letter down. My hands were not steady, and I am not a woman who tolerates unsteady hands in herself; I inked coastlines for a living, and a trembling line is a lie on paper.

Tomas had the master's hands. I looked toward the bedroom, where the boy slept, and memory began rearranging itself with terrible efficiency. The way he held his charcoal stub—gripped low, precise, the way Guillaume had corrected my grip a hundred times. The patience with which he'd watched me work. The rosemary. The note: *forget the face.* She hadn't written *forget the boy.* She had written *forget the face*—hers. Because her face was known. Because she was Delacroix's daughter, and the men who had made a mapmaker drown were still watching for anyone who looked like him.

She hadn't abandoned her son to a stranger. She had delivered him to the only person in Marseille she believed would finish the atlas—and she had chosen me without ever meeting me, on the strength of her father's letters. Somewhere in that bundle there must be mention of the apprentice with the borrowed surname. Somewhere he had written, *if I cannot finish it, the girl will.*

I found it in the last letter, dated six weeks before his death. Four lines only.

*"Céleste. The apprentice I spoke of—Elodie—is more than I hoped. Stubborn, careful, and dishonest in exactly the right ways. If the day comes, give her the plate and give her your forgiveness for the trouble. She will need both. —Papa"*

*Dishonest in exactly the right ways.* I laughed, and it came out wet, and I wiped my face with the heel of my hand because crying over correspondence is undignified even when no one is watching. Especially then.

* * *

I did not sleep. I sat at the desk until the candle drowned in its own wax, reading the letters again in order, building the man I had thought I knew into the man he actually was. He had not been a solitary old cartographer with a soft spot for orphans. He had been a father conducting a nineteen-year campaign of love by post, hiding his daughter the way he hid his best work—in plain sight, behind false bottoms, trusting the right people to look twice.

And I had spent a year believing I was alone in this. Believing the grief was mine alone, the danger mine alone, the atlas a burden I had inherited by accident of employment.

Inheritance. That was the word, wasn't it. Not employment. Inheritance. He had written me into his will the way he wrote everything—precisely, in advance, expecting me to be stubborn enough to carry it out.

When the sky went grey at the edges, I went and stood in the doorway of my own bedroom and watched Tomas sleep. He had kicked off half the blanket and clutched the toy boat against his chest like a talisman, and even unconscious, his hands were curled around it in that precise, deliberate grip.

"Your grandfather," I said softly, "was a magnificent, reckless, insufferable man, and you have his hands, and I am apparently your family now, which neither of us agreed to and both of us are stuck with."

He stirred. "Are you talking to the boat again?"

"I talk to many things. Sleep."

But I stood there a while longer, doing arithmetic I didn't want to do. Because the letters solved one mystery and opened a worse one. Céleste Delacroix was alive. She had written, seven years ago, that she intended to find the plate. And eight months ago, a woman smelling of rosemary had left her son at my door with a coded drawing and vanished—which meant that somewhere between *I intend to find it* and *forget the face*, something had gone wrong for her too. Something bad enough that she couldn't come herself. Something that might still be hunting her.

And if they had followed her—if the men who drowned my master had tracked his daughter to Marseille—then they might be watching the house already. Watching for the plate. Watching for the boy.

Watching for the apprentice who was dishonest in exactly the right ways.

I blew out the candle, lifted the floorboard, and added the letters to the atlas, wrapped together in oilcloth. Then I sat back on my heels in the dark and admitted the thing I had been circling since midnight:

I could no longer smuggle documents out of this city. I had to smuggle a family.

And the first place to start was finding out what had happened to Céleste—which meant tracing the seal on that note, the pressed wax stamped with a crest I almost recognized and couldn't yet place, the way you almost recognize a coastline from the wrong side of the ship.

Somewhere out there, a woman was hiding from the same men who had killed her father. And I had her son, her father's atlas, and a growing suspicion that the three of us were going to end this together—whether the Crown, the military, or common sense approved or not.
