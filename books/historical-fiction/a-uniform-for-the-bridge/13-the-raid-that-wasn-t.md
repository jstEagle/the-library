## 13. The Raid That Wasn't

Mother Anselme woke me by the simple expedient of not waking me at all.

I had been sleeping in the convent's guest cell for three nights now—officially as a widow in mourning, unofficially as a woman who had learned that sleep comes easier within walls that have heard ten centuries of secrets and kept most of them. The bell for prime did not ring. That was the first wrong thing. Saint-Victor's bells were punctual the way death is punctual; you could set your despair by them.

I lay still and listened. Beneath my window, boots. Not sandals, not the soft shuffle of novices going about their holy business—boots, many pairs, with the particular cadence of men who had been told where to walk and were enjoying the telling.

Someone hammered on the convent door. Not knocked. Hammered, the way a fist speaks when it has rank behind it.

I was out of bed before my mind finished assembling the sentence *they've come for the boy*, which is a strange sensation, being dressed by panic. My stays went on crooked. I put Tomas's shoes in my pocket because a barefoot child running through a crypt makes no sound, and I had learned from Baptiste that the harbor teaches its people to think in terms of sound: what carries, what drowns out, what forgives.

Tomas slept in the cell next to mine, curled around his toy boat like a barnacle around its rock. I shook him awake and pressed my hand over his mouth before he could ask anything, which was fortunate, because he opened his mouth to do exactly that.

"Soldiers," I whispered. "We're playing the quiet game. You know the rules."

He nodded. He was very good at the quiet game; he'd had six years of practice before I ever met him, though I hadn't known that then. Children who arrive on doorsteps with notes saying *forget the face* are graduates of a school no one should attend.

Mother Anselme was already in the corridor, a candle in her hand and an expression on her face that I can only describe as liturgical annoyance. She looked at me, looked at Tomas's shoes peeking from my pocket, and jerked her chin toward the end of the hall without one word wasted. Somewhere below us, wood splintered—the outer door giving way to something heavier than courtesy.

"The crypt," she breathed. "The old stair behind the refectory. And Delacroix—" she caught my sleeve. Her grip was stronger than her seventy years had any right to negotiate for. "Whatever they're looking for, it isn't here. Make certain it stays that way."

It was the closest she would ever come to saying *I know what you carry, child, and God help us both, I'm complicit now*. I squeezed her hand and took the stairs.

* * *

The crypt of Saint-Victor is older than Marseille's name for itself. The monks will tell you it dates to a martyr, and there are bones stacked along its walls in orderly strata—femurs here, skulls there, arranged with the tidy resignation of people who understood that even after death, someone would want the shelving done properly. There is a black stone sarcophagus that pilgrims kiss, and there are side chambers where the air tastes of stone and candle smoke and the particular patience of the dead.

I knew the place because Mother Anselme had shown me on my first night, with the offhand pride of a woman giving a tour of her wine cellar. "When the Saracens came," she'd said, "when the plague came, when the King's men came—which happened more often than the Church liked to admit—we put the living down among the dead, and the dead said nothing. They never do. It's their single virtue."

Now I stood in that patience myself, Tomas's hand in mine, our backs against a wall of femurs, listening to the search happen above us.

They were thorough. That was the worst of it. A sloppy raid I could have laughed at later—a kicked-over bench, some torn vestments, a sergeant shouting about Papists. But these men moved through the convent with the methodical calm of clerks. Doors opening. Footsteps counting rooms. A voice, flat with authority, asking questions I couldn't make out and Mother Anselme answering in a voice pitched like a Sunday sermon: unhurried, faintly bored, granting nothing.

Tomas pressed against me. In the dark his eyes were two points of reflected candlelight from the stub I'd brought, shielded inside my cupped hand so that we existed in a coin of light no wider than a plate.

"Elodie," he whispered, which broke the rules of the game, and I let it break, because his whisper had a crack in it.

"I'm here."

"Are they looking for us?"

I considered lying to him. I want to record, honestly, that I considered it—that the lie assembled itself in my mouth, ready-made, sweetened for a child's swallowing. And then I remembered every adult who had ever lied to me kindly, and how each lie had cost me a little more faith than the truth would have, and I threw it away.

"Yes," I said. "But they won't find us. Do you know why?"

He shook his head.

"Because I'm very good at hiding things." I smiled at him in our little coin of light. "It's practically my profession."

"What was your profession?"

"Maps."

His brow furrowed with the gravity he brought to all questions. "Maps of what?"

That was the question, wasn't it. Maps of what. Maps of seawalls that stood or didn't stand, maps that decided which houses fell and which families scattered, maps that had killed my master and might yet kill a stevedore with a broken hand if Baptiste had given anything away—I pushed that thought down into the dark where I kept the thoughts I couldn't afford. Down among the bones, metaphorically speaking.

"Maps of places," I said. "So people can find their way home."

Above us, something heavy scraped across stone. The search had reached the nave. I heard a soldier's voice, young and irritated: "Another chapel. They're all chapels. It's a convent, Sergeant, it's *made* of chapels."

"And under the chapels?" the sergeant asked, and my heart performed a maneuver I did not authorize.

"Under?"

"These old places. They dig. Nuns dig like moles."

There was a silence in which I could feel Tomas stop breathing, and I confess I stopped too, both of us suspended like the dust in our coin of light.

Then Mother Anselme's voice floated down through the floor—not loud, but placed, the way a good singer places a note in a stone room—and she said something in Latin, long and rolling, and I caught *sanctuarium* and *morte* and a saint's name, and whatever she said, it landed with enough institutional weight that the sergeant grunted and moved on.

I exhaled slowly. So did Tomas, a small shuddering sound.

"She lied," he whispered, awed.

"She invoked jurisdiction," I said. "It's the same thing with better grammar."

* * *

Hours pass differently in a crypt. Above ground, hours are things you spend. Below ground, they accumulate around you like sediment. We sat against the bone wall—Tomas eventually across my lap, because six-year-old legs surrender to gravity long before six-year-old courage does—and we played the games you play when moving means dying: naming things we could see in the dark (nothing, endlessly), guessing the time by the ache in our knees (wrongly), reciting the dockside songs Baptiste had taught him until I threatened, gently, to add a verse about stevedores' manners.

Once, footsteps crossed directly overhead—slow, deliberate, pausing—and a voice said, "What's this stair?" and another voice said, "Sealed since the last century, look at the mortar," and the first voice said, "Check it anyway," and there came three blows of something heavy against the old door at the top of our stair, and the mortar above the lintel sifted down onto us like gray snow.

Tomas grabbed my arm hard enough to bruise. I put my lips against his ear.

"If that door opens," I breathed, "we go into the side chamber, behind the ossuary wall. There's a gap. I measured it the first night. You'll fit. I'll follow. And then we stay silent until the world ends or they leave, whichever comes first."

"You *measured* it?"

"I told you. Profession."

The blows came again. Then a third voice—older, wearier, carrying the tone of a man who has argued with sergeants before and lost more often than he admits—said, "Leave it. Order says sweep the convent, not excavate it. We've got the arsenal gate at noon and I'm not explaining to Captain Ferrand why we're late because we wanted to poke a crypt."

"But sir, the stair—"

"The stair's been shut longer than your grandmother's been dead. Move."

The footsteps receded. The mortar-dust settled. Tomas unclenched my arm finger by finger, and I noticed my own hand had found the back of his head and was holding it against my shoulder, and I did not tell it to stop.

Here is what I learned in that hour, among the bones, and I set it down plainly because it surprised me: I was not afraid for myself. I catalogued my fear carefully, the way I'd catalogue a coastline, and found that nearly all of it pointed at one small boy. Fear for myself had a flavor I knew—sour, familiar, an old companion since my master died. This was different. This was larger and simpler and infinitely more dangerous, because it was the kind of fear that makes people do stupid, magnificent things. The kind that had made Baptiste hold out his hands to be broken rather than give a name. The kind that had made my master walk into a military office with a survey in his coat rather than let the Crown's lies stand unchallenged.

I had spent two years perfecting invisibility. Two years of closed shutters, of delivering finished plates to no one, of being a shadow in my master's old rooms. And it had taken one child on a doorstep and four hundred bones in a basement to teach me the lesson everyone else in Marseille seemed born knowing: nobody survives alone, and nobody who loves anyone is ever truly hidden. To love is to be visible. To protect is to draw a target and stand in front of it.

Well. I thought, pressing my cheek to Tomas's hair. If I must be a target, let me at least be drawn accurately. I am, after all, a cartographer.

* * *

The raid ended the way weather ends—not with a departure but with a thinning, until you realize the thing is gone. Boots diminished. Voices rose back up to street level. Somewhere in the late afternoon light that crept down the sealed stair's edges, the great doors boomed shut—or were shut—and the convent resumed its shape around us like water closing over a stone.

Mother Anselme herself unbarred the crypt stair. She had a crowbar. I will never forget it: this tiny, iron-spined woman in full habit, levering open a door sealed since the last century because she judged that the danger above had passed and the danger of two people suffocating in stale air had not. She looked down at us—me stiff as driftwood, Tomas asleep against my collarbone, having finally surrendered to exhaustion somewhere between the third and fourth hour—and she said:

"Well. The dead kept their mouths shut. As promised."

"We owe them," I managed.

"We owe them nothing. They're dead; it's what they're for." She reached up and lifted Tomas from me with unexpected gentleness, cradling him the way you'd handle a reliquary. "Come. There's soup. The soldiers ate half our stores and left a receipt signed by a lieutenant who clearly expects the abbey to file a complaint with the War Ministry. I intend to do exactly that, at length, in Latin, until someone pays us."

"Mother Anselme." I stopped on the stair, my legs trembling now that trembling was permitted. "They were looking for a boy. Weren't they? Not papers. Not a fugitive man. They described him."

She paused. Candlelight moved on her face.

"They described a child of about six, dark-haired, arrived recently at this house," she said. "And a woman. Red-haired. Who might be traveling with him." She watched me absorb that. "You already knew about the woman."

I had suspected. Since the checkpoint at the arsenal, since Lieutenant Roche had waved us through with a manifest he must have known was false, I had suspected that the net contained more than one knot. Now the shape of it was confirmed: they weren't hunting the atlas alone. They were hunting the family that had carried it. Someone—Roche's superiors, Captain Ferrand, whoever wrote the falsified seawall reports—had decided that the safest way to bury a survey was to bury everyone who'd ever touched it.

"They'll come back," I said.

"They'll come back better prepared, and next time they may bring a magistrate instead of a sergeant, which is worse. Magistrates don't kick doors; they make ownership of doors illegal." Mother Anselme shifted Tomas's weight. "Which is why, Delacroix, the boy cannot stay. Neither can you. I hid fugitives through two wars and I know the arithmetic: shelter buys days, not victories. What does your stevedore friend say about boats?"

The question landed like a dropped anchor.

"Baptiste," I said slowly, "has been talking about boats for a week. Fishing boats, tide windows, a route past the harbor watch that the customs men don't bother watching because it smells too much of sardines." My mind was already drafting it, the way my hand drafted coastlines—quick strokes, corrections, the whole passage taking shape. "He wanted the plates moved tonight, actually. Before the demolition crews finish mobilizing. He said—"

I stopped. Because I suddenly remembered exactly what Baptiste had said, standing in the convent kitchen two nights ago with his splinted fingers wrapped in a rag: *The watch rotates at slack tide. If those papers are going to the council in Paris, they go by water, and they go soon, because come the new moon the whole quarter burns.*

"He said soon," I finished. "And I think soon means tonight."

Mother Anselme looked down at the sleeping boy in her arms, then at me, and something passed over her face that was not quite a smile but was adjacent to one—the expression of a woman who has just added two more names to a list she keeps somewhere behind her eyes.

"Then eat your soup quickly," she said, "and pray the dead keep their mouths shut one night more."

Below us, in the dark, four hundred years of bones said nothing at all. Above us, Marseille went on with its evening—bells, fish carts, the demolition crews sharpening their schedules—and somewhere out on the black water, a fishing boat was waiting to learn whether it would become the most important vessel in France.

I only hoped we'd reach it before the harbor watch did.
