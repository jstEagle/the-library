## 15. What Baptiste Paid

There is a particular smell to the hold of a naval brig, and I will tell you what it is, since I had nothing else to do for six hours but catalogue it: tar gone sour, bilgewater, old rope, and the collective fear of every man who ever sat where I was sitting. They could bottle it and sell it as a warning to children.

They'd taken my boots at the gangplank, which I found insulting rather than frightening. A man's boots are his opinion of himself. Without them I was just a stevedore in stockinged feet, and the deck planks were cold enough to remind me of it with every step.

The fishing boat—old Perrin's *Sainte Anne*, may God forgive me for what they did to her name—had been seized at the harbor watch within sight of the mole. I'd watched from the quay like a fool, because there was nowhere else to go and because some stubborn animal part of me refused to leave until I knew whether the cargo had made it into the lieutenant's boat or theirs. It went into theirs. Two soldiers carried the oilskin packet up the gangway like it was a relic of some saint, and I thought, *well, that's the end of that*, and then I thought about Elodie Delacroix's face when I'd promised her, and then I stopped thinking and walked toward the watch house before anyone had to fetch me.

I want that on the record, whoever keeps records down here. I walked.

* * *

The man who questioned me was called Ferrand—I learned this from him saying it twice, the way men say their own names when they are proud of them—and he had the face of an accountant who had been promoted past his temperament. Not cruel by nature. Worse: diligent.

"Baptiste Morel," he said, reading from a paper. "Stevedore. Twenty-two years on these quays. No convictions."

"Clean as a nun's ledger," I agreed.

"Then explain why you were standing on the quay at dawn watching a seized vessel with the expression of a man watching his own funeral."

"I was watching my boat," I said, which was true, and which was also the first lie, because it wasn't mine, it belonged to half the dockworkers who'd put coins together for Perrin's hull, but a lie built out of truths is the only kind worth carrying.

Ferrand looked at me over the paper for a long time. He had that trick, the quiet look, meant to make you fill the silence with your own hanging. I'd unloaded ships behind men like him for two decades. Silence doesn't frighten a man whose whole trade is waiting.

"You know what was in that packet," he said finally.

"I know what was supposed to be in it," I said. "Dried cod. Some of it even edible."

He sighed, and I understood something important right then: he didn't have proof. If he'd had proof, we wouldn't be having a conversation at all; we'd be having a cell. The conversation itself was the confession of his weakness, and I held onto that knowledge like a rope in bad weather.

"The survey of the seawall quarter," he said. "Drawn by the mapmaker Delacroix before his death. Stolen documents relating to Crown fortifications. You were seen receiving papers from a woman at the Rue de la Calade three nights ago. You were seen again at the customs house. And your name appears—" he tapped the page "—in a ledger."

"A ledger."

"Of conspirators."

"Well," I said, "I do keep accounts. That's the trouble with honest work; everything you do leaves a number somewhere." I smiled at him. It cost me something, that smile. Smiling at a man while your stomach drops through the deck is a skill, and I'd learned it from shipowners.

* * *

They broke my hand in the third hour.

I'll tell it plainly, because I've noticed that men who tell such things plainly survive them better than men who dress them up. They put my left hand flat on the table—the left, thank God, I'm right-handed for the hook and the rope—and Ferrand explained, in the tone of a man reading out shipping rates, that the mallet was not a threat but a schedule, and that the schedule would continue until the names came.

And here is the thing I discovered about myself, lying there with my hand under a naval mallet in a room that smelled of tar and fear: I was more angry than afraid. Angry at the waste of it. Twenty-two years those fingers had worked—knots, hooks, hawsers, the shoulders of drunk sailors steadied on dark stairs—and now some clerk in a clean coat proposed to end their career over paper. Over *maps*. Men drown for gold, for women, for God. I was going to lose a hand for ink.

"Names," said Ferrand.

I gave him names. I gave him a great many names, delivered slowly and with the gravity of a dying man's testament: Jean Aubert, who died of fever in '43; Old Marco, dead eight years; a fishwife named Catarina who'd moved to Toulon; three men whose names I invented on the spot and whom I sincerely hope exist somewhere, living honest lives, unaware of their service to the Crown. I watched Ferrand write them all down, and I confess there was a black pleasure in it, watching diligence record nonsense.

"The woman," he said. "The one at the Rue de la Calade. Describe her."

That one I couldn't dodge with the dead. But I could bury her in the ordinary.

"There's forty women on that street," I said. "You'll have to be more particular."

"You know which one."

"Washerwoman?" I offered. "Fish-seller? The one who sells chestnuts has a mole, if that helps."

Ferrand set down his pen. He picked up the mallet. And he said, quietly, almost kindly—which frightened me more than shouting would have—"Baptiste Morel. You have a sister. Marguerite. She lives above the sailmaker on the Rue Sainte. You have a nephew, Henri, aged nine, who runs messages along the quay for a sou apiece."

The room got very small and very cold.

"We know the families of the seawall quarter pay you to carry their petitions," he went on. "We know you've been asking questions about the demolition order since before it was proclaimed. We don't need your confession, Morel. We need only enough to justify what comes next. Give me the names, and the sister stays a name on a census. Withhold them, and she becomes a line in my report."

Now. Let me be honest, since honesty costs me nothing further in this place. In that moment I saw Marguerite's kitchen—the chipped blue plates, Henri's chalk marks on the doorstep where he played hopscotch—and I felt the pull of it, strong as a current. Any man who tells you he wouldn't feel that pull is a liar or has no one. Love isn't loyalty because it's pure. Loyalty is love that holds its shape under load, the way a hawser does, and every hawser groans.

But I also knew—because I'd spent twenty years learning how power talks when it wants something—that a man who offers mercy in exchange for names never intended the mercy, and never needed the names. He needed only to teach everyone watching that loyalty can be bought at the price of family, so that next time no one would need breaking at all.

So I did the arithmetic a stevedore does: how much weight can this rope bear, and what happens to everyone below me if it snaps.

"Marguerite," I said, "sells thread buttons and cheats at belote. Henri is afraid of gulls. Neither of them knows anything except that I come to dinner on Sundays and complain about my back. Put that in your report."

Ferrand lifted the mallet.

I looked at my hand. Then, because I am who I am, I looked at *him* instead, and said, "Mind the knuckles. A man needs the index finger to swear properly."

* * *

It makes a sound. Nobody tells you that. There's a crack, yes, but after the crack there's a sound like wet rope tearing, and it goes on inside you long after the blow, and I bit through the leather strap they'd given me and made a noise anyway, because God made bodies and bodies answer.

When I could see straight again, my hand was a ruin wrapped in someone's shirt, and Ferrand was standing at the window with his back to me, and I understood that even diligent men have moments when the schedule sickens them.

"The cod," he said, without turning. "Who packed it?"

"Perrin," I said. "He's been packing cod for thirty years. Ask anyone."

"The papers inside the cod."

"What papers? I ordered cod. I received cod. If the Crown has found papers in my cod, I suggest you interrogate the cod."

He turned around then, and for a moment I saw the young man underneath the accountant, the one who'd joined the service for reasons that had probably seemed noble once. "Do you understand what you're protecting?" he asked. "Lines on vellum. Houses. Huts, most of them, rotting on a seawall the engineers say is unsound. Do you think the Crown razes quarters for pleasure?"

"I think," I said, through the pain, slow, because my mouth wanted to hurry and hurry is how men lose, "that the engineers who called it unsound never walked it. I think the survey says marsh where there's rock, because rock means compensation and marsh means eviction. I think twenty-two families are about to learn that the difference between their homes and a wasteland is whatever a clerk writes. And I think a man died drawing the truth of it, and that truth is currently in a room upstairs, and that every hour it sits there, you are the one protecting a lie. Not me. You."

Silence. The lamp hissed.

"You should have been a lawyer," Ferrand said at last, and there was something in it—not respect exactly, but its cousin.

"I should have been rich," I said. "Everything else I've managed to be anyway."

* * *

They put me in a cell with a straw pallet and a bucket, and I sat with my ruined hand in my lap like a bird with a broken wing, and I did the thing I do in bad weather, which is count.

Counting is a stevedore's prayer. I counted the knots I could still tie one-handed—eleven, if you allow sloppiness. I counted Sundays until Henri's birthday—five. I counted the families on the seawall—twenty-two, always twenty-two, I'd carried their petitions so often the number lived in my teeth. And then I counted the people who would be sorry if I gave names, and the list ran long, longer than I'd realized: the widow Couteau who fed stray boys and questions alike; the nuns at Saint-Victor; Perrin, whose boat was already lost; the girl Delacroix with her ink-stained fingers and her eyes like a woman carrying a cathedral in a satchel. Even the boy, Tomas, who'd gripped my thumb on the quay with the fierce strength of small things determined to matter.

A found family, Elodie had called us, once, on the quay, half-laughing, embarrassed by her own sentiment. I'd laughed too. Dockworkers don't get sentimental; sentiment is a luxury paid for in advance.

But sitting in the dark with my hand throbbing like a second heart, I understood that she'd been right and that I'd been wrong about the direction of it. I'd thought family was what you were born into and defended out of habit. It isn't. Family is what you build, plank by plank, favor by favor, Sunday dinner by Sunday dinner—and the building is only proven real when the storm tests the joints. Anyone can love when love is cheap. The test is the mallet. The test is whether, with your sister's name dangling like bait and your hand on the table, you still say *ask the cod*.

I had passed the test. My hand hadn't, but hands are replaceable in ways that people are not. Well. Not replaceable. Endurable. There's a difference, and I intended to spend the rest of my life learning it.

Somewhere above me, boots crossed the floor—two sets, moving fast, and a voice I half-recognized saying something urgent about *the girl* and *the convent* and *the seal*. I pressed my ear to the cold stone like a fool, like a lover, catching fragments:

"—the note the boy carried—she kept it—"

"—the wax seal, Lieutenant, match it—"

Roche. That was Roche's voice, strained thin. And someone answering him, someone senior, someone saying a word that dropped through the stone like an anchor:

"*Delacroix.*"

I sat back against the wall and laughed, quietly, so the guard wouldn't hear—a madman's chuckle in a wet cellar with a busted hand, grinning like I'd won at cards. Because I knew that name. Everyone on that quay knew that name. The dead mapmaker had no family, that was the story, that was what we'd all believed.

Apparently the Crown knew better.

Which meant somebody was about to go looking for a daughter who'd spent years being nobody—and I knew, the way you know weather coming off the water, that whatever happened next, it would start with a seal on a scrap of paper, and it would end at a door on the Rue de la Calade.
