## 16. The Face They Were Told to Forget

The seal was a bee.

Not a grand bee, not the Napoleonic sort that would come a half-century later to buzz all over empire — a small, lopsided wax bee, pressed by hand into cheap tallow, one wing chipped where the stamp had been dropped on stone too many times. I had seen it before. I had seen it every day for eleven years, in fact, pressed into the green wax of letters that arrived from Lyon, from Toulon, from a dozen ports my master never visited and always answered within the fortnight.

I had assumed they were from his wine merchant.

I sat at his desk — my desk, I corrected myself, though the correction sat in my mouth like an unswallowed stone — and turned the note over for perhaps the hundredth time since the night Tomas arrived. *Forget the face.* Four words, a seal, no signature. The paper was Provençal, bought cheap, the ink mixed thin to stretch it. Whoever wrote it had been careful about everything except the wax.

Wax, you see, is vanity's confession. A person can disguise their hand, disguise their words, disguise even their fear, but give them a stamp and they will press their whole self into it.

I knew this bee. I had drawn it once, badly, at nine years old, when I found the stamp in the third drawer of the map chest and asked what it meant. Master Aubertin had taken it from me gently, the way he took everything — scalpel, compass, secrets — and said, "It means someone remembers me kindly." Then he locked the drawer and did not speak of it again, and I, being nine, let it go.

Eleven years later, alone, with a dead man's atlas under my floorboards and a sleeping child in the widow's spare bed, I opened the drawer.

The lock had rusted through years ago. I felt almost insulted by how easy it was.

* * *

Inside: the stamp, its brass handle worn smooth as a knuckle. Beneath it, a packet of letters tied with fishing twine, maybe forty of them, addressed in a hand I knew intimately because I had spent six years learning to imitate it — but not his. His letters were the *receiving* kind; these were the sending. The address on the topmost read: **To C., at the sign of the Blue Heron, Sète.**

C.

I made tea I did not drink and read them in order, which is the only way to read another person's heart without doing it violence.

The earliest were warm in the way bread is warm — ordinary, sustaining, unremarkable. Accounts of tides. Complaints about the Intendance. A joke about a surveyor who fell in the Vieux-Port measuring a depth he could have asked any fisherman. And then, in the fourth letter, a sentence that stopped my hand:

*She has your way of looking at a horizon as if it owes her money.*

She.

The letters were to a wife. No — I checked — not to a wife. To a woman named Constance, addressed in the manner of a husband, but there was no marriage line anywhere, and by the seventh letter I understood why: *If your brother-in-law asks again about the house in Sète, tell him it belongs to a cousin. Let him believe we are what he fears least.*

A hidden family. My master — old, dry, celibate-seeming, wedded to his vellum like a monk to his cell — had kept a woman in Sète for fifteen years, and a daughter, and the daughter had a way of looking at horizons.

I put the letters down. I picked them up. I laughed once, alone, in a dead man's study, and it came out wrong, somewhere between grief and fury, the sound of a hinge that has been oiled too late.

Because here is the thing nobody tells you about being an apprentice: you spend so long earning your place in a household that you begin to believe you know its whole architecture. I knew which floorboard sang, which shutter stuck, how many cups he owned (four; he used two). I thought I had mapped him the way he'd taught me to map coastlines — every inlet charted, every hazard marked. And here was an entire province I had never surveyed, sitting in a drawer three feet from where I slept, humming with a life I hadn't been told existed.

He had trusted me with fortifications worth a court-martial. He had not trusted me with a family.

Or — and this thought arrived later, colder, and stayed longer — he had protected me from it. The way you don't mark a reef on a chart if knowing it would only tempt ships toward it.

* * *

The daughter's name was Marguerite.

I learned it in the eleventh letter, dated eight years back: *Marguerite walks now and pulls the cat's tail with a scientist's persistence. She will make either a naturalist or a tyrant.* In the fifteenth: *M. draws maps of the garden from memory and accuses me of moving the fig tree when her geography disagrees with reality. I have never been prouder nor more afraid.* Afraid. Of what? The letter doesn't say. But I had spent months reconstructing the last year of Master Aubertin's life, and I knew what he feared by then: the seawall survey, the falsified reports, the men in the Intendance who needed the quarter declared marsh so the Crown could raze it without compensation. I knew the weight of paper. Apparently he'd had more than one person to be afraid *for*.

The letters thinned after that. Longer gaps. Shorter notes. The last one, dated fourteen months ago — four months before his death — was five lines long:

*C. — Stop writing to the house. If anything comes for me here, burn it unread. M. must not come north. Tell her the city is a bad chart and I am a worse harbor. With all the love I have failed to say properly, A.*

Burn it unread. I looked at the letter in my hands, unburned, unread for fourteen months, and understood suddenly why the drawer had been locked: not to hide the family from me, but to hide the instruction from whoever came searching after he died. If the Intendance had found proof of a daughter — a granddaughter — there would have been leverage. There would have been a child in Sète who could be questioned, followed, held.

Instead there was a note on my doorstep and a boy clutching a drawing.

I did the arithmetic I had been avoiding since Chapter One of this misfortune, so to speak. Tomas was six. Marguerite would be twenty-four, twenty-five. A grandson. The master's grandson, delivered to the master's apprentice, carrying the missing plate of the master's atlas in a child's fist like a relay baton passed in the dark.

Forget the face.

Oh, Constance. Oh, whoever you are. You didn't leave a child because you couldn't keep him. You left him because keeping him visible was keeping him findable, and findable, given what he carried, meant dead. You gave him to the one address in Marseille you believed still honored my master. You erased yourself out of his life the way cartographers erase themselves from their own maps — the blank space where the maker should be, all craft and no signature.

And I — I want to be honest here, because a memoir that flatters its author is just a map with the reefs left off — I was *furious*. Fury first, understanding second, and the gap between them wide enough to fall into.

Fury, because I had spent a winter loving a child who might be taken from me by a woman with a better claim and a living face. Because "forget the face" had sounded like abandonment, and abandonment is a wound that keeps its own weather. Because she had known — she must have known — what leaving him on that doorstep would cost the person who answered the door. She had chosen me the way you choose a mooring post: something solid, something already fixed in place, something that won't mind holding the line while you sail away.

I minded. For about a day, I minded enormously.

Then I re-read the fifth letter, in which my master wrote: *You ask what I would do if the work ever endangered you both. I would become invisible to you gladly, and count invisibility a fair price for your safety.* And I recognized the sentence, because it was my own creed, worn smooth by my own use. The doctrine of the closed door. The belief that love is safest at a distance, that the people you protect best are the people who never learn your handwriting.

She hadn't abandoned him. She'd done exactly what I would have done. She'd done exactly what I *had* done, for eleven years, to every neighbor on the rue du Lacydon — kept them at arm's length for their own good, and called the loneliness discretion.

Invisibility was always an illusion. I had written that truth myself, eventually, in blood and near-arrests. It seemed she'd learned it too.

* * *

Tomas came home from the widow Couteau's at dusk with biscuit crumbs in his collar and a new word — "capstan" — deployed with the pride of a scholar. I fed him soup. I watched him blow on the spoon with the fierce concentration of a bombadier, and I tried to see Marguerite in the set of his brows and failed, and then saw her anyway, in the way he looked past my shoulder at the window, at the harbor lights, as if checking that the world outside still agreed to exist.

"Did you have a mama?" he asked, apropos of nothing, the way children ask the questions that cost the most.

"I had a mother," I said. "She died when I was older than you."

"Did she leave you a picture?"

The question went through me like a needle finding the seam. "No," I said carefully. "But she left me a comb. Ivory. I still use it."

He considered this. "My mama left me a boat," he said. "But soldiers took it at the gate."

"They did," I said, and did not say: *and inside its hollow hull was the truest map in France, and a lieutenant lied to save it, and I have not slept properly since wondering why.*

"Do you think she'll come back?" he asked, in the tone children use for questions they already know the answer to — flat, armored, testing whether the adult will lie.

Here is what I wanted to say: *No, because she is a coward. Yes, because she loves you. I don't know, because I have never met her and she has made herself a blank space on the chart of this city.* Here is what I actually said, because I had spent the afternoon reading forty letters from a man who loved badly and honestly and too late:

"Yes," I said. "People who leave things behind come back for them. That's what leaving means. It's not letting go. It's *entrusting*."

He looked at me with my master's exact skepticism — the same narrowed eyes I'd faced across a drafting table when my coastlines came out too decorative — and said, "That's what nuns say."

"The nuns steal all their best material," I said, "from drunk stevedores and disgraced cartographers. Eat your soup."

He ate his soup. And I sat there in the lamplight, furious and full of longing in equal measure, holding a secret that had changed shape twice in one day: first the atlas, then the boy, and now the woman. Three documents, really. The paper, the child, and the absence where she should be. Any one of them could hang us. Together they were a family — illegitimate, improvised, drawn in the margins — but a family, with borders I had not chosen and could not survey.

* * *

I worked late. Old habit. I took out the final plate — Tomas's drawing, my master's seawall, the thing three checkpoints and one broken stevedore's hand had been spent to move — and pinned it beside the atlas's open binding, and for the hundredth time studied the little ciphered marginalia along its edge. Numbers, mostly. Depths and bearings, and beneath them, in the smallest hand, a sequence I'd assumed was a calibration key.

Tonight, with Constance's letters fresh in my mind, I tried something else. I read the sequence not as numbers but as a date and a place. Day-month. A tide-table reference for Sète.

The plate wasn't just a survey. It was a letter. Coordinates that resolved, if you had the tide tables for a port two hundred miles west, into a rendezvous: a place, a week, a window between high waters. My master had hidden his family's location *inside the evidence*, the one place the Intendance would never think to look, because they wanted the numbers and would never read the poetry beneath them.

He'd left a map to himself. Or rather — to her. An address for the day it was safe to come home.

And I realized, with the cold sliding certainty of ice forming on a mooring line, that I was not the only one who could read it. Somewhere in Marseille, the men who killed my master for less than this were still hunting the missing plate. If they ever got it into their hands with a set of Sète tide tables and half an hour's leisure, the hiding would be over — hers, the boy's, all of it.

Which meant the plate had to reach the council before it reached them. Which meant the third checkpoint, the harbor watch, Baptiste's seized boat, and whatever remained of our ruined plan had to be rebuilt in days, not weeks.

There was a knock at the door. Two knocks, pause, one — Baptiste's signal, the one we'd agreed on in happier chapters of this disaster. I blew out the lamp, put Tomas's toy boat over the plate, and went to answer it, my heart already running ahead of me down the dark stairs.

Baptiste stood on the step, gray-faced, his splinted hand cradled against his chest like something rescued from a fire, and behind him in the shadows stood a figure in a hood who raised her head just enough for the lamplight from the widow's window to catch her chin.

"You'd better hear this from me before the soldiers tell it wrong," Baptiste said. "They're not done with the convent. And madame—" he glanced at the hooded figure, "—says she knows where Roche's loyalty finally broke."

The figure pulled back her hood, and I looked — for the first time, face to face — at the face I had been told to forget.

She had my master's eyes.

"Mademoiselle Delacroix," said Marguerite Aubertin. "We need to talk about my father's atlas, and we need to talk tonight."
