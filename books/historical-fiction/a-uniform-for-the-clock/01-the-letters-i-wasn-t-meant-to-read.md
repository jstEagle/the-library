## 1. The Letters I Wasn't Meant to Read

The kettle in the rooming house at Kettering had a whistle like a dying thrush, and Rosalind Joubert had learned to time her mornings by it. While it climbed toward its thin complaint, she sat at the window with Edith Marchmont's correspondence spread across her knees, a bone-handled knife in one hand and a saucer of steam-cooled water at her feet, and she performed the small, precise surgery that had become as natural to her as breathing.

Three letters that morning. A heavy cream envelope from Manchester with a crest she didn't recognize — some municipal worthy or other, wanting Mrs. Marchmont to address their daughters' institute. A thinner one, cheap paper, the handwriting cramped and urgent, postmarked from a town she'd had to squint at twice: Marlow Bend. And a plain brown wrapper containing what felt like a pamphlet, though the return address was a private residence in Hampstead.

She worked the knife along the flap of the Marlow Bend letter first. The trick was patience. Steam softened the gum; haste tore the paper; torn paper meant questions, and questions were the one currency Rosalind could not afford. She held the envelope over the cooling kettle-spill until the flap gave up its grip with something almost like relief, then slid out the single sheet inside.

*Dear Mrs. Marchmont,* it began, *I write on behalf of several persons here who cannot write openly...*

Rosalind read it three times before she allowed herself to understand it. It was an invitation of a peculiar kind — not to speak, but to *listen*. There was trouble at the mill, the writer said. There had been trouble for some time. Names could not be put to paper, and the writer trusted that Mrs. Marchmont would understand why even this much was dangerous.

There followed a sentence Rosalind would carry with her for weeks afterward, though she did not know it yet:

*The river keeps our secrets in winter, but the river also keeps us prisoner, and we would ask you to come while the coming is still possible.*

She read it once more, folded it along its original creases, and returned it to its envelope. Then she mixed a drop of the glue she kept in a brown bottle labeled *Furniture Polish — For Oak* — a joke she permitted herself precisely because no one would ever look closely enough to find it funny — and pressed the flap down with her thumb, smoothing it flat against the blotter until it dried indistinguishable from every other sealed envelope in England.

By the time Edith knocked on the connecting door at half past seven, Rosalind was dressed, the letters were squared in their leather case, and the saucer had been washed and dried and returned to its place beside the washstand. Her face wore the expression it always wore in the mornings: attentive, competent, slightly bored. It was the face of a woman who had spent a night doing nothing more remarkable than sleeping.

"Miss Joubert." Edith Marchmont filled doorways the way weather fills a valley — not by force exactly, but by sheer atmospheric insistence. She was fifty-one years old, built like a pulpit, and she wore her gray traveling suit the way other women wore armor, which is to say without ever appearing to notice it was there. "Is the motor behaving?"

"The motor is behaving, Mrs. Marchmont."

"And the post?"

"Manchester confirms for the fourteenth. A pamphlet from London — I've set it aside for your reading on the train. And a letter from..." She paused, and let the pause do its work. "...a correspondent who prefers to remain unnamed."

Edith's eyes came around to her, sharp and gray and entirely without surprise. "Did they say why?"

"They said the matter was delicate."

"Hm." Edith took the case, and for one suspended moment Rosalind watched her weigh whether to open it there in the doorway. She didn't. She never did. That was the arrangement, unspoken and therefore unbreakable: Edith Marchmont did not examine her own mail in front of her driver, and her driver did not volunteer anything beyond the geography of envelopes. "We'll discuss it on the road. Kettering first, then north. You've checked the map?"

"Twice."

"Then let's not keep the twentieth century waiting."

* * *

The motorcar was a Humber, secondhand, purchased by the movement two years ago with money from a legacy whose donor's name Edith spoke only in tones of reverent irony. It was painted a somber bottle green and had opinions about hills. Rosalind drove it the way she did everything else — carefully, thoroughly, with an attention to detail that would have looked, to any observer, like nothing more than good training.

No observer ever asked why a woman of twenty-six who had been educated past all sensible use for her class should be content to drive a suffragist lecturer up and down the length of industrial England. No observer ever wondered about the gap between the girl who had left her father's house in Guildford at nineteen with one trunk and no references, and the composed professional who now adjusted goggles and checked oil levels and navigated the lanes of five counties without a map's help.

Rosalind preferred it that way. Observers were dangerous. Observers asked questions. And questions led backward, through the years, to things Rosalind had spent seven years making sure nobody thought to look for.

Because here was the truth of it, the truth she kept folded away more carefully than any letter: Rosalind Joubert read everything. Had always read everything. Had learned to read the way some children learn to steal bread — early, secretly, and with a hunger that made the risk irrelevant.

Her father's house had been full of papers. Bills, sermons — he'd been a clergyman, God rest his joyless heart — correspondence with churchwardens and bishops and, in the last years, with solicitors whose letters arrived on black-edged stationery that even a child could understand meant debt. Nobody had ever told Rosalind she wasn't to read them. That was the thing nobody understood about surveillance: it requires no conspiracy. It required only a house where everyone was too tired, too proud, or too preoccupied to notice a quiet girl in the corner with her eyes moving.

By fourteen she had read her father's entire correspondence and understood, before he did, that they would lose the house. By sixteen she had read the letters of every boarder her mother took in after the move to Reading, and knew which marriages were failing, which sons were drinking, which daughter had been sent away in disgrace and which daughter was merely said to have gone to stay with an aunt. By nineteen, when she ran — and *ran* was the word, though she told people *left*, and told herself *departed* — she carried with her not just the trunk but the certainty that the world was made entirely of documents, and that whoever read them owned the people who wrote them.

It was not a comfortable certainty. But it was hers.

She had fallen into Edith Marchmont's service by way of a typewriting agency and a lie about shorthand speed that had turned out not to matter, because what Edith needed within a month was not a typist at all but someone who could drive, and within six months, someone who could be trusted absolutely. The trust had been offered on the strength of Rosalind's discretion regarding a quarrel between two committee women — discretion Rosalind had possessed anyway, since the quarrel's details were already in her head and repeating them would have served no purpose.

What Edith had never known — what Rosalind intended to carry to her grave — was that the trust had been repaid with larceny. From the first month. From the very first leather case of correspondence, when Rosalind had steamed open eleven envelopes in a Kettering rooming house not so different from this morning's, telling herself it was a single lapse, a curiosity, an aberration.

Seven years ago now. Fourteen hundred letters, if she had ever dared count them, and she had not.

* * *

They reached the Kettering Temperance Hall by ten, where Edith was to speak to a mixed audience on *The Citizen's Right to the Vote*, and Rosalind installed herself at the back with the coats and the umbrella stand and the hall's single grumpy caretaker, and listened to the speech she could have delivered herself from memory.

She watched the audience instead. This was her other habit, the twin of the reading: watching. The hall held perhaps two hundred souls, mostly women, a scatter of men in the back rows wearing expressions ranging from tolerant amusement to outright hostility. Rosalind catalogued them automatically. The woman in the third row who laughed a half-second before the jokes landed — she'd heard the speech before, probably at another stop, probably a local organizer. The young woman near the front taking notes in shorthand — a journalist, or a spy; the difference mattered less than people assumed. The two men by the door who weren't listening at all but scanning faces — those she marked and dismissed as mere hecklers, unless...

Unless. There was always an *unless* in this work. Edith had received threats enough over the years that Rosalind had developed a private taxonomy of menace: the drunk who shouted, the fool who wrote, and the quiet man who simply stood at the back of a hall with his hat on and his eyes working. The first two were noise. The third was weather worth tracking.

These two were noise. They left before the question period.

Afterward, in the cloakroom fug of the hall, while Edith accepted handshakes with the gracious exhaustion of royalty, Rosalind distributed leaflets and made conversation and collected, without appearing to, the names of three local women who had asked questions slightly too informed about parliamentary procedure. One of them would write to Edith. Rosalind would read the letter when it came. She always did.

"You were watching the man in the gray coat," Edith said that evening, over cold mutton in the rooming house parlor. Not a question. Edith Marchmont did not ask questions she had already answered.

"He left early."

"They usually do. Gray coats are cowards." Edith cut her mutton with the efficiency of a surgeon and the appetite of a stone. "You noticed the shorthand girl as well."

"Fourth row. Left side. She wrote down your figures on property qualification but not your peroration."

"Which tells us?" Edith's mouth did something at the corner that was, in Edith's economy of expression, equivalent to another woman's raised eyebrow.

"That she's writing a report, not a profile. Someone sent her to check your numbers."

"Good Lord." Edith set down her fork. "Do you know, Miss Joubert, in seven years you have never once ceased to surprise me. What did I ever do before you?"

"You managed," Rosalind said, "perfectly well."

"I blundered," Edith corrected. "There's a difference." She resumed eating, and the conversation might have ended there, comfortably, in the mutual fiction of employer and employee, except that Edith laid down her fork again and looked across the table with that gray directness that Rosalind had long ago stopped trying to withstand. "The Marlow Bend letter. You read it carefully?"

The words went through Rosalind like ice water, and she had seven years of practice at not showing it. "I read all the post, Mrs. Marchmont. It saves you time."

Something flickered across Edith's face — something Rosalind could not name and did not dare try to, because to name it would be to look at it directly, and looking directly at things was how one got burned. "Yes," Edith said slowly. "I imagine it does."

And that was all. Edith finished her mutton, pronounced the coffee undrinkable, and retired, leaving Rosalind alone in the parlor with the fire settling and her own heartbeat loud in her ears.

Had she meant it? The question circled like a moth. *You read it carefully?* An innocent inquiry — Edith was forever asking after logistics. Or had something in Rosalind's voice that morning given her away? Or — worse, far worse — had Edith known all along, these seven years, and simply never cared, the way one does not care about the habits of a useful animal?

She sat by the dying fire until the coals went gray, turning it over, finding no answer, and understanding finally that the not-knowing was itself a kind of punishment. A woman who reads other people's letters must live forever inside the uncertainty of whether anyone has read *her*.

* * *

Two days later they were in Nottingham, and the Marlow Bend letter had been discussed, dissected, and provisionally accepted. Edith would go north after the Midlands tour concluded — "a captive audience is still an audience," she'd said of mill towns, in the tone she used for jokes that weren't — and Rosalind had begun, quietly, to assemble the itinerary.

That evening's post contained four items. Rosalind took them up to her room, boiled water in the small copper pan she traveled with — the landladies of England had long since stopped remarking upon it, assuming some delicacy of the female constitution — and set to work.

A bill from the printer in Manchester. A circular from the WSPU, mass-produced, its contents predictable. A note from the shorthand girl — *Miss Vera Ashworth, reporting as instructed* — confirming Rosalind's read of her completely; she was a stringer for a Liberal paper, checking figures, harmless. And the fourth—

The fourth was from Hampstead. The plain brown wrapper she had seen before, in the Kettering batch, forwarded on. Inside it, wrapped again in tissue, was a bundle of papers held with a pin, and a covering note in a hand Rosalind recognized from years of reading it: Edith's own, written to herself, or rather to her London contact, and misdirected — or perhaps not misdirected at all, a possibility Rosalind examined and set aside and examined again — into the general post.

The note said only: *For the winter count. Keep these where they can be found quickly.*

And beneath the note, the papers themselves: figures. Columns of them. Dates, and beside the dates, numbers, and beside some of the numbers, initials. Rosalind read them twice, three times, her lips moving slightly, her breath gone shallow in the lamplit room.

They were not attendance figures. They were not subscription lists. They were not anything a suffrage organization had any lawful reason to compile.

At the bottom of the final page, in different ink, someone had written a place-name — *Marlow Bend* — and underlined it twice, and the ink of the underline had been dry so long it had cracked along its own length.

Rosalind sat for a long time with the papers in her lap and the lamp burning low, and outside the window Nottingham went about its business, carts and voices and somewhere a barrel-organ grinding out a tune she couldn't name. She thought of the cramped handwriting in the first Marlow Bend letter. *Several persons who cannot write openly.* She thought of the river keeping secrets in winter.

Then she resealed the wrapper, perfectly, with the oak furniture polish, and set it atop the leather case where Edith would find it in the morning, and lay awake until nearly three o'clock listening to the rain on the glass and telling herself that whatever the winter count meant, it was not her affair. She was a driver. She was a reader of other people's mail, yes, but only a reader — a ghost at the edges of other lives, present nowhere, responsible for nothing.

In three weeks they would be in Marlow Bend. The letter had said to come while the coming was still possible.

Rosalind fell asleep at last with the phrase turning over and over behind her eyes, and dreamed, though she would not remember it, of a river rising gray and patient over a bridge that was no longer there.
