## 2. Marlow Bend, Last Stop Before Winter

The road into Marlow Bend descended through country that seemed to be holding its breath. Rosalind Joubert felt it before she could name it — a stillness in the hedgerows, a quality of silence in the fields — and she adjusted her grip on the steering wheel of the Humber and glanced once at the woman beside her, who was reading a pamphlet proof as though the world beyond the windscreen were merely weather.

Edith Marchmont read in cars the way other women read in drawing rooms: entirely, imperturbably, with the small frown of a hostess finding a fault in the china. She did not look up when the road narrowed. She did not look up when they passed the first sign of trouble, which was a dead dog at the roadside, or the second, which was a boy of perhaps ten who stood in a gateway and watched them pass with an expression no ten-year-old should own.

"Three days," Edith said now, folding the proof along its crease. "I speak Thursday evening at the mechanics' hall, I meet the weavers' committee Friday morning, and Saturday we are on the train home and you may have your Sunday to yourself."

"And if the meeting is broken up?" Rosalind asked. She kept her eyes on the road. "There's talk of it. Mrs. Pankhurst's people had stones thrown at them in Bolton last month."

"Then we shall have had a very exciting Thursday." Edith smoothed the proof against her knee. "Rosalind, you worry the way other people knit. It keeps your hands busy and produces nothing anyone can wear."

Rosalind did not answer. She had learned, in eighteen months of driving Edith Marchmont across six counties, that argument was a currency Edith spent rather than earned; she preferred to hoard her own. Instead she watched the town assemble itself out of the valley ahead of them — first the chimneys, eight or nine of them, standing up from the river mist like the fingers of a hand; then the slate roofs climbing the hillside in terraces; then the bridge, iron-bellied and green with river scum, spanning the water where the valley pinched shut between two shoulders of moor.

The mill sat astride the river like a spider on its web. Rosalind had seen mills before — she had grown up within earshot of one, though that was not a fact she offered to anyone — but this one was larger than any she had known, and newer, its brick still dark red where the soot had not yet claimed it, and around it the town curved obediently, streets bending toward it the way stems bend toward light.

"They say the owner's daughter runs the office herself," Edith remarked, as Rosalind took the turning by the war memorial. "Constance Vane. Twenty-six, unmarried, and according to my correspondent, 'a woman who reads everything.' We shall get on famously."

"According to your correspondent," Rosalind said, "or according to the letter you read twice and then burned?"

Edith's mouth curved without opening. She was not a woman who laughed easily; her amusement was generally a matter of architecture rather than expression. "You imagine I don't notice you noticing," she said. "It's one of the things I keep you for."

This was as close as Edith Marchmont ever came to acknowledging what Rosalind did with the mail, and Rosalind had long since stopped pretending she didn't know that Edith knew. It was one of the unspoken economies of their arrangement, like the way Rosalind was paid partly in wages and partly in proximity to a life larger than her own. She drove; she managed the luggage; she steamed open envelopes over the kettle in whatever lodging they were given and read what was inside, and resealed each one so perfectly that even Edith's London secretary had never caught her at it. She told herself it was prudence. A driver who knew what was coming could drive around it. A woman alone in the world, dependent on the temperament of a famous suffragist, needed to know which letters were dynamite and which were only ash. That was what she told herself, in the voice she used for telling herself things.

What she did not say aloud, ever, was that she would have done it anyway.

* * *

They lodged, as arranged, above a draper's shop on the high street, with a widow named Mrs. Cattermole who talked the way water runs — constantly, and finding every low place. Rosalind carried the cases up while Edith conducted the preliminary survey of the rooms, and by the time she came down again, Edith had already extracted three items of intelligence: that the mill had taken on two hundred new hands since summer; that there had been "trouble" in the weaving shed that nobody would name; and that the constable, Mr. Pike, was a decent man doing his best, poor soul, under circumstances that Mrs. Cattermole's raised eyebrows declined to specify.

"Circumstances," Edith repeated, delighted, when Rosalind reported this. "You see? The whole town is writing us letters already. We need only learn to read them."

Rosalind went out before dusk to walk the town, because walking a town was the other thing she did instead of knitting. Marlow Bend was built for industry and had been dressed up afterward in municipal respectability — a bandstand, a library endowed by the Vanes, gas lamps with frosted glass — but beneath the dressing the bones showed through. The streets ran steeply down toward the mill. The houses were back-to-back, their doors opening directly onto the pavement, so that domestic life happened in public: women on steps shelling peas, children playing a game that involved chalk lines and shouting, a man washing at a standpipe with the unselfconsciousness of someone whose privacy had never been an option.

And everywhere, the watching.

She noticed it gradually, the way one notices being followed. At first it was only that faces turned toward her — a stranger, a lady in a good coat, obviously connected somehow to the speaker everyone knew was coming. But it was more than curiosity. There was something rehearsed about it, something coordinated in the way conversations paused as she passed and resumed a half-beat too late. Outside the mill gates, two men stood smoking in attitudes of elaborate idleness, and both tracked her with their eyes while carefully not turning their heads. On the corner by the chapel, another man stepped out of a doorway as she approached, then thought better of it and remained, arms folded, filling the doorway the way a cork fills a bottle.

She passed a row of shops near the bottom of the hill and saw the windows. Not boarded — boarded would have been honest — but blackened: washed with some dark paint from inside, or curtained with sacking, so that the glass gave back nothing. Three shops in a row, all dark-eyed, all open for business, their doors propped wide in welcome. She stopped, ostensibly to study the library's endowment plaque, and used the reflection in the plaque's polished brass to watch the street behind her. The man from the chapel corner had drifted forty yards closer without appearing to move.

*Hospitality,* she thought. *With sentries.*

At the bakery she bought a loaf she didn't need, because buying things opened conversations, and asked the woman behind the counter whether there had been much trouble in the town lately. The woman's hands went still on the counter. Not long — a second, perhaps less — but Rosalind had spent years watching faces across steam-opened envelopes, and she knew what stillness meant.

"Trouble, miss?" The smile arrived a moment after the words, like a guest who'd lost his way. "We're a quiet place. Busy folk. Nobody has time for trouble."

"No, of course not," Rosalind said, and paid, and left, and heard behind her the soft bell of the door and then, distinctly, the murmur of voices dropping.

* * *

Thursday morning brought rain, a fine grey drizzle that made the mill chimney smoke lie flat along the valley, and with it came the first callers. Edith received them in Mrs. Cattermole's front parlour, which had been hastily improved with a vase of chrysanthemums and a doily the size of a cartwheel, and Rosalind served tea and listened.

The first caller was a clergyman, mild and worried, who hoped Miss Marchmont understood that his parish contained many mill families whose livelihoods depended on industrial harmony, and that he trusted her remarks would be constructive. Edith assured him that truth was always constructive, in the tone of a woman assuring a child that medicine tastes of strawberries.

The second caller was a deputation from the workers' committee itself: four men and a woman, who arrived together and sat close together on the edge of their chairs, and whose spokesman, a weaver named Ellery with forearms like ship's cable, spoke in a lowered voice even with the door closed.

"We're grateful to you, ma'am, for coming," he said. "I'll not pretend otherwise. But you should know how it stands. There's men at the gates — not mill men, mind. Men paid to stand. And there's been—" He stopped. His companions looked at their knees. The woman, middle-aged, with a face like a closed ledger, looked at Ellery, and something passed between them that Rosalind, watching, could not read.

"There's been what?" Edith asked gently.

"Accidents," the woman said, before Ellery could speak. Her voice was flat as slate. "That's the word used."

"That's the word *used*," Edith repeated, and Rosalind saw her go very still in the particular way she did when something entered her ledger. "Mrs.—?"

"Prosser. My husband worked the carding room eleven years."

"*Worked*," said Edith.

Mrs. Prosser met her gaze with eyes that had learned economy. "The meeting tomorrow," she said. "Will you name names?"

"If I'm given names."

"You won't be. Not tomorrow." Mrs. Prosser rose, gathering gloves and reticule with the finality of a woman closing shop. "But you'll be given something. After. When we know how you are with it."

The deputation left. The clergyman had gone. Rain ticked at the parlour window, and somewhere below, the draper's shutter rattled up for the day's trade, and Edith Marchmont sat looking at the doily as though it might contain instructions.

"Well," she said at last, brightly, terribly. "A captive audience is still an audience, Rosalind. Whatever else this town is, it is listening."

"It isn't listening," Rosalind said. She surprised herself by saying it. "It's holding its breath. There's a difference, and you should know the difference before tomorrow night, because I walked the town yesterday and there are men outside the mill whose job is standing, and windows painted black, and a woman in a bakery who lied to me so smoothly she must practice."

Edith looked at her for a long moment, and Rosalind braced for the knitting remark, the rebuke about worrying.

Instead Edith said, quietly: "Blackened windows?"

"Three shops. Maybe more. From inside."

Edith rose and went to the window and stood looking down at the high street, at the umbrellas moving below like slow black beetles, at the far end of the view where the mill stood with its chimneys combing the rain.

"When I was a girl in Manchester," she said, "there was a mill yard where the grass never grew. My father said it was the smoke. It was years before I understood it was the ash from the refuse burner, and that the reason nobody told me was that everybody knew." She turned. "Towns like this don't paint their windows because they're ashamed, Rosalind. They paint them so they can see out without being seen to look. Somebody in Marlow Bend is watching for something, and doesn't want it known that they're watching."

"Watching for what?"

"I intend to find out." Edith picked up the folded proof from the table and tapped it once against her palm, a gesture Rosalind knew well: the gavel before the session opens. "Tomorrow I speak on the franchise, as planned. And after the meeting, Mrs. Prosser will decide what I am 'with.' Which means, my dear, that between now and tomorrow night, we must be worth deciding for."

* * *

That evening Rosalind carried the post upstairs — two letters forwarded from London, a circular from the Lancashire federation, and a plain brown envelope with no return address — and set the kettle going in her narrow room with the ease of long habit. The fire was small; the window rattled in its frame; below her floorboards she could hear Mrs. Cattermole locking up, and beyond that, faintly, the mill working a night shift, a sound like distant surf that never quite broke.

She steamed the brown envelope first.

Inside was a single sheet, unsigned, in a careful commercial hand: dates, initials, figures. Nothing legible to a stranger — a column of numbers with dashes after some of them, and at the head, in the same hand, two words: *winter count.* Below the numbers, a line: *Bridge closes with the rains. Send nothing further until spring.*

Rosalind read it three times. Then she held it near the lamp, angled to catch any impression from a page written above it in a pad, but the sheet had travelled alone; there was nothing. She read the numbers again. Seven figures, descending. Dashes after three of them. It meant nothing and it meant everything, the way arithmetic does when you know it is counting something.

She thought of Mrs. Prosser's face like a closed ledger. *Accidents. That's the word used.*

She resealed the envelope with the iron and the paste brush, pressing the flap down while the gum was warm, aligning the edges to the fraction of a millimetre that was her signature on the work. She did it perfectly, as she always did. Then she sat for a while with the letter in her lap, listening to the mill's surf-sound and the rain, and felt the particular loneliness of her position arrive, as it always did at the end of such days: that she knew more than almost anyone, belonged to no one, and could tell no one anything at all.

Downstairs, the front door bolt went home. Out in the dark, past the blackened windows, Marlow Bend settled into its watchful sleep, and somewhere across the valley the river, swollen with the week's rain, ran fast and rising toward the bridge.
