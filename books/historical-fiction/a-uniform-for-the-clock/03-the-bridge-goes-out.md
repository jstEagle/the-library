## 3. The Bridge Goes Out

The rain began on the second night, a soft grey insistence against the windows of the Temperance Hall where Edith Marchmont had held two hundred textile workers past closing time with nothing but her voice and a chalkboard. Rosalind Joubert stood at the back of the hall as she always did — near the door, coat over her arm, hat in hand, the posture of a woman who was furniture — and watched the crowd the way Edith never could. Edith watched faces for belief. Rosalind watched them for something else: for the man in the third row who did not applaud but did not leave either; for the woman who glanced, twice, at the blacked-out window; for the two young men by the stove who spoke to no one and were spoken to by no one, and whose boots were too good for mill hands.

She had learned long ago that a town tells you everything it cannot say, if you watch its doors instead of its mouths. Marlow Bend had been telling them since the moment the motorcar crossed the bridge on Tuesday afternoon. The town said *we are glad you've come* with bread and spare rooms and a borrowed garage for the car. And underneath, in a smaller voice, it said *and we are sorry for what you will see*.

Now the rain came down like a verdict read slowly.

By morning the river had risen six feet. Rosalind saw it from her window above the Harker printing shop — the same window through which she had already noted, on the first night, the lamplight burning until three o'clock behind wallpaper samples stacked against an interior wall — and what she saw stopped her mid-button. The Marlow Bend river was not a river anymore. It was a brown animal the width of a street, shouldering whole trees along, grinding against the cut-stone piers of the bridge with a sound like slow applause.

She dressed quickly and went down. Tomas Harker was already in the shop doorway, arms folded, watching the water with the expression of a man reading a letter he had received many times before.

"It does this," he said, without turning. "Every few years. It'll crest tonight, maybe tomorrow."

"The bridge?"

He shrugged in a way that answered her.

Rosalind looked down the street. The mill's hooter had not sounded — she had learned its voice by now, the seven-o'clock summons that emptied half the town toward the gates — and the absence of it sat strangely, the way a missing clock tick sits in a room. Men stood outside the mill gates anyway, hands in pockets, looking at the water as if it might explain their wages to them.

Edith appeared at eight sharp, immaculate, unhurried, a woman whom weather happened to other people. "The meeting tonight is cancelled," she announced to the hallway at large. "Which means we shall have to make our own audience." She caught sight of the river through the doorway and paused, one gloved hand on the banister, assessing it the way she assessed a hostile newspaperman — measuring how much damage it could do and how much use.

"We ought to cross today," Rosalind said. "While there's still a crossing."

"On that?" Edith nodded at the flood with something close to amusement. "My dear Rosalind. The ferryman wouldn't take his own mother out in that."

"Then we take the road upriver. There must be a ford—"

"There is a ford, eleven miles north, which will be under nine feet of water by noon, if it isn't already." Edith removed her gloves finger by finger, a ritual Rosalind knew meant the conversation was concluded. "We are here, Rosalind. The work is here. The bridge will be back."

But the bridge would not be back, and by evening everyone in Marlow Bend knew it.

* * *

It went at twenty minutes past ten that night, and Rosalind heard it before she understood it.

The sound came up the hill and through the floorboards of the Harkers' house — not a crash, nothing so simple, but a long groaning exhalation, timber and iron giving up their argument with the water all at once, followed by a silence so complete that every dog in the town began to bark at it. Then the cheering.

That was the part Rosalind never forgot afterward, the thing she set down years later in the margin of her own memory: they cheered. She went to the window and saw figures running down the street toward the broken span, lanterns bobbing, and heard young men shouting with a joy she could not at first account for — until she understood that they were not celebrating destruction. They were celebrating severance. For as long as the bridge was gone, the mill was an island of exactly the size of Marlow Bend, and whatever happened inside it this winter would happen without witnesses from outside.

Or perhaps, she thought, watching the lanterns gather at the broken end of the bridge like moths around a wound, they simply cheered because the water had done loudly what they could only do quietly — and there is a relief in that, even when it frightens you.

Tomas Harker stood in the street below her window, soaked to the shoulders, having run down with the rest. He did not cheer. He looked at the gap where the bridge had been — a clean absence, the far bank a smudge of lamplight belonging now to another country — and then he looked up, and found her face at the window without seeming to search for it, and turned away.

She would think about that look often in the weeks to come. That night she only thought: *he is glad we are trapped, or he is afraid of what we will see while we are trapped, and I do not yet know which.*

* * *

In the morning the ferry was suspended.

Rosalind went down to the landing herself, because Edith had asked her to confirm the schedule, and because confirming schedules was one of the small competencies behind which Rosalind hid her larger self — the way another woman might hide behind embroidery. The ferryman, a broad grey-bearded man named Cobb whose hands were rope-hardened and whose eyes avoided hers, stood at the head of his sagging jetty with his arms crossed against the current as though he personally were holding the river back.

"Spring," he said.

"That's four months."

"That's spring." He spat into the brown water with something like respect. "Cable's under strain, landing stage is gone downstream, and I'm not putting a soul across that for any wage the county'd pay. Come thaw, maybe. If the pilings held. Half of them won't have." He looked at her then, briefly, with an expression she catalogued and kept: not hostility, not rudeness. A kind of weary apology, as though he regretted the inconvenience the river was causing her specifically, and would be unable to prevent anything else it caused anyone.

"You're certain there's no other crossing."

"I'm certain the last man who told me about another crossing was drunk, and the crossing drowned him." He almost smiled. "You'll be stopping with the Harkers, then? Good folk. Quiet folk." The way he said *quiet* made it sound like a profession.

Rosalind walked back up the hill through a town that had changed shape overnight. Not physically — the streets were the streets, the mill still squatted over everything with its chimneys writing slow grey script against the sky — but relationally. On Tuesday, Marlow Bend had been a stop between engagements, a pin on Edith's map. Now it was a room, and they were all locked in it together, and every person she passed seemed to know it. The butcher gave her an extra rasher with her order and would not meet her eye. Two women fell silent as she approached their doorway and resumed their conversation only when she had passed, at a volume calculated to be just audible: *"—well, they say the inquiry won't sit till after thaw, so there's time yet—"*

Time yet. Time yet for what? She filed the sentence away, as she filed away everything, in the great unsorted drawer of things she noticed and did not understand.

At the Harkers' door she met Mrs. Harker — Marta, though Rosalind did not yet feel entitled to the name — carrying a tray of bread rolls with flour to her elbows, and Marta said, "Well. You're one of us till spring now," and smiled, and the smile was warm, and underneath the warmth ran something else, something that made Rosalind think again of the lamplight behind the wallpaper samples, and the word *quiet*, and the two men by the stove.

*One of us.* As if there were a us, and as if Rosalind's membership in it had been decided by a river without consulting anyone — least of all Rosalind.

* * *

She raised it with Edith that afternoon, in the rented room above the print shop that served as their sitting-room, office, and battleground. Edith was at the table with her correspondence spread out before her in the ordered ranks she maintained everywhere — letters to answer, letters to forward, letters to burn, each stack squared to the table's edge with a ruler's precision. Rosalind sometimes thought that if you tipped Edith Marchmont upside down, correspondence would fall out of her the way sand falls out of an hourglass, in measured quantities, forever.

"The ferry's suspended until spring," Rosalind said. "There's no road crossing. We are, for all practical purposes, marooned."

"Yes." Edith signed a letter without looking up. "Isn't it remarkable?"

"It isn't remarkable, it's a problem. Your Manchester engagement is in nine days. The Federation expects you. There are people who have travelled—"

"People travel for me every week of the year, and every week of the year I disappoint some of them, and the cause survives." Edith laid down her pen and finally looked at her, and Rosalind braced herself, because she knew the look. It was the look Edith wore when she had already decided and was merely waiting for the world to finish arguing. "Consider what we have here, Rosalind. Four hundred women who work fourteen hours a day in rooms where the lint is thick enough to chew. A meeting hall that holds two hundred and was full on a Tuesday night in November. A town that has never once heard a suffragist speak, and now cannot get rid of me." She permitted herself the smallest of smiles. "A captive audience, my dear, is still an audience."

"They're captive because the bridge is out, not because they've chosen to hear you."

"All audiences are captive to something. Boredom. Hope. Bad weather. I have spoken in halls where they came for the heat and stayed for the argument, and I count those among my best nights." Edith gathered her letters, squared them, bound them with string. "Besides. Think of what the winter offers that the season never does. In London I am a spectacle, scheduled between a bishop and a luncheon. Here I am a guest. Guests learn things. Spectacles don't."

"And what is it you expect to learn?"

For a moment — a moment Rosalind would return to many times in the months ahead, turning it over, examining it from angles, testing it for cracks — Edith's gaze moved to the window. Down toward the mill. Toward the blacked-out windows on Cutter Street, perhaps, or the gates where men stood in the cold not working, or simply toward the river, which had taken the bridge and given them, in exchange, all the time in the world.

"Whatever Marlow Bend wants to tell me," she said. "If it wants to tell me anything at all."

It was, Rosalind thought later, a careful answer. Too careful. But she was twenty-six years old and her talent — her gift, her affliction, her single true skill — was reading words, not people, and so she let it pass, and packed away her objection with all the others, and went downstairs to tell Mrs. Harker they would want the rooms through March.

Marta Harker received the news standing in her kitchen doorway, wiping her hands on her apron, and said, "I'll air the linens," in a tone that suggested the linens had been aired for some other purpose entirely, long before any river made its decision.

* * *

That night the rain stopped, and the frost came, and Marlow Bend lay under a sky so clear and hard that the stars seemed printed on it — set in type, Rosalind thought, giddy with tiredness, proofed and corrected, no errors admitted. From her window she could see the gap in the bridge by moonlight, the severed ends reaching toward each other across the black water like two sentences that would not be joined again until someone found the right conjunction.

Downstairs, faintly, the lamplight behind the wallpaper samples burned past midnight again. She heard the small rhythmic sounds that came with it — not voices, nothing so definite; a shuffling, a sorting, a sound like hundreds of small metal objects being counted.

And below that, deeper in the town, the mill's night furnaces breathed their orange glow against the low clouds, and the river carried on its patient work beneath the ruined bridge, and somewhere in the dark a dog barked twice and stopped, as if corrected by a hand.

Rosalind stood at the window a long while, doing what she always did: watching, filing, keeping. She told herself it was habit. She told herself it was self-protection — that a woman alone in the world survived by knowing more than she was known.

But the truth, which she was not yet honest enough to name even to herself, was simpler and stranger: she was lonely, and the town below her — with its counted sounds and its silenced doorways and its secret burning past midnight — was the most interesting thing that had ever happened to her.

The trap had closed gently, almost politely, the way a good host draws a chair to the table.

She sat down on the cold window-seat, pulled her coat around her, and settled in to watch.
