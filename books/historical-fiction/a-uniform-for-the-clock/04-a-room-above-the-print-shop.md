## 4. A Room Above the Print Shop

The room Edith Marchmont had procured for them was above a print shop on Chandler's Row, and Rosalind knew it before the motorcar had fully stopped, because she could smell it — ink and hot metal and the particular sweetness of paper stock, a smell that reminded her of nothing so much as her father's study in the years before everything went wrong, when letters still arrived bearing good news and she was too young to read them over anyone's shoulder.

"Chandler's Row," Edith said, consulting the card Mr. Pell had pressed into her hand at the ferry office. "A Mrs. Harker takes lodgers. The shop is the family's. Quiet street, apparently." She said *quiet* the way she said most things, as though testing the word for hidden load-bearing qualities.

Rosalind set the brake and looked up at the building. It was narrow and three storeys tall, its brick stained dark at the base where the river breathed on it, and the ground floor windows were papered over from the inside — not blackened like the mill offices down the road, but covered in printed sheets, advertisements for mourning cards and wedding invitations and a butcher whose prices were, according to the poster, unbeatable in the whole of Marlow Bend. A painted sign above the door read **HARKER & SON — JOB PRINTING** in gold leaf gone greenish with age, the ampersand larger than either word it served.

"It's respectable-looking," Edith allowed, which from her meant almost nothing and possibly the opposite.

"The sign is older than I am," Rosalind said.

"You noticed that in four seconds."

"One notices things when one is paid to drive."

Edith gave her the look — the one that suggested she suspected Rosalind noticed rather more than driving required — and stepped out onto the cobbles before Rosalind could come round to open the door herself. That was another thing about Edith Marchmont: she opened her own doors in towns like this one, and let Rosalind open them only where being driven mattered more than being seen to be self-sufficient.

* * *

Mrs. Harker was a small woman built like a comma, all curve and pause, with flour on her forearms and eyes that went over both of them once, quickly, the way a compositor checks a galley for flaws before locking it in the chase. Her handshake was brief and dry.

"Two rooms," she said. "I'd only promised the one, but my Tomas can sleep in the shop if there's a rush job on, which there generally is." She said this without complaint, the way one reports weather.

"That's generous," Edith began.

"It's practical." Mrs. Harker wiped her hands on her apron. "You'll take breakfast at half seven, dinner at one, and I'll thank you not to bring your politics into my kitchen. Whatever you're here about." She looked at Edith directly as she said it, and something passed between them — two women who had each decided the other was manageable.

"We're here about textile workers," Edith said pleasantly.

"Everyone's here about something." Mrs. Harker turned for the stairs. "Mind the third step. It talks."

The stairs were steep and smelled of lamp oil, and the third step did indeed talk — a long groan like a man asked to reconsider his opinions. Rosalind counted doors as they climbed: the landing, then two doors left, one right, and at the back of the house a narrow door marked only by a strip of leather nailed across it as a handle. Behind that door, faintly, came the rhythmic clank-and-hiss of machinery already running, though it was barely past noon.

"Your son works late?" Rosalind asked.

"He works early," Mrs. Harker said. "And late. And in between, when he thinks nobody's counting." She opened the door to their rooms. "Baths are Tuesdays. The water's soft, which is more than the town can say for itself. Anything else?"

"No, thank you," Edith said, and Mrs. Harker left them, her footsteps descending with the evenness of someone who had walked those stairs ten thousand times and intended to walk them ten thousand more.

* * *

Their rooms were at the front of the house, two of them knocked into one by the removal of some interior wall, with a window overlooking Chandler's Row and a second window at the side that looked down into a light-well shared with the printing shop below. There were two iron bedsteads, a washstand, a wardrobe with a mirror clouded at one corner like breath on glass, and a writing desk positioned — Rosalind noted immediately — so that whoever sat at it faced the wall rather than the window. Someone had thought about sightlines. Someone had thought about what a person at that desk might not wish to be seen doing.

She unpacked Edith's things first, as always: the grey travelling dress hung to shed its creases, the correspondence case locked and placed beneath the spare pillow, the pamphlets stacked spine-outward in order of increasing radicalism, because Edith liked to be able to put her hand on escalation without looking. Then her own: one bag, lighter than Edith's hatbox, containing clothes she kept deliberately unremarkable and a small tin of mending supplies she never used because mending other people's letters had ruined her patience for mending stockings.

It was while hanging her own single spare blouse in the wardrobe that she found the wallpaper samples.

They lined the inside of the wardrobe door — a dozen rectangles of pattern pasted to card, florals and stripes and a trellis design, the sort of thing a landlady might keep to show lodgers what the rooms could look like *if*. But the paste was fresh, newer than anything else in the room, and the samples overlapped slightly, each one covering the edge of the last, and underneath the fourth one — a fussy little rose pattern — the cardboard sounded wrong when her fingernail tapped it. Hollow-ish. Thin-ish. Not quite the sound of card against solid wood.

She should have stopped there. She was, after all, a woman whose entire professional existence depended on leaving other people's secrets exactly where she found them, undisturbed, unremarked. She had steamed open perhaps two thousand envelopes in six years and resealed every one so perfectly that she had once watched a postmistress run her thumb along a flap Rosalind had lifted the night before and pronounce it sound. She knew the grammar of concealment the way musicians know scales.

But the tap had sounded wrong, and curiosity in Rosalind Joubert was not a mood; it was a condition, chronic and untreatable, diagnosed young and never once in remission.

She lifted the rose sample away from the door.

Behind it, recessed into the wood, was a shallow tray — no deeper than a cigar box — and in the tray lay printer's type. Dozens of pieces of it, lead-dark, sorted by size into little ranks, each letter standing on its foot like a congregation at attention. E's and T's mostly, she saw, which any compositor would tell you were the commonest letters in the English language and therefore the ones you kept closest to hand. There was a smaller compartment of spaces and quads. And tucked at the back, folded twice, a single sheet of cheap paper printed on one side in small, dense type.

She read it. Of course she read it. She read everything; it was the one honest thing about her.

It was a handbill. *NOTICE TO THE WORKING MEN OF MARLOW BEND*, it began, and went on in language so mild it could have been issued by a church committee: a meeting, an address, a reminder that *the law protects the peaceful assembly of British subjects*. No names. No date. The blank space where the date should have been told her more than the text did — this was a proof, pulled to check the setting, waiting for the moment to be chosen. And beneath the last line, in the corner where a printer signs his work or pointedly doesn't, there was a tiny ornament: a clock face, its hands set at midnight.

She replaced the sample, smoothed it flat with the heel of her hand until the edges sat exactly as they had, and closed the wardrobe. Her heart was behaving disgracefully for such a small discovery.

* * *

Down in the light-well, the press had stopped. In the new quiet she heard voices — a man's voice low and level, and Mrs. Harker's answering it, and then a third voice, younger, laughing briefly at something before the laugh cut off as though someone had remembered where they were.

Rosalind went to the side window and looked down into the well.

The yard below was cobbled and cluttered: a handcart, a stack of wooden paper crates going soft with damp, a rain barrel with a duck floating on it with the serenity of a creature who has found the one dry spot in Marlow Bend. Against the far wall, under a lean-to roof, stood a press — an iron hand-press, old-fashioned but lovingly kept, its frame painted bottle-green, and beside it a composing stone where a man was standing with his back half-turned, breaking up a forme with a brass rule, sliding the type back into its cases with quick, economical movements.

That would be Tomas, presumably. He was younger than she'd expected — twenty-five, perhaps, with his mother's comma-built compactness and a smudge of ink along one jaw that he clearly didn't know was there. He worked the way she imagined a surgeon worked: fast, sure, no wasted motion, the sort of competence that looks like boredom from the outside. Once he paused, lifted a piece of type to the light, squinted at it, and set it aside in a separate box — damaged, she guessed, a letter with a cracked face that would print a flaw into a thousand impressions if he let it stay.

He kept a separate box for flawed type. She found that she liked him for it, in the distant way one likes a stranger seen through a window, and then immediately distrusted herself for liking him, because liking people made her careless, and careless was the one thing she could not afford to be. Careless people got caught reading other people's mail. Careless people ended up on trains back to places they'd sworn off forever.

The man's voice again — the low one — and Tomas answered, and though she caught no words, the rhythm of it was familiar: two people talking around a subject rather than through it. She had heard Edith do it a hundred times with magistrates' wives and mill owners. Talk-around had a music all its own.

Then the man left by the yard gate, and Mrs. Harker came out with a covered dish, and Tomas ate standing up beside the press like a horse at a nosebag, and the ordinary domesticity of it all was somehow the most suspicious thing Rosalind had seen since crossing the bridge into Marlow Bend. Because ordinary people did not hide common printer's type behind wallpaper samples in a lodger's wardrobe. Ordinary printers signed their work, dated their bills, kept their notices in drawers like everyone else. You hid type in a secret compartment when the type itself was evidence. When the words it had set were words that somebody — the mill, perhaps, or the constable, or whatever power in this town wore whichever face — would pay money, or worse, to trace back to the hand that set them.

She thought of the blackened windows near the mill. The men in doorways who watched without appearing to watch. The accident ledger Mr. Pell hadn't mentioned but whose absence had a shape, the way a missing tooth has a shape.

* * *

At dinner — boiled mutton, potatoes, bread so fresh it steamed — Edith held forth gently to Mrs. Harker about the meeting scheduled for Friday evening in the mechanics' institute, and Mrs. Harker received the information the way a wharf receives cargo: without visible movement, everything stored somewhere out of sight.

"There'll be trouble," Mrs. Harker said, not unkindly. "There always is, when someone comes to talk about the mill."

"I've spoken in harder towns."

"I don't doubt it." Mrs. Harker refilled Edith's tea without asking. "My late husband used to say a town is like a boiler. Looks calm enough from outside. All the pressure's inside, and it goes wherever the metal's thinnest." She set the pot down. "Friday suit you? I'll do a cold supper, so nobody's waiting on me."

"You're very good."

"I'm very careful," Mrs. Harker corrected, "which looks similar from a distance."

Later, when Edith had gone up to draft her speech and Rosalind carried the tea things out to the kitchen, she found the household's youngest member drying plates — a girl of maybe fourteen with ink on her knuckles and her brother's quick hands, introduced at the door that afternoon only as *our Nell*. Nell dried plates and hummed and glanced sideways at Rosalind with frank, unembarrassed curiosity, the way children and cats assess newcomers.

"You're the driver," Nell said.

"I'm the driver."

"Do you like it? Driving?"

"It's the best seat in the house." Rosalind set the cups on their saucers. "Your brother keeps the press downstairs busy."

Nell's cloth stopped moving for precisely one second — a pause so small that anyone who wasn't professionally trained to notice pauses would have missed it entirely — and then resumed. "He keeps it busy," she agreed. "There's always bills to print. Weddings and funerals and the butcher's prices." A grin. "Unbeatable in the whole of Marlow Bend."

"So I've heard. Repeatedly."

"Mam had that poster made the year I was born. She says it's the best advertisement we'll ever need." Nell stacked the last plate. "She says a lot of things."

From the kitchen doorway, Mrs. Harker's voice arrived like a hand on a shoulder: "Nell. The flour wants shifting before it weevils." And Nell went, cheerful and obedient, and Rosalind was left alone with the teacups and the distinct impression that she had just been removed from a conversation for her own protection — or someone else's.

* * *

That night, lying in the iron bedstead listening to the house settle, Rosalind conducted her usual inventory, the ritual that took the place of prayer: the letters read today (three, all mundane — one from a London contact named only as F., discussing train timetables; one from a suffrage branch secretary full of committee grievances; one, oddly, with no return address, containing a single sheet on which was written nothing but a column of figures), the letters unread (none — she never slept with an envelope unopened within reach), the risks taken today (one: the wardrobe), the risks avoided (several; she was getting better at avoidance, or perhaps just wearier).

The column of figures bothered her. Thirty-one numbers, arranged in rows, no headings, no explanation. She had turned the page over twice looking for a watermark, a date, a smear of context. Numbers were how powerful people talked when they didn't want to be understood, and Edith had read that letter at breakfast with the same expression she wore for committee grievances — mild, patient, faintly bored — and had burned it in the grate afterward, which she almost never did. Edith kept everything. Edith believed, with an almost theological fervour, in keeping everything, because paper was ammunition and ammunition kept.

But she had fed that letter to the fire and watched it curl, and Rosalind had sat across the room pretending to mend a glove and memorizing the figures instead. All thirty-one of them. It was the one kind of theft she had never been able to stop committing: taking things into her memory where no one could audit them.

Below her, through the floorboards, she could hear the shop. Not working — resting, the way machines rest, ticking faintly as the iron cooled. But once, near midnight, there came a different sound: footsteps crossing the floor below, unhurried, deliberate, and then a knocking. Not on a door. On wood — a beam, perhaps, or the wall itself. Four knocks. A pause. Two knocks. A pause. Four again.

And then, from somewhere further off — the lane outside, she thought, or the next house over — an answer came back. Faint, but unmistakable in its structure. Four. Two. Four.

Tomas's footsteps returned across the floor and faded toward the front of the house, and the shop went silent, and Rosalind lay in the dark with her heart going like the press downstairs and understood that she had not been lodged above a jobbing printer at all.

She had been lodged above whatever Marlow Bend used in place of a voice.

She thought of the type in the wardrobe, the unsigned proof with the clock set at midnight, the separate box for flawed letters, Nell's one-second pause, Mrs. Harker's *careful* — and she thought, with a sensation that was equal parts dread and something warmer she refused to name yet, that tomorrow she was going to have to go down into that shop and find a reason to stay.
