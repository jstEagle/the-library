## 5. What the Mill Pays For Silence

Tomas Harker counted the money twice, the way his father had taught him, and then a third time because the sum itself had taught him to distrust it.

Forty-one pounds, six shillings, and some coppers that smelled of the mill office — of ink and iron filings and the particular sourness of men who handled other men's wages all day. He stacked it in denominations on the counter of the print shop, in the grey light before opening, while outside the fog came up off the river like something breathing. The fog was always thickest on pay mornings. He had never decided whether that was weather or mercy.

The shop smelled of lead and paste and lamp-oil, and beneath those, faintly, of his mother's lavender water, which she still dabbed on the doorframe every Friday though there was no one left to notice but him. Tomas noticed. Tomas noticed everything; it was the family affliction. His father had noticed the price of paper rise a farthing and adjusted the whole winter's orders around it. His mother noticed which constable walked past the window too slowly. And Daniel — Daniel had noticed everything so completely, so hungrily, that noticing had killed him.

No. That was not true, and Tomas made himself correct it, as he corrected crooked type, every time the thought set wrong. Noticing had not killed Daniel. Noticing had merely failed to save him.

He put the money into the tin box with the false bottom — the same box, God help them, that held the forme for last month's *Winter Count* when it wasn't in use — and he thought about the ledger.

Not the shop's ledger. The mill's.

* * *

There were three ledgers at Marlow Bend Mill that mattered, though only two were ever spoken of aloud. There was the wage book, fat and greasy-thumbed, that recorded what a man earned and therefore what his silence cost. There was the accident ledger, slim and elegant, bound in green cloth, kept in Mr. Vane's outer office where any inspector could see it and be reassured by its very thinness. And then there was the third ledger, the real one, which was not bound at all but existed in the collective memory of four hundred workers, passed mouth to mouth like a psalm nobody wanted to know by heart.

Tomas knew all three. He had known the third one best, once, because Daniel had been writing it down for years — in pencil, in the margins of hymnals and wage slips and the backs of playbills Tomas printed for the Mechanics' Institute — before Tomas ever set a line of type to the purpose.

*Weaver girl, card room, hand caught in the mule. They said she fainted.*

*Tallow man scalded at No. 2 vat. Back at work in nine days with the arm wrapped. Nine days. Count it yourself.*

*Old Prewitt, doffer, fell from the staging. They wrote: slipped on his own bootlaces.*

His father had found one of those scraps once, years ago, and gone white as unsized paper, and burned it in the stove, and said nothing for a week. That was the Harker way. You saw, you remembered, you kept your head down and your hands moving, and you survived. The shop had survived three recessions, a fire in '89 that took the roof and half the back wall, and old Vane's father's attempt to buy them out and shut them down — all by the simple discipline of being careful. Careful as a man walking a plank over a flooded river.

And then Daniel had stopped being careful.

Not loudly. Daniel had never done anything loudly; he was the quiet one, the younger one, the one with the long fingers and the habit of looking at you a beat too long after you'd finished speaking, as if your words needed inspecting for flaws. He worked the card room from the age of fourteen. He learned to read off Tomas's proofs, standing behind the composing stone while Tomas locked up funeral notices and flour advertisements, and he read the way some men drank — steadily, secretly, with a growing thirst. By nineteen he could set type better than Tomas's apprentice. By twenty-two he was keeping his lists.

By twenty-four he was dead.

Two winters ago this December. Everyone in Marlow Bend could date their losses the way farmers dated hailstorms, and everyone could date theirs to the same fortnight — the cold snap when the river froze at the weir and the mill ran double shifts to make up the orders lost to ice on the canal.

Daniel had come home on the Thursday. That much was certain, because he had eaten supper — mutton and boiled potatoes, and he'd complained about the potatoes the way he always did, gently, making it a joke between him and their mother — and he had sat afterward with the newspaper he couldn't afford and read every word of it, including the shipping news, which interested him for reasons he never explained. Then he'd said he was going out to see a fellow about a dog, which was what he always said when he was going to see a fellow about nothing a Harker ought to know about, and he had kissed their mother's cheek, and he had gone out into the dark.

He did not come home Friday. Or Saturday. On Sunday the mill announced that a workman had suffered a fatal accident in the engine house during the night shift, name withheld pending notification of kin, condolences extended, a week's wages paid to the family in consideration.

A week's wages. Forty-one pounds, six shillings, if a man lasted twenty years. Tomas had counted it out on a morning exactly like this one, fog on the glass, coppers smelling of the office, and he had understood with a clarity that had never since left him that the figure was not payment for a life. It was payment for silence. It was the going rate. It was what the mill believed a man's name was worth, and the answer, apparently, was less than the cost of the coffin — which the family was expected to arrange themselves.

Except they had never notified any kin, because no kin had ever been told anything at all. The body — or the story of a body — had been closed away inside the green ledger as neatly as type inside a chase. *Fatal accident, engine house, night shift.* Four words. Four words, and somewhere a compositor at the *Marlow Bend Advertiser* had set them in brevier, and the town had read them over breakfast, and that had been the whole of the record.

Their mother had waited up for six months. She left the lamp lit in the front room until the oil ran dry, every night, until Tomas began quietly refilling it himself rather than let her learn the darkness was permanent. She still set three plates on Sundays. She would deny it if asked. He had stopped asking.

Because here was the thing Tomas could not prove and could not stop believing: Daniel had not died in the engine house. Daniel had been afraid of the engine house since boyhood — the heat, the noise, the way the big flywheel threw shadows like a bird of prey across the floor. He'd traded shifts away from it for years. Men did not trade shifts away for a decade and then volunteer on the coldest night of the winter.

What Daniel had done instead — what Tomas believed with the faith of a man who has nothing else — was go into the mill after hours to copy the green ledger. Because he'd heard, from someone, from somewhere, that the accident book was kept in duplicate, and that the second book — the honest one, the one with dates that preceded the injuries they described, the one that showed a weaver's crushed hand recorded a week before the machine that crushed her had supposedly been repaired — lived in the foreman's office drawer, and that Brack kept the key on his watch chain like a relic.

Daniel had gone to steal the truth. And the truth had cost forty-one pounds, six shillings, and a lamp left burning for six months.

Tomas had never written any of this down. That was the discipline. That was the plank over the flood. He printed the *Winter Count* — eight hundred words, unsigned, unattributed, sold for a penny under the counter to men who folded it into their caps — and in eight hundred words he had never once put a name in, neither the dead nor the living nor his own. Names got people visited. Names got shops inspected for seditious literature. Names got brothers' disappearances confirmed retroactively, if confirmation was even possible, which he had begun to doubt.

But lately the lists Daniel had started — the ones Tomas had inherited along with the pencil stubs and the hymnal margins — had begun to grow again. Three names since September. A scald, a crush, and a fall that the ledger called a faint. And the fog this morning smelled like the fog of two winters ago, and the mill whistle had sounded the half-hour wrong twice this week, and Tomas stood at his counter counting money that was not his brother's blood price but felt like it anyway, and thought: the green ledger is thinner than it should be. Somebody is tearing pages out now. Somebody wants even the fiction to shrink.

* * *

He opened the shop at seven because opening late drew eyes, and eyes were the one commodity Marlow Bend produced more cheaply than cloth. By half past he had sold a hundred visiting cards to Mrs. Ellery the draper's wife, run off five dozen invoices for the corn chandler, and taken an order for memorial cards — which he always priced low, and never asked about, because in Marlow Bend memorial cards were a growth industry and asking questions about growth industries was how a printer became a statistic.

The bell above the door went at ten, and Constable Pike came in with his cap in his hands, which meant either courtesy or apology, and with Pike it was usually both.

"Morning, Tom."

"Aldous." Tomas set the last galley of the corn chandler's invoices aside. "You're up early. Something happen?"

"No." Pike looked at the composing stone, at the walls with their samples of wallpaper — the pretty French papers, the florals and stripes, behind two of which lived things no wallpaper salesman had ever shipped — and then looked carefully at nothing, which was Pike's specialty. "Just walking my beat. Thought I'd say the frost'll be hard tonight. Pipes froze at the King's Arms last night, whole cellar flooded. Might want your taps dripped."

"I might," said Tomas, who understood perfectly, and who also understood that Pike understood that he understood, and that this was the closest thing to friendship the law allowed a policeman in a company town.

Pike lingered. He turned his cap in his hands. He was a big man gone soft at the middle, with a face like a dropped loaf, and he had pulled Daniel out of the card-room fire of '11 — carried him out over his shoulder with his own sleeves burning — and Tomas had never mentioned it and Pike had never mentioned it and between the two silences there existed a debt larger than either man could comfortably stand near.

"They'll be calling the inquiry soon," Pike said, to the wallpaper. "Come spring. Official. Magistrate from over at Ashcombe, clerks, all that. Put it all in the record, tidy as you like."

"Tidy," Tomas agreed.

"Fellow came ahead of it, I hear. A clerk. Getting rooms at the George." Pike's voice did the thing it did when it was delivering information while pretending to be discussing weather. "Asking after conditions. Asking after — sentiment."

"Sentiment," said Tomas again. "That's a word for it."

"Well." Pike settled his cap on. "Drip your taps."

He went out, and the bell spoke above him, and Tomas stood in the smell of lead and lavender and thought about the word *sentiment*, which was what power called grief when it wanted to file it.

* * *

At noon he climbed the stairs to the flat to eat, and found his mother at the window with her sewing, watching the fog, and the third plate already laid beside his own.

"Mutton," she said, as if answering a question from an hour ago. "And there's bread."

They ate mostly in the comfortable silence of people who have shared a table for thirty years, and it was only when she rose to fetch the teapot that she paused at the dresser and touched — lightly, with one finger, the way you'd touch a bruise to check whether it still hurt — the small tin box that sat among the crockery. Not the box downstairs. A smaller one, rust-spotted, that had held Daniel's pencil stubs and his margin-scrapings, which Tomas had rescued from under his brother's bed and never had the heart to move further than arm's reach from wherever he slept.

"You've the look of him today," she said, not turning around. "When he used to count things in his head. You get the same wrinkle. Here." She touched her own brow. "Like arithmetic hurts."

"It does, sometimes."

She nodded as though this were a complete and satisfactory exchange, and poured the tea, and sat, and said, apropos of the fog, "Mrs. Tregenna's nephew's girl is coming to stay. Up from the coast. She says the roads are dreadful and the bridge won't hold the winter."

"The bridge holds every winter."

"This year it's older." Her needle found its thread again. "Everything's older. Even the lies wear out, Tom. That's the one comfort."

He looked at her — small, straight-backed, seventy-one, sewing a hem for a child not yet born into a town that ate its children — and he almost told her. Almost opened the box on the dresser and spread Daniel's pencil ghosts across the table and said, *Mam, I'm going to print them. Every number, every date, every name I can find. The inquiry is coming and they'll sand it smooth unless there's ink underneath, and I mean to be the ink.* He almost said it because she deserved to hear it from him before she read it folded inside a stranger's cap.

But the discipline held, as it always held, and he said only, "More tea?" and she said yes, and the third plate sat gleaming between them like a question nobody answered.

* * *

It was dusk when the visitor came — a woman's knock, brisk, three beats, and when Tomas opened the door he found the lady chauffeur from the Marchmont party standing on the landing with a leather case under her arm and fog in her hair. Miss Joubert. The one with the steady eyes who watched doors the way he watched compositors' hands.

"Begging pardon for the hour, Mr. Harker. Mrs. Marchmont's compliments — she's had a letter come by the evening ferry before it closes, and she wonders — she asks whether the shop could run off a few copies of a circular. Speech text. For the meeting tomorrow." A pause, precise as a full stop. "She'd pay the usual rate. And she said to tell you—" the woman's gaze flicked past his shoulder, into the warm lamplit interior, and back, "—that she understands some jobs are more delicate than others, and pays accordingly."

Tomas held the door and did not step aside yet. Outside, the fog pressed close against the stairwell, and somewhere down by the river the mill whistle blew the six o'clock, and he thought: *she knows*. He didn't know how he knew that she knew — whether Edith Marchmont had simply guessed at what lived behind the wallpaper samples, or whether the letter in that leather case contained something that would make guessing unnecessary — but the certainty arrived whole, the way certain convictions do, wearing its coat already.

"Tell Mrs. Marchmont," he said slowly, "that the shop takes delicate work. That we take it seriously." He stepped back and let the lamplight fall outward onto the stairs. "And tell her the rate depends on the reading."

Miss Joubert's expression did not change — but something behind it shifted, the way type shifts in a loose forme, one line sliding almost imperceptibly against another — and she said, "I'll give her your message exactly," in a voice that suggested she gave very few messages exactly, and followed him down into the smell of lead and paste and lavender, where the gas jets were waiting to be lit, and the night's real work had not yet begun.
