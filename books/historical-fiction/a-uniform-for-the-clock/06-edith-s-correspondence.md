## 6. Edith's Correspondence

The flood had been in the river three days when Rosalind resumed her old habit.

She told herself it was housekeeping. That was the word she preferred — *housekeeping* — as though the envelopes she opened with steam were so many dusty rooms to be set in order. The kettle in the Harkers' back kitchen was small and copper-bellied, quicker to boil than the great iron range at home, and Rosalind had come to prefer it for precisely that reason. Speed mattered. Edith Marchmont rose at seven, dressed by half-past, and took her correspondence at the parlour table by eight o'clock sharp, which left Rosalind a window of perhaps forty minutes between the post's arrival and its presentation. In six years she had never once missed the window. It was the one discipline in her life she had chosen for herself, and she guarded it the way other women guarded reputations or jewellery — jealously, secretly, without apology.

She carried the day's mail up the narrow stairs two letters at a time, laid them on the towel spread across her bed, and set the kettle going. The room above the print shop smelled of ink and lamp oil and, faintly, of the wet wool drying on the line below; the flood had taken everyone's washing twice over. Through the floorboards she could hear Tomas Harker moving among his cases, the soft metallic whisper of type being distributed, a sound like rain on a distant roof. She had begun to find it soothing. She had not yet decided whether that frightened her.

The kettle sang. She poured a thin ribbon of steam across the flap of the first envelope and worked the bone-handled knife along the seam, gently, the way she imagined a surgeon opening a patient — respect for what lay beneath, and no intention of leaving a mark. The glue lifted clean. She read.

Most of it was what it always was: arrangements, invitations, the small administrative machinery of a movement that ran on paper and stubbornness. A Mrs. Aldous-Whitby (no relation, Rosalind noted, to the constable, though she amused herself with the coincidence) offering her drawing room in Reading for a February meeting. A treasurer's note about subscriptions in arrears. A cutting from a Manchester paper enclosed by an admirer, with a covering letter whose prose ran to gush. Rosalind read all of it anyway. She read everything. That was the rule she had made for herself years ago and never broken, because the one time you skimmed would be the one time it mattered.

She resealed each envelope with a sliver of gum arabic from the little tin in her reticule, pressed it flat beneath the brass paperweight she kept on the washstand for the purpose, and laid it in the *done* pile face down. By quarter past seven she had dealt with nine letters and was beginning on the tenth when she came upon the thick one, the one addressed not in a correspondent's hand but in Edith's own — outgoing, then, returned or copied — and her hands went still above the steam.

It was addressed to a Miss Verity Oakeshott, care of an office on Chancery Lane, London. Rosalind did not know the name. In six years of reading Edith Marchmont's correspondence she had never once encountered the name Verity Oakeshott, and Edith did not have correspondents Rosalind hadn't met. That was another of Rosalind's quiet certainties, one of the load-bearing walls of her private architecture: she knew every brick of the woman's epistolary life. To find an unfamiliar name was like coming home to find a door in a wall where no door had been.

The seal had already been broken and rejoined — clumsily, she thought at first, and then, looking closer, not clumsily at all but deliberately, the wax scored and reset so that it might appear tampered with by someone less careful than herself. Or more careful. Rosalind sat with that for a moment. There were only two explanations: either Edith had sealed it badly, which Edith Marchmont did not do, or Edith had wanted whoever intercepted this letter to know it had been read — and to wonder who else had read it before them.

Rosalind unfolded the pages.

*My dear V.,*

*You will forgive brevity; the town is flooded in every sense and I write by lamplight while our hostess's girl sleeps above us.*

Rosalind paused at *our hostess's girl*. Herself. So Edith knew — or assumed — that her assistant read her letters. Had known all along? Had counted on it? Rosalind felt the peculiar vertigo of a spy discovering herself observed, a sensation not unlike catching her own reflection in a shop window when she had believed herself invisible. She made herself continue.

*You asked in your last for numbers, and I will give you what I have, though I warn you they are softer than stone and harder than rumour. The winter count for Marlow Bend stands thus: eleven dead in six winters that I can substantiate, four of them since Michaelmas two years past. Three drowned or crushed at the sluice gates in the November floods of '11 — the mill called it one accident and paid out once. A boy of sixteen, name withheld even from me, mangled in the carding room last spring; the ledger says "injury," the grave says otherwise, and I have seen the grave. Two more I cannot yet name but can number, if you follow me, and I think you do.*

*The mill keeps no list. That is the whole art of it. No names, no dates entered after the fact, no coroner summoned where the family can be bought. What cannot be recorded cannot be inquired into, and what cannot be inquired into did not happen. You know the argument; you have heard it made in committee rooms with better manners and worse consequences.*

*I am sitting on this until spring. Say nothing, print nothing, and above all send no one. An inquiry convened now, with the bridge washed away and the town sealed in ice, would find nothing but grateful widows coached into silence and a magistrate inclined to take the mill's word for arithmetic. Better to let them convene their tidy little hearing in April and produce their tidy little report, and to have our answer ready when the country asks why the report is a lie. Patience, V. We have waited six years for Marlow Bend to make itself useful. It has finally obliged.*

*Burn this. — E.M.*

Rosalind read it twice more.

Then she got up, crossed to the window, and stood there a long while with her arms folded tight against her ribs, watching the brown water slide past the end of Mill Lane where the bridge used to be.

*Eleven dead in six winters.* The numbers sat in her the way stones sit in a shoe — small, hard, impossible to ignore once noticed. She thought of the blackened windows she'd seen on their first evening, the men in doorways who watched without waving. She thought of Tomas Harker's careful hands, and the way he'd said *the mill pays its debts* in a voice that had closed like a ledger. She thought of Mrs. Quill the landlady mentioning, in passing, that the churchyard had needed new ground consecrated two winters running, and how nobody had remarked on it.

And underneath all of it, colder than the river: *I am sitting on this until spring.*

Edith knew. Edith had known for months — longer than months, if the count reached back six years — and had said nothing. Not to the town, not to the widows, not to her. Edith had stood at the textile workers' meeting three nights ago and spoken for forty minutes on dignity and franchise and the long patience of history, and had gone home afterward and written *say nothing, print nothing, send no one* to a woman in Chancery Lane whom Rosalind had never heard of.

Patience, V.

Rosalind looked down at the letter in her hand and understood, with the clarity that comes only after a shock has finished arriving, that she was holding something far heavier than paper. This was not gossip. This was not even evidence, exactly. This was arithmetic — human lives converted into strategy, weighed against a national cause, and found convenient. *Better to let them convene their tidy little hearing.* The widows of Marlow Bend were to be permitted their grief in April rather than December because April suited the movement's calendar. Somewhere in London a report would be issued and denounced, and the denunciation would sell newspapers and fill halls, and eleven people would remain under consecrated ground with no names in any ledger except the one now folded in Rosalind Joubert's hand.

And she — she had been the instrument of it. Six years of steaming envelopes, of resealing seams perfectly, and she had never once stopped to ask what the perfect sealing served. She had told herself she read for hunger, for curiosity, for self-protection, and all of that had been true, and all of it had also been cover. She had been the lock on Edith's door, turning smoothly for anyone who knew the trick of it.

Had Edith chosen her for exactly that? Hired a driver who could keep silent and read quickly and leave no mark on the wax? The thought arrived unbidden and refused to leave. *Our hostess's girl sleeps above us* — not *my driver*, not *Miss Joubert*. *Girl.* As though she were furniture. As though she were part of the machinery, reliable and unseen, like the kettle or the press below.

No. That was unfair, probably. Probably Edith trusted her; probably *girl* was only Edith's shorthand, the same shorthand that reduced whole towns to useful instruments. But the word stung nonetheless, and Rosalind had learned long ago that a sting was information. She did not swat it away.

* * *

She resealed the letter. It took longer than usual, not because the work was difficult but because her hands wanted to shake and she would not permit them to. Gum arabic, press, thirty seconds under the weight. When she turned it over the scored wax caught the lamplight and she examined it again, closely, tilting it toward the flame.

Someone else had opened this letter before her.

Not Edith — the scoring was too crude for Edith's deliberate work. Someone with strong fingers and no patience for finesse, someone who had wanted the recipient to know the letter had been read and had not cared much beyond that. She turned it in the light. The flap had been forced slightly, wrinkled where a thumb had pressed too hard. A man's thumb, she judged. Large.

Her mouth went dry.

There was exactly one person in Marlow Bend with large thumbs, a grudge, and a professional interest in what Edith Marchmont wrote to London. Foreman Brack had made a point of being present when they arrived; had offered to carry their luggage with a smile that never reached anywhere near his eyes; had said, *We keep a close eye on visitors here, miss, especially ones with printing connections,* in a tone that pretended to be a joke.

If Brack had read this letter — if Brack knew that Edith Marchmont held eleven names' worth of ammunition against his mill and was choosing to withhold it — then Edith's strategy of patient silence was already compromised, and compromised in the worst way. Not exposed, which might have shamed the mill into caution, but *known*, which meant the mill could prepare. Could coach its widows. Could burn whatever records survived. Could ensure that when the magistrate came in April, the tidy report would be tidier still.

Or — and Rosalind made herself consider it, because considering unpleasant things thoroughly was the one talent she fully trusted in herself — the letter had been opened and resealed by Edith herself, twice over, as theatre. A message within a message: *let them see that I see.* Edith Marchmont played chess with correspondence the way other women played solitaire.

Rosalind set the letter atop the done pile, squared its edges, and sat down on the edge of her bed with her head in her hands.

Below her, through the floorboards, the sound of type continued: that soft rain-like whisper, patient, indifferent. Somewhere in this building, behind wallpaper samples, was a press capable of putting names into ledgers where the mill had left none. She had noticed the trays her second evening — noticed, catalogued, and filed the observation away the way she filed everything, without acting. She had told herself it was none of her affair. She was a driver. Her business was roads and schedules and the flawless resealing of other people's secrets.

But eleven people were buried without names, and the woman she had served for six years had decided their deaths were more valuable as a spring scandal than as a winter truth, and somewhere in the dark a man with large thumbs was reading the mail ahead of both of them.

The clock on the mantel — the Harkers kept good clocks; printers always did, she was learning — ticked toward half past seven. In twenty minutes Edith would sit at the parlour table and receive her correspondence with every appearance of surprise, and Rosalind would hand it to her with every appearance of innocence, and neither of them would say a word about what passed between them, because that was the arrangement, the unspoken contract of six years.

For the first time, Rosalind wondered what would happen if she broke it.

She dressed. She brushed her hair, pinned it, checked her cuffs. She gathered the letters into their leather folder, squaring the Oakeshott letter in the middle of the stack where it would look like nothing at all, and paused with her hand on the door.

One thing she allowed herself, standing there in the smell of ink and lamplight: she memorised the letter. All of it. Every figure, every phrase, the address in Chancery Lane, the instruction to burn. She had spent six years cultivating a memory that could hold a page entire, and it had never once been for anything but her own private keeping. Now, for the first time, the habit had a purpose she could name.

She went downstairs and handed Edith her morning mail, and watched Edith's eyes flick — just flick, no longer than a heartbeat — to the middle of the stack, and then to Rosalind's face.

"Anything of interest, Rosalind?" Edith asked mildly, breaking the scored seal with her thumb.

"The weather, ma'am," Rosalind said. "And the flood. Nothing else."

Edith smiled, and began to read, and Rosalind stood by the window with her hands folded, wondering which of them was lying, and how many others in this waterlocked town already knew the winter count — and what, exactly, the man with the large thumbs intended to do about it.
