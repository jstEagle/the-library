## 7. The Magistrate's Clerk

The clerk arrived on a Thursday, which was the day the Harkers' shop smelled most of glue and vinegar, and he arrived the way official things always arrive in a river town — by water, wrapped in oilcloth, preceded by rumor.

Rosalind heard him before she saw him. She was in the print shop's front room ostensibly to collect Edith's correspondence from the Marlow Bend post office — a duty she had performed in eleven towns without once being thanked for it — when a boy of perhaps nine came pelting down Bridge Street shouting that there was a gentleman at the Crown and Anchor with a government satchel and a mustache like a hedgehog. The boy said *government* the way other children said *dragon*, with equal parts terror and delight, and then he vanished into an alley as though the words themselves might be traced back to his mouth.

Rosalind stood very still in the doorway of Harker & Son, Job Printing, and felt something turn over in her chest that she could not immediately name. It took her several seconds to identify it, because she had not felt it in years: hope. Brief, treacherous hope — the thought, unbidden and unwelcome, that if a government man had come to Marlow Bend, perhaps someone had finally written to someone who mattered about the winter count.

Then she corrected herself, because she knew better than almost anyone alive how governments wrote letters. She had steamed open enough of them to know their grammar. Governments did not send clerks to small towns out of moral urgency. They sent them out of embarrassment management, or land disputes, or because a Member of Parliament had received a letter from a constituent whose cousin had drowned, and the Member wished to be able to say, at the next session, that the matter was *in hand*.

She walked up Bridge Street through the thin cold rain that had been falling since Sunday, and she watched the town watch her watching it. That was the thing about Marlow Bend that no visitor ever quite articulated: it did not merely contain watchers; it was composed of them, the way coral is composed of polyps. Men stood in doorways with pipes they were not smoking. Women leaned from upper windows with linens they had already finished shaking. And every eye, Rosalind noticed now with a new and colder clarity, tracked past her to the Crown and Anchor's door, where a light burned even though the morning had barely managed to decide to begin.

Inside, the public bar smelled of wet wool and yesterday's beer. The gentleman with the satchel sat alone at a corner table with a cup of tea he had clearly ordered as a prop, because he held it in both hands and drank from it never. He was younger than his mustache suggested — twenty-eight at the outside — and he wore his coat buttoned to the throat despite the warmth of the room, and he had spread upon the table before him, with the careful geometry of a man arranging an altar, a leather writing case, a blotter, three pens, and a small tin of ink.

"Mr. Pell," Rosalind said, having read his name off the oilcloth tag the boy had neglected to mention but which dangled from the satchel's handle in neat copperplate. "I'm Rosalind Joubert. I drive for Mrs. Marchmont. She'll want to know what you've come about."

Something flickered across the young man's face — relief, mostly, and beneath it a quick professional disappointment that his name had been so easily taken. Clerks, Rosalind had learned from their correspondence, hated to be legible.

"Mrs. Marchmont." He pronounced it as though checking it against an internal list. "The suffragist."

"The speaker," Rosalind said mildly. "Among other things."

He closed the tea untouched, gathered his altar into its case, and stood. "I'd rather explain once than twice, madam. If your mistress is agreeable, I'll call this afternoon. Official capacity." He produced a card as though it might bite him. *Mr. Ambrose Pell, Clerk to his Worship the Magistrate, County Sessions.* The card stock was good. The ink was cheap. Rosalind filed both facts away in the part of her mind that never stopped filing, the part that had made her useful and solitary in roughly equal measure.

"You've come ahead of the ferry," she observed. "The bridge is out. Nothing official crosses until spring, they told us."

"I crossed at Harrow Weir," Mr. Pell said. "By arrangement. His Worship felt the inquiry should not wait on the weather." A pause, and then, with the first honest note she would hear from him all day: "Nor on anyone's memory improving."

* * *

Edith received him in the Marchmonts' borrowed parlor at the Harker house — for the flood had settled them, all three of them, into the rooms above the print shop, Edith in the best bedchamber with the river view, Rosalind in the narrow one behind it, and the whole arrangement wearing the thin disguise of hospitality stretched over the skeleton of siege. Edith sat with her hands folded and her spine doing what her spine always did, which was to suggest that she found the furniture adequate but the century disappointing.

Mr. Pell set up his little altar on the parlor table and spoke for eleven minutes without once consulting notes, which impressed Rosalind against her will. He had done this speech before, in other towns, to other frightened committees. It had the worn smoothness of a stone carried in a pocket.

An inquiry, he explained, would convene in the county sessions come spring — April, or May at latest, once the roads allowed. Its remit was *labor conditions in the textile trades of this district*. It would take written evidence and oral testimony. It would publish findings. His Worship was personally committed to thoroughness. Forms were available for any person wishing to submit evidence; the forms required a full name, an address, and an occupation.

"And witnesses will be protected," Edith said. It was not quite a question.

Mr. Pell's pen hovered over his blotter. "His Worship's inquiry is a court of record, madam. Evidence given is evidence given publicly."

"Meaning that a widow who testifies that her husband died in the number three shed will walk home afterward past the foreman who signed the report calling it a fall on the ice. In January. In a shed with a roof over it."

"Meaning, madam—" and here Mr. Pell looked, for one naked moment, like a young man who had rehearsed a speech and discovered mid-delivery that he believed less of it each time he gave it— "meaning that the law provides what the law provides. His Worship is aware of the... sensitivities. Which is precisely why the inquiry sits in spring. Feelings run high in winter. In spring, the district will have had time to reflect."

"To forget," Rosalind said, from her chair by the window.

Both of them looked at her. She had not meant to speak. Or rather, she had meant to — some part of her had been composing the sentence since the word *spring* left his mouth — but hearing it aloud in the quiet parlor, with the rain ticking against the glass and the smell of ink drifting up through the floorboards from the shop below, she felt it land in the room like a dropped glass.

Mr. Pell recovered first. "Reflection," he repeated, with dignity, "is not forgetting, madam."

"In my experience," Rosalind said, "it usually is. Reflection is what we call it when we have decided in advance what we will remember."

There was a silence of a particular quality — the silence of a man deciding whether he has been insulted by a servant. Edith, to her credit, let it stand a moment longer than courtesy strictly required, and Rosalind understood that she was being permitted her sharpness the way one permits a dog one bark, and filed that understanding away too, in the drawer where she kept the growing ledger of small condescensions.

"Spring," Mr. Pell said finally, "is when the inquiry sits. The forms are available at the post office. Evidence may be submitted in writing under seal, should any person wish it, though of course unsigned material carries little weight." He packed his pens with small precise clicks. "His Worship asks me to convey that he comes to Marlow Bend with no preconceptions whatsoever."

"No preconceptions," Edith echoed pleasantly. "How restful for him. Most men bring at least one."

After he had gone — after the door had closed on his umbrella and his careful footsteps on the stairs — Edith turned to Rosalind with an expression Rosalind had learned to read the way sailors read cloud banks.

"Well?" Edith said. "You didn't like him."

"He's not the point. He's a pen with legs. The point is what writes him." Rosalind moved to the window. Below, through the gap between buildings, she could see the river, brown and swollen, carrying branches and fence rails and one entire henhouse downstream toward the sea, unhurried, entitled, taking whatever it pleased. "They sent him *before* the ferry resumed, Edith. They spent money and trouble getting one clerk across at Harrow Weir in February. You don't do that for a routine inquiry. You do that when you want the announcement made while the bridge is still down — while nobody can leave to petition, nobody can write to London papers, nobody can fetch a barrister. The inquiry is announced in a locked room."

"Or," Edith said, "his Worship is merely diligent."

"Diligent men don't cross flooded rivers to announce a spring sitting." Rosalind kept her voice level. She had long ago mastered the trick of disagreeing with Edith Marchmont in a tone indistinguishable from agreeing with her; it was, she suspected, why she still had the position. "Someone wants the town to know the inquiry is coming and know that nothing can be done about anything until it does. Four months, Edith. Four months of forms and reflection. By April the widows will have been visited. The ledgers will have been tidied. Anyone inclined to testify will have been reminded what happens to families of witnesses in mill towns. Spring isn't a season. It's a strategy."

Edith was quiet for a moment, and Rosalind watched her do the arithmetic — she could see it happening behind her eyes, the same cool columned arithmetic Rosalind had admired for two years and trusted absolutely and, lately, begun to fear.

"Then we use the time better than they expect us to," Edith said at last. "If they've given us four months of a captive audience, we'll fill every week of it. Meetings. Testimony gathering. Names, Rosalind. What that inquiry will need more than anything is names, given openly, on the record, and we'll have them ready."

"And when the names are given openly," Rosalind said, "the people who own this town will know exactly whom to ruin first."

"That," said Edith Marchmont, in the tone she used for principles rather than people, "is the price of the record."

Rosalind looked out at the river again. The henhouse had gone from view. Somewhere below her feet, faintly, she could hear Tomas Harker's press beginning its afternoon work — the rhythmic iron breathing of it, a sound she had lived above for three weeks now without fully noticing, the way one stops hearing one's own heartbeat. She thought of the type trays hidden behind the wallpaper samples, and said nothing.

* * *

That evening she went down to the shop, ostensibly about paper.

It had become their ritual, hers and Tomas's, though neither of them called it that. She would descend the stairs on some errand — paper, ribbon, a question about a printing estimate Edith needed for leaflets — and he would be working, and she would stay, and they would talk about nothing with the particular intensity of two people circling a something. Tonight the shop was warm from the stove, and Tomas was setting type by lamplight, his sleeves rolled, his forearms black to the elbow, moving among the trays with a fluency that seemed to Rosalind, who had grown up clumsy, like a kind of music played on wood and lead.

"Pell came to the Anchor first," Tomas said, without looking up. "Bought nothing. Asked the landlord whether the town had 'many literate residents.'" His fingers walked along a tray, plucking letters. "Landlord told him everyone here reads except the magistrate's witnesses."

"What did Pell say?"

"Said, 'That can be arranged.'" Tomas set a line in the stick, checked it upside down against the light with a squint, and only then glanced at her. "You think I'm joking. I'm not. That's what he said. Word for word. The Anchor's landlord tells me everything; he owes me for wedding invitations he couldn't pay for in 1911."

Rosalind turned the phrase over. *That can be arranged.* Spoken lightly, perhaps. A clerk's joke in a public bar. But she had read ten thousand sentences written by men like Ambrose Pell, and she knew how jokes worked as instruments. A joke was how a threat traveled in daylight without leaving fingerprints.

"Tomas," she said. "What would happen here if the inquiry sat in spring and nobody testified?"

He set down the composing stick and looked at her properly, and she saw in his face the thing she had been learning to see these past weeks — the grief that lived in him dressed as caution, wearing caution's coat and hat, waving caution's hand at passersby.

"Nothing would happen," he said. "That's the whole art of it, Miss Joubert. Nothing would happen. There'd be a nice room in the sessions house, and a nice lunch, and his Worship would hear from the mill owners and the vicar and the schoolmaster, and somebody would say the workforce is contented, and somebody else would say accidents are regrettable but few, and in June there'd be a report saying conditions are satisfactory, and it would go in the county archive between a drainage survey and the minutes of a poultry committee." He picked the stick up again. "And my brother stays a statistic without a name. Forever. Because the record was written by men who bought their tea and never drank it."

Rosalind stood in the lamplight and felt the sentence go through her like a needle finding the place in the fabric where the seam must start.

"The record," she repeated quietly.

"The record," Tomas agreed. Then, softer, watching her: "You understand records, don't you? I've seen how you look at paper. You don't read like the rest of them. You read like someone keeping accounts."

She should have deflected. She had a career's worth of deflections stored and sorted, and in eleven towns she had deployed them flawlessly. But standing in the warm ink-smelling dark of the shop, with the stove ticking and the river muttering beyond the walls and the words *that can be arranged* still lodged in her ear like a splinter, Rosalind Joubert heard herself say:

"Somebody has to remember exactly, or the forgetting wins."

Tomas held her gaze a moment longer. Whatever he saw there — and he saw a great deal more, she suspected, than she had ever meant to show anyone — he nodded once, slowly, and turned back to his tray.

"Four months," he said. "Then we'd best not waste the winter."

Upstairs, later, Rosalind sat at her narrow desk with Edith's mail spread before her and her kettle steaming, and she did not steam open a single envelope. Instead she took out a clean sheet of the shop's good rag paper and wrote at the top of it, in her small exact hand, a heading she had never expected to write:

*Evidence — Marlow Bend — Winter 1913.*

Below it she wrote the date, and the names of the dead she knew so far, and drew lines for the ones she would learn.

Outside, the rain went on falling on a town that had just been formally informed that its memory had an expiration date. Rosalind dipped her pen, and began — carefully, secretly, in a hand no one would ever trace to a driver of carriages — to make sure that it didn't.
