## 8. Constance Vane, Mill Owner's Daughter

The ledgers did not lie, which was precisely why Constance Vane kept two of them.

Her father's books — the ones bound in red calf and displayed, when occasion demanded, to bank managers and magistrates' clerks with the pride of a man showing off grandchildren — recorded the mill as a benevolent institution. Wages paid on time. Safety lamps issued. A charitable disbursement here, a widow's pension there, each figure entered in her father's own hand, the numerals fat and self-satisfied.

Her own set lived in a green exercise book she had bought at the stationer's under the pretext of keeping accounts for the Dorcas Society. It sat in the bottom drawer of her desk, beneath a folder of invoices for lamp oil, and it recorded what the mill actually was: every injury reported to the office and then quietly reclassified; every compensation negotiated down by Foreman Brack with his talent for making grief feel like an imposition; every man carried out of the weaving shed who did not come back, entered not by name but by the date, because the dates were all she could get, and because the dates, arranged in a column, told their own terrible story.

Two winters ago there had been three entries in December. Last winter, five.

She had begun the second book three years ago, quite by accident. A clerk named Pell had misfiled an accident report in with the dye orders, and Constance, reading it before returning it, had noticed that the report described a hand caught in the carding machine on the fourteenth of November — while the official ledger, which she knew almost by heart, recorded no accident at all until the twenty-first of November, and then only a scald from the boiling house. Two accidents, two men, one entry. She had gone back through six years of files that night with a cold lamp and colder hands, and by morning she understood that the discrepancy was not slovenliness. Slovenliness scattered. This was patterned. This was *tidy*.

She had not confronted her father. That was the thing people who wrote about daughters of mill owners — and nobody wrote about them, but if they had — never seemed to grasp. You could not confront a man like Edmund Vane about anything, because he would not defend himself; he would simply look at you with mild disappointment, the way one looks at a machine producing slightly substandard cloth, and thereafter something in the house would be subtly rearranged against you. Her allowance. Her correspondence. The locks on the study door. He would never raise his voice. He had never once raised his voice. The foremen did that for him.

So she kept the green book, and said nothing, and ran his office with an efficiency that made him boast of her to visitors — *my Constance keeps better books than any man I've employed* — and she smiled at that, and died a little more behind the smile each year, and waited for she did not know what.

* * *

The charity event was Mrs. Aldous Pike's idea — no relation to the constable, though the town found endless amusement in it — a winter bazaar in aid of the Marlow Bend Infirmary, held in the Mechanics' Institute hall because the church hall smelled of damp. Constance had been assigned the raffle table, on the principle that a Vane at the raffle table doubled the takings, guilt being worth more than generosity in a company town.

It was at the raffle table that she first noticed the woman who was not selling anything.

She stood near the doorway with the stillness of someone accustomed to waiting — tall, plainly but well dressed, dark hair pinned with an economy that suggested either poverty or discipline and was, Constance thought, both. She was watching the room. Not idly, not the way ladies watched rooms while arranging their faces to be seen watching. She was reading it. Her eyes moved from group to group with a methodical, unhurried attention, lingering on the two foremen's wives whispering by the tea urn, on Mr. Brack standing too close to the mayor, on the door, on the windows, on the exits. Counting. Weighing.

Constance knew that look. She wore it herself across her father's breakfast table.

"You'll be Miss Marchmont's driver," Constance said, when the woman drifted within range of the raffle table. It was not quite a question. "Rosalind Joubert. I'm told you've been driving her since Harrogate."

"Since before Harrogate." The woman's voice was level, unadorned. Up close there was something watchful even in her stillness, as though conversation were a document she read before signing. "You have me at a disadvantage, miss."

"Constance Vane."

Something moved behind the driver's eyes — quickly, professionally suppressed, but not so quickly that a fellow professional missed it. *Vane.* The name landing like a coin dropped in a quiet room. Constance had watched that same flinch on a hundred faces since she was a girl, and had long ago stopped minding, or had told herself she had.

"Yes," Rosalind Joubert said. "I know."

"Everyone knows." Constance gestured at the raffle prizes arrayed before her: a hamper, a shawl, a clock in a case that did not keep time. "Would you buy a ticket? All proceeds to the infirmary, which the mill endows, which means half your ticket price will return to my father as gratitude and the other half will purchase bandages for injuries his machinery caused. It is a very efficient arrangement."

The words were out before she had decided to say them, sharp and bitter as vinegar, and she braced for the withdrawal, the careful blankness, the *excuse me, miss*. Instead the driver looked at her — really looked, a slow deliberate survey — and took out a penny.

"One ticket," she said. "For the bandages."

They regarded each other over the tin box of raffle tickets, and Constance felt the strange vertigo of recognition — not of the woman herself, whom she had never met, but of something underneath. There was a quality certain people had, she thought: they stood in rooms full of talk and heard none of it, because they were listening to the silences between sentences. Reading the paper that everyone else merely glanced at. She had spent her whole life among people who read documents literally, obediently, and she had learned young to be alone among them. Here, unexpectedly, at a raffle table, was another solitary reader.

"Miss Marchmont is speaking Thursday," Constance said, making change. "At the weavers' meeting. My father has agreed to let the hall be used." She paused. "He agreed rather easily. You may draw whatever conclusion you like from that."

"I usually do."

"I imagine you do. I imagine you draw conclusions professionally." Constance folded her hands on the raffle table. "Tell me, Miss Joubert — does she let you read her letters?"

The question was a blade thrown casually, hilt-first or otherwise she could not yet tell, and she watched it land. The driver's face did nothing — that was the impressive part, the face did absolutely nothing — but her weight shifted onto her back foot, a quarter-inch of retreat, the tell of a woman recalculating a room. And Constance thought: *there. There it is.*

"Why do you ask?"

"Because I run my father's office," Constance said, "and I open his post, and I file his copies, and twice this month I have burned papers he asked me to burn without reading them first. Which is how I know that when a man says *burn this unread*, the paper matters more than anything else in the drawer." She held the driver's gaze. "I am not accusing you of anything, Miss Joubert. I am establishing that we are the same kind of animal. It saves time later."

"Does there tend to be a later?"

"In Marlow Bend there is always a later. Nothing here resolves quickly except injuries." She said it flatly, and saw the driver hear it — really hear it, the way one hears a number in a ledger that shouldn't be there. "My brother stopped coming home—" No. Not hers. She corrected course so smoothly that only a fellow reader would catch the seam. "A weaver's son I grew up with stopped coming home two winters ago. There was a funeral with a closed coffin and a ledger entry reading *fall, stairs*. The stairs in the carding annex are four steps and carpeted. I have measured them. Twice."

Silence between them, and beneath the silence the bazaar went on — the piano stumbling through a hymn arrangement, children shrieking at the bran tub, the great warm indifferent noise of a town enjoying its charity. Constance was aware suddenly that she had said more in ninety seconds than she had said aloud in a year, and to a stranger, a servant, a woman whose loyalty belonged to another camp entirely. It was reckless. It was also, she realized with a sensation like a joint setting after long disuse, the first honest sentence she had spoken since autumn.

"You measured the stairs," Rosalind Joubert said at last. Not a question. An act of witness.

"Four steps. Carpeted. One could hardly fall badly on them if one tried." Constance picked up the raffle tin and shook it, and the pennies rattled like type in a tray, though she did not know yet why that image would matter to her. "So. We are the same kind of animal, you and I. We read what we are not given to read. The difference is that I am locked inside the house where the papers live, and you are free to drive away whenever the bridge allows."

"The bridge doesn't allow much, just now."

"No." Constance allowed herself the smallest smile. "Floods are inconvenient that way. They keep us all in Marlow Bend together, learning things about our neighbours." She slid a raffle ticket across the table — number 47, ink still wet. "Thursday evening, Miss Marchmont speaks. My father will sit in the front row looking generous. Foreman Brack will stand at the back looking bored. Both performances will be flawless. If you should want to know what the town actually thinks of her visit, don't listen to the applause. Count who leaves during the third question."

"And if I want to know what the town thinks of the mill?"

Constance looked past her, to the far wall of the hall, where the infirmary's subscription board hung — gilt letters on black velvet, *GIFT OF E. VANE, ESQ.*, above a list of donors in descending order of importance. Below it, unremarked, hung the framed photograph of last year's infirmary staff, eleven nurses and a matron, smiling stiffly around a stretcher.

"Then count the beds," she said. "Thirty-two beds, endowed by my father's generosity. Ask yourself why a town of four thousand needs thirty-two."

The driver followed her gaze, and Constance watched her do it — watched her take the number in, turn it over, file it somewhere behind those patient grey eyes alongside everything else she had counted since stepping off the ferry. Thirty-two beds. Four thousand souls. A column of figures that added up to something no one said aloud.

"Thank you for the ticket," Rosalind Joubert said finally, tucking number 47 into her coat pocket with a care that suggested she intended to remember it. "And for the arithmetic lesson, Miss Vane."

"We animals of the same kind should help each other with our sums." Constance turned to the next customer — Mrs. Pell, wanting three chances on the shawl — and the moment closed over like water. But as the driver walked away, unhurried, pausing at the infirmary board as if admiring the gilt lettering, Constance caught herself watching her go and thinking not *who is that woman* but *what has she already read in this room that I haven't?*

That night, in the bottom drawer, beneath the lamp-oil invoices, Constance opened the green book and made a new entry. Not an injury. Not a date. Just a name, written small and careful in the margin beside the week's figures:

*R. Joubert — Marchmont's driver — knows how to count.*

She looked at the line a long time, pen hovering. In her father's books, every entry served him. In hers, every entry served the truth, or her idea of it, which might amount to the same ruin. She did not yet know whether the driver was an ally or a hazard, whether Miss Marchmont's business in Marlow Bend was salvation or exploitation wearing salvation's coat, whether the woman who read other people's letters would one day read *hers* — the green book, the true columns, the dates that told their story.

She blotted the line, closed the book, and locked the drawer.

Outside, the river rose in the dark, patient as a clerk, taking its toll of the bridge. And in the morning there would be post to open, figures to enter twice — once for her father, once for the truth — and a raffle ticket numbered 47 that would or would not win the clock that didn't keep time, and Constance Vane, who had spent three years waiting for she did not know what, went to bed with the unfamiliar, dangerous feeling that the waiting was nearly over, and that whatever came next would begin, as all important transactions in Marlow Bend began, with a letter.
