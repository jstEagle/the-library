## 9. The Accident Ledger

The request came on a Thursday, in the voice Edith Marchmont used for ordering tea.

"Rosalind, I shall need you this evening. After supper. Bring your good pen and the ink you mix yourself — the iron-gall, not that violet nonsense from the stationer's. What we write tonight may need to last."

She said it on the stairs of the Harker house, one hand on the banister, her grey head tilted as though she were remarking upon the weather or the quality of the stair carpet. Below them, Mrs. Harker was beating a rug against the porch rail with the steady violence of a woman who had made her peace with dust and intended to make it surrender anyway. The sound came up through the floorboards like a second heartbeat.

Rosalind had learned to read Edith Marchmont's voices the way she read everything else — completely, compulsively, and without permission. There was the platform voice, which could fill a hall and bend it; there was the drawing-room voice, which could flay a Member of Parliament so finely he would thank her on his way out; there was the private voice, dry as August grass, which she used for Rosalind alone. And beneath all of these, rarely surfaced but always audible if one listened — and Rosalind always listened — was the fourth voice: the one that gave orders she did not wish to be heard giving.

This was the fourth voice.

"Tonight," Rosalind said. Not a question. She did not permit herself questions anymore; they invited answers, and answers invited obligations, and obligations were how a woman became someone else's instrument while believing herself merely useful.

"Tonight," Edith agreed. "Wear the dark skirt. And Rosalind—" She paused on the landing, and for a moment something moved behind her face like fish under ice. "Whatever you copy, you will copy exactly. No improvements. No omissions. I know your habits."

It was the nearest thing to an accusation Edith had ever allowed between them, and it passed so quickly that Rosalind might have imagined it, except that her hands went cold, and her hands never lied to her even when her mouth did.

* * *

They waited until the house had settled. Mrs. Harker's footsteps retired overhead at half past nine; Tomas Harker worked late below — Rosalind had heard the press going at odd hours all week, a rhythmic thump-and-hiss through the floor that she told herself was job work, wedding invitations, invoices, the honest bread of small-town printing — and by eleven the only sounds were the river, swollen and restless beyond the black windows, and the tick of the mantel clock in the parlour, which ran four minutes fast and had done so, Mrs. Harker confided, since the Coronation.

Edith had taken the front parlour as her study. She sat now at the table with a single lamp turned low and a shawl around her shoulders despite the warmth of the stove, and beside her, wrapped in a linen cloth like something brought home from the butcher's, lay a bundle of papers.

"You will want to wash your hands first," Edith said. "Not for cleanliness. For grip. Ink slips on greasy fingers, and if you smudge a date we have lost a man."

"A man," Rosalind repeated.

"A number of men." Edith unwrapped the cloth with the deliberation of a surgeon. "Sit where you are, please. Do not touch anything until I hand it to you, and when I hand it to you, hold it by the edges only. Fingerprints can be explained away. A smudge cannot."

Rosalind sat. The lamp threw their two shadows enormous up the wallpaper — the same wallpaper, she noticed, that lined the little room above the print shop, sample papers pasted over older patterns, a house forever covering itself with new faces. She had been in Marlow Bend eleven days. Eleven days of closed bridge and rising water and men who watched from doorways and women who smiled a beat too slowly before answering. Eleven days, and she had begun to understand that the town's courtesy was not warmth. It was a sentry's discipline.

"Do you remember," Edith said, "what I told you about the winter count?"

"I remember you didn't tell me about it."

Something flickered across Edith's mouth — annoyance, or perhaps its cousin, respect. "I am telling you now. Because after tonight you will know it whether I tell you or not, and I prefer my secretaries informed rather than curious. Curiosity is a leak with a pretty face."

She drew out the first paper and laid it flat, turning it so Rosalind could read it upside down, which was, Rosalind reflected, the position she had read most documents in for six years — reversed, intercepted, meant for other eyes.

It was a mill payroll sheet. Ordinary. Columns of names reduced to initials, weeks ending in dates, sums paid out. But down the right margin, in a different hand entirely — cramped, hurried, the writing of a man who expected to be interrupted — ran annotations. *Lost two fingers, L hand. Compensated £3.* *Scald, arm. Returned to work same day.* *Head wound, stitch'd by Dr. Prewitt privately. No entry.*

"No entry," Rosalind read aloud.

"Now this." Edith slid a second sheet from under the cloth. It was torn along one edge, raggedly, as if ripped from a bound book in haste or desperation, and it was this sheet she smoothed flat with both palms, twice, the way one smooths a thing one has looked at too many times already.

It was a ledger page. The heading, in a formal copperplate, read: *Vane Mills — Register of Accidents, 1911–1912.*

And beneath the heading, the entries. Dates running down the left column. Descriptions in the centre. And in the third column, where a diligent clerk should have written outcomes — *recovered*, *dismissed*, *deceased* — the copperplate wrote instead, again and again, in the same untroubled hand:

*Fallen from gantry. Fatal.*
*Caught in carding machine. Fatal.*
*Boiler scald, extensive. Fatal.*
*Fallen into pit gear. Fatal.*

Seven entries. Seven times *fatal*. Seven dates between October 1911 and February 1912 — the winter, Rosalind thought, and then stopped thinking it, because her mind had already gone ahead of her and found what her eyes were still walking toward.

The dates were wrong.

Not wrong the way a forgery is wrong. Wrong the way a confession is wrong. She checked them against the payroll sheet, against the annotations in the cramped marginal hand — *lost two fingers, June 1911; scald, arm, August 1911; head wound, November 1911* — and the injuries recorded as survived, compensated, mended, were dated *after* the accidents recorded as fatal. A man who lost two fingers in June of 1911 had, according to the register, fallen from the gantry and died the previous December. A man scalded in August had been boiled alive in January. The dead men of the official ledger were, by the mill's own wage records, alive and working months after their deaths — injured, yes, hurt badly enough to be paid off in three-pound instalments, but breathing, eating, drawing wages.

Unless, said a voice in Rosalind that she did not want to hear, unless the wages were drawn by other men wearing the same names. Unless the names themselves were the fiction. Unless the register was not a record of accidents at all but a record of *payments* — deaths dressed as accidents, entered backwards, dated conveniently, the way one backdates a receipt.

"Read me the fourth name," Edith said quietly.

Rosalind read it. *Harker, J. Fallen into pit gear. Fatal. 14th December, 1911.*

The clock ticked. Downstairs, or perhaps it was the river, something knocked softly against wood.

"Tomas Harker's brother," Edith said. "Jonah. Except Jonah Harker did not fall into any pit gear, because Jonah Harker was seen alive in the spring of 1912 by no fewer than four witnesses, one of whom is the widow Mrs. Cobb, who will swear to it before God and any magistrate you like. He left the valley in March and died of consumption in Sheffield the following autumn, poor soul, far from any machinery whatsoever. But the register needed a death that December. Something had happened in the pit gear that December — something the mill wished very much to account for — and Jonah's name was available, and Jonah's family was quiet, and quiet families are the mill's favourite raw material."

Rosalind's hands were still holding the page by the edges, exactly as instructed, and she found that she could not put it down. It was not reverence. It was the reader's paralysis, the terrible vertigo of a person who has spent six years reading other people's letters and has finally opened one addressed to her — to her specifically, to the woman she actually was beneath the competence and the steamed envelopes and the perfectly resealed wax — saying: *you knew this world existed, and you thought yourself its tourist.*

"The page is stolen?" she asked. Her voice came out level. She was proud of that later.

"It was given. Which is better and worse. A clerk in the mill office — I will not tell you who, not yet — has been copying pages for us since September. This one reached me ten days ago, sewn into the lining of a hatbox of ribbon samples. You resealed that box yourself, as it happens. Your stitching is very fine."

Eleven days. The box had come and gone eleven days ago, and every hour since, this page had been lying wrapped in linen three feet from where Rosalind slept, and she had known nothing, and worse — she understood now, with a cold settling feeling — she had been *meant* to know nothing. All those months of reading Edith's correspondence, all those evenings with the kettle and the letter-knife, and Edith had known. Of course she had known. You do not run a network for thirty years and fail to notice that your secretary opens your post faster than your secretary should. Edith had let her read. Had fed her, perhaps. Had used her hunger for knowing as a leash.

And yet.

"And the inquiry," Rosalind said. "The magistrate coming in spring. This page—"

"This page is why there will be an inquiry at all, though the magistrate does not know it. London wants the matter handled quietly — an inquiry into 'labor conditions,' held after the thaw, when memories are conveniently waterlogged. Our friend in London believes a page or two, produced delicately, will secure reforms. Wages, hours, perhaps a safety clause." Edith's voice was level, but Rosalind heard the load-bearing beam beneath it. "The widows of this valley believe otherwise. The widows believe seven men died whose names appear nowhere, and that a ledger exists which says so, and that a ledger which lies about the dead will lie about anything."

"What do *you* believe?"

Edith looked at her for a long moment. In the lamplight her face was all planes and decision, a face built for verdicts.

"I believe that a document this dangerous must exist in more than one place, in more than one hand, or it does not exist at all. The original stays hidden. What I require is a fair copy — your hand, not mine — exact to the letter, every date, every spelling, every blot reproduced where reproduction matters. Three copies. One for London. One for the widows' committee. One—" and here the fourth voice returned, soft and absolute, "—for insurance, kept by someone no one will ever think to search. That is you, Rosalind. You will memorise where you hide it and then, ideally, forget it consciously, so that no amount of questioning can extract what your memory refuses to hold."

"You're asking me to become a hiding place."

"I am asking you to become an archive. There is a difference, and the difference is courage."

Rosalind took up her pen. She mixed the ink herself, as instructed — the iron-gall, measured drop by drop, vinegar and gum — and the smell of it rose sharp and old-fashioned, the smell of documents meant to survive their authors. Outside, the river went on worrying the pilings of the vanished bridge. Somewhere below them, faint as a pulse through the floorboards, the press thumped once, twice, and went silent, as if it too were listening.

She copied. Entry by entry, name by name, date by fatal date. And as her pen travelled down the page she felt the dread in her chest — the animal dread of proximity to danger — begin, grain by grain, to change its nature. Dread, she discovered, is a fuel. Fed carefully, it burns toward something else. By the time she reached the fourth entry — *Harker, J.* — the burning had a direction. By the seventh she was not copying a stolen page. She was swearing testimony, line by line, in ink she had made herself.

Because she had read the marginalia too. *Lost two fingers, L hand. Compensated £3.* Somebody's left hand, missing two fingers, somebody who went back to the machine the same week because three pounds buys bread and machines do not wait. She had read thousands of letters in six years and told herself it was self-protection, habit, hunger — anything but what it was. Now the page lay before her telling her plainly: *reading is not neutral. Every letter you open belongs to someone who could not write it safely.*

The last entry dried. Edith inspected each copy against the original, her lips moving slightly, then folded them into three separate envelopes, none of which bore addresses, all of which would travel by different hands to different places.

"Excellent," she said, and meant the copies, and perhaps also the secretary.

But Rosalind, capping her pen, was looking at the linen cloth, now empty, and thinking about Tomas Harker downstairs among his type trays hidden behind wallpaper samples, thinking about a brother who had died twice — once in a forged ledger, once truly, in Sheffield, unmourned by any record at all — and thinking, with a clarity that felt less like thought than like a door opening onto cold air:

Edith wanted the page copied for London, for committees, for reform.

The town wanted something else. The town had a press.

She said nothing. She folded her hands. But the door stayed open behind her ribs all night, and through it she could hear, distinctly, the sound of the river taking the bridge apart piece by piece — and beneath it, patient, rhythmic, waiting for her, the thump and hiss of type being set in the dark.
