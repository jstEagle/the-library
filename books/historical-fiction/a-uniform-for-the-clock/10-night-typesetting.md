## 10. Night Typesetting

The clock above the shop said a quarter past one, and Tomas Harker did not believe it. The old regulator had run slow since the winter his brother died — he had never corrected it, could not have said why, except that some part of him felt the whole house ought to lag behind the world by seven minutes, in mourning, the way a bell tolls after the blow rather than before.

He turned down the lamp until it was a coin of amber on the composing stone, and he began to set type.

*The Winter Count*, number four. Four issues in two years, each one printed between midnight and the grey hour, each one folded into flour sacks and fish boxes and hymnals and carried out of Marlow Bend by hands that had no idea what they carried. His mother believed he was mending watches for the vicar. His sister Nell knew better; Nell fed the press its paper. And their father — their father pretended not to see the ink stains under Tomas's fingernails, which was its own kind of knowledge, and its own kind of mercy.

Four pages this time. Eight hundred words about the count itself: eleven names, eleven deaths in six winters, none of them entered in the mill's ledger as anything but *misadventure*. Eleven names set letter by letter in ten-point Caslon, because if you were going to commit a crime against Mr. Vane's peace of mind you might as well commit it beautifully.

Tomas worked the way his brother had taught him — stick in the left hand, thumb braced, letters lifted from the case with a motion like plucking fruit. *E-L-L-E-N.* He was on the fourth name when the floorboard at the top of the stairs spoke.

It didn't creak. Creaking was accidental. This board *spoke* — a low deliberate groan, the voice of a plank that had been trodden carefully around for two years by everyone in the house and therefore announced any foot that forgot to avoid it. Tomas's hands went still over the case.

Miss Joubert's room was at the top of those stairs.

She had been with them nine days. A driver, Edith Marchmont's driver, quiet as furniture, polite as winter. She took her meals with her eyes down and her answers short, and she walked through the shop each morning with her gaze fixed straight ahead past the cases and the stone and the paper cutter as though they were windows she had no interest in looking out of. Too little interest, Tomas had thought more than once. People who truly didn't care looked around. Only people guarding something kept their eyes so disciplined.

And now she was coming down the stairs at one in the morning, and every story Tomas could build about that ended badly.

He considered the lamp. Considered dousing it, standing in the dark, letting her find an empty room and draw her own conclusions or none. But the forme sat locked on the stone, four columns deep, eleven names shining faintly in the lamplight, and hiding it would take longer than she'd take to descend. So he did the only thing left, which was nothing. He stood where he was, hand resting flat on the type case, and waited.

Her step on the last stair was slow. Not the tread of someone come to catch him — catchers came quick, hungry for the moment. This was the step of someone who had stood at the top of the stairs a long while first, deciding.

She appeared in the doorway with her coat still on, as if she'd meant only to pass through the house and out some other door, and her eyes went — not to him, not to the press, but to the stone, to the forme, reading it upside down and backwards the way printers do and no one else can.

"The *t* in *Wentworth*," she said quietly. "Third column. It's an *f*."

Tomas looked down. He read the line twice, his heart doing something strange and arrhythmic against his ribs. She was right. In the fourth name on the list of the dead — Ellen Wentworth, drowned, aged nineteen — the third letter was wrong, and he had been staring at that line for twenty minutes without seeing it, because your own errors are invisible to you; they sit in the eye like dust on a mirror.

"It is," he said.

"You'd want to know before you pulled it." She still hadn't moved from the doorway. "Eighty copies, isn't it? You don't want eighty misprints going into the world with her name misspelled. Her family would be the ones to notice."

Something in Tomas's chest unclenched and re-clenched differently. He had prepared, in the three seconds available to him, for blackmail, for flight, for the constable's knock. He had not prepared for a woman standing in his doorway at one in the morning correcting his typography with tenderness in her voice.

"Who told you?" he asked. "Mrs. Marchmont? Did she send you down?"

"No one sent me." She came further into the room then, slowly, and unlatched her coat but didn't remove it. "I couldn't sleep. The river's loud tonight. I heard the press frame creak when you locked the chase — I've been hearing it for three nights, actually. I thought I was dreaming it the first two."

"Three nights," Tomas repeated. "You've known three nights."

"I wanted to know what you were printing before I decided what knowing meant."

"And now?"

She crossed to the composing stone and looked down at the page the way she must look at everything — thoroughly, greedily, the way Tomas's brother used to read, taking each word into himself as though words were food and he'd been starving. When she reached the list of names her lips moved slightly, counting them.

"Eleven," she said. "I'd guessed nine."

"Two died away from town. Sent home to relatives. The count keeps them anyway." He heard his own voice go rough. "That's the whole point of it. The mill counts only what happens inside its walls. We count what the mill causes."

She nodded slowly, and then she did something that undid him more thoroughly than any accusation could have. She reached across the stone, drew out the offending *f* with a pair of tweezers from his own rack, returned it to the case in its proper compartment — *f*, row three, he noted, she knew the lay of a California job case, she'd known it instantly — and selected the correct *t*, setting it into the word Wentworth with a small precise click.

Then she stepped back, embarrassed, as if she'd touched something sacred without permission. "Forgive me. That was presumptuous."

"No," Tomas said. "That was the most presumptuous thing I've ever seen anyone do, and it was also exactly right." He found he was almost laughing, giddy with the strangeness of it. "Where did you learn the case?"

"I didn't, precisely. I watched a printer once, in Lyon, when I was young. I watch things." She said it plainly, without apology, and something passed behind her eyes — a door opening briefly onto a long corridor of watched things — and then closing. "It's how I earn my keep. Watching."

"Watching for whom?"

"That depends on the day."

* * *

They stood either side of the stone like two people at a deathbed, which in a sense they were; the page between them was full of dead names and meant to make living trouble. Outside, the river ground against the pilings of the drowned bridge, patient as a debtor.

"You understand," Tomas said at last, "that what you've just done can't be undone. You know what this is. If Brack or any of Vane's men learned you'd stood here—"

"I know what it is." She touched the edge of the forme, barely, the way you'd touch a sleeping child. "*The Winter Count.* I saw the name of it in a letter."

Tomas went cold from the feet up. "Whose letter?"

For a moment he thought she wouldn't answer. Then she lifted her chin in a way that seemed to cost her something, the way lifting a weight costs the shoulder that bears it daily.

"Mrs. Marchmont's. I read her post. All of it. I've done it for years — steamed open, read, resealed. It's how I keep us safe, or that's what I've told myself. It's how I keep *her* safe." Her voice stayed level, but her hands, he noticed, did not; they had closed around each other at her waist like two people holding on in high wind. "There are letters about the winter count. Numbers. Going to London. She knows more than she says, Mr. Harker, and she says very little even to me."

The room tilted slightly. Nine days in his mother's clean house, eating his mother's bread, and all along she'd been a reader of sealed things — a spy, or something adjacent to a spy, the kind of person who made his skin crawl on principle. And yet. And yet she had come downstairs not to expose them but because she couldn't sleep for the sound of a press being locked, and she had corrected a widow's name before it could go wrong in the world.

"My brother," Tomas heard himself say, "used to say there were two kinds of people who open other people's letters. Thieves, and historians. He said the difference is that thieves keep what they find and historians put it back where the future can reach it." He looked at her. "Which are you?"

"I don't know yet," Rosalind Joubert said. It came out of her quickly, unguarded, and it seemed to frighten her more than anything else she'd said. "I genuinely don't know yet. That's why I'm here instead of asleep."

It was, Tomas would think later, the most honest sentence spoken in that room all night, including his own.

* * *

They printed the edition together.

It happened without discussion, the way water finds a channel. He showed her the bed of the press — the old Albion, older than both of them, bought second-hand by their grandfather and patched with grandfather's stubbornness ever since. She learned the pull in three sheets, her body understanding the lever the way it apparently understood everything, quickly and completely and without fuss. He laid out dampened paper; she received it, squared it, offered it. By the fifth pull they had stopped speaking entirely, and the shop filled with the rhythm of the thing — the roll of the tympan, the bite of the platen, the wet dark smell of oil-based ink in a closed room — and Tomas felt something he hadn't felt since the winter the count gained its sixth name.

He felt accompanied.

Not watched — he'd been watched plenty, by Pike on his rounds, by Brack's men idling outside the chapel, by the whole watching architecture of a company town. Accompanied was different. Accompanied meant another pair of hands that knew what the work required. Accompanied meant that when a sheet pulled crooked, four eyes caught it instead of two.

On sheet thirty-something, she caught one. "The impression's going soft on the left margin," she said, tapping the ghosted corner. "Ink or packing?"

"Ink. Rollers want re-inking." He did it, and watched her examine the fresh roller pass with the same hunger she'd brought to the forme. "You like this," he said. It wasn't a question.

"I like things that are exact." She wiped her inky fingers absently on her already-ruined cuff. "Everything else in my life is approximate. Everything else is *almost true*, or *true enough*, or *true if nobody checks*. This—" she touched the freshly printed sheet, the black still glossy, eleven names crisp and immortal, "—this is checkable. This will still say what it says in a hundred years, and it will either be right or wrong, and we can make it right. Tonight. With our hands."

Tomas thought of his brother again — he thought of him always in this room, but usually as an absence, a shape cut out of the air above the stone. Tonight, for the first time, the shape filled in. *Thieves and historians,* Will would have said of her, grinning. *Give her time, Tommo. The case doesn't care where your hands came from.*

By four o'clock the stack was done: eighty-one perfect copies of *The Winter Count*, No. 4. They cleaned the press together, washed the rollers, distributed the type back into the case — her fingers stumbling only twice now — and locked the empty shop against the night. The sky beyond the blackened window had gone the colour of dishwater. Somewhere upriver a mill whistle blew the half-hour, indifferent, official, wrong by seven minutes just like everything else in Marlow Bend that claimed to tell the truth.

"There'll be more," Tomas said, folding his apron over the rail. "Editions. If you want to forget what you saw tonight, the forgetting has to start now."

"I'm a very poor forgetter." She paused at the foot of the stairs, and in the guttering lamplight her face was younger than it ever was at breakfast, less armoured, almost startled by its own evening. "Mr. Harker. The name in the fourth position. Ellen Wentworth. Was she the one from the flax room?"

"She was my neighbour's daughter," Tomas said. "She was nineteen. She was afraid of deep water her whole life, and they sent her to clear the sluice gate in February."

Rosalind Joubert stood very still. Then she said, "Then she deserved a correct *t*," and went up the stairs, and this time the talking floorboard spoke beneath her feet and she let it, made no effort to silence it at all, as though she had decided that some things in this house should be allowed to announce themselves.

Tomas banked the lamp and stood a moment in the dark shop, listening to the river worry the broken bridge.

He should wake his father. Should tell him the Marchmont woman's driver knew everything, had known for three nights, had chosen — chosen — the side of the press. Should weigh the risk as his father weighed risk, cautiously, in ledgers.

Instead he climbed the stairs himself, and outside her door he stopped, and knocked softly — two, pause, one — the old pattern, the pattern that meant *friend, all's well, sleep*, the pattern Pike had answered from the street in the bad winter when the foremen came asking questions.

From within, after a moment, came the answer. Two, pause, one.

She had learned it in nine days. Or perhaps, Tomas thought, lying down in the dark with ink still in the creases of his knuckles, she had known it all along — some people are born knowing the knock; they're just waiting all their lives for someone to answer it.

Three days later, a clerk arrived on the last ferry before the ice, carrying a leather portfolio stamped with the county arms, and asked at the mill office — loudly, so the street could hear — when the magistrate's inquiry into labour conditions might conveniently convene.

Tomas heard the news from Nell and felt the floor shift beneath the future. Whatever the inquiry was for, it wasn't for the dead. And whatever Rosalind Joubert decided she was — thief or historian — she would have to decide it soon.
