## 11. A Uniform for the Clock

The morning after the night typesetting, Rosalind woke to the smell of ink and frying onions, and for a moment could not remember which life she was in.

She lay in the narrow bed above the Harker print shop and listened to the house assemble itself around her: Mrs. Harker's tread on the stairs, the clatter of the grate being laid, Tomas already below — she could tell by the particular rhythm of his coughing, the way a person learns to identify a household by its sounds. In Edith's houses she had always known the sounds too, but they were different there. Servants' sounds. The careful hush of a household arranged so that no one's labor was ever visible. Here everything announced itself. The pipes knocked when the water ran. The floorboards confessed every step.

It should have been intolerable to her, that noise. She had spent six years perfecting the art of being unheard — the soft-closed door, the breath held at the right moment, the envelope steamed open over a kettle and resealed so flawlessly that even Edith Marchmont, who noticed everything about people and nothing about her driver, had never once suspected. Silence had been her uniform. She had worn it the way other women wore mourning: daily, without comment, until it fit like skin.

And yet here, in this rattling, honest house above a shop that printed funeral notices by day and sedition by night, the noise felt less like an intrusion than an invitation.

She dressed quickly and went down.

* * *

Tomas was at the composing stone with his sleeves rolled, setting type for a batch of invoice headings — legitimate work, the work that kept bread on the table and suspicion off the premises. His hands moved with a fluency Rosalind had watched now for three nights running and still could not parse: pick, turn, place; pick, turn, place; each letter finding its slot as though the tray were an extension of his fingers rather than a wooden case full of several hundred identical-looking pieces of lead.

"You're staring," he said, without looking up.

"I'm learning."

"Learning what?"

"Which letters are which." She came and stood at the edge of the stone, hands clasped behind her back the way she stood at the motorcar, waiting for instructions. "You hold them upside down. And backwards."

"That's how they read right way up on the page." He glanced at her then, one eyebrow raised. "Everything true comes out backwards first. You have to set it wrong to make it right."

"That sounds like something you'd say at two in the morning over a galley of names."

"It is something I say at all hours. Ask my mother." But he shifted sideways along the stone, making room, and the making-room was itself a sentence she understood perfectly. "Go on, then. If you're going to stare, you may as well be useful. Take this stick." He pressed the metal composing stick into her hands — heavier than she'd expected, cold, worn smooth at the grip where ten years of his thumb had polished it. "We'll start with your name."

"My name."

"Everyone's first word is their own name. Printers are no different from children, we just spell better." He fished through the upper case, plucking out letters and laying them in her palm. "R. O-S-A-L-I-N-D. Mind the S, it's cracked at the foot, been dropped twice before you ever came here. Like most things in this town."

She set the letters into the stick the way he showed her, feet together, nick up — *nick up*, he said twice, because she forgot — and when she locked them off with the quoin and lifted the little column of type out, it wobbled dangerously and she nearly dropped it and Tomas caught it with one hand without any apparent hurry at all.

"First rule," he said, steadying it between them. "Never let go of the type. Whatever happens — fire, flood, constable at the door — you don't let go. A line of type is a promise. You let go of it, and nobody can prove you ever made it."

Rosalind looked at the small reversed word in his scarred hands. ROSALIND, reading itself correctly only in the mirror of his eyes.

"And if nobody wants the promise kept?" she asked. "If the whole world would rather it stayed backwards?"

"Then you print it anyway," Tomas said, "and let the world learn to read in a mirror."

* * *

That afternoon Mrs. Harker sent her on an errand to the chandler's, and on the way back Rosalind did what she always did with errands: she took the long route past the mill.

Marlow Bend Mills rose out of the river mist like a cathedral built by someone who hated God — four storeys of black brick, windows ranked like teeth, the great wheel turning with a patience that felt personal. Smoke stood straight up from the stacks in the windless cold, a pillar marking a country no map acknowledged. She counted, as she had taught herself to count since reading the winter count in Edith's correspondence: four windows boarded on the third floor. The loading bay where the cart had come at dawn two weeks ago, too fast, canvas tied down over something the shape and size of a man. The foreman's office with its light burning late.

Brack was on the steps, talking to two men in mill coats. He saw her looking. He smiled at her — a wide, easy smile, the smile of a man who has never once doubted his welcome anywhere — and touched his cap.

Rosalind touched hers in return and walked on, her heart going hard and small in her chest, and understood suddenly why Tomas never looked at the mill. It wasn't fear. It was arithmetic. A man who looks at the thing that killed his brother spends all his strength looking, and has none left for printing.

She had spent her whole life looking. Perhaps it was time she learned to spend it differently.

* * *

Supper that evening was mutton, potatoes, brown bread, and an argument about whether the church bell was a quarter-tone flat, conducted with such passion by Mr. Harker senior and contested so fiercely by young Alice that Rosalind found herself laughing before she could stop — a real laugh, unguarded, shocking her own mouth. Mrs. Harker looked at her over the teapot with an expression Rosalind couldn't read, and later, clearing plates, said quietly:

"She hasn't laughed once since she came. Did you notice, Tom? Not once."

"Mum."

"Well, I notice things. Someone in this family has to do it out loud."

Rosalind, drying dishes at the sink, kept her head down and her ears burning, and thought: *this is what it costs, being seen. This low continuous fever of it.*

She had not been seen in six years. She had told herself she preferred it that way. Standing in the Harkers' kitchen with a tea towel in her hands, she admitted the truth she'd been carrying like contraband: preference had nothing to do with it. Invisibility was not a choice she had made. It was a condition that had been imposed, and then, because humans can accustom themselves to anything, defended.

Edith needed a driver who was furniture. The movement needed a courier who was air. And Rosalind had made herself so good at nothingness that she had begun to believe nothing was what she was made of.

But last night, at two in the morning, she had corrected a typo in a seditious broadsheet and Tomas Harker had looked at her across the forme and seen her — seen the whole architecture of attention she'd built, the hunger in it, the loneliness — and he had not flinched. He had handed her the composing stick as though she had a right to hold things.

She wanted to hold things.

* * *

He found her in the shop after supper, when the others had gone up. He was doing something to the press — oiling the rails, checking the tympan — and he didn't look at her when he spoke, which she had learned was his way of saying the important things.

"There's a coat hook by the door," he said. "You'll have noticed it's empty."

"I hadn't."

"You had. You notice everything; it's your affliction." He wiped his hands on a rag, slowly. "There used to be an apron on it. My brother's."

Rosalind went very still.

"He wore it eight years. Ink in the weave so deep it never washed out — you could read his whole apprenticeship in the stains. When he stopped coming home, my mother wanted to burn it. I said no. I said—" He stopped, jaw working. "I said we weren't a family that burned things. We're a family that keeps records."

"Tomas—"

"So it sat in a drawer for two winters, and my mother pretended not to know, and I pretended not to see her pretending." He turned and looked at her directly then, and his face was doing something complicated — grief and decision wrestling in the same features. "Last week I took it out and I boiled it and scrubbed it and hung it by the stove, and I've been asking myself ever since whether I'm a fool. Because an apron isn't a gift, Miss Joubert. An apron is an enlistment. You put it on, you're one of ours, and everything that falls on us falls on you. The raids. The mill. Brack. All of it."

"I know what falls on this family," Rosalind said. "I've read the ledger page. I've read the winter count. I know exactly what this town does to people who keep records."

"Do you?" Something sharp came into his voice, quick as a compositor's hand. "Because knowing a thing from a letter and knowing it from a knock on your own door at midnight are two different educations."

"Yes," she said. "They are." And then, because he deserved more than caution, because he had given her the composing stick and the truth about promises: "I've spent six years reading other people's letters, Tomas. Steaming them open. Resealing them. Every secret that passed through my hands, I held, and I did nothing with any of it except keep it safe for people who used secrets as currency. I'm very well educated in watching. I am entirely uneducated in standing." She swallowed. "I would like to learn."

Tomas studied her for a long moment. Then he reached behind the press and brought out the apron — canvas gone soft with boiling, bleached pale where the ink had finally surrendered, but still bearing, if you knew how to look, the ghost-map of eight years' work: the dark crescents at the hip where a quoins wrench rode, the spatter down the left side, the worn knee.

"Everyone here wears the same uniform," he said. "The clock doesn't care who you were before. Mill girl or magistrate's clerk or — whatever you were, driving that woman around the country reading her post. In this shop the clock strikes the same hour for everybody, and everybody answers it the same way: with their hands." He held it out. "It won't fit. Daniel was broader than you. You'll grow into it or pin it. Either way, it's yours."

*Rosalind Joubert*, she thought, *you have been offered uniforms before.* The chauffeur's greatcoat, cut to make her shapeless. The maid's grey when the movement traveled light. Each one a costume for disappearing in. She had put them on and thanked people for the honor of being erased.

This was different. This was stained with someone's dead son, boiled clean by his living one, and offered not to hide her but to count her.

Her hands shook slightly as she took it. She pulled it over her head, and it hung loose, absurd, enormous — and warm, somehow, warm from the drawer, warm from two winters of a family refusing to burn its record.

"How do I look?" she asked, and her voice wasn't entirely steady.

Tomas considered her with a printer's gravity. "Backwards," he said. "Which means it reads true."

* * *

She wore it to lock up. That was the thing she remembered afterward, in all the years that followed — not the giving of it, but the small domestic ceremony of the wearing. She went around the shop with the apron on and helped Tomas shutter the windows, sweep the stone, wash the rollers, and the apron swung against her knees with every step like something alive, like something learning to walk in her.

At the door, keys in hand, she paused.

"The Winter Count," she said. "The next number. There's room in it for more than the ledger page. Edith's letters — the ones I told you about, the winter count correspondence — there are numbers in them going back further than anything you have. Names the ledger never carried. Dates. I could copy them out. Properly. From memory and from the originals both."

Tomas had been reaching for the gas bracket. He stopped.

"You're offering to steal from her."

"I'm offering to stop stealing *for* her." Rosalind heard the words leave her mouth and marveled at them — at how easily they came, once a person was wearing the right clothes. "There's a difference. I think I've known it for some time. I think maybe I came to Marlow Bend to learn it."

Outside, far off across the black fields, the mill whistle blew the ten o'clock — long, mournful, indifferent. The clock that didn't care who they were.

Tomas looked at her for a moment, and then he laughed — short, disbelieving, delighted — and shook his head.

"Mrs. Harker said you hadn't laughed since you came," he said. "Wait till I tell her what you just volunteered for."

And it was only later, climbing the stairs with the apron folded over her arm like a sleeping animal, that Rosalind let herself hear what she had actually done: drawn a line through her own history, signed herself onto a list. Somewhere south of the flooded river, Edith Marchmont slept surrounded by letters Rosalind had read and resealable secrets, and tomorrow morning Rosalind would pour her tea and drive her nowhere and say yes ma'am and no ma'am, and all the while her pockets would fill with another woman's paper.

The clock struck the half-hour in the hall below — the shop clock, five minutes fast, because Tomas kept it fast on purpose. *Keeps us honest,* he said. *A deadline you can see coming.*

Five minutes fast. Rosalind fell asleep counting the minutes she might have left, and did not yet know that by week's end, a magistrate's clerk would arrive on the far bank with an inquiry in his bag and spring in his calendar — and that the clock she had just enlisted under was already running.
