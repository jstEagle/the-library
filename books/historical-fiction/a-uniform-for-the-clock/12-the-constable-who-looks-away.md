## 12. The Constable Who Looks Away

The report lay half-finished on Aldous Pike's desk, and he had been looking at the same sentence for the better part of an hour.

*On the night of the 14th inst., constable did observe a light burning in the premises of Harker & Sons, Job Printers, contrary to—*

Contrary to what? That was the trouble. There was nothing contrary about it. A man might burn a lamp in his own shop at any hour he pleased, and no statute in the King's name said otherwise. Pike dipped his pen, held it over the page, and set it down again without making a mark. The ink dried on the nib while he stared past it, through the frosted window of the constabulary office, at the mill chimneys standing against the grey sky like four fingers of a hand that never opened.

Marlow Bend in winter smelled of coal smoke and wet wool and, underneath it all, the river. Pike had been born to that smell. His father had worked the looms for thirty-one years until his lungs gave out two years short of his pension, and the mill's ledger — Pike knew this because he had once asked to see it, and been shown a single line reading *J. Pike, weaver, discharged (illness)* — had recorded neither the illness nor the thirty-one years nor so much as a farthing of gratitude. He had joined the constabulary at nineteen because a uniform was a way out of the weaving shed, and for twenty-two years he had told himself that keeping order in a town was its own kind of work, honest work, even when the order being kept was not entirely just.

He was fifty-three years old now, and he no longer told himself anything. He simply wrote his reports.

He took up the pen again and read the sentence once more. *A light burning.* It was true. There had been a light. There had also been, if his ears served him as well as they always had, the small rhythmic clatter that came through the floorboards of the street at that hour and from no other — the sound of a press running, quick and soft, like a heart beating under a coat. He had stood in the lane below the Harker shop for the better part of twenty minutes on the night of the 14th, collar up, snow gathering on his shoulders, listening to that heartbeat and doing precisely nothing about it.

That was the thing his reports could not say. *Constable did stand in the snow and listen, and found the sound a comfort, and went home.*

He crumpled the paper and began again.

*On the night of the 14th inst., constable made a routine patrol of Bridge Street and found all quiet.*

All quiet. Four words. In the old days, in his father's day, a report like that would have shamed him. But he had learned — slowly, the way one learns any craft worth learning — that vagueness was a form of mercy available even to men in uniform. A specific report was a hook; a vague one was a closed door. The magistrates in the county seat wanted specifics: names, dates, times, quantities. They wanted Marlow Bend delivered to them in parcels, tied with string. Pike had spent two years delivering them fog instead, and the fog had kept more people out of prison than his handcuffs had ever put in.

He signed the report, blotted it, and set it with the others in the tray marked for Thursday's post. Then he sat back in his chair and let himself think about the fire.

* * *

It had been eleven years ago, the autumn of '02. Pike remembered the date the way other men remembered their wedding days, because it was the day he had learned what kind of man Tomas Harker was, and the knowledge had sat in him ever since like a splinter too deep to draw.

The carding room had gone up just after the evening shift changed — a lamp knocked over, or a belt slipping its bearing, no one ever established which — and by the time the alarm bells were ringing properly the second floor was a wall of flame and the stairwell was a chimney drawing fire upward into the spinning loft above, where forty-odd hands were still at their frames, most of them women, several of them girls no older than fourteen.

Pike had gone in. He wanted that understood, if only by himself, in the privacy of his own recollection: he had gone in. He had gotten six of them out through the loading door before the heat drove him back coughing, and then he had stood in the yard doing the arithmetic every policeman dreads — counting the faces coming out against the faces that should be coming out — and come up seven short.

Tomas Harker had gone in after that. Not a constable, not a fireman, just a weaver's son of twenty-two with a leather apron on and his sleeves rolled, who had gone up the outside of the building on the iron ladder like he'd been born to it, and had come down twice with someone over his shoulder, and had gone up a third time when the ladder was already smoking at the brackets, and had not come down at all for eleven minutes, during which Pike had stood at the bottom shouting himself hoarse while the crowd behind him screamed and prayed.

When Tomas finally appeared, it was through the window rather than the ladder — the bracket had burned through — with the last girl across his back and one hand hooked through the rung of the window above, lowering her by the wrists to the men waiting on the cart below. And then, instead of dropping the eight feet to the cobbles himself, he had reached back inside the window frame, and Pike had seen why: a boy, an apprentice, pinned behind a fallen timber, too frightened to move toward the smoke. Tomas had braced himself in the burning window like a man leaning into a wind, and pulled the boy out, and only then let go, landing badly, turning his ankle to jelly, and getting up anyway to make sure the count came right.

Forty-seven souls. Forty-seven out. Pike had counted them himself, twice.

The mill had given Tomas five pounds and a printed certificate of merit, suitable for framing. Pike had given him something else, though he couldn't have said what — a nod across a crowded room, perhaps, the particular nod that passes between men who have seen each other at the bottom of things. It had deepened over the years into something neither of them named. They drank at opposite ends of the same public house. They spoke of weather, of football, of the price of coal. Once a year, on no anniversary either acknowledged, Tomas bought Pike a pint without asking what he was having, and Pike drank it without asking why.

And now Pike stood in the snow outside Tomas's shop at midnight, listening to a clandestine press turn, and wrote *all quiet* in his report book, and went home to a cold supper and a wife who asked him, gently, whether everything at the station was all right, and said yes, and meant no, and knew she knew.

* * *

There were complications, of course. There were always complications, and lately they arrived with the regularity of the post.

Chief Constable Harrow, three weeks sober and therefore insufferably precise, had begun asking questions at the Monday meetings. Nothing pointed. Nothing actionable. Just the sort of questions a careful man asks when he has been told to be careful by someone who pays for care. *Anything of note on Bridge Street, Pike? The Harker establishment keeps late hours, I'm told. Printing is a night trade, isn't it?* Delivered while looking at his notebook rather than at Pike, which was worse than eye contact, because it meant Harrow had already decided what the answer would be and was merely giving Pike the opportunity to provide it.

"Quiet enough," Pike had said. "Printers keep odd hours. Bakers too."

"Hm." Harrow had turned a page. "Mr. Brack mentions them."

Brack. The foreman's name sat in Pike's stomach like a swallowed stone. Brack had come to the station twice that month, uninvited both times, filling the little office with his bulk and his grievance and his smell of machine oil and chewing tobacco. He wanted men posted outside the Harker shop. He wanted warrants. He wanted, though he dressed it in the language of loyalty to the firm, a reckoning with a family that had never done him any harm — and Pike understood, in the way policemen understand such things, that the harm ran the other direction and had run there a long time. A man passed over, laughed at, reminded daily of his smallness in a town where his authority was supposed to mean something. Such men did not want justice. They wanted witnesses to their importance, and the Harkers' press was a witness that could not be intimidated, only destroyed.

"I'll look into it," Pike had told him, which was the oldest lie in the constabulary, and Brack had looked at him a long moment with those pale eyes that missed less than people assumed, and had said, "You've been looking into it a long time, Constable. Looking's not finding."

No. Looking was not finding. Finding was a choice, and Pike had been choosing not to find for months, and the cost of the choosing was beginning to show itself in ways he hadn't anticipated.

His wife noticed first. She always did. Margaret had a way of saying nothing at breakfast that said everything, and one morning in late January she set down his tea and stood at the window with her back to him and said, "You were out past eleven again Tuesday."

"Patrol."

"On Bridge Street."

"The route runs there."

She turned. She was not an accusatory woman; she was a frightened one, and fear wears differently on different faces. "Aldous. If they take this town apart looking for that press, and it comes out you knew — if it comes out you stood in the lane and heard it running and walked away—" She didn't finish. She pressed her lips together and looked at him the way she had looked at him across their daughter's coffin, and Pike understood that she had already grieved some future version of him, the version in the dock, or the version dismissed in disgrace, and that she was asking him not to make her attend the funeral.

"They're printing names," he said quietly. "Of dead men. Of the ones the mill calls accidents."

Margaret closed her eyes. When she opened them, the fear was still there, but something else had come in beside it, unwelcome and stubborn. "Your father," she said, "was a name in a ledger. One line. *Discharged, illness.*" She picked up his empty cup and carried it to the sink, and that was the whole of the conversation, and it had settled the matter more thoroughly than any argument could have.

* * *

The third complication wore better clothes than Brack and was therefore more dangerous.

Miss Constance Vane came to the station on a raw Thursday afternoon, alone, in a grey coat that must have cost more than Pike earned in a quarter. She did not sit when offered a chair. She stood before his desk with her gloved hands folded and regarded him with eyes that catalogued the room — the report trays, the key board, the cracked inkwell — the way an auditor catalogues figures.

"Constable Pike. My father's office will be requiring copies of your monthly reports. All of them. Going back to October."

"Those are matters for the chief constable, miss."

"My father speaks to Chief Constable Harrow. I speak to whoever actually writes the reports." Her voice was level, unhurried, almost gentle, which somehow made it worse. "Yours, I'm told, are admirably brief."

"They say what needs saying."

"Do they." She unfolded her hands and laid one glove on the desk, lightly, the way one might lay down a card in a game whose stakes she had not yet disclosed. "My father believes there is a seditious press operating in this town. He believes it is on Bridge Street. He believes the constabulary has been — how shall I put this — unhurried in locating it." A pause, measured to the half-second. "I have read your reports, Constable. Before requesting them, naturally. One reads what one can obtain." Something moved behind her eyes then, quickly, and was gone — a flicker of something that might, in a different face, in a different life, have been kinship. "They are remarkably uninformative documents. Almost an art form. I wondered if you were aware how remarkable."

Pike kept his face still. Twenty-two years in the uniform taught a man stillness. "Reports record facts, miss. If a street is quiet, I write it quiet."

"If a street is quiet," Constance Vane repeated, softly. She retrieved her glove. At the door she paused, without turning, and said — and this was the part that would keep him awake for nights afterward — "My grandfather died in the '89 fire. The one before your big one. No inquiry was ever held. The ledger says *accident.*" Then she was gone, coat and grey sky and all, leaving the office smelling faintly of violet water and leaving Pike with the unmistakable understanding that the mill owner's daughter had just told him, in her cold, oblique way, that she was not entirely her father's creature — and that she had noticed exactly what he was doing with his reports, and had not yet decided whether it made him an enemy or an ally.

* * *

That night he walked the route again. He told himself it was habit, and it was, but habit is only truth wearing comfortable clothes.

Bridge Street under snow, the lamps haloed in ice-fog, the town holding its breath the way it had held it since the flood took the bridge and cut Marlow Bend off from the world like a finger pinched shut in a fist. He passed the Harker shop. Dark windows, dark doorway, and behind the wallpaper samples in the front room — he had seen them once, delivering a summons about a disputed bill, and had noted the depth of the shelves, deeper than wallpaper needed — whatever the Harkers kept hidden slept its hidden sleep.

Or seemed to. As he drew level with the door, he heard it: the tap-tap-tap of a composing stick, quick and light, and under it the murmur of voices, two of them, one low and male and one — he slowed — one lighter, quicker, a woman's voice, young, with an accent that put her from nowhere near Marlow Bend. The Marchmont woman's driver. The one who stood beside the motorcar with her hands folded and her eyes going everywhere, missing nothing.

Pike stood in the shadow of the awning across the lane, snow settling on his shoulders again, and listened to strangers typeset sedition above a print shop while the town slept around them, and felt the old familiar split open inside him — the constable on one side, the weaver's son on the other, and between them eleven years of a debt that no amount of *all quiet* could ever repay.

He thought about the fire. He thought about forty-seven souls coming down the ladders and out the loading doors because one man had climbed into the smoke three times. He thought about his father's one line in the ledger, and about Miss Vane's grandfather, another line, another *accident*, and about the widows on Weavers' Row who crossed the street when Brack came walking.

Then he took out his report book, and in the light of the street lamp, in his careful round hand, he wrote:

*Night of the 21st. Patrol of Bridge Street and environs. Nothing to report.*

Nothing to report. He looked at the words a long time. They were a lie, and they were a mercy, and they were, he suspected, a rope — one end already knotted somewhere he could not see, in Brack's pale eyes or Harrow's sober notebook or the Vane offices above the mill, waiting for the day someone pulled it taut.

He pocketed the book and turned for home. Behind him, above the print shop, the light burned on, steady and small, and Aldous Pike walked away from it through the falling snow, guarding it with his silence the only way left to him — and knowing, with the certainty of a man who has read enough ledgers to know how they end, that the day was coming when silence would not be enough, when Brack or Harrow or the law itself would come asking questions his vagueness could no longer answer, and he would have to choose, at last, on the record, between the uniform he wore and the town it had failed.

Three days later, the knock came at his own door — the particular knock, two sharp and one soft, that only one living man in Marlow Bend knew — and Pike knew, before he opened it, that his season of looking away had just ended.
