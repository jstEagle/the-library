## 13. Letters to London

The rain had been at the windows of the Harkers' front room for two days without pause, a grey insistence that turned the river town into something half-drowned and half-remembered. Rosalind sat by the glass with Edith's correspondence spread across her knees — eleven letters that had arrived before the bridge went out, plus three written since and waiting for a ferryman who would not come until spring.

She had read them all twice. The third reading was not for information but for confirmation, the way one presses a bruise to be certain it still hurts.

*The winter count must reach London before the inquiry sits,* wrote Mrs. Alderney-Voss, her looping hand as imperious as her reputation. *Edith — I cannot stress this strongly enough. Numbers are nothing without provenance. The documents themselves must cross my threshold. Copies are for newspapers; originals are for history.*

Rosalind set the letter down and looked out at the water where the bridge had been. There was a particular quality to a missing bridge: the eye kept returning to the gap, expecting iron and timber, finding only brown flood churning past the stumps of the far abutment like a sentence with its verb removed.

Everything Edith's network wanted was on this side of that gap. Everything they wanted it to become was on the other.

"You've made that face before," Edith said from behind her. She was at the small writing desk she'd claimed upon arrival, drafting yet another speech for an audience that consisted, currently, of the Harker family, Constable Pike when he pretended not to listen, and Mrs. Ogilvie from the chapel, whose hearing was selective in ways Rosalind had come to admire. "In York, 1911, when the train was delayed. And again in Bristol when the hall doubled its hire fee overnight."

"I was thinking about the mail," Rosalind said.

"The mail," Edith repeated, in the tone she reserved for things she considered beneath serious consideration.

"Which cannot leave Marlow Bend." Rosalind gathered the letters, squared their edges against her knee out of habit — a habit so deep she sometimes thought it predated memory — and rose. "Mrs. Alderney-Voss wants the originals. The inquiry convenes in spring, the moment the ferry runs. If the documents sit in this house until then, they sit in a town where Foreman Brack employs six men whose principal qualification is their fists."

Edith turned in her chair. She had aged well into her authority the way certain buildings age into landmarks — people stopped seeing the wear and saw only the permanence. "And your solution, Miss Joubert? You've been composing it since breakfast. Don't imagine I can't tell."

The rebuke was gentle, almost affectionate, and it did what Edith's gentleness always did: it made Rosalind feel simultaneously seen and managed. She crossed to the window. Below, through the wet glass, she could see the Harker shopfront, its gold lettering softened by rain — *T. HARKER & SON, PRINTERS AND BOOKBINDERS* — though there was no son anymore, and hadn't been for two winters, and the sign said nothing about what happened upstairs after midnight behind wallpaper samples.

"There is a press," Rosalind said, "forty feet below this room."

The silence that followed had texture. Rain filled it, and the tick of Edith's travelling clock, and somewhere downstairs the thump of the jobbing press running off funeral notices or price lists or whatever honest work paid the Harker rent.

"I'm aware," Edith said at last. "I am also aware of what that press prints, which is why I have never asked about it, and why you will not ask me about it now."

"I'm not asking. I'm proposing."

"Then propose carefully, because I suspect I already know, and I find I dislike it."

Rosalind had rehearsed this argument in her head for three days, walking the flooded lane to the chapel and back, standing in the print shop while Tomas showed her how the type cases were organised — *everything a compositor needs within reach of his stool, that's the first law* — and every rehearsal ended at the same point, the point where duty and sense diverged and she had to choose which one she answered to.

She chose. Out loud, for the first time in her employment, she chose against Edith Marchmont.

"Alderney-Voss wants provenance," Rosalind said. "Originals. But the originals are worthless if Brack burns them, and he will burn them, because burning them costs him nothing while we're trapped here and costs him everything once the magistrate arrives. So the question isn't how we get the papers to London. It's how we make sure that even if the papers die here, the truth doesn't."

"And the press?"

"Print it here. All of it. The ledgers, the winter count, the names. A full edition, thousands of copies, distributed before the ice goes out. Let every farmhouse between here and Thornleigh have it under their jam jars by the time the inquiry clerk unpacks his briefcase. Then it won't matter what happens to the originals — the record will exist in too many hands to burn."

Edith stood. It was, Rosalind knew, a tactical movement; Edith used her height the way generals used artillery. "You are proposing," she said, "that I entrust evidence of corporate manslaughter — evidence I have spent eight months cultivating, at considerable risk, through considerable danger — to a clandestine provincial press run by a grieving printer and his mother, in a town controlled by the very men we are accusing."

"Yes."

"You are proposing that I trade the certainty of London for the hope of Marlow Bend."

"I'm proposing that London is across a river that has eaten your certainty, madam, and will keep it until April."

Something flickered in Edith's face — not anger, Rosalind realised, but something adjacent to respect, arriving reluctantly, like a guest who has come to the wrong house. Edith Marchmont did not enjoy being contradicted. She tolerated it from allies she considered essential and forgave it in subordinates she considered promising, and Rosalind had spent four years carefully occupying the second category while quietly becoming the first.

"It would end the national strategy," Edith said. "Alderman-Cross has lined up three newspapers. The story was to break all at once — the count, the ledgers, the inquiry embarrassed into widening its terms. One provincial sheet, however brave, is a candle, not a lamp."

"One provincial sheet," Rosalind said, "is also the only paper in England whose readers are the widows."

That landed. Rosalind watched it land — watched Edith's jaw tighten around a rebuttal and swallow it, because they both knew the arithmetic of the movement had a flaw in it, a flaw Edith carried like a stone in her shoe: the workers were subjects of the campaign, never its audience. Speeches were given *about* them, letters written *on* their behalf, and the great organs of London opinion would consume their dead as content, refined and anonymous.

"They'd need protection," Edith said finally. "Legal, financial—"

"Which you could provide from prison as easily as from a platform, if it comes to that."

"Don't be glib."

"I'm being accurate. You taught me accuracy."

Edith laughed — one short syllable, surprised out of her — and then mastered herself and looked at the rain for a long moment. When she spoke again her voice had changed registers, dropping from the platform voice to the real one, lower, wearier, older.

"If it fails," she said, "it won't fail cleanly. Brack's men won't content themselves with smashing type. And the Harkers — Tomas especially — have more to lose here than either of us. He buried a brother this town refuses to name. If our printing puts him in the river next to Daniel, no victory in London will balance that ledger."

"No," Rosalind agreed. "But leaving the evidence in a trunk until spring balances nothing either. Madam — Edith. You told the chapel women last week that silence is the mill's first product and fear is merely its packaging. I believed you. I still do."

Edith looked at her for a long time. Outside, the flood moved with its patient criminal energy, carrying fence rails and a drowned henhouse past the window like offerings to some indifferent god.

"Draft it," Edith said at last. "The proposal. For Alderney-Voss — she'll want it argued properly, with contingencies, or she'll eat us both alive by return post, whenever return post resumes. And Rosalind—" the use of her bare name, rare as a blue moon, stopped her at the door of the sentence, "—if we do this, you understand you are no longer the woman who reads the letters. You become one of the people the letters are about."

Rosalind felt that settle over her like a coat cut to her exact measure.

"I've been one of them since the bridge went out," she said. "This just makes it official."

* * *

Downstairs, the shop smelled of ink and damp wool and the particular sweetness of hot metal. Tomas Harker was at the stone, cleaning the rollers with a rag gone black as tar, his sleeves rolled over forearms scarred in the pale way of old burns. He didn't look up when she came in — Tomas rarely looked up; he listened with his whole body instead, she'd noticed, the way deaf men and compositors learn to.

"Miz Marchmont's decided something," he said. "You've got the face you get when she decides."

"She hasn't decided. She's agreed to consider." Rosalind hopped up to sit on the paper shelf — a liberty she'd begun taking sometime in the second week and which Mrs. Harker senior had ratified with a nod that constituted, in that household, a knighthood. "I want to put something to you before it's settled, because if it is settled and you say yes, I don't want you saying it because I asked."

Tomas wrung out the rag. "Go on."

"Your press. Your risk. The winter count, the ledgers, the statements — printed here, in full, before the ferry runs. Not a leaflet. An edition. Five hundred copies if the stock allows, distributed wide enough that burning it becomes arithmetic nobody can do."

The rag stopped moving. In the quiet Rosalind could hear the rain working at the gutters and, faintly, Mrs. Harker's tread overhead, pacing out the length of the front room the way she did when she was worried and would rather pace than say so.

"You know what's under the wallpaper samples," Tomas said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I know what's under the wallpaper samples."

"Then you know I've already said yes to smaller versions of that question, twice, and both times I told myself it was the last time." He hung the rag on the stove rail and finally looked at her, and his eyes in the lamplight were the colour of the river — brown, patient, hiding depth. "My brother went into the mill a healthy man of twenty-six and came out of it in a box the foreman signed for as 'internal complaint.' The accident ledger says he slipped on a stair. There isn't a stair in that building he hadn't climbed ten thousand times. When you ask me about risk, Miz Joubert, understand that the risk already happened. What's left is just the choosing of sides."

"Then choose," Rosalind said, and heard her own voice steady itself against the tremor underneath. "But know what you're choosing. Because this isn't the leaflets. This is names — every name, dates, causes, the falsified inspection reports. This is the kind of printing that ends with men kicking doors in."

Tomas smiled, and it transformed his face the way weather transforms a landscape, briefly and completely.

"Miz Joubert," he said, "men kicked our door in during the Reform Bill riots and my grandfather set type by candlelight while they did it. The Harkers have been scaring powerful people since before this mill had a chimney." He held out his hand, ink-blackened, palm up. "Five hundred copies. Six hundred, if Mother lets me buy stock on credit, and she will, because she's meaner than I am about Daniel."

Rosalind took the hand. His grip was warm and certain, and something passed through it that she did not have vocabulary for yet — the beginning of a belonging she had not applied for and did not fully trust.

"There's a condition," she said. "From me, not from her. The widows' statements — the ones we collect — they go in under their own names. Full names. No 'widow of,' no initials. If these women are going to be brave, the least we can do is spell them correctly."

Tomas studied her for a moment, and then nodded slowly, as though she had recited some old rule of the craft back to him.

"That's typesetting," he said. "That's exactly typesetting — getting every name right, letter by letter, because the whole truth is only ever as good as its smallest word." He wiped his hand on his apron and reached for his coat. "Come on, then. If we're doing this, we're doing it before Brack's spies remember how to count the reams we order. And you'll want to learn the case layout, because five hundred copies means someone besides me setting type, and my mother's hands aren't what they were."

"Me?"

"You proposed it." He was already opening the door onto the rain, onto the street where the flood had drawn its line and the town waited behind it like a held breath. "Rebels set their own type, Miz Joubert. That's the whole difference between rebels and complainers."

Behind him, the lamplight fell across the type cases — the shallow wooden seas of sorted letters, each character in its compartment, waiting in perfect order for a hand bold enough to make words of them.

Rosalind stepped down into the wet street toward them, and did not look back at the stairs leading up to the safe, silent room where she had spent her life reading other people's words.

Above them both, unseen, Edith Marchmont stood at the window of the front room, watching her secretary walk into the print shop like a woman walking into her own name — and began, deliberately, to draft the letter that would scandalise London.
