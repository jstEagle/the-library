## 14. Constance's Second Set of Books

The office above the mill yard smelled of pipe smoke and cold tea, and Constance Vane had learned to read it the way other people read weather.

Her father's chair stood empty behind the great desk — he was at the works, shouting at someone about the dye vats — and so the room belonged to her for perhaps two hours, which was more time than she usually got. She sat at the smaller desk by the window, the one that faced away from the yard, and worked through the correspondence with a fountain pen she kept filled but rarely used to sign anything. Signing was her father's privilege. She only prepared. She only tidied. She only made the machine run smooth enough that no one ever asked what oil kept it turning.

Three letters lay finished in the out-tray. A reply to the insurance brokers, declining further coverage on the carding room — her father called it waste; the machines there were old, he said, and old things did not burn. A note to the vicar confirming the Vane subscription to the winter coal fund. And a short, courteous letter to a Mr. Ellery of the *Northern Textile Journal*, assuring him that reports of labor unrest at Marlow Bend were, as always, greatly exaggerated.

Constance had written *greatly exaggerated* and then looked at it for a long moment before blotting it.

She knew what the carding room was like in February. She had walked through it once, uninvited, when she was nineteen, and the foreman Brack had escorted her out by the elbow with a smile that did not reach his eyes, saying the lint was bad for ladies' lungs. She had heard the coughing though. She could hear it still, some nights, when the wind came off the river and carried sound up from the workers' rows instead of down toward the town. It was not a cough any doctor would have called industrial. It was simply the sound of people breathing badly and going on breathing anyway, because the alternative was hunger.

She opened the ledger drawer.

Not the main ledger — the one the clerk brought up every Friday, bound in green cloth, its figures round and reassuring. She kept that one balanced herself; it was arithmetic, and arithmetic did not lie unless you asked it to. The drawer beneath held the working papers: wage sheets, repair requisitions, the accident forms that came up from the floor whenever something went wrong, each one already filled out in Brack's blunt handwriting before it reached her desk. *Slipped on wet flooring. Negligence of operative. No fault attributable to machinery or management.* Over and over, in variations, like a hymn sung badly by many voices.

She had begun keeping her own book eighteen months ago, quite without deciding to. That was the thing she could never explain afterward, if anyone ever asked — which no one did, because no one suspected her, because suspicion required imagination and her father had none where his daughter was concerned. He thought her cold. He thought her efficient. He thought, insofar as he thought about it at all, that she was the son he'd never had, and that this flattered her.

It did flatter her. That was the worst part.

But one evening in October of last year she had been checking a repair requisition against a wage sheet and found that a man listed as receiving full wages through November appeared nowhere in the accident forms at all — and yet a widow's name appeared on the charity list that same month, marked *mill fatality, discretion advised*. Two books, two truths, and the man existed in neither except as an absence shaped exactly like a person.

So she bought a cheap notebook from the stationer's, brown-covered, the kind schoolchildren used, and she began copying. Not everything. Only the discrepancies. Only the places where the green ledger said one thing and the paper trail said another. She told herself it was prudence — that a director ought to know where the bodies were buried, figuratively speaking, so as not to trip over them. She told herself it was for her own protection. Both statements were true. Neither was the whole truth, and she knew it, and she copied anyway, her pen moving fast in the lamplight while downstairs her father laughed at some joke with men whose laughter she had long since learned to distinguish from approval.

Now the notebook lived inside the lining of her sewing basket, under a half-embroidered cushion cover she had no intention of finishing. She took it out most evenings after dinner, when the house was shut up, and added to it by candlelight, checking her figures twice because an error in a book like hers was worse than useless — it was ammunition against her.

This morning, the discrepancy was fresh.

* * *

Brack came up at eleven, wiping his hands on a rag he should have left downstairs, and stood in the doorway without knocking because knocking was for equals.

"Miss Vane. Your father wants the quarterly safety abstract ready for the insurers."

"He'll have it by Thursday." Constance did not look up. "I have a question about September first."

"September what?"

"September the first. Carding room. There's an accident form dated the third — scalding, operative named Wexler — and a repair order dated the second for a steam joint on line four." She turned the papers toward him. "The joint failed on the second. The form says the operative's own carelessness caused the injury on the third. Which is it?"

Brack's face did something small and quick, like a shutter catching wind. "Joint was patched the second. Failed proper the third."

"Then why does the repair order say *renewed*, not *patched*?"

"Because I renewed it after."

"After it injured a man."

"After it was already going to injure him, miss, whether we wrote it down or not." He said it flatly, without heat, and that was the terrible part — the boredom in it, the routine. "Machines wear. Men get hurt. Paper gets filled out. That's how a mill runs. You want the insurers asking questions every time a fitter burns his arm?"

"I want the dates to agree."

"The dates agree where it matters." He came a step closer and put both hands flat on her desk, and she smelled the dye works on him, iron and mordant, a smell she privately liked because it was honest even when he wasn't. "Look here, Miss Vane. I've run this floor eleven years. Your father trusts me with it. Now you're a sharp one, no doubt about it, sharper than he gives you credit for, and that's fine — sharp is useful. But sharp cuts both ways. You start pulling threads on paper, someone's going to ask why you're pulling them. Someone's going to wonder what a young woman keeps her own little notebook for."

The word *notebook* went through her like ice water.

She did not flinch. She had practiced not flinching in mirrors until it stopped being practice. But her mind raced backward through the week — the basket on the hall table Sunday while the vicar called; the drawer in her room, unlocked, the afternoon the new housemaid came to lay the fire. Had the maid seen? Had Brack a cousin among the staff, the way he had cousins everywhere?

"You're mistaken," she said, "if you think I keep anything beyond my father's correspondence."

"Mistake's a funny word." He straightened, folded the rag into quarters, tucked it in his apron pocket. All very deliberate, all very unhurried. "Here's what I know, miss. I know your name's on nothing. Every paper in this office bears his signature or mine. If trouble comes — inquiry, inspectors, lawyers, whatever's coming now the flood's got the town talking — nobody looks at you. Nobody needs to. But if you go pulling threads—" he smiled, and it reached his eyes this time, which was worse, "—then somebody might mention that Miss Vane keeps private accounts. Might suggest she's been copying company papers for purposes unknown. Might suggest she's been in correspondence with agitators — there's plenty of those about just now, they tell me. And who'd swear otherwise? Not me. Not your father, once I've explained it to him careful. A woman alone with a notebook. Juries have hanged better cases than that."

"You'd ruin me to protect a lie about a steam joint."

"I'd protect the mill." He said it simply, almost gently, and meant it, and that frightened her more than the threat had. "The mill feeds four hundred families. It fed yours since before you were born. Whatever it costs to keep it standing is cheaper than letting it fall, and the cost so far has been paper. Don't make it anything else."

He touched his cap-brim — the gesture of respect performed over the ruins of respect — and went out. His boots rang down the stairs, unhurried, a man with all the time in the world, because the time was his.

* * *

She did not cry. She sat very still for a quarter of an hour and listened to the mill through the window — the looms' deep collective heartbeat, the shunting of trucks, a whistle — and understood that she had just been measured for a cage and found to fit.

That was Brack's genius. Not violence, though he could manage that too; she had seen what happened to men who crossed him on the floor, the reassignments, the piece-rates quietly cut. No — his genius was architecture. He built situations in which every door out of a room led back into it. Threaten him openly and she proved the notebook existed. Go to her father and she became, in the retelling, either hysterical or complicit, and Brack would hold the telling. Do nothing and the September form would stand, and the next one, and the next, and the widows would go on appearing on charity lists marked *discretion advised*, and she would go on writing *greatly exaggerated* to newspapers and meaning it less each time until she meant it not at all.

Unless.

Unless the threat itself was information. Unless the fact that Brack feared a notebook — feared it enough to come upstairs and spend words on a clerk's daughter — meant the notebook was worth more than she had ever let herself believe. Men did not threaten over trivia. Men threatened over leverage.

She waited until her father came up at one, ate his lunch standing, complained about the dye lots, signed the out-tray without reading it — he never read what she prepared; that was the whole architecture of their household — and went back down. Then she locked the office door from the inside, which was not unusual; she often worked through the afternoon undisturbed, and the clerk knew better than to knock before three.

The sewing basket was in her room. Fetching it meant crossing the landing past the housemaid, who was doing the brasses and curtsied and said nothing, and Constance carried it back with the calm of a woman transporting buttons. In the office, in the gray light, with her coat hung over the door's fanlight to kill the glow, she opened the notebook and read herself.

Eighteen months of entries. Forty-one names. She counted them again, hoping the number had changed overnight. It had not. Forty-one men and boys entered in her hand — name where she could find it, date, injury, the official cause beside the probable one, the wage record beside the grave record. Some names she had reconstructed from parish registers and the charity lists; three were only occupations, because no paper anywhere admitted they had existed. *Card-tenter, aged approx. 17, autumn 1911.*

Forty-one, and the green ledger recorded nine accidents in the same period, all minor, all the operatives' fault.

She had known these numbers the way one knows the distance to the moon — accurately, abstractly. Seeing them written in her own neat column, in daylight, made them real in a way that frightened her afresh. This was not prudence anymore. This had not been prudence for a year. This was evidence, and evidence belonged to someone, and she had never asked herself to whom it ought to be delivered — because delivering it meant choosing, and choosing meant becoming the kind of person who chose, and she had spent thirty years being the kind of person who prepared things for others to sign.

Brack thought she would hide it now. That was his error, and she catalogued it with something close to pleasure: he believed fear was permanent. He had never watched a frightened person become furious instead, slowly, over years, in a room with a window facing away from the yard.

But fury was not a plan. She turned to the September pages and began, carefully, in a fair hand indistinguishable from her ordinary office script, to copy the whole sequence — repair order, accident form, wage entry for Wexler, and the widow's allowance docket from the charity list that proved the mill had paid out for an injury its own records said never happened. One page, complete, self-explanatory to anyone who could read figures. Not the whole book. Not yet. She did not know yet whom the whole book would ruin besides her father — it would ruin him, she understood that now; whatever happened, that page had turned — and she would not hand forty-one deaths to strangers until she knew what the strangers wanted done with them.

The copy took her until the light failed. She blotted it, folded it once, and sat holding it, feeling its slight weight, which could not possibly be the weight it actually was.

There was a charity event Thursday — the winter coal fund's annual meeting, at the Assembly Rooms, the one occasion when the whole town's hierarchy gathered under chandeliers to congratulate itself on the cold. Her father would attend. So would the magistrate's people, she'd heard; the inquiry clerk was making calls on the principal households. And so, according to the gossip columns she pretended not to read, would the suffragist woman and her driver — the Marchmont party, stranded by the flood like everyone else, giving talks at the workers' institute that Brack called sedition and the vicar called regrettable.

A driver, the column had said. A Miss Rosalind Joubert, who reads and writes all correspondence for Mrs. Marchmont.

Constance looked at the folded page and thought: everyone in this town reads other people's papers. The difference is only what they do afterward.

She slipped the copy inside the lining of her glove — flat, thin, invisible against her wrist — and went down through the darkening mill yard to the carriage house, where she told the coachman she would want the trap Thursday evening, and gave the horses sugar from her bare hand, and did not let herself think about whose side anything served until she had to.

Thursday, then. Chandeliers, and the whole watching town, and one stranger who read letters for a living.

She would know her when she saw her. Readers always knew.
