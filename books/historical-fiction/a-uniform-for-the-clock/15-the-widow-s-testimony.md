## 15. The Widow's Testimony

Rosalind had spent years learning to read what people did not say to her face. She could parse a letter's hesitation from the pressure of a pen nib; she knew the difference between a hand that rushed because it was late and a hand that rushed because it was afraid. But sitting across a kitchen table from Mrs. Ada Quill, whose husband had gone into the mill on a Tuesday in November and come out of it feet first, Rosalind discovered that listening was an entirely different discipline — and one she was shamefully bad at.

"Take your time," she said, and immediately regretted it, because it was the sort of thing people said when they wanted you to hurry.

Ada Quill did take her time. She sat with her hands flat on the oilcloth, as though holding down something that wanted to fly away, and she looked at a point slightly to the left of Rosalind's ear where a calendar hung, advertising seed corn for a spring that felt very far off. The kitchen smelled of boiled cabbage and lamp oil. Outside, the flood had receded enough to show the tops of fence posts along the lane, black and crooked, like a row of men standing in water up to their chests.

"He weren't a careless man," Mrs. Quill said at last. "People say that about them as dies, I know. But he truly weren't. He'd been on the frames eleven year and he never once took a shortcut, not even when Brack pushed the pace. He used to say to me — he'd say, *Ada, them machines don't know your name. They only know whether you're watching.*"

Rosalind wrote it down. The pencil felt clumsy in her fingers after years of reading other people's finished sentences. This was different. This was raw ore, still in the ground.

"And then the eleventh of November," she prompted gently.

"The eleventh of November." Mrs. Quill's hands pressed harder on the table. "The belt on the second-frame drive had been slipping a fortnight. He reported it. He reported it three times, and each time they wrote it in the book and each time nobody come. Then that morning they told the men to run the frames at full speed because there was a rush order, and a belt that slips don't slip worse when you push it, does it? It snaps."

She stopped. Rosalind waited. The clock on the mantel — a cheap tin thing, its face yellowed — ticked through eleven seconds before Mrs. Quill spoke again.

"They told me he were killed outright. That he never knew nothing. And I believed them, because it were kinder, and because who was I to say otherwise?" Her voice stayed level in a way that frightened Rosalind more than weeping would have. "But Tom Ellery were working the frame next to his, and Tom told his wife, and his wife told me over the washing line, that my Walter lived four minutes. Four minutes, miss. And in them four minutes nobody come to him, because the foreman had sent the nearest men to the far end of the floor so's the machines wouldn't stop turning while they fetched the doctor from two streets off. They didn't want the line down longer than it had to be."

Rosalind's pencil had stopped moving. "You're certain of this? Of the account?"

"I'm certain of Martha Ellery, and Martha don't lie about washing-line things." Mrs. Quill finally looked at her directly, and her eyes were dry and terribly clear. "And I'm certain of this too: when the inquiry man come round last winter — the company's inquiry, mind, not the magistrate's — they asked me how Walter supported me, and what his wages was, and whether I'd be wanting the cottage or would be moving to my sister's. Not one question about the belt. Not one. They wrote *accidental death by misadventure* in their book and gave me eleven pound and told me the mill had been good to our family."

"Was it?"

"No." The word came out small and absolute, like a stone dropped down a well. "It paid us. There's a difference, and I only learned it the hard way."

* * *

There were seven widows in Marlow Bend whom Tomas Harker considered safe to approach, and by safe he meant three things: that they had no sons still employed at the mill, that they were not dependent on charity the mill dispensed through the church, and that they could be trusted not to speak of the visit until after the paper came out. Rosalind spent four days going from kitchen to kitchen with a notebook in her bag and a cover story ready — she was collecting information for Mrs. Marchmont's speech on industrial conditions, which was true, though it left out rather a lot.

Edith herself did not accompany her. "My face is known," she said, arranging her correspondence with the crisp economy Rosalind had watched for two years without ever quite understanding. "A widow who talks to me is a widow marked. A widow who talks to a driver, a secretary, a young woman of no consequence—" She caught herself, and something flickered across her face, some rare and quickly-suppressed discomfort. "Forgive me. You know what I mean. You are invisible here, and that is a gift. Use it."

*A young woman of no consequence.* Rosalind carried the phrase out into the grey afternoon and turned it over like a coin she wasn't sure was payment or insult. It should have stung. It did sting, a little. But beneath the sting was the old familiar comfort: invisibility had always been her armor. No one had ever read Rosalind Joubert the way she read everyone else. To be of no consequence meant to be safe.

The second widow, Mrs. Priddy, talked for an hour and said almost nothing — grief had worn her smooth as river stone, and everything came out in generalities about hard times and God's will. The third wouldn't open her door beyond the width of a chain. The fourth, Mrs. Sorrell, talked freely but remembered nothing useful, dates sliding through her account like fish through fingers, until Rosalind learned to anchor her — *was that before or after the Christmas shut-down, Mrs. Sorrell? Was the boy's funeral on a Sunday?* — and discovered that memory, given pegs to hang on, grew suddenly precise. That was the lesson of those days, the one she wrote in her notebook in the evenings by lamplight: people did not remember in order. They remembered in wounds. Your work was to find the wound and then build the scaffolding around it, date by date, name by name, until the shape of the truth stood up on its own.

By Thursday evening she had six statements. Six accounts of belts unrepaired, of guards removed to save time, of men sent back onto floors slick with oil within hours of someone else's blood being mopped from them. Six payments of between nine and fourteen pounds. Six certificates reading *accidental death by misadventure*, in six identical clerical hands, as if grief were a form to be filled out in triplicate.

She copied them all twice, in her smallest hand. One set went into the false bottom of her trunk. One set went to Tomas Harker, folded inside a wallpaper sample book, and she watched him read them at the shop counter with the type trays pulled out around him like a moat, and she watched his jaw tighten, and she understood that for all the Harkers had suffered and suspected, seeing it written plainly — names, dates, sums — was a different thing entirely. Suspicion was fog. This was stone.

"We'll need more than this," Tomas said quietly. "Six widows, all saying the same pattern — Brack'll say it's collusion, women egging each other on for compensation they'll never see. He knows that trick. He's used it himself." He tapped the statements. "What breaks a pattern is a name attached to it. A name they can't dismiss."

"That's exactly what none of them will give," Rosalind said. "Tomas, I sat in those kitchens. Half of them made me promise before I'd got my coat off that nothing would go in print connected to them. One woman asked me if I was from the mill. When I said no, she asked if I was *sure*."

"I know." He looked tired. The press ran at night now, most nights, and it showed in the shadows under his eyes and the ink stains worked so deep into his knuckles they looked like bruises. "But we've time yet. Ask again. Not for the same thing — ask them what they'd want written if it *were* safe. Let them say it out loud once. Sometimes that's where courage starts. It has to be rehearsed like anything else."

* * *

The seventh widow was Mrs. Hannah Voss, and everyone had warned Rosalind against her.

"She's not right since it happened," Mrs. Sorrell said, pressing a heel of seed cake on Rosalind as though grief were best treated nutritionally. "Stands at her gate of an evening watching the mill chimney, regular as church. Won't take the compensation money — won't touch it. They've had the vicar round and everything."

"She called Brack a murderer to his face," Mrs. Ellery added, lowering her voice as if the word might carry. "In the street. Last summer. He just smiled at her, which was worse somehow, and walked on, and she stood there shouting till a constable moved her along gentle-like. Gentle, mind. Even Pike was gentle with her. Everybody's gentle with Hannah Voss, and it's the gentleness that's eating her alive, if you ask me. Being pitied ain't being heard."

Rosalind climbed the hill to the Voss cottage on Friday morning, under a sky the color of dishwater, past gardens drowning slowly in the flood's retreat. The house sat at the top of the lane with a view of the whole town laid out below it — and the mill above everything, its chimney standing over Marlow Bend like a finger raised in permanent admonition.

Hannah Voss answered the door before Rosalind knocked twice. She was perhaps fifty, tall, straight-backed, with white hair cut short in a way that was practical rather than fashionable and hands that had clearly done fieldwork as well as housework. She looked at Rosalind for a long moment.

"You're the Marchmont woman's girl."

"I'm her driver and secretary, yes. My name is—"

"I know your name. Town knows your name. Town knows everything, that's the trouble with it." But she stepped back and held the door open. "Come in, then, if you've come. Don't stand on my step letting the neighbors count you."

Inside was spare and fiercely clean, and on the mantel stood a framed photograph of a young man in mill clothes, his collar soft, his smile wide and unguarded. Below it, incongruously, lay a single typeset letter — actual metal type, arranged and inked and printed on a card, spelling out a name: *JOSIAH VOSS, 1881–1912*. Rosalind recognized the font. She had seen it, or its brothers, behind the wallpaper samples in Tomas's shop.

Mrs. Voss caught her looking. "Your printer friend did that for me," she said. "Wouldn't take payment. Said Josiah deserved better letters than the ones the mill put on his certificate." She sat down heavily in the chair by the cold hearth and indicated the other. "Well. You've seen six of them already, I hear. The others. So you'll have your little pattern by now, won't you. Belts, guards, wages, nine to fourteen pound, accidental death by misadventure."

Rosalind sat. "Then you know why I'm here."

"I know why you're here. What I don't know is what you think you're doing." Mrs. Voss leaned forward. "I'll tell you what I think. I think you're collecting sorrows to put in a pamphlet that'll make some London ladies feel properly exercised, and then you'll all go home across that bridge when it opens, and I'll still be here, standing at my gate, watching that chimney. That's what I think. Am I wrong?"

The words landed harder because they were half true, and Rosalind flinched from them honestly. "Partly wrong," she said. "Not wholly. I can't promise you London. I can't promise you the bridge, or the magistrate, or any of it. But I'm not collecting sorrows, Mrs. Voss. I'm collecting evidence. There's a difference, and the difference is that evidence has to survive scrutiny, and sorrow doesn't have to survive anything. Sorrow's allowed to just be true."

Something shifted in the older woman's face — a fractional loosening, like ice deciding whether to become water. "That's a careful answer."

"I'm a careful person."

"Are you." Mrs. Voss studied her with an attention Rosalind found almost unbearable, as though being read were a punishment she had coming. "Careful people are usually frightened people. I was careful forty years. Careful in the fields, careful with the money, careful what I said at market. And then they handed me a certificate with my husband's life reduced to five words in a clerk's handwriting, and do you know what I discovered, Miss Joubert? I discovered that careful had gotten me nothing. Nothing kept. Nothing changed. Careful was just fear wearing a tidy dress."

She rose, went to a drawer, and took out a bundle of papers tied with butcher's string. She did not untie it. She simply held it in both hands, on her lap, like something living.

"Josiah worked the boiler house. Twelve years. In the last year he told me the safety valve had been sticking — sticking, and the gauge glass cracked, and every time he reported it they told him to watch it close. *Watch it close.* Those were the words. He wrote them down himself in his book, because he had a notion something would happen and he wanted it in his own hand." She untied the string at last, slowly. "Something happened. December the third, 1912. Three men dead, not one. But the certificate says scalding, accidental, misadventure, and the coroner was a man who owes the mill his land rent, and nobody asked about the valve. Nobody except Josiah's book, which I've kept hidden in the flour bin for fourteen months because I thought — I genuinely thought — that they'd burn the cottage down around me to get it."

Rosalind's breath caught. *The flour bin.* She thought of her own hiding places, the false bottom of her trunk, the hollowed spine of a Bible, and recognized the grammar of concealment in another woman's house.

"Why show me?" she asked quietly. "If you believe I'm here for a pamphlet and a departure."

"Because you didn't argue with me when I said it." Mrs. Voss's mouth did something that was nearly a smile and mostly not. "A liar would've argued. A liar would've said *oh no, Mrs. Voss, we'll change everything.* You just sat there and took it." She opened the book — a common ledger, mill-issue, with Josiah Voss's careful hand marching down the pages: dates, observations, the words *watch it close* repeated like a refrain, and then, on the final written page, a sentence that stopped Rosalind's heart: *If anything comes of this, tell Hannah it was not my carelessness and she must not let them make it hers.*

"He wrote that the morning he died," Mrs. Voss said. Her voice did not break. Rosalind wished, obscurely, that it would; the steadiness of it seemed a violence done to nature. "He knew. He walked to work knowing, because we needed the wage, and because he trusted — God help us both — that twelve years counted for something."

She closed the book and put both hands flat upon it, the way Ada Quill had flattened her hands on the oilcloth, holding down what wanted to fly.

"So. Here is my condition, Miss Joubert, and it is not negotiable, and you may carry it back to your Mrs. Marchmont and your printer friend and anyone else who thinks it's madness. If my Josiah's words go in your paper — his name, his book, the valve, all of it — then my name goes with it. Full name. Hannah Voss of Mill Lane. Printed plain, spelled right, where Brack can read it over his breakfast."

"Mrs. Voss—" Rosalind began. *They burned paper stock last month,* she almost said, and stopped herself, because that was knowledge she shouldn't have. "They could take your cottage. Your pension, such as it is. They could—"

"They could do what they like." For the first time, fire came into her voice, low-banked and old and utterly without hysteria. "That's the whole point you clever people keep missing. They can *already* do what they like. That's what the ledger proves. My silence hasn't bought me safety; it's bought them comfort. Fourteen months I've stood at that gate like a monument to being manageable, and do you know what I've decided, standing there?" She leaned forward. "Courage isn't the opposite of fear. Courage is what fear does when it's finally had enough. And mine, Miss Joubert, has had enough."

Rosalind looked at the book, at the photograph above the hearth, at the printed card with its honest metal letters. *Everyone here wears the same uniform.* She thought of the apron hanging on its hook in her room, offered and hesitantly accepted, and understood suddenly that Tomas had not given her a garment. He had given her a door, and she had been standing in the doorway for weeks, one foot in, one foot out, careful as ever.

"If we print it," she said slowly, "there may be consequences. For you, for the Harkers, for all of us. I won't dress that up."

"I'd think less of you if you did." Mrs. Voss pushed the ledger across the table. "Take it. Copy it tonight. Bring it back tomorrow before dark — I'll know if it's been out of your hands any longer, I've a good nose for my own house. And Miss Joubert." Rosalind paused at the door, the ledger heavy against her ribs like a second heartbeat. "The others. The six. When my name's in it, plain, some of them will find theirs. Fear's catching, same as cholera — but so's courage. It just needs one household to catch it first and survive the fever. Make sure mine survives."

Walking back down the hill with the ledger inside her coat, Rosalind passed the mill at shift-change. The gates spilled men out into the wet street, faces grey with lint-dust and fatigue, and above them the chimney went on breathing its slow smoke into the dishwater sky. She found, to her surprise, that she was no longer afraid of it — or rather, that the fear had changed its shape, hardened from something diffuse into something with edges, almost a comfort. Fear with edges could be gripped.

At the shop, Tomas read Josiah Voss's book in silence, and when he reached the final line he set it down very carefully and was quiet a long moment.

"Her name goes in," he said at last. "Full name, as she says. And we set it in the largest size we've got."

They were still planning the layout — Rosalind sketching columns on the back of a wallpaper sample while Tomas tested his damaged fingers against the composing stick — when the knock came at the street door. Three sharp raps, a pause, then two more: an odd rhythm, urgent, unlike any customer's knock. Rosalind saw Tomas go still, his head tilting, and then drain of color as he crossed the room and lifted the sash an inch.

Constable Pike stood outside in the rain, collar up, eyes moving past Tomas into the shop with a look Rosalind could not read — and did not like.

"Tomas," Pike said, low. "You'll want to hear this inside. And you'll want to hear it now."
