## 16. A Raid That Missed

The order came down on a Tuesday, in the way such orders always came — sideways, unwritten, wrapped in the language of men who wanted their hands to stay clean.

Pike was standing at the duty desk in the constabulary office, mending a tear in his left glove with waxed thread, when Brack filled the doorway. The foreman did not enter so much as occupy, the way he occupied every room, shoulders first, as though the walls had been built for his convenience and might be moved if he disapproved of them.

"Aldous." Brack never called him Constable Pike unless there were strangers present. "Mr. Vane's been hearing things. Paper. Printed paper. Sheets going round the parish like autumn leaves. You know anything about that?"

Pike drew the thread through the leather, kept his eyes on the work. "I've heard talk."

"Talk's cheap. Paper isn't. Somebody's setting type somewhere in this town, and setting type takes a press, and a press takes a room, and a room has a door, and a door—" Brack smiled, and it was the smile he used on the new lads at the mill, the one that made clear the sentence would finish itself whether or not you helped it along, "—has a man whose job it is to open it."

"There are four printers listed in the parish register," Pike said. "Harker and Son does job work. Bills of lading. Funeral cards."

"Funeral cards," Brack repeated, tasting the words. He came further into the office, and his voice dropped into something almost companionable, which from Brack was worse than shouting. "Funny you should say funeral. Mr. Vane wants a search. Friday morning, early, before shop hours. Harker's premises. A thorough one. And he wants it done proper — a constable who knows how to look at a thing without breaking it, so there's nothing to cry about after. Not some ham-fisted lad who'll leave them a grievance to print about." He set a folded paper on the desk between them. It bore no signature. "You're the man for careful work, Aldous. Always have been."

*Careful work.* Pike looked at the paper and thought: this is what they pay me for, and this is what they think they're buying, and only one of those things is true.

"Friday," he said.

"Friday," Brack agreed. "Early enough that nobody sees us coming. Though I suppose if word got out somehow, well." He shrugged, a great slow shrug like a hillside shifting. "Then we'd know they'd something to hide, wouldn't we."

He went out. Pike sat with the glove in his lap, needle poised above the leather, and listened to the rain beginning against the window — the same rain that had eaten the bridge, that had penned the whole valley in with its secrets, and he understood suddenly that the flood had not trapped him here. It had trapped everyone equally, and that was the trouble. There was nowhere for any of them to run, so they had all of them, in their different ways, decided where to stand.

He had known Tomas Harker for nineteen years. He had pulled him out of the carding room fire in '09 with his collar smoking and his lungs full of lint-smoke, had carried him out over his shoulder through a door too narrow for two men, and in the years since Tomas had never once mentioned it — which was its own kind of debt, the kind that didn't compound interest because neither man would allow the ledger to be opened. But debts didn't need ledgers. They lived in the body. They lived in the fact that when Pike walked past the print shop on his rounds, the bell above the door gave a particular double-tongued ring, and inside, always, someone was watching him go by.

Friday. Two days. He turned the glove over, began the other seam, and let his mind do what it had learned to do in eleven years of walking Marlow Bend's streets: find the shape of a thing before anyone asked him to name it.

* * *

Wednesday evening he walked the long way home, past the churchyard, past the undertaker's yard where the coffins stood in their racks like a congregation waiting patiently for the sermon to end. He had passed that yard ten thousand times and never thought twice about it. Now he stood at the fence in the failing light and counted.

Three coffins in the rack. One of them new pine, unfinished, lid not yet fitted — the kind built ahead for a family that couldn't be caught short. The Harkers' mother, old Mrs. Harker, had been failing all winter; the town knew it the way towns know everything and say nothing. A coffin for her was no more remarkable than bread on a shelf.

But a coffin was also the single object in the world that a constable could not lawfully open without an order from the coroner, and Brack, for all his appetite, would not dare touch a dead woman's box with the inquiry coming in spring. Even Brack had a sense of the weather.

Pike stood at the fence until his hands were cold, and then he did the arithmetic he had been avoiding for months, the arithmetic that had been running under every vague report he'd ever filed, every description he'd softened, every *could not confirm* he'd written in a hand neater than the truth deserved.

They were going to put the press in a coffin.

And he was going to have to stand in the shop on Friday morning while they did it, or just after, and look Brack in the eye and see nothing.

Unless.

* * *

Thursday night, past eleven, when even the mill's night shift had gone quiet and the fog off the river had come up the high street thick as wool, Aldous Pike put on his greatcoat and walked to the print shop. He did not wear his helmet. A helmet announced a man; he wanted to arrive as just another shape in the dark.

The upstairs windows of the Harker house were dark except one — the back room, where a candle burned low behind frosted glass. Rosalind Joubert's room, the landlady at the Bell had told him once, in the gossipy way landladies told constables things they shouldn't. The Marchmont woman's driver. Quiet one. Reads everything, that one. Pike had filed that away too, in the drawer where he kept the things he wasn't ready to know.

He stood beneath the shop's side door — the narrow one that opened onto the alley, the one deliveries came through — and raised his knuckles.

The code was older than either of them. It belonged to the fire of '09, to the nights after, when Tomas, half-recovered and restless, had taken to walking the beat with him because sleep wouldn't hold him and Pike hadn't the heart to tell him a convalescent shouldn't be out in the cold. They'd invented it one night outside the Bell, bored, half a pint apiece, a game of knocks to pass the time: two quick, pause, three slow — *all's well, nothing stirring.* Three quick, pause, two slow — *trouble, come now.* And one knock, alone, spaced long between repetitions — *run.*

Two-and-three meant nothing. That was the beauty of it. A neighbor knocking a friendly pattern on a shutter. Nothing a magistrate could read, nothing a lawyer could parse, nothing that existed anywhere but between two men who had shared smoke and fear and the peculiar intimacy of a rescue.

Pike knocked it twice. Two quick. Pause. Three slow. Then again. Then again.

Above him, the candle behind the frosted glass guttered — he saw the shadow of movement across it, a figure rising. He stepped back into the alley's darkness and waited, hands in his pockets, a constable off-duty loitering by no particular arrangement.

The side door opened four inches. Tomas Harker's face appeared in the gap, lamplight behind him turning his hair to wire. He was fifty-one and looked older, the way mill men did, but his eyes were quick and they went straight past Pike's face to the empty street beyond, reading it the way Pike read streets.

"You're late," Tomas said quietly. "For a social call."

"I'm early," Pike said. "That's rather the point."

For a moment neither spoke. Somewhere down the alley a cat overturned a bin lid and both men flinched identically, and something in that identical flinch — nineteen years compressed into a single nervous twitch — broke whatever ceremony had been holding them apart.

"Brack was in the office Wednesday," Pike said, low and fast. "Vane wants the shop searched. Tomorrow morning, seven o'clock, before you open. They'll want it done clean — no broken locks, no damaged stock, nothing to complain about after. Brack will lead it himself with two of the mill men dressed as special constables, and me." He heard his own voice flatten. "And me, doing the careful looking."

Tomas absorbed it the way he absorbed everything, without visible motion, like a wall absorbing weather. "What will you be careful about finding?"

"That depends entirely on what isn't here tomorrow at seven."

"And if they ask why I'm moving furniture at dawn?"

"Tell them your mother's coffin is going to the churchyard Saturday and you're clearing the passage. Which will even be true, near enough." Pike hesitated. This was the part he had rehearsed on the walk over and still could not make sound like anything but surrender. "Tomas. If they find it — if it goes wrong tomorrow — I can't protect you from the consequences. I can only make sure they don't find it. After that you're on your own, and I'll have to write whatever report keeps my coat on my back. Do you understand what I'm telling you? I'm not offering to be brave. I'm offering to be exactly as much of a coward as I can safely arrange."

Something moved in Tomas's face — not quite a smile, but the ground a smile might later be built on. "Eleven years of reports full of fog, Aldous. I'd worked out the arrangement already."

"The coffin—"

"Was my sister-in-law's notion. She says grief is the one honest disguise left in England." He glanced back into the dark of the shop, toward the stairs, and lowered his voice further. "There are others in this house besides me now, you understand. The Marchmont woman. Her driver. People with names that would travel."

"Then they'd best all be very busy with furniture at dawn," Pike said. "I'll keep Brack in the front room as long as I can. He likes to talk. I'll give him reasons."

He turned to go. Tomas caught his sleeve — lightly, the grip of a man who handled delicate machinery all day and knew exactly how little pressure things required.

"Why?" Tomas asked. Not accusatory. Genuinely asking, as though the answer mattered to something larger than the two of them. "Nineteen years, and you've never once crossed the line where I could see you cross it. Why tonight?"

Pike looked up the alley toward the river, invisible in the fog, toward the gap where the bridge had been, where the water ran over the broken stumps day and night with a sound like patient applause.

"Because I wrote the fire report in '09," he said. "The official one. Carding room, lamp flame, unfortunate accident, no negligence found. And there were four men on the night shift that should never have been locked in, and I wrote *no negligence found,* and I've been writing it ever since, in one form of words or another, for eleven years." He freed his sleeve gently. "Tomorrow morning I'm going to stand in your shop and fail to find a printing press. It isn't courage, Tomas. It's the smallest correction I know how to make. Take it."

He walked away without waiting for an answer, because he was afraid of the answer, and because the fog had begun to thin toward the east, and dawn — the bad dawn, the searching dawn — was closer than either of them wished.

* * *

Friday came gray and hard-frosted, breath hanging in the air like evidence.

At six minutes past seven, Brack arrived at the front of the print shop with two mill men in borrowed constables' coats that fit them like sacks, and Pike beside him carrying the unsigned paper he had been given, which authorized nothing and would impress no one who read it carefully, and which was therefore perfect, because Brack did not read carefully and hoped no one else would either.

Inside, the shop was in admirable chaos. Sawdust on the floorboards. A trestle table dragged half across the passage. Tomas Harker in his shirtsleeves, sweating despite the cold, directing the two apprentices in the maneuvering of an enormous empty packing crate. Upstairs, faintly, a woman's voice sang a hymn — Mrs. Harker, or someone being Mrs. Harker for the occasion. The Marchmont lady herself sat in the parlor in a high-collared dress of severe black, mending lace, and greeted the search party with the weary courtesy of a duchess interrupted by tradesmen, and Brack, who feared three things in this world and titled ladies were two of them, actually removed his hat.

"Coffin's in the yard," Tomas said, wiping his hands on his apron, answering the question no one had asked yet. "Mother's. Going to the churchyard Saturday morning. Reverend Culpepper's expecting it. You're welcome to look at the paperwork if the law requires."

"No need," said Brack, and Pike watched him not-look at the yard door, watched the calculation move behind his small eyes — a dead woman's box, a clergyman, a spring inquiry that Vane wanted quiet — watched the calculation come out, as it had been designed to come out, against touching it.

They searched anyway. Pike performed his role with a craftsman's precision. He examined the type trays and admired the neatness of the job work — bills of sale, delivery dockets, a stack of funeral cards for a Mrs. Ellery, tastefully bordered. He opened drawers that contained nothing, measured a cupboard with his eye and pronounced it shallow, tapped a wall and got back the dull honest thud of plaster on brick. Behind him, Brack pulled books off shelves and riffled them upside down like a man searching a hymnal for the devil, and found nothing, because there was nothing to find, and because Pike, arriving first at each likely place, had already arrived and already left it looking searched.

In the yard, the coffin lay on trestles under sacking, and one of the mill men drifted toward it, and Pike said, mildly, "That'll be sealed and screwed by the undertaker before it moves. You wanting to explain to the widow Harker's neighbors why the constabulary prised her lid off?" and the man drifted back, and the moment closed over like water.

By nine o'clock it was done. Brack stood in the middle of the shop floor, dusted to the elbows, cheated and unable to name the flavor of the cheating, and settled for menace. "Paper doesn't print itself, Harker. When we find where it comes from — and we will —"

"Mind the step," Tomas said courteously, and shut the door behind them.

* * *

Pike filed his report that afternoon. *Premises of T. Harker, printer, searched this date upon information received. Jobbing stock examined. No printed matter of a seditious character discovered. Premises otherwise engaged in preparation of funeral effects. Search conducted thoroughly. Nothing found.* He read it over twice, and for the first time in eleven years the flat officialese of his own hand gave him something other than shame. *Nothing found.* It was true. It was the truest sentence he had written since 1909.

He was blotting the page when the clerk brought in the morning post, and among the circulars and the mill's correspondence lay a plain envelope, no crest, addressed in a hand he almost recognized — a small, disciplined, unornamented hand, the hand of someone accustomed to copying documents quickly and exactly.

Inside was a single sheet, unsigned:

*Constable Pike — The shop is grateful. Gratitude is poor payment, so instead: information. The count in the winter sheets is low. The ledger page Edith Marchmont holds lists injuries dated weeks before the accidents they became — proof the mill knew and waited. And there is a second set of books. Someone close to the owner keeps them. When you are next asked to find nothing, remember that the truth is not only hidden in cellars. Some of it sits behind very tidy desks, waiting for someone brave enough to open a drawer. — A friend of the shop*

Pike read it three times. Then he fed it to the stove, watched the corners catch and curl, and sat for a long while with his gloved hands — the left one still bearing Thursday's mended seam — folded on the desk.

A second set of books. Someone close to the owner. He thought of the Vane household, of the daughter he had seen perhaps six times in eleven years, always at a distance, always exact, always watching the watchers with eyes that missed less than they admitted.

Outside, the frost was melting off the window in bright runnels, and somewhere far off, under the hills, the river was working at the bridge stumps, patient as a clerk, taking the town apart and putting it back together in a shape none of them had chosen.

Pike took out a fresh sheet of paper and began, carefully, to draft the vaguest report of his career.
