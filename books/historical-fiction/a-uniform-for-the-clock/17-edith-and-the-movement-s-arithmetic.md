## 17. Edith and the Movement's Arithmetic

Edith Marchmont had learned long ago that the truth was a currency, and like all currencies it lost value when spent carelessly.

She sat in the small back room of the Harker house that had been given over to her use — a desk, a lamp, a chair that creaked when she shifted her weight, and a window that looked out on the black bulk of the mill against the grey February sky. The flood had receded from the streets but not from the bridge; the river still ran high and brown and fast between the broken stumps of the span, carrying ice the colour of old bone down toward the lowlands. No ferry. No post beyond what the railway managed on the far bank, and the railway station was a half-day's walk through mud that swallowed boots to the ankle.

Marlow Bend was sealed, then. Sealed like a letter.

She smiled at her own thought and dipped her pen.

The room smelled of ink and cold tea. Below her, she could hear the muffled sounds of the print shop — Tomas Harker's tread, the clatter of type being distributed back into its cases, the particular silence of a family that had learned to move quietly around its own griefs. Edith catalogued these sounds without meaning to; she catalogued everything. It was how she worked. A woman who could hold ten thousand details in her head could afford to write very little down, and what she wrote down could be burned.

Her correspondence lay before her, sorted into three piles by an old habit no one had ever been meant to notice. The first pile was innocent: letters to suffrage chapters in Leeds and Manchester, arrangements for meetings she would now miss, a note of sympathy to a widow in Bradford whose husband had died — genuinely, boringly — of pneumonia. The second pile was sensitive: names of women who sheltered organizers, sums routed through accounts under false descriptions, the small plumbing of a movement that had learned to survive inspection.

The third pile was Marlow Bend's.

She drew it toward her and read again the letter she had drafted three times already, addressed to Lady Vivienne Ashcombe in London, who controlled the purse-strings of the Parliamentary Committee and who had never once in eleven years asked Edith a question she did not already know the answer to.

*My dear V.,* the draft began. *The situation here is worse than reported, and better documented than we dared hope.*

That much was true, and it was the only part she could safely send.

Edith set down her pen and pressed her fingers to her eyes. Behind her lids she saw the arithmetic — for it was arithmetic, whatever else anyone chose to call it, and she refused to flatter herself by calling it anything softer. On one side of the ledger: a river town of perhaps four thousand souls, two-thirds of them dependent on Vane's mill, exhausted, frightened, half of them persuaded that the deaths were God's will or bad luck or their own carelessness. On the other side: a nation. Forty million people who had never heard of Marlow Bend and never would — unless.

Unless something happened here that could not be sanded smooth.

She had been in this work for twenty-two years. She had watched inquiries open and close like mouths chewing and swallowing and producing nothing. She had seen coroners' verdicts of *misadventure* stacked so high they could have furnished a wall. She had stood at the funeral of a girl of sixteen in Blackburn — crushed in a carding machine that the foreman knew was faulty — and listened to the vicar say that the Lord giveth and the Lord taketh away, while the mill owner's carriage waited outside with its blinds drawn, as if death were something to be ashamed of rather than profitable.

Nothing had changed after Blackburn. Nothing had changed after any of them. Because the dead were buried privately, the widows paid off quietly, and the record — the record was written by men who were paid to write it, and filed by clerks who were paid to forget it, and the whole machine ran on the principle that outrage is a perishable commodity and distance is a preservative.

London was distant. London would forget Marlow Bend by Easter unless —

Unless there was blood in the water. Unless there was a name everyone knew. Unless there was *martyrdom*, which was the only ink that did not fade.

Edith rose and went to the window. The mill's chimneys stood against the sky like fingers held up in warning. Somewhere behind those walls, Constance Vane kept her father's books, and somewhere in the town below, women who had spoken to Rosalind Joubert's careful notebook were deciding, night by night, whether courage was worth the price of bread.

*They are witnesses,* Edith thought. And witnesses, in her long experience, survived to be discredited. Witnesses grew tired. Witnesses took settlements. Witnesses were bought, or frightened, or simply wore out, and their testimony dissolved like sugar in the great brown river of public indifference.

But martyrs — martyrs were permanent. Martyrs did not recant. Martyrs could be printed, painted, sung, carved into stone above meeting-hall doors. The movement did not need Marlow Bend to be saved; the movement needed Marlow Bend to be *remembered*, and those were tragically different things.

She had known this for years, in the abstract way one knows the cost of everything. She had simply never before had to apply the figure to faces she ate breakfast near.

* * *

The knock came at half past nine — two knocks, a pause, one more. Rosalind's knock. Edith said, "Come," and composed her face into the expression she thought of as her public face even though they were alone, because with Rosalind one was never entirely alone; Rosalind saw everything and said almost nothing, which made her either the perfect employee or the most dangerous kind of witness, and Edith had never fully decided which.

Rosalind came in carrying the post that had come up on the milk cart from the far bank — a thin handful, the river's tax on correspondence. "Three letters. One's from your sister, I think, from the hand. One looks like committee business." She paused. "And one has no return address, but the postmark is Marlow Bend itself, if you can call it a postmark. It was left at the ferry landing, apparently, with a penny for whoever crossed."

"Give it here."

The envelope was cheap paper, badly sealed, addressed in a laborious hand: *Mrs. E. Marchmont, c/o the printer's, High Street.* Edith slit it with her letter knife and read it twice. Her face did not change. Rosalind, standing by the door with the other letters, watched her the way she always watched — patiently, completely, missing nothing — and Edith felt the familiar prickle of being read by someone she employed to drive a motor car.

"Well?" Rosalind said.

"It's from a woman named Ada Quill. Her husband went into the dye vats last November."

"I know Mrs. Quill," Rosalind said quietly. "I took her statement. For the — for the paper." She corrected herself a half-second too late, and Edith noted the correction too.

"She writes that Brack came to her house yesterday. That he told her if her name appears anywhere — in any paper, at any inquiry — she'll lose the compensation she hasn't yet received, and her brother loses his place at the mill, and no one in Marlow Bend hires a Quill again." Edith folded the letter along its existing creases. "She asks me to take her name off anything we've written."

"And will you?"

The question hung there, plain as a nail. Edith met Rosalind's gaze. Twenty-six years old, steady as a spirit level, and looking at her now with an expression Edith recognized because she had worn it herself once, facing Mrs. Pankhurst across a table in 1907, asking whether the movement could really justify the window-breaking, and receiving in answer not an argument but a lesson: *the argument comes later, dear. First the noise.*

"We'll discuss it at the meeting tonight," Edith said.

"That isn't what I asked."

"No," Edith agreed pleasantly. "It isn't." And she turned back to her desk, dismissing her, and heard the door close with a softness that was somehow louder than a slam.

She sat for a long time with Ada Quill's letter in front of her. Then she took up a fresh sheet and began, at last, the letter to Lady Ashcombe that she actually intended to send.

*My dear V.,*

*I must ask you to prepare the Committee for a difficult season. What is happening in Marlow Bend is not a dispute about wages. It is a systematic concealment of industrial death — I have documents, and I have names, and I am arranging for both to reach you in a form that cannot be ignored.*

*You will ask me, naturally, why I do not simply present the evidence to the magistrate's inquiry when it convenes. I will answer you honestly, because you of all people will understand: an inquiry is where evidence goes to be buried respectably. The mill owns the town's memory the way it owns the town's labour. By spring, the witnesses will have been managed. The verdict will be misadventure, or at worst negligence, with a fine of fifty pounds and a paragraph on page nine of the regional papers.*

*What we require instead is a scandal. Scandals do not die of natural causes. If the town can be made to speak — publicly, together, in defiance of the company — then the story acquires a shape the newspapers can carry. If the town is silenced instead, then the silencing itself becomes our text. Either way, Marlow Bend serves the cause. I confess I take no pleasure in writing that sentence. I take less pleasure in believing it.*

*Send what funds you can spare. Do not send lawyers yet. Lawyers arrive after the funerals; we must decide first whether there are to be any.*

She stopped. Read the last line. It was honest, which was rare in her correspondence, and honesty in a letter was a dangerous thing, and she almost struck it out.

She left it in. Lady Ashcombe respected nothing so much as a woman unafraid of her own arithmetic.

* * *

In the afternoon Edith walked the town, because she always walked the towns, because a motor car showed you what a place wanted you to see and your own feet showed you what it was. She went up the High Street past shuttered windows — the glazier doing brisk business in opaque glass since the autumn — and down past the chapel where the relief committee met, and stood a while outside the mill gates watching the shift change, the men coming out grey-faced into the grey light, each one glancing at the gatehouse clock as he passed, as if confirming that his life still belonged to somebody else's schedule.

Brack was at the gate. She knew him by reputation and by sight now: a heavy man with a foreman's way of standing, weight forward, hands loose, a man who had been passed over once and had decided that the world owed him the difference. He saw her looking. He touched his cap with a mockery so faint it might have been manners, and smiled with teeth that did not involve his eyes.

Edith smiled back, and in the exchange each of them understood the other perfectly, which was the closest thing to intimacy either would offer the other for some time.

At the corner of Mill Row she found what she had come out to find: the Quill house, narrow and damp-walled, a child's coat on the line despite the cold, a woman in the doorway shelling nothing, just moving her hands to keep them busy. Ada Quill saw her and went inside. The door did not slam. It closed the way doors close in frightened houses — carefully, apologetically, as if even the wood could be blamed for what it witnessed.

*There it is,* Edith thought, standing in the street with her good coat and her good boots among people who had neither. *There is the figure in the equation.*

Ada Quill, thirty-one years old, four children, one brother, one dead husband, and a terror so complete that she had written to the one person she thought might protect her, begging to be erased. If Edith protected her — withdrew her name, kept her testimony out of print, let the inquiry pass her by — then Ada lived out her life in Marlow Bend, poor and safe and forgotten, and her husband's death remained what the ledger said it was: an accident, November the ninth, cause recorded as misadventure, name not required.

And if Edith did not protect her — if the Winter Count printed her statement with her full name, as the widow herself had once demanded before fear got its hands on her — then either the town rose, or Ada Quill was destroyed, and possibly both, and either outcome was ink worth having.

*Either way, Marlow Bend serves the cause.*

She had written it to V. that morning. Now, standing in the mud of Mill Row, she made herself say it inside her own head without the cushioning of prose, and found that it weighed exactly what she had known it would weigh.

The trouble with twenty-two years of strategy, she reflected, walking on, was that it worked. Every hard choice she had ever made had been justified by results. The woman imprisoned in Manchester whose hunger strike had filled the papers. The girl at Blackburn whose funeral Edith had turned into a meeting that filled the chapel twice over. Each time, the arithmetic had been ugly and each time it had been correct, and the sum of all that correctness was a career, a movement, a cause within sight of the franchise — and a woman who could stand in a street where a widow had just hidden from her and think, calmly, *this is useful.*

When had calculation become so easy? When had the faces become figures? She suspected it happened gradually, the way a typesetter's fingers go numb — not all at once, but trade by trade, until one day the hands do the work and the heart is free to attend to other things.

Tonight, she thought. Tonight at the meeting she would put it to the room. Not the arithmetic itself — one did not show one's working to people whose lives were the figures — but the decision. Print the names or withhold them. Let the town choose its own weapon. That was defensible. That was democracy, of a kind.

And if the town voted wrongly?

Then, she supposed, she would learn what she had taught others: that leadership means sometimes letting people make the mistake you've already priced in.

* * *

The meeting was held in the back room of the print shop, because the chapel was watched and the relief hall was watched, and Tomas Harker's premises had the advantage of a wall shared with the coffin-maker's yard and a cellar door that opened onto two different streets. Eleven people came: the Harkers, Rosalind, three mill women Edith knew by face, a chapel deacon with a stammer that worsened when he lied, and old Mrs. Tallow, who was deaf in one ear and pretended to be deaf in the other when gossip was going.

Edith laid out the situation plainly, because plainness was her gift. The inquiry would convene within weeks of the thaw. Brack was applying pressure household by household. Ada Quill had recanted. Two other witnesses, she had learned that evening, were wavering. The question was simple: did The Winter Count print the testimonies under full names, as promised, or withhold them and rely on the documents alone?

"The documents can be dismissed as stolen," said the deacon, stammering over *stolen*. "P-papers can be called forgeries. But a living woman, standing up —"

"A living woman can be ruined," said one of the mill women, flatly. "Ask Ada Quill."

"Ada Quill was ruined the day the vat cover wasn't latched," said the other mill woman. "The question is whether she gets to be ruined loudly or quietly."

They argued. Edith let them argue. This was technique, and she knew it was technique, and she permitted herself the small dishonesty anyway: let them feel they had decided, so that whichever way it broke, the responsibility would be distributed and hers would be only the largest share.

It was while the room argued — voices low and overlapping, Tomas Harker standing by his cases with his arms folded, Rosalind silent at the edge of the lamplight with her notebook closed under her hand — that Edith stepped into the passage to fetch the second folder of documents, and heard, through the door she had not quite shut, the voice of the older mill woman saying:

"And what does Mrs. Marchmont think? Properly, I mean. Not what she says. What's she counting?"

A pause. Then Rosalind's voice, quiet and level and terrible:

"She's counting us."

Another pause.

"I've driven that woman for two years," Rosalind went on, in the tone of someone reading aloud from a document she had memorized. "I've steamed open every letter that's come through her hands since Leeds, because I told myself it was caution, and then because it was habit, and then because I couldn't stop. And I'm telling you what I've read. She doesn't want this town saved. She wants this town *printed*. There's a difference, and she knows the difference, and she'd tell you herself it's a fair price if you asked her straight out. A martyr fills a thousand halls. A witness fills one courtroom and goes home."

The room had gone silent except for the stove ticking.

Edith stood in the dark of the passage with the folder in her arms, and did not go in, and did not leave.

She should go in. She should push open the door and meet it head-on, the way she met everything, turning betrayal into debate and debate into resolution. That was what Edith Marchmont did. That was who Edith Marchmont was.

Instead she stood quite still in the cold passage, holding her papers against her chest like armour that had stopped fitting, listening to her own arithmetic come back to her in a voice she had hired to drive a motor car — and understanding, with the clarity that arrives only after it is too late to pretend otherwise, that Rosalind Joubert had just told the room the truest thing said in Marlow Bend all winter.

From inside, Tomas Harker spoke at last, slowly, the words coming up out of some deep place:

"Then I think, ma'am, before we vote on anything — somebody ought to ask her straight out. And somebody ought to make sure we can believe the answer."

Footsteps moved toward the door.
