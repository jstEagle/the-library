## 18. What Rosalind Read

The shop was closed on Sundays, but the type never rested.

Rosalind had learned that in the three weeks since she'd first sat down at the stone with Tomas beside her, correcting her spacing, teaching her the weight of a word. Even when the press stood silent and the shutters were drawn against the street, there was work to be done: sorts to be redistributed, cases to be cleaned, the endless patient arithmetic of letters returning to their homes. She found it steadying in a way nothing else had been since Marlow Bend closed its bridge behind them. The type did not care about floods or inquiries or the arithmetic of movements. It only wanted to be put back where it belonged.

She was doing exactly that on the last Sunday of February when Tomas came down the stairs from the living quarters, still buttoning his collar, and stopped halfway across the floor.

"You set the widow Marsh's name wrong again."

Rosalind looked down at the galley she'd been proofing by lamplight. *Marsh.* She'd written *Mash.* The lamp hissed. Somewhere above, Mrs. Harker was singing a hymn off-key with great conviction.

"I'll fix it."

"You've fixed it twice." He came closer, took the galley from her hands with a gentleness that made it worse somehow, and held it up to the light. "You're careful everywhere else. Numbers, dates, Brack's name spelled right every time, which I'd have thought would be the hard one." He set it back on the stone. "But her name keeps slipping. Three times now."

"It doesn't mean anything."

"No," he agreed. "It probably doesn't. But you've been carrying something for days, Rosalind, and you carry it like a woman who's used to carrying things alone, which is to say badly." He pulled out the stool at the end of the composing stone and sat, elbows on knees, waiting.

That was the trouble with printers, she thought. They read for a living. They could not help noticing what was set crooked.

---

She should tell him. She had known she should tell him for eleven days, ever since the night she'd overheard Edith in the parlor calculating what Marlow Bend might be worth as a martyrdom — but that was its own confession, owed to Edith or perhaps to no one. This one was older. This one went back years, back before the flood, before the print shop, before she'd ever heard the name Harker spoken aloud in anything but the flat tones of Edith Marchmont's correspondence.

It went back to the steaming kettle and the bone folder and the long grey afternoons on trains between speaking engagements, when the envelopes lay open on her lap like mouths.

She had told herself a hundred stories about it. That she read the letters for security — a chauffeur ought to know what threats were coming. That she read them because Edith surely knew and didn't mind, because no one sealed anything that carelessly unless they wanted it found. That she read them out of hunger, plain and simple, the hunger of a woman with no family and no country of her own who had learned that other people's paper was the closest thing to a window she would ever get. All of those were true. None of them excused what she was about to confess.

Because among the hundreds of letters she had opened with her breath and her patience — letters from duchesses and schoolmistresses and angry vicars — there had been ones from mill towns. From Lancashire. From the Rhondda. Letters written on cheap paper in handwriting gone shaky, describing locked exits and falsified safety reports and men sent back into rooms where the air itself was flammable. Letters asking whether Miss Marchmont's movement could send someone, or even just send word, just make some noise that might make the owners careful for a season.

And Rosalind had read them, resealed them perfectly — she was very good at resealing; it was, she sometimes thought bitterly, the single finest skill she possessed — and passed them up to Edith without a word, because they were not addressed to her, because it was not her place, because the letters were Edith's to answer and Edith's alone.

One of them had come from Marlow Bend.

She hadn't known it then. She knew it now, had known it since the night in chapter of her own memory she thought of as *the ledger*, when Edith had handed her the stolen accident page and she'd recognized the handwriting on the covering note that had arrived eight months ago, unsigned, postmarked from this town, describing "a boy hurt on the carding machines" and begging someone, anyone, to look into whether the mill's doctor was writing down what he saw.

A boy on the carding machines. Two winters ago. She had read that letter on the 4:10 from York with it open on her knee, and she had reseal it so well that even Edith never noticed the crease, and she had said nothing, and somewhere in Marlow Bend a family had waited for an answer that never came.

Tomas's brother had stopped coming home two winters ago.

---

"I need to tell you something," she said, "and then you may want me off your premises, and I'll go quietly."

Tomas didn't move. "Go on."

She wiped her palms on her apron — his apron, the one he'd given her, which made it worse — and made herself stand straight, because if she was going to do this she would do it standing, like testimony, though there was no magistrate here and no record being kept except the one inside her own chest.

"When I worked for Miss Marchmont," she began, and her voice came out steady, which surprised her, "I read her correspondence. All of it. For four years. I steamed the envelopes open and I read every letter and I sealed them again, and I got so good at it that nobody ever knew. Not her, not anyone."

The hymn upstairs had stopped. Or perhaps Mrs. Harker had merely reached a quieter verse. The lamp hissed on.

"Why?" Tomas asked. Not angry yet. Asking, the way he asked about a misprinted line — wanting the cause before the consequence.

"Hunger," Rosalind said. "Habit. Fear. Take your pick; all three are true. I had nobody. Reading other people's letters was the nearest thing I had to being let into their houses. I told myself it was harmless. I told myself half of it was practically my duty — knowing what threats were coming, knowing who was watching her." She swallowed. "I told myself whatever I had to tell myself to keep doing it, which is, I suppose, what everyone tells themselves."

"And?" Tomas said softly. "There's more. There's a reason you're telling me this tonight instead of any of the hundred nights you could have."

"There's more." Her mouth had gone dry. "Among the letters there were some from mill towns. Workers' families. People asking for help — real help, inspectors, publicity, anything. Letters describing dangerous conditions, falsified reports. And I read them all, and I resealed them, and I gave them to her, and that part isn't mine to apologize for, that was her mail and her cause. But—" 

*Say it. Say the whole of it. You didn't steam open envelopes for four years to learn how to leave things half-read.*

"But eight months ago a letter came from Marlow Bend. Unsigned. Postmarked here. It described a boy injured on the carding machines, and it said the mill's own doctor wasn't writing down what he saw, and it asked whether anyone — anyone at all — would come and look." Her voice cracked on the last word and she let it. "I read it on a train between York and Leeds. And I sealed it back up and I handed it to her and I never said a word about it to anyone, because it wasn't mine. Because it was addressed to her and it was her decision and I was only the driver."

Tomas had gone very still. The kind of stillness she had seen in him once before, the day they'd printed the widow Marsh's statement and his hands had trembled setting the figures of what the mill paid per funeral.

"My brother," he said, "worked the carding room."

"I know."

"He was hurt two winters ago. They told us he fell on the stairs. We believed it, because why wouldn't we believe it? Everyone believes a staircase." His voice was level in a way she distrusted profoundly, the way you distrust ice that looks solid. "Are you telling me that letter was about him?"

"I don't know. It wasn't signed. It said *a boy*. It said the carding machines." She made herself hold his eyes. "I'm not telling you I could have saved him. I don't know that. Maybe Miss Marchmont read it and couldn't act, maybe the letter got lost in everything else, maybe there was nothing anyone could have done across that distance. I'm not telling you I know the shape of what I lost by keeping quiet. I'm telling you that I read it, and I kept quiet, and I've been keeping quiet about keeping quiet since the day I walked into this house. And you've been feeding me and teaching me and giving me your brother's apron—"

"That was our father's apron."

"—and I have let you do all of it while sitting on a letter that might have warned you." She stopped. There was nothing left. The whole ugly truth of it stood in the lamplight between them: four years of opened envelopes, and one envelope in particular, creased so neatly, sealed so perfectly, carried so carefully into silence.

The clock in the corner ticked. Upstairs, Mrs. Harker had started the hymn again from the beginning.

---

Tomas rose from the stool.

For one absurd moment Rosalind thought he was going to strike her — not because he seemed violent, but because she had spent four years reading the private weather of strangers and had never once learned what people did with rage that had nowhere lawful to go. But he didn't strike her. He walked to the window, put his hand flat against the shuttered glass as if checking the flood's height, and stood there with his back to her.

"Four years," he said.

"Four years."

"You read everything. The threats against her. The plans. The names of everyone who wrote to her trusting her."

"Yes."

"And you're good at it. Resealing. You said."

"Very good."

He turned around. In the lamplight his face was doing something complicated, grief and anger and — this was the part she couldn't bear — something that looked almost like recognition. "Do you know what I keep thinking? I keep thinking about the winter count. About how we started printing it. Do you remember what I told you, the first night you found us running the press?"

"You said the mill writes the accidents down wrong on purpose. You said somebody has to write them down right."

"Somebody has to *read* them right, too. That's the other half of it. A record nobody reads is just paper in a drawer. The mill counts on that — on paper piling up that no one will ever open." He came back toward the composing stone slowly. "So here's my trouble, Rosalind Joubert. Half of me wants to throw you out on the flooded street for sitting on that letter. And the other half — the half that's been watching you correct typos at two in the morning, the half that watched you take the widows' statements like their words were breakable — that half is thinking something I don't much like thinking."

"What?"

"That you may be the best reader we've got." He laughed, short and bitter, without any humor in it. "You heard me. Four years of opening other people's mail, and you can read a town like this faster than any of us can. You spotted the pattern in the accident ledger before I did. You knew Constance Vane was keeping second books before she'd admitted it to herself. And I hate that. I hate that the thing I'm angriest about is the thing I most need."

"Tomas—"

"Don't." He held up a hand. "Don't make it easy. I'm not forgiving you tonight. I want to say that plainly, because you seem like a woman who'd rather have it plainly." He sat back down on the stool, and suddenly he looked tired in a way that had nothing to do with the hour — tired the way a man is tired who has carried a coffin once already and has just learned the ground under it was softer than he thought. "My mother is asleep upstairs. She thinks Danny fell on the stairs. Someday soon she's going to read the winter count with his name in it, and she's going to ask me how we know, and I'm going to have to tell her that part of how we know came from a letter a stranger read and sealed away. So no. Not tonight."

Rosalind nodded. Her eyes were burning and she did not wipe them, because she had decided she would not hide anything else in this room.

"Here's what I'll give you," Tomas went on. "You told me. Nobody made you. There's no inquiry coming for you over it, no warrant, no reason on God's earth except that it was eating you alive and you chose to let it out rather than feed it another week. I've lived in this town my whole life, Miss Joubert, and I can count on one hand the number of people who've done a thing like that — handed over the worst of themselves when they could have buried it deeper." He picked up the galley with *MASH* still mocking them both in mirror-image type. "So you stay. You keep working. But you set that widow's name right every time from now until the day we close this shop, and when the edition comes out, you write the account of what happened to Danny Harker yourself. Your hand on the type. Your name under it, if it comes to that. That's not forgiveness. That's terms."

"Terms," Rosalind repeated. Her voice came out rough. "I accept them."

"And one more thing." He looked up at her, and for the first time all night something moved behind his eyes that wasn't grief. "This instinct of yours — reading what isn't yours, seeing what people hide. You're going to swear to me you'll use it for us now. Openly. No more kettles, no more steam, no more sealing things away perfect. If you see something in this town that needs saying, you bring it into the light and we set it in type together. Can you do that?"

"Yes," she said. And found, examining herself honestly, that she meant it — that the old hunger had not died but had changed its food. She did not want envelopes anymore. She wanted galleys. She wanted her work where anyone could read it and blame her for it.

"Then get the *r* from the job case," Tomas said, nodding at the galley. "*Mash* to *Marsh*. Third time tonight. Make it the last."

She set it. Her hands were shaking and the letter went in slightly crooked anyway, and he watched her do it and did not correct her grip, and when it was done he laid the galley on the stone between them like a small treaty signed in metal.

Above them, Mrs. Harker's hymn had ended. The house creaked toward sleep.

They were still there — side by side at the stone, not speaking, the distance between them narrowed by one honest letter and not yet closed — when the knock came at the front door below. Three slow raps. Then, after a pause, three more, soft and deliberate, in a rhythm Rosalind didn't recognize.

Tomas's head came up sharply. "That's Pike's knock," he said. "His warning knock. Something's wrong."
