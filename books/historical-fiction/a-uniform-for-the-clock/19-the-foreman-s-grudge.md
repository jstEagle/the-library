## 19. The Foreman's Grudge

The thing about being passed over, Silas Brack had learned, was that nobody ever told you to your face. They simply stopped telling you things.

He'd been at Marlow Bend Mill nineteen years. Nineteen years of wet wool and machine oil, of standing in the weave-shed with the damp coming up through his boots from November to March, keeping three hundred hands in line with nothing but his voice and the reputation of his fists. He'd broken two strikes before the war scare made men skittish about striking at all. He'd kept the looms running through the flood year when half the town said the river would take the mill itself. And when old Mr. Vane had needed a new works manager — a man to sit behind a desk with a window, a man whose boots stayed dry — he'd gone and hired some soft-spoken cousin from Leeds who'd never pulled a knot out of a tangle in his life.

Brack hadn't said anything. That was the trick of it. He'd shaken the cousin's hand and called him *sir* and smiled the way you smile when a tooth is dying but the dentist isn't paid for yet. And every morning since, he'd come down to the shed floor and found that the men looked at him differently. Not with less fear — they still feared him fine, fear was the one currency that never inflated — but with a kind of pity. Pity was worse than hatred. Hatred meant you were still a power. Pity meant you were a lesson mothers used: *see what happens to a man who stays too long on the floor.*

So when the inquiry clerk had come through in February with his little black book and his questions about "labor conditions," Brack had not been worried. He'd been almost grateful. Here, finally, was an instrument. The magistrate's men would write their tidy record, the mill would pay its small fines or none, and the whole ugly winter would be folded up and put away like so much damaged cloth. He'd sat across from the clerk in the office Constance Vane ran like a cold engine and answered every question with the smoothness of long practice. *Isolated incidents. Regrettable, but isolated. The company has always prioritized safety within commercial realities.* The clerk wrote it all down. Men like that always did. They wanted the record short so they could go home.

What he had not accounted for was the women.

* * *

It started, as most of his troubles did, with a name overheard.

He'd been coming out of the Anchor on the Thursday night — not drinking, never more than two, he'd learned years ago that a foreman who drank lost the edge that made men obey — when he heard them on the corner by the shuttered bakery. Two weavers' wives, heads together, shawls pulled up against the river damp. He wasn't trying to listen. He'd stopped by instinct, the way a man stops when he hears his own name in another man's mouth, even if it isn't there.

"—her own name, though. Right in it, plain as Sunday."

"Martha Quill. I couldn't believe it either. Printed it and all. My Tom says the whole second shift read it out loud in the gatehouse."

Martha Quill. Widow of Joseph Quill, card-room hand, dead fourteen months past in what the ledger called a *shafting entanglement* and what everyone in Marlow Bend knew to call what it was — a guard taken off the shaft because it slowed the line, and never put back, because putting it back meant admitting it had been off. Brack remembered the day. He remembered holding Martha Quill's arm at the inquest while she shook, and telling her the coroner would want her calm, and feeling her shake stop not because she'd calmed but because something in her had gone hard and cold as a frozen bearing.

She'd testified. Under her full name. In print.

Brack stood in the dark by the bakery with his collar up and thought about what that meant. It meant the Harkers' rag had gotten to her. It meant someone had sat with her — and he had a fair guess who; the Marchmont woman's driver had been seen calling at houses along Cordwainer Lane with a notebook, polite as a curate — and gotten her to say the words *the guard was off* into a listening ear. And now those words were sitting in a printed sheet circulating through the town like blood through a wound, and when the magistrate came in spring — weeks early now, they were saying, the bridge repairs ahead of schedule — that testimony would be waiting for him like a knife laid point-up on a table.

He should have gone home. He told himself later, many times, that he should have gone home, slept, and come at it in daylight like a manager instead of like a man with nineteen years of pity soaking into him like damp into plaster.

Instead he walked to Cordwainer Lane.

* * *

Martha Quill's house was narrow and leaned slightly toward the river, the way all the old houses did, as if craning to watch the water that fed them and killed theirs. There was a lamp burning behind the front curtain. Through it, silhouetted, he could see her at the table, and a shape beside her that took him a moment to place — the Marchmont driver. Joubert. The quiet one. The one who stood at the back of rooms holding doors and looking at nobody, which Brack had long ago learned to distrust more than the ones who looked at everybody.

They were bent over papers. Even from the street, even through cheap curtain-cloth, he could see the geometry of it: two heads close, pages between them, the small economy of people making a record.

Brack stood in the road a long time. A dog barked twice somewhere and gave up. The river worked at its pilings below, patient, indifferent, doing in a century what he wanted to do in a night.

Here was the arithmetic he ran, and he ran it honestly, because whatever else Silas Brack was, he was not a liar in his own head. The mill employed six hundred souls. Six hundred wages, six hundred hearths, six hundred families eating because the looms turned. Every man who spoke against it spoke against his neighbors' bread. That was the speech he'd have given the magistrate, and he'd have half believed it, which was the most dangerous kind of half.

But underneath the arithmetic, in the cellar where he kept the things he didn't count, there was this: Joseph Quill had died because Brack himself had signed the order to run the line fast through the spring rush. The guard had been off because Brack had approved the removal — a temporary measure, he'd written, pending review, and the review had drowned in the same paperwork as everything else, and then Quill's sleeve had caught, and the review became a widow and two fatherless children, and Brack had stood at the funeral with his hat off thinking: *that could have been me, if the sleeve had been an inch longer, if the rush had been a week later.* And he had gone back to the mill on Monday and signed the next order, because what else was there? You couldn't unring a bell. You couldn't uncatch a sleeve. All you could do was keep the machine turning and tell yourself the alternative was six hundred empty hearths, until telling yourself was the same as believing, until the story was load-bearing and the whole of your life stood on it.

And now a widow with a lamp and a quiet driver with a notebook were pulling the story down beam by beam.

He didn't knock. Knocking was for men with business. He had only grievance, and grievance doesn't knock.

* * *

He went instead, that night, to the paper stock.

He'd known where the Harkers kept it for months — not because anyone told him, but because he was the sort of man who noticed where things were stored. Reams of cheap newsprint came up from Bristol on the carrier's wagon twice a month, and the Harker boy signed for them, and they went into the dry cellar under the print shop on Mill Street through the side hatch. Paper was the whole secret of a printing operation. Presses broke; presses got replaced. Type scattered and reset. But no paper, no paper. A press without stock was furniture.

Thursday nights the shop ran late — he'd heard the press going past midnight more than once on his walks, that arrhythmic iron heartbeat, *clump-clump-clump*, muffled by brick and by their carefulness. Thursday nights the family slept in the back after a run, all four of them, tired as dogs.

He waited until Friday's small hours. Then he let himself down the side alley with a storm lantern turned low and a bottle of turpentine from the mill stores in his coat pocket, and he stood at the hatch and felt, briefly, like a burglar in his own town.

That feeling lasted exactly as long as it took him to remember the cousin from Leeds sitting behind the window with his dry boots.

The hatch lock was old. Brack carried a ring of keys that opened half of Marlow Bend — foremen acquired keys the way rivers acquired silt — and the third one turned it. Inside, the cellar smelled of size and dust and ink, that particular papery smell he'd hated since boyhood, schoolrooms and sermons and everything written by men who never sweated. The reams were stacked against the far wall in neat columns, wrapped in brown paper, labeled in pencil in a woman's hand — the sister, probably, the sharp-eyed one who kept the accounts. *500 — fine. 300 — coarse. Rags, mixed.*

He poured the turpentine slowly, working along the base of the stacks, letting it soak into the brown wrapping. It smelled sharp and clean, like the mill's own spirit, like the solvent they wiped the machines down with at year's end, and there was a horrible rightness to that, a symmetry: he was cleaning. This was maintenance. The town was a machine and these reams were a seized bearing and you removed a seized bearing or the whole line burned, that was simple engineering, any fool could see—

A floorboard creaked above him.

Brack froze, lantern hooded, one hand flat against a stack of paper that was worth more than his quarterly wage. Above, footsteps crossed the ceiling, paused, crossed again. The shop dog — some brindled mutt that slept in the lobby — whined once and went quiet.

Then a voice, muffled through the floorboards, a young man's voice, sleep-rough: "Tomas? That you down there?"

Silence. Brack counted ten heartbeats. The footsteps moved away toward the back of the house, and a door closed, and the house settled back into its breathing.

He should have lit it then. The match was in his fingers. One strike, one touch, and the whole winter's ammunition for the other side goes up in smoke, and when the inquiry asks how the underground paper was supplied, let them answer that.

He struck the match. Held it. Watched the flame stand in the cellar draft, steady, absurdly domestic, like a candle on a supper table.

And what stopped his hand — what he would examine for the rest of his life and never fully forgive — was not conscience. He'd spent too many years around death to flatter himself with conscience. It was the pencil writing on the wrappers. *Rags, mixed.* Someone had written that label. Some tired person, up past midnight setting type by hand letter by letter, had taken thirty seconds to label their paper stock so they'd know the fine from the coarse. It was such a small, human, stupid piece of care that for a moment Brack saw the cellar not as an arsenal but as a kitchen — saw the Harkers as what they mostly were, a family printing truth in a town that punished it — and the match trembled.

Then he thought of Joseph Quill's sleeve. Of the sound. Of the inquest, and Martha Quill's shaking arm going still under his hand.

He blew the match out.

Not mercy. He told himself it was tactics, and partly it was: a fire would bring the constable, and Pike — Pike was wrong somehow lately, Pike's reports had gone vague as weather — a fire might bring questions he couldn't steer. Better, said tactics, to take the paper without fire. Let them wake to a cellar of nothing. Let the town read the last Winter Count and find no more coming. Starve the press, don't burn it. Burning made martyrs; emptiness made despair, and despair was quieter.

But tactics was only half of it, and he knew the other half's name, and he left it unnamed as he hauled the first stack up the ladder.

* * *

It took him three trips, wheeling the barrow from the print-shop cellar to the mill's waste furnace across two miles of sleeping town, and by the third trip the sky was graying at the east like a bruise coming up, and his arms ached, and somewhere in the middle of it he had stopped pretending to himself entirely and admitted what he was doing: not protecting the mill. Avenging himself. On the widow, on the driver, on the cousin with the dry boots, on the whole arrangement of a world that had watched him keep the line running for nineteen years and rewarded him with a desk job given to somebody else's nephew.

The furnace was already warm from the night shift. He fed the paper in armload by armload, and the flames took it greedily, brown wrappers curling black, and the heat came out at him like a crowd's breath, and he stood there feeding his enemy's winter into the fire and felt — this was the shameful part, the part he buried deepest — *good*. Not righteous. Good the way a man feels good after vomiting: emptied, purged, lighter, and no better for it.

When it was done he raked the ashes flat and walked home through streets going pink with dawn, and at his door he met the milk cart, and the milk boy nodded to him respectful as ever, *morning, Mr. Brack,* and Brack nodded back and understood suddenly that the boy would nod the same way tomorrow, and the day after, no matter what. That was the horror of it. The world would keep respecting him. Nothing outside him would change. Only inside, where the match had trembled, would anything be different, forever.

By nine o'clock the town would know. By ten, Tomas Harker would be standing in his empty cellar with his cap in his hands. By noon the widows would know why. And by evening, Brack guessed, the quiet driver would be at the Harker house again, notebook open, and this time she wouldn't be writing down testimony.

She'd be writing down him.

He set his lantern on the hall table and stood a moment in his own doorway, listening to the mill start up across the water, the great building clearing its throat, three hundred hands taking hold of three hundred shuttles, everything running, everything paid for, everything fine.

Somewhere above the print shop on Mill Street, a floorboard creaked.
