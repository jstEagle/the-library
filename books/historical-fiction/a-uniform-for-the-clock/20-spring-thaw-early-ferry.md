## 20. Spring Thaw, Early Ferry

The river told them first.

Rosalind heard it before she understood it — a change in the voice of the water below the Harkers' window, the low grinding mutter of March giving way to something quicker, brighter, more purposeful. She had lived beside the Marlow Bend current long enough now to know its moods the way she once knew the creak of Edith's motorcar: the sullen winter drag, the brown and swollen patience of it, water moving like a man walking through mud with his hands in his pockets. But this morning the river had lifted its chin. Ice-floe gone. The shallows showing their gravel teeth again. And on the far bank, where for five months there had been nothing but the drowned stumps of the old bridge pilings and a ferry chain hanging slack as a dead man's arm — there were men.

She stood at the window with her hair half-pinned and watched them, small figures in the grey light, swarming over the new stone abutments that had risen through the winter like something the flood itself had coughed up. Hammers ticked faintly across the water. A crane arm swung. The span that was not supposed to be finished until the first week of May — the span whose incompleteness had been, for months, both a prison wall and a shield — was reaching out from the far shore like a hand.

They came down the stairs two at a time when she called them. Tomas arrived still buttoning his collar, his sleeves rolled, ink already on his left wrist from some midnight errand he hadn't mentioned. Mrs. Harker followed with a kettle she had no intention of putting down until she'd seen for herself. Even young Alice pressed her nose to the glass below the adults' shoulders, fogging it, wiping it, fogging it again.

"They said May," Tomas said flatly. "The engineer himself stood in this kitchen and said the frost wouldn't let them pour the last footing till April was done."

"Then someone has paid the frost to change its mind," Mrs. Harker said, and set down the kettle harder than she needed to.

Rosalind said nothing at first. She was doing what she always did, the habit that had made her useful to dangerous people and dangerous to comfortable ones: she read the scene the way she read an envelope, looking for what was written between the lines. Men working at speed. Men working in numbers larger than a county council would fund out of patience. Men who had been sent.

"It's the inquiry," she said finally. "They want the bridge ready so the commissioners can come up by rail and cross without delay. They're building the road to their own hearing." She turned from the window. "Which means we know the date before they announce it. They'll convene within a week of the span opening. Perhaps less."

Tomas looked at her, and she saw the thought arrive behind his eyes the way dawn arrives — not all at once, but all along the ridge at the same moment. "Then we set the type now," he said. "Tonight."

"Not tonight," Rosalind said. "Now. This morning. Everything we've gathered, everything we've promised to gather — the widow's statements, Pike's report if he'll risk it, whatever Constance Vane can safely give us — it goes into the forme this week or it never goes in at all. Once the magistrate is sitting in the town hall with his clerks and his shorthand men, once the official record starts turning like a sausage machine—" She stopped, because she could hear Edith's cadence in her own voice, the campaign rhetoric she'd absorbed through a hundred closed carriage doors, and hated it a little. "Once they start writing it down their way, our paper becomes a curiosity instead of a verdict. It has to land while the truth is still wet."

Mrs. Harker had gone very still by the stove. It was Alice who broke the silence, in the blunt way of children who have grown up around grief and learned to handle it like firewood. "Is Papa's name going to be in it? Spelled right?"

Rosalind crouched to the girl's height. Through the winter she had learned — slowly, clumsily, a woman built for reading learning to listen — that the questions children asked were the ones adults spent whole lifetimes avoiding. "Spelled exactly right," she said. "I'm setting every letter myself. And I'll show you the tray it comes from, if you like. J is one of the prettiest letters we have."

Alice nodded once, gravely, and went back to the window to watch the bridge grow.

* * *

By ten o'clock the shop smelled of hot metal and cold fear, which Rosalind was coming to understand was simply the smell of printing done under deadline.

The back room had transformed since the night she'd first found them mid-run, since the night she'd corrected a turned letter instead of reporting what she saw. Wallpaper samples still hung along one wall, the innocent shop-front of the trade, but behind them the type cases stood open in ranks, and the stone where the forme would be locked waited like an altar waiting for its offering. Tomas moved among the cases with a composure she envied, pulling sorts with fingers that knew their alphabet better than most men knew their prayers.

"The widow's statements first," he said. "Eleven of them. Then the ledger pages Constance copied — those go last, closest to press time, in case she needs to pull anything back. Then the names."

"The names," Rosalind said. "All of them?"

"All of them. Every one the count holds, back six winters. Even the ones nobody's sure about — we print them with a question mark rather than leave them out. A question mark is honest. An absence is just another kind of lie." He set a capital T in the composing stick with a small precise click. "You'll do the widows' text. Your hand's faster than mine now, and steadier than you think."

It was, she realized, the third time he'd paid her that particular compliment without seeming to notice he'd paid it — your hand's faster, your eye's better, you catch things I miss — and each time it landed somewhere in her chest that no compliment had ever reached, because every other praise she'd ever earned had been for concealment. *So careful, Miss Joubert. So discreet. Nothing gets past you.* Skills of the spy, praised by people who wanted spies. Skills of the printer, praised by people who wanted the truth legible.

"I'll need the originals again," she said. "The widow's statements. For accuracy."

"They're where they've been since the raid." He didn't say *the flour bin*. They had stopped saying *the flour bin* aloud weeks ago, after the constable's search, after the coffin, after the night Pike knocked twice-and-a-half on the street door and gave them ten minutes that had saved everything. Some words you retired like worn-out type, melted down, recast into other sentences. "But Rosalind—" He stopped, stick in hand, and looked at her with an expression she'd learned to dread because it preceded the only kind of conversation she was bad at: an honest one. "When the bridge opens, Edith will want you on the first boat south. Her letters are piling up. Her people will send for her the moment the crossing resumes. And she'll expect—"

"She'll expect her driver to drive," Rosalind finished.

"She'll expect her reader to come home."

The word sat between them. *Her reader.* He had never used it as an accusation, even after the confession in January, even after she'd knelt in this same room amid the type trays and told him eleven years of steamed envelopes, of resealed wax, of secrets passed to her employer that might have warned his brother's widow, might have saved — no, she refused that arithmetic; Edith had taught her too well how arithmetic lies — that might have changed things. Forgiveness had come to him the way spring came to the river: not all at once, but visibly, day by day, ice letting go of the banks. Some mornings it froze again. Most mornings it ran.

"I'm not leaving before the press run," she said. "Whatever Edith wants."

"And after?"

She picked up a composing stick to give her hands work, though her hands didn't need an excuse anymore. That was the thing about typesetting nobody warned you: it occupied the body so completely that the mind either went quiet or went true. Hers had started going true sometime in February. "After," she said, setting an *A*, "I suppose I'll find out whether I'm a woman who reads other people's letters or a woman who prints her own."

He grunted — the Harker family grunt, all four of them shared it, she'd caught herself practicing it in the mirror — and turned back to his case. But at the door she heard him add, quietly, to the room or to her or to the type itself: "The shop runs better with two setters. That's not sentiment. That's scheduling."

* * *

Edith received the news of the bridge standing in her rooms above the vicarage with the stillness Rosalind had learned to read as calculation rather than calm.

She was at the window, hands folded at her waist, watching the same far-bank activity Rosalind had watched at dawn. Five months of captivity had not softened Edith Marchmont; it had distilled her, the way winter distills a tree down to its argument. She had spoken at nine meetings in Marlow Bend since December, more than in any three months of her free life, and her voice had acquired a new grain, a river-town roughness, as though the place had gotten into her throat.

"Weeks early," Edith said. "Someone bought the contractors a reason to hurry. The company, I should think — they want their inquiry held while the mill's books look tidy and Brack's witnesses are rested and rehearsed." She turned. "You intend to print before it convenes."

It wasn't a question. Rosalind had stopped being surprised that Edith knew things; she had merely stopped pretending she didn't know how. "We intend to try. There's a great deal of metal to set, Edith. Eleven testimonies, the ledger copies, the names. If we began tonight we'd be lucky to lock the chase inside a fortnight, and the fortnight may not exist."

"And if it doesn't?" Edith crossed to the table where her correspondence lay in its neat stacks — the stacks Rosalind had once opened with steam and a bone folder and a heart that beat carefully, carefully. "If the commissioners arrive to find your little sheet already in every pocket from here to the county seat, what precisely do you imagine happens? I'll tell you. The inquiry becomes a trial of the printers. The record fills with discussion of criminal libel and unlawful assembly and the character of one Tomas Harker, and the ninety-one names become evidence *against* the dead rather than for them. I have watched this happen, Rosalind. In the gas workers' strike, in the match girls' year. The powerful do not answer documents. They answer movements. And a movement requires—" she paused, selecting, "—timing."

"There's a word for what you're describing," Rosalind said. "I've been reading your mail too long not to know it. The word is *arithmetic*. You did it in January. Lives against cause. Martyrs against witnesses." She kept her voice level; she had practiced. "I stood outside that door and heard you decide that a dead town makes better headlines than a living one. I've had two months to decide what I think of it. Here's my finding: the widows didn't die so their husbands could serve the movement. Their husbands died because a ledger had no column for human beings. I won't help build another one."

The silence stretched. Outside, the hammers ticked across the water.

Edith sat, slowly, and for a moment — a genuine moment, the first in months — she looked her age, and older, and tired in the bones where the tiredness actually lived. "You sound like me at twenty-four," she said. "Before I learned that rage keeps badly and strategy keeps well." She looked up. "But hear me, girl, because I'll only say this once. I am not asking you to stop the paper. I am telling you that if it fails — if the press is taken, if the men are charged, if the edition burns in the yard — then everything we have built in this valley burns with it, and I will not be able to save it, and neither will you. Print if you must. But print knowing the cost is real and will be paid in full."

"I've known the cost since November," Rosalind said. "I've read enough letters to recognize a bill when it comes due."

At the door, Edith spoke again, softer. "Rosalind. When the ferry runs — my people will wire London within the hour. Funds, lawyers, the national committee. All of it, finally, across that bridge." A pause. "There will be a seat in the car going south. There has always been a seat."

Rosalind stood with her hand on the latch and felt the whole winter balance on that sentence — the warm motorcar, the purposeful journeys, the competent loneliness dressed up as service, the envelopes she'd never be asked to steam again because Edith would never again trust her with them, and beneath all of it the small dark truth she'd carried since the confessional night upstairs at the print shop: that she had loved being indispensable more than she had ever examined what she was indispensable *to*.

"I'll see you at the hearing," she said, and went out.

* * *

The afternoon brought Constance Vane, which meant the afternoon brought danger wearing good gloves.

She arrived at the shop's front counter in her father's trap, alone, ostensibly to order wedding invitations — a fiction so threadbare that Mrs. Harker rang it up with a perfectly straight face and a price for cream card stock that existed nowhere on earth. Then Rosalind walked her through to the back room, because there was no longer any point pretending to each other, and there had never been any point at all since the charity evening in October when Constance had looked at Rosalind across a table of subscription lists and said, mildly, *You read upside-down. So do I.*

Constance set her reticule on the composing stone and drew out an envelope of thin paper, handled the way one handles a live coal. "Two more pages," she said. "Dated invoices. Safety repairs ordered and signed off — *before* the dates on the accident reports they were supposedly responding to. My father's signature on one. Brack's initials on the other." Her voice stayed level; Constance's voice always stayed level; Rosalind had come to understand that the levelness cost her more than shouting would cost another woman. "And a warning. Brack has been asking questions about the shop. Not through channels — through the yard, over drinks, the way men ask when they've already decided and want only the date. He knows the coffin trick failed to interest anyone official, and he's humiliated, and humiliation in that man does not fade. It ferments."

"He burned our paper stock in February," Tomas said from the case. "He's not subtle."

"No. Which is why he's dangerous. Subtle men negotiate." Constance looked at Rosalind, and for a heartbeat something moved under the mill-owner's-daughter mask — not softness exactly; kinship, maybe, the cold recognition between two women who had made careers of other people's paperwork. "How long to set all of it? Truthfully. Don't spare me."

Rosalind did the sums she'd been doing since dawn, against the hammer-ticks coming through the wall, against the memory of the engineer's promise of May broken like ice. "If nothing interrupts us — five days. Six to be safe."

"And what will interrupt us?"

"Brack," said Tomas.

"The inquiry," said Rosalind.

"My father," said Constance, "if he realizes his daughter has been robbing him. Though I confess at this point I've stopped being certain which of us he'd punish more severely — the thief or the witness." She slid the envelope across the stone. "Set it. Print it. Put my name on the testimony page if it comes to that. I've spent my life keeping books that balanced on paper and lied in fact. Consider this the correcting entry."

She left the way she'd come, gloves buttoned, spine straight, a woman carrying dynamite in a handbag and daring the world to inspect it. At the counter she paused, half-turned. "Miss Joubert. When this is over — whichever way it goes — I should like to learn to set type. I'm told it occupies the hands and frees the mind."

"It does both," Rosalind said. "Though not always in the order you'd hope."

Something almost like a smile crossed Constance Vane's face, and then she was gone, and the bell above the door settled, and the three of them stood in the back room around the stone with the whole truth lying on it in thin paper, weighing less than a loaf of bread and heavier than the bridge.

* * *

They worked into the evening, and the evening into night, and by lamplight the forme grew the way the bridge grew across the river: course by course, joint by joint, two structures racing toward the same meeting.

Rosalind set the widow Ellen Crale's statement first, because Ellen had insisted on her full name — *Ellen Crale, née Whitlock, of Canal Street, wife of Joseph Crale, kilnman* — had insisted the way other people sign oaths. Setting it letter by letter, Rosalind found she could hear the woman's voice in the click of each sort landing in the stick, and she understood suddenly why Tomas had given her this work and not the ledgers. The ledgers were evidence. This was resurrection. You did not give resurrection to a beginner.

Tomas worked beside her in the lamp-yellow quiet, his broken-knuckled hands steady, and once, near midnight, he reached over without looking and turned a letter she'd set upside down — a *p* where a *d* belonged — and she laughed, actually laughed, at three in the morning over a piece of metal the size of a fingernail, and heard her own laugh come back off the wallpaper samples sounding like someone she'd never met before this winter and intended to keep.

Alice slept on a cot in the corner. Mrs. Harker brought tea at intervals measured in devotion rather than clock time. Outside, the river ran bright and quick, and the hammers had stopped, and the far bank showed a line of lanterns where the night crew worked by torchlight, hurrying the span toward them.

Near dawn, Rosalind set the last line of Ellen Crale's statement and sat back and read it in the galley, backwards, as printers do, and then forwards, as everyone else will:

*My husband went into that mill on the fourth of December and did not come out, and the book says he was never there.*

Below it there was space — a few inches of empty stone, waiting.

"What goes there?" she asked.

Tomas came and stood beside her and looked at the blankness for a long time. "The last name," he said. "Whatever it turns out to be. We leave the space open until the forme locks. In case there's one more we don't know about yet. In case someone finds courage between now and press day." He touched the empty stone gently, the way you'd touch a grave you hoped wasn't finished being dug. "That's the difference between our book and theirs, Rosalind. Theirs is closed the moment it's convenient. Ours stays open until it's true."

Through the front window, grey light was coming up over the water, and on the river a barge was moving — the first barge in five months, riding the new channel, and behind it, unmistakable even at distance, a string of workmen hauling cable hand over hand, the ferry chain rising off its winter bed.

The crossing would run within days. The commissioners were already packing their files in the county seat. Somewhere in the mill yard, Brack was drinking and deciding.

Rosalind wiped her hands on her apron — *her* apron, the uniform of the clock — and reached for the next stick of type.

They had five days. Maybe six. Maybe fewer.

The forme had a space in it that was still waiting to be filled.
