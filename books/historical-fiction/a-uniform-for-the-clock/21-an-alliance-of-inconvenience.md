## 21. An Alliance of Inconvenience

The ledgers weighed less than Constance had expected. She had imagined them heavy as sin — a satchel of stone she would carry to her grave or to the gallows — but copied into a cheap exercise book in her own small, careful hand, the whole ruin of Marlow Bend Mills fit inside twenty pages. Her father's empire, reduced to pencil. There was a lesson in that, and she did not care for it.

She waited until dusk to leave the house. This was not sentimentality; it was arithmetic. Her father dined at seven with Brack and the mill's solicitor, and the three of them talked past ten about the inquiry's schedule, about which widows might be bought and which must be frightened, about the magistrate's fondness for port. Constance poured the wine, noted who drank how much of it, and excused herself at half past nine on the pretext of a headache. The headache was real enough. It had lived behind her eyes since November.

She took the river path rather than the high street, because the high street had eyes. The flood had gone down weeks ago, leaving the banks scoured and pale, heaped with dead reeds and the small debris of other people's lives — a chair leg, a child's boot, half a shutter from some house upstream. The water moved slow and brown and patient, like a man walking behind you at a fixed distance. Above it all, the mill stood black against the last grey light, its windows lit floor by floor for the night shift, so that it looked less like a building than a great animal breathing.

*My father's animal,* she thought. *Fed on what?*

On the winter count. On names that never entered any ledger she had been permitted to see — until she had found the second set, the true one, locked in the drawer of a desk her father believed she dared not open. She had copied it over four nights while the household slept, her candle shaded with a tin plate, her hand steadier than she had any right to expect. Injuries dated before the accidents that supposedly caused them. Compensation payments entered as "charitable relief" to women whose husbands were listed nowhere at all. And one column, in Brack's blunt fist, headed only with a dash and a number: eleven, twelve, thirteen. Thirteen men in two winters whom the mill's official record insisted had never been hurt at all.

She had told herself, copying those pages, that she did not yet know what she would do with them. That was a lie even then, and she knew it now, turning down the lane behind Chandler's Row where the print shop sat with its windows dark and its sign — HARKER & SON, JOB WORK DONE NEATLY — hanging crooked on one chain, as it had hung, she suspected, through everything.

Her heart was behaving badly. She made it stop.

* * *

She knocked twice, then stood back into the shadow of the doorway, out of the lamplight that leaked under the door like spilled honey. For a long moment nothing. Then footsteps, unhurried, and Rosalind Joubert's voice through the wood, level as a ruled line:

"Who is it?"

"Constance Vane."

The silence that followed had texture. Constance could feel the woman on the other side of the door deciding things — weighing her, pricing her, remembering every reason not to open it. She could hardly blame her. If their positions were reversed she would have bolted the door and put a chair under the handle.

"I'm alone," Constance said. "I walked the river path. No one saw me come down Chandler's Row — or if they did, they'll assume I've lost my way, which will be thought a great joke at dinner tomorrow." She heard herself being crisp and hated it; crispness was armour, and armour told people there was something underneath worth protecting. "Please, Miss Joubert. I haven't much time before I'm missed."

Bolts. Two of them. Then the door opened a hand's width, and Rosalind Joubert looked out at her with the lamp held low so the light fell upward across her face, making her look like a portrait of someone deciding.

"You shouldn't be here," Rosalind said. It was not a refusal. Constance had spent her life listening to refusals dressed as courtesies and courtesies dressed as refusals, and this was neither. This was a warning offered as fact.

"No," Constance agreed. "May I come in regardless?"

A pause. Then Rosalind stepped back, and the door opened, and Constance Vane crossed the threshold of Harker & Son for the first time in her life.

* * *

The shop smelled of ink and oil and cold metal, and beneath that, faintly, of flour and lamp-black and the particular staleness of rooms where people had been living carefully. A press dominated the far wall — smaller than she'd expected, older, its iron frame painted green many coats ago — and beyond it a staircase went up into lamplight where voices murmured and stopped the moment her heel clicked on the boards.

"They know I'm here," Constance said.

"They know someone's here." Rosalind closed the door and shot both bolts. "They don't know who. Give them a moment to decide whether to come down armed with a composing stick or merely with questions."

"And which will you recommend?"

Rosalind looked at her then, fully, and Constance understood why Edith Marchmont kept this woman close without ever quite introducing her. There was nothing soft in her face to hold on to, and nothing cruel either — just an attention so complete it felt like being read aloud. "I'll recommend," Rosalind said, "that we hear what you came to say before anyone decides anything. Sit down, Miss Vane."

"Mrs.," said Constance, out of pure habit, and then, surprising herself, "—though my husband has been dead six years and the title never fit. Constance will do. We're about to be conspirators; formality seems wasted."

"We're not yet anything." Rosalind pulled a stool from under the counter and set the lamp between them, so that its light belonged to neither of them equally. Upstairs the voices resumed, lower than before, and then a door shut and there was only the two of them and the tick of the shop's clock — an ugly thing with a painted face, keeping honest time above a dishonest town.

Constance set the exercise book on the counter. She found she could not push it across. Her hand stayed flat upon the cover, holding down twenty pages of her father's crimes as though they might otherwise fly up like birds.

"You'll want to know why," she said.

"It occurred to me," Rosalind said.

* * *

She gave Rosalind the whole of it, because she had decided on the river path that half-truths would be worse than useless here — these people dealt in documents; they would smell a doctored account the way Tomas Harker smelled bad type. She told her about finding the second ledger in October, when she had gone looking for the coal accounts and found instead the column of dashes and numbers. She told her about Brack coming to the office in January with a safety report already signed and asking her father's clerk to date it forward, and how she had said nothing, and how the saying-nothing had begun to feel like signing. She told her about confronting the foreman — she watched Rosalind's stillness sharpen at that — and about the conversation afterward, in the yard, when Brack had smiled at her with his hat in his hands and explained, very reasonably, what would become of a woman whose reputation rested on her father's name if certain stories got out. Stories about her mother. Stories that weren't even true. True ones were never necessary; Brack understood that better than most theologians.

"He didn't threaten me exactly," Constance said. "He offered to protect me from himself. It was almost elegant."

"And after that you decided to copy the ledger."

"After that I stopped sleeping, and sleepless women make decisions. Mine was that if I was going to be ruined either way, I preferred it be for something." She looked at her hand on the book. "I told myself I hadn't chosen a side. That I was simply gathering facts, the way one gathers anything — against a rainy day. But you don't copy thirteen deaths in pencil by candlelight over four nights unless you already know which way the rain is falling."

Upstairs, a board creaked. Neither of them looked up.

"Why bring it here?" Rosalind asked. "You have lawyers. You have the inquiry itself — you could walk into that room and put these pages on the magistrate's desk yourself, under oath, with your name attached and your father's solicitor unable to lay a glove on you until cross-examination. Why a print shop on Chandler's Row?"

Because the inquiry sands things smooth. Because she had sat in the gallery two days running and watched testimony go into the record clean and bloodless, grief rendered as "conditions," death rendered as "an unfortunate occurrence during ordinary operations." Because she had watched the magistrate write *noted* in his small dry hand and move his pen along. But she said none of that first. What she said was:

"Because your paper prints names."

Rosalind said nothing, and the clock ticked, and somewhere upstairs a type case slid shut with a sound like a breath held and released.

"The Winter Count," Constance went on. "Yes, I've read it. You needn't look alarmed — my father's had copies since December and reads them aloud to Brack in order to laugh, which tells you more about them than about the paper. I've read every number. I checked them against my ledger. They're right. Every figure, every date. Whoever sets that type does it properly." Her voice did something unsteady on the last words and she let it. Honesty was the coin here; she had brought nothing else. "The inquiry will record conditions. Your paper records people. I want the names printed. All of them. Spelled correctly. Before my father's lawyers can file the official version so deep in the county archive that no one ever digs it out again."

"And your part in it?"

"My testimony. Under my own name, if you'll have it — I know what that costs, and I've counted it twice." She finally pushed the book across the counter, and the loss of its weight under her palm was so sudden she nearly reached for it back. "Everything in there is copied fair from the original. The original stays where it is, hidden, because if your shop is searched again—" she saw Rosalind's face change minutely and knew she had guessed right about the raid, about the smashed press, about all of it, "—then I lose a copy and you lose nothing that can't be recopied. I'm not brave, Miss Joubert. I'm thorough. It's the nearest thing."

* * *

Rosalind turned the pages slowly, and Constance watched her read the way one watches a surgeon work: with respect and horror in equal measure. At the fourth page Rosalind's finger stopped on a line and stayed there a long time.

"This entry," she said. "December the ninth, two winters back. The dash-column number five."

"What of it?"

"Do you know what happened on December the ninth?" Rosalind did not look up. "There's a man upstairs who could tell you. He's the brother of number five. The ledger calls him an unnamed operative lost in a flue collapse. His name was Daniel Harker. He was twenty-six, and he liked to whistle while he worked, and his brother has spent two years printing a paper to say so."

Constance felt the colour leave her face and made no effort to call it back. "I didn't know the names were recoverable. My father's clerk swore the accident records were destroyed."

"They were. That's rather the point of us." Rosalind closed the book but kept her hand flat on it, mirroring Constance's earlier gesture, and perhaps it was deliberate. "Here's what happens now. I take this upstairs. Tomas reads it. Mrs. Marchmont reads it — and understand, Miss Vane, Mrs. Marchmont has her own arithmetic, and you may find it colder than you'd like. Then they decide. Not me. I drive the motorcar and mind the correspondence and, lately, set type badly. The decision belongs to the people whose names are in that column."

"That's fair."

"It isn't fair. Nothing here is fair. It's merely how it must be done." Rosalind stood, and Constance stood with her, and for a moment the two of them regarded each other across the counter across the little green-painted press across everything their fathers' generation had built between them. "One question more. When it goes wrong — and it goes wrong, we have recent experience — when Brack comes for the shop again, or your father disowns you publicly, or both in the same week: will you stand? Or will you discover, in the bad moment, that you were only ever a tourist in this?"

Constance considered lying, found she couldn't be bothered, and answered the truth. "I don't know. I've never been tested at anything except arithmetic. But I'll tell you what I do know. Every night for four months I have poured wine for men who are buying widows, and smiled, and gone upstairs, and copied. If that isn't standing, it's at least leaning very hard in your direction."

Something moved at the corner of Rosalind's mouth — not quite a smile, but the ground where one might later be built. "That," she said, "is the first honest thing anyone's said to me in this town that wasn't sworn testimony." She picked up the exercise book and held it against her chest. "Wait here. Or rather — don't wait here. Come upstairs. If you're to be chosen or refused, be chosen or refused to your face."

* * *

The room above the shop was warm in a way the mill office had never managed: stove, kettle, a table scarred with ink rings, type trays stacked against one wall and — she noticed, because noticing was her disease — a section of wallpaper samples hung slightly proud of the plaster, and behind its edge, the glint of more trays. People had been sleeping here. People had been hiding here. A woman with grey-streaked hair sat mending a shirt by the stove with the serenity of a general; a young man with bandaged knuckles sat at the table before a spread of galleys, his right hand splinted, setting type anyway with his left, slowly, letter by letter, with the patience of a man doing penance or prayer.

Tomas Harker looked up at her, and Constance braced for hatred and found something worse: assessment. He looked at her the way he presumably looked at a line of doubtful type — checking each character against the copy.

"So," he said. "The mill owner's daughter."

"Tomas." The grey-haired woman's voice carried no heat, only instruction.

"No, it's a fair opening." He gestured with his good hand at the chair opposite. "Sit, miss. Let's see what you've brought us besides trouble."

Rosalind put the exercise book in front of him and said, quietly, "Read page four first."

And Constance Vane, who had run her father's office for nine years, who had faced down creditors and inspectors and one memorable deputation of striking dyers, sat down in a printer's kitchen among printers and folded her hands like a schoolgirl and waited to learn whether she was, at last, on anyone's side at all.

Tomas read. The stove ticked. The mending woman threaded her needle and did not pretend not to listen. And when Tomas reached the fifth line of page four — the ninth of December, two winters back — his splinted hand came down flat on the table, gently, the way a man touches a grave, and he said, in a voice scraped nearly empty:

"Say the number again. Out loud. I want to hear it in someone else's voice for once."

"Five," Constance said. "Daniel Harker. Flue collapse. Entered as no injury sustained."

The silence that followed was long, and full, and when it broke it was the mending woman who broke it, laying down her needlework and looking at Constance across the warm room with eyes that had clearly done their own arithmetic long ago.

"Well then," she said. "Get her a cup of tea. If she's to hang with us, she'd best learn how we take it."

But it was Rosalind Joubert, standing by the door with the lamp behind her, who asked the question that Constance would remember all her life — asked it mildly, almost kindly, as though inquiring after the weather:

"How quickly can you have the originals ready to move? Because the ferry lands Thursday, and the magistrate arrives on it — and Brack has just posted a man on Chandler's Row."
