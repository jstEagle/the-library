## 22. The Type Is Set

The shop had never been so quiet at two in the morning, and Tomas Harker had worked enough two-in-the-mornings to know the difference between an empty room and a full one holding its breath.

The forme lay locked in its chase before him on the stone bed of the Albion press — iron frame, wooden quoins wedged tight, every letter of every name standing shoulder to shoulder in a field of lead. He had built formes for eleven years in his father's shop and three years more in this one, wedding announcements and funeral notices and the price lists for Brack's company store, and he had never once felt what he felt now, which was that the thing on the bed was not paper waiting to happen but a person waiting to speak.

He put his hand flat on the type. Cold, of course. Metal always was. But under his palm he could feel the relief of it, letter by letter, like reading by touch the way his mother had taught him to check a locked forme — *run your fingers over it, Tomi, if you can feel every line proud of its neighbors, nothing will shift.* Every line stood proud now. Every line would stand proud when four hundred copies of The Winter Count came off this bed before dawn, and every one of those names would be spelled correctly, because Rosalind Joubert had gone through the entire edition twice with a composing stick's worth of patience and had found nine errors, including a transposition in Ellen Wray's married name that would have shamed them in front of the whole town.

He thought about that. A woman who could read a page of hand-set type the way other people read a printed book, who caught a swapped *a* and *e* buried in the fourth column, and who had learned to set type in a single night by candlelight while his hands hung useless in splints. There was a word for what she was to the shop now and he had been circling it for weeks without landing on it. Sister was too small. Apprentice was wrong; apprentices needed teaching longer than that. He settled, as he often did, on simply not saying it, and letting the apron hanging on its nail say what he couldn't.

* * *

They had finished locking up the chase at half past eleven, all four of them, working by the light of two lamps turned low and hooded toward the wall so the glow wouldn't show in the street. Rosalind had done the final tightening of the quoins with the key, because Tomas's right hand still couldn't close properly around anything smaller than a teacup, and there had been a moment — he'd remember it as long as he remembered anything — when she gave the key its last quarter turn and then just stood there, both hands resting on the iron frame, head bowed, the way people stand over graves or cradles.

"Say something, then," Marta had whispered, "or I'll start crying into the ink."

"It's locked," Rosalind said. "It's all locked." And her voice had cracked on the second sentence, just slightly, just enough, and Tomas had understood that she wasn't talking about the type.

His father had been the one to break the spell, the way fathers do. Old Ambrose had looked at the forme, then at the clock — the big regulator on the back wall that had kept the shop's time since before Tomas was born, the one that gave the town its joke about the Harkers, *uniform for the clock*, everyone dressed alike and ruled by the same hands — and said, "Two hours' sleep. All of you. We pull the first proof at four, we read it twice, and if it reads true we print at first light. Nobody prints tired. Tired men drop type and tired men get caught."

So they had gone up the stairs, and the house had creaked itself quiet, and Tomas had lain in bed listening to the river. The flood was down to its winter voice again, a mutter under the ice sheets at the bend, but it was still there, still holding the bridge closed, still holding all of them inside the town with the thing they had made. In six days, maybe seven, the engineers from Ashford would finish the new span and the magistrate's party would come across it with their ledgers and their tidy questions, and after that nothing they did tonight would ever be possible again.

It was now or it was never, and it was now, and sleep would not come near him.

He got up without lighting a lamp, out of habit older than thought, and went back down the stairs to sit with the forme in the dark.

* * *

Sitting in the dark beside a locked chase is a strange kind of prayer, if prayer is counting your dead.

He knew the edition by heart now — not the words, though he knew those too, but the architecture of it. Four pages, demy octavo, eight hundred words to a side. Page one: the statement of purpose, written in Rosalind's hand and set by his father, laying out in language plain enough for a man with a fourth-grade education exactly what the mill had paid for silence and how the payments were recorded. No rhetoric. Edith Marchmont had wanted to write it herself, and to her credit had accepted when Tomas told her, gently, that her name on the masthead would kill the paper before it drew breath. *This sheet has no editor but the record,* the statement began, and Tomas had set that line himself, letter by letter, and had liked the weight of it in his hands.

Page two: the accident ledger, copied from Constance Vane's second set of books, compared against the stolen page Rosalind had copied for Edith months ago, cross-checked against the coroner's sparse notices and against the memories of every widow who had agreed to talk. Nineteen names where the official record showed six accidents and no names at all. Nineteen. He had set each name in roman, full size, no abbreviations — *Jonas Harker, 1898* — and setting his own brother's name had been the only time in the whole job he'd had to stop and walk to the window and breathe until his eyes stopped burning. Jonas, who had gone into the carding room a strong man of twenty-six and come out on a plank carried by men who were told to say a belt slipped. Eleven years ago, and the type still shook a little under his fingers when he touched those letters, and he touched them now in the dark, finding them by memory: third column, upper left, where a brother belongs.

Page three: the testimonies. Five widows, five statements taken down by Rosalind in their own kitchens, each one ending the way Mrs. Wray had insisted hers end — *printed under my full name, Ellen Wray, née Cutler, of Bridge Street, and let them come and tell me to my face it didn't happen.* When Rosalind had read that closing line aloud to the shop, old Ambrose had taken off his cap, which was his way of applauding.

And page four: Constance Vane. Her copied figures. Her signed attestation, witnessed by Rosalind and by Pike — Pike, God help them all, the constable putting his mark to it, his career folded up and laid on the table like a coat he didn't expect to need again. *I, Constance Mary Vane, being the daughter of the owner of Marlow Bend Mills, do attest that the following figures were kept in my father's private office and differ from those filed with the borough...*

Tomas ran his thumb along the edge of the chase and thought about what happened at first light.

* * *

The plan had been argued to death and back, and in the end it was simple because simple plans survive bad nights. At first light, Marta would open the shop front as she did every Saturday, shutters back, lamp lit, the ordinary face of an ordinary business. The first hundred copies would go out folded inside the orders for job work already promised to customers — invoices, circulars, a funeral notice for the Petch family, God forgive them for using the dead man's mourning as a wrapper. From there, hand to hand. The choir loft at St. Anne's, where Mr. Denby would find forty copies tucked under the hymnals before morning service. The taproom of the Anchor, behind the bar, in a crate marked EMPTY BOTTLES. The schoolhouse. The ferry landing, weighted under a tarpaulin, so that when the bridge opened and the outside world came pouring in, the first thing the magistrate's clerks would hear on every side would be their own names read back to them.

And fifty copies, wrapped and addressed, ready for the moment the bridge reopened — going to newspapers in Ashford and London, to members of Parliament whose addresses Edith had supplied from her network, to the coroner, to the bishop, to the man himself, Josiah Vane, who would open his copy at his own breakfast table and find his daughter's signature looking back at him.

"You understand," Edith had said the night they finalized it, standing in the print shop with her hands clasped behind her back like a general reviewing troops, "that once this is out, there is no taking it back. Not with money, not with lawyers, not with running. Whatever comes next comes to all of you."

"That's rather the point, ma'am," Marta had said, and Edith had looked at her for a long moment and then laughed — actually laughed, a short surprised sound — and said, "Yes. I believe it is. Forgive me. I've spent thirty years asking frightened people to be brave and I'd forgotten what it sounds like when they simply are."

Tomas hadn't laughed. He'd been thinking about the press. About the sound it makes, the great wheeze and thump of the platen, audible — he was almost certain — from the street if the wind stood right. They would muffle it with blankets, they'd printed the last three issues that way, but this run was four hundred copies against their usual two hundred, and four hundred takes ninety minutes of continuous pulling even with everyone working, and ninety minutes is a long time for a town to pretend it doesn't hear a heartbeat coming from the Harker shop.

Let them hear it, Marta had said. Let them know somebody in this town still has a pulse.

* * *

The floorboard at the top of the stairs creaked, and Tomas didn't reach for the poker or the lamp or any of the things a careful man reaches for, because he knew the weight of that step. Rosalind came down in the dark the way she did everything, precisely, quietly, and stopped when she saw him sitting beside the forme.

"I heard you get up," she said. "I thought I'd better come count the spoons."

"Counting spoons?"

"Marta's phrase. For checking whether someone's gone strange with worry." She crossed the room and sat down on the low stool across the stone from him, pulling her shawl close. Up close, in the grey dark, she looked younger and older at once — younger in the face, older around the eyes, the way people look when they've been carrying something heavy in perfect silence. "Are you gone strange?"

"Ask me at first light."

She nodded and didn't fill the silence, which was one of the thousand reasons the shop had taken her in without a vote being held. Most people couldn't sit in a dark room with another person and not pour noise into the gap. Rosalind could. She'd sat with him in the hospital ward for hours saying nothing, just turning the pages of a book of poems she'd brought, and somehow the nothing had been the whole medicine.

After a while she reached out and laid two fingers on the chase, the way he had, feeling the letters stand proud.

"Nineteen names," she said softly.

"Nineteen."

"When I set the last one — Ada Petch — I thought about her little girl. She was at the inquiry gallery door on Thursday, standing in the cold, watching who went in." Rosalind withdrew her hand. "Tomas, if this goes wrong—"

"Don't." He said it more sharply than he meant to, and in the dark he heard her take it without flinching. "Sorry. But don't. We've done the arithmetic every way it can be done, and every sum comes out the same. If we stay quiet, the inquiry writes six accidents and moves along, and in ten years nobody can prove otherwise, because paper beats memory every time. That's the whole trick of people like Brack. They don't need to make you forget. They just need to control what gets written down." He put his good hand on the chase frame. "My father says a printer is the man who decides which version of yesterday survives. Well. This is our yesterday. It goes out tomorrow."

Rosalind was quiet a moment. Then: "Your father sounds like Edith when he talks like that."

"He'd hate hearing that."

"She'd hate hearing it too. They're very alike. That's why they circle each other like cats." She smiled faintly, and he heard rather than saw it. Then her voice changed, dropped into something more careful. "There's one thing I haven't said to anyone yet. I checked the street twice tonight before I came down. There was a man standing by the tannery wall — just standing, not walking anywhere, collar up. When I looked again he was gone. Could have been nothing. Men stand places."

"Could have been Brack's."

"Could have been. Or a drunk. Or nobody." She paused. "I'm telling you because you should know I'll be awake till first light anyway, and I'll be at the window."

Tomas looked at her — the shape of her in the darkness, this woman who had arrived in Marlow Bend as a chauffeur, a shadow behind Edith Marchmont's motorcar, a pair of hands on a wheel, and who now sat in his family's shop at three in the morning guarding a box of lead letters like a sentry at a vault. Somewhere in these weeks she had stopped observing the town and joined it, and none of them had marked the exact day, the way you never hear the exact moment ice becomes water.

"There's flour in the bin," he said, "and the statements are in it, and if anything happens to the rest of us, you know where everything is. The distribution list. Edith's London addresses. The spare chase."

"I know where everything is," she said quietly. "I've read everything there is to know about this shop. You knew that about me when you gave me the apron."

"We knew," Tomas said. "That's why we gave it to you."

* * *

At ten minutes to four, the household assembled in the lamplight without anyone calling them — his father first, cap already on, then Marta with her sleeves pinned back, then Edith Marchmont, who had insisted on being present and had been permitted on condition that she touch nothing, and finally Rosalind, coming down from the window with frost still melting on her shawl and a single sentence: "Street's empty. Let's wake the town."

Old Ambrose unlocked the press, squared the tympan, and looked around at all of them the way he'd looked at them the night Jonas died — measuring, memorizing, refusing to hurry. Then he took the first dampened sheet, laid it against the points, and pulled the bar.

The platen came down with its great familiar thump, muffled under blankets to a heartbeat, and the shop filled with the smell of ink and the sound of The Winter Count being born — one copy, then ten, then twenty, the pile growing on the stone slab like snow falling upward, like the past assembling itself into a weapon, like nineteen names getting ready to walk out of a locked box of lead and into four hundred pairs of hands.

On the eleventh pull, somewhere down Bridge Street, a dog began to bark. And beneath it, faint but unmistakable in the frozen air, came the sound of boots — several pairs, moving fast, coming toward the shop.
