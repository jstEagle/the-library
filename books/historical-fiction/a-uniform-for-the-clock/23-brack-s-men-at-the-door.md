## 23. Brack's Men at the Door

The lamp in the print shop burned low that night, the way Tomas liked it when he was setting type — a small yellow island of light in a sea of darkness, so that the metal gleamed and the letters seemed to rise up out of the trays like fish coming to the surface of a pond to be counted.

Rosalind sat at the composing stone with a stick of type in her hand and her tongue between her teeth, picking out letters by feel now more than by sight. She had been learning for three weeks, and her fingers had begun to develop opinions. The *e* tray wanted tidying; someone — she suspected young Alfie Harker — had been careless about returning his sorts. The *q* and the *p* lived too close together for comfort, as they always did, waiting for a tired hand to confuse them.

"Mind your spacing," Tomas said from across the room, not looking up. He could hear a bad line being set the way a musician can hear a wrong note through a wall.

"My spacing is improving."

"Your spacing is *surviving*. There's a difference." But he said it with the corner of his mouth turned, and he turned back to his own galley, and the shop settled again into its comfortable rhythm: the tick of metal on metal, the creak of the floorboards above where the Harker family slept, the wind worrying at the shutters the way it had all winter, patient and persistent, like a creditor.

Outside, Marlow Bend lay quiet under a sky the color of wet slate. The flood had receded enough that men spoke of the bridge again — three weeks, perhaps four — and Rosalind had noticed that everyone said those numbers in the same careful voice people used when speaking of a diagnosis. Three weeks to set every name. Four weeks until the magistrate arrived with his clerks and his leather folios and his tidy appetite for conclusions.

The forme stood locked in its chase on the imposing stone near the window: twenty-six columns of testimony, four pages of ledger figures copied in Constance Vane's precise hand, and at the bottom of the last page, set only that evening, the roll of names. Forty-one names. Rosalind had set eleven of them herself, letter by letter, and she knew she would never forget the weight of them — how a man's whole life could be held in two inches of type no taller than a thumbnail.

"It's good," Tomas said suddenly, meaning the forme, meaning everything. He took off his spectacles and rubbed his eyes with the heels of his hands, leaving smudges of ink at the temples like a priest's ash. "It's the best thing this shop has ever done. My father would have—" He stopped. He often stopped there. Two winters ago lived in that unfinished sentence, and Rosalind had learned not to reach across it.

"He'd have been proud," she said anyway.

"Mm." He set his spectacles back on. "Proud doesn't keep a press running. But yes."

Above them, a floorboard groaned — old Mrs. Harker turning over, or the house settling against the cold. The clock in the corner, a battered regulator that had once hung in the county courthouse and been sold off for scrap when it ran slow, showed half past eleven.

Rosalind would remember that afterward. Half past eleven, and the wind, and the smell of ink and cold iron, and Tomas humming something tuneless under his breath while he checked his quoins. She would remember it because it was the last ordinary minute, and because she had spent it thinking about *p* and *q*.

Then she heard the boots.

Not one pair — many, moving without any attempt at softness, which was itself a message. Men who meant to sneak wore wool socks over their shoes or came bareheaded and apologetic through the front door with a warrant in their fists. Men who meant to be heard came down the lane ringing like a church bell, announcing themselves to every household on the street so that no one would open their door, so that no one would see, so that everyone would have the decency to be asleep.

"Tomas." Her voice came out flat and strange, as if someone else were using her throat. "Tomas, listen."

He froze with his hand on the chase. For a long moment neither of them moved, and the boots grew louder, and beneath them now she could hear the low mutter of voices, and one voice above the others — broad, aggrieved, familiar from the mill yard — saying something she couldn't make out but whose shape she knew the way you know a dog's growl.

"The back," Tomas said. "Out the back, over the wall, Mrs. Petch's garden, the gate sticks but—"

"And you?"

"I'm the printer of record, aren't I?" His smile was terrible and bright. "Someone has to answer the door."

"They'll kill you."

"They'll break my fingers, more likely. It's the traditional courtesy." He was already pushing her toward the rear of the shop, past the paper racks, toward the door that opened onto the yard where the coal shed leaned against the wall like a drunk against a lamppost. "Rosalind. Listen to me. The statements — the widows' originals — where are they?"

"In my room. In the flour—"

"Go. Now. If they take me, they can't take what's written down. That's the whole arithmetic of this business, isn't it? That's why we're here." He gripped her shoulders once, hard, ink transferring from his fingers to her sleeves in five dark prints she would look at later and count like wounds. "Over the wall. Don't stop in the lane. Go to the church if you can't think of anywhere else — Pike drinks there Thursdays, and tonight feels like a Thursday to me."

The first blow landed on the front door before he finished speaking — not a knock but a shoulder, a great muffled boom that shook dust from the rafters. Then another. Then the sound of wood giving, splintering along its grain, and cold air rushed through the shop carrying lantern light and shouting and the smell of the river.

Tomas shoved her through the back door and slammed it behind her.

* * *

She went over the wall badly, catching her skirt on a nail and tearing it to the knee, landing in Mrs. Petch's frozen cabbages with a crunch that seemed loud as gunfire. From the other side of the brick came the full noise of the raid now: glass breaking — the front window, the big one that faced the street, gone in a single blow — and men's voices raised not in anger exactly but in *work*, the brisk cheerful voices of men doing a job they'd been paid to do and intended to do efficiently. That was the worst of it. Not rage. Industry.

She should have run. Every instinct, every lesson of her careful hidden life, said run — down the lane, away, survive, live to read another day. Her legs even started, carrying her three steps toward the gate before her mind caught up with her body and asked it a question it couldn't answer: *the flour bin.*

The statements were not in her room. She had moved them days ago, after the business with the burned paper stock, after Brack's retaliation made it clear he read their movements as well as they read his. She had carried the bundle — fourteen statements, each signed with a full name at the widow's own insistence, Annie Grell's hand shaking but unbroken at the bottom of hers — downstairs in the dark and hidden them in the kitchen flour bin, under half a sack of flour, because it was the one place in the Harker house no mill man would ever look. Mill men didn't bake. Mill men didn't cook. Mill men considered kitchens beneath their notice, the way they considered women and printers beneath theirs.

And she was standing in Mrs. Petch's cabbages realizing that the flour bin sat in the kitchen, and the kitchen opened onto the shop, and the shop was filling with men who smashed things.

She went back over the wall.

Later she would try to reconstruct the decision and find no reasoning in it at all — only a picture, sudden and complete, of Annie Grell's signature, the *G* looped like a bird taking flight, and the certainty that if that paper burned then the woman had testified for nothing and the forty-one names were only ink again, ink and grief and nothing anyone could hold. So she went back, and she did it quietly, and she hated herself a little even as she did it, because going back was not bravery, it was bookkeeping, and she had been a bookkeeper of other people's secrets for so long she no longer knew how to leave one behind.

The kitchen was dark and already cold, the back door standing open where she'd come through, letting the night pour in. Through the doorway to the shop, lantern light swung and juddered. She crossed the floor on the balls of her feet, lifted the lid of the flour bin, plunged both arms into the cold soft white to the elbow, and drew out the oilcloth bundle, flour sifting off it in a pale stream. She pressed it inside her coat, against her chest, where it lay like a second heartbeat.

Which was when the lantern swung around and found her.

* * *

There were six of them in the kitchen doorway, and she knew three by sight from the mill yard — big men, mill men, with the particular pallor and stoop that the spinning rooms gave a body — and the fourth was Brack himself.

Foreman Silas Brack stood with his arms folded and his head slightly tilted, watching her with the expression of a man examining goods he intends to buy cheap. He was not large. She had expected him larger, somehow; villains assembled in the mind out of their deeds tend to arrive oversized. He was instead a compact, grey-templed man with a foreman's neatness about him — clean collar, clean nails, a small scar through one eyebrow — and his eyes were the eyes of a man who had been passed over once, publicly, and had built a cathedral of grievance on that foundation and worshipped in it daily ever since.

"Well," he said. "The lady typist."

Rosalind said nothing. Her heart was going like a trip-hammer and she made herself breathe slowly, the way Edith had taught her years ago before her first public appearance: *they can hear fear in your breathing before they see it anywhere else.*

"You're the one drives the suffragist about," Brack said. "I've seen the motorcar. Fine animal, a motorcar. Eats money." He stepped into the kitchen, and the men parted for him with the ease of long habit. "You know what's funny? I've had my eye on this shop since autumn. Watched the comings and goings. And I kept saying to myself, Silas, says I, there's a girl goes in and out of there who walks like a servant and looks at things like an owner." He smiled. It did not improve his face. "Reading everything, are you? That's your trade, I hear. Reading other folk's letters."

From the shop beyond came a crash of wood — the type cases going over, a hundred pounds of lead and oak scattering across the floor in a sound like the end of an alphabet.

"Where's the forme?" Brack asked pleasantly.

"I don't know what you mean."

"No. Course you don't." He nodded to the biggest of the men. "Bring her through. Let her watch. Women learn best by watching — that's official doctrine where she comes from, I expect."

They walked her through into the ruin of the shop, her hand pressed flat over the oilcloth bundle beneath her coat, and the sight of it went through her like cold water through cloth.

The front window was gone, starlight and streetlight pouring in over the wreckage. The cases lay overturned, type everywhere, ten thousand letters strewn across the boards like dead insects, like snow that spelled nothing. The imposing stone had been tipped onto its face. One of the men was systematically working along the shelves with an iron bar, and the sound of each shelf letting go was deep and final, a note struck on some enormous instrument. The paper stock — the new stock, bought with Edith's pledged funds, a month's worth of white possibility — had been dragged into the middle of the floor and was soaking in a basin of ink water, grey and spreading.

And Tomas was on his knees beside the press.

They had already worked on him. She could see that in the angle of his head, the blood on his mouth, the way one arm hung wrong. But he was still upright, still kneeling rather than lying, and two men held his right arm stretched out over the bed of the press, flat, palm down, the way one holds a page to be read.

Brack crouched in front of him, almost tenderly.

"Now," Brack said, in the voice of a schoolmaster about to deliver a lesson he has given many times, "you're a craftsman, Mr. Harker, and I'm told craftsmen value their hands. So we'll do this the simple way. The forme comes out of the chase, goes into my bag, comes with us, and goes in the river. And your hands stay yours. Or—" he patted the press bed, twice, gently, like patting a horse's neck— "we take the forme anyway, and we take the loan of your fingers as payment for our trouble. Your choice. Democracy in action."

Tomas looked at Brack, and then — Rosalind saw it happen, saw his eyes travel deliberately across the ruined room and find her standing between two thugs with flour on her sleeves and a bundle of names against her heart — and she understood that he was checking. Not whether she was safe. Whether she was *still holding it.* Whether the arithmetic held.

His gaze came back to Brack, and he smiled through the blood, and Rosalind thought: *he learned that from me. That exact smile. The one that means I know something you don't.*

"Do your worst," Tomas said. "The edition's already been set twice. I can set it in my sleep. I can set it with nine fingers. I can set it with none, and dictate to her—" a jerk of his head toward Rosalind— "who reads and writes better than any man you've got. You've brought an iron bar to a fight against movable type, Mr. Brack. Movable. That's the whole point of it."

Brack straightened up. Whatever he had expected — begging, perhaps, or bargaining, the currencies he dealt in — he had not expected craft to be offered as a weapon, and for one visible moment he did not know where to put it.

"Break them," he said shortly, stepping back.

What followed, Rosalind made herself watch, because she believed — with a ferocity that surprised her — that someone ought to. The official record would never hold it. The magistrate's clerks would write *an altercation* and *minor injuries sustained*, and the town would say the printer fell down his own stairs, and so she stood in the wrecked shop with flour drying on her sleeves and watched every blow, and filed it, and numbered it, the way she had once numbered the contents of Edith's correspondence. Some part of her had always known that all her secret reading had been training for this: a memory built to carry what power preferred to drop.

They broke his right hand with the iron bar, on the press bed, because Brack had a sense of theater. They broke two fingers of the left for thoroughness. Tomas made sounds she would hear for the rest of her life, and he did not say one word, and when it was done and the men stepped back breathing hard, he knelt there cradling his ruined hands and said, quite clearly:

"Forty-one names. Can your bar spell, Brack?"

Brack's men took the forme — wrenched it out of the chase whole, quoins and all, and dropped it in a sack that clanked like a bell — and they took the ink rollers and the composing sticks, and one of them, younger than the rest, hesitated over the overturned case of twelve-point type and actually reached toward it, some printer's instinct stirring even in a wrecker, before Brack barked his name and he let it fall.

Then they were gone, out through the broken window and the broken door, boots ringing away down the lane, and the silence they left behind was total, absolute, a silence with weight and texture, pressing on the ears.

* * *

Rosalind moved.

Later she could not say what order she did things in. She got the door of the shop closed against the cold. She righted a chair. She knelt by Tomas and took his wrist with a care that made him laugh — a horrible, gasping laugh — and say, "They're hands, Joubert, not porcelain," and then stopped laughing because it hurt too much. She bound them with strips torn from a rag used for wiping rollers, tight and clumsy, and his blood came through the cloth almost at once, warm against her fingers, and she noted with the strange detached precision that had carried her through the whole of it that his blood smelled of ink, or the ink of blood, the two had gotten mixed somewhere in him years ago.

"Above," he managed. "Wake the house. Alfie — get Dr. Loomis, tell him — tell him a press fell on me. He'll understand. He's delivered half this street's babies, he won't ask questions."

She woke the Harkers, and the house rose around them in candlelight and coats thrown over nightclothes — old Mrs. Harker with her grey plait down her back, moving through the wreck of her son's trade without a word, setting bottles upright, righting what could be righted. Alfie, sixteen and furious, was sent out the back with instructions, and went over Mrs. Petch's wall in his stocking feet like a cat.

And through all of it, the bundle lay against Rosalind's chest, flour-caked and intact, fourteen statements, forty-one names, one signature looped like a bird taking flight.

When the shop had emptied of everyone but the two of them — Mrs. Harker gone up to boil water and fetch the good laudanum from the tin marked *CAMPHOR*, the candles guttering — Tomas sat slumped against the side of the wrecked press with his bandaged hands in his lap like something laid out for burial, and looked up at her.

"You went back over the wall," he said. "Alfie saw you from the garden. Why?"

Because she had read other people's letters for seven years and kept them all in her head and never once saved anything, she thought. Because she had finally been handed something worth keeping. Because a woman named Grell had insisted on her full name at the bottom of a page and Rosalind had promised, without promising aloud, that the name would matter.

"Someone had to count," she said.

Tomas closed his eyes. "The statements are safe?"

"Safe."

"Then we've lost nothing but the furniture." He tried to lift his right hand and failed, and looked down at it with an expression she had never seen on him — not despair, nothing so clean — a kind of appalled curiosity, a craftsman studying damage to his tools. "Well. She's dead, that old girl." He touched the press frame with his forehead, briefly, the way a man touches a coffin. "Thirty years she ran. My father bought her off a chapel that couldn't pay its debts. Printed wedding notices on her. Funeral notices. Everything honest and dull." He laughed again, softly, terribly. "And one true newspaper. One."

Footsteps in the lane — quick ones, and Rosalind was on her feet with a length of iron bar before the door darkened, before she registered the knock: two short, one long. The code.

Constable Aldous Pike stood in the ruined doorway with his lantern raised, and behind him the street lay empty and pretending to be asleep. He looked at the wreckage. He looked at Tomas. He looked at Rosalind, standing armed among the ruins with flour on her sleeves and ink on her cuffs and blood on her hands that wasn't hers, and whatever he saw in her face, he took off his hat.

"I was slow," he said. "I'll not pretend otherwise. Who?"

"Brack. Six men. They've the forme and they'll drown it in the cut before morning." Tomas's voice was fraying now, the shock arriving on its own schedule. "Aldous — write it down. Tonight, while it's fresh. All of it. Names."

Pike's jaw worked. "An official report takes time to—"

"Not the official report." Tomas's eyes were closing. "*Yours.* The one you keep in the drawer under the spare cartridges. The one where you've been writing the truth since October because you haven't the stomach for lies and not the courage for the truth either. Write this one in ink."

Pike stood very still. Then he put his hat back on, which in him was a kind of salute, and said to Rosalind, "Miss — the doctor will want help. And miss?" He hesitated at the threshold, an old man-shaped shadow against the broken window. "Whatever you're keeping safe in that coat of yours. Keep it somewhere new by morning. Brack counts heads in this town. He'll be counting flour next."

Then he was gone into the dark, and Rosalind stood alone in the wrecked print shop, holding a dead press's future against her heart, listening to Tomas breathe, and began — before the doctor came, before dawn, before anything — to plan where a bundle of names might sleep tomorrow.
