## 24. Broken Hands Still Count

The ceiling of the infirmary ward was painted the color of weak tea, and Tomas Harker had spent three days learning every water stain on it the way he had once learned a case of type — by rote, by repetition, until the thing became familiar enough to be endured.

His hands lay on top of the blanket like two strangers' hands. The left was splinted from wrist to knuckle, three fingers bound together with linen and pasteboard. The right had escaped with fewer breaks but more ruin: the knuckles of his first two fingers were swollen to the size of damsons, and the doctor had said — in that tone doctors used when they wanted to prepare you without frightening you — that he would need to be patient, that the small bones took longest, that a compositor's work required *delicacy*.

*Delicacy.* Tomas had almost laughed. It had come out as a cough instead, which hurt his ribs, which reminded him of the boot that had put them there.

He thought about the chase. That was the worst of it — not the ribs, not the hands, but thinking about the forme locked in its iron frame, all those lines of type he had set himself over eleven nights, every name spelled correctly because spelling the names correctly was the whole point of everything they did. Brack's men had carried it out of the shop like pallbearers with a stolen coffin. Somewhere it sat now, or would sit soon, in an office drawer or a stove, waiting to be melted down into silence.

Except.

Except that a press could smash type but not memory. Except that a man who had set the same edition twice already — once in draft, once clean — knew where every line began and ended. Except for Rosalind Joubert, who had walked into the ward that first morning carrying a candle stub, a pencil worn down to a nub, and an expression Tomas had come to know better than his own: the expression of someone who had decided something and would not be talked out of it.

"You're dictating," she had said, before he'd finished saying hello. "Today. Before the swelling makes your memory soft."

"It isn't soft."

"Everything goes soft if you wait long enough." She had pulled the chair up to the side of the bed with the particular scrape of wood on floorboards that meant business, and she had opened a cheap exercise book across her knees, and she had said, in the voice of a woman taking minutes at a meeting she intended to run herself, "Begin at the masthead. *The Winter Count*, number four. Go."

So he had begun.

* * *

The ward at St. Agatha's Cottage Hospital held eight beds, six of them occupied by men whose injuries the mill's ledger would describe, if it described them at all, as *sustained in the course of ordinary employment*. Two of those men were Harker cousins' neighbors; one had been in the shop the night Brack's men came through the door and had taken a chair leg across the shoulder for the trouble of trying to stand between them and the press. He slept most of the day now, breathing carefully around the bruise.

The matron, a narrow woman named Sister Ellery with forearms like a laundress and eyes like a customs officer, had made her position clear on the first evening: no visitors after eight, no visitors who raised their voices, and absolutely no *business* conducted in her ward.

"We're not conducting business," Rosalind had told her, with the smoothness Tomas recognized from watching her manage Edith Marchmont's correspondence. "We're reading to him. He can't hold a book."

Sister Ellery had looked at Rosalind's exercise book, at the pencil, at Tomas's splinted hands, and then back at Rosalind, and something had passed across her face — not belief, exactly, but a decision to spend her disbelief elsewhere. "Reading," she had repeated, flatly. "Mind the other patients." And she had gone back to her medicines, and that had been the end of the negotiation, because there are nurses in every town who understand perfectly well what is happening and choose, quietly and permanently, to see something else. Tomas had learned during the fire two winters ago that Sister Ellery was one of them.

So each afternoon, when the light through the tall windows went from white to amber, Rosalind came with her book and her pencil, and Tomas dictated, and the fourth number of *The Winter Count* assembled itself line by line in a hospital bed while its printer lay unable to hold so much as a spoon.

It was slower than typesetting. God, it was slower. A compositor works with his eye and his hands in partnership; the hand knows where the e lives in the case, knows the weight of a line by feel before it's ever locked up. Dictation was setting type through a wall — through Tomas's mouth, through Rosalind's ear and pencil, through whatever translation happened between the two. They got perhaps forty lines of final copy in a good hour, and every one of them had to be read back twice.

"The widow Mrs. Anselm," Rosalind read back on the second day, her voice low and even under the ward's ambient sounds of rattling trolleys and labored breathing. "*My husband Daniel worked the carding room eleven years. He came home on the Thursday of the flood week with both hands bandaged and was sent back Friday morning or lose the week's wage. He did not come home Friday night.* Full stop. Then: *The mill paid for the funeral and asked me to sign a paper. I signed it because I had four children. I am signing my name here because I have none left to protect.*"

Tomas lay still and listened to his own memory come back through another person's mouth. "Period after *protect*," he said. "And I want the paragraph break before *the mill paid*. It has to stand alone. It's the ugliest sentence in the whole edition and it deserves its own room."

Rosalind made the mark. She didn't flinch. She never flinched anymore — he'd watched that change happen over the winter, watched the woman who arrived in Marlow Bend reading other people's letters with careful, defended eyes become a woman who wrote *I have none left to protect* in a widow's voice without looking away. Something in him that wasn't broken at all turned over when he thought about it.

"There's a comma problem in the next line," she said. "*Sent back Friday morning or lose the week's wage* — do you want the *or* italicized?"

"No italics. The words are plain. That's the point of them."

She nodded and wrote, and the pencil moved with a new confidence he hadn't heard in it three days ago. Already she was hearing punctuation the way a setter hears it — feeling where the breath should fall.

"You'll be able to set this yourself within the month," he said, before he could decide not to say it.

"I'll be able to write it down. Setting is different."

"It's different for a fortnight. Then it's the same thing with your hands."

"My hands will be doing the driving," she said, dry, "and Edith Marchmont will want them back on a wheel the moment the bridge opens."

"The bridge is open."

"Yes." She looked down at the page. "Yes, it is."

Neither of them said anything else about the bridge. There would be time — there had to be time — for the conversation about what she did next, whether she went back to London and the motorcar and the letters she'd sworn off reading, or whether she stayed in Marlow Bend where warrants might still find her and a print shop stood smashed to matchwood. But not today. Today there were forty lines to get down before Sister Ellery's eight o'clock, and Tomas found that the work itself had become a kind of answer, or at least a place where the question could safely wait.

"Next section," he said. "The ledger pages. Start with the entry for November of the year before last."

* * *

On the fourth evening, Sister Ellery brought an oil lamp and set it on the bedside table with the air of a woman conceding territory inch by inch. "One lamp," she said. "Not the big one. And you—" she pointed the medicine glass at Tomas "—will sleep when I say you sleep."

"Yes, Sister."

"And you—" she turned to Rosalind, who had begun arranging her papers by some system invisible to everyone else in the world "—will stop pretending this is reading aloud. If you're going to lie in my ward, lie competently."

Rosalind, to her credit, only blinked once. "Yes, Sister."

Ellery left, and Tomas let out a breath that moved his ribs in the old bad way. "She knows."

"She's known since Tuesday. She's deciding not to know it louder than before." Rosalind trimmed the wick, and the flame stood up straight and threw their shadows huge against the tea-colored ceiling. "There's a whole town of people doing that right now. Deciding not to notice things loudly. It turns out it's a skill, like anything else."

In the lamplight her face had gone gold at the edges and shadow in the hollows, and Tomas thought — not for the first time, but with a new and unwelcome clarity — that he was in a great deal of trouble of a kind entirely unrelated to Brack's men.

"Where were we," she said.

"Ledger entries. We finished November."

"November is done." She turned the page. "Go on."

He closed his eyes and went back into the numbers. This was the part that frightened people, he knew — the part where the Winter Count stopped being stories and started being arithmetic. Names, dates, injuries described in the mill's own euphemisms beside the injuries as they actually were. *Struck by falling bale, bruising only* — beside a doctor's record of three cracked ribs and a punctured lung. *Caught in shafting, minor laceration* — beside a man who had come off the machines with two fewer fingers and been docked a half-day's wage for the interruption.

He gave her the figures and she took them down, and somewhere past the middle of the third entry he realized his voice had gone rough and stopped, and cleared his throat, and started again, and she let him do it without comment, which was its own kindness.

When the last figure of the section was down she read the whole run back, and the ward was so quiet — the other patients asleep or pretending, the lamp steady, frost beginning to fern the window glass outside — that the numbers seemed to hang in the air like smoke.

"That's the spine of it," Tomas said. "Everything else hangs on those pages."

"Then the spine's safe." She capped the pencil. "Two copies exist now, in two different handwritings, in two different buildings. Brack would have to burn half of Marlow Bend."

"He'd try, if he thought of it that way."

"He doesn't think in copies. He thinks in objects. He thinks smashing the press ends it." Her mouth curved, thin and fierce. "Men like that always think the machine is the thing."

"The machine *is* the thing," Tomas said. "Or it was." He looked down at his splinted left hand, at the stranger's fingers lying on the blanket. "I keep reaching for the case. In my sleep, mostly. Waking up with the hand curled around nothing. Eleven years I've set type, and my body hasn't learned yet that there's nothing to reach for."

Rosalind was quiet a moment. The lamp hissed faintly.

"Teach me," she said.

"You're learning. You've been learning since Tuesday—"

"Not the writing down. I mean properly. When the shop's rebuilt — and it will be rebuilt, your mother has already ordered timber and she frightens me more than Brack does — teach me the case. Where the letters live. How to justify a line. All of it."

"Why?"

It came out blunter than he intended, and he watched her take the question seriously rather than take offense, which was also new, also hers.

"Because someone has to know it," she said. "Because you taught me that the record doesn't keep itself — someone sets it, someone chooses the type size and where the names go and whether they're spelled right, and I spent years believing the record just *was*, like weather. It isn't. Someone makes it. I'd like to be someone who makes it." She paused, and the lamplight caught something guarded moving behind her eyes. "And because if they come again — and they may, before this is done — there should be two people in that shop who can rebuild the edition from memory. Not one man with breakable hands."

Tomas looked at her for a long moment.

"All right," he said. "But you learn it properly. No shortcuts. You start with distribution — putting the type away — because everyone wants to start with composing and composing is the easy part. Distribution teaches you where things live."

"Where things live," she repeated, as though filing the phrase somewhere permanent. "All right."

"And you'll hate it. Everyone hates distribution. My father made me do it for six months and I ran away twice."

"Where did you run to?"

"The flour warehouse. I'd sleep in the empty bins." He smiled despite the ribs. "You'd have liked it. Very quiet. Smelled honest."

Something crossed her face at the word *flour*, quick as a fish under ice — he didn't know then what it meant, wouldn't know until later, that she had hidden the widows' original statements in the Harkers' own flour bin while the shop was being wrecked above her head, that she had stood in the wreckage with paper against her heart and lied to Brack's face. Later she would tell him, and he would think of this moment and understand what the flicker had been. For now it passed, and she said only, "Then I'll expect the full curriculum. Six months of distribution and all."

"Six weeks, if you've got the ear. And you've got the ear."

"How would you know? You've heard me take dictation for four days."

"Because you hear where the commas go," he said. "Most people have to be taught that. You already listen in paragraphs."

She looked down at her book, and in the lamplight he saw her ears go pink, and found this so unexpectedly delightful that he forgot to be careful and laughed, which cost him three ribs' worth of pain and was worth every farthing.

* * *

Sister Ellery gave them until nine that night, an act of charity she disguised by spending twenty minutes noisily counting bandages at the far end of the ward. The lamp burned low. Rosalind read back the day's pages one last time, her voice dropping quieter and quieter as the ward settled, until she was barely above breath, and Tomas followed every word with his eyes closed, correcting nothing, letting the edition exist for a few minutes purely as sound.

"*...and I am signing my name here because I have none left to protect.* Mrs. E. Anselm, Carding Row. *...he was a good man and the mill's paper says otherwise.* Mr. J. Pike, testimony given the eleventh. *...they can smash the press. They cannot smash the count.*"

She stopped there. That was the end of the draft — the closing line, the one Tomas had set three times now, the line that had survived two smashed presses and one beating and would survive, God willing, this one too.

"They can't," he said quietly. "That's not bravado. That's arithmetic. There are four of us who know the edition entire — me, you, my mother, and young Alfie who helped distribute. Brack would need to be thorough indeed."

"Thorough men frighten me less than desperate ones."

"Desperate ones make mistakes."

"So do tired ones." She gathered her papers and squared their edges against her knee, and the little ritual gesture was so domestic, so much a part of the shape their evenings had taken, that Tomas felt the words rise in him before he had voted on them.

"Rosalind."

She looked up. The lamp had burned nearly to the wick's last inch, and her face was half gold and half dark.

"When the bridge opened," he said, "you stayed."

"I stayed."

"Edith went back to London on the early ferry. Constance Vane is testifying and her father's lawyers are circling. There is nothing keeping you in Marlow Bend except—" he stopped, and re-set the line, the way you re-set a line that won't justify. "Except what you choose."

"No," she agreed. "There isn't."

The ward clock ticked. Down the row, a patient murmured in his sleep. Tomas became aware that he was holding his breath and that holding his breath moved the ribs, and that he was going to say this anyway.

"I'm not good at this," he said. "I'm good at type. Type sits still and does what it's told and means exactly what it says, and I've spent my whole life liking it for those reasons. People are — people justify badly." His splinted hand twitched on the blanket, reaching for a case that wasn't there. "But I want to say it plainly, because plainness is the whole trade, isn't it. Say the thing. Spell the name right. Don't hide it in the small print."

"Tomas—"

"I'm in love with you. Have been since you corrected my typo in February and pretended you hadn't. I'm telling you now because tomorrow the inquiry starts, and Brack still has men, and I have two hands that don't work, and if something happens to either of us before this edition is printed I refuse to be a man who died with the type still in the stick." He breathed carefully. "That's all. You don't have to say anything back. I'm not setting this line to be answered. I'm setting it because it's true, and truth belongs on the record."

The lamp guttered, steadied, guttered again. Rosalind sat very still. He watched her the way he watched a line of type he wasn't sure of — patiently, without pressing, giving it room to settle.

Then she reached across and turned his splinted hand over, gently, palm up, and laid her own open hand beside it on the blanket — not clasping, not yet, just adjacent, the way two columns of type stand side by side before the quoins tighten.

"Read it how you like," she said, and her voice was low and completely steady. "I stayed when the bridge opened. I'm staying now. I hid the widows' statements in your family's flour bin with Brack's boot on the step behind me, and the reason I wasn't afraid was that I kept thinking — the shop smells like Tomas, the shop sounds like Tomas, if I'm standing in the shop then I'm standing where he stands, and that's safer than anywhere." Her thumb moved, very slightly, against the edge of his bandage. "So. Read it how you like."

Down the ward, Sister Ellery blew her nose with the delicacy of a woman who had heard nothing whatsoever and intended to continue hearing nothing, and began pushing her trolley toward them with the unmistakable music of visiting hours ending.

Rosalind withdrew her hand and stood, gathering her papers, and by the time the matron's shadow fell across the foot of the bed she was once again entirely the composed Miss Joubert, correspondence secretary, driver of motorcars, keeper of other people's secrets — except that Tomas could see, in the lamplight's last red coin of flame, that she was smiling, and that the smile was not composed at all.

"Eight o'clock tomorrow," she said. "We finish the widows' statements, and then I want the layout. Column widths, rules, the lot. If I'm to learn the trade, I'll learn it whole."

"If you're to learn the trade," Tomas said, settling back against the pillows with his heart hammering in a way that had nothing to do with his ribs, "you'll need an apron. Come tomorrow, and mind you bring chalk — there's a slate board in the corner nobody uses, and I'm going to teach you the case map from memory, letter by letter."

"A case map. From a slate. In a hospital ward."

"Every compositor learns it somewhere. Mine was a flour warehouse." He was grinning now, and it pulled at his split lip and he didn't care. "Yours can be St. Agatha's. Put it in the paper someday. *Learned at the bedside.*"

She paused at the end of the bed, papers hugged to her chest, and looked back at him down the length of the ward — at the splinted hand and the swollen knuckles and the man attached to them who had just, in the plainest type he owned, set his heart on the record.

"Tomorrow, then," she said. "Bring your memory. I'll bring the candle."

And she went, out past Sister Ellery's trolley and through the double doors into the cold blue dark, leaving Tomas alone with the tea-colored ceiling, the dying lamp, and the extraordinary discovery that a man with two broken hands could still feel as though every finger of them were resting, lightly and precisely, on the keys.
