## 25. Edith Chooses Sides

Edith Marchmont had spent forty years learning to count.

It was the first lesson the movement had taught her, though no one had ever said it aloud: that a cause was an act of arithmetic. That every speech, every arrest, every hunger-strike in Holloway was a figure in a column, and the column must always sum toward the larger number — the franchise, the future, the thousand years of women yet unborn who would inherit what she suffered to win them. She had counted when her husband left her, counting the cost of her name against the value of her voice. She had counted at every by-election, weighing one lost seat against ten gained converts. She had counted the women broken on prison feedings and called it investment, because the alternative was to call it waste, and waste was unbearable, and so it became arithmetic instead.

She had been counting since the night the bridge went out, standing at Rosalind's shoulder as the brown water took the span, thinking not *we are trapped* but *a captive audience is still an audience* — turning even catastrophe into capital before the floodwater finished rising.

She was still counting on the morning of the raid. She did not stop until she stood in the wreckage of the Harkers' print shop, and then the numbers simply refused to come.

* * *

She heard it before she understood it — that was the strange thing about violence, Edith thought afterward; it announced itself in fragments, in sounds without names. She had been upstairs in the Harker house, dressed, half-dressed rather, still pinning her collar, when the noise came up through the floorboards: a splintering crash like a cartload of crockery dropped from a height, then shouting, then a sound she knew from Wembley and Westminster and a hundred police lines — boots, many boots, moving with purpose and impunity.

By the time she reached the stairs, Rosalind was already there, white-faced, holding the door frame with both hands as though the house itself might tip.

"They've gone down the lane," Rosalind said. "Tomas is hurt. Mrs. Harker's sent for Dr. Llewellyn."

Edith took the stairs too fast for a woman of fifty-eight and did not care who saw her do it.

The shop below looked as if the river had come indoors. The press — the old Albion, black iron, older than Tomas himself, polished each Saturday morning with a devotion Edith had privately found quaint — lay with its iron cheek broken open, its bed cracked clean across. Type was everywhere. That was the detail that struck her first and held her longest: the type, thousands of little leaden letters scattered across the floorboards like dead insects, weeks of patient composition flung into chaos by a single sweep of a man's boot. A forme of the new edition had stood locked in the chase; it was gone entirely, pried out and carried off, and where it had sat there remained only a rectangle of cleaner wood, pale as a scar.

Paper stock — reams of it, bought dear over months — had been fed into the stove and burned only half through, so that the room smelled of scorched rag and ink and something else, something animal, that Edith identified after a moment as blood. Tomas was propped against the counter with Mrs. Harker kneeling over him and his right hand cradled against his chest, the fingers already swelling wrong, and he was looking not at his ruined hand but at his ruined press, and he made no sound at all.

Edith had seen men weep. She had never seen a man refuse to, quite like this.

"Tomas," she said.

He tried to stand. Mrs. Harker pushed him down with the authority of a woman who had buried one son and would not be lectured by the fates regarding the other. "Don't you dare," she said, to both of them equally.

"Brack's men?" Edith asked.

"And two constables to hold the door while they worked," Rosalind said. Her voice was level in the way Edith had long ago learned to read as anything but. "Pike wasn't among them. Draw your own conclusions."

Edith drew none. For once in her life she drew nothing at all. She stood in the middle of the destroyed shop, in her half-pinned collar, and looked at the arithmetic she had been doing all winter — the widows' statements filed away for London, the evidence held back until the national moment was ripe, Marlow Bend as a staging ground, a depot, a means — and she watched the sum collapse under its own weight, the way a bad ledger collapses, all at once and irrecoverably.

The movement's arithmetic said: *wait.* Wait for the inquiry, wait for the ferry, wait for the moment of maximum leverage. The movement's arithmetic said: *one town, however grievous, against a hundred years.* The movement's arithmetic had served her faithfully through two imprisonments and the death of three friendships and the whole slow erosion of her private life, and it had never once asked her to look at a man's hand.

But there was the hand. Swollen, misshapen, held like something borrowed. And there were the letters of the alphabet scattered on the floor around him — she could see an *e*, a small *o*, a capital *W* near the leg of the counter — and it occurred to Edith Marchmont, with the force of revelation, that she had spent forty years arguing that women were persons before they were instruments, and had somehow permitted herself to treat this whole town as an instrument, and that the hypocrisy was so total, so architectural, that she could not see how she had lived inside it.

"I am going to say something," she announced, to the room generally, "and I want it witnessed."

Rosalind turned. Mrs. Harker looked up from her husband's hand. Even Tomas lifted his head.

Edith straightened her collar herself, the last unfinished pin be damned. It was, she reflected later, the most honest gesture of her adult life: dressing herself, for once, without assistance, without ceremony, while everything she owned of consequence stood ruined around her.

"The funds I hold in trust for the spring campaign," she said, "will come to Marlow Bend. Not a portion of them. All of them." She heard Rosalind's breath catch and pressed on before courage could reconsider. "The lawyers who represented us in Holloway — Fenwick and the young one, Ashworth — will be on the first train, or the first boat across the flooded fields if trains won't run. Whatever it costs. Whatever it costs, do you understand me? I have spent this winter calculating, and my calculation has produced—" she gestured at the broken press, the burned paper, the hand— "*this*. A smashed press and a beaten printer and an edition stolen out of a locked chase, and every hour I waited, I called prudence."

"You weren't wrong to wait," Rosalind said quietly. "There's an argument—"

"There is always an argument," Edith said. "That is precisely the disease. There is an argument for everything, and the arguments are how cowards dress." She stopped, hearing her own cruelty, and amended: "How *I* have dressed. Do not mistake me, Rosalind. I am not renouncing strategy. Strategy is how the weak fight the strong. But there is a difference between strategy and surrender dressed in strategy's clothes, and I crossed that line somewhere around Christmas, and it has taken an iron press breaking in half to show me the crack."

Mrs. Harker said, into the silence, "You'll be arrested."

"Almost certainly."

"They'll take the money's trail back to London. To the movement. They'll use it to paint every suffragist in England as an agitator of mill towns."

"Yes," Edith said. "They will try. Let them." She crouched — knees cracking, dignity abandoned — beside Tomas, so that their eyes were level. His face was grey with pain and something worse than pain, and she spoke to him as she had spoken to precious few people in forty years: without rhetoric.

"I cannot give you back your press," she said. "And I cannot give you back your brother, though God knows the ledger I kept this winter owed him a debt I never entered. But I can tell you that you will not face Brack, or the inquiry, or the magistrate's tidy record, alone, and unfunded, and unnamed. Your town's dead are going into the record, Tomas Harker. With names. Spelled correctly. I will spend the last shilling of my credibility and the last pound of the movement's treasury to make it so, and if they arrest me for it, I shall make my trial a second edition."

Tomas looked at her for a long moment. Then, wincing, he extended his uninjured left hand, and she took it, and neither of them said anything further, because nothing further was required.

* * *

Dr. Llewellyn pronounced it a fracture of two metacarpals and a dislocation, set it badly in the kitchen because the surgery light was poor, and gave Tomas laudanum that he refused more than half of, saying he needed his head clear. Clear for what, he didn't say, and nobody asked, because everybody knew: the edition existed now only in his memory, letter by letter, page by page, and a man's memory was the one press Brack could not smash.

Edith sat with him while the others saw the doctor out. The kitchen smelled of coal smoke and set bone. Outside, the flood had begun its long retreat, leaving mud-lines on the walls like the rings of some enormous tree marking terrible years.

"When I was in Holloway," Edith said, "the second time, they moved me to a cell with a window facing the wall. Six feet of brick. The authorities considered it humane, because the cell next door had a view of the yard." She folded her hands on the table. "I learned something in front of that wall. I learned that a person can endure almost anything except being made useless to the people she loves. Hunger, force-feeding, the loss of reputation — these are prices. Uselessness is a different currency altogether. It buys despair outright."

Tomas said, carefully, "You're telling me you understand."

"I'm telling you I intend to be useful," Edith said. "Which is rarer."

He managed something that was nearly a smile. "Your arithmetic's changed, then."

"My arithmetic has resigned," Edith said. "I'm keeping accounts by hand from now on. Slower. More mistakes visible." She rose, and paused at the door. "Rest the hand. Dictate nothing tonight. Tomorrow we begin again — and this time, Mr. Harker, we print everything."

* * *

That evening she found Rosalind in the yard behind the house, standing at the woodpile in the blue dusk, doing nothing, which for Rosalind was the equivalent of another person's weeping. Edith came to stand beside her. For a while, neither spoke. The river muttered below the bank, lower now, obedient again, as though it had not eaten a bridge.

"You'll lose London over this," Rosalind said at last. "The executive committee. Half of them think provincial agitation dilutes the national message. You told me that yourself, in October, in exactly those words."

"I remember." Edith watched a bat make its first erratic circuit of the chimney. "I told you a great many things in October, most of which I said in the voice of a woman who believed she was wise. Wisdom, I find, is largely the art of being right about other people's lives." She was quiet a moment. "You overheard me, in January. In the parlor. Don't trouble to deny it — you have never in your life successfully denied anything to anyone who reads correspondence for a living. You heard me weigh this town against the cause and find the town lighter."

"I heard you doing sums," Rosalind said. "Everyone does sums."

"Not everyone arrives at the wrong answer and calls it principle." Edith turned to face her. In the last light Rosalind's face was unreadable, as always, but her hands were not — they were clasped tight, knuckles pale, and Edith thought: *there is a woman who has spent her whole life making her hands lie still.* "I owe you an apology, Rosalind, though I suspect you'll find receiving one harder than I find giving it."

"For what?"

"For letting you believe I was the fixed point. Mentors enjoy being mistaken for lodestars; it spares us the indignity of being human. I taught you discipline and I withheld from you doubt, and doubt was the better lesson. If you have spent this winter wondering whether loyalty to me and loyalty to the truth can coexist—" she exhaled— "then let me settle it now, publicly, while the mud is still on my boots: they can, but only when the truth wins any argument between them. Choose the truth. Even when the truth is inconvenient to me. *Especially* then."

Rosalind was silent long enough that Edith began, uncomfortably, to compose retreats. Then she said, "The widow's statements. From the raid night. They're safe. I hid them."

Edith stared. "Where?"

"Somewhere Brack's men wouldn't think to look, and wouldn't dare search if they did." A ghost of Rosalind's rarest expression — not a smile, but its foundation stone. "Every name survives. Every testimony. He stole our type, Edith. He didn't steal our words."

For the second time that day, Edith Marchmont felt something in her chest unclench that had been clenched since the bridge went out — since long before, if she were honest, since the first letter she had ever asked Rosalind to carry without asking what it contained. She put her arm through her driver's arm, an intimacy neither of them had permitted themselves in nine years of shared roads, and they stood together in the darkening yard like two conspirators against the coming century.

"Then here is what happens tomorrow," Edith said. "Fenwick wires back by noon. The money moves by Friday. And when the magistrate convenes his tidy inquiry and begins sanding the truth smooth for the official record — as he will, they always do — we will discover whether a smashed press prints louder than a whole one."

Below the bank, the river ran on, carrying the winter away toward the sea. Somewhere across it, ferries were being readied, repairs running ahead of schedule, officials arriving — and Edith, who had spent her life reading situations the way her driver read letters, felt the shape of the coming days pressing against her like text against wet paper.

The inquiry would open within the week. And someone, she suspected — watching Rosalind's profile go sharp and resolute against the last grey light — was already deciding to speak.
