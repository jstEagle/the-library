## 26. The Inquiry Opens

The magistrate arrived on a Tuesday, on the first ferry to run since the thaw, and he arrived the way weather arrives in Marlow Bend — announced for days, inevitable, and changing nothing that mattered.

Rosalind watched from the window of the print shop as Mr. Justice Harrow's party came up from the landing: four men in good coats, two clerks with leather satchels, a stenographer carrying a typewriter in a wicker case as carefully as if it were an infant. Behind them came a photographer, which was new. The inquiry intended to be seen to happen. That was the whole of its architecture.

"They'll take the courtroom," Tomas said at her shoulder. His hands were still wrapped — Brack's men had seen to that — but his voice had recovered its dry steadiness. "Judge's chambers have been aired since Saturday. Mrs. Ellery scrubbed the bench herself."

"Does she know what it's for?"

"Everyone knows what it's for." He turned from the glass. "That's not the same as knowing what it will do."

Below them, Marlow Bend had put on its company manners. Bunting over the draper's. A boy sweeping the cobbles outside the Crown & Anchor where the magistrate would lodge. The town looked like a woman tidying before a visit from relatives who had never once brought anything but judgment. And underneath the tidying, Rosalind could feel it — the same held breath that had lived in this town since the floods went down, tighter now, because spring had come and the reckoning with it, and nobody believed yet that the reckoning would be honest.

She didn't believe it either. But she was about to spend three days watching precisely how dishonesty was manufactured, and she meant to learn every step of the process.

* * *

The courtroom filled by half past nine: mill families in their church clothes, foremen in a block near the front like a phalanx, Constance Vane seated alone one row behind her father — a placement so deliberate it might have been choreography. Edith Marchmont sat near the aisle in dove grey, hands folded, every inch the visiting lady of consequence. She did not look at Rosalind. She had not looked at Rosalind directly since the night of the smashed press, when she had stood in the wrecked shop among the scattered type and said only, *We will need lawyers,* as if the sentence were a promise and a penance both.

Mr. Justice Harrow was a small man with a large manner. He took the bench, arranged his papers twice, and opened proceedings with a statement that Rosalind copied into her notebook word for word because it deserved preserving:

"This inquiry convenes to establish the facts concerning labor conditions at Vane Mills over the preceding eighteen months, with particular reference to accidents occurring upon the premises, and to determine whether any irregularity in the record thereof warrants further action."

*Irregularity in the record.* Not deaths. Not lies. Irregularity — as though the ledger had developed a tremor, a handwriting problem, something a firm hand and a fresh page might set right.

The first witness was the mill's timekeeper, a soft man named Prosser who answered every question with the serenity of a man reciting times tables. Yes, there had been accidents. Yes, they were recorded. No, he could not speak to circumstances; he recorded hours, not causes. When counsel for the inquiry asked whether the accident of November the fourth — the one that had taken Daniel Harker's arm below the elbow — had been logged, Prosser produced the page without being asked, smooth as a card trick, and the clerk read aloud: *Employee injured. Machine fault. Corrective measures undertaken.*

*Machine fault.* Rosalind wrote it down and underlined it twice. She had read Daniel Harker's own account of that morning, taken down in this very room above this very shop while his brother sat listening with his wrapped hands flat on the table. There had been no machine fault. There had been a guard removed three weeks earlier to speed the line, and an order — verbal, of course — not to speak of it.

But the guard removal existed nowhere except in testimony, and testimony, Rosalind was beginning to understand, was a material like wet clay. It held whatever shape the strongest hands pressed into it.

By noon the pattern was established and running like a loom. Witnesses for the mill testified to safety measures, benevolence, the generosity of the Christmas coal allowance. Witnesses for the families were handled gently, almost tenderly — *we understand your loss, madam, no one doubts your grief* — and then sanded. Had the deceased been drinking? Was he known to disregard instructions? Had he perhaps been feeling unwell that day, working despite illness, poor fellow? Each question left a small groove, and the grooves ran all one direction, and by afternoon the shape emerging from the clay was of a modern, humane works beset by unlucky men.

Widow Cottle came last that first day, and Rosalind watched her go into the box with her black gloves clenched in her lap, and watched the sanding begin.

"You say your husband complained of dizziness prior to the incident," counsel said.

"He said the fumes were bad in the dye room. Everyone said so."

"But he continued to work."

"We all continue to work. That's what wages are."

A ripple through the gallery — the first honest sound the room had made all day. The magistrate's gavel came down like a lid.

Rosalind sat with her pencil motionless against the page. In her bag, folded inside a copy of *The Times* that she carried for exactly this purpose, were eleven statements. Eleven widows and witnesses, names and dates and causes, sworn before Pike — Pike, who had signed each one with a face like a man signing his own death warrant, and who had then filed his official report that morning, and whose report Rosalind had not yet read but could already write in her sleep.

The paper had all of it. *The Winter Count* had all of it — the corrected edition, the one Tomas had dictated from a hospital bed with his broken hand propped on a pillow, the one Rosalind had typeset by candlelight learning the case letter by letter until her fingers knew the way home without her eyes. Two hundred copies had gone out into pews and pubs on Sunday. People had stood in the gallery today wearing black armbands with white numbers pinned to them, and the numbers were the winter count, and the magistrate had already ruled that "display of partisan materials within the court" would result in expulsion.

The paper had everything. And watching Widow Cottle's answers get trimmed and planed into planks for someone else's building, Rosalind understood, with a coldness that started behind her sternum and spread outward, that it was not enough.

Paper could be seized. Paper could be dismissed as scurrilous, unsworn, the work of agitators. Paper could burn — she had seen the ash of the Harkers' stock still darkening the yard behind the shop. What paper could not do was look the magistrate in the eye and refuse to be sanded.

Someone had to stand in that box and say the names, the dates, the causes, out loud, on the record, under oath, where no clerk could file it into a drawer and no counsel could plane it smooth without the whole room hearing the grain resist.

She looked across the courtroom and found Edith looking back at her, and for a moment neither of them blinked, and Rosalind thought: *she knows what I'm thinking.* Edith Marchmont had spent thirty years making women speak in public. She knew better than anyone alive what it cost and what it bought.

Edith's gaze slid away toward the window. It was the most eloquent thing she had done all day.

* * *

They walked home along the river road because none of them could bear the crowded high street. The water was down to its banks again, brown and purposeful, carrying sticks southward with the indifference of a machine that has finished one job and awaits the next. The bridge rose ahead of them, scaffolded, half-healed, its new timbers pale as scar tissue against the old black stone.

"It will reopen fully next week," Edith said. "The engineer told me. They've worked miracles."

"Mm."

"The London papers are asking whether I've been detained against my will. There's a paragraph forming about my courage in remaining."

"There is," Rosalind said. "I've drafted three of them myself."

Edith stopped walking. The river made its noise. For a long moment neither spoke, and then Edith said, quietly, "You despise me again."

"I don't despise you. I've stopped pretending I understand you, which is more restful." Rosalind kept walking. "You stayed because a captive audience is still an audience. You're staying now because martyrs photograph well. And meanwhile there are eleven statements in my bag and a courtroom full of people watching grief get filed under 'irregularity,' and you have said nothing. You, who can hold five hundred strangers in the palm of your voice."

"I have said nothing," Edith agreed, "because the moment I speak, this becomes *her* inquiry. The famous suffragist, the movement, the cause. Every column inch goes to me. Every question becomes *why is she here* instead of *what happened here.* I have spent my life learning the difference between a voice and a noise, Rosalind, and right now mine is a noise."

"That's arithmetic," Rosalind said. "I've heard your arithmetic before."

"Yes," Edith said. "And you overheard it once to your cost, and I have regretted that you heard it ever since — not because it was false, but because it was incomplete." She turned to face the river. "Here is the rest of it. If I testify, they will destroy me, and they will enjoy doing it, and the town will watch a stranger be destroyed on its behalf and feel relieved that it wasn't one of their own. Relief is the most dangerous thing a jury can feel. But if someone from *inside* stands up — someone whose name is already on the mill's books, whose husband or son or father is already in the churchyard — then the sanding stops. Because you cannot call a widow a liar about her own dead. Not even Harrow. Not even in this room."

Rosalind's bag felt suddenly very heavy against her hip. Eleven statements. Eleven women who had given their words to a girl who used to read other people's letters for a living.

"Widow Cottle gave hers under her full name," Rosalind said. "She insisted. Before the press was smashed, she stood in this kitchen and said, *print all of it, I buried him, they don't get to bury the reason too.*"

"And if called, she'll repeat it."

"She shouldn't have to be called. She should volunteer." Rosalind heard her own voice change, drop into something harder than she recognized. "But she won't, unless someone shows her the door is open. Unless someone walks through it first."

Edith looked at her for a long moment. The river ran. Somewhere behind them the bridge scaffolding clanked in the wind, patient as a clock.

"You are about to do something irreversible," Edith said.

"I've been doing irreversible things since October. I steamed open my first envelope in 1911." She adjusted the strap of her bag. "The difference is that now everyone will know."

* * *

The print shop that evening smelled of ink and lamp oil and the ghost of smoke. The borrowed press from Netherfield stood in the middle of the floor like a guest of honor, and around it the Harkers' surviving type lay in cases that Tomas had cleaned himself, one-handed, refusing help, because — he said — a compositor's case was his own business and no one else's, broken hands notwithstanding.

Rosalind found him setting a correction — a widow's name misspelled in the reprint, *Cottrell* for *Cottle*, and he had caught it himself, of course, he caught everything — and she stood in the doorway a moment watching him work before she could make herself speak.

"Tomas."

He glanced up. Whatever was in her face made him set the stick down and give her his whole attention, which from Tomas Harker was a ceremony.

"They've posted the witness list for tomorrow. Pike showed me." She laid the copied list on the case. "Prosser. Two foremen. The company doctor. Then recess for Harrow's dinner." She swallowed. "No families. Not one. He's going to close the record tomorrow afternoon and call it thorough."

Tomas read the list the way he read type — top to bottom, left to right, missing nothing. Then he looked up.

"You're going to ask to be heard."

"I'm going to stand up tomorrow and state my name and ask to give evidence, and when Harrow says the list is closed, I'm going to say that I hold sworn statements from eleven residents of this town and that an inquiry which closes its ears to them isn't an inquiry, it's a eulogy." Her heart was going hard enough that she could hear it. "They can expel me. They can charge me with contempt. But the stenographer will have to type what I say, and the photographers will have to photograph it, and the gallery will hear every name before they drag me out. On the record, Tomas. Under my own name. That's the only thing the paper can't do alone."

His jaw worked. He looked down at his bandaged hand, flexing fingers that wouldn't quite close.

"When they beat us," he said finally, "you hid the widow's statements in the flour bin. I asked you afterward why you didn't run with them. You said—" he closed his eyes briefly— "you said, *paper survives by staying.*"

"I remember."

"Well." He picked his composing stick back up, and his mouth did the thing it did instead of smiling. "Then stay. And when they throw you out — and they will throw you out — walk past the press on your way to the door. Slowly."

"Why?"

"Because tomorrow night we print whatever you manage to say, and I want you to see how much type is standing ready." He nodded toward the far wall, where the cases waited, locked in their frames, letters lying in their beds like soldiers. "Two hundred and forty ems of it, set and justified, waiting on one line."

"What line?"

"The one where somebody says it out loud," Tomas said. "We've had it ready for three weeks."

Outside, the river ran south, and the bridge stood half-mended in the dusk, and somewhere across town a magistrate was dining on the town's best mutton, believing his record was already written.
