## 28. Constance on the Stand

The witness box in the Marlow Bend assizes room had been built for a man's hands. Constance Vane noticed this before anything else — the rail too high for her forearms, the ledge beneath it angled so that any paper she laid there slid slowly toward her lap as though even the furniture were trying to make her drop something. She had spent eleven years at her father's desk adjusting chairs that did not fit her, ledgers ruled for taller eyes, a telephone cradle set at a height that required her to stand on her toes to reach it. She was practiced at making ill-made things serve.

She wore grey. Not mourning grey and not the dove-grey of a woman hoping to be pitied, but the flat, unremarkable grey of an invoice — the colour of a thing that exists only to be read. She had chosen it at half past four that morning, standing before her mirror with three dresses across the bed behind her, and she had understood, with the cold clarity that had governed her whole life, that whatever she wore would be used against her, so it might as well be nothing at all.

The room smelled of wet wool and pipe smoke and, faintly, of the river, which had been receding for weeks but still left its signature in the plaster. The gallery was full. She had not expected that. She had expected the tidy half-room of a routine inquiry: the magistrate, the clerk, a scattering of mill men. Instead every bench pressed against the next, and faces she knew from pew and market stood shoulder to shoulder with strangers — London men with notebooks, a woman in green near the back whom Constance recognised from the newspapers as one of Mrs. Marchmont's lieutenants, and, in the third row, Rosalind Joubert, sitting very straight, gloved hands folded, watching her with those unreadable eyes that Constance had decided, sometime during their second conversation, were the most honest pair of eyes in Marlow Bend precisely because they worked so hard to be dishonest about everything else.

Her father sat at the long table below the bench with his lawyer, Mr. Callum-Sedge, a man built like a bank front — broad, columned, granite-faced, with small quick hands that never stopped moving, shuffling papers he had already read. Ambrose Vane did not look at his daughter. He looked at the wall above the magistrate's head with the expression of a man enduring weather. That, too, was familiar. She had been weather to him since the day she was born: present, occasionally inconvenient, best planned around.

"Constance Mary Vane," said the clerk, and the formality of her full name went through her like a bell through water.

She took the oath. Her voice held. She had rehearsed nothing except the holding.

* * *

Mr. Pettifer, counsel assisting the inquiry, began gently, as a man tests ice. He established who she was, where she sat, what she had charge of. Eleven years in her father's office. Correspondence, payroll oversight, the charity accounts, the workers' benevolent fund — "which you administered personally?"

"Entirely," she said. "My father signed what I put before him."

"And you kept the books."

"I kept *a* set of books." She let the article do its work. In the gallery someone shifted; a notebook opened somewhere with a sound like a moth unfolding.

Pettifer allowed the pause to lengthen until the room understood it, then asked for the books themselves. They had been surrendered two days earlier under warrant — Constance had made certain they would be found rather than handed over, because found things carried the smell of discovery, and discovery was the one perfume this inquiry could not resist. The clerk passed the ledger up. The magistrate turned pages whose columns Constance could have recited in the dark: dates in her own hand, figures in her own hand, and beside them, in red, the entries that had no business existing anywhere — payments entered twice under different headings, safety inspections logged as completed on dates when the inspection party had been documented elsewhere, and running down the margins in her smallest, most controlled script, the reconciliation notes. *Paid to Brack, per verbal instruction. No voucher. Injury claim settled privately — see loose schedule C.*

"Can you tell His Worship what this book represents?"

"It represents what the mill actually paid," Constance said, "as distinct from what the mill reported paying."

"And why does such a distinction exist?"

"Because a company keeps two truths the way a household keeps two clocks," she said. "One for the family and one for callers. I have simply copied down what the second clock says when no one is calling."

A murmur ran the length of the gallery and was suppressed. On the table below, Callum-Sedge wrote one word and underlined it twice. Constance did not need to see it. It would be *character*. They always began there.

* * *

They began there within the hour.

Callum-Sedge rose with the unhurried menace of a man who had never once needed to raise his voice, thanked her warmly for her service to the firm — the warmth itself an insult, delivered like a hand on the back steering her somewhere — and then asked whether she had ever been treated unfairly by her father.

Constance had prepared for forty questions. This was not among them, and its obliqueness told her everything about how the morning would go.

"I don't understand the question."

"You are thirty-four years old, Miss Vane, unmarried, still living under your father's roof, still drawing your salary from his firm. A capable woman, by all accounts — indeed you tell us yourself you run his office. And yet after eleven years of capability, the ownership of Vane Mills remains exactly where it has always been. Is that not a grievance?"

"My father's arrangements for succession are his own affair."

"They are, they are." He smiled at the magistrate, sharing a small joke about daughters everywhere. "But grievances make strange historians, do they not? Let us look at yours. You say these books record secret payments. Secret from whom?"

"From the shareholders. From the insurers. From this inquiry, until now."

"From your father?"

"Yes."

"Your father, who sleeps forty feet from your bedroom door."

"The house is large."

"The house is large," Callum-Sedge repeated, savouring it, turning to the room as if offering them the phrase like a sweet. "Miss Vane, you will forgive me, but we are being asked to believe that a daughter living in intimacy with the head of this firm — taking his meals, managing his diary, handling his post — kept a parallel set of accounts for years without his knowledge. Is that really more plausible than the alternative?"

"Which alternative?"

"That you invented them. That this—" he touched the ledger with two fingers, delicately, as if it might be contagious, "—is the work of a clever and wounded woman, compiled after the fact, dressed up with hindsight and red ink, to wound a father who denied her what she believed was hers."

The gallery had gone very quiet. Constance felt the silence press against her like deep water, and she understood that this was the moment the whole edifice would stand or fold — not on the arithmetic, which was unimpeachable, but on whether twelve benches of mill-town people believed a woman like her was capable of doing what she had done for any reason other than spite.

She thought, absurdly, of her mother's sewing scissors, which had cut equally well forwards and backwards and which her mother had claimed could not tell the difference between the two directions. She thought of Rosalind Joubert's steady gaze in the third row. She thought of the widow Hartley's hands, which she had seen once through an office window, knotted together in a lap, waiting for a settlement that Constance herself had processed and filed and never once questioned, because questioning was not in her job description, and she had been so very good at her job description.

"I did not invent them," she said. "And I can prove the manner of their keeping, if Your Worship will permit a demonstration."

The magistrate inclined his head. Constance took the ledger back from the clerk's tray, opened it to March of the previous year, and set it on the sliding ledge, pinning the page flat with both forearms against the too-high rail — the box finally serving her, now that she had stopped asking its permission.

"Every entry in this book is dated on the day it was made, not the day of the transaction. You will note the ink changes. My office stock changed suppliers in the autumn before last; the early entries are in violet-black, the later ones in blue. Any document compiled retrospectively would show uniform ink. Further, each entry carries my marginal reconciliation against source papers — vouchers, wage slips, telegrams — most of which are already in the possession of this inquiry under the earlier warrants. Your own clerk can match them entry by entry. I did not write history, Mr. Callum-Sedge. I recorded it as it happened, in real time, because I did not yet know what I was recording."

"And when did you know?" Callum-Sedge asked softly. "When did the recording become accusation?"

Constance met his eyes. They were pale and patient and entirely without malice, which made them worse than malicious; they were the eyes of a man doing a job, and she had spent her life among men doing jobs.

"In February," she said. "When I reconciled the fund for the widow Hartley against the accident report for her husband, and found that the report described a fall on stairs that were not installed until six weeks after the date of his death."

The words went into the room and detonated quietly, the way true things do. Somewhere in the gallery a woman began to cry and stopped herself almost immediately, out of habit.

Callum-Sedge paused — a genuine pause, she noted with professional respect; he had not expected the specificity, and specificity is expensive to answer — and then shifted ground with the smoothness of a card sharp changing decks.

* * *

He dismantled her for two hours.

He took her apart along every seam she had known he would find and several she had not. He produced letters she had written in her father's name accepting the safety reports at face value — *your diligence is much appreciated, Mr. Brack* — and asked how a woman so diligent had failed to notice what she now claimed to know. He established that she had accepted dividends from the mill all her adult life, that her house, her clothes, her very presence in that witness box were purchased with the profits of the enterprise she now condemned. He read aloud, with devastating gentleness, from a letter she had written to a London cousin describing her father as *"the finest and most honourable man I have ever known,"* dated fourteen months ago.

"Were you lying then, Miss Vane? Or are you lying now?"

"I was ignorant then."

"Ignorant. In your own office. With your own books under your hands." He shook his head sorrowfully, letting the jury of the room draw the picture: either fool or traitor, and neither fit for belief. "You will forgive me, Miss Vane, but a witness who was deceived for eleven years by a man sleeping forty feet away, or who deceives us today — I am struggling to find the version that flatters you."

"You needn't flatter me," Constance said. "You need only count."

Laughter broke somewhere in the gallery — one bark of it, quickly strangled — and the magistrate rapped for order, but the damage to Callum-Sedge's architecture had been done, and she saw him register it in the fractional tightening of his jaw. So he went lower. He always would have; she had watched him work enough cases to know he kept a spade for the bottom of every grave.

He brought up her mother — dead nine years, spoken of carefully, the grief weaponised rather than named — and suggested that the daughter's judgment had been "unstable" since that loss. He brought up the charity event in October where, he implied, she had been seen in "close and frequent conversation" with persons hostile to the firm, and here his glance flicked toward the third row and Rosalind Joubert, and Constance felt the question arrive before it was asked: *were you recruited? bought? seduced?*

"Who approached whom, Miss Vane? Did these people come to you, or did you go looking for a use for your grievance?"

"No one approached me. I came to the evidence myself, which is more than can be said for anyone employed by the mill."

"You were alone in that office for years—"

"I was never alone in that office," Constance said. "There were always the widows. They came to the side window every quarter-day. I counted out their money myself. I looked into their faces while my books explained to me, in red ink, why their husbands' deaths had been worth less than a new boiler." She heard her own voice change and did not fight it; she let the room hear it change. "You ask whether I was deceived or am deceiving. Neither. I was *complicit*, Mr. Callum-Sedge — complicit in the way all clerks are complicit, which is to say completely, and comfortably, and without ever once being asked to sign my name to anything that would trouble my sleep. That is the genius of the second set of books. They keep everyone's conscience clean by keeping everyone's knowledge dirty. I am testifying today because I finally chose which of the two I could live with."

The silence afterward had texture. It had weight and grain, like the felt on the pressing tables in the finishing rooms. The magistrate wrote for a long time. Callum-Sedge consulted his brief, and for the first time all morning the small quick hands were still.

"One further matter," he said at last, quieter now, and Constance understood she had cost him money and he intended to recover it. "These books of yours. They were surrendered under warrant two days ago. Where were they kept, prior to that?"

"In my possession."

"Not in the office safe?"

"No."

"Where, then?"

Constance hesitated. It was a calculated hesitation, measured to the half-second, because the truth was dangerous in a direction no one in this room had yet thought to look, and she needed to give the next act of this business its opening without opening it herself.

"In a place I judged secure."

"A place. Or with a person?"

"With persons I judged trustworthy."

"Persons." He smiled thinly. "Persons connected with the clandestine printing operation this inquiry has heard rumoured — the so-called *Winter Count*?"

"I couldn't say what persons print what in this town," Constance said. "I only know that when my father's foreman burned the Harkers' paper stock in retaliation for a widow's testimony, the widow testified anyway, and when the press was smashed, the edition appeared anyway. Perhaps the persons you're worried about have discovered what I discovered, Mr. Callum-Sedge: that some things, once counted, cannot be uncounted."

She stepped down. Her knees were steadier than she had any right to expect. As she passed the long table, her father looked at her for the first time that day — one look, brief and level, and in it she read not hatred, which she had braced for, nor grief, which she might have forgiven, but appraisal: the look of a man re-costing an asset, deciding what remained salvageable.

It told her two things. It told her he believed, now, that she had beaten him this morning. And it told her he had begun, already, to calculate what he would do about it.

* * *

In the corridor outside, the cold hit her like a wall and she realised how much of the room's heat had been hers. Rosalind Joubert was waiting by the window at the passage's end, hat in hand, and fell into step beside her without a word until they reached the bend where the stair went down toward the street and the river beyond, brown and swollen and glittering.

"You held," Rosalind said.

"He didn't touch the books."

"He didn't dare. But he'll look for them again — the originals, the ones you kept back. He asked where, and you wouldn't say, and a man like that doesn't lose a thread because a witness ties it off. He pulls it later, when the court isn't watching." Rosalind's voice dropped. "Where are they, Constance? Truly."

Constance looked out the window at the bridge — the new timbers pale against the old stone, the ferry landing empty, the road open at last to London and its lawyers and its warrants. Then she looked at the narrow lane beyond the churchyard, where a rebuilt shop window showed, innocently, wedding invitations and funeral notices and a fresh stock of paper, ordered only this week, stacked higher than any legitimate trade required.

"Somewhere Brack hasn't thought to look yet," she said. "But he will. He burned their stock once, and burning taught him it works. If we mean to keep those pages, someone must move them tonight — before his men get the same idea I just gave him."

Rosalind was already pulling on her gloves.
