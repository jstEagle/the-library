## 29. The Stolen Forme

The forme had been gone four days when Rosalind learned where it slept.

She learned it the way she learned everything in Marlow Bend — sideways, through something meant for other eyes. She was carrying Edith's correspondence to the post office on Wednesday morning when she passed Brack's office on Mill Row and saw, through the window he was too arrogant to curtain, a boy sweeping the floor around a long wooden tray set face-down against the wall like a picture hung by someone who didn't understand pictures.

She knew that shape. She had spent three nights watching Tomas lock that shape into the chase, had learned to read its geometry from across the room: the dense black rectangle of the headline column, the ragged pale country of the widow's statements, the small square island where the names were set alone, each one spelled correctly, because Tomas had said — his broken hands wrapped in linen, his voice flat with pain and pride in equal measure — *the names are the whole argument, Rosalind. Everything else is commentary.*

It was sitting in Foreman Brack's office like a stolen painting propped behind a sofa, and Brack evidently did not know what he had.

That last realization arrived slowly and then all at once, the way floodwater arrives. Brack's men had smashed the press and taken the forme because taking things was what they did; they understood paper as evidence and presses as machines, but the forme itself — lead and wood, mirror-writing, meaningless unless you knew it — would have looked to them like nothing more than a tray of metal scraps. They hadn't destroyed it because destroying it would have required understanding it, and understanding it was precisely what none of them could do. It sat in Brack's office not as a trophy but as litter. He was keeping it out of spite, the way a man keeps a dog's corpse in a sack because he cannot think of anything else to do with it.

Rosalind stood on the pavement outside Mill Row with the letters warm against her ribs and let herself feel, for exactly four seconds, the wild bright animal joy of knowing something the enemy did not know he possessed.

Then she made herself walk past the window without turning her head, and began to plan.

* * *

The problem, laid out that evening on the Harkers' kitchen table in pencil on the back of a wallpaper sample, came down to three facts.

Fact the first: the inquiry was in recess until Monday. The magistrate, Mr. Havelock-Bray — a man whose name sounded like a law firm and whose face suggested he had been assembled from other, more successful men's spare parts — had adjourned proceedings to "consider the evidence," which in practice meant considering how much of it could be quietly folded away. The courthouse stood empty. More importantly, the town's attention stood empty; everyone who mattered was resting, everyone who watched was tired of watching, and Marlow Bend had sunk into the peculiar Sunday-afternoon torpor that follows four days of official listening.

Fact the second: Brack kept irregular hours at best. He was a creature of the mill floor and the public house, not the desk. His office was locked, but the lock was a mill lock, bought by the gross, and Tomas — speaking from his sickbed with the serene authority of a man who had been breaking into things since before Rosalind could drive — pronounced it "a lock for keeping honest men honest."

Fact the third, and the one that turned the whole scheme from suicide into merely probable death: Constance Vane had keys.

"She has keys to everything," Rosalind said. "Her father built her an office inside his own so she'd stop asking questions about his accounts. She has a key to the corridor. She may have a key to his wing."

"And you trust her." Tomas said it without inflection, which was worse than if he'd said it with contempt.

"I trust her arithmetic," Rosalind said. "Her father's conviction costs her less than her silence does now. She's already burned the bridge behind her at the inquiry. She held on the stand for two hours under that lawyer's knife. People who hold like that don't turn around afterward."

"People do all kinds of things afterward."

"Yes," Rosalind said, and thought of every letter she had ever steamed open, every confidence she had ever sold to her own curiosity, every year of reading other people's hearts without their knowledge. "They do. That's rather my point. I'm asking you to extend to her the credit no one extended to me."

Tomas was quiet for a long time. His hands lay on the table, bandaged to the fingers, useless-looking and terrible, and Rosalind understood suddenly that his hesitation was not really about Constance Vane at all. It was about being helpless. It was about the forme sitting in Brack's office containing every word of the widows' testimony — words he had set himself, letter by letter, with hands that might never set type again — and about having to hand the rescue to someone else because his hands were ruined.

"You'll need someone to stand watch in the corridor," he said finally. "Someone with a reason to be in that building on a Saturday."

"Constance can stand watch. She has a reason to be anywhere in that building."

"I mean someone else. Someone whose presence doesn't depend on Vane goodwill. Pike walks the courthouse yard at half six every evening since the inquiry started — old habit, checking the doors. If Pike sees a light where there shouldn't be one, he'll either raise the alarm or he'll look at the sky for a while and then walk on. We won't know which until we're caught or not caught." He looked up. His eyes were fever-bright and very steady. "I can't come with you. So I'm sending you with my blessing instead, and I hate it, and I want you back here by seven o'clock with that forme under your coat, or I want you never to have gone at all. Those are the only two outcomes I've prepared speeches for."

Rosalind almost laughed. Almost. What stopped her was the look on his face, which was not the face of a man making a joke but the face of a man handing over something fragile — a daughter, a brother, a future — into weather he couldn't control.

"Seven o'clock," she said.

* * *

Saturday came up grey and mild, the river running high and brown and quiet, the town wearing its weekend face. Rosalind spent the morning doing ordinary things with exaggerated care — mending a glove seam, walking past the courthouse twice at a pace designed to bore anyone watching, buying bread — while inside her chest a small mechanism ticked faster and faster, counting down toward six o'clock.

At five she met Constance Vane in the churchyard, because there was nowhere in Marlow Bend less likely to hold a watcher on a Saturday afternoon except perhaps the river bottom, and even that had watchers lately.

Constance arrived punctually, in a grey dress chosen — Rosalind suspected deliberately — to make her look like a woman visiting a grave, which in a sense she was. She carried a prayer book she clearly had no intention of opening.

"Well?" she said. No greeting. Constance Vane did not believe in greetings; they were, she had once told Rosalind at the charity event where they had first recognized each other, *time spent pretending not to know why you've come.*

"The forme is in your foreman's office. Bottom shelf, against the east wall, face down. I saw it Wednesday."

"You're certain it's ours?"

"It has our headlines in reverse and Mrs. Ellery's testimony spelled correctly, which is more than any court reporter has managed."

Something moved at the corner of Constance's mouth — not quite a smile; Constance rationed smiles the way her father rationed wages. "And you want me to open the door for you."

"I want you to tell me whether your key opens it. If it doesn't, I'll want to know which lock it is so Tomas can advise me, though his advice will consist mostly of swearing."

"My key opens Brack's office." Constance said it flatly, and then, because she was incapable of leaving power unused: "He asked Father for a better lock after the raid and Father refused. He said Brack's paranoia was costing enough already." A pause. "My father is not a subtle man, whatever else he is."

"No."

"He keeps ledgers, though. Subtle ones." Constance looked out over the graves, at the brown river beyond the wall, and Rosalind watched her decide to say the next thing. "I've been thinking about what happens after Monday. If Havelock-Bray folds it all away — and he will, he's been folding since the first morning — then the only record that matters is the one in that forme. And if the forme is found in my father's foreman's possession during the recess, unexplained—" She stopped. Started again, colder. "I am not helping you steal it, Miss Joubert. I am helping it escape. There's a legal difference. My father's lawyers taught me that."

"There's no difference at all," Rosalind said, "but I'm grateful for the fiction. Fiction is how most true things get done."

For a moment Constance looked at her with an expression Rosalind had seen once before, on the night of the charity event — the look of a woman recognizing furniture from her own house in someone else's rooms. Then she took off one grey glove, finger by finger, and held out her hand.

In the palm lay two keys on a loop of string. One was small and brass. The other was larger, iron, heavy, and wrong — a key to a door Rosalind hadn't asked about.

"The brass is Brack's office," Constance said. "The iron is the side door by the coal chute. In case you need to leave differently than you entered. Plans that only work forward aren't plans; they're hopes." She closed Rosalind's fingers over the keys herself, briefly, formally, like a doctor setting a bone. "Corridor lamp is gas; the valve is at the far end near the stairs, and it takes eleven seconds to relight if someone turns it off, which I will do at ten to six. You'll have darkness and then light and then darkness again. Use the middle part."

"How long?"

"As long as I say. I'll be in my father's office with the door open, working late on accounts, visibly, audibly — I shall hum, Miss Joubert. I have decided to hum. If anyone comes up those stairs, they will find the mill owner's daughter innocently humming over a ledger, and they will apologize, and they will leave, and I will have bought you whatever minutes the humming buys." She put her glove back on. "One condition."

Rosalind waited. With Constance there was always one condition, and it was never the one you feared.

"When it's printed again — the final edition, the one that goes everywhere — my name goes in it. Not as the mill owner's daughter making amends. As the keeper of the second set of books. Full name. Spelled correctly."

"Spelled correctly," Rosalind promised, "is the house specialty."

* * *

At ten to six the courthouse smelled of cold stone, dust, and the ghost of four days of official listening — tobacco and wet wool and the particular staleness of rooms where men have been deciding things. Rosalind came in through the front, because coming in through the front was the bravest thing available and therefore, per Tomas's doctrine, the safest: a woman with a legitimate errand, a note in her pocket addressed to the magistrate's clerk regarding some misplaced correspondence of Edith Marchmont's, a face that belonged in this building now whether anyone liked it or not.

The clerk had gone home. The hall was empty. Her footsteps went up the stairs ahead of her like a warning shouted too late.

On the upper landing the corridor ran east–west, offices on both sides, gas lamps spaced along the wall, all of them burning with the low orange patience of public money. At the far end, the door marked **J. BRACK, FOREMAN** in painted block letters that someone had executed with more care than the man deserved.

The corridor lamp at the stair-end went out. Darkness rolled down the hallway toward her like a tide. Eleven seconds, Constance had said. Rosalind counted them in heartbeats, which was a mistake, because her heart had abandoned all reasonable tempo, and by the time she reached twelve the lamp had sputtered back alight and the corridor lay in shadow, half-lit, the lamps between her and Brack's door still burning — Constance could only reach the one valve — and the shadows lying across the floorboards like fallen rafters.

She walked. She did not run; running was a sound. She walked the way she had learned to walk past windows she was reading, the way she had learned to walk through Edith's drawing rooms full of people she was cataloguing — a walk calibrated to be forgettable.

The brass key slid into Brack's lock and turned with a click that seemed, in the silence, loud as a shot. She eased the door open a hand's width, slipped through, and pulled it nearly shut behind her.

Inside: desk, chair, coat on its peg, a smell of pipe smoke and boot oil and something faintly chemical — the ink, she realized. Brack had brought the forme here and somewhere nearby there was still ink. On the wall, a calendar from the mill supplier, a buxom woman holding a bolt of cloth. On the floor by the east wall, face-down exactly as she'd seen it—

Gone.

Rosalind stood very still. The room rearranged itself around her absence. Where the forme had lain Wednesday there was now a wet ring on the floorboards, dark, recent — and beneath the desk, shoved back as if in haste, a long wooden shape covered with a horse blanket.

Not gone. Moved. Moved recently — the blanket's fold was still sharp, the dust on the floor showed drag-marks no older than a day. Brack had shifted it. Perhaps he'd finally looked at it properly. Perhaps someone had told him what it was.

*Don't think,* she told herself, and knelt, and pulled the blanket off.

There it was. The forme, locked in its chase, iron frame scarred, type packed tight as a field of wheat seen from above — mirror-world writing, backwards and beautiful. The headline column. The statements. The names, each one spelled correctly. Tomas's three nights of broken-handed memory, sitting in the dark under a horse blanket in a foreman's office.

It weighed as much as a sleeping child. She got her arms under it and lifted, and the effort drove the breath out of her in a thin whistle she clamped shut between her teeth. Too big for her coat. Too big for any coat. She would carry it in her arms, openly, through the corridor, and if anyone saw her she would say — she was actually constructing the sentence, *the magistrate asked me to collect the exhibits* — when the floorboard in the corridor creaked.

Footsteps. Heavy ones. Coming up the stairs.

Rosalind did the arithmetic in half a second and hated the answer: the forme was too heavy to carry fast, the window was too narrow and too high and overlooked the yard where Pike walked at half six, and hiding under the desk was the first place any searcher would look. Which left the cupboard in the corner — a tall thin cupboard, door ajar, smelling of turpentine, containing paint pots and a coil of rope and approximately four inches of empty space beside a man-shaped absence.

She got the forme into the cupboard first, wedging it upright against the paint pots, and then got herself in beside it, and pulled the door to within a finger's width of closed just as the outer door opened.

Brack came in the way he did everything, like weather — a shove of cold air and pipe smoke and self-regard. He crossed to his desk. He opened a drawer. And then he stopped, and Rosalind, watching through the gap in the cupboard door, saw his head come round slowly toward the corner where she stood, toward the blanket she had not re-draped, crumpled on the floor like a shed skin.

She had left the blanket. Of course she had left the blanket. She had left it because she'd had four seconds, and now Brack was looking at the crumpled blanket on his floor with the expression of a man whose dog has just told him something.

He crossed the room. He bent. He picked the blanket up, turned it over once, and Rosalind watched him arrive at the same conclusion she had arrived at four days ago in reverse: that something had been under here, and was not under here now, and that the something had legs.

His eyes went to the cupboard.

Rosalind's hand found the coil of rope in the dark and closed around it, uselessly, the way a drowning man closes around water. Through the crack she could see Brack's boots planted two feet away, could see his shadow fall across the gap in the cupboard door, growing, darkening it—

And then, from the corridor, clear and carrying and utterly unhurried, came the sound of a woman humming.

Not just any humming. Constance Vane hummed the way she did everything — precisely, audibly, with the metronomic confidence of someone who had rehearsed. She was moving down the corridor toward Brack's office at a stroll, humming a hymn, and her voice arrived at the doorway ahead of her body.

"Foreman Brack." The door swung wide. Constance stood framed in it, ledger under her arm, lamp-light behind her. "You're here late. Good. I've a question about the February colliery order that neither you nor my father will answer in writing, so I've come to ask it in person."

Brack straightened. Rosalind saw the shadow shift, saw the suspicion fight the deference and lose — lose slowly, grudgingly, the way a dog loses a fight with its own chain. "Miss Vane. This isn't — I wasn't expecting—"

"No one ever is. February. Colliery order. The figure in your book is forty pounds different from the figure in mine, and mine comes from Father's own safe, so yours is either theft or error, and we're going to establish which before Monday, because Mr. Havelock-Bray's clerk has requested the freight accounts and I will not have this family embarrassed by a foreman's arithmetic."

It was magnificent. It was, Rosalind thought, pressing herself into the turpentine-smelling dark, the single most effective interrogation she had ever witnessed, conducted entirely for an audience of one hiding in a cupboard. Brack was already fumbling at his desk drawers, already defensive, already retreating into the terrain where he was weakest and she was strongest — figures, records, the paper trail he despised. Constance drew him to the window, made him open the freight book, made him defend column after column, her voice never rising, never hurrying, a metronome of accusation.

And under the cover of it, Rosalind did the hardest physical thing she had ever done: she lifted the forme again — arms screaming, palms finding the iron edges of the chase — and eased the cupboard door open inch by inch, and crossed the office behind Brack's back, past the window where his reflection should have betrayed her and didn't because the lamp was behind him and the glass gave back only the room's bright interior, and reached the door, and stepped through it into the corridor, and pulled Brack's door softly, softly shut.

The hymn continued. Constance had moved on to the second verse. Rosalind walked down the half-lit corridor with thirty pounds of stolen truth in her arms, past the dead lamp, past the landing, down the stairs, out through the hall, into the grey evening where the river ran high and brown and quiet and the streetlamps were just coming on.

Behind her, muffled through two floors, she heard the humming stop, heard Constance's voice say, distinctly, *"Well? Are you going to show me February or not?"* — buying her another minute, another two, spending her own safety like coin because she had promised to and because she was the kind of person who kept promises shaped like that.

Rosalind did not go out the front. She remembered the iron key. She went down the back passage, past the coal chute, and let herself out the side door into the yard, where — she discovered, heart seizing, feet stopping — Constable Pike stood at the gate, hat in his hands, watching her.

For three seconds, the whole world balanced on a pin.

Pike looked at her. Looked at the forme in her arms — unmistakable now, in the open air, a printing chase if you knew what one was, and Pike had grown up above a stationer's and knew perfectly well what one was. He looked at it for a long moment. Then he looked up at the sky, which was doing nothing whatsoever, and examined it thoroughly, and turned his back to her, and said to the evening in general, in a voice pitched to carry exactly as far as the gate and no further:

"Half past six. Yard's all secure. Nothing stirring."

Rosalind walked past him. At the gate she paused, and he did not turn, and she said, very quietly, "Thank you, Constable."

"Don't thank me," Pike said to the sky. "I never saw you. Nobody ever sees anybody in this town. That's the whole trouble with it."

* * *

Tomas was waiting at the shop door when she came up Mill Lane, and he didn't ask if she had it. He saw the shape of the bundle under her shawl and simply stood aside to let her pass, and it was Mrs. Harker who took it from her arms — took the whole weight of it easily, this small grey woman who had been lifting type trays for thirty years — and set it on the bench among the wreckage with the tenderness of a nurse receiving a returned soldier.

Tomas touched the chase with his bandaged hands, both of them together, the way you'd touch a forehead to check for fever. He didn't say anything for a while.

"Constance?" he said at last.

"Held the door. Held the man. Held the whole building." Rosalind flexed her aching arms. "She wants her name in the next edition. Full name, spelled correctly."

"Then it goes in," Tomas said. And then, looking at her with an expression she was going to spend years learning to read: "You understand what tonight means. Brack knows it's missing by tomorrow morning. He'll tear this street apart. Whatever we print now, we print with the whole town watching us do it — and the borrowed press comes Tuesday."

"Tuesday," Rosalind said. "Three days."

"Three days," said Tomas, "and every name spelled correctly."

Above them, through the floorboards, they could hear Mrs. Harker already clearing space on the bench, and somewhere out in the dark the river kept moving, high and brown and patient, carrying winter downstream toward the sea.
