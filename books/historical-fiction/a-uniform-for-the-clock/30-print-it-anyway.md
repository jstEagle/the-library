## 30. Print It Anyway

The press arrived in a coffin.

Not a figure of speech — Tomas Harker insisted on that distinction later, when people asked how they'd managed it. An actual coffin, pine and black-lacquered trim, consigned to the Harker shop from a cabinetmaker in Wexford Mills with the paperwork reading *funeral goods — handle with care*. The undertaker owed them. Everyone owed everyone something in those towns along the river, and debts had a way of traveling where people couldn't.

Tomas had met the cart at the churchyard gate at eleven o'clock at night, his hands still bandaged from Brack's men and his ribs strapped so tight he could barely bend to lift the lid. He hadn't bent. He'd stood there while Pike's replacement — a young constable named Ellery who asked no questions because he'd been told asking questions was bad for advancement — held the lantern, and the two of them watched the coffin-carriers set it down among the graves as if delivering it to the earth itself.

"Weight of a sleeper," one of the carriers said, meaning: we know what we carried.

"Sleeping," Tomas agreed.

They pried the lid inside the vestry, out of the lantern light. Beneath the straw lay a Chandler & Price platen press, eight by twelve, its iron arms greased and wrapped in oilcloth, disassembled into four pieces like a body laid out for burial. A crate of type followed it, and a stone for the imposing table, and a sack containing rollers that had been packed in bran to keep them from drying out. The cabinetmaker had even included a note, folded small: *She was my father's. She prints true. Bring her back or don't bring her back, but print.*

Tomas read it twice and put it in his breast pocket, over his heart, where he kept the things he intended to answer someday.

* * *

The shop smelled wrong when they brought her in. That was the first thing he noticed — the smell of violence that three days had not lifted. Brack's men had gone through the place like weather through wheat. The old Albion press lay where they'd left it, its frame cracked through the cheek, its tympan split, iron twisted in a way that spoke of men who knew nothing about presses except how to break them. Type was scattered across the floorboards in drifts, letters kicked into corners, some ground under boots into shapes no alphabet would claim. The forme itself — the locked edition, weeks of work, every testimony, every ledger line, every name — was gone, carried off to Brack's office as evidence against them, which was the joke of it, since evidence cut both ways and Brack had never learned to read either direction.

Rosalind was already there when they came in with the coffin-pieces. Of course she was. Tomas had stopped being surprised by that sometime around the third week of knowing her. She sat at the composing stone with a candle burning low and the widow's statements spread before her — the originals, rescued from the flour bin, smelling faintly of the pantry — and she looked up when the door opened and said only:

"You found one."

"I found one."

"Is she true?"

"She prints true." He set the crate down and began unbuckling the straps on his bandaged hands, which took longer than it should have. "Her father's press. From Wexford Mills."

"Wexford." Rosalind turned the word over. "That's thirty miles. Someone drove thirty miles in the dark with a press dressed as a dead man."

"The dead man made good cover. Nobody stops a coffin. Nobody wants to look inside one." He got the last strap loose and flexed his fingers, wincing. Two knuckles on the right hand had healed crooked. The doctor said he'd be lucky to hold a pen without pain; nobody had thought to ask about a composing stick. "We'll need to reassemble her. It's a night's work if the hands are good. Mine aren't."

Rosalind stood. She had ink on her fingers already — he noticed everything about her hands now, the way a man who has lost the use of his own notices other people's — and there was a look on her face that he had come to recognize as the beginning of an argument she was going to win.

"Then teach me the frame," she said. "You direct. I'll lift."

* * *

They worked by three candles and a lamp shielded behind a flour tin, because light leaking onto the street had been what betrayed them before, or part of what had, and Tomas had learned his lessons the expensive way. The shutters were closed. The door was barred. Constance Vane had sent word through the undertaker's boy that Brack's men were drinking at the Anchor until midnight and that their foreman liked to make his rounds past the shop around two, alone, out of habit or spite, nobody could say which. So they had until two. After two, they had whatever luck allowed.

The press went together the way Tomas's mother used to describe bread rising — slowly and then all at once. First the bed, bolted to the new-imposed stone. Then the frame, uprighted with a groan of iron that sounded too loud in the shuttered dark and made all three of them freeze and listen to the street until the silence confirmed itself. Then the platen arm, hung and pinned, and the spring assembly, and the rollers, which came out of their bran bed soft as sleeping animals.

Rosalind worked without complaint and without fumbling, which surprised him less than it should have. He had watched her hands for weeks now — copying documents in Edith Marchmont's back room, setting type beside him during the long nights before the raid, learning the chase and the quoins as though the trade had been waiting for her all along and she had simply arrived late. There were people, his father used to say, who are born readers and people who are born printers, and the second kind are just the first kind who couldn't stand to leave the words sitting still.

His sister Maren worked the type. She was faster than any of them, always had been, and her hands were unbroken, and she moved down the galley like water finding its level. They were resetting the whole edition from memory and from paper — mostly from memory, because the stolen forme could not be recovered, not yet, not tonight, though Rosalind had plans for it that she shared with no one and that Tomas pretended not to see forming behind her eyes.

The testimonies came back first. Mrs. Aldous's account of the fire doors chained in November. The widow Penhale, who had demanded her full name in print — *Sarah Penhale, née Croft, of Bridge Street* — and whose statement Rosalind read aloud once, quietly, before locking it into the galley, the way you'd read a psalm. The winter count itself, the numbers, month by month, two winters' worth, each death matched to the falsified inspection reports that Constance Vane had copied in her father's own office with her father's own pen.

And then, near three o'clock — past the hour of danger, past the time when Brack should have passed and somehow hadn't, a fact none of them remarked upon because remarking on luck is how you lose it — they came to the end.

The final page. The roll of names.

It had always been the heart of the sheet, the thing the whole paper existed to carry: the men the mill called accidents and the town called by name. Tomas had set those names himself, twice now, once for the first edition and once in memory after the smashing, and both times he had done it with the same care a man gives a headstone, because that was what they were. Headstones in lead. The mill kept no ledger of names; therefore the press would.

But this time, when Maren reached the last lines, she stopped.

"Tomas." Her voice was strange. "Come look."

He crossed the room, ribs protesting, and looked at what she was looking at, and understood immediately that Rosalind had gotten there ahead of them. There was a corrected galley on the stone — the names, reset, but not merely reset. Amended. Checked. Beside the galley lay the parish registers borrowed from Reverend Lyle, open, weighted with the flour tin, and a list in Rosalind's small exact hand: spellings verified against baptismal records, birth dates, marriage entries. Names that had appeared in the first edition with the casual errors of oral memory — *Josiah Brant* for *Josiah Braunt*, *Ellen Marsh* for *Ellin Marshe*, the Penhale boy's middle initial wrong — every one of them corrected now against the ink of the parish book, against the way the names had actually been written at the font and the grave.

Every name. Spelled correctly.

"It matters," Rosalind said, not looking up from the register. She had heard them approach; of course she had. "I know it seems like pedantry. But I've spent years reading other people's mail, Tomas. I know what gets lost in transmission. A man's name misspelled is a man half-erased. The mill erased them whole. We don't get to do it by accident what they did on purpose."

Tomas looked at the galley for a long time. His throat did something he chose not to acknowledge.

"Your hands," he said finally. "Set the last line yourself."

She looked up then. In the candlelight her face was tired and lit from below and entirely certain, the way it had been the day she proposed printing the evidence locally instead of smuggling it out — the day she first rebelled, though she'd never have called it that. "I'm not a printer," she said. It wasn't modesty; it was a question wearing modesty's coat.

"You've been a printer since the night you fixed my typo and didn't tell me you'd done it until I caught it at press." He stepped back from the stone. "Last line's yours. Family tradition. Whoever sets it signs it, whether they sign it or not."

Maren handed her the composing stick the way you hand someone a key to a house they're already living in.

* * *

Rosalind set the names one at a time, and Tomas watched.

He watched her take each slug of lead from the case without hunting for it — she had memorized the California job case layout days ago, in silence, the way she memorized everything — and he watched her check each letter against the register before placing it, and he watched her do the thing that told him more than anything else: she read each completed line backward, in the mirror-world of composed type, hunting errors, because a printer reads backward so the world can read forward. Nobody had taught her that. She had deduced it, or absorbed it, or been born knowing it, and when Tomas saw it he had to turn away for a moment and pretend to check the rollers.

The last line went in near dawn. *John Harker, aged nineteen.* His brother. The reason for all of it, though the reasons had multiplied since, had grown children and grandchildren of consequence until the original reason seemed almost small — but it wasn't small. It had never been small. Nineteen years old, and the accident ledger recorded *fatality, boiler scald, worker unidentified,* and two winters later the press in a shuttered shop identified him, letter by letter, correctly, in the presence of witnesses.

Rosalind locked the line into the forme. She tightened the quoins with the key, tested the lockup with the little lift-and-shake that Tomas had shown her, and it held. Solid. True.

"He's spelled right," she said quietly.

"Yes," Tomas said. "He is."

Outside, the sky over the river had gone the grey of unsized paper. Somewhere a cock was making its argument. They had perhaps two hours before the town woke, before the inquiry clerks opened their tidy ledgers, before Brack finished his drink or his rounds or whatever had kept him away tonight and remembered what he'd sworn to finish. The forme sat on the bed of the borrowed press like a held breath.

"Rollers," Tomas said, and his voice came out steadier than his hands.

Maren inked. Rosalind fed the first sheet — cheap stock, the last of the paper they'd hidden in the coffin with the press, God bless the cabinetmaker's thoroughness — and Tomas pulled the bar.

The impression bit clean. He felt it through the whole machine, through the floor, through his broken ribs: the press saying yes. Sheet after sheet after sheet, the rhythm finding itself, three people and a dead man's press making copies of the truth in a town that had tried to bury it twice. The smell of ink rose up and covered, at last, the smell of what Brack's men had done.

By the time the light came strong enough to see dust in the air, five hundred sheets lay stacked and damp on the stone, and Rosalind Joubert stood over them with ink to her wrists, reading her own typesetting forward for the first time, every name whole.

Tomas was reaching for the stack to begin folding when the knock came at the door — not the warning-code, not Pike's pattern, not the undertaker's boy. Three slow blows of a fist that meant *we know you're awake in there.*

Through the shutters, in the early light, they could hear men gathering in the street.
