## 31. The Town Reads

The paper came into Marlow Bend the way floodwater came — quietly at first, then all at once, then everywhere.

It began, as most things in that town did, with the Harkers' shop. Tomas had printed four hundred copies of *The Winter Count* on the borrowed press from Ashford Vale, working through the night while Rosalind set type by candlelight and Constance Vane stood watch at the window with a lamp turned low. By dawn on Thursday the edition was folded, stacked, and gone — distributed not by any plan that could be traced, but by the oldest network a town possesses: women who walked to market, boys who ran errands, men who stopped at one another's gates to say nothing loudly enough.

By Friday morning it was everywhere.

---

In St. Aldhelm's Church, the verger found them in the pews before the eight o'clock service. He was an elderly man named Prewitt who had swept that floor for thirty years and had buried more mill workers than he could count without his ledger, which he kept, privately, in the same drawer as the parish accounts — because no one else would record the names either.

He found the folded sheets tucked into the hymn boards, one per pew, laid flat and square as communion wafers. The masthead was plain. No ornament, no flourish. Just:

**THE WINTER COUNT**

*Being a true accounting of those lost to the Marlow Bend Mills, with their names, their injuries, their dates, and the testimony of their widows.*

*Set and printed in Marlow Bend by hands that will not sign away the truth.*

Prewitt stood in the aisle holding one copy and did not read it then, because the congregation was arriving and there were candles to light. But during the first hymn — *Guide Me, O Thou Great Redeemer*, sung as it always was with the enthusiasm of people who needed guiding — he watched Mrs. Ellery Hobbes open hers.

Mrs. Hobbes was seventy-one years old and had been a widow since the winter of '11, when her son Alfred went into the carding room a healthy man of twenty-three and came out on a plank carried by four of his fellows. She had been told it was his heart. She had never believed it, but belief unproven is only grief wearing a different coat, and she had worn that coat for two years in silence.

She read the paper through the whole of the sermon. Reverend Cattrell, preaching on perseverance, noticed her bent over it and assumed she'd fallen asleep, and said nothing, because Mrs. Hobbes had earned the right to sleep in whatever posture she chose.

What she was reading was Alfred's name. Spelled correctly. With the date — the ninth of January, 1911 — and beneath it, in small careful type: *Struck by a broken drive-shaft guard that had been reported defective eleven days prior. Repair order cancelled by Foreman Brack as "non-essential."*

Mrs. Hobbes read it twice. Then she took off her glove — slowly, the way one does when handling something holy or dangerous — and touched the ink to see if it would come away on her finger.

It did not. It was set properly, in good ink, on decent stock. It was real.

When the congregation rose for the final hymn, Mrs. Hobbes rose with Alfred's name folded against her heart, and she sang louder than she had sung in two years, and several people around her wondered why, and three of them unfolded their own copies to find out.

---

At the Anchor and Keel, the public house nearest the mill gate, the landlord Dan Fitchett found six copies tucked under the bar mats when he opened at half past five. He read the first one standing in the grey morning dark with the taps cold behind him, and by the time he finished, his hands weren't steady.

Fitchett had served this town's men for nineteen years. He knew what they drank after a shift and what they drank after a funeral, and he knew the difference between the two silences that settled over the taproom — the tired silence and the frightened one. For two winters the frightened silence had been winning. Men drank with their shoulders up around their ears. They talked about football and weather and never about the mill, because the mill was everywhere and nowhere you could point at, and pointing got your hours cut, and cut hours got your family hungry.

He put the copies under the bar. At opening time he said nothing. At noon he laid one face-up on the bar beside the mustard pot, and by one o'clock every man in the room had read it, and nobody had spoken above a murmur, and the silence in the Anchor and Keel was neither tired nor frightened.

It was the third kind. The kind that comes before something.

Old Sam Tredwell, who had worked the looms forty years until his lungs gave out, read the list of names twice and then read the testimony of Widow Marsh — the widow who had refused to speak except under her full name, Margaret Ellsworth Marsh, of Number Fourteen Weavers' Row — and when he reached the line where she described finding her husband's boots by the pit of the dye vats, still laced, because they hadn't let her see anything else, Sam Tredwell set the paper down very gently, as though it might bruise, and wept into his pint without shame and without sound, and no man in the room looked at him, and every man in the room understood.

"Four hundred copies," someone said near the window. "Where's t'other three hundred and ninety-four?"

Nobody answered. But that evening, copies appeared in the chapel vestry, in the reading room of the Mechanics' Institute, under the door of Dr. Loxley's surgery, inside the cover of the *Marlow Bend Advertiser* at the stationer's — slipped there between the delivery boy's rounds like a letter hidden in a Bible. A fishmonger found one wrapped around his order of haddock and read it standing in his apron before he could think to stop. A schoolmistress confiscated one from a fourteen-year-old boy named Wilf, read it in the empty classroom after hours, and returned it to him the next morning with the words *"Keep this somewhere safe"* and a look the boy would remember the rest of his life.

The mill's foremen collected copies where they could. Brack himself burned a small pile in the yard of the mill office on Friday night, and the smoke smelled of cheap newsprint and rage, and everyone within sight of the fire understood exactly what it meant: that burning a thing proves you are afraid of it.

---

The magistrate's inquiry convened in the county courtroom on Monday, and it was there that the paper performed its strangest and most necessary work — for the inquiry had been designed, from its first day, as a machine for forgetting.

Mr. Ambrose Pell, counsel for the Marlow Bend Mills, was a tidy man with a tidy manner, and he conducted the proceedings like a gardener deadheading roses. Witnesses were called in careful order. The mill's safety officer testified that conditions were "consistent with industry standards." The works doctor testified that accidents were "regrettable but infrequent." A foreman testified that safety reports were filed monthly and acted upon promptly. Each testimony was sanded smooth as it was given; each rough edge was planed away by Pell's soft questions; each number that might have alarmed was converted into a percentage, and each percentage into reassurance.

The gallery held perhaps sixty townspeople, admitted by ticket, and the tickets had been distributed through channels the mill controlled. But tickets pass through many hands, and some of those hands belonged to women who had spent a week passing papers instead.

So it happened that on Tuesday afternoon, when Pell called his final witness of the session — a clerk from the pay office, summoned to establish the mill's generous provision of compensation payments — and asked him to confirm that the sum of £14 had been paid to the widow of a worker killed in February of 1912, a woman in the third row of the gallery rose to her feet.

She was Margaret Ellsworth Marsh. She wore black, and her hands were folded, and her voice when she spoke was not loud but it carried, the way things carry in a room built for listening.

"Fourteen pounds," she said. "And my husband's name is not in your ledger. It is in mine."

Pell turned, unhurried, the way a man turns toward a draught. The magistrate, a Mr. Justice Harrow, frowned and rapped for order.

"Madam, this is a formal proceeding. If you have something to say, there is a—"

"He is in the paper," Mrs. Marsh said. "*The Winter Count.* His name is Josiah Marsh. He died on the fourth of February, 1912, in the dye house. Your records call it a scald. The paper says the vat valve had been jammed open for three days because the repair part was ordered and then cancelled, and I know that's true because the man who cancelled the order told my neighbor so, and my neighbor told me, and I am telling you." Her voice did not rise. It only grew more precise, like water finding its level. "You may write down fourteen pounds. But the town has written down everything else, and the town's book is the one that will last."

There was a moment in which the courtroom held absolutely still — the particular stillness of a room where something irreversible has just occurred. Then, in the second row, another woman stood. Then a third, further back. Not speaking. Only standing, one after another, the way a field stands when the wind moves through it, each of them a widow or a sister or a mother of a name on that list, rising to her feet in the sight of the law so that the law could look upon what it had agreed not to count.

Justice Harrow looked at them for a long moment over his spectacles. Whatever he intended to say, he did not say it. He adjourned early, citing the hour, and the gavel fell with less authority than it had risen.

---

Rosalind Joubert watched all of it from the back of the gallery, beside Tomas Harker, whose hands were still bandaged from the night Brack's men had come, and beside Edith Marchmont, who sat with her spine straight and her gloved hands quiet in her lap and her eyes bright in a way Rosalind had learned, over these months, to read as carefully as any letter.

Edith did not speak until they were out on the courthouse steps, in the cold gold light of late afternoon.

"Do you understand what happened in there?" she said. Her voice was low, and it trembled — not with fear, Rosalind realized, but with something Edith Marchmont had perhaps not felt in twenty years of movement politics. Wonder. "Thirty years I've argued that the cause needs martyrs, needs symbols, needs arithmetic. And in there—" she gestured back at the doors, at the muffled sound of the town moving beyond them, "—in there it wasn't arithmetic. It was Margaret Marsh standing up and saying *my husband's name is Josiah*. That is worth more than every speech I have ever given, and I could not have made it happen. They made it happen themselves."

"They had help," Tomas said quietly. His bandaged hand moved, briefly, toward Rosalind and then stopped, and settled instead on the courthouse railing.

"The paper," Edith said. "Yes. Four hundred copies of ink and paper." She shook her head slowly. "I have spent my life believing the pen must be aimed by strategy. I begin to suspect it only needs to be honest."

Rosalind said nothing. Rosalind was watching the street.

Because across the square, outside the mill office, a constable stood talking to two of Brack's men, and Constable Pike's face wore an expression Rosalind had seen once before on a man delivering bad news to a family — the expression of a man carrying something heavy and knowing he must soon set it down. And even as she watched, Pike turned away from Brack's men, crossed the square against the wind, and came directly toward the courthouse steps with his hand already inside his coat, where a folded document was waiting.

He stopped at the bottom step. He looked up at the three of them — the suffragist, the printer, and the woman who had once read other people's letters for a living and now stood among printers and widows as one of their own.

"I'm sorry," Aldous Pike said, and his voice carried the terrible gentleness of a man who has chosen, at last, and knows the cost. "But you'll want to hear this before anyone else tells you."

He drew the paper from his coat. Even at that distance, even folded, Rosalind could see the seal of the county magistracy breaking the fold, and the word at its head, printed in the formal hand the law reserved for its gravest instruments.

Warrants.

* * *

Three names, Pike said. And the third name was not Tomas Harker's, and was not Edith Marchmont's.

It was hers.
