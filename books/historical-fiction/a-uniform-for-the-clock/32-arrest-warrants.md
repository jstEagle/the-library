## 32. Arrest Warrants

The news came the way the flood had come — first a sound, then a certainty.

Rosalind heard it before she understood it: running feet on the cobbles below the print shop window, a boy's voice cracking with urgency, the word *warrants* repeated three times as though repetition could make it less real. She was at the table in the Harkers' back room, setting type by lamplight for the reprint edition, her fingers black to the second knuckle, and for one foolish moment she thought the boy was selling something. Papers. Chestnuts. News of the bridge.

Then she heard Tomas's name, and Edith's, and hers — *the printer's assistant, name unknown* — and the lamp flame seemed to lean sideways in the draft of it.

She set down the composing stick very carefully, as if the small metal letters might shatter like glass.

* * *

By noon the whole town knew. By two o'clock, so did the shape of it: warrants sworn out before the visiting magistrate's clerk, charging Edith Marchmont with conspiracy and sedition, charging Tomas Harker with printing libel against the Marlow Bend Mill Company and its officers, and charging — the language had a lawyerly delicacy that made Rosalind's stomach turn — *an assistant to the said printer, identity to be established upon apprehension.*

Upon apprehension. As though she were a fugitive already. As though the ink on her fingers were evidence, which of course it was.

She stood at the window above the shop and watched the town absorb the blow. Mrs. Pell from the chandlery crossed the street twice to avoid passing the courthouse steps. Two men in mill coats stood outside Brack's office with their arms folded, not talking, just standing — sentries, or an audience waiting for a show. A constable Rosalind didn't recognize nailed a notice to the post by the ferry landing, and even at this distance she could read the official flourish of it, the tidy bureaucratic hand that would write down whatever it was told and call it history.

The bridge was open. That was the thing her mind kept returning to, circling it like a bird over carrion. The bridge had reopened nine days early, the carpenters still hammering as the last plank went down, and the first cart had crossed on Tuesday morning with a load of coal and a driver who doffed his cap to nobody. The ferry was suspended again — unnecessary now — and the river ran brown and fast beneath the new timber, carrying winter out toward the sea.

And every hour, wagons came across it. Some carried goods. One, yesterday, had carried lawyers in grey suits who did not remove their hats indoors. Today it would carry, if the boy's account was right, officers with papers.

Rosalind counted what she had without meaning to. It was an old habit, the arithmetic of escape — how much money in the tin, what trains ran from the junction beyond the bend, whether her name on a warrant would follow her past the county line or die at the river's edge like so much of Marlow Bend's business did. She had money enough. She had a face that opened doors in towns where Edith Marchmont's did not. She had, if she left tonight, a good twelve hours' start on any man sent after her.

She had never once been arrested. In eleven years of driving for Edith — through locked gates and jeering crowds and one memorable night in a police station in Bristol that Edith had treated as a salon — Rosalind had always been the one outside the cell, holding the keys and the alibi and the clean reputation like tools in a kit. *Miss Joubert is merely my driver,* Edith would say, and Rosalind would nod, and the constable would nod, and the machinery of respectability would close over them both like water.

It had seemed, once, like safety. Now it felt like a costume she'd worn so long she'd forgotten she was wearing it.

* * *

Edith took the news standing up, which surprised no one.

They gathered in the Harkers' front room — Rosalind, Tomas with his bandaged right hand cradled against his waistcoat, old Mrs. Harker with her arms crossed, and Edith by the cold fireplace with her spine straight as a rule. Through the thin wall they could hear Constance Vane's voice next door, low and rapid, dictating something to someone; since her testimony she had taken a room at the inn rather than sleep under her father's roof, but she still conducted half her life by proxy, sending notes and messages like a general who had defected but not yet surrendered her signals corps.

"Conspiracy," Edith said, tasting the word. "Sedition. How flattering. In Manchester they only ever managed unlawful assembly."

"They managed a fortnight in Holloway," Rosalind said quietly.

"And I wrote two articles from my cell that were worth more than everything I'd published that year." Edith turned, and her eyes found Rosalind with the particular precision she reserved for the moments when she wanted something. "You are not named. You understand that. An unnamed assistant is a fiction the clerk invented because he couldn't be bothered to ask questions. If you cross that bridge tonight, there is nothing in the world that can touch you. No warrant follows a woman no one can describe."

Tomas looked up sharply. Mrs. Harker's arms tightened across her chest.

"That's the plan, then," Rosalind said. It came out flatter than she intended, and Edith — who missed nothing, who had built a movement on missing nothing — heard the flatness and misread it entirely.

"It isn't cruelty, Rosalind, it's arithmetic. We need the paper distributed in London. We need someone in the capital the moment the verdicts come down, someone the newspapers will actually print. You have contacts I've spent years cultivating and cannot use from a prison cell—"

"I know what we need."

"—and sentiment does not distribute pamphlets." Edith softened by exactly one degree, which for Edith was a great deal. "I am asking you to do the hardest thing, which is to leave while leaving is possible. I have asked it of braver people than you and they hated me for it, and they were right to hate me, and they did it anyway, and the cause survived."

"The cause," said Mrs. Harker, into the silence, in a voice like a dropped stone. "My son is dead and my other son has a broken hand and a warrant, and you stand in my parlor talking about the cause as if it were a horse you're trying to keep from going lame."

"Marian—" Tomas began.

"No. Let me say it, since no one else will." The old woman's voice didn't rise; that was the frightening thing about her, Rosalind had learned. Everything terrible she said arrived at conversational volume, wrapped in courtesy, like razors posted in envelopes. "You came here in fine clothes with fine ideas, Mrs. Marchmont, and I don't regret the shelter we gave you, because my Tomas believed in you and I believe in him. But don't stand there and tell that girl to run as if it were a gift you're giving her. It's a bill you're asking her to pay so your ledger balances."

The room went very still. Somewhere next door, Constance's voice had stopped.

Edith, to her credit, did not flinch. She regarded Marian Harker for a long moment with something almost like respect — the respect of one commander recognizing another — and then said, evenly, "You may be right. It changes nothing about what must be done."

"It never does," said Marian. "That's what makes people like us dangerous, isn't it? Nothing ever changes what must be done."

* * *

Rosalind walked out into the afternoon because the walls had become unbearable.

Marlow Bend in late thaw had a smell she'd come to know too well — river mud and coal smoke and the sour sweetness of the mill's dye vats, all of it cut now with the green insistence of things growing where they shouldn't, in gutters and roof-cracks, indifferent to magistrates. She walked past the courthouse without looking at it, which required effort; she walked past the ferry landing, where the new bridge stretched its fresh-timbered length across the water like a sentence waiting to be finished.

A woman was crossing it. Rosalind watched her go — a stranger, a traveling dress, a basket on her arm — walking away from Marlow Bend with the unhurried stride of someone whose name appeared on no document anywhere. Halfway across, the woman stopped to look at the water, then continued. By the far abutment she was just a figure again, and then not even that.

*That could be me tomorrow,* Rosalind thought. *That could be me tonight.*

She tried to feel what she supposed she ought to feel — fear, primarily, and under it the tug of duty toward London, toward Edith's network, toward the distribution lists and newspaper contacts that constituted, in Edith's cosmology, the difference between truth spoken and truth heard. And she did feel those things. Fear sat in her chest like swallowed river water, cold and heavy. Duty tugged at her like the current under the bridge.

But underneath both, steady as the type in her fingers, was something else, something that had been growing in her since the night she'd corrected a typo at a clandestine press and Tomas Harker had looked at her — not at Edith's driver, not at the observer, at *her* — and said, *good catch.*

She thought about the flour bin. On the worst night of their lives, with Brack's men breaking the press in the front room and Tomas bleeding on the floor, Rosalind had hidden the widows' original statements in the flour bin in the Harkers' kitchen, and no one had ever found them, and those pages — Hannah Pell's shaking hand, Widow Crouch's furious one, all of them signed with full names because courage, Rosalind had learned, was contagious — those pages were the true record, the one that mattered. She had protected them without being asked. Nobody had told her to. It had simply become obvious, somewhere between the first letter she'd steamed open years ago and the first widow she'd sat with last month, that some things were hers to carry now.

If she crossed that bridge, she carried them alone, into a city that would file them and lose them. If she stayed—

"You look like a woman doing sums," said a voice behind her.

Constance Vane stood on the landing in a grey coat, hatless, wind pulling at her hair, looking out of place and entirely unbothered by it. Since her testimony she had acquired a certain rawness around the eyes, as though she'd scrubbed off a varnish and hadn't yet decided what to wear instead. But her voice was as level as ever.

"I'm deciding whether to run," Rosalind said. There seemed little point in anything else.

"Ah." Constance came to stand beside her at the rail. Below them the river shoved past, brown and purposeful. "My father's lawyer says that if I recant, everything goes away. The books, the testimony, the unpleasantness. He said it very kindly, which is how I knew he meant it." She turned the words over like coins. "I spent twenty-nine years learning to be my father's daughter. It turns out I wasn't any good at it. Recanting would just be failing at something easier."

"Is that why you came? To tell me not to run?"

"I came because you're the only person in this town who reads the way I do, and I wanted to say it before one of us is in prison." Constance smiled faintly at the water. "We recognized each other at that charity event months ago, you know. Two women reading other people's papers. I've thought about it often since. What a strange, narrow fellowship that is. And how much wider it turned out to be." She straightened. "But yes. Also: don't run."

"You don't know what you're asking."

"I know precisely what I'm asking. I'm asking you to do what I did, which is to discover that the person you were keeping safe — the efficient one, the invisible one, the one who drives the carriage and seals the letters — was never anyone you wanted to be." Constance looked at her directly. "The warrant doesn't name you. That's not mercy, Rosalind. That's the establishment telling you that you don't exist. Every year of your life, someone has told you that you don't exist. The bridge is just the newest version of it."

Rosalind said nothing. The river filled the silence, and let her.

* * *

Evening came down over Marlow Bend like a hand closing.

In the kitchen, Marian Harker made supper as if warrants were weather, which was its own kind of sermon. Tomas sat with his splinted hand resting on the table and worked through the distribution list one-handed, writing names in his careful printer's script, and twice Rosalind caught him watching her with an expression he didn't trouble to hide — not pleading, exactly. Waiting. He had learned, in the hospital and since, that Rosalind Joubert could not be argued into anything; she could only arrive.

Edith had gone to the inn to confer with Constance and the lawyer who had come over on Tuesday's wagon, and the house felt larger for her absence, the way a room feels larger when a fire goes out.

Rosalind climbed to the back room and sat down at the case of type.

The forme for the reprint edition stood locked in its chase beside the lamp — the whole rebuilt edition, every widow's name, every ledger line, the final page set by her own hand two nights ago with Tomas dictating from memory, his broken hand useless in his lap, his voice steady as a metronome while she fumbled the letters into place and learned, line by line, a craft that had become a country. Her fingers knew where everything lived now. B and Q in their boxes, the thins and ems, the little steel world of it. Eleven years she had driven a carriage through other people's lives, reading everything and belonging nowhere, and it had taken a smashed press and a candle stub to teach her that hands could hold more than reins.

She picked up the composing stick. Set a line, almost idly, without thinking: *ROSALIND JOUBERT, PRINTER'S ASSISTANT.* Looked at it. The metal letters caught the lamplight and threw it back sharp and small, her own name rendered in something durable at last.

Then she broke the line apart, letter by letter, and put each piece home.

Not because she'd decided. Because she hadn't — not fully, not finally — and a typesetter learns early that you don't lock the chase until you're sure the line is true.

Below her, through the floorboards, she could hear Tomas moving around the shop below, putting the place in order for whatever came at dawn. Once he stopped, and she imagined him looking up through the ceiling at the sound of her type falling back into its boxes, and she found she could not tell whether the sound would read to him as farewell or as preparation.

She sat until the candle guttered, doing the last of the sums.

Money enough for London and beyond. Trains from the junction. Twelve hours' start, maybe more, because men with warrants moved at the speed of paperwork and she moved at the speed of a woman who had spent a decade learning how to leave places unnoticed. Against all of it: a flour bin, a forme in a chase, a broken-handed man downstairs who had given her an apron and called it a uniform, a town full of widows who had signed their names knowing what signatures cost.

And Edith's voice, patient and terrible: *an audience is still an audience.* Edith had stayed in Marlow Bend when the bridge went out because a trap, politely closed, was still a podium. Rosalind had thought it vanity at the time. She understood it better now. Staying was not always the failure to flee. Sometimes staying was the whole argument.

The candle died. In the dark she sat with her hands flat on the table, on either side of the empty composing stick, and listened to the river.

* * *

Dawn came grey and early, and with it, boots on the cobbles.

Rosalind was dressed — she had not truly slept — and at the window before the sound resolved itself, and saw them coming up the street from the ferry landing: four men, two in constable's coats and two in mill coats pretending not to be mill coats, walking with the unhurried confidence of men carrying paper that outruns resistance. They stopped at the corner. One consulted a document. Then, deliberately, unhurriedly, letting the whole street see, they turned toward the print shop.

Downstairs, a chair scraped. Tomas's voice, quiet: "Marian. The back stairs."

Rosalind was already moving.

She took the stairs two at a time into the kitchen, where Marian stood at the flour bin with the lid in her hands and an expression that asked nothing. Between them, wrapped in oilcloth, lay the widows' statements — the original pages, the true ones, signed in full. Rosalind gathered them against her chest. Through the wall came the knock at the shop door, formal and heavy, and then Pike's voice — Aldous Pike, who owed Tomas his life, who had filed the report that ended his own career — saying the words that made it real:

"Tomas Harker, I have a warrant for your arrest. Open the door."

Marian's hand found Rosalind's arm, gripped once, hard. "The bridge," the old woman whispered. "It's open. Go now, girl, and don't you dare look back—"

But Rosalind was looking at the back door, and past it at the yard, and past the yard at the gap between buildings where the river showed silver-grey through the morning mist, and at the long timbered span crossing it — open, free, unguarded, a road out of Marlow Bend wide enough for a woman carrying nothing but her own clean, unwritten name.

The knock came again.

She stood in the doorway with the statements held to her chest, one foot on the threshold, and the whole of her life narrowed to the width of that doorframe — flight on one side of it, family on the other — and the seconds went by like planks underfoot, each one bearing her weight and creaking.

Behind her, in the shop, she heard the bolt drawn back.

And Rosalind Joubert stepped—
