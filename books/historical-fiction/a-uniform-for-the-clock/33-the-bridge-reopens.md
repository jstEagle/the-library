## 33. The Bridge Reopens

The bridge was finished three days before anyone had promised it would be, and the town came out to look at it the way people look at a wound that has finally stopped bleeding — with gratitude and a certain suspicion that it might open again.

Rosalind stood at the near end of the span in the grey light of an April morning, hands in the pockets of the coat Edith had once called "serviceable," which was Edith's word for anything Rosalind owned that she did not herself choose. Below her the river ran brown and swollen still, but obedient now, held between its stone banks as though the winter's violence had been a misunderstanding both parties wished forgotten. The new timber of the decking was pale where the old wood had been dark, and the joints were visible — fresh planks scarfed into old ones, the repair legible to anyone who cared to look. Rosalind cared to look. Rosalind had spent two years caring to look at things other people preferred seamless.

A cart went by first. Then a woman with a market basket, walking quickly, not looking down through the gaps at the water. Then two mill men in their Sunday coats, though it was Thursday, heading for the ferry landing on the far side and the train beyond it, and one of them glanced back at Marlow Bend with an expression Rosalind recognized because she had worn it herself for years: the look of a person leaving a room quietly and hoping no one asks them what happened in it.

*They're crossing to safety,* she thought, and heard the sentence in her own voice, flat and factual, the voice she used for reading aloud documents that required no comment.

Because that was the arithmetic of this morning, and everyone in the crowd understood it without saying so. The warrants had issued on Tuesday. Edith Marchmont, Tomas Harker, and *the printer's assistant, name unknown* — that last phrase typed onto the official form with a clerk's tidy indifference, as though anonymity were a garment one could simply decline to wear. The magistrate's inquiry sat in the courthouse like a clock someone had wound and walked away from, ticking toward verdicts already drafted. And now the bridge stood repaired, the ferry running, the road south open and drying in the spring wind, and every person named on those warrants had before them a plain choice dressed up as a simple one: cross, or stay.

Edith would stay; that was never in question. A trial was a platform, and Edith Marchmont had never in her life declined a platform. Tomas would stay because his press was here, or what remained of it, and because his brother lay in the churchyard under a stone that said only HARKER, the ledger's habit of namelessness following the dead even into consecrated ground.

But Rosalind. Rosalind was *the printer's assistant*, and the assistant, unlike the principals, had options. No one had ever photographed her. Her name appeared nowhere in Edith's papers — she had made certain of that years ago, out of the same instinct that had taught her to steam open envelopes and leave them looking untouched. She could walk across that bridge this morning, board the afternoon train, and be in London by Friday with nothing against her but her own knowledge. She could go back to being invisible. Invisibility, she had learned, was a kind of safety that felt almost exactly like freedom if you didn't examine it too closely.

She had been examining it very closely indeed since Tuesday night.

* * *

The thought had come to her first as it always came — practically, coldly, itemized like a packing list.

You are not needed, the list began. The statements are hidden; you memorized them anyway, all forty-one pages, the way you memorized everything, because memory was the first vault you ever owned. Tomas can recite the edition in his sleep — he proved that from a hospital bed with two fingers bandaged together, dictating while Rosalind's hands shook over the type tray. Constance Vane will testify whether or not Rosalind is in the gallery; a woman who has decided to burn her own name off her father's books does not require an audience. Pike has filed his report; whatever becomes of him now is between Pike and his conscience, and neither needs her.

And Edith — Edith, who had looked at the smashed press and said, *I have been counting lives like coins and calling it strategy* — Edith did not need a driver anymore. That was the thing beneath the thing. For six years Rosalind had been useful: hands on the wheel, eyes on the letters, a presence so reliable it became furniture. But usefulness was not the same as belonging, and the bridge offered her the oldest bargain of her life — the same one she'd been taking since girlhood, when reading other people's mail taught her that it was safer to know everything and be known by nothing.

Cross, said the list. Cross, and live.

Then, on Wednesday morning, Mrs. Ellery the widow had come to the shop — the widow who had demanded her full name in print, Sarah Ellery, née Fenn, forty-one years old, husband Daniel killed in the carding room in January of 1911 and recorded in the accident ledger as *damage to machinery, minor* — and found Rosalink setting type alone, and put her hand flat on the composing stone the way other women touched altars.

"They'll ask me to say it was an accident," Mrs. Ellery said. "At the inquiry. They've already sent a man round twice, polite as you please, offering to settle the pension arrears if I keep my answers short." She looked at Rosalind then, and her eyes were dry and terrible. "I can hold out in the witness box. I've held out eleven years. But I'll tell you what keeps me upright at night. It's knowing there'll be a record that isn't theirs. You printed my Daniel's name spelled right. Nobody in authority ever bothered to spell it right."

*I read your letters too,* Rosalind had almost said. *I read everything.* But the confession belonged to Tomas first, and Tomas had given his grudging grace weeks ago — *I'm not going to pretend that doesn't sit wrong in me, but I watched you set type till four in the morning with your hands cut, and I reckon a person is more than their worst habit* — and so instead she had said only: "It will be in the record. However they sand it down, we'll print it again. As many times as they sand it."

Mrs. Ellery nodded once, satisfied, and left, and Rosalind stood at the case with a capital E in her hand and understood that something in her had already decided, some committee of the self convening and voting without informing the rest of her.

* * *

Now it was Thursday, and the bridge was open, and she stood at its head watching the exodus.

More people crossed than she expected. Not fugitives — ordinary people, mill families with bundles and children, a draper locking his shop behind him, a young couple who paused at the midpoint to look back and then, as if ashamed, did not look again. The flood season had stranded hopes as well as bodies; there were engagements waiting in other towns, jobs, mothers ill, apprenticeships lapsed. Marlow Bend had held its people prisoner politely all winter, and now the door was open and they filed through it.

But others stayed. That was the thing that undid her, standing there in the wind off the water. An old man she knew by sight from the shop — he bought their cheap job-printing, funeral cards mostly — stood on the near bank with his cap in his hands and watched the crossing with an expression of quiet satisfaction, as though staying were itself a small act of testimony. A cluster of mill girls in shawls stood near the toll house, and when a foreman — not Brack, Brack had fled after the paper stock burned, but one of Brack's lesser creatures — walked past them toward the bridge, they did not move aside quickly enough, and he had to pass through them, and none of them looked at him, and he hurried.

The town was choosing, publicly, in the open air, and most of them were choosing to remain in a place that might yet cost them everything, because remaining was also a way of speaking.

"Deciding which side of the water you're on?"

Pike had come up beside her without sound, as he always did, his constable's boots worn soft. He looked tired in a way that had passed beyond exhaustion into something settled, almost peaceful. His resignation letter was in his pocket; Rosalind knew because he'd shown her, in the manner of a man showing a doctor a symptom.

"I'm deciding," Rosalind said, "which version of myself gets to survive."

"Mm." Pike looked out at the river. "I spent twenty years thinking those were the same decision. Stay in uniform, keep your head down, soften what you write, and call it survival." He turned the cap in his hands. "Then I wrote one report the way it should have been written, and lost everything except myself. Best trade I ever made. Worst morning of my life." He put the cap on. "For what it's worth — nobody over there is going to thank you for crossing. And everybody over there—" he nodded back toward the town, the courthouse with its flag limp on its pole, "—already does."

"That's not fair," Rosalind said. "To make it about gratitude."

"No," Pike agreed. "It's not. It's about the record. You taught me that, or the Harkers did, or the widows — somebody here did, I've lost track." He touched his hat brim. "I'll be in the gallery tomorrow, first row, where they can see me. Retired constables make excellent scenery."

He walked away toward the courthouse, unhurried, a man going to stand witness at his own funeral and finding he rather liked the flowers.

* * *

Rosalind stayed on the bridge.

She walked out to the middle, where the new planks met the old, and stood over the water with her hands gripping the rail. The current below was strong still, pulling southward toward the world — toward trains and London and Edith's national movement with its committees and its careful arithmetic, toward the version of history in which Rosalind Joubert was a footnote at best, a driver, a pair of hands on a wheel, *name unknown*. The river would carry her there easily. It wanted to. Rivers always want to.

And behind her, northward, the courthouse clock showed half past ten, and above the print shop the rebuilt loft window caught the light, and somewhere in the churchyard a stone said HARKER with no Christian name, and in a flour bin in a ruined shop forty-one pages of testimony waited in a widow's own handwriting for someone to read them aloud into the official record, slowly, clearly, under her own full name.

Love against duty — she had framed it that way for weeks, in the privacy of her head, and it had felt suitably tragic, suitably literary. Standing on the bridge she saw it was wrongly framed altogether. There was no love pulling her backward and duty pulling her forward. It was duty on both banks, and the only question was which duty she recognized as hers: the duty to be safe, which had governed her whole life and called itself prudence, or the duty to be *counted* — to stand in the witness box as Rosalind Joubert, printer's assistant, reader of letters, and let the record show that the people who kept honest accounts in this country were not the magistrates.

Tomas had told her once, handing her the apron: *Everyone here wears the same uniform; the clock doesn't care who you were before.* She understood now that the reverse was also true — that the moment you put it on, everyone was entitled to know exactly who you were. That was the price of the apron, and she had paid it gladly in the shop, and now the courthouse was asking for the same payment in public.

The wind came up the river valley, smelling of thawed earth and coal smoke, and it pushed at her back, toward the town, as if even the weather had taken a side.

Rosalind Joubert turned around.

She walked back off the bridge the way she had come, past the toll house, past the mill girls who watched her do it — one of them, she noticed, smiled — and up the high street toward the courthouse steps, where a clerk was posting the morning's order of proceedings, and the first line of it, in the magistrate's own tidy type, read:

*In re: the deaths occurring at Marlow Bend Mills, 1909–1913.*

Not *labor conditions*. Not *the matter of certain pamphlets*. Deaths. Occurring. Someone had already begun correcting the record, and it was not the magistrate.

Rosalind stopped at the bottom of the steps, took out the list in her head, and burned it item by item — not needed, not named, not missed — until there was nothing left but her own name, spoken inwardly in full for perhaps the first time in years: *Rosalind Joubert. Of the print shop. On the record.*

Then she went inside to find out how late one could enter one's name on a witness list, and whether a court that had planned a tidy week could be made, overnight, to hear everything.
