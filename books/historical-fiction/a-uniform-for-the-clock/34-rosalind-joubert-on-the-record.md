## 34. Rosalind Joubert, On the Record

The courthouse at Marlow Bend had been a wool warehouse before the mill bought it and gave it columns, and it still smelled faintly of lanolin and old sweat beneath the lemon oil the clerk had applied for the occasion. Rosalind noticed this as she waited in the corridor outside the inquiry room, because noticing things was what she did — had always done, in secret, in service of other people's secrets. Today she would do it in the open.

Her hands were steady. That surprised her. She had expected them to tremble all morning, had imagined herself gripping the rail of the witness box like a drowning woman grips driftwood. Instead her fingers lay quiet in her lap, and she understood why: for the first time in eleven years, there was nothing left to hide them from.

The corridor filled slowly. Widows in their good black dresses, hats brushed but old, arriving in twos and threes like birds returning to a hedgerow they had been driven from. Mrs. Aldersey with her crooked umbrella. The young widow Prewitt, whose husband had been twenty-three when the carding room took him. They nodded to Rosalind as they passed — not the nod one gives a stranger, not even the nod one gives an acquaintance, but something smaller and more deliberate, a dip of the chin that said *we know why you are here*.

Edith Marchmont sat on the bench nearest the doors, flanked by the London lawyers she had finally summoned, and watched Rosalind with an expression that was difficult to read. Pride, perhaps. Or the particular anxiety of a woman who has spent decades calculating outcomes and has just realized the most important variable has stopped taking her advice.

"You don't have to give them everything," Edith had said last night, in the Harkers' back parlor, her voice low and reasonable, the voice of a woman who had talked a hundred frightened people into braver courses than they'd intended. "The statements will speak. Let the widows be the story."

"They *are* the story," Rosalind had answered. "But someone has to say how the statements came to exist. Someone has to say they were taken down word for word, kept safe through a raid, copied by candlelight by a man with broken hands. If I stand up there and present them like a conjuror presenting a dove, Vane's counsel will pick them apart before luncheon. Provenance matters, Edith. You taught me that."

"I taught you discretion."

"You taught me that records win. You said it in Sheffield in 1909, when they tried to call our minutes forgeries. You said: *the side that keeps honest paper wins.*" She had held Edith's gaze then, as she held it now through the closing door. "I am keeping honest paper, Edith. It is only that the paper is not ours alone anymore."

The bailiff called her name.

Not *the secretary*. Not *Miss Marchmont's driver*. Not *a witness for the town*, which was how the summons had read, drafted by men who assumed she would prefer anonymity the way one assumes a lady prefers a fan. The clerk had asked her, twice, whether she wished to be entered under any other description, and both times Rosalind Joubert had given the same answer, and both times she had felt the strange pleasure of the syllables leaving her mouth in public: *Joubert. R-o-s-a-l-i-n-d J-o-u-b-e-r-t.* Her mother's name, her father's name, hers alone, spoken into the record of a river town she had meant to leave in October.

The courtroom was fuller than it had been on the first day of proceedings, when the magistrate — a dry, punctual man named Havelock, whose spectacles caught the gaslight like coins — had opened with remarks about establishing "an accurate account of labor conditions" that everyone understood to mean *a short one*. Now the benches were crowded with mill families, with printers' apprentices let off early, with Constance Vane sitting apart in grey, her gloved hands folded, wearing the face of a woman attending a funeral she had helped to arrange.

Rosalind walked the length of the room and did not look at anyone except the magistrate, because she had learned long ago that looking at people while telling hard truths was a courtesy you paid them, and today she intended to pay every courtesy she owed.

She took the oath on the Bible offered her, her hand flat on the worn cover, and when the clerk asked her name for the record she gave it clearly, spelling it out letter by letter, and heard it scratch onto the page behind him — the small, dry sound of ink becoming history.

"State your occupation," said Mr. Greave, counsel for the mill, a heavy man with a heavy watch-chain, who had spent two weeks sanding testimony smooth.

"I am a chauffeuse," Rosalind said. "And since November, a compositor." A murmur moved along the benches; the word *compositor* meant something different here than it did in London drawing rooms, and everyone in the room knew it. "Before either, I was a reader."

"A reader?"

"I read. Professionally and otherwise. For eleven years I have read every letter that passed through my employer's household — steamed open, transcribed where necessary, resealed. I am telling you this myself, Mr. Greave, so that you needn't spend your afternoon discovering it."

Greave's smile was thin and prepared; he had clearly been saving this discovery like a coin in his pocket. "You confess, then, to a systematic violation of the privacy of—"

"I confess to nothing you can charge," Rosalind said, "because the letters concerned are in this room's interest now, not mine. But yes. I read everything. I have read everything since I was nineteen years old and learned that the world tells its truth in envelopes." She turned slightly toward the magistrate, addressing Havelock directly, because he was the man keeping the record and the record was the whole point. "I want it understood why. Not excused — understood."

Havelock's pen paused. "Proceed."

"When I began reading other people's letters, I did it out of fear. I was nobody — a driver, a servant, a woman with no family and no standing, employed by a woman whose enemies would have liked very much to ruin her. Reading was armor. If I knew what was coming, I could protect us both." Her voice held level; she had rehearsed none of this aloud, but she found it came easily, the way a road comes back to you after years. "Somewhere in those years, the fear became habit, and the habit became something else. I learned that towns like this one keep two sets of records — not the mill owner and his daughter, though they keep theirs too, but the town itself. There is the record the coroner writes, and there is the record the widow knows. There is the accident ledger, and there is the kitchen table. And for as long as I have been alive, the second record has been considered unfit for filing."

She lifted her satchel — Tomas had oiled its buckles specially, the night before, his splinted fingers clumsy with care — and drew out the folder of statements.

"These are testimonies. Fourteen of them, sworn before Constable Pike on dates between the fourth of December and the second of March, each signed by its maker. They concern deaths at Marlow Mills over the past six winters. I will read them in full."

"Mr. Chairman," Greave rose, "these so-called statements were collected by interested parties, transcribed by persons unknown, in circumstances—"

"In circumstances, Mr. Greave," Rosalind said, without heat, "of a flood that closed your bridge, a press that your client's foreman smashed, and a printer currently lying in the cottage hospital with four broken fingers. The circumstances are precisely why the statements exist. Had the post run freely, they would have gone to London instead, and you would never have seen them. You may thank the weather."

There was laughter — quickly suppressed, but real — from the gallery, and Havelock rapped his gavel and did not look entirely displeased about it.

"Read your statements," he said.

* * *

She read for two hours.

She read Mrs. Aldersey's account of the night in January 1909 when two men carried her husband home wrapped in sacking and told her he had slipped on the loading dock, though the sacking smelled of the boiler house and his burns said otherwise. She read the widow Prewitt's statement, taken in a voice that shook until Rosalind had asked her to dictate rather than write, and then had not shaken at all — *he went into the carding room a boy and they brought me out a parcel*. She read the statement of a man named Oakes, who described the winter count — the tally kept in the foreman's office, marks on slate, wiped clean each spring — and whose own brother's mark had been among the wiped.

She read names. All of them, spelled correctly, which required her to pause three times to confirm pronunciations with the gallery, and which she did without embarrassment, because getting a dead man's name wrong in front of his widow was the one failure she refused to risk.

When she reached the final statement — the widow Hartley's, the one who had refused to speak at all unless printed under her full name — Rosalind looked up and found the woman herself standing at the back of the room, upright in black, hands clasped at her waist, watching. Rosalind read her name aloud, first and last, and Mrs. Hartley inclined her head a half-inch, and Rosalind thought: *this is what courage does. It stands at the back of rooms and waits for you to catch up.*

Then she laid the folder down, and faced Greave.

* * *

He was good. She had known he would be.

"So," Greave said, circling back after his objections were overruled, "you admit to years of clandestine correspondence-reading. You are the confidential agent of a woman under warrant for sedition. You lodged with a family operating an illegal press. And we are to accept that your collection of — forgive me — *kitchen-table gossip* constitutes evidence?"

"Mr. Greave," Rosalind said, "in eleven years of reading other people's mail I have developed one professional skill above all others: I can tell a true letter from a false one. I have read love letters written by people who despise each other and business letters written by thieves, and the true ones always sound like this—" she touched the folder "—and the false ones always sound like the mill's accident ledger, which your own junior clerk confirmed last week was ruled in a hand inconsistent with its dated entries. I did not write these statements. I wrote them *down*. The difference is the whole difference, and the widows will swear to it, and Constable Pike has already sworn to it, and your client's daughter has put her signature to ledgers that agree with them to the shilling."

She saw Constance, at the edge of her vision, sit very still.

"And one thing more." Rosalind turned to Havelock. "You asked at the opening of this inquiry for an accurate account. I want to place one more item on the record — my own account, freely given. On the night of the raid, I concealed original documents in a flour bin in the Harker kitchen while Foreman Brack's men destroyed the press upstairs. Those documents are the originals of the statements just read. I hid them because hiding them was right, and I will not pretend otherwise, and if the law wishes to charge me, my name is in your book already, spelled correctly, and I will answer to it."

The silence afterward had weight, like deep water. Somewhere in the gallery a woman began, quietly, to weep, and no one hushed her.

Havelock wrote for a long moment. Then he removed his spectacles, polished them on his sleeve — the first human gesture Rosalind had seen him make — and replaced them.

"The statements are received," he said. "Entered in full. Names included." He looked at Rosalind over the rims of his glasses, and something in his dry face shifted, almost imperceptibly, toward respect. "This inquiry was convened, Miss Joubert, to establish what happened in this town. You have made it considerably more difficult for it to conclude otherwise."

"That," said Rosalind Joubert, stepping down from the box in her plain coat with her oil-stained hands visible at her sides, "was the intention."

She walked back up the aisle through a room that was rising to its feet — not cheering, not yet, but rising, widow by widow, printer by printer, the way Mrs. Hartley had stood at the back — and as she reached the doors she saw the clerk dip his pen again, and heard the small dry sound of her name going onto the page a second time, entered now not as a witness merely, but as the author of the record.

Outside, the thaw had come early, and the river ran loud and brown and free beneath the repaired span. In the corridor, Tomas Harker was waiting, his bandaged hands cradled like something newly hatched, and he did not say anything at all — only held out, gravely, the folded proof sheet of tomorrow's edition, on which the headline stood in type she had set herself:

*THE RECORD, CORRECTED.*

And pinned beneath the courthouse notice board, fresh ink still shining, a new writ of summons: the magistrate's clerk calling next — *one Constance Vane, to give evidence concerning the books of Marlow Mills.*
