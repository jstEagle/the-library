## 35. Verdicts

The verdict came down on a Tuesday, and by Wednesday the whole town had an opinion about it.

Rosalind heard the news the way news traveled in Marlow Bend that spring: not from a newspaper, which still could not be trusted, but from the mouths of women coming out of the baker's shop with loaves tucked under their arms like infants. The mill owner — Mr. Ambrose Vane himself, whose name had been spoken for years only in lowered voices or not at all — had been indicted. Not convicted. Indicted. There was a difference, and everyone understood it, and everyone said the word *indicted* anyway, slowly, tasting it, because it was the first word of its kind anyone had ever been able to say about that house on the hill.

She stood in the doorway of the rebuilt print shop with a proof sheet in one hand and watched Mrs. Ellery cross the square at her usual furious pace, then stop, mid-stride, as though she had forgotten where her feet were taking her. Then Mrs. Ellery turned around entirely and walked back toward the courthouse steps, where a small crowd had gathered to do nothing in particular — just to stand there, in the pale April light, looking up at the building as if it were a church.

*Indicted.* Rosalind said it herself, quietly. It sounded like something being unwrapped.

Behind her, inside the shop, the borrowed press sat where it had lived for three weeks now, smelling of someone else's oil and someone else's ink. Tomas's hands were still bandaged. He had learned to pull proofs left-handed, badly, and had declared the results "characterful." Constance Vane's copied ledgers lay locked in the flat file drawer beneath the stone, wrapped in oilcloth, waiting for whatever came next. Everything waited now. The inquiry had adjourned for deliberation, and the town had discovered that deliberation was worse than any testimony — all those weeks of speaking, and then silence, and then this: a word passed mouth to mouth through a bakery queue.

Rosalind went back inside and set the proof sheet down on the stone.

"Vane's indicted," she said.

Tomas did not look up from the galley he was squaring with his knuckles, awkwardly, using the edge of his palm where the fingers wouldn't bend. "I know."

"You know?"

"Pike came by at seven this morning." A ghost of a smile. "He stood out front for ten minutes pretending to inspect our signage. Then he told us through the door." Tomas set the galley down carefully. "He said he wanted us to hear it before it was in the papers. His words: 'You'll want to print it right.'"

Rosalind looked at him. "That man is going to give himself a hernia, bending that far."

"He's given himself worse things already." Tomas flexed his bandaged hand, testing it, wincing, testing again — a habit now, morning and night, like prayer. "An indictment isn't a verdict, Rosalind. Don't let them start celebrating yet. The assizes sit in June. There'll be lawyers, and money, and a jury brought in from somewhere that's never smelled a dye-house."

"I know."

"But?" He caught something in her voice.

"But they read the names yesterday," she said. "In open court. All of them. The clerk read the winter count into the record — every name, spelled correctly, because I checked the list myself against the widows' statements before I handed it up." Her throat tightened unexpectedly; she made herself go on. "Forty-one names, Tomas. Read aloud in a courtroom, into a stenographer's machine, so they exist now in official ink. Whatever happens in June — whether he walks or hangs — those names are in the record. Nobody can sand them out without it showing."

Tomas was quiet a moment. Then he reached over with his good hand and squeezed her shoulder once, hard, and let go, and that was the entire ceremony. In the Harker family, Rosalind had learned, grief and joy were both handled the same way: briefly, firmly, and without commentary, because commentary invited collapse and there was always work.

"Set the headline," Tomas said. "Big as the forme allows. And spell his name right too. We're not the ones who leave things off records."

* * *

Brack ran on Thursday.

Not fled — ran. There was a difference, and Marlow Bend, which had spent two generations learning the mill owner's vocabulary, took a certain pleasure in insisting on it. Fleeing was what gentlemen did when scandal threatened; it involved steam tickets and letters of introduction and a dignified absence later explained as *health*. Running was what Brack did, because Brack had never been a gentleman no matter how many men he'd broken, and running meant a cart at four in the morning loaded with what he could carry and a horse whipped too hard on the north road and a foreman's coat left hanging in the mill office with the brass buttons still on it.

Constable Aldous Pike reported it himself, in person, standing in the print shop doorway with his hat in his hands like a schoolboy called before the headmaster. He had grown almost comfortable coming here, Rosalind thought — or had grown comfortable with being seen coming here, which in Pike's economy amounted to the same thing and cost him more.

"Left a note," Pike said. He produced it, unfolded it, held it out to Tomas rather than reading it aloud, as if the words might contaminate the air. Tomas read it and passed it to Rosalind without expression.

*You'll hear I done wrong and maybe I did,* the note said, in a laborious carpenter's hand. *But you wasn't down in the pit yard the winter the belts went. Ask Vane who signed the order to keep the line running. It weren't me that owned the mill.*

"It's a confession that blames everybody but himself," Rosalind said.

"It's a confession," Pike said. "First words he ever put on paper about any of it. That counts for something in a court, or it don't, but it counts for something." He put his hat back on, took it off again. "I've wired ahead to Northampton and to the coast. He won't get a boat. Man like Brack, he doesn't know how to be anywhere he isn't known. He'll surface inside a week, wanting his grievance heard by somebody."

"And if he doesn't?" Tomas asked. "If he stays gone? Is that justice?"

Pike considered this with the unhurried honesty that had become, over these strange months, his most disarming quality. He looked out at the street, where two of the widow women were watching the shop door with frank interest — they had appointed themselves an informal honor guard since the raid, taking turns, armed with nothing but presence and, in Mrs. Cotterill's case, a rolling pin she claimed was for the bakery.

"No," Pike said finally. "It's not justice. Justice would be him standing in that dock answering for Danny Harker and the thirty-nine others while the whole town watches. Him running means we got the truth on the record and the man himself still loose in the world, and both those things are true at once, and I've stopped expecting the world to sort itself into one column or the other." He settled the hat at last. "But I'll tell you what I told the magistrate. For eleven years I wrote reports saying nothing happened in Marlow Bend. Now the reports say everything. If that's the only justice I get to deliver in my career, it's more than I earned and I'll take it."

He left. Through the window Rosalind watched him pause on the pavement to nod to Mrs. Cotterill, who regarded him with the rolling pin visibly lowered by perhaps two inches, which from her was a knighthood.

"Ambrose Vane's lawyer will say Brack acted alone," Rosalind said. "That note hands them the argument gift-wrapped."

"Yes," Tomas said. "And Constance's ledgers will say otherwise. She kept dates, Rosalind. Dates and initials. Brack couldn't have ordered a broom without Vane's initials beside it." He looked down at his bandaged hands. "They'll try. Let them try. We've got a press and a town that reads now. That's more than most men ever take into a courtroom."

* * *

Edith Marchmont received her sentence on the Friday, and received it, Rosalind thought, like a bouquet.

The charge was obstruction — concealing evidence, impeding the inquiry's early proceedings, the technical architecture of everything Edith had actually done, stripped of its grandeur and dressed in the plain gray clothes of statute. The magistrate, a dry man named Hollowell who had arrived with the inquiry and would leave with it and remembered nothing of Marlow Bend fondly, pronounced six weeks' imprisonment and a fine, and delivered the sentence in the tone of a man closing a ledger he wished had never been opened.

Edith stood to receive it in a gray suit chosen, Rosalind suspected, precisely for the photograph. When Hollowell finished, she thanked him. Thanked him. And then, before the court officer could reach her elbow, she turned to the gallery — packed, that day, with half the working ward of Marlow Bend — and said, clearly, in the voice she used from platforms:

"Gentlemen of the press will wish to note that the movement's funds remain intact and its officers remain at their posts. Six weeks is ample time to finish my book. I thank the court for the quiet."

Someone in the back laughed — one short bark — and was shushed, and then several people laughed together at the shushing, and Hollowell banged his gavel at a room that had already stopped listening.

Rosalind watched from the third row, between Mrs. Cotterill and a dye-house girl named Ada Fenwick whose brother's name was fourth on the winter count. She had come intending, she realized, to feel something complicated: anger at the arithmetic Edith had once done in her hearing — *martyrs serve better than witnesses*, overheard through a door, a sentence Rosalind had carried for weeks like a splinter — tangled up with gratitude for everything Edith had built and everything she had finally, smashingly, given to Marlow Bend when the press lay broken and Tomas lay bleeding. But what she mostly felt was tired affection. Edith Marchmont had been given six weeks in Hollowell's custody and had converted it, within nine seconds, into publicity, platform, and copy. Some women were born to be martyrs. Edith had been born to be a publisher of martyrs, and she would run the movement from a cell in Hollowell's custody the way other women ran households — thoroughly, tyrannically, and by correspondence.

Afterward, in the corridor, permitted five minutes under a matron's eye, Edith gripped Rosalind's hands hard enough to leave marks.

"You look terrible," Edith said fondly. "Have you been sleeping, or only typesetting?"

"Both, badly."

"Good. Nothing worth doing looks healthy." Her eyes went over Rosalind's shoulder to where Tomas waited near the doors, flanked by his solicitor — the movement's solicitor, sent down from London within a day of Edith's change of heart, paid from funds Edith had pledged by letter from her cell before she'd even been assigned one. "His hearing is Monday. Tell him—" She stopped. Edith Marchmont, who had sentences prepared for parliaments, stopped, and started again. "Tell him the movement does not abandon its own. Tell him whatever Monday brings, the appeal is already drafted, the counsel retained, and the funds—"

"He knows, Edith. I've told him. You've written him twice."

"Three times. He didn't answer the third."

"He was setting type one-handed. He answers everyone eventually. It's how he prays."

Edith's grip tightened. For a moment something moved behind the famous composure — not weakness; Rosalind had learned better than to call it that — something more like the fear of a general on the eve of a battle she could no longer command from the field. "Six weeks," Edith said. "A great deal can go wrong in six weeks."

"A great deal can go right," Rosalind said. "I've read your mail for four years, Edith. I know exactly what you're capable of in less time than that."

Edith laughed — a real laugh, startled out of her — and the matron cleared her throat, and the five minutes ended. At the door, turning back for one last instruction, Edith pointed at Rosalind with the finger she used for punctuation.

"The paper," she said. "Keep printing it. While I'm inside, *The Winter Count* speaks for the movement in this county. Write it accordingly. And Rosalind—" the voice dropped, just for her, "—the byline. Put names on it. Yours. Tomas's. The widows'. No more anonymous sheets. Anonymous is how they beat us. Named is how we win even when we lose."

Then the matron's hand was on her arm, and Edith Marchmont walked out of the courthouse upright, chin level, into a small crowd that had waited an hour in the cold to see her do it, and Rosalind stood in the corridor and understood that she had just witnessed Edith's idea of a triumphal procession, and that the horrible thing, the thing Rosalind would never fully forgive and never stop admiring, was that it worked.

* * *

Tomas Harker's trial lasted one day.

The charge was seditious libel — printing matter tending to incite disorder — arising from the first clandestine issues of *The Winter Count*, the ones run on the hidden press behind the wallpaper samples, before any of it had a name on it. The prosecution's case was thin and knew it: two confiscated copies, an informant's statement about a coffin, and Foreman Brack, absent, whose sworn deposition was read aloud in his stead and collapsed under twenty minutes of the London solicitor's patient, surgical questioning. The defense called the widows. It called Constable Pike, who testified for forty minutes about everything he had failed to find in eleven years of searching, in such careful detail that the courtroom understood every failure as an inventory. It called Constance Vane, who took the oath in a black dress and answered in a voice like cold water, and when the prosecutor sneered — gently, expensively — about a daughter testifying against her father's interests, she looked at him and said, "Sir, I am testifying for the record. My father's interests have had eleven years of representation. I am here on behalf of the deceased."

The jury was out for fifty minutes.

They returned a conviction. Of course they did — the law was the law, and the sheets had been printed unlicensed, and juries in that era of England convicted printers the way rivers ran downhill. The foreman announced it apologetically, almost inaudibly, looking at the dock instead of the bench.

What followed, Rosalind would remember all her life.

The magistrate rose to pronounce sentence — and the gallery rose with him. Not shouting. Not disorderly, though the bailiffs braced for it. They simply stood: Mrs. Cotterill with her Sunday hat, Ada Fenwick with her brother's name pinned inside her collar, the dye-house men with their caps off, the widows in a row like a fence, and behind them, filling the back of the courtroom, people Rosalind didn't know — farmers from upriver, shopkeepers, two clergymen, a woman in mourning who had traveled, someone said, from three towns away because she had read the paper. Forty or fifty people standing in silence while the magistrate's sentence died in his throat.

He sat back down. He conferred with his clerk. He conferred again. And then Mr. Hollowell — dry, irritated, wishing himself anywhere else — performed the small miracle that magistrates perform when a room full of ordinary people makes law impossible to administer politely: he suspended the sentence. Bound over, good behavior, twelve months, the conviction entered and immediately neutered. "The defendant," Hollowell said acidly, glaring at the gallery as though they had personally inconvenienced him, "will consider the tolerance of this court to be exhausted."

Tomas, asked if he had anything to say, said only: "Thank you, sir. The next edition comes out Thursday, and every name will be spelled correctly."

The laughter this time was not shushed. It filled the courtroom like water finding its level, and Hollowell did not bang his gavel, because there was nothing left to bang it against.

* * *

That evening the shop was full and loud and lit too brightly, someone's accordion going badly in the corner, Mrs. Cotterill presiding over a table of food that could have fed the inquiry. Rosalind escaped eventually to the little room above the stairs where she slept, and stood at the window with her coat still on, watching the lampposts come on along the square.

Imperfect justice, she thought. Vane indicted, not convicted — the trial months away, the money already moving. Brack gone into the world with a confession that blamed everyone. Edith in a cell, triumphant. Tomas a convicted felon whose crime the entire town had attended the sentencing of as if it were a play. None of it clean. None of it the thing she had once imagined justice must be, back when she was a chauffeur reading stolen letters alone in parked motorcars — back when she had believed, without ever putting it into words, that somewhere existed a version of events that was simply *true*, and that if you got close enough to it, the world would have to agree.

The world did not agree. The world deliberated. The world handed down indictments and suspended sentences and let foremen run in the night. And yet — she pressed her forehead briefly against the cool glass — the names had been read into the record. The widows had stood. The town had stood. Something had shifted in the foundations of Marlow Bend that no verdict could pour back.

There was a knock at her door. Tomas, cap in his bandaged hands, looking embarrassed.

"We're doing the forme for Thursday tonight," he said. "Constance brought the last ledger page. And there's—" he hesitated, and Rosalind saw that beneath the embarrassment was something carefully concealed and enormous. "There's space on the front page, bottom corner. Edith says a paper should name its own. I've set the masthead. Come down and see who's on it."

Rosalind pulled on her coat. Downstairs, the accordion stumbled into a tune everyone seemed to know except her, and under the lamp the forme waited, locked and gleaming, and Tomas stepped aside to let her read the column of names set fresh in eight-point: *Edited by T. Harker. Reports by R. Joubert.*

Her own name. In metal. In ink that would not come out.

"Spell it right," she said, and her voice cracked on the last word, and Tomas — grinning, holding the composing stick like a scepter — said, "I checked it four times. Now come and learn the back page. You're setting it yourself, and the clock's running."
