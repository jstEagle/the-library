## 37. Letters Afterward

The letters began arriving in October, when the light over the river turned to brass and the first cold mornings made the windows of the Harker house bloom with frost-ferns that melted away by nine.

They came by the ordinary post, which was itself a kind of miracle. Rosalind had spent so many years of her life handling mail as though it were contraband — steaming envelopes open over a kettle's spout, reading what she was never meant to read, sealing each flap down with a paste so precisely matched to the gum beneath it that no eye in England could have told the difference — that the simple act of taking a letter from the morning basket and opening it in plain sight, at the kitchen table, with Tomas watching and Mrs. Harker kneading bread beside her, still produced a small private astonishment. She would slide her finger under the flap and feel something loosen in her chest, some old clamp she had carried so long she had forgotten its weight until it was gone.

*This one is mine to read*, she would think. *This one was sent to me.*

The first letter came from Margaret Ellery.

Rosalind knew the name before she broke the seal — of course she did; she had typed it herself in the final edition of *The Winter Count*, set it letter by letter in the borrowed press at Ashcombe, checking the spelling twice against the widow's own hand because every name had to be spelled correctly, that had been the whole point, the whole terrible beautiful point. Margaret Ellery, whose husband Daniel had gone into the carding room on a Tuesday in January two years past and come out feet-first with the foreman already composing his account of the accident before the body was cold. Margaret Ellery, who had stood up in the inquiry gallery when Rosalind read her statement aloud, and said, in a voice that did not shake nearly as much as Rosalind's hands had shaken holding the page: *That is my name, sir, and that is my husband's name, and I will say both of them myself if no one else will.*

The letter was four pages long and written in a round, careful schoolroom hand, the kind learned from a copybook and never abandoned.

*Dear Miss Joubert,* it began. *I do not know if you will remember me, but I remember you. I remember your voice most of all. It was not a grand voice like the magistrate's, nor a soft one like the vicar's when he read poor Daniel's service as though hurrying through a prayer for rain. It was an even voice. A voice that read my words as though they were worth the same as any lord's.*

*I am writing to tell you that I have taken work. You will perhaps be surprised to hear it is at the new mill office, doing the books for Mr. Vane's receivers, who are honest men or at least men being watched very closely now. I thought I should hate it, sitting among those ledgers. But I find I do not hate it at all. I sit where Constance Vane used to sit, and I keep the numbers true, and every column that balances feels like a small stone laid on a grave. The other women in the office call me Mrs. Ellery and stand straighter when they say it. They know what my name cost to put in print.*

*I have enclosed a second letter in this one. It is addressed to my sister in Leeds, who has not written since the inquiry, because her husband works at a mill there and is afraid. If you would be so kind as to post it yourself rather than leaving it to me — well. I think you will understand why a letter passing through certain hands might be safer coming from yours.*

Rosalind sat with that last paragraph for a long while.

There had been a time — not so long ago, though it felt like another woman's life — when such a request would have been impossible to make and impossible to answer. A time when Rosalind Joubert was a shadow attached to Edith Marchmont's motorcar, a competent nobody whose entire gift to the world was that she could be forgotten in a room. Now a widow in Marlow Bend trusted her with a letter the way you'd trust someone with a child. Not because Rosalind had power. Because Rosalind had *read* — had read everything, shamefully, hungrily, for years — and out of all that trespassing had finally built the one thing she'd never had: a reputation for knowing what letters carry, and carrying them anyway.

She posted Margaret Ellery's letter herself, walking it to the post office on the high street, and she wrote back the same evening:

*Dear Mrs. Ellery — I remember you. I expect I always will. Your sister's letter has gone under its proper cover, and I have answered yours properly too, openly, with my name at the bottom where anyone may see it. I find I like writing my name. It took me thirty-one years to learn the trick of it. — Yours, Rosalind Joubert*

* * *

Constance Vane's letters came from abroad, and they were different creatures entirely.

The first arrived from Florence in November, written on hotel stationery thick as linen, in that swift slanted script Rosalind remembered from the charity event two winters ago — the night Constance had looked across a table of subscription lists and seen in Rosalind exactly what she was. *A fellow reader of other people's papers.* It had been a warning then. It was a joke now, or near enough to one that they could both laugh at it, which was perhaps the strangest thing the inquiry had left behind: that the mill owner's daughter and the suffragist's driver should have become, through perjury and print and one breathless half-hour prying a stolen printing forme out of Foreman Brack's office cabinet during the recess, something like friends.

*My dear Rosalind,* Constance wrote,

*Florence is everything they promise and I distrust it thoroughly. The light is golden, the wine costs less than tea, and everyone I meet asks what my father does as though "industrialist" were a charming hobby, like breeding spaniels. I have taken rooms near the Arno and begun translating trade reports into Italian for a firm that does not ask questions, which suits me perfectly, as I have spent my whole life answering them.*

*I will confess something I could not confess even in the witness box, where they had me by the throat and Father's lawyer was performing his little surgery on my character. When I copied that ledger page — the one with the injuries dated before the accidents they described — I genuinely did not know whose side I was copying it for. I sat at my father's desk with the tracing paper trembling in my hands and understood that I was about to make myself an orphan in every way that mattered, and I could not tell you, even then, whether I did it for justice or for spite. I had been so angry for so long. Perhaps justice is only spite that has found better handwriting.*

*Here is my news, and you must not laugh. I have met a woman here, a typesetter — an actual typesetter, exiled from Milan after the general strike — and she has shown me her composing stick and taught me to set my own name, and I stood there in her workshop with ink to my knuckles and thought: this is what Rosalind felt. This is what she found. I hated that press for ruining my family and now I want one of my own. There is no accounting for the heart. Father would say it proves I was always a traitor. I prefer to think it proves I was only ever waiting.*

*Tell Tomas his hands are spoken of in Florence now, if you can believe it. My typesetter heard the story of the Winter Count from an exile's newspaper and asked if the compositor who reset the whole edition from memory was still living. "Barely," I told her, "and complaining about it," which she said was the mark of a true craftsman.*

*Write to me about ordinary things. Tell me what Mrs. Harker is preserving. Tell me if the frost has taken the roses. Tell me anything at all, so long as it is true and unimportant. I have had enough of important truths to last several lifetimes.*

*— Your friend in exile, C.V.*

Rosalind laughed aloud at that last line, alone at the kitchen table with the lamp turned low, and then found, somewhat to her surprise, that her eyes were wet.

She answered at length. She told Constance about the quince jelly Mrs. Harker had put up that September, forty jars of it, enough to sweeten the town through a siege. She told her about the frost taking the roses exactly as predicted and Mrs. Harker declaring she'd never liked them much anyway. She told her about Pike — Constable Aldous Pike, whose career had ended the day he filed the report he'd spent months refusing to write, and who now kept the peace unofficially at the rebuilt print shop, drinking Tomas's tea and pretending he wasn't proud of himself. She told her that Edith Marchmont was organizing from prison with an energy that suggested the cell had been constructed specifically to contain a woman who refused to be contained.

And she told her — because Constance had asked for true and unimportant things, and this was both — that Rosalind had begun, tentatively, absurdly, to think of the room above the print shop as home.

* * *

Edith's letters were unmistakable. They arrived on thin prison paper, ruled in faint blue lines, written in that upright imperious hand that had signed a thousand movement circulars, and they read like dispatches from a war that Edith was single-handedly winning.

*Dear Rosalind,* (never *my dear*; Edith economized even in affection),

*Holloway agrees with me better than my doctors would like. The governor's wife takes my correspondence class. Three wardresses are members of the movement now, though naturally I cannot say how I know. My cell looks out on a wall, and I have organized the wall.*

*You will be pleased to hear that Marlow Bend has become a text in our training lectures. I describe it thus: we believed the cause required us to pass through places like your town; we did not understand that the cause was *made* of places like your town. That sentence has done more recruiting than a year of my speeches. There is a lesson in that which I am still digesting, and which you are entitled to serve back to me cold, whenever you like. You earned it. I was wrong about the arithmetic. Martyrs are cheaper than witnesses only on paper, and paper is precisely where I lived.*

*Now to business, because you know me. The appeal fund wants a statement from the printers — three sentences, no more, describing the smashed press and the stolen forme, in language a provincial newspaper will print without editing. Tomas will grumble that craftsmen shouldn't write propaganda. Overrule him. And Rosalind — write it yourself. I have read everything you ever set, including the things you didn't know I'd noticed, and your sentences land where they're aimed.*

*(Yes. I knew. From the second month. You resealed the envelopes with wheat paste instead of gum arabic — a small perfectionism, and perfectionists cannot help themselves. I let it continue because a woman who reads everything in secret will one day either betray you or save you, and I judged you worth the wager. I judge better than I calculate. Remember that when you next weigh my faults.)*

*Give my regards to the Harkers. Tell Mrs. Harker her parcel of quince jelly contravened six regulations and I ate every jar's worth. Tell Tomas the movement owes him its spine.*

*— E.M., Holloway, still unbowed*

Rosalind read that middle paragraph four times.

So Edith had known. Had known from the beginning — had *hired* her, or kept her, partly *because* of the steaming kettles and the wheat paste and the perfect seals. All those years Rosalind had believed herself invisible, a ghost in the machine of the movement, and all those years she had been an instrument Edith had chosen with open eyes. It should have angered her. She probed at the feeling, turning it over like a coin to test the metal, and found that underneath the sting there was something else: a strange, reluctant relief. Even her trespassing had belonged to someone. Even her secrets had been part of a design larger than her loneliness.

She did not forgive Edith entirely — the arithmetic chapter, the night she'd overheard martyrs weighed against witnesses in that level calculating voice, was not a thing forgiveness came easily to — but she wrote back honestly, which was more than she'd ever managed in the old days.

*You knew about the letters and said nothing for years,* she wrote. *We are even, then, in the currency of concealment, and I propose we declare the account closed and start fresh. The statement for the appeal fund is enclosed — four sentences, not three, because I have never once obeyed your word limits and I don't intend to begin in your absence. The town sends its regards. The bridge holds. Winter is coming and for the first time in my life I am not afraid of being shut in somewhere.*

*— Rosalind Joubert, Marlow Bend, at home.*

*At home.* She looked at those two words for a long moment before sealing the envelope — openly, with the flap pressed down flat, no paste, no kettle, nothing hidden — and decided to leave them.

* * *

More letters came as autumn deepened. A note from Annie Croft, the youngest of the widows, announcing her engagement to a joiner from Ashcombe who had helped ferry the borrowed press across the ford on that desperate night, and asking — shyly, in a postscript — whether Rosalind would attend the wedding, because "you put our names right, miss, and Da always said whoever puts a thing right ought to see how it turns out." A card from the Leeds sister of Margaret Ellery, brief and stiff, thanking her for safe passage of a letter and adding, in a loosened hand at the bottom: *my husband read the Winter Count twice and has stopped saying "these things happen."* A scrawled unsigned note that simply said *the shop looks right with the light in the windows again* and that Rosalind suspected, from the handwriting, was Pike.

Rosalind answered every one. This was the discipline of her new life, and she kept it as strictly as she had once kept the old one: letters opened the day they arrived, read at the table in full view, answered within the week, names written plainly at the foot of every page. Mrs. Harker teased her about the stack of correspondence forever migrating between the kitchen and her room above the shop — "you'll wear a groove in the stairs, girl" — but Rosalind noticed that the teasing had warmed into something like pride. The household had adopted her habit as its own. Tomas brought her stamps from the stationer's without being asked. Young Nell Harker, twelve years old and ferociously curious, had appointed herself keeper of the pen tray.

It was Nell, on a raw gray afternoon in late November, who came pounding up the outside stair with a letter in each fist, breathless.

"There's two! One's foreign — look at the stamps, there's a *lion* on it — and one's from Holloway, I know the governor's frank, I've seen it on Miss Marchmont's others —"

"Then we'll read the lion first," Rosalind said, "to keep the suspense honest."

The foreign letter was from Constance, and it contained news. Rosalind read it standing by the window, and read it again, and felt the particular tightening behind the ribs that she had long ago learned meant: *this matters; pay attention.*

*My dear Rosalind — short letter, big news. My Milanese typesetter and I have found premises. An actual shop, on an actual street, with a press that weighs as much as a piano and prints as true as Tomas could wish. We mean to publish — properly, lawfully, in Italian and English both — the kind of paper the Winter Count was: the names, the numbers, the truth the official record sands smooth. She has the craft. I have the capital and, you'll be amused to hear, the family name, which opens doors here that would be bolted in England. A traitor's name travels well abroad.*

*Which brings me to the favor. We want a correspondent in Marlow Bend. Someone who knows the town, knows the record, knows what got left out of the inquiry and why. Someone whose prose lands where it's aimed — I quote a mutual acquaintance in Holloway. One letter a month, more when there's news. Payment in ink and gratitude, which I'm told is the going rate.*

*Think it over. But think quickly, because we go to press in the new year, and I should very much like our first English page to carry a Marlow Bend dateline. — C.V.*

Rosalind lowered the pages. Through the frosted window she could see the street below, the rebuilt shopfront with its new sign — *HARKER & CO., PRINTERS* — and the lamplighter just beginning his rounds, and beyond the rooftops the dark line of the river, running free and unfrozen toward towns that had never heard of Marlow Bend and soon, perhaps, would.

Nell was watching her with her chin practically on the table. "What is it? Bad?"

"No." Rosalind found she was smiling. "No, it's — an invitation. Of a kind."

"And the other one?" Nell nodded at the Holloway letter, still unopened. "Aren't you going to look? It might be important."

"It might," Rosalind agreed, and turned it over in her hands, feeling its thinness, its weight, the small crisp authority of the prison frank. Edith would want an answer about the appeal statement. Edith would want, in her imperious economizing way, to know everything. And Edith would want it soon, because Edith organized even the walls of her cell.

But downstairs, the lamps were lit and the smell of supper was rising through the floorboards, and below that, faintly, the familiar iron rhythm of the press — Tomas testing the rollers on some job or other, keeping his mending hands busy, keeping the clock honest. Tomorrow the shop would print its first lawful edition under the new sign, a real commission from the parish council, no hiding, no coffins, no code-knocks. Rosalind had promised to be there for the first pull of the lever. Everyone would be there. It would be, Mrs. Harker had declared, an occasion.

She tucked Constance's letter into her pocket — an answer would take thinking, and thinking went better with company — and slit the Holloway envelope with Nell's proffered scissors, and unfolded the thin blue-lined page.

It was shorter than Edith's usual dispatches. Rosalind's eyes went down it once, quickly, the old trained speed of a woman who had read ten thousand letters she shouldn't have — and then slowly, the smile fading into something quieter and steadier.

*Dear Rosalind — the appeal fund is settled, the statement printed as written, all four sentences. Now the real business. The Home Office has denied Tomas's suspended sentence petition for review — routine, they claim — and there is talk of revoking the suspension outright when the session resumes. I have lawyers working, but lawyers move at the speed of money, and money moves at the speed of publicity. If the printers mean to fight it — and I know they do — they will need their story in print within the month, and they will need it written by someone the papers cannot dismiss.*

*You know what I am asking. You know I would not ask it lightly. — E.M.*

Rosalind stood for a moment in the cold bright window, the letter in her hand, listening to the press turning over below like a great patient heart.

Then she put on her coat, took both letters, and went down the stairs to tell the family.
