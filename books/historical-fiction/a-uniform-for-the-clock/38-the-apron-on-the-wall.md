## 38. The Apron on the Wall

Tomas Harker had hung the apron on the third nail from the door before the paint on the new sign was dry.

He told himself it was a practical arrangement — the nail was there, the apron needed a home, and hooks were scarce in a shop that had been rebuilt from the studs. But that was a lie he permitted himself the way his father used to permit himself one pipe after supper: not because he believed it, but because the ritual of it steadied something in him. The apron belonged to Rosalind Joubert. It had been hers through the smashed press and the candlelight typesetting and the long spring when every name in Marlow Bend learned to spell itself correctly in public. And it hung now by the door like a coat of arms over a gate — small, worn at the ties, ink-stained across the bib in shapes that only a printer would recognize as letters.

Customers asked about it. They always asked about it. Mrs. Ellery from the wool shop wanted to know if it was a mourning garment; the boy who delivered paper stock thought it might be a scarecrow's shirt; Constable Pike, coming in for his weekly order of calling cards, stood before it for a full minute without saying anything at all, which Tomas understood to be Pike's way of saying everything.

"It's a uniform," Tomas told them all. "Everyone here wears the same one."

It was true. There were six aprons now, hanging on pegs behind the counter, cut from the same bolt of drill cloth Edith Marchmont's movement had sent down from London along with a cheque that Tomas's mother had wept over and then banked within the hour, because grief taught a family to be quick with money. Six aprons for six printers. His mother wore hers rolled at the waist because she was small. His sister Ada wore hers splattered to the shoulder because she had never once in her life been careful with ink. And Rosalind's hung by the door, retired, because Rosalind no longer worked at the case — or rather, because the work had changed shape, the way water changes shape when it finds a lower place to go.

The first lawful edition went to press on a Tuesday in October, two years almost to the week since Brack's men had come through the door with hammers.

Tomas remembered the date the way other men remembered birthdays. He had spent two months in the cottage hospital with his right hand wrapped in splints, listening to Rosalind read back the edition line by line while she set type left-handed and badly, and he remembered thinking, through the laudanum fog, *she is learning my trade with her wrong hand out of loyalty to mine*, and that thought had done more to mend him than any surgeon's work. Now his hand ached in cold weather and the knuckles sat slightly wrong in their skin, but they held a composing stick as steady as ever, and this morning they were setting the front page of the *Marlow Bend Courier* — lawful, licensed, stamped with the registrar's mark, sold openly at the stationer's for tuppence.

Lawful. He turned the word over the way he'd once turned a piece of type, feeling its weight, checking it for flaws. For eleven years the Harker press had lived two lives: the honest one above the counter — invoices, hymn sheets, wedding invitations, the constable's calling cards — and the secret one below the wallpaper samples, where The Winter Count had been born in the dark and nearly died there. Now there was only one life. The secret room had become a stockroom. The wallpaper samples had come down, and beneath them the plaster bore the pale rectangles of their hiding, which his mother refused to have repainted. "Let it show," she said. "A house should remember what it survived."

The forme was locked. He checked it a fourth time anyway, running his thumb along the quoins, feeling the furniture sit snug. Forty-eight columns of ordinary news: the harvest figures, the bridge tolls since the reopening, a notice from the magistrates' clerk about road repairs, an advertisement for Doctor Fenn's cough syrup that Tomas had accepted partly for the revenue and partly because printing an advertisement for cough syrup felt like the most gloriously boring thing he had ever done. And below the fold, set in the same size as everything else, no larger, no bolder, the thing that made this edition unlike any the town had seen:

**THE WINTER COUNT, PUBLISHED IN FULL. ELEVEN NAMES. VERIFIED BY THE INQUIRY OF 1914.**

No banner headlines. No black borders. Just the names, set in the body size, in the body columns, exactly where the mill's accident ledger had refused to put them for thirty years. That had been Rosalind's instruction, and Tomas had argued with her about it for two nights before conceding she was right.

"If you shout," she had said, "they can call it agitation. If you set it quiet, they have to call it news."

So it sat there among the cough syrup and the road repairs, eleven names in eight-point, spelled correctly, each one followed by the date the ledger had claimed and the date the widows knew to be true. It looked like nothing. It looked like a parish register. And that, Tomas understood, was the whole trick of it: legacy did not live in monuments. It lived in the ordinary record, printed plainly, kept faithfully, handed on.

* * *

His mother came down at half past seven with tea and the particular silence she used when she wanted him to notice something.

"You've done it again," she said.

"Done what?"

She nodded past him. He turned. Rosalind stood at the door in her coat, hat still on, holding a parcel under one arm, and she had stopped — stopped dead, the way a person stops when they walk into a room and find their own funeral being planned — looking at the apron on the nail.

"I meant to take it down," Tomas said, and heard how feeble it sounded. "I kept meaning to. It seemed — " He searched for the word. "It seemed like it would be rude to move it."

Rosalind crossed the shop slowly. She touched the bib of the apron with two fingers, the way you touch a letter that has travelled far to reach you. The ink stains were mostly hers — the great smear across the pocket was from the night she'd set the final line of the reprinted edition, working alone until dawn while Tomas lay in hospital and Ada slept at the foot of the stairs. She had never washed it. Printers washed their aprons; everyone knew that; ink came out or it didn't and either way you started clean. But Rosalind had folded it and laid it on his desk the morning she'd testified, and he had hung it up instead, and neither of them had spoken of it since.

"It looks ridiculous," Rosalind said.

"Yes."

"Like a tavern that keeps a stuffed pike on the wall."

"That's exactly what I told Mother."

"She said let it stay, I expect." Rosalind took off her hat. Under it her hair was shorter than it had been in 1913 — she'd had it cut in London during the trial weeks, scandalizing Edith Marchmont, who had written a four-page letter objecting to it and then, at the bottom, added a postscript asking for the name of the barber. "You know what people will say."

"They'll say the Harkers sentimentalize."

"They'll say worse than that. Mrs. Ellery already thinks it's a shroud." Rosalind hung her coat on the second hook, beside the apron, so that the two of them dangled together, wool and drill cloth, the living garment and the retired one. Then she unpinned the parcel she'd carried in and put it on the counter. "Which is why I brought these."

Tomas untied the string. Inside were five aprons, new, the same bolt, the same cut — but smaller. Child-sized.

He looked at her. She looked back with the expression she'd worn the day she'd corrected his typo at three in the morning without asking permission: calm, certain, daring him to object.

"The school," she said. "Miss Pettigrew has agreed. Saturdays, nine o'clock, the shop is theirs. Type-setting, inking, feeding — the old platen, mind, nothing near the cylinder. We start with their own names. Everyone sets their own name first. That's the rule."

"Whose rule?"

"Mine. I decided it just now." She was already unbuttoning her cuffs, rolling her sleeves, moving behind the counter as though the intervening years had been a weekend. "There are nine children signed up. Two of them are Brack's grandchildren."

Tomas set down the parcel very carefully. Of all the sentences she might have chosen, that was the one that landed deepest. Old Brack had fled before the verdict, gone to ground somewhere north, and the family had stayed — stayed in the town whose story their grandfather had tried to burn, stayed under names the town knew perfectly well, stayed in the particular poverty of the unforgiven. And Rosalind had put the Brack children on her list without comment, without flagging it, as though forgiveness were simply another item of jobbing work to be run through the press.

"Ada will hate it," Tomas said. "Children at her stone."

"Ada will love it. Children are the only audience that laughs in the right places." Rosalind tied on one of the working aprons from the pegs — her own new one, the sixth, which had hung unworn since Edith's cheque arrived. She knotted it at the back with a sharp tug. "Besides. Someone has to teach them why the aprons match."

"Why do they match?"

"Because the clock doesn't care who you were before." She said it lightly, quoting him back to himself, but her eyes stayed on his face a moment longer than the joke required. "That's what you told me. On the worst night of my life, you handed me an apron and said the clock doesn't care. Do you remember?"

"I remember you cried into it."

"I did not cry into it. I cried *near* it. There's a difference, and a compositor should be precise." She pulled the galley of the front page toward her and began to check his setting, column by column, the way she'd learned to do it — fast, ruthless, correct. "Your em-quad here is loose. And you've set 'Magistrates'' with the apostrophe after the s, which the clerk will write to complain about, and I for one refuse to give him the satisfaction."

Tomas watched her work and felt the thing he always felt watching her work, which was too large and too private to say aloud, and which he therefore said aloud, because between the two of them the unsayable had long ago been converted, by practice, into the merely difficult.

"Marry me properly, then, if you're going to keep rearranging my shop."

"I am rearranging your shop *because* I married you properly. The vicar has the certificate. You were present." She found the loose space and tightened it without looking up. "Ask me again when the edition's off the press. A man shouldn't propose over a bad em-quad."

* * *

They pulled the first proof at twenty past ten.

Tomas worked the handpress himself — the new one, bought secondhand from Leeds with part of Edith's cheque, iron-framed and honest — while Ada fed and Rosalind stood at his elbow in the position she'd occupied on every clandestine night of the old press: reading the sheet as it came off, catching errors by eye faster than he could catch them by hand. His mother kept accounts at the desk by the window, where the light was good and where she could see the door, because she was seventy-one years old and had earned the right to keep an eye on doors.

The sheet came up damp and grey-black. Eleven names in the body columns. The harvest figures. The cough syrup.

Ada took the first copy off the tympan and held it at arm's length, frowning at it as though it had personally disappointed her. "It doesn't look like anything," she said.

"No," said Tomas.

"Eleven men dead and it sits there next to an advert for cough syrup."

"Yes."

"And that's supposed to be the victory?" But even as she said it, Ada was reading the names, all of them, her lips moving slightly, and Tomas saw her finger stop on the fourth one down — *Harker, Josiah, m., foundry, 12 February* — their uncle, their father's brother, the man who had stopped coming home two winters running before anyone said the word *dead* aloud. Their father had set that name himself, the night the forme for the reprinted edition was locked, and had pressed so hard on the final letter that the *n* still showed a hairline crack in its shoulder. It would print that crack for as long as the type lasted, and when the type finally wore out they would recast it, and the recast letter would be perfect, and some part of Tomas grieved that in advance.

"That's the victory," Rosalind said quietly. "It looks like nothing. That's how you know it won. Everything that ever tried to hide these names made them enormous — made them secrets, made them scandals, made them worth killing a print shop over. Now they're just Tuesday. Now they're just the paper. In fifty years someone will find this in a box and think it's dull, and they'll be right, and that's the point. Dullness is what safety looks like when it's finished."

Tomas's mother spoke from the window desk without raising her eyes from the ledger. "When did she get wise? She was a chauffeur."

"I was always wise," Rosalind said. "I was merely employed."

They printed four hundred copies. It took until mid-afternoon, and by the end the shop smelled the way Tomas's childhood had smelled — oil and damp paper and the cold-iron breath of the press — and his bad hand ached in a way he had stopped resenting, because the ache was the price of the work and the work was the life, and he had learned, at cost, not to confuse pain with punishment.

Ada bundled copies for delivery: the stationer's, the two public houses, the church vestry where the vicar had agreed, after much prayer, to leave a stack on the table by the poor box. Rosalind set aside three copies — one flat in the archive drawer with the inquiry transcript, one for Edith, who would read it in her rooms and write back within the week with corrections, because Edith Marchmont had never in her life received a document without annotating it, and one for the wall.

"For the wall?" Tomas said.

"For the frame." Rosalind nodded at the apron on its nail. "If it's going to hang there like a heraldic device, it ought to have something underneath it. Every coat of arms comes with a motto. We'll frame the first page and hang it under the apron, and in fifty years some child setting their own name on a Saturday morning will look up and ask what it means, and whoever's minding the shop that day will tell them."

"Tell them what?"

Rosalind considered. Outside, October light lay along the street in long amber bars; somewhere a cart rattled over the cobbles toward the bridge that had been broken and then repaired and then simply, blessedly, forgotten; Mrs. Ellery passed the window, slowed, looked at the apron, shook her head, and moved on, and would buy her copy at the stationer's tomorrow like everybody else.

"Tell them it was ordinary," Rosalind said. "Tell them it was hard, and it was ordinary, and both of those things are true at once, and neither cancels the other. Tell them the clock kept honest time and everyone wore the same apron and nobody was a hero, they just refused to stop. That's the whole story. That's the motto."

Tomas took down the apron from the nail — finally, gently, two years late — and shook the dust from it, and hung it back up straightened, centered, deliberate. Not a relic anymore. A standard.

"Nine o'clock Saturday," he said. "Don't be late. You're teaching the Brack children yourself."

"I know," Rosalind said, and reached for her hat, and paused at the door with her hand resting briefly on the apron's shoulder — the way you greet an old colleague, the way you thank a thing that has held together when everything else came apart. "And Tomas — the frame. Make it plain wood. Nothing gilt."

"What else?"

"What else indeed." She smiled, set her hat at the angle that had once scandalized a movement, and pushed open the door onto the street, where the first copy of the first lawful edition was already travelling from hand to hand through the town that had finally, at last, learned to read its own name.
