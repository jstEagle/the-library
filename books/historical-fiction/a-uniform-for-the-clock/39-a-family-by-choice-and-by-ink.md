## 39. A Family, By Choice and By Ink

The supper was Mrs. Harker's idea, though she would never have claimed it as such. She had simply begun, three days before, to mention in an offhand way that there was a ham in the cellar that wanted eating, and that the potatoes were keeping better than expected this year, and that the long table upstairs could seat eight as easily as six if someone thought to fetch the extra chairs down from the attic. By Thursday it had become, without anyone announcing it, a supper. Rosalind understood the mechanism perfectly. She had spent years watching people arrange gatherings they could not bear to name, because a named thing could be disappointed, and an unnamed thing was only an evening.

She arrived early anyway. Old habit — she had never once in her life arrived anywhere at the hour stated on an invitation, having learned long ago that the first quarter-hour was where the true business happened, the unguarded conversation, the hostess's face before it settled into its company shape. But when she came up the outside stair to the Harkers' flat, carrying a bottle of the decent sherry she'd been saving for no occasion in particular, she found the room already full and already warm, and nobody performing anything at all.

Tomas was at the stove.

This was the thing she still stopped to look at, even now, even after months: Tomas Harker standing over a pan with his sleeves rolled, stirring something with a wooden spoon held in two ruined hands. The mill mob had done their work well that night; the bones had knitted crooked, and the fingers did not close all the way, and the doctor in Thornbury had said plainly that he would never set type again at speed. But a spoon required no speed. A spoon forgave everything. He stirred with a slow, deliberate motion, like a man setting a line of type one letter at a time, and when he heard the door he said, without turning, "If that's Constance, tell her the gravy will be late and it will be her fault for bringing opinions."

"It's me," Rosalind said.

"Then the gravy is early." He turned, and his face did the thing it always did now — a quick lightening, quickly governed, as if joy were a commodity to be spent carefully. "Hang your coat. Mind the cat."

There was no cat. There had never been a cat. It was a joke between them dating from the night she'd hidden the widow's statements in the flour bin while Brack's men were breaking the door below, when she'd invented, in a whisper, a fictitious animal as cover for her crouching — *mind the cat, she scratches* — and Tomas had played along without missing a beat, telling the intruders the flat was full of cats, which had the unexpected benefit of making them reluctant to search anything. The cat had lived in their speech ever since, invisible and immortal, requiring nothing but occasional minding.

Rosalind hung her coat. Below them, through the floorboards, came the familiar sounds of the shop closing for the evening — the press covered, the type locked away, young Peter Harker whistling three bars of a hymn and then abandoning it, as he always did, out of what Rosalind suspected was principle rather than forgetfulness.

Mrs. Harker emerged from the pantry with her arms full of plates and her mouth full of instructions. "Rosalind — the good glasses are in the cupboard behind the wallpaper samples, and don't let your Edith near the stove, she'll reorganize it into a campaign and we'll never find the salt again."

"She's not my Edith," Rosalind said, automatically, and then heard how it sounded, and Mrs. Harker heard it too, and raised one eyebrow with the unhurried precision of a woman who had raised four children and set type for thirty years and could therefore read any composition placed in front of her, however small.

"Not yet," Mrs. Harker said, and carried the plates to the table.

* * *

Edith Marchmont had been out of prison for eleven days.

Rosalind was still learning how to be around her. The sentence had been short — the magistrate, embarrassed by the publicity, by the crowds outside the courthouse, by the fact that half of Marlow Bend had stood in the rain holding copies of *The Winter Count* — and Edith had served seven weeks in a facility she described, in her single letter from within, as "tolerably run but appallingly edited." She had come out thinner and greyer at the temples and somehow larger, the way a building looks larger once you know what fires it has survived. She had gone straight from the gate to the train, and from the train to Manchester, where she had given a speech through a window to a crowd of two thousand, and from Manchester to Marlow Bend, arriving unannounced on the Harkers' doorstep with one carpet bag and the expression of a woman who intended to be useful whether anyone wanted her or not.

She was useful. That was the disorienting part. Rosalind had braced herself for the old arithmetic — for the Edith who calculated martyrs against witnesses, who weighed a town's grief on scales calibrated in London. But the woman who sat now at the Harkers' table, arguing mildly with Peter about whether a comma belonged before *whereupon* in next week's lawful edition, was a different instrument entirely. Smaller. Sharper at the point. She had seen the smashed press, Rosalind knew. She had stood in the wreckage of it with her hat still on and her gloves still buttoned, and something in her had recalculated, and whatever equation she ran now ran in favor of staying.

"You've put it in the wrong place entirely," Peter said. He was sixteen and had his mother's eyebrows and his uncle's stubbornness and, lately, most of the typesetting work, his hands being the only ones in the house that still obeyed him fully.

"I have put it exactly where Fowler puts it," said Edith.

"Fowler has never had to justify a line to a hostile reader at midnight."

"And you have?"

"I have watched my uncle try."

Tomas, from the stove, said, "Leave Fowler out of this. Fowler wasn't beaten for a comma."

A small silence followed — not an awkward one, but the particular silence of a family touching a scar gently, on purpose, to confirm it has healed enough to touch. Then Edith laughed, actually laughed, a short unguarded bark that Rosalind had heard perhaps five times in four years of service, and said, "No. He wasn't. Very well, Mr. Harker. Move your comma."

Rosalind poured the sherry. Four glasses, then a fifth for Constance, who was due on the later ferry.

* * *

Constance Vane arrived as the potatoes came out of the oven, smelling faintly of river fog and wearing the expression she wore everywhere now, which was neither triumph nor regret but a kind of cleared ground — the look of a woman who had burned a bridge behind her and found, to her surprise, that she liked the view from this side.

Her father awaited trial. The indictment had come down in the spring, along with the foreman Brack's flight — north, everyone assumed, though no one said so above a whisper, and Constable Pike's report, that astonishing document of plain truth filed with visible self-destruction, had made certain that whispers were all that remained of Brack's respectability anywhere in the county. Constance had testified. Rosalind had been in the gallery and had watched the lawyer take her apart piece by piece — her spinsterhood, her ambition, her "unnatural hostility toward her own father" — and had watched her sit with her gloved hands folded and answer every question in the same level voice she used for dictation, until by the third day it was the lawyer who looked unravelled and Constance who looked merely bored.

She had not gone back to London. That was the other surprise. She had taken rooms at the inn, ostensibly to manage the estate's affairs during the trial, and then the trial ended and she was still there, and then it was summer and she was still there, and she had begun — of all things — to keep the accounts for the print shop. Voluntarily. Without being asked. She had appeared one Tuesday with a ledger under her arm and informed Tomas that his books were "a crime scene" and that she could not be expected to witness such disorder weekly without intervening.

"The gravy," Tomas announced, setting it down, "is late, and it is entirely Miss Vane's fault for being punctual, which unsettles everything."

"How dreadful for you," Constance said, taking her seat. "I'll write a letter of complaint."

"Don't," said Rosalind. "I've read her complaint letters. They're devastating."

And then — because the sentence was out before she could weigh it, and because this table had a way of pulling truths out of her like splinters — the whole table laughed, including Constance, including Edith, and Rosalind sat in the middle of the laughter and felt something loosen in her chest that had been tight for years.

Because it was true, and they all knew it was true, and knowing it was true cost her nothing here. That was the miracle she kept waiting to have explained. For four years she had read other people's letters in secret, steaming each envelope over the kettle, resealing each flap with a breath and a prayer, hoarding a thousand lives in a locked room inside herself. Here at this table, her strangest and most shameful talent was simply a fact among facts, mentioned the way one mentioned a good memory for faces or a tolerance for cold water. Tomas knew. Edith knew — had known, Rosalind eventually learned, almost from the beginning, and had hired her anyway, or perhaps because of it, a revelation that had taken Rosalind a full month to stop being furious about. Mrs. Harker knew and had responded by asking Rosalind to read aloud the draft of every notice before it went to press, because "you catch things, love, you catch the words that sound wrong even when they're spelled right."

She caught things. That was what she was for. It had taken a flooded bridge, a murdered ledger, a smashed press, and a courtroom to teach her that the thing she had done in shame was the thing she did best, and that done openly it was not spying at all. It was attention. It was care, in its least glamorous uniform.

They ate. The ham was excellent. The gravy, in fairness, was worth the wait.

* * *

Afterward there was washing-up, which happened the way it always happened in that flat: everyone claimed some portion of it and everyone did it badly except Mrs. Harker, who supervised from a chair with the serene tyranny of the genuinely indispensable. Edith dried. This had caused a small constitutional crisis the first time — Edith Marchmont, drying dishes with a tea towel, in a printer's kitchen — but she had silenced protest by observing that she had dried dishes daily in prison and found it "the only honest labor I was assigned," and no one had argued with her since.

Peter went down to the shop to lock up properly. Through the window Rosalind could see him crossing the yard in the last light, and beyond him the town of Marlow Bend going about its evening — lamps lit in windows that had been blackened two winters ago, the mill standing dark and idle against the sky like a ship run aground, the churchyard where a coffin had once carried a press to safety looking, in the dusk, like just a churchyard, grass grown ordinary over everything.

That was the thing about ordinary. She had spent so long thinking of it as a defeat. Ordinary had seemed like the thing that happened to people who failed to matter — the quiet life as consolation prize. But sitting here, drying nothing, helping no one, watching a boy lock up a lawful press beneath a flat where a widow's testimony had once hidden in a flour bin, she understood that ordinary was not the absence of history. Ordinary was what history looked like when it was won. Somebody had to live in the aftermath or the winning meant nothing. Somebody had to eat the ham.

"Tomas," she said, later, when the others had drifted to the front room — Edith and Constance deep in an argument about postal rates that both of them were clearly enjoying far too much to want to win — and she found him at the window, looking down at the street with his crooked hands resting on the sill. "Your brother. Eli. Do you think he'd have liked this? All of it — the paper, the trial, the—" she gestured vaguely at the warm room, the clink of cups, "—this."

Tomas considered it. He always considered things properly; it was why his corrections were never arguable.

"He'd have liked the supper," he said at last. "He'd have complained the gravy needed more pepper, and Mother would have told him to season it himself, and he wouldn't have, because complaining was the seasoning." His voice was steady. Two winters ago it would not have been. "But he'd have printed it all the same. Every name. He was the one who started counting, you know. Before there was a paper. He just wrote the numbers on the back of his pay slips and kept them in his boot. I used to think it was a strange thing to do with your boots." He looked down at his hands, opened and closed them once, the way he did when they ached, which was always now. "Turns out it was the sanest thing anyone in this town ever did."

Rosalind put her hand briefly over his — brief, because they had agreed without ever discussing it that tenderness between them should be doled out like scarce type, a letter at a time, each one set precisely — and he turned his hand palm up beneath hers and left it there, and they stood at the window together and watched the lamps of Marlow Bend burn.

* * *

It was nearly ten when the knock came.

Not the coded knock — three-and-two, the warning rhythm Pike and Tomas had arranged during the bad months, which they had never formally retired because, as Tomas said, you didn't retire a fire escape just because the fire was out. Just an ordinary knock, polite, twice.

Peter answered it. There was a murmur on the landing, and then he came into the front room holding an envelope, looking puzzled.

"This came for the shop. Not post — hand-delivered. Boy brought it down from the ferry landing, said a gentleman paid him tuppence and didn't leave a name."

"A gentleman," Constance repeated, in the tone she reserved for the word *gentleman* as applied to strangers.

The envelope was cheap paper, travel-stained, addressed in a careful hand: *To the Printers of The Winter Count, Marlow Bend.* No street. No number. As if the sender trusted that in this town those two lines were a complete address — which, Rosalind reflected, they probably were.

"Read it," Mrs. Harker said.

Rosalind took it. Nobody asked her to be careful of the seal; nobody suggested she steam it open first. She slid her thumb under the flap the ordinary way, the honest way, and drew out a single sheet written in pencil, the handwriting of a person accustomed to writing in bad light.

She read it once silently. Then, because the room had gone quiet in the particular way rooms go quiet when they are waiting for her specifically, she read it aloud.

*"To the Printers — We have read your paper. It reached us folded inside a hymnal, which I am told is how such things travel. We are six men and four women in a village you will not have heard of, forty miles upriver, where there is a quarry, and the quarry has a ledger of its own, and our ledger is longer than yours. We have no press. We have one man who can set type, poorly. We have names — nineteen of them — and widows willing to stand by them. The question is whether a paper can be born by post. If you can teach us what you know, we will learn it. If you cannot come to us, perhaps we can come to you. Tell us if the door is open. — A Reader."*

Silence. The clock in the corner ticked through several of its honest seconds.

"Well," said Mrs. Harker at last, into the quiet, in the voice she used for deciding things that had already been decided, "we'll need a bigger table."

Edith was already reaching for her writing case. Constance had pulled the lamp closer and was doing sums on the back of an envelope — fares, freight, the cost of paper by the hundredweight — with the fierce contentment of a woman whose talents had finally found honest employment. Peter was saying something about spare type, about how much they could spare, about how much you could teach by letter if the letters were long enough, and Rosalind realized she was smiling, and that the envelope in her hands felt less like mail than like a seed.

Forty miles upriver. Nineteen names. Widows willing to stand.

She looked around the table — the printer with his broken hands, the sister-in-law already clearing space in her mind for guests, the boy calculating lead, the heiress doing arithmetic, the suffragist sharpening a pencil — and understood that the door was not merely open. The door had been open for some time. They had simply all been waiting, without saying it, for someone to knock.

"We'll answer tonight," Rosalind said. "Before the last post."

She went to find paper, and her pen, and the good ink — and paused at the doorway, looking back at the lamplit room, at the family assembled there by choice and by ink, and thought: *this is how it spreads. Not by speeches. By suppers.*

Outside, the river ran dark toward the sea, carrying spring on its back.
