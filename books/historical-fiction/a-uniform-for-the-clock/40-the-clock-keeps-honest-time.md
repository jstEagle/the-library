## 40. The Clock Keeps Honest Time

The letter lay before Rosalind Joubert on the kitchen table above the print shop, and for a long moment she simply looked at it — at the address written in her own hand, plain as daylight, in ink the colour of ordinary rain.

*Mrs. Ada Fenwick, 14 Chandler's Row, Marlow Bend.*

No steam required. No kettle humming on the stove with its lid half-lifted, its breath waiting to be pressed into service against gummed paper. No knife slid beneath a flap, no hairdryer of breath, no careful unpeeling of another person's sealed intentions. The envelope sat there shut, and what was inside it belonged entirely to Ada Fenwick until Ada Fenwick chose to open it — which was, when one considered the matter properly, the entire point of an envelope at all.

Rosalind had spent eleven years learning how to open other people's letters without leaving a mark. It had taken her four years to learn how to leave them closed.

She turned the envelope over once, twice, checking her work out of old habit. The flap was pressed flat, the seal — a plain drop of wax now, not even her old trick of matching the original colour — sat centred and honest. Inside were three pages: news of the spring edition, a note about the widow's pension fund that Constance had finally pried loose from the county board, and a small sketch Tomas had made of the new press, labelled in his blocky capitals *FOR ADA, WHO TAUGHT US THE FIRST RULE: COUNT EVERYONE.*

Ada Fenwick had taught them more than that. She had taught them that a name spoken aloud could be a kind of armour.

Outside the window, Marlow Bend went about its morning in the grey-gold light of early autumn. The river had dropped since spring; you could see the stonework of the new bridge piers above the waterline now, pale scars where the flood-masonry met the old. A cart rattled past the corner below, and somewhere up the lane the school bell rang — the same bell that had rung through every year of the town's long silence, ringing still, indifferent as ever to what people did or didn't say beneath it.

Rosalind rose, tucked the letter into her coat pocket, and took her hat from the peg by the door.

On the way out she passed through the shop itself, because she always did, because the route from the stairs to the street ran directly across the room where her second life had been set in type. The press stood clean and oiled along the far wall, its iron arm resting like a sleeping animal. The type cases gleamed. And beside the door — nailed there by Tomas himself, with two bright brass screws he'd polished unnecessarily — hung the apron.

It had faded from navy to something like storm-cloud. The strings were frayed where Rosalind's hands had worried them through three years of night work. A scorch mark from the candle incident of the reprint week marked the left hip, and no amount of washing would take it out, and nobody wanted it taken out. Tomas called it the shop's coat of arms. Constance, on her visits from London, called it sentimental clutter and then, when she thought no one was looking, touched it once with one finger as though confirming it was real.

Rosalind let her hand rest on it briefly in passing. Canvas worn soft as skin. *Everyone here wears the same uniform,* Tomas had said, that first night, holding it out to her like an offering he wasn't sure she'd accept. *The clock doesn't care who you were before.*

The clock, as it happened, cared very much about being on time this morning. The post left Marlow Bend at half nine, and it was twenty past.

* * *

The walk down Fore Street took seven minutes if one didn't dawdle, and Rosalind had stopped dawdling only recently, having discovered in herself, at thirty-four, a surprising punctuality of spirit. Three years ago she would have found reasons to pass the mill wall slowly, reading the notices pinned there — she read everything, everything, it was the condition of her life — but the notices these days were parish business and a lost sheep, and the mill wall itself had been repainted a municipal cream that fooled no one into thinking anything had been forgiven, only that someone had decided the town should stop flinching at the sight of it.

Old habits of observation died hard, though. She noted, without meaning to:

That Mrs. Calloway's curtains were open again, first time since her son's name had gone into the spring edition. That the bench outside the chemist had been repaired, and someone had carved a small W into its arm — Walter, probably, for Walter Pike, whose father had died the previous February and whose grave the whole town had attended, constables and printers and widows alike, standing shoulder to shoulder in the rain while the vicar, who had once looked very hard at his sermon pages during certain inquiries, said aloud the things Aldous Pike had put on paper at the cost of his career. That the ferry landing was quiet, because the bridge held now, and the ferryman had gone back to fishing, which he'd always claimed he preferred anyway.

And that Brack was gone — truly gone, not just from the streets but from the town's vocabulary. He had fled the indictment in the spring and been traced as far as Newcastle and then nowhere at all, and Marlow Bend had done the healthiest thing available to it: it had stopped saying his name. Not out of fear. Out of a kind of collective editorial decision, as though the town had learned from the Harkers that history is a thing you typeset, and some men simply didn't earn the space.

At the corner by the bakery, Rosalind passed the noticeboard where the current issue of *The Winter Count* hung behind glass now — lawful, licensed, printed openly under the Harker imprint with a subscription list of four hundred names. The headline concerned drainage rates, of all things. Drainage rates! And yet crowds sometimes stood before that glass reading about drainage rates with the particular attention of people who remembered when reading a sheet of newsprint in this town could cost you your window, your stock, your teeth. The ordinariness was the monument. Rosalind understood that better than anyone. They had fought for three years to make the truth so boring, so routinely printed, so *expected*, that no magistrate would ever again be able to convene an inquiry and find the record conveniently blank.

A boy of perhaps eight stood at the board now, sounding out the drainage article syllable by syllable. His mother waited, patient, letting him struggle. Rosalind slowed — she couldn't help it; she read everything, including children reading — and saw that the boy's lips shaped the word *count* correctly on the first try.

Her chest did the thing it did. She walked on.

* * *

The post office occupied the old wool merchant's premises near the bridge approach, and it was there, in the queue behind the fishmonger's boy and a woman posting cakes to a daughter in Leeds, that Rosalind allowed herself the luxury of remembering.

Because the morning was ordinary, and ordinary mornings were when it came back strongest.

She remembered the first letter she had ever steamed open — she'd been nineteen, newly hired, terrified of being dispensable, and she had told herself it was diligence, checking the post for anything that might embarrass Miss Marchmont. It had been a love letter from a married man, tedious and sad, and Rosalind had resealed it perfectly and hated herself a little and done it again the next day, and the next, for years. Eleven years of kettles and knife-blades and the particular shame of knowing too much about strangers. Eleven years of being the most informed person in every room and permitted to say nothing. She had thought of it then as competence. She knew now what it had actually been: loneliness wearing competence as a disguise, a girl with no family and no country — half French, wholly unclaimed — making herself necessary to a woman who needed her useful but never close.

Edith Marchmont had needed her useful right up until the arithmetic chapter, the terrible fortnight in the winter of their captivity when Rosalind had stood outside a door in the Harker house and heard her mentor calculate, in that crisp campaign voice, how many deaths a town could afford against how many votes a nation required. Martyrs served better than witnesses. Rosalind had carried that sentence like a stone for months. She had nearly carried it all the way back across the reopened bridge, away from Marlow Bend, away from the presses, away from the choice — except that at the middle of the span she had stopped, in the wind, with safety on one side and the courthouse on the other, and understood that duty was not the direction Edith pointed. Duty was the direction the truth pointed. She had turned around.

And Edith — Edith had redeemed herself in the end, had emptied the movement's war chest into Marlow Bend's defence, had accepted her sentence for obstruction with a smile she gave the photographers and called it excellent publicity, and had written to Rosalind from Holloway every month since, letters that arrived open on the prison governor's inspection and were therefore, for the first time in their acquaintance, letters Rosalind received exactly as intended. There was a justice in that which Rosalind savoured privately. Edith Marchmont, the great correspondent, finally writing to someone who read her honestly — because she had to, because the state read over both their shoulders, and because Rosalind had earned the right to be trusted with the unsealed version.

*My dear R,* the last one had begun. *They have moved me to the wing with the library, which I am assured is not a reward and does not feel like one. I am cataloguing. You would laugh. I find I am good at it, and I find also that I think of you whenever I handle another woman's papers, and I do not steam them open, and I want you to know that I understand at last what you gave me by staying. Give my regards to the press. — E.M.*

Rosalind had read that letter four times. Openly. On the kitchen table. With tea going cold beside her. It was, she suspected, the happiest correspondence of her life.

The queue moved. The woman with the cakes finished her transaction with a lengthy discussion of string. And then it was Rosalind's turn, and Mr. Pettifer behind the counter — who had sold stamps through scandal, flood, inquiry, and insurrection without once changing his expression — held out his hand for the letter.

"Mrs. Fenwick," he read, and glanced up. "Chandler's Row."

"That's right."

"Penny halfpenny." He weighed it professionally in his palm, out of habit rather than necessity. "Thick one. Good news, I hope?"

"News," Rosalind said, and found she was smiling. "Counted properly."

He stamped it — the franking stamp, thump of brass and ink, the little sound she had once feared above all sounds, the sound that meant a letter's contents were now beyond her reach and everyone's — and dropped it in the basket with the rest of the world's sealed business.

And that was all. That was the whole ceremony. A letter, addressed, posted, on its way to its addressee, carrying nothing hidden inside it but what it said.

Rosalind stood a moment longer than the transaction required, one hand still resting on the counter's worn edge, and let the moment be as small as it was. Because that was the lesson of the whole affair, the lesson of Marlow Bend and the winter count and the coffins and the flour bin and the smashed forme and the borrowed press running hot till dawn: the truth did not need to be dramatic to be kept. It needed to be kept *routinely*. By ordinary hands. On ordinary mornings. By people who showed up at half nine to post the post.

The clock doesn't care who you were before. But it keeps honest time for whoever stays to wind it.

* * *

She came back up Fore Street the long way, past the churchyard, where she had developed the habit — not morbid, she insisted to herself, merely archival — of checking the newer stones. The Harker brothers' stone stood where Tomas had placed it, with both names cut deep and the dates correct, because Tomas had checked the dates himself against the accident ledger page Rosalind had copied all those winters ago, the page dated before the accidents it described. Under the names, in letters smaller than the mason had first advised but which Tomas had insisted upon:

*COUNTED.*

One word. The whole epitaph. The mason had asked twice whether he was sure, and Tomas had said, "Print it," in the voice he used at the press, and the mason had printed it.

Beyond the wall, the river ran low and clear over its stones, and the bridge crossed it — the bridge, the whole argument of her life rendered in masonry. People crossed it constantly now, without thinking, carrying parcels, arguing about football, living lives in which the question *do I stay or do I go* had never once presented itself as a matter of testimony. Rosalind watched a young couple cross toward the London road with suitcases, emigrating from the town if not the country, and felt none of the old ache. Leaving was allowed now. That was the difference. Staying was a choice only when leaving was possible, and the bridge, in the end, had given the whole town that freedom at once.

She climbed the hill to the shop as the church bell struck the quarter hour.

Tomas was at the case when she came in, setting type for Thursday's edition with his sleeves rolled and his ruined-and-recovered fingers moving with a fluency that still astonished her — the doctors had doubted, the doctors had been wrong, and Tomas had spent the first month of recovery dictating to her from his sickbed and the second month silently proving he wouldn't need dictating again. He glanced up as she entered, read her face the way he read a galley proof, line by line.

"Posted?"

"Posted."

"Good." He returned to his composing stick, then paused. "Ada write back yet about the fund?"

"She will. And when she does, I'll read it, and she'll know I've read it, and she'll write the next one anyway." Rosalind hung her hat on the peg, next to the apron. "That's the arrangement now. Everything in the open, and correspondents anyway. It turns out people don't need secrets to need each other. Nobody tells you that."

Tomas snorted — his version of profound agreement — and slid a line of type into the stick. She watched the words assemble upside down and backwards, mirror-writing becoming truth under pressure, and felt the familiar settling in her chest, the one that had replaced the old restlessness. For eleven years she had been a reader of other people's words. Now she was a keeper of her own. It had turned out to be the same skill, practiced in the opposite direction: attention, care, the refusal to let a thing go unsaid or mis-said or said only by the victors.

From upstairs came the sound of the kitchen door, and then footsteps, and then Constance Vane's voice — she arrived Thursdays, unannounced, with ledgers — calling down the stairwell: "Is that coffee I smell or is this household slipping? Also there's a letter on your table, Harker, it was wedged in the door, and yes, before you ask, I looked at the postmark and nothing else, I am *reformed*, don't look at me like that—"

Tomas caught Rosalind's eye over the composing stick, and they shared the look of people whose family has arrived making noise, which is the best kind of arrival there is.

"Coming up?" he asked.

Rosalind untied the apron from its peg — Thursday meant she'd be helping collate — and tied it on, the strings finding their old knots without instruction, canvas worn to the shape of her.

"In a minute," she said. "First I'm going to stand here a moment and listen to the clock."

The shop clock — a cheap thing, a wedding present from the widows' fund to the rebuilt shop, chosen precisely because it was cheap and loud and honest — ticked away above the press, keeping its time, keeping everyone's time, asking nothing about who anyone had been before.

Rosalind listened to it the way she had once listened at keyholes: completely, gratefully, with nothing to hide.

Then she went upstairs to her family.
