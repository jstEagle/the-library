## 1. The Weight of Sealed Things

The summons came on a morning so gray that the Republic of Vessany seemed already half-dissolved into it, its rooftops fading like ink left too long in water.

Jude Ashgrove was eating bread in the courtyard of the courier house when the clerk found her — a small, damp man named Pell who had never once looked her directly in the eye, which she considered his finest quality. He set a sealed packet on the table between them, heavy as a brick and twice as unwelcome.

"For Corvenne," he said. "Final diplomatic dispatches. You leave within the hour."

Jude chewed, swallowed, and considered the packet the way one considers a snake that has not yet moved. "How final?"

Pell blinked. "I'm sorry?"

"The dispatches. Are they final-final, or final in the way everything is final these days? The treasury was 'final' last month. The eastern garrison was 'final' twice."

"I only carry messages," Pell said, with the wounded dignity of a man who had made an entire philosophy out of not knowing things.

"Then we have that much in common." She wiped her hands on her coat and took the packet. It bore three seals: the chancery's, the foreign ministry's, and one she didn't recognize — a stag rampant on a field she couldn't name, pressed deep enough into the wax to survive a river crossing, a war, or possibly both.

She weighed it in her palm. Couriers learned to read weight the way priests read scripture. Light packets meant letters; letters meant opinions, and opinions could be survived by anyone. Heavy packets meant documents. Documents meant consequences. And this one had the heft of a thing that would be quoted at tribunals someday, if any tribunal survived to hold one.

"You'll want the northern road," Pell said, already backing away. "Through Harrow Bridge. The checkpoints—"

"I know where the checkpoints are," Jude said. "I've been through them since before you grew your first chin hair."

He retreated. She finished her bread. Somewhere across the city, a bell tolled the hour, and she noted — not for the first time — that it tolled slightly flat, as if even the bells had given up.

* * *

There was a creed among couriers, though no one ever wrote it down, because writing things down was against the creed. It went roughly thus: *You are furniture. You are weather. You are a woman with a satchel, and women with satchels are everywhere and therefore nowhere.* The moment someone remembered your face, you were no longer a courier. You were a witness. And witnesses, in the eleventh year of the Republic's unraveling, had a way of becoming archives that got burned.

Jude had been very good at being nobody for nine years. She had a talent for it the way some people had a talent for music — an ear for forgettability, a genius for the unremarkable. She wore gray because gray was the color of nothing. She walked at the pace of a woman going somewhere dull. When officers asked her questions, she answered them in a voice so flat it slid off memory like rain off slate.

*You're a courier?*

*I'm a courier.*

*Carrying what?*

*Boring things.*

And they would laugh, or wouldn't, and wave her through, because boredom was the best armor ever forged and it weighed nothing at all.

Tomas had hated the creed. Her brother had been two years older and constitutionally incapable of invisibility. He laughed too loudly, argued with officers he should have saluted, and once — memorably — corrected General Marrec himself on the order of battle at Salven Ford, in front of the general's entire staff, using chalk and the floor of a tavern. He'd been right, which made it worse. Tomas Ashgrove had never met a wall he didn't want to argue into a door.

"They'll remember you," she'd told him, the night before his last deployment. "That's the whole trick of surviving this. Don't be remembered."

"And what do *you* survive as, Jude?" He'd been grinning, wine-drunk, chalk dust still on his cuffs. "A shadow? You'll outlive the whole war and no one will know you were ever in it."

"That's the idea."

"That's a *death*, is what that is. Just spread over more years."

He'd kissed the top of her head and gone off to be visible, and the Republic had remembered him exactly long enough to send her the letter — official seal, clerk's hand, four sentences. *Killed in action at the Kessel line. Remains unrecoverable. The Republic thanks him. The Republic thanks you.* Four sentences for twenty-six years of a person who argued walls into doors.

She had kept the letter for a month and then burned it in the courier house stove, which was where the Republic's gratitude belonged.

* * *

She packed light, as always. Spare shirt, flint, the forged passes that cost her more than her boots, and the packet, wrapped in oilcloth and strapped against her ribs where it rode like a second heartbeat. Before dawn she stood in the courtyard while the stable boy saddled her mule — a bad-tempered creature named Chancellor, because the quartermaster had a sense of humor and poor survival instincts.

The city was quieter than she'd ever known it. That was the thing about a republic dying: it didn't scream. It just grew quiet, district by district, lamp by lamp, until the silence itself became the news. Shopkeepers stood in their doorways watching her pass with expressions she couldn't classify — pity, maybe, or envy, or simply the blank arithmetic of people counting what they had left.

At the north gate, the guard — a boy of perhaps seventeen, wearing a uniform that had fit someone else first — checked her papers without really reading them.

"Corvenne?" he said.

"Armistice," she said, in her flattest voice. "Boring things."

He waved her through, and she rode out under a sky the color of dishwater, thinking: *one more delivery*. Nine years of one-more-deliveries. This one would be like the others. Heavy paper, distant men, signatures that changed nothing she could see from where she stood.

Chancellor clipped along at his insulted amble. The road north ran beside the river, brown and swollen with spring, carrying the country's meltwater toward the sea with more urgency than the government had shown about anything in years.

* * *

By evening she reached the inn at Ferrenholt — a low stone building hunched at the river's bend, the last warm place before Harrow Bridge and the first checkpoint. She chose the corner table by habit, back to the wall, sightline to both doors. She ordered stew and paid for a room and did all of it in the voice of a woman no one would trouble to describe later.

The inn was fuller than usual — refugees moving west, deserters moving anywhere, and a scattering of soldiers drinking with the desperate loudness of men whose pay had stopped arriving. Jude kept her hood down and her shoulders round and ate her stew and listened, because listening was free and information was the only currency that hadn't collapsed.

They were talking about Corvenne. Of course they were. The whole country was talking about Corvenne, the way a family talks about the doctor's verdict.

"—signing's tomorrow night, they say. The delegation's already there."

—"Marrec's terms, Marrec's terms. Man negotiated the surrender before we'd lost the war properly—"

"—heard the neutral observers are coming in from Ostrava. Real ones, with seals and everything—"

Jude tuned it out. Armistice talk was above her station, and she had built her life on having no station. Whatever was in the packet against her ribs, it would be signed by men in clean rooms, and she would deliver it, and she would ride home invisible, and the Republic would go on dying at its own pace without her name attached to anything.

She was climbing the stairs to her room when the man stepped out of the shadow of the landing.

He was scarred along the left side of his face — old scars, knife work, badly stitched by someone in a hurry — and he moved like a soldier who'd learned to move quietly after the army taught him loudly. He didn't touch her. He didn't need to. He simply held something out in the lamplight, and her body went cold before her mind caught up.

Letters. A bundle of them, soft-worn at the folds, tied with cord. And even at arm's length, even upside down, even across nine years and one burned letter of her own — she knew the handwriting.

The slanted capitals. The *k* that always looked like it was trying to escape the word. Tomas had written ten thousand letters to her from every posting in the Republic, and she had read every one until the pages went soft.

These were dated after Kessel. After the letter. After the grave that held no remains.

"Tomas Ashgrove," the stranger said quietly. "You're his sister. I need to tell you what he found — and what's waiting for you in that satchel."

Downstairs, the soldiers laughed at something. The river ran dark toward Corvenne.
