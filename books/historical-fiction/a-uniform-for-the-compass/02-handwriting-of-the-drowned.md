## 2. Handwriting of the Drowned

The Ferryman's Rest sat where the road met the Vell, and like everything else in the last days of Vessany, it had learned to lower its voice. The sign creaked. The windows were shuttered against the early dark. Inside, a handful of travelers ate their stew without looking at one another, which Jude considered the highest form of hospitality a country could offer.

She took the corner table with her back to the wall, ordered the stew, and did what she always did: became furniture. A courier's first lesson was that no one remembers a woman eating alone if she eats the way chairs stand — patiently, without complaint, without story. She had been invisible for eleven years. It was the closest thing she had to a talent.

The dispatch case rode against her ribs under her coat, strapped there like a second heart. Three documents. One armistice. Two days to Corvenne.

She was halfway through the stew when the scarred man sat down across from her without asking.

He had the kind of face weather leaves behind after it's finished with everything else — deep lines, a burn along the jaw that had healed badly, eyes that were doing arithmetic. He put his hands flat on the table, palms up, empty.

"You're going to tell me I have the wrong table," he said. "You'll be polite about it. You're very good at polite."

"I haven't said anything."

"That's my point." He leaned forward slightly. "Jude Ashgrove. Courier. Third run through Corvenne this year, though you vary your route so nobody counts. You don't drink on duty. You tip too exactly. And you've been sitting here for ten minutes without once looking at the door, because you already memorized it when you came in."

Jude set down her spoon. "That's an impressive list for a stranger. Also a confession. You've been watching me, which means you're either a thief or a fool, and thieves sit closer to the exits."

"Thieves want your case." His voice stayed low, even, unhurried. "I want five minutes. Then you can walk out and never think of me again, if that's what you choose."

"Choose is generous. You're sitting between me and the door."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth — not quite a smile. "Old habit. Sorry." He shifted his chair six inches left, clearing her line. "Better?"

It was better. She hated that she noticed, and hated more that some tired animal part of her relaxed by a degree. "Five minutes," she said. "Then I finish my stew, and it's very good stew, and you'll have wasted your evening."

"Ilan," he said, as if introducing himself to a priest. And then he reached inside his coat.

Her hand went to her knife before her mind caught up. But what he drew out was not a weapon. It was letters — four of them, soft-cornered from handling, tied with the plain twine Tomas had always used because ribbon cost money and he'd been raised by a mother who counted every coin twice.

The room went strange. The fire noise flattened. The stew steam rose in a straight, indifferent line.

"Where did you get those."

"From him."

"Tomas Ashgrove died two years ago at Serevan Field." Her voice came out level, which surprised her. Eleven years of practice will do that. "I received the notice. Official seal. My brother's effects returned in a box the size of a hat."

"I know the notice. I carried it." Ilan laid the letters on the table between them, untied them, and pushed the top one across. "I was in his company. I'm the reason he trusted couriers at all, actually. He talked about you. Said his sister could vanish in a crowded room and reappear three provinces away holding something nobody else could hold."

"Stop talking."

"Read it first."

She didn't want to read it. Reading it meant admitting the handwriting existed outside her memory. For two years the letters had lived only in her head — the way Tomas crossed his *t*s like fence posts leaning into wind, the way he doubled back over his own words when he was excited until the ink went gray, the small crooked *d* he'd made since childhood because he'd broken that finger falling out of the pear tree and refused, out of stubbornness, to let the finger win.

She looked down.

*Dear Jude,* it began, and the *D* was slightly crooked, and the *d* in *dear* leaned like a man against a doorframe, and the ink was gray in two places where he'd gone back over himself because he was excited, because he'd found something, because—

The stew went cold in front of her. The whole inn went cold. Somewhere behind the shutters the river kept moving, patient as ever, carrying things downstream whether anyone claimed them or not.

"This is a forgery," she said.

"Yes," Ilan agreed mildly. "That's rather the point."

She looked up. He waited. He was good at waiting; she recognized the trade.

"Explain."

"Tomas survived Serevan. Wounded, captured, traded in a prisoner exchange nobody reported because reporting it would have embarrassed someone. By spring he was working records for General Marrec's office — clerking, cataloguing, the sort of work they give men who limp." Ilan tapped the second letter. "And in March he found the draft terms of the armistice. The real ones. The ones being sent tonight to Corvenne under diplomatic seal."

Jude felt the floor tilt, gently, the way a boat tilts. "What do you mean, the real ones?"

"The dispatch you're carrying was written by Marrec's faction. The signature block is genuine — the Chancellor's hand was traced from standing orders, stroke by stroke, by a man in their pay who does nothing else. The terms themselves were rewritten. Cession of the eastern garrisons. Disarmament of the Second Army. Corridors for Marrec's troops to withdraw south with everything portable, leaving the rest of us to whatever comes across the border." He said it plainly, without heat, the way men report weather that has already destroyed their fields. "Tomas compared the drafts against the Chancellor's own correspondence. The forgery is good. It isn't perfect. But it's perfect enough that no one at the signing will question it — unless the originals arrive first."

"And Tomas? Where is my brother while his letters do the talking?"

Ilan's hands went still on the table. That stillness told her before his words did.

"He got the evidence out. Four copies. I carry two; a woman named Sera carries the other two by the coast road. He stayed behind to cover our leaving." A pause, measured like everything else about him. "The last letter is dated nine days ago. I won't pretend past that."

Jude looked at the letters again. Four of them, spread now across the scarred wood like a hand of cards. She read the third one slowly, letting the fence-post *t*s and the stubborn crooked *d* walk through her chest one at a time. *If you're reading this, you took the job before I could warn you, because of course you did. You never ask questions. It's the thing I love most about you and the thing that's going to get you killed, and I've decided to gamble on the loving part.*

A laugh came out of her — small, cracked, entirely involuntary — and the innkeeper glanced over, and Jude made her face smooth again by reflex. But something had split underneath the smoothing. Something load-bearing.

For eleven years she had believed the world ran on a simple mercy: sealed orders, unasked questions, invisibility. Carry the thing. Don't read the thing. Be the chair, the shadow, the woman eating stew. It had kept her alive through a war that ate everyone who insisted on mattering. And now her dead brother's handwriting was telling her that the thing she was carrying — the most important delivery of her career, the one that would end the war — was the murder weapon itself.

"If I turn back," she said quietly, "they'll know. Marrec's people issued the mission. They'll have watchers on the roads behind me."

"They'll have watchers ahead of you too. Three checkpoints between here and Corvenne, and each one staffed by someone who's seen your face at least once." Ilan met her eyes. "I can't cross them for you. I've tried the bridge already; it's why I'm short a horse and long on scars. What I can give you is the letters, the comparison notes Tomas wrote, and the name of the neutral delegation's registrar in Corvenne — a man named Odile Ferrant. Evidence means nothing unless it enters the record before the ink dries."

Jude sat with her cold stew and her warm, impossible grief and did the only arithmetic that mattered. Deliver the forgery and live comfortable, complicit, invisible forever. Or carry the truth through three gates guarded by ghosts of her own acquaintance, and become — permanently, fatally — visible.

*Tomas.* Writing to her from beyond his own death notice. Still trying to protect her. Still doubling back over his words because he was excited.

She untied the dispatch case from her ribs, opened it, and for the first time in eleven years, read a sealed order all the way through.

Then she looked up at Ilan. "Tell me about these checkpoints."

His answer was simple, and it was worse than anything she'd imagined: "The first one's the bridge at Velleford. The officer there is Captain Bren. He knows you, Jude. He's dined at your mother's table."

* * *

They spent the night at opposite ends of the common room, not speaking, because speaking together marked them both. Jude slept two hours with the letters strapped where the dispatch had been and the dispatch hidden in the false bottom of her saddlebag, wrapped in oilcloth among spare socks like laundry that happened to be treason.

At dawn she bought bread, fed her horse, and watched the mist lift off the Vell to reveal the far bank — and there, small in the gray light, the tollhouse and the barricade and the flag of the Republic snapping in a wind that no longer belonged to it.

Captain Bren stood at the barricade checking papers, stamping them with the bored rhythm of a man who has stamped a thousand and will stamp a thousand more. From this distance he might have been anyone.

From this distance was about to end.
