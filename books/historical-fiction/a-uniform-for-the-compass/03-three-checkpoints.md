## 3. Three Checkpoints

The bridge at Sarn's Ford had a name once — the Bridge of the Two Sisters, some old story about drowned girls and lanterns — but now it was just a plank of empire laid across the water, and Corporal Dennet Voss stood on it for his ninth consecutive hour of checking papers and finding none of them interesting.

He was thinking about lunch when the courier came.

She was small, mud-colored, wrapped in that particular way women of no importance wrapped themselves: shawl up, eyes down, everything about her arranged to be forgotten before you'd finished looking at her. Voss had seen a hundred like her. He waved her into the queue with two fingers.

"Purpose of travel?"

"Deliveries." She held out a satchel. "Linen thread and buttons for the mercer in Ostenhale."

"Buttons," Voss repeated, with the weariness of a man who had personally inspected four thousand buttons in the last month. He flipped the satchel open.

And then he looked at her face.

Just for a second — she'd lifted her chin to answer him, and the light came off the river and caught her full on, and something old and cold walked up Voss's spine. He knew that face. Not from a wanted poster. From somewhere warmer than that. A courtyard, maybe. Rain. Someone laughing —

"You from the capital?" he asked.

"Never been," said Jude Ashgrove, who had been born eleven miles from where he was standing, and whose brother had once stolen apples off this very corporal's windowsill while she laughed herself sick behind the wall.

Voss stared at her. The feeling wouldn't resolve. It was like reaching for a name and coming back with only its shape. He was still reaching when the sergeant shouted about the cart queue backing up, and by the time he turned around, the button woman had her papers stamped and was walking away with the unhurried gait of someone who knows exactly how fast is suspiciously slow.

"Hold on," Voss started.

She didn't hold on. And he let it go, because his lunch was getting cold, and because whatever ghost lived in that face, ghosts couldn't be arrested.

* * *

The trouble at the second crossing wasn't the guard. It was the money.

Ilan had pressed three Vessany crowns into her hand at the inn — good ones, heavy, unclipped — and told her to buy her way past Lieutenant Brakke like a respectable smuggler. *Slip them under the passport, don't look at his hands, look bored.* Jude had done this before. It usually worked.

What she hadn't accounted for was that Brakke was new, ambitious, and terrified of being caught *not* taking bribes.

"Crowns?" he said, holding them up to the lantern as though they might confess. "Are these — are you attempting to bribe an officer of the Provisional Authority?"

"I'm attempting to tip an officer," Jude said, "for his efficiency."

"My efficiency?"

"You've searched my bag twice. Very thorough. Very efficient."

Brakke went the color of boiled ham. Behind him, two soldiers exchanged the flat, delighted looks of men about to watch a colleague step in something. Jude understood, with the clarity that arrives at the worst moments, that she was going to be strip-searched in a shed, and that the documents sewn into her coat lining would end her life and Ilan's mission both.

Then a third soldier shouldered in — older, gray-whiskered, radiating the boredom of twenty years' service — and read the situation in one glance.

"What've we got, Brakke? Buttons again?"

"Linen," said Jude.

"She tried to *bribe* me—"

The old soldier took the forged pass from her satchel — the one Ilan had made, bearing a seal that would fool nobody for more than ninety seconds — and studied it with the expression of a man reading a letter from a dead friend.

"Courier's pass," he said. "Republic issue." He handed it back. "You know the Republic's finished, girl. Signing's tomorrow."

"Then I'm delivering to history," Jude said, and immediately wanted to bite her own tongue off for saying something so absurd, but the old man huffed — almost a laugh — and jerked his thumb toward the road.

"Let her through. If she's carrying secrets, they're dead men's secrets anyway." He looked at her one moment longer. "You remind me of someone. Can't place it. Go on."

Jude walked through the checkpoint with her heart hammering so hard she could feel it in her teeth, and did not breathe properly until the lanterns were a mile behind her.

* * *

Sergeant Aldous Renn had been stationed at the third gate long enough to know every kind of traveler: merchants, spies, widows, liars, and the occasional honest man, always lost. So when a scarred stranger came up the road at dusk, walking too evenly for a man bleeding from a knife wound under his ribs, Renn knew exactly what kind of traveler he was looking at.

"Evening," said the stranger.

"Evening. Papers?"

"I'm afraid I've left them somewhere."

"That's unfortunate."

"It happens." The stranger smiled, easy as anything, and Renn noticed his hand was pressed casually against his side, and noticed also that beyond his shoulder, down the darkening road, a figure in a shawl was slipping through the drainage culvert with the practiced grace of a woman who had been doing this since she was nine years old.

Renn had served the Republic his whole life. He'd watched it rot from the inside, watched Marrec's men swagger through towns they claimed to be defending, watched the armistice delegations arrive in carriages finer than anything the country they were surrendering could afford. Some part of him — the part that still remembered his oath, or maybe just the part that liked a bit of drama — decided not to notice the culvert.

"You're bleeding," he said instead.

"Am I?" The stranger glanced down. "So I am. Occupational hazard."

"Whose occupation?"

"A courier's friend."

Renn sighed and rang the bell for his men. "Take him to the infirmary. Gently. He fell."

"He hasn't fallen yet, Sergeant," said the stranger.

"He will. Everyone does." But as they led Ilan away, Renn looked down the road, at the culvert, at the empty dark where the shawl-woman had been, and felt that old cold walk up his own spine. A courtyard. Rain. Laughing. He shook it off.

Whoever she was, she was gone, and Renn found — to his mild surprise — that he hoped she made it.

* * *

Jude ran.

Not at first — first she walked, fast, the way you do when running is what guilty people do — but when the lights of Corvenne rose over the last hill, gold and terrible, all her discipline broke at once and she ran like the girl she'd been before Tomas died, like someone with nothing left to lose except the truth.

Because she'd counted the days wrong. Or Ilan had lied. Or the world had simply moved faster than couriers do — it didn't matter which. The banners over the city gates weren't Republican blue anymore. They were Marrec's iron-gray, hung there already, waiting, certain. And through the open doors of the signing hall she could see them: the long table, the candles, the neutral delegation in their foreign coats, pens uncapped, parchment smooth and blank and ready to become law.

*Tomorrow*, the old sergeant had said. But tomorrow had come early, or tonight had never been tonight at all. The forgery wasn't being prepared. It was happening *now*. Somewhere in that room, a clerk was laying out the false originals beside the true copies, and in ten minutes the ink would be dry, and in ten years no living soul would remember there had ever been another version of events.

Except her. Except the papers sewn against her ribs, warm as a second heartbeat.

A guard moved to bar the hall doors. Jude didn't slow down. She pulled the shawl from her head, straightened to her full height — five feet and one inch of invisible woman becoming suddenly, catastrophically visible — and said, in a voice she hadn't used since childhood, loud enough to turn every head in the hall:

"My name is Jude Ashgrove. Courier, fourth rank, Republic of Vessany. And I am carrying the *original* orders."

The pens stopped.

Somewhere among the delegates, a chair scraped back. Somewhere near the head of the table, General Marrec's faction went very still, the way men go still when they realize the past has arrived without an appointment.

And Jude Ashgrove, who had spent eleven years learning how to pass through rooms unnoticed, walked into the brightest room in the republic to be seen.
