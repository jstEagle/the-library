## 4. What Survives the Signing

The doors of the signing hall were taller than any door had a right to be, and they were closing.

Jude ran the last hundred yards of Corvenne's marble concourse with her boots slapping the stone and the satchel bouncing against her ribs like a second heartbeat. Somewhere behind her — two streets back, maybe three — Ilan was leaning against a wall with a bullet through his shoulder and a grin on his face, telling her to *run, don't look back, you're terrible at looking back anyway.* She had looked back. She always would.

A footman in neutral gray caught her elbow at the threshold. "The session is closed, madame."

"It's open." She held up the satchel. "It's been open for eleven years. I'm just the first one who's come to say so out loud."

He blinked at her, this poor man whose job description had never once included a bleeding courier with a republic in her bag, and in that blink she was through.

* * *

The hall smelled of ink and lilies, which struck Jude as exactly wrong — ink for what they were doing, lilies for what they were burying. Delegates sat along a horseshoe table: Vessany's rump government on one side, pale as candle wax; Marrec's officers on the other, polished and patient as knives in a drawer; and between them, the neutral delegation from the Concord, five men and two women in dove-colored coats who existed, officially, only to witness.

Pens hovered over parchment.

Jude knew pens. For eleven years she had carried them by the thousand — orders, decrees, surrender terms, death lists — and she had learned that nothing in this world became real until a pen touched paper. Everything before that moment was rumor. Everything after it was history.

"Forgive me," she said, walking down the center aisle as though she had been invited, because confidence was just another kind of uniform, "but you're about to sign the wrong document."

The hall turned toward her the way water turns toward a dropped stone.

General Marrec rose first. Of course he did. He was a broad man made broader by his coat, with the calm face of someone who had already won and was merely waiting for the paperwork. "Guards," he said mildly. "This woman is deranged."

"I'm not," said Jude. "That's rather my point. I'm perfectly sane, I'm perfectly sober, and I've read both versions of your armistice." She reached the horseshoe table and set the satchel upon it, gently, the way Tomas used to set down sleeping things. "There are two versions. The one in front of you, which surrenders the fleet, the arsenals, and the eastern garrisons before any battle has been lost. And the one signed by President Halven's own hand six days ago, which surrenders none of those things."

"Lies," said an officer near Marrec, but his voice cracked on the word like green wood.

"The originals," Jude said, "with seals intact, chain of custody documented at every relay station between here and the capital. Because that's my trade, gentlemen. Sealed things. I don't read them. But I count them." She looked at the Concord delegation, at their careful, witness-shaped faces. "And on the night of the ninth, the dispatch case that left the capital held fourteen documents. The case that arrived at General Marrec's headquarters held twelve. Two documents went missing in transit through territory under Colonel Brase's command — and these two—" she opened the satchel, and her hands were steady, which surprised her, "—are those documents."

She laid them on the parchment the delegates were about to sign.

Silence has many textures. This one was the texture of a pen being set down.

* * *

They did not believe her at first, which was fair; no one had ever believed her of anything, invisibility being her life's work. But the seals were Halven's, and Halven's private secretary was present and went grey as porridge recognizing them. And there was the custody ledger, page after page in her neat courier's hand — notations she had made without knowing why, dates and seal-counts recorded out of habit, out of the dull piety of a woman who believed the record mattered even if no one ever read it.

Someone was reading it now.

Marrec argued. He argued well, for forty minutes, about forgery and treason and the madness of couriers. But the Concord delegates were not soldiers; they were professional doubters, and doubt was a door Jude had finally learned to open instead of slip through. By the time the afternoon light went amber in the tall windows, the signing had stalled — formally "suspended pending authentication of disputed instruments," which is how history says *someone got caught* when it wants to be polite.

Marrec passed her on his way out, flanked by his officers, escorted now rather than escorting. He stopped close enough that she could smell his bay rum.

"You understand," he said quietly, "that the republic dies either way."

"Yes," said Jude. "But not the truth of it. There's a difference between dying and being murdered, General. Even the dead are entitled to know which happened to them."

His jaw worked once. Then he walked on, and the doors closed behind him with the same tall, terrible sound, though it meant something different now.

* * *

An old woman of the Concord delegation — Madam Ostrava, her card later said, First Witness of the Northern Bench — remained after the others filed out. She looked at Jude across the littered table for a long moment.

"You'll need to sign the deposition," she said. "Chain of custody. Your name against every entry."

Jude stared at the proffered pen.

Eleven years. Eleven years of sealed cases and borrowed passes and faces worn like coats left in hallways. She had signed receipts under false names, sworn oaths under other ones, been *the courier*, *the woman with the satchel*, *no one, officer, no one at all.* Her name lived in her mouth like a coin she'd swallowed young and never dared spit up.

Her hand shook. She let it.

"Jude Ashgrove," she wrote. It came out larger than she intended, the letters sprawling like something newly hatched. "Courier, Fourth Relay Division. Late of Vessany."

Madam Ostrava read it upside down and almost smiled. "First time?"

"How could you tell?"

"You hold the pen like it might apologize." The old woman capped it and slid it back across the table. "It won't. That's the trouble with names. They never take anything back."

* * *

Ilan was sitting up when she found him, in a surgeon's tent smelling of carbolic, his shoulder bound and his color somewhere between parchment and fury at being parchment-colored. A bottle of the Concord's medicinal brandy stood at his elbow, three-quarters gone in the service of medicine.

"They told me you did it," he said. "Walked into the hall. Just walked in."

"There wasn't time to think of a better plan."

"That was your better plan?"

"It was my only plan wearing a better plan's coat."

He laughed, then winced, then laughed anyway, and Jude sat down on the cot's edge and found, to her enormous surprise, that she was crying — not dramatically, not beautifully, just leaking, the way a roof leaks after the storm is over, when the damage finally gets around to announcing itself. Ilan, to his credit, pretended not to notice, and handed her the brandy, and that was its own kind of tenderness.

"Tomas planned all of it," he said softly. "The letters, the copies, the route. He said, *if anything happens to me, find my sister. She'll complain the whole way, but she'll get it there.*"

"He undersold me," Jude managed. "I only complained half the way."

* * *

They buried the letters on the third morning, beside the river where Ilan had first shown them to her, in a tin box that had once held tea. No marker. Tomas had asked for none — *the river will remember me better than any stone,* he'd written, *stones can't swim.*

Jude knelt in the cold mud and pressed the earth flat with both palms.

"I kept you invisible too long," she said. "I'm sorry. I thought it was safer for you. I think mostly it was safer for me."

The river said nothing, rivers being famously discreet, and she took a strange comfort in that.

Then she stood, brushed off her knees, and shouldered the satchel — lighter now, nearly empty, holding only a change of shirt and a deposition copy and the strange new weight of her own name.

"North?" Ilan asked.

"North," said Jude. "I hear the roads up there are full of people who've lost their country. Someone ought to carry their stories somewhere they'll be believed." She glanced at him sideways. "I'm told I'm very good at getting things past checkpoints."

"And terrible at looking back."

"Working on it." She didn't look back. But at the crest of the hill, she stopped, once, briefly, and faced south — toward the fallen city, the buried letters, the brother whose handwriting had cracked her world open like ice in spring — and stood there in full view of everything, visible as noon, until she was ready.

Then Jude Ashgrove walked north, under her own name at last.
