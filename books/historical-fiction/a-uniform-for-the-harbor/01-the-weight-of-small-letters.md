## 1. The Weight of Small Letters

The lamp burned low and yellow over the cutting bench, the way Oscar liked it best—low enough that the shadows pooled in the gravers' troughs and made the brass of her tools look old, older than it was, older than she was. She had learned to cut by bad light. Her father said a cutter who needed daylight was no cutter at all, and though he had said it in one of his gentler moods, years ago, before the drinking settled into its present architecture of his days, she had taken it as law. Bad light was honest. It showed you only what you had actually done, not what you hoped you'd done.

She turned the steel blank in her left hand, studying the profile she had already begun: a lowercase *g*, double-story, for the text face they were cutting for the merchant house of Blom & Sons. The counter—the small enclosed bowl of the letter—wanted opening. It was too dark, too tight. A reader would never notice. A reader never noticed anything, that was the whole tragedy and glory of the trade; they noticed only when something was wrong, and then they blamed the ink or their eyes or the weather. But Oscar noticed. She had spent eleven years teaching herself to see what no one else saw, because seeing what no one else saw was, in the end, all she was permitted to own.

Her graver came down into the steel in a long, controlled stroke. Shavings curled up like pale eyelashes and fell away.

Below her, through the floorboards, she could hear her father moving among the cases. Not working—he hadn't truly worked since Martinmas—but moving, touching things, pouring. The bottle lived behind the specimen drawers now, where he believed no one knew. He thought himself clever about it. He poured into a tin cup he kept beside the galley pot, and he poured quietly, and he believed the quietness made it invisible. But sound travels through pine floors and iron joists like water through sand, and Oscar had catalogued his sounds the way she catalogued letters: the scrape of the drawer, the glug-and-stop, the particular heaviness of his boots when he crossed from the case to the window, the pause at the window—he always paused at the window, looking out at the harbor as if it owed him an apology—and then the return, slower each time, to the composing stone where he would stand and move pieces of type around without ever locking up a form.

He called this working. Some nights she let him believe she believed him.

Outside, the wind came off the water with the smell it carried all through that winter and spring—tar, herring, cold iron, the green rot of the pilings at low tide. The harbor city had no name she used for it in her own thoughts anymore; it was simply *here*, the way a hand is here, attached. Beyond the rooftops she could hear the bells of St. Nikolai counting nine, and under the bells, fainter, the creak of ships at their moorings, and under even that—a sound she wasn't sure wasn't imagination—the far-off murmur of men talking in taverns about Paris. About Vienna. About Milan and Berlin and the barricades everyone insisted were coming, or already come, or never coming at all, depending on which tavern and which hour.

Revolutions happened elsewhere. That was the first article of her private creed, held alongside more practical ones: that steel cuts better after oiling, that lamplight lies about depth, that her father loved her in whatever broken currency he still possessed. Elsewhere meant France, meant Italy, meant the German states across the water where students threw paving stones and princes packed their carriages. Here there was only the harbor, the herring fleet, the customs house with its gray flag, and the eternal arithmetic of freight and tariff. Here men complained in the same breath they queued for work. Here nothing had changed in her lifetime except the price of lead and the number of lamps on the quay.

Here, she cut type. That was enough. She told herself it was enough so often and so evenly that the telling had worn smooth, like a pebble in a pocket, and she no longer felt its edges.

* * *

The shop of Eriksen & Son occupied the ground floor and half a cellar of a narrow house on Binderstraße, three doors up from the cooper's yard, close enough to the water that in easterly gales the spray salted the front glass. The sign above the door read ERIKSEN & SON — TYPEFOUNDERS AND LETTER-CUTTERS, the letters themselves cut by her father's master hand thirty years ago and gilded twice since, both times by her. There was no daughter on the sign. There had been no daughter anywhere in the shop's accounts, its ledgers, its baptismal records, or its conversations for eleven years, and there was no use pretending this fact sat easily, because it did not sit at all—it stood, the way a splinter stands in flesh, and she had simply stopped trying to dig it out.

Oscar Eriksen was twenty-four years old and had been a boy since she was thirteen.

It had not begun as a decision. Decisions were what other people's lives were made of. It had begun as a death and a grief and a practical lie, told once, in a season so blurred with mourning that neither of them could afterward say who had spoken first. What she remembered—and memory, she knew, was also a kind of type, cast and recast until the original matrix wore out—was standing in the churchyard in November with the mud pulling at her hem and her mother's coffin going down, and her father gripping her shoulder hard enough to bruise, and saying, in a voice scraped hollow: *I can't lose you both.* And her, thirteen and furious at God and wanting with her whole scalded heart to be what her father needed, answering: *Then you won't.*

After that it was clothes, and a name shortened, and hair she cut herself with sewing scissors in front of the speculum her mother had left, and a voice she lowered by degrees, the way one lowers a body into water. After that it was binding cloth and practice at walking, at sitting, at the particular economy of male gesture—men, she learned, touched their faces less; men let silence sit longer before filling it; men were expected to be clumsy with tenderness, which was the one part that came easily, because tenderness had nowhere safe to live in her anyway. And after that, hardest of all, it was the world itself agreeing with the lie, because the world will agree with almost anything if agreeing costs it nothing.

The trade made it possible. That was the irony she chewed on in sleepless hours: the very craft she'd fought to inherit was the craft that hid her. Type-cutting was solitary work, done bent over a bench with the eyes down and the hands speaking. Customers came to buy the product, not to study the maker. Her father introduced *his son* to factors and shipmasters and the harbormaster Lindqvist, who bought type for his manifests, and none of them looked twice, because why would anyone look twice? A thin youth, all elbows and concentration, with steel dust worked into his knuckles and his father's grey eyes. The world saw exactly what the world expected to see, and Oscar learned that invisibility was not the absence of being seen. Invisibility was being seen perfectly and misread entirely.

She set the graver down and flexed her fingers. Outside, a dog was barking at something near the cooper's yard, and the bells had finished their count, and the wind had shifted, bringing the smell of rain.

*Anders*, she thought, the way she thought of him every evening at this hour, between one stroke and the next. Her brother. The true son. Three years dead—or lost, or drowned, the words mattered less every year—gone down with the brig *Svanhild* somewhere in the North Sea with a cargo of grain and nineteen other men, and the Admiralty's letter had come in a fine copperplate hand to say so, and her father had drunk for nine days straight and then stood up on the tenth day and gone back to the shop as if the sea owed him a debt it would eventually be shamed into paying. Anders had been the heir in every document but the ones that mattered. Anders had been charming and careless and tall, the son who was meant to take the punchcutter's graver and instead took ship after ship because he couldn't bear the smell of molten type, and the last thing he had said to her, leaning on the quay rail with the gulls screaming, was: *Keep the shop warm, little brother. I'll be back before you've cut your masterpiece.*

Three years. She had cut four faces since. None of them felt like a masterpiece. She sometimes suspected she would die without cutting one, and that this, too, would be filed under the general tax of being who she was.

* * *

Downstairs, a chair scraped. Then another sound she knew too well: the tin cup set down on wood with the exaggerated care of a man managing his own balance. She listened without meaning to, the way one listens for a child crying in the night—not from duty but because the ear has its own loyalties.

"Oscar." His voice came up the stairwell, roughened but not yet slurred. Early in the evening, then. "Are you cutting?"

"I'm cutting."

"Bring it down when you're done. I want to see the counters."

This was ritual. He always wanted to see the counters, and he always held her proofs to the lamp and squinted, and he always found one thing to correct and one thing—exactly one—to praise, because praise beyond one thing would make her soft and correction beyond one thing would make her careless, or so he claimed, quoting some master of his own apprenticeship whose name changed with each telling. She did not mind. This was the closest thing to love their household had produced in eleven years, and she had learned to receive it in the spirit in which it was minted.

She blew out the bench lamp, wrapped the blank in oiled cloth, and went down.

The shop at night was her favorite country. By day it belonged to customers and commerce and her father's public face, broad and booming, the craftsman of repute. At night it belonged to the matrices and the moulds, to the smell of hot metal ghosting even in the cold, to the great flat stones worn into shallow valleys by decades of grinding, to the wall of specimen sheets—her father's faces going back forty years, each one named for a woman he had known or a storm he had survived. *Greta*. *Maren*. *The Easter Gale*. Her mother's name was on that wall twice: *Lise Antiqua* and *Lise Titling*, cut the year of her marriage, when Elias Eriksen still believed the world could be improved one letter at a time. Oscar passed them every night and touched neither, the way one passes a grave one visits too often to mourn freshly.

Her father stood at the composing stone with the tin cup at his elbow and a stick of type in his hand, setting and distributing nothing. He was sixty-one and had been handsome the way sea-worn timber is handsome, and drink had reddened the map of his face but not yet wrecked his hands, and his hands—she caught herself looking at them as she always did, with envy and terror in equal measure—were still the finest hands in the port. They could feel a burr of one ten-thousandth of an inch. They had cut letters that were being printed tonight in six cities, carrying invoices and love letters and sermons and shipping manifests, and not one soul alive could name the hands that made them.

"There," he said, taking the oiled cloth from her and unwrapping the blank with a care he gave to nothing else in his life. He held it to the candle, tilting it so the light raked the steel. "The counter's shut. You've shut the counter again."

"It opens in the finishing."

"It opens in the finishing," he agreed, and handed it back, and looked at her with those grey eyes that were hers, that were the one inheritance no ledger recorded. "You cut tight, boy. Always have. You cut like you're afraid the letter'll escape you."

"Letters don't escape."

"No," he said. He turned back to the window, where the first spits of rain had begun to stipple the glass. "No, they don't. People do."

She waited. With her father, waiting was a tool like any graver: you applied steady pressure along a line and eventually the material parted. He drank. Rain thickened against the panes. Somewhere down the street a door slammed and a man laughed, high and loose, one of the taverns emptying early because of the weather.

"They say there's to be a blockade," her father said at last, to the window rather than to her. "Naval. Come spring, if the talks fail. Lindqvist heard it from a naval man buying type for dispatch forms. Blockade of our own harbor. Our own navy, sealing us like a jar of herring."

"That's talk."

"It's always talk until it isn't." He drank again. "In Paris they drove a king out of his palace in February. Did you know that? Out. Into the mud. A king, in the mud, with common men standing on the palace furniture in their boots. Boots on the chairs of kings, Oscar." He said his son's name with a strange tenderness, and she felt the familiar doubling, the vertigo of hearing herself addressed as someone else. "There are men in this town who think such things could happen here. Fools. And there are men in this town—" he tapped the cup against the stone, once, twice, a small bright ringing, "—who think it would be right if they did."

"And which are you?"

He didn't answer directly. Instead he swept his arm at the shop—at the furnaces banked for the night, the racks of matrices gleaming dull silver, the press with its iron screw, the specimen wall with Lise's names on it—as a general might survey troops he distrusted. "A man with a trade," he said, "is a man with a chain. A fine chain. A golden chain. But a chain." He smiled, and the smile frightened her a little, because it was not his smile; it was younger and harder and belonged to some man she had never met. "In Paris, they say, the printers put out pamphlets and inside a week a hundred thousand men knew the truth at once. One week. One press can be worth an army, they say. Worth a hundred armies, because armies only burn things, and presses tell men why to burn them."

"The talk will pass," she said. "It always does. Kings come back. Prices settle. We cut type, Father. We cut type for Blom & Sons, and in April we deliver, and none of it—" she gestured at the window, at the invisible continent of noise beyond it, "—none of that touches us."

He looked at her for a long moment, and in the candlelight his expression was unreadable, and she understood suddenly that she had just revealed herself completely—that her contentment, her creed of *elsewhere*, was itself a confession of everything she had arranged her life around: safety, stasis, the unexamined room. He might have said something. For one suspended second, something moved behind his eyes like a fish turning under ice.

Then he drained the cup and set it down and became, again, her father, the tired craftsman, the widower, the man of one subject and one grief.

"Go up, then," he said. "Cut your tight little letters. Mind the shop tomorrow; I'm for the Guild hall at noon, there's a meeting." He waved a hand, vague and final. "Talk. Just talk."

She climbed the stairs with the blank in her pocket and the word *meeting* ringing in her ears like a dropped piece of type, small and hard and rolling somewhere out of sight beneath the floorboards, into a dark she could not reach.

At her bench she relit the lamp and took up the *g* again and set the graver to the steel, and for an hour the world narrowed to the width of a stroke, and she was almost—almost—content.

Near midnight the rain arrived in earnest, drumming on the roof slates, drowning the harbor sounds. And through it, late, later than any honest customer, later than any neighbor, came footsteps on the cobbles below, unhurried, deliberate, stopping at the door of Eriksen & Son.

She did not look up. But her hand, without her willing it, went still above the steel.
