## 2. A Stranger at the Composing Stick

The rain came in off the harbor the way it always did in October—not falling so much as arriving sideways, flung against the shutters like a creditor demanding entry. Oscar Eriksen heard it through two floors of old timber and did not look up from her work.

She was cutting a lowercase *g* at half a pica, which required the whole of her attention and more than half of her patience. The punch lay clamped in the vise before her, a bar of type metal no longer than her thumb, and under the graver's edge she was shaving away everything that was not a letter. This was the whole art, her father liked to say when he was sober enough to say anything: you do not make the letter. You remove what is not the letter. She had been eleven years old when he first said it, and she had understood even then that he was talking about something other than type.

Downstairs, a chair scraped. A bottle found a table. The sounds of her father's evening arranged themselves in their familiar order, and above them the lamplight made its small yellow room around her hands, and Oscar cut her letter, and the world held still.

Revolutions happened elsewhere. Everyone said so. They happened in Paris, where they were always happening; they happened in Vienna, in Berlin, in the great smoking cities to the south where men wore their grievances like cravats. Here in the harbor there was only herring and tar and the navy's gray ships at anchor, and the customs house with its polite thefts, and the long wet winter coming on as it came every year. Here there was a print shop on a street too narrow for carts, three floors of it, smelling of lead and ink and lamp oil, and in its upper room a young man of twenty-four who was not a young man at all, cutting letters by lamplight while his father drank himself toward the grave one measured glass at a time.

She blew the filings from the punch and held it up. The counter of the *g*—the little enclosed eye of it—was clean. Clean as anything her brother could have done, and better, though no one living knew that comparison but herself.

Anders. Even the name had gone quiet in her, worn smooth as a sea pebble from three years of not saying it. Dead at sea. Lost off the Skaw with nine others when the *Maren* went down in the spring ice. There had been a service; there had been black cloth; there had been their father's face, which had closed like a ledger after a bad year and never properly opened again. And there had been Oscar, standing in the pew with her collar scratchy at her throat and her hair pinned back in the way that read as masculine at any distance beyond an arm's length, thinking the terrible thought she had never confessed to anyone: *now I am truly the only son.*

She had loved him. She wanted that written somewhere, even if only in the privacy of her own accounting: she had loved Anders Eriksen, and his dying had been grief, real grief, grief like a stone in the boot. It was only that grief and relief had arrived holding hands, and she had never managed to separate them.

A knock at the shop door below.

Oscar's graver stopped mid-stroke. Her father's evenings had a grammar, and this sound did not belong to it. No customer came at this hour, in this rain, without being announced by daylight first. The knock came again—three blows, patient, deliberate, the knock of someone who intended to stand there until answered.

"Father," she called down the stairwell. "Someone's at the door."

The scraping chair. A mutter. Then, distantly, the bolt drawn back, and the cold coming up through the floorboards like a thing let in.

She should have stayed at her bench. That was the discipline of her life—stay at the bench, keep your hands moving, keep your eyes down, be furniture, be fixture, be the son who says little and cuts true. For eleven years the discipline had held. But something in the quality of the silence that followed the opening door—a silence with a shape to it, a listening shape—pulled her to the head of the stairs, and then, telling herself it was only caution, only prudence, only a daughter's duty to hear who entered her father's house at night, she descended into the dark.

* * *

The stranger stood just inside the doorway, dripping.

He was perhaps thirty-five, built like a man who had done labor and then stopped doing it, with the softness of a clerk over the shoulders of a stevedore. His coat was good wool ruined by weather; his boots were worse. He carried a leather satchel clutched to his chest with both arms, the way a man carries either money or papers, and water ran off him onto Father's floorboards in a dark spreading map.

"You are Eriksen." Not a question. His accent was southern—Prussian, maybe, or Saxon—and he spoke Danish with the care of a man using borrowed tools. "Erik Eriksen. The typefounder."

"I am he." Father's voice had rearranged itself into the register he kept for strangers, sober-adjacent, dignified. He was standing very straight for a man an hour into the bottle. "You're wet through."

"I've been wet for three days." The stranger's eyes went past Father's shoulder, up the stairs, and found Oscar.

It was a glance like all the others—the quick inventory men performed without knowing they did it, sorting her into the category *young man, unremarkable, dismiss*. She had been sorted ten thousand times. She knew the glance the way she knew the weight of a graver. But this man's version of it lingered half a heartbeat too long, and something behind his eyes moved, some small recalibration, and Oscar felt the old vertigo, the sense of walking on deck ice, before he looked away and she told herself she had imagined it. She had imagined it a hundred times before. Imagination was the tax invisibility paid.

"You'll be wanting something," Father said. "At this hour, in this weather, you'll want something."

"I want your press." The stranger said it plainly, the way other men asked for directions. "I'm told the Eriksen press is the finest working press between here and Copenhagen. Hand-built. Your own castings. I'm told you keep it oiled even now, even with trade the way it is."

"Who told you that?"

"A man who buys your type." He smiled without warmth. "Names are currency these days, Herr Eriksen. I don't spend them cheaply."

Father grunted, which was as close as he came to agreement. "And what would you print, that can't wait for morning?"

"Pamphlets."

The word dropped into the room like a coin into a well. Oscar, halfway down the stairs and half in shadow, felt the word go past her and down, felt the depth of the water it fell into. Pamphlets. In this year, in this autumn, from this man's mouth, the word had teeth.

"Seditious ones?" Father asked. There was something in his voice Oscar had not heard in years—something quickened, curious, hungry. She catalogued it with a printer's instinct for new sorts, and filed it where she filed everything that frightened her.

"The authorities would call them so," the stranger said. "I'd call them true ones. May I come out of the draft?"

Father hesitated exactly long enough to preserve his dignity, then stepped aside. The stranger crossed to the fire and stood before it, steaming gently, and set his satchel on the worktable with a care that told Oscar everything: whatever was inside mattered more than his coat, his boots, his comfort, possibly his life.

"My name is Voss," he said. "I've come about your other child."

The room tilted.

Not literally—the floorboards held, the fire burned, the rain kept its rhythm on the shutters. But something in Oscar's balance gave way, silently, the way a pier pile gives under ice, unseen until the whole structure lists. She gripped the rail.

"Your son," Voss said, unbuckling the satchel. "Anders."

"That boy is dead." Father's voice had gone flat and hard, a slammed drawer. "Three years drowned. If you've come to sell me grief—"

"I've come to sell you proof." From the satchel Voss drew a packet of letters tied with tarred cord, and laid them on the worktable, and untied the knot with fingers that were steady in the way of fingers that have practiced steadiness. "He writes to me. He has written to me for two years. He is alive, Herr Eriksen. Alive, and close, and in trouble, and I have come because you are the only man in this harbor who can help me help him."

Oscar did not move. She did not breathe. She stood on the stairs in the smell of wet wool and lamp oil and watched Voss open the first letter, and her entire consciousness narrowed to a single point: the paper in his hands, the paper turning, the moment when the writing would show.

She knew her brother's hand the way she knew her own pulse. The left-leaning slant, the letters that crowded each other like men pushing through a gate. The peculiar *d* with its flat crown. He had written her letters once, from the coast towns, full of gulls and wages and complaints about the cook aboard the *Maren*. She had kept them. She had burned them, later, one at a time, in the stove, because keeping them was dangerous—because her own replies, if anyone ever found them, were written in a hand that was almost his, taught to her by him across a winter table, and the two hands together told a story no one was meant to read.

The page opened under the lamplight.

There it was. The crowding letters. The flat-crowned *d*. The ink blotted where his pen always paused at the ends of lines, impatient, pressing hard.

Alive.

The word rang in her like a struck punch, ringing and ringing, and beneath the ringing came a flood of things she had no time to feel—joy with fear's face on it, grief un-dying, fury at this stranger for carrying the news through her father's door instead of hers—because Voss had begun to speak again and every word demanded her attention, and because above all, above everything, she must not let her face do anything at all.

A sister hearing her dead brother lives might weep, might cry out, might drop to her knees. A son hearing it might do any of those things too, but differently, and the difference was the whole architecture of her life, and so she stood absolutely still on the stairs and let the news break over her like weather, outwardly unmoved, inwardly drowning.

"Well?" Father said.

His voice was strange. Oscar risked looking at him. He had not reached for the letters. He stood with his arms folded and his jaw set, and his eyes—his eyes were shining. Not with tears. With something hotter.

"Read it yourself," Voss said, offering the page.

"I'm asking you to tell me."

Voss studied him a moment, and whatever he saw seemed to satisfy him. "Your son survived the wreck. He came ashore near Stralsund with nothing. He fell in with men—good men, serious men—who fed him and hid him and put ideas in his head, or perhaps the ideas were already there and only needed feeding. He's been among them since. Printing." A pause, delicate as a compositor's. "Among other trades."

"Printing," Father repeated. The word sounded different in his mouth than it had a minute ago. Warmer. Possessed.

"He needs to leave the country. There's a ship—the last civilian berth out of this harbor before the navy closes it, and they will close it, within the week. One berth. He means to take it. But to get here, to move freely, to leave anything behind worth leaving—" Voss touched the satchel, the letters, the invisible machinery of his purpose. "He needs a press. Your press. And hands to run it. For a few nights only."

"And why," Father said slowly, tasting each word, "should the Eriksen press serve your cause?"

For the first time, Voss smiled fully. It transformed him—made him look younger and more dangerous at once, a man who had found the door he'd been knocking on all evening.

"Because your cause and mine are about to be the same cause, Herr Eriksen. Because this spring the streets of every city from here to Palermo shook with men who discovered they outnumbered their masters. Because next spring they will shake again, and this harbor will shake with them, and the man who owns the only press worth the name in this town will not be a printer." He leaned forward slightly, rain still dripping from his hair, eyes bright as fresh-cast type. "He will be an arsenal."

Oscar watched her father receive this. Watched him straighten another inch, watched the drunkard's sag leave his spine as though the words themselves were stays. Eleven years she had known him as a diminished man, a widower, a craftsman whose best work was behind him and who knew it, drowning quietly in a narrow street. Now something stood up inside him, wearing his body, and looked around the shop like a landlord.

"One berth," he said. "One ship. And you want my press printing for—how many nights?"

"As many as the tide allows."

Father turned and looked up the stairs, directly at her, and Oscar made her face into the blank competent mask she had worn since childhood, the face that said *the son is present, the son says nothing, the son will do as asked.*

"Oscar," Father said. "Come down properly. Stop lurking like an apprentice."

She descended the last steps into the lamplight. Up close, Voss smelled of rain and river mud and something faintly of gun oil, and up close, his second glance at her was longer than his first, and there was no mistaking it this time: a flicker, a question, quickly banked. He knew something, or suspected something, or was simply the kind of man who noticed everything and spent nothing. She could not tell which, and the not-telling sat in her stomach like swallowed lead.

"This is my son," Father said, and there was pride in it, sudden and startling, as if the stranger's words had already begun paying dividends. "My heir. The best cutter of punches in this harbor, and I include myself. Whatever the press does, his hands will do it."

"Then his hands are exactly what I hoped for." Voss extended his own hand, and Oscar took it. His grip was dry now, warm, and it held hers a fraction past courtesy. "Oscar Eriksen," he said, trying the name. "A good strong name."

"It serves," she said, and her voice came out level, and she marveled at that from a great distance, the way one marvels at a bridge holding in a storm.

Voss gathered the letters back into their packet—but as he tied the cord, one sheet slipped loose and slid across the table's edge, and Oscar caught it without thinking, reflexes trained by ten thousand dropped sorts.

Anders's handwriting filled the page, top to bottom, alive, urgent, the crowding letters pushing through the gate. And at the bottom, beneath the signature she knew better than her own—

*Give this to whoever reads it. But if my sister is alive, give it to her.*

The room went white at the edges. The rain, the fire, her father's breathing—all of it receded, and there was only the line of ink, and the word *sister*, and the floor tilting beneath her like a deck in weather.

She looked up. Voss was watching her. Watching her with those patient, spending-nothing eyes, and his expression did not change at all, and that was how she knew.

He had let it fall.
