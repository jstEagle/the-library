## 3. Dead Men Write Neatly

The letters had been in Voss's coat for eleven days, and he had read them so many times he could recite them the way a priest recites a liturgy—without meaning, without feeling, the words worn smooth as river stones. He carried them against his chest now, in an oilskin packet that had crossed two borders and one frozen estuary, and as he climbed the crooked stair to Eriksen's type-foundry, he found himself thinking not of revolution, nor of the berth he needed, but of Anders's handwriting. The way it never changed. Not in Copenhagen, not in the mud of Schleswig, not in the last letter written from a safehouse in Lübeck with the ink still cheap and brown. *Dear Oscar,* it began, every time. *Dear Oscar,* to a man Voss had never met.

That was the first strangeness of the Eriksen shop, and he catalogued it the way he catalogued everything: carefully, without showing his hand. The younger Eriksen had answered the door above, a lean figure with ink-stained fingers and eyes the color of harbor water, and had looked at the letters in Voss's hand for exactly as long as it takes a man to recognize his own name—and then had looked away, and said nothing, and led him downstairs into the warm smell of metal and lamp oil as though nothing at all had passed between them.

Voss had been a courier for three years. He knew what recognition looked like. He also knew what it cost, and he filed the knowledge away where he kept everything: under *later.*

* * *

The workshop below was a cathedral built by a drunkard. That was Voss's second thought, and it arrived with something close to reverence. Punches hung in ranked cases along the walls like reliquaries—hundreds of them, steel gravers and files and half-finished matrices laid out on felt with a precision that spoke of either genius or madness. A casting machine squatted in the corner, brass and iron, older than the war, older probably than the century. And at its heart, on a scarred oak bench beneath a green-shaded lamp, lay a single piece of type no bigger than a thumbnail, cut so fine that Voss had to lean close to believe it. A lowercase *a*. Just an *a*. But the bowl of it was perfect, the terminal tapering to a hair, and when he held it up to the light it threw a shadow finer than anything the French foundries were producing, finer than anything coming out of Berlin.

"Careful," said the young Eriksen. The voice gave Voss his third strangeness—a voice pitched low and even, trained low, trained even, the way a man trains a dog not to bark. "It's unhardened. You'll leave a print."

"I'm sorry." Voss set it down. "I've never seen work like it."

"My father's trade."

"Yours?"

The pause was small. A hair's width of silence, the kind most men would step over without noticing. "The family's," said Oscar Eriksen.

From upstairs came the sound of a chair scraping, then a thud, then laughter—the loose, wet laughter of a man who has been drinking since noon and intends to continue through the dark. Voss watched the young man's face do something complicated and quick: a flinch, a tightening, a settling, all in the space of a breath. It was like watching water close over a stone.

"He'll be down directly," Oscar said. "You wanted the press."

"I want more than the press." Voss drew out the oilskin packet and laid it on the bench between them, next to the unfinished *a*. "But we can begin there."

* * *

He made himself wait. This was the discipline of his trade—not the running, not the hiding, but the waiting, the long stillness before the thing you have carried for a thousand miles finally leaves your hands. Old Eriksen came down the stairs with the tread of a much larger man, broad-shouldered and red-faced, with white side-whiskers and the booming cheer of a merchant who believes the world is finally arranging itself in his favor. He took Voss's measure in one glance, decided he was a customer, and poured three glasses of schnapps from a bottle he kept behind the casting machine.

"To the new age," Father Eriksen said, and drank.

Voss drank. He did not yet know what the new age would cost this man, and he would remember later, always, that he drank to it willingly, that he sat in the lamplight of this beautiful ruined workshop and began to spend a family the way a gambler spends chips.

"The letters," he said. He untied the oilskin and dealt them onto the bench like cards. Four letters. Cheap paper, brown ink, the dates spanning eighteen months. "Read them."

Old Eriksen picked up the topmost sheet and squinted at it, holding it far from his face the way aging men do. His lips moved. Voss watched him arrive, slowly, at the signature, and watched the color drain from the red of his face like wine from a broken glass.

"Anders," the old man said. Just the name. Then again, louder, as if volume could make the dead more plausible: "*Anders.*"

"He is alive," Voss said. "Or he was in October, which is the last I can vouch for."

The old man sat down heavily on the casting stool, which groaned. The younger one did not sit. Voss noticed that too—Oscar had gone very still beside the bench, hands folded, the posture of someone holding themselves together with thread.

"He went down with the *Freja*," the old man said. "Three years ago. We had the notice. We had—" His voice cracked and reformed around something harder. "We buried him, sir. There's a stone."

"There's a stone because the sea returned nothing else. The ship broke up off Rügen in a gale. Eleven men reached shore on wreckage. Your son was one of them." Voss kept his voice level, unhurried, a ferryman's voice. He had told this story four times now—to mothers, to a wife, once to a father who fainted—and each telling had taught him where to place the pauses. "He was half-dead for a month. Fever. When he recovered, he couldn't go home."

"Why not?" The old man's face was flushing back toward red, anger arriving to fill the space grief had opened. "Why wouldn't he come *home?*"

"Because dead men travel freely, Herr Eriksen. A living deserter travels badly. Your son had signed articles with a shipping line whose owners are friends of the Admiralty, and when the war took him they took his name with him. He surfaced in Stettin. There were men there—printers, students, dockworkers, men who believed what your pamphleteers only whisper. He fell in with them. He learned the trade of it." Voss tapped the letters. "Read the third one. Read what he asks."

The old man read. His lips moved again. Voss already knew what the letter said; he could see it forming on the old man's mouth like a reflection in water. *They will seal the harbor within weeks. The navy is coming up from Kiel. There is one ship that may yet clear the blockade and I need a place on it. I need the press, Father. I need you to let Voss use it, and I need you to trust him, because I do, and I am asking you to do for me the one thing I cannot ask in person: to help me disappear properly this time, alive, on my own terms.*

"Well," the old man said, and laughed, but the laugh had gone strange, high, fevered. "Well! My son. Alive. And mixed up in—all this." He gestured vaguely upward, toward the city, toward the year 1848 with all its smoke and shouting. Then his eye fell on the letters again and something shifted in his face, something Voss recognized with a professional chill, because he had seen it a hundred times in the past two years, in cellars and back rooms from Hamburg to Vienna. It was the look of a man discovering that history might be a vehicle rather than a weather. The look of a man realizing he might *drive.*

"They'll want pamphlets," the old man said. It wasn't a question. "Your committee. They'll want ten thousand of them. They'll want the harbor workers reading them at dawn. They'll want—" He stood up, swaying slightly, and his voice gathered itself into oratory. "They'll want a *voice*, and I have a foundry, sir, I have the finest matrices between here and Leipzig, and I have hands—" he seized his son's shoulder, and the younger man did not move, did not flinch, merely endured the grip like a post endures a rope— "*hands* that can cut anything, set anything, print anything God or the devil cares to put on paper."

"Herr Eriksen." Voss raised a palm. "Let me be plain about what I'm offering and what it costs."

"Coin," said the old man, waving it away. "I don't care about coin."

"You should. Coin is the least of it." Voss laid it out anyway, flatly, the way he laid out everything: "Two hundred marks now, two hundred when the run is finished. Use of the press for four nights. And afterward—passage. One berth on the ship that clears the blockade, for whoever your son names. That is the whole of it."

The old man was barely listening. Voss could see the arithmetic running behind his eyes, and it was not the arithmetic of money. It was larger. It was the sum of every slight the old man had ever swallowed from guildmasters and customs clerks and naval officers, all of it suddenly payable, all of it suddenly collectible, with interest. Voss had watched this transformation too many times to mistake it. Grief opens a man. Power fills the opening. And the man who walks in wearing mourning very often walks out wearing armor.

But it was the quiet one Voss was watching. All through the old man's swelling speech, Oscar Eriksen had not looked at the letters' contents. He had looked at the handwriting. At the *Dear Oscar*, at the brown ink, at the particular forward slant of a brother's hand—and Voss saw the fingers tighten once, whitely, and release.

There it was, then. Voss thought. *Oscar.* The name in the salutation, and no flicker of surprise on the face of the man it was written to. Which meant either that Anders wrote to his brother often enough that the name had lost its power to startle—or that the person standing in front of him, in the ink-stained apron, with the perfect *a* drying on the bench, was not what the name suggested.

Voss was a careful man, but he was not a cruel one, and some doors he left shut on principle. He filed it under *later*, alongside everything else, and turned back to the father.

"You understand," he said quietly, "that printing these pamphlets is treason. Not rhetoric. Treason, with a gallows attached. If the authorities find the press running seditious copy, they won't fine you, Herr Eriksen. They'll take the building, the tools, and possibly the hands that used them."

"Then let them come." The old man spread his arms, and the lamp threw his shadow huge across the matrices and punches, across the whole shining arsenal of the shop. "Let them come and see what a free press looks like. Let them come and find that the common man has *learned his letters.*"

In the corner, the younger Eriksen said nothing at all. But when Voss glanced over, he found those harbor-water eyes fixed on him—not on the father, not on the letters—with an expression Voss could not immediately classify, and did, eventually, and wished he hadn't. It was the look of someone watching a fire start in a house full of people she loves.

*She,* thought Voss, and put it away again, fast, deep, under lock.

"Four nights," he said. "Beginning tomorrow."

"Tonight," said the old man.

* * *

Voss left after midnight through streets that smelled of salt and coal smoke, and walked the long way to his lodging along the quay, because he needed the cold and he needed to think. The harbor lay black and still, masts against the stars, and somewhere beyond the breakwater, unseen, the ships of the blockade were already assembling in the dark water like a fist slowly closing.

He should have felt triumphant. He had done what he was sent to do. The press was secured; the pamphlets would be printed; the words would go out into the morning like birds released. Somewhere in a cellar in Stettin, Anders Eriksen was waiting for news that his family had answered, and Voss would send it, and it would be true: *they have the letters, they have the press, they are with us.*

And yet.

He kept returning, walking the quay, to the image of the young man's face at the moment the letters hit the bench. Not joy—that he had expected, hungered for almost, having carried this resurrection in his coat for eleven days. Not grief, or not only grief. What he had seen was older than both: the look of a person receiving news of a death that happened years ago and had never quite stopped happening. As if the letters had not brought a brother back but had instead confirmed something Oscar Eriksen had known in the marrow all along—that the people we love come back changed, and that the change begins the moment they leave.

And underneath that, threaded through it, something sharper. Because Voss had watched the old man read the salutation—*Dear Oscar*—and had watched him not react at all, had watched him sail straight past his own son's name on the page as though names meant nothing, as though the person standing beside him had never been called anything but what he called him now. *Boy. Son. Hands.* The old man had read the letter twice and never once spoken that word aloud.

Some families, Voss thought, keep their dead alive in the house without knowing it.

He stopped at the rail near the customs house and looked back at the town, at the crooked gables and the one lit window high on the hill where the foundry lamps still burned—he could see them from here, two small squares of gold, working late, the father and the son setting the first lines of the new age. Tomorrow those lamps would throw light on treason. Tonight they were just lamps.

He touched his chest, where the empty packet lay, lighter now by four letters and heavier by everything else. One berth. He had promised one berth, and he had watched, in the lamplight, a family of three hear that number and each silently begin their own counting. The father counted himself. The son—no, the *daughter*, whispered the locked drawer in his mind, and he pressed the lid down—the daughter had counted her brother, and then, briefly, unbearably, herself, before looking away from the arithmetic entirely.

One berth. Three claimants. And a harbor closing its hand at week's end.

Voss turned up his collar and walked on into the wind, and behind him the foundry windows burned steady and gold, printing nothing yet, promising everything.
