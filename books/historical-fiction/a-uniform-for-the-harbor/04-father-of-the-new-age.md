## 4. Father of the New Age

Henrik Eriksen had not been drunk in three days, and this was itself a kind of intoxication.

He noticed it on the second morning, standing at the shop counter with a ledger open before him, when his hand did not shake. For thirty years it had shaken—after the midday aquavit, after the evening brandy, after the long gray hours between when he told himself he was only resting his eyes. His hand had shaken so long that steadiness felt like an insult, like wearing another man's coat. But there it was: level, certain, capable of drawing a straight line across the page.

He knew why. He would not say why, even to himself, except in the language of grievance that was his native tongue. The times were changing. The world was tilting toward men who worked with their hands and minds and had been paid in scraps for both. Paris in February. Vienna in March. Berlin. Even Copenhagen, where the king himself had bowed to a constitution as though it were nothing, as though forty years of Henrik Eriksen's swallowed rage had been a coin tossed into a fountain and now, miraculously, the fountain gave it back.

He poured no drink that morning. He wanted his hands steady for whatever came.

The bell above the shop door rang at half past four, in the hour when the rain turned silver and the lamplighters were still abed. Henrik looked up from the ledger expecting old Fru Dahlman with her funeral invitations, or one of the shipping clerks wanting a hundred visiting cards by Friday, and instead saw a man standing just inside the door, dripping onto the floorboards as though he had walked out of the harbor itself.

"You are Eriksen," the man said. Not a question. "Eriksen the elder."

"And you are wet."

The man smiled. He was perhaps thirty-five, lean as a pike, with the kind of face that made you want to check whether your purse still hung where you'd left it—and yet there was something else under that, something almost gentle, the way a hook is gentle until it sets. "Voss," he said. "I've come about your press."

"My press is spoken for. It prints wedding announcements and customs forms and the obituaries of fishermen." Henrik closed the ledger. "If you want cards printed, come back tomorrow like a Christian."

"I don't want cards." Voss crossed the room without invitation, and Henrik—Henrik, who had thrown drunken sailors out of this shop with his own two hands—found he did not stop him. Voss stopped before the press itself, the great iron frame of it, older than Oscar, older than the marriage, older than the grief, and laid one hand flat against its crossbar the way a priest touches an altar. "I want ten thousand copies of the truth, and I want them before the harbor closes."

"Ten thousand of what truth?"

Voss turned. And here was the thing Henrik remembered afterward, the thing he would turn over in his mind during all the bad nights to come: the man's eyes went first to the window, checking the street, then to the stairway door behind which Oscar worked, and only then to Henrik himself. As if Henrik were the third consideration in his own shop.

"The truth about the blockade," Voss said. "About the grain the burghers are hoarding while they negotiate their exemptions. About the men your naval friends intend to impress off these quays to fight a war that isn't theirs. Ten thousand copies, Mr. Eriksen, and every one of them signed *A Citizen of the Harbor*."

Henrik laughed. It came out wrong—rusty, too loud, the laugh of a man whose throat has forgotten the motion. "You want me to print sedition. In my shop. Under my name."

"Under no name. That's rather the point of the press, isn't it? Any fool can sign his name. A printer makes ten thousand strangers speak with one voice." Voss drifted along the type cases, trailing one finger over the compartments, reading them like a blind man reads faces. "Good foundry stock. Some of it worn. You cut your own matrices?"

"Some."

"Then you're not a printer. You're a type-founder who got saddled with a press." Voss looked up, and his expression changed—the hook setting, gently, gently. "Do you know what I did before this? I was a schoolmaster in Lübeck. Latin and arithmetic to boys who will grow up to own everything I'll never have. Do you know what they called me? Nothing. They called me *Herr Voss*, which is to say, nothing with a hat on. And then February came, and suddenly every man who could read aloud was worth more than every man who owned a ship." He tapped the press frame. "That is what you're sitting on, Eriksen. Not iron. A voice. The loudest voice in this city that isn't owned by the burghers or the crown."

Henrik heard himself say, "It's my son who runs the composing room. The cutting is mine."

"Then you have a dynasty." Voss smiled again. "Which is more than most revolutions can promise a man."

They stood in the smell of ink and rain, and Henrik felt something move in his chest that he had not felt since he was young enough to believe the world would notice him eventually. He reached—for once without thinking of it—for the bottle under the counter, and stopped, and put his hand flat on the wood instead. Steady. His hand was steady.

"How much?" he asked.

They negotiated in the back room, over aquavit that Voss refused and Henrik, defiantly, poured for himself and did not drink.

Voss paid in coin—foreign coin, some of it, French francs worn smooth, Danish rigsdaler fresh from somewhere they should not have come from—and he paid well, Henrik noted, far better than the work was worth, which meant either generosity or purchase. He laid the money down in small stacks and pushed each stack across the table as he named its purpose: paper. Ink. Oilcloth to wrap the bundles. A boy to run copies to the fish market at dawn.

"And the rest?" Henrik asked, nodding at the final stack, the largest.

"That's yours."

"For what?"

"For being the man who owns the press." Voss leaned back, and the lamplight caught the planes of his face and made him look, briefly, like a portrait of somebody important. "Every enterprise needs a respectable front. You are a tradesman of thirty years' standing. No magistrate in this city looks twice at you. That respectability—that's worth more than my coin, and I pay it accordingly."

Respectability. Henrik turned the word over and found it bitter and sweet at once. Thirty years of respectability, of bowing to customs officers and calling magistrates *sir*, of watching Lindqvist rise from harbor clerk to harbormaster on the strength of a smile and a wife with connections—all of it, apparently, currency. All of it, suddenly, worth something. Not to him as he had been. To him as he was about to become.

"There's a condition," Henrik said. He heard his own voice and was surprised by it: firm, unhurried, the voice of a man across a bargaining table rather than beneath one. "My son works the case. Whatever you print, he sets the type. I don't let strangers into my matrices."

Something flickered across Voss's face—there and gone, too fast to read. "Agreed."

"And I set the headlines myself." Henrik surprised himself again. This had not been in his mind a moment ago; it arrived whole, like a memory of something he'd always wanted. "Display type. The big sizes. If this thing goes out with my city's name on it, the voice will be mine. My cutting. My hand."

Voss studied him for a long moment. Then he laughed—a real laugh, delighted, and Henrik hated how much he wanted it. "There he is," Voss said softly. "I was wondering when the printer would show up from behind the drunkard." He gathered his coat. "Tomorrow night, then. After dark. Bring your boy."

"He has a name."

"Of course he does." Voss paused at the door, rain-wet again already, haloed in the gray light. "Bring him anyway. Names are cheap this year, Eriksen. It's voices that cost."

The bell rang. The door shut. Henrik sat alone with five stacks of foreign coin and his own reflection dim in the dark window, and he discovered that he was smiling.

He told himself it was for Anders. That was the shape he gave it, the decent shape: his son alive, his son needing him, the press serving family blood. He held that thought up to the lamp and examined it, the way he'd examine a new-cut punch, turning it in the light for flaws. And he saw the flaw immediately, because he was a craftsman, and craftsmen know when a piece is cast true and when it isn't.

It wasn't Anders he thought of. It wasn't even the money.

It was the headline. *THE HARBOR SPEAKS.* Four words in sixty-point, his own matrix, his own hand, hammered into lead and then into paper and then into ten thousand fists raised on ten thousand corners. Somewhere in this city tomorrow, a man he'd never meet would shout those words, and they would be *his letters*, shaped by his graver, and the man shouting would never know, and it wouldn't matter, because the voice would be in the world regardless, unmistakable, immortal. Kings got statues. Printers got alphabets. And an alphabet, Henrik understood with a clarity that frightened him, outlives the man who cuts it, the way a father outlives—no. The way a father *is* survived. That was the whole of it. That was the whole secret ambition of his gray life, folded small inside the drinking: not power, exactly. Permanence. To be the hand that shaped what everyone else would speak forever after.

The revolution hadn't come to give men bread. Henrik saw that now. It had come to give men alphabets.

He swept the coin into the strongbox. On the topmost stack his fingers lingered—not taking, just resting, the way a man rests his hand on a door he intends to walk through soon.

Upstairs, faintly, he could hear Oscar moving in the workroom. He pictured his son bent over the stone, sleeves rolled, that careful face lit gold by the lamp—so serious, so steady, so much the heir Henrik had made of him. Pride moved through him, real pride, and underneath the pride something else, something with teeth, that whispered: *when the new age comes, remember who printed it. Let the committees argue. Let Voss spend his francs. The type is yours. The voice is yours. When they build the monument, make sure they spell the name correctly.*

*ERIKSEN,* he thought. *In sixty-point.*

He took the bottle out from under the counter one last time and looked at it a long while. Then he set it on the shelf where the lamplight caught the glass, unopened, like a trophy or a warning—he couldn't tell which, and for the first time in thirty years, he found he didn't care to know.

Tomorrow night they would print fire.

He went upstairs to tell his son.
