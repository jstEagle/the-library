## 5. What the Sister Cut

The type had a memory of its own. Oscar had learned this before she learned her letters—learned it with her hands, in the dark of the shop, running her fingers along the grooves of the sorting case the way other children ran their fingers along banisters. Each letter had its compartment, each compartment its small history of wear: the *e* worn smooth from overuse, the *z* still sharp as the day her father cut it, the *k* chipped at the shoulder because Anders, aged six, had dropped the case and wept more over the letter than over his own scraped knee.

She was thinking about that *k* now, three years after Anders went into the sea, because grief did not arrive all at once. It came back in pieces, like salvage washed up on the harbor stones.

It came back especially on nights when Voss's letters sat in the drawer upstairs and she could not stop hearing them—the neat, upright hand, the careful serifs of a boy who had been taught penmanship by a printer and pride by no one. *Dead men write neatly,* she had thought, and then she had gone cold all over, because she knew exactly how a dead man kept his hand so steady. She had been one herself for twenty years.

The lamp guttered. Outside, the rain had settled into the steady gray drizzle that made the harbor look like a pewter plate left out too long. Downstairs, her father's chair creaked, and glass met wood with the particular tenderness he reserved for drink. He was gentle with bottles. He had never once been gentle with anything else that mattered.

Oscar set down her graver and let herself remember—not the way she usually did, in snatches, slammed shut like a drawer—but properly, all the way through, because Voss had cracked something open in her and the crack would not close.

* * *

Her mother died in March, the year Oscar turned nine, in the week when the ice went out of the harbor and the whole city smelled of thawing fish and river mud. The fever took her in four days, and what Oscar remembered most clearly—not the funeral, not the coffin, not even her mother's face at the end, which had already begun to look like someone else's—was the sound of the shop below the sickroom. All through those four days her father had worked. She had heard the press thumping away like a great patient heart, hour after hour, printing funeral notices for other people's dead while his wife died above him.

He printed two hundred and forty broadsheets for the merchant Kessel's family, black-bordered, immaculate. He charged half price. When it was done he climbed the stairs, and the doctor met him at the landing, and whatever the doctor said made him sit down on the step like a man whose legs had been unscrewed.

Afterward there were the practical matters, and the practical matters were where her father lived, when he could live anywhere at all. There was no money for long mourning. There was a shop, a press, a trade that required hands and eyes and years of apprenticeship to pass on. There was Anders, eleven years old and sickly that winter, coughing in a way that made the doctor purse his lips. And there was Oscar, nine years old, tall for her age, with her father's own heavy jaw and her mother's dark hair cut short because lice had gone through the tenement row that autumn and her mother had sheared them all like lambs.

The lie, when it came, was almost gentle. That was the thing Oscar could never make anyone understand, later, when she tried to explain it to herself. It had not begun as cruelty. It had begun as arithmetic.

They stood in the shop on the morning of the fourth day after the burial. Her father had not slept; he looked like a man carved by someone in a hurry. He held her by both shoulders and looked at her for a long time, and she remembered thinking, with the strange clarity of children, *he is counting something.*

"Your brother is ill," he said finally. "The doctor says another winter like this one—" He stopped. Started again. "A trade needs an heir. That is how it works. That is how everything works."

"Yes, Father."

"If you were a boy," he said, "you could learn the letters. You could take the shop when my hands go. No one would question it. No one questions a son." His grip tightened. "You have your mother's hair. But under that cap, in that coat, in the dark—in the dark, Oscar, who is to say?"

And she—who had spent her whole short life watching her mother be talked over, priced out, pitied, sent to the back room when customers came—she understood before he finished speaking. Not the shape of the whole thing, not the twenty years of it, but the first small step of it, the way you understand a staircase by understanding one stair.

"You want me to be Anders," she said. "Not really. Just—on paper. To the street."

"I want the shop to live," her father said, and his voice broke on the word *live*, and she loved him so much in that moment that she would have agreed to anything, and did.

"It will just be for a little while," he said. "Until your brother is strong. Until things are easier."

That was the first sentence of the law. Every law since has begun the same way: *just until.*

* * *

The first year was the hardest and also, strangely, the sweetest. Her father taught her the graver the way he had always meant to teach a son—with a patience he had never shown anyone, because patience, he believed, was a form of inheritance and you did not waste inheritance on daughters. He put the steel punch in her hand and closed her fingers around it and said, "You feel for the grain. Metal has a grain, same as wood, same as people. Cut against it and it fights you. Cut with it and it thinks it wanted to obey all along."

She thought later that he had described himself without knowing it.

He taught her the alphabet backward and forward, the way the cases were sorted, why the *q* lived next to nothing and needed its box to itself. He taught her to read upside down and backward, fluently, so that setting a line of type felt like reading water. And every night he said goodnight to her at the foot of the stairs using the name they had agreed on, and every morning she came down as that name, and the street learned it, and the guild clerk wrote it in his ledger, and the church register—her father paid the sexton quietly, a man with debts—recorded the baptismal correction that made her officially, legally, in the eyes of God and the Crown, her brother's twin.

Oscar Eriksen. A fine name. It fit her like a coat cut for someone else, and she wore it anyway, and within a year she stopped noticing the seams.

Anders got better. That was the joke of it, the bitter kernel at the center. The fever passed; the cough dried up; by spring he was running the quay with the stevedores' sons, brown-legged and loud. And her father looked at his healthy son and his useful daughter and found—she watched him find it—that the arrangement suited everyone. Anders could be a child. Oscar could be an heir. The shop prospered. What harm? What harm, when the girl herself had stopped flinching at the name?

But she noticed the seams again the day the schoolmaster came.

She was twelve. A customer—a bookseller named Holm, a kind man with ink-stained cuffs—had brought his friend the schoolmaster to see the famous Eriksen punches, and the schoolmaster, peering at her across the workbench, said, "Remarkable hands for a boy your age. Where does he study?"

"He doesn't," her father said. "I teach him. Books are a distraction from the trade."

"A boy with those hands should have Latin," the schoolmaster said. "The guild examinations—"

"The guild examinations require nothing but proof of competence," her father said, in the voice he used to end conversations, "and I can promise you his competence."

Later, alone, Oscar asked him why he'd refused. Latin meant standing. Standing meant the guild council, the master's certificate, the right to cut matrices under her own mark instead of his. She had wanted it with a wanting that frightened her, that sat in her chest like swallowed coal.

"Because they ask questions at examinations," her father said. "Questions require answers. Answers require—" He waved a hand. "Paperwork. Consistency. One loose thread, Oscar, and the whole coat comes apart. You are safer being excellent than being examined. Do you understand?"

She said she understood. She did not. She understood only that the lie had grown teeth, that it now decided things without consulting either of them, that it had opinions about Latin.

* * *

By fifteen she had stopped thinking of it as a disguise at all, most days. That was the trap, though she wouldn't see the trap for years: the disguise worked so well that it became furniture, and furniture becomes invisible, and invisible becomes inevitable. She bound nothing—she had never needed to bind anything; she was built narrow and flat as a plank, a fact her father regarded as providence—and she spoke in the low register she'd practiced against the rain barrel, and she walked with her heels heavy, and none of it required thought anymore, which meant all of it required thought, forever, because the moment she relaxed was the moment some sharp-eyed customs officer or gossipy matron would catch the thread.

There were moments. There was the midwife's daughter, Lene, who at fourteen had kissed her behind the rope walk and then pulled back, frowning, saying, "You're not like the others," and Oscar had laughed and said, "No one is," and walked home fast with her heart hammering, and never let it happen again. There was the army conscription lottery the year she turned sixteen, when her father produced papers proving a childhood fever and a lame foot, bribing two doctors and sweating through his shirt the entire afternoon, and afterward drank for three days straight and called her, once, weeping, by her true name—*my girl, my girl*—before passing out with his face in the sawdust. She had knelt beside him and felt, for the last time, uncomplicated love.

Uncomplicated. Even then it wasn't quite true. Because kneeling there in the sawdust she had thought, with a clarity that shamed her: *he cries for the danger to the shop. He cries because if they take me, the presses stop.* And she could not unthink it, and she could not decide whether it was true, and the not-deciding was its own slow poison.

Tenderness curdles the way milk curdles: not all at once, not dramatically, but by degrees, in warmth, until one day you pour it out and it will not go back.

* * *

Anders left at nineteen. That was the wound under all the other wounds, the one the sea salted.

He had never wanted the trade. This was no secret; he complained about the smell of ink the way other boys complained about church. He wanted the water. He signed onto a coastal brig the spring the ice went out—March again, always March—and their father raged for a day and then, remarkably, relented, because a sailor son reflected well on a printer, gave the shop a maritime romance, and besides, Anders had said the fatal, sensible thing: *"You've got Oscar. You don't need me."*

*You've got Oscar.* As if she were a tool, well-maintained, interchangeable with any other tool. As if the whole architecture of her life—the name, the cap, the voice, the buried Latin, the buried Lene, the buried girl—were simply a machine her father had built and could point to, satisfied.

She had stood on the quay and watched the brig shrink, and Anders had waved from the stern, easy and golden in the morning light, and she had waved back with a smile she manufactured the way she manufactured everything, and inside her something had said, very quietly, in her mother's voice: *One of us gets to leave.*

Three years later the sea sent back word that it had taken him. A storm off Rügen; two ships lost; the list posted at the customs house with ERIKSEN, A., ordinary seaman, among the drowned. Her father had read the list standing in the crowd, and Oscar had watched his face do something terrible and private, and that night he had drunk himself under the table and woken up calling her by her real name and then, catching himself, correcting it, correcting it, correcting it, as if the mistake were the disaster—as if she were.

That was the night the curdling finished. She lay awake in her attic room and understood, at last, with no tenderness left to soften it: the lie had never been for her. It had been for the shop. She was not his son. She was his *succession*. And now that Anders was dead, she was not even that—she was a placeholder, a regent, keeping the seat warm for a king who would never come, cutting type in the dark for a man who could not look at her without seeing his own grief wearing a cap.

She had gotten up and gone downstairs and cut type until dawn. It was what she did instead of screaming. The letters were small and perfect and hers, whoever she was.

* * *

Now she sat in the same lamplight, three years older, with a stranger's promise folded in the drawer above her head and her brother's handwriting burning behind her eyes.

*Dead men write neatly.* But Anders was not dead. Anders was alive, and writing letters, and asking—for what? For the press, for coin, for help? Voss had said *the Eriksen press*, and her father had lit up like a lamp with a cracked chimney, dangerous and bright, and Oscar had stood in the corner being nobody, being the son, being the furniture, while men discussed the fate of everything she had bled for as though she were a bench they happened to be sitting on.

She looked down at her hands. Ink in the creases, graver callus on the right middle finger, the small white scar on the thumb where her father's first lesson had slipped. Twenty years of hands. They had built a life for a boy who did not exist, and the boy had become her, and she had become the boy, and somewhere underneath there was still a girl with her mother's dark hair who had wanted Latin, who had wanted Lene, who had wanted—to be asked. Once. Just once, to be asked what she wanted, rather than told what she was.

Downstairs, the chair creaked. Glass met wood.

And from the street below, through the rain, came a knock at the shop door—three blows, deliberate, at an hour when honest printers were abed—and Oscar blew out the lamp and sat in the dark with her heart going like the old press, and thought: *Whatever comes through that door, it will not wait for my father to answer it.*

She was already moving down the stairs before she decided to move.
