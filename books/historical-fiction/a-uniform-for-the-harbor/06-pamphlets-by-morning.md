## 6. Pamphlets by Morning

The order came at dusk, and it came in Father's voice, pitched higher than I had heard it since my mother was alive.

"Light every lamp," he said from the foot of the stairs, and then, because the shop had always answered to me as much as to him: "Oscar. Light every lamp."

I lit them. Seven of them, counting the one on the composing stone that I kept trimmed low for fine work, because a steady flame is worth more than a bright one when you are setting type by hand. The oil went fast that night; I remember thinking we would need to buy more, and thinking, in the same breath, that we would be buying it with money Voss had left in a canvas bag heavy enough to make the drawer stick.

The pamphlet copy lay on the stone where Voss had set it down that afternoon, squared to the edge of the marble like a man who respected a compositor's habits even if he did not respect anything else. *To the Sailors, Dockers, and Workingmen of the Harbor.* Below it, in smaller type: *You Are Told the Port Must Close for Your Protection. Ask Whose Protection.*

I read it twice before I touched a single sort.

It was good copy. That was the first betrayal of the night — not the politics, not the sedition, but the plain fact that whoever wrote it understood rhythm. Short lines against long. A question where an accusation would have blunted itself. I stood over the manuscript with my thumbs hooked in my apron strings and caught myself doing what I always did with any text put in front of me: hearing it in metal. Hearing which words would want italic emphasis, where the paragraph breaks should fall, how the broadside should be gridded so the eye went down it like a man going down a ladder he trusted.

"You've read it three times," Father said. He had come up behind me without my hearing, which meant he had been drinking steadily since supper and had crossed into the careful stage, the stage where his hands were still true. "Do you find fault with it?"

"I find fault with nothing yet," I said. "I haven't set it."

He laughed — a short bark, delighted with himself, with the night, with the century. "Then set it. Set it large. The headline across the full measure. Let them read it from across the street." He turned toward the stairs, then back, and there was something in his face I had never seen there before, or rather something I had seen only once, years ago, bent over my mother's deathbed: a man entirely certain that God was watching him personally. "My son," he said, "tonight you print history."

*Your son*, I thought, and said nothing, and began to distribute type.

* * *

There is a pleasure in composition that I have never been able to explain to anyone who has not felt it, and I will not try to excuse it now. The case stands open before you, ninety-odd little brass-and-lead rooms, each holding its letter upside down and backward, and your hand learns them the way a musician's fingers learn a keyboard — no, better than that, the way a sailor's hands learn rope in the dark. You do not look for the e. You reach for the e. The sentence arrives in your mind already translated into weight and space, and your hand moves through the case faster than thought can follow, and the line grows in your composing stick letter by letter until you lock it with the quoins and hold in your palm a small dense brick of language that will outlast everyone in the room.

That night I set the finest work of my life for a cause I did not believe in.

I want that recorded plainly, because everything afterward depended on it. I did not sabotage the type. I did not set it crooked, or loose, or ugly, though God knows the thought came to me around midnight, standing over the galley with my back aching and the seventh lamp burning low. I could have made that broadside look like what it was. I could have made it look cheap and frantic, the work of frightened men, and some part of the harbor would have read it and shrugged. Instead I gave it a two-column grid with a hairline rule between, because the eye trusts a rule. I gave the headline letterspacing wide enough to breathe, because grandeur is mostly spacing. I cut — actually cut, with the graver, at two in the morning — a small woodblock ornament of a rising sun for the foot of the sheet, six lines deep, because the copy ended on the word *dawn* and I could not bear to leave the word alone.

Father watched me cut that block and said, softly, "Where did you learn to draw?"

"From you," I said, which was true, and also a lie, because he had taught me the graver and the gouge but the drawing — the sense of what a line wants to be — that had come from somewhere he had never been shown, some room in me he had never entered because he believed the door led nowhere.

He nodded, satisfied, and went downstairs to count the money again.

* * *

Voss returned at one o'clock, dripping, smelling of tar and cold river. He did not knock; the door had stopped being locked sometime after supper, which should have frightened me more than it did. He stood in the doorway shaking rain off his coat like a dog and looked at the galley of set type, and I watched his face do something complicated.

"That's beautiful," he said.

"It's legible," I said.

"It's beautiful." He came closer, close enough that I could see the rain still beaded in the wool of his collar, and looked down at the composed pages the way men look at things they intend to take. "Anders told me about you. He said—" He stopped himself, and I let him stop, and the silence between us filled up with everything Anders might have said and neither of us could afford to hear.

"He said I was dead," I offered, dry. "Three years drowned. It saves writing letters."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. "He said you were the best compositor on this coast. He said your father took the credit, and you let him, and he never understood why."

The lamp flame leaned in the draft from the door. I reached out and trimmed the wick without looking away from Voss, because my hands needed employment. "Perhaps he didn't ask."

"No," Voss said. "He wouldn't have. He was nineteen and certain of everything." He pulled the wet canvas bag from inside his coat and set it on the stone beside the type — payment, or the second half of it — and then, instead of leaving, he pulled the stool from the corner and sat down on the far side of the stone, out of my light, the way an apprentice knows to sit. "How long to run five hundred?"

"Two hundred an hour once the forme is locked. Paper's the question. Where did you get paper?"

"The chandler on Södergatan owes the cause a favor."

"The chandler on Södergatan sells wrapping stock. It'll show through." I heard myself say it and hated how quickly I said it, how naturally the craftsman in me stepped forward to serve, offering improvements, volunteering. "Rag paper. There's a consignment in the cellar from the customs house job last spring. Heavy enough to take ink on both sides."

Voss looked at me for a long moment. In the lamplight his eyes were the color of the harbor in November, and they were not stupid eyes, whatever else could be said of him. "You could refuse," he said quietly. "You know that. No one would think less—"

"My father has agreed," I said. "The press prints what the press is paid to print. That's the trade. It's been the trade since Gutenberg." I turned back to the stone. "Paper's in the third crate. Mind the damp."

He got the paper. Of course he got the paper. And at half past one the press began to speak.

* * *

You have never heard a hand press working through the small hours unless you have lived above one, and even then you have heard it as lullaby, as weather. This was different. This time I was the one turning the bar, and Father pulling sheets, and Voss laying them on the drying lines strung across the shop like strange grey laundry, and the whole building groaned and ticked around us with the sound of its own industry. Platen meeting platen. The suck and release of damp paper. Five hundred impressions, and by the fourth hour my shoulder had a fire in it and my palms were printed faintly with the texture of the tympan, and I found I had stopped thinking about the words entirely.

That is the thing I most wanted to avoid and could not: the words disappear. When you compose, the meaning lives in your hands and not your head, and after enough hours the head goes quiet and there is only the work, and the work does not care what it says. *Ask whose protection.* *Dawn.* The sun ornament, six lines deep, my own graver-work, repeating itself down the drying lines in rows — dawn, dawn, dawn — until the word came apart into shapes and the shapes came apart into ink and the ink was simply black, honest black, the same black that had printed hymnals and shipping manifests and my mother's funeral card, all from this same press, all from these same hands.

Somewhere in the fifth hour Father began to sing. Low, tuneless, a marching song I didn't know, keeping time to the pull of the bar. Voss glanced at me across the press. Neither of us said anything. But I saw in his face what I had begun to feel in my chest, a cold draft under a warm door: the sight of a man being carried somewhere by his own noise.

At seven, with the light coming grey through the east windows, we hung the last sheet. Five hundred broadsides, drying in ranks. Father stood among them with his arms folded, ink to the wrists, and he looked older and younger at once, worn and lit, a man who had found at fifty-one the religion he'd been drinking toward his whole life.

"They'll be pasted up by noon," Voss said. "Every wall from the fish market to the customs house."

"Let them come and arrest the paper," Father said, laughing. "Arrest the ink. Arrest the sun." He touched the woodblock ornament on the nearest sheet with one ink-black finger, almost tenderly, and I understood with a lurch that he thought the sun was his.

I washed the forme. That is the compositor's last duty and her first mercy: you break the type apart, letter by letter, back into the case, and the argument that raged all night becomes silence again, becomes ninety small rooms of sleeping lead. I distributed every sort while the men talked downstairs about committees. My hands knew the way home. E to its room. D to its room. The whole furious sentence, taken apart and put away, as if it had never been said.

Except that five hundred copies existed now. Except that by noon they would be on walls, in fists, in the pockets of men who would carry them into places I could not follow. You cannot distribute a morning. What is printed stays printed. I stood at the case with a handful of e's and understood, finally and completely, that I had not been forced into anything. My father had agreed, yes. Voss had paid, yes. But the type had been set by these hands, beautifully, expertly, and no power on earth could make that anyone's doing but mine.

Complicity, I learned that night, does not feel like guilt. It feels like competence. It feels like a job well done.

Downstairs, the front bell rang — early customers, or the wrong kind — and Father's voice rose to answer it, bright and unafraid, a man inviting the world in to see what he had made. I put the last sort in its room and started down the stairs to see who had come.

It was Lindqvist. The harbormaster stood in our doorway with his hat in his hands and his eyes already on the drying lines, counting.
