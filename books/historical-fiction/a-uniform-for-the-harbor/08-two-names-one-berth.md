## 8. Two Names, One Berth

The news came the way bad news always comes to a printing shop: on paper, carried by someone who would not meet your eyes.

Voss arrived at dusk on Thursday, shaking rain from his coat in the doorway like a dog coming off a boat, and he did not go to Father first. That was the thing I noticed before anything else—the small wrongness of it. For six days he had been our guest, our hired hand, our fellow conspirator in ink, and every evening he had made his report to Father Eriksen as though Father were a general and he a lieutenant delivering dispatches. Tonight he crossed the shop floor past the composing stone where Father sat sharpening his opinions aloud for the apprentices' benefit, and he came to me.

"Oscar." He said my name quietly, but not so quietly that I missed what he was doing with it. Not *Eriksen*. Not *boy*. He had learned my name the night we hid the press type in flour sacks, and he had used it only twice since, each time when he wanted something.

I kept my burnisher moving over the forme. "If you've come to complain about the ink again—"

"There's a ship."

The burnisher stopped. I hadn't decided to stop it; my hand simply understood before I did.

"The *Svanen*," Voss said. "Lindqvist's own vessel, or near enough. She sails on the morning tide Sunday. One civilian berth has been granted—one—and the harbor closes after her. After that, nothing leaves this port that the navy doesn't carry out as cargo or prisoner."

"One berth." The words tasted like lead shot. I set the burnisher down with exaggerated care, because if I didn't give my hands a task they would begin to shake, and I had spent twenty-six years teaching them never to shake. "Anders is alive. Anders needs it. So there is no arithmetic here, Voss. There is one answer."

"That's what I told him."

I looked up. Rain ran down the window behind him and the lamplight caught it, so that he seemed to stand inside a curtain of falling glass. "Told him what?"

"That you'd say that." Something moved across his face—not quite a smile, more the place where a smile had been excavated out of harder material. "He argued. He's good at arguing now. Three years among committee men will do that to a person."

"He was good at arguing at fourteen. He argued his way out of confirmation class twice."

"And now he argues about whether you should have the berth instead."

* * *

Father had heard the word *ship*. He rose from the composing stone with the ponderous majesty of a man who had recently discovered himself to be important, and crossed the floor wiping his hands on his apron—a clean apron, I noticed; he had taken to wearing a clean apron since the pamphlets began, as though ink were beneath him now rather than the family bread.

"A ship," he said. "So. The *Svanen*. Lindqvist's tub." He said the harbormaster's name the way he'd begun saying all official names lately, with a curl of contempt that sat strangely on a man who had sold that same harbormaster job-printing for fifteen years. "When do we print the farewell broadside? The people should see the movement's press mark the moment. *The harbor closes, but the word goes free.*" He tried the sentence out loud, tasting it, and I watched him fall in love with it between one syllable and the next.

"There is no broadside, Herr Eriksen." Voss turned to face him, and I saw him square himself the way a man squares himself before a wave. "There is a berth. One. And the question of who fills it."

Father blinked. It was genuinely astonishing to watch—the idea entering his mind and finding no furniture there to sit on. "Who fills—" He laughed, short and hard. "Why, I do. Naturally."

The rain filled the silence.

"Naturally," I repeated, because someone had to say it back to him, and Voss seemed struck dumb, and the apprentices had been sent home an hour ago, and God apparently had no other clerk on duty.

"I am the printer of the cause," Father said, and here was the terrible thing: he did not say it defensively. He said it the way a bishop says *I am consecrated*. "Every pamphlet in this city that matters came off this stone. My hands set it. My shop sheltered it. When the histories are written—and they will be written, boy, that is what printing *is*, the writing of history while it bleeds—my name is in those pages. Do you think the committee sends its martyrs' children to beg passage from naval officers? No. They send ships. They sent one ship, and it is mine by right of work."

"You're not on any list," Voss said flatly. "I've seen the lists."

"Lists can be amended. That is what committees are for." Father smiled at him, and it was the smile he used on customers who balked at price—warm, patient, faintly pitying, the smile of a man who knows the other party will come around because the alternative is being wrong. "You'll speak to them. You have their ear. Tell them the Eriksen press asks only what it has earned."

"It isn't yours to ask for."

The words dropped into the room like a sort into the wrong case. Father's smile stayed fixed but everything behind it moved. "Excuse me?"

"The berth belongs to Anders. That was the arrangement from the beginning—the letters, the money, all of it. Anders Eriksen, returned from the dead, sails out before the blockade takes him. That is what the network promised. That is why I'm standing in your shop drinking your terrible coffee."

"Anders is dead," Father said. "Dead men don't sail. Dead men don't—" He stopped. Recalculated. I could see the abacus working behind his eyes, beads sliding: if Anders lived, then Anders had let them grieve three years; if Anders lived, then Anders had written to strangers and not to his father; if Anders lived, then Anders was a radical with a price on his name, which was either a scandal or a glory depending on which newspaper won. "Anders," he said again, softer, testing the shape of the resurrection. "My son."

Something in my chest went very quiet and very cold, the way the shop went quiet when the stove went out overnight and you came down in the morning to find the type frozen in its case.

"Yes," Voss said. "Your son. Who asked me to tell you—" and here Voss hesitated, and I understood with a lurch that he was editing, choosing, doing to the truth what I did to metal, cutting away everything that wouldn't fit the line, "—that he thinks of the family. That he wants the family safe."

"What else did he say?" I asked.

Voss looked at me. And I knew—I knew the way I know a kerned pair from a crashed one—that there was more, and that the more was addressed to me, and that he was deciding in front of my father whether to deliver it.

"He said," Voss said slowly, "that the berth was never meant for him alone. That a berth is a small thing, and a family is large, and that if the right person wanted it—" 

"The right person is me," Father said.

"—then the committee would look at other names. Other lists." Voss had not taken his eyes off me. "There are ways to move a name up a list. There are ways to put a name on a list that wasn't there before. Harbormasters keep imperfect books."

"Lindqvist keeps perfect books," I said. "I've printed his manifests. He counts everything twice."

"Then he can be persuaded to count once."

Father made a sound of disgust. "Bribery. Is that what the new age comes to? I thought we were building a republic of virtue, and instead it's the old trade with redder flags." But even as he sneered, I watched his hands—his beautiful, ruined, ink-stained hands—curl slightly toward his chest, guarding something. Guarding the berth. He had already spent it in his mind; already stood on the deck of the *Svanen* in exile, the honored printer of the revolution, welcomed in some freer port with speeches and commissions, while the shop—

While the shop sat empty behind him. While I—

I did not finish the thought. I had gotten very good, over twenty-six years, at not finishing certain thoughts.

* * *

Later—much later, past midnight, when Father had gone upstairs to sleep the sleep of the anointed and the shop held nothing but the banked stove's red eye and the smell of oil and metal—Voss found me at the stone. I was setting type I didn't need to set, a practice card, my hands wanting work the way other people's hands want drink.

"He wrote to you," I said without looking up. "Not the speech for Father. The real letter. What did it say?"

Voss leaned against the frame of the press. In the low light he looked older than he had the first night, rain-soaked and desperate on our doorstep; the desperation had burned down to coals and the coals had a longer patience in them. "He said: *Tell Oscar.*"

My hands went still on the stick. Just my hands. The rest of me stayed perfectly ordinary, a tired apprentice at a late bench.

"Not *tell my father*. Not *tell the family*." Voss's voice was quiet, pitched under the tick of the cooling stove. "*Tell Oscar.* He said you'd understand. He said—" Voss paused, and I realized he was deciding whether to give me the exact words, and that he was the kind of man who believed exact words were a debt. "He said: *Oscar always does the sums. Ask Oscar what the sum comes to.*"

The type in my hand trembled, one letter clicking against its neighbor, the smallest sound in the world.

Dread arithmetic. That was what Anders had named it, without knowing he was naming it, without knowing that I had been doing it since the stranger walked in with his letters. One berth. Three Eriksens. A father who believed heaven owed him a deck to stand on; a brother three years drowned and risen, who had asked for me by name—or almost by name, by the name I wore like a coat buttoned to the throat. And underneath it all, the figure no one else could see, the number that made the whole column refuse to balance: that if I took that berth, I would take it as Oscar Eriksen, son, heir, the lie entire, and I would carry it across the sea and live inside it until it grew over me like bark. And if I gave it away—if I gave it to Anders, or to Father, or to the sea itself—then I stayed. I stayed with the pamphlets and the lists and Lindqvist's perfect books, and I stayed as whatever I had been all along, which was the only self I had ever actually inhabited.

One berth. Two names. And mine, the true one, belonging to neither column.

"Tell him," I said finally, and my voice came out level, which cost me more than shouting would have, "tell him the sum isn't finished. Tell him I'm still counting."

Voss straightened. At the door he paused, hand on the latch, rain loud beyond it. "For what it's worth," he said, "he didn't hesitate. When he wrote your name. Whatever you think the sum comes to—he'd already counted you in."

The door closed behind him. I stood a long time in the dark shop, listening to the rain, and then I went back to the case and began pulling letters, one by one, sorting them true.
