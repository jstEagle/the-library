## 9. The Committee of the Faithful

The cellar beneath the Eriksen shop smelled of lamp oil, wet stone, and the particular sourness of men who had been arguing in secret for too long. Voss counted them as they came down the stairs one by one—nine, then ten, then eleven—and noted which ones looked at the floor and which looked at him. The ones who looked at the floor were the careful ones. They would survive.

He had chosen the cellar for its acoustics before he'd chosen it for anything else. Sound rose through the floorboards into the print shop above, but it did not travel sideways into the neighboring buildings; he had tested this himself two nights ago, standing in the dark with his ear pressed to the coal chute while Oscar Eriksen walked overhead, her footsteps crossing and recrossing like a shuttle through a loom. She worked late. He was beginning to understand that she always worked late.

"Sit," he said, and they sat, on crates and upturned barrels and a bench some previous tenant had built and abandoned. A single lantern hung from a nail driven into a joist, and its light made the cellar feel smaller than it was, made every face look like a face carved in tallow.

They were dockers, mostly. Two clerks from the customs house who would not give their names even here. A woman named Fru Halvorsen whose husband had died at the customs house riot three years back—not *the* riot, not yet, that was coming, but an earlier, smaller one that no pamphlet had ever bothered to commemorate. She sat nearest the lantern and she did not fidget, and Voss marked her as the only person in the room besides himself who understood what they were about to do.

Anders's letters had described these meetings as something between a church service and a war council. Anders wrote beautifully about such things—he wrote beautifully about everything now, which was itself a kind of warning—but the reality was more mundane and more fragile: eleven frightened people in a damp room, waiting for someone to tell them that their fear was actually courage.

Voss let them wait. It was a technique he had learned watching better men than himself, and then watching those men hang.

* * *

Eriksen came down last, and he came down like a man descending into his own coronation.

Voss had seen the transformation begin days ago, in the shop, over the type cases, when the old man first held a proof of the pamphlet in his ink-stained fingers and read his own words set in his own type. Something had lit behind his eyes then, and it had not gone out since. It burned now, steady and wrong, as Eriksen took off his coat with the deliberation of an actor and folded it over a barrel as though the barrel were a chair of state.

"My friends," he said.

He had never met most of them before tonight. *My friends.*

"You have been told that nothing can be done. You have been told that the navy owns the water and the garrison owns the streets and the merchants own everything else, and that a man of no property, a man of no name—" here he spread his hands, and Voss saw the ink stains, saw how he displayed them, turned them outward like wounds or medals—"a man with nothing but his hands must accept what is given to him and be grateful."

Murmuring. The clerks exchanged a glance.

"I say to you: the age of gratitude is over." Eriksen's voice filled the cellar the way water fills a hull—not all at once, but finding every low place. "I have spent thirty years cutting letters. Small letters. Letters so small you could lose them in a crease of your palm. And I will tell you what I learned at my case, night after night, while the great men of this city slept: *letters are power.* Whoever shapes the words shapes the thought. Whoever prints the word puts it into a thousand hands, and a thousand hands become ten thousand, and ten thousand hands—" he closed his own fist, slowly, savoring it—"can close around the throat of this harbor."

Fru Halvorsen said nothing, but she leaned forward slightly, and Voss watched her lean and wished she hadn't.

"The press is yours," Eriksen declared. "I say it plainly, before God and before you: the Eriksen press belongs to the cause. Print whatever the committee decides. I will cut new type if the old proves insufficient—I will cut type at any hour, I will cut type until my eyes fail—for there is no work more holy than the work of putting truth into the common hand."

It was a good speech. That was the trouble. Voss had heard a hundred good speeches in a hundred cellars in a hundred cities, and he had learned to measure them not by their effect on the crowd but by their effect on the speaker. By that measure, Eriksen's speech was catastrophic. The old man believed every word, and belief of that temperature does not warm; it burns through the vessel holding it.

The vote, when Voss called for it, was unanimous. Of course it was. Eleven people in a lamplit cellar do not vote against a man who has just promised them a printing press.

* * *

Afterward, while the others climbed the stairs in twos and threes, careful as thieves leaving a bank, Voss stayed by the lantern and pretended to study a copy of the pamphlet proof. He heard the shop door open above—the bolt, the hinges, the bolt again—and then quieter sounds: the scrape of a stool, the soft ring of type being sorted back into its case. She was working. At midnight, after a committee meeting held in her own cellar, she was sorting type.

He climbed the stairs and found her exactly where the sound had placed her, at the composing stick, her sleeves rolled, her cap slightly askew, the lamplight catching the fine dust of metal on her forearms. She did not look up.

"They're gone," he said.

"I know. I hear the door." Her hands kept moving, quick and exact, each character finding its slot with a small click like a lock turning. "Your committee voted well."

"You weren't there to vote."

"I don't need to be there. My father votes for me. He has been voting for me my whole life." She set a line, checked it against the copy with a glance that missed nothing, and reached for the next. "You got your press."

"We got a press. There's a difference, and the difference is whether anyone can take it away."

That made her look up. Her eyes in the lamplight were the color of the Baltic in November, and just as difficult to read. "You think someone will try to take it?"

"I think," Voss said carefully, "that your father stood in front of eleven strangers tonight and gave away a thing that isn't entirely his to give."

The click of the type stopped.

"It's his shop," she said. "His press. His name over the door."

"And yet you're the one who set tonight's pamphlet. Every line of it. I watched you. You corrected his punctuation twice without asking him, and he didn't notice, because he doesn't notice, because noticing is your half of the labor and glory is his." Voss kept his voice level. This was the part he was bad at—the part where you told someone a true thing at the moment it could still help them, knowing they would hate you for it. "I've seen this before. In Rostock. In Danzig. A man gets a taste of being listened to, and it goes to his head like spirits to an empty stomach. Your father drank for thirty years downstairs in this shop. Now he's found a stronger drink, and the whole city is the tavern."

"He's a good man."

"He was, maybe. Good men are the easiest ones to remake. Bad men already know what they are." He stepped closer, lowered his voice though there was no one left to hear. "Listen to me, Eriksen. I came here to buy passage for a dead man, and instead I've bought a press with a fanatic attached to it. The committee thinks it owns the press. Your father thinks the cause owes him. And somewhere between those two ideas, someone is going to decide that the shop, the type, the whole establishment—it's all expendable. Men like your father, once they've promised everything to a cause, eventually promise the one thing left. Themselves. And everyone standing near them."

Oscar set down the composing stick. For a long moment she said nothing, and Voss watched her face do something complicated and private—the expression of a person weighing a warning against a loyalty, and finding the scales rigged.

"You don't know him," she said finally. "You've known him nine days."

"I knew men in Rostock for nine years."

"Then you know that men change back, too." She picked up the stick again. The conversation was over; her hands said so. "Go home, Herr Voss. Sleep. Tomorrow we print five hundred more, and my father will stand at your committee and make speeches, and none of it will matter, because revolutions happen elsewhere. They always have."

She bent to her case. The type clicked. Clicked. Clicked.

Voss stood in the doorway a moment longer, wanting to say something else and knowing there was nothing—no argument sharp enough to cut through a daughter's hope, no proof that wouldn't arrive too late to be believed. He had delivered the warning. That was all a man like him could do: deliver warnings to people who mistook them for insults, and then, afterward, remember that he had warned them.

Outside, the rain had started again, fine and cold off the harbor. Somewhere beyond the breakwater, past the dark water, the naval squadron lay at anchor with its guns run out, patient as arithmetic. The port would close within the week. One ship would sail.

Voss pulled his collar up and walked into the wet streets, and behind him, through the shuttered window of the Eriksen shop, the small bright sound of type being set went on and on—click, click, click—like a clock counting down to something none of them had yet agreed to name.
