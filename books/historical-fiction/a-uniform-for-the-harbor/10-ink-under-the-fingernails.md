## 10. Ink Under the Fingernails

The knock came at half past nine, three quick raps and then a fourth, delayed—the pattern Voss had taught them, though he was not due until morning.

Oscar set down her graver mid-stroke. The letter *k* she was cutting sat unfinished on its punch, one arm missing, and she stared at it a moment as if the flaw might tell her something. Her father was downstairs. She heard him rise from his chair by the stove, heard the bottle neck knock against the table edge, and then he was on the stairs, moving faster than a man his age and his drinking ought to move.

"Through the yard," Voss said through the door, low and quick, before Father could even lift the latch. "Lindqvist's men are sweeping the quarter. They started at the customs house an hour ago. They'll reach this street before midnight."

Father opened the door only as wide as the chain allowed. "Sweeping for what?"

"For pamphlets. For presses. For printers." Voss glanced past him, up the stairs, and found Oscar standing in the doorway of the workroom with the lamplight behind her. "For us."

* * *

They moved the press in pieces.

Oscar had taken it apart twice in her life—once to replace a broken spring when she was fourteen, once to clean out the flooded cellar water after the storm year—and both times her father had stood over her narrating, as though the knowledge flowed from him downward into her hands. Tonight there was no narration. Tonight there was only the work: the screw turned back, the platen lifted free, the carriage unbolted from the bed, each piece wrapped in sacking and old aprons and carried down the outside stair to the coal cellar, where the hatch opened onto the alley and the night came in cold as well water.

Her father wanted to argue about where. He wanted the pieces under the floor of his own bedroom, because it was *his* press, *his* house, and if they were going to take it from him they would have to take it from under his sleeping body. Voss talked him out of it with the patience of a man feeding a fire one stick at a time—bedrooms were searched first, always; cellars last, because coal dust ruined a man's uniform and soldiers knew it—and Father yielded, but yielded the way ice yields, all at once and dangerously, going out to stand watch at the front window with his coat over his nightshirt and his face pressed to the glass like a boy's.

That left Oscar and Voss in the coal cellar with the dismembered heart of the shop.

"We need them off the floor," she said. "If they do come down here, loose boards read as loose boards. But a fresh stack of sacking against the wall reads as nothing at all."

He looked at her. In the single candle she'd brought down, his face was half shadow, and rain still stood in beads on his shoulders where he hadn't stopped to dry. "Show me," he said.

So she showed him. They built a false wall of sacking bales against the true wall, nested the heavy iron—the frame, the bed, the platen—in the hollow between, packed the gaps with coal shoveled from the bin until the metal wore a skin of black, and laid the smaller pieces flat beneath the coal itself. The type cases she would not bury. Type buried in damp dies; type was her father's fortune and hers, cut over thirty years, some of the punches older than she was. She carried the cases up two flights to the attic instead, wedged them behind the chimney breast where the bricks held warmth, and covered them with her mother's old quilts, which no searcher would disturb because no searcher cared what a printer slept under.

When she came back down, her arms streaked black to the elbow, Voss was standing in the middle of the cellar with the candle, looking at the space where the press had been.

"It's strange," he said. "I've helped hide three presses now. It never stops feeling like a burial."

"It isn't a burial." She took the shovel from him and began smoothing the floor, dragging the coal dust back into its ordinary drifts. "A burial stays buried. This is a held breath."

* * *

They did not go up.

That was the practical truth of it and neither of them pretended otherwise. The sweep was coming; the front room was a fishbowl with her father's face pressed to the glass; the attic was type and quilts and no exit. The cellar had the alley hatch, and the hatch needed watching, and someone had to sit in the dark with the candle guttering and listen for boots. Two someones, because one pair of ears was not enough and because—though neither said this either—her father upstairs, drunk and vigilant, was worth less than nothing as a sentry. He would challenge a patrol single-handed. He would *welcome* them, half the time, if they came to praise him.

So they sat in the coal cellar on an overturned flour barrel and a crate that had once held Swedish paper stock, and waited.

The candle they let burn low and then pinched, because light under the door was a confession. After that there was only the grey seep of the harbor lamps through the hatch seams, enough to make shapes and not enough to make faces. Somewhere above them her father's boots went back and forth across the floorboards, slow, metronomic, a man pacing out the length of his own importance.

"They'll pass us," Voss said after a while. "Lindqvist wants printers, not coal merchants. And he—" He stopped.

"He knows us," Oscar finished. "I know. He bought his wedding announcements from my father. And his mother's funeral bills." She rubbed at her fingernails with her thumbnail, working at the ink-black coal dust ground into the quick. It wouldn't come out. It never fully came out; after a day at the cases her nails were always faintly ringed, and she had learned young to keep her hands closed in company. "Do you know what I was doing when you knocked?"

"No."

"Cutting a new *k*. Our old k punches are worn soft at the join—it fills in when we print small sizes, closes up into an *h*. I've been meaning to recut it for two years." She flexed her fingers in the dark. "I was thinking about the curve of an arm on a piece of metal four millimeters tall while men with bayonets walked toward this street. That's what this life does to you. It makes the letters bigger than the world."

"Is that a complaint?"

She considered it honestly, which was more than she usually gave anyone. "No. That's the terrible thing. I think it might be the only way I'd want to live. Just—smaller than this. Without the bayonets."

The silence that followed was not uncomfortable, which surprised her. With most people silence was a debt waiting to be collected. With Voss it seemed simply a room he was willing to sit in.

"You cut your own punches," he said at last. "Not just type. The matrices too."

"My father taught me. Or—he taught his son, rather." The lie moved through her mouth so easily now it hardly cost anything, and yet tonight, in the dark, beside this man who had carried her brother's handwriting folded against his chest through three countries, it cost something. She felt it go through her like a swallowed stone. "There wasn't money to buy matrices from the foundries in the south. So we struck our own. Every letter in this shop exists because someone bent over a bench with a file for eleven hours."

"And your father did that? Eleven hours a letter?"

"I did most of them," Oscar said, "after the first year."

Voss was quiet for a moment. In the seam of lamplight she saw him turn his head toward her—not sharply, not with the sudden hunger of a man catching a lie, but slowly, the way a man looks at a horizon when the weather changes.

"After the first year," he repeated.

"The first year he was grieving," she said, before he could build anything out of it. "My mother died. He couldn't hold a graver steady for months. His hand shook. Someone had to finish the order for the Rostock hymnal or the shop went under, and I had been setting type since I was seven because my brother—" the stone again, larger, and she made herself swallow it whole— "because there were only the two of us children and children in a shop work."

"Anders set type too," Voss said quietly. "Before he shipped out. He told me. Said his sister was better at it than either of them."

The cellar seemed to tilt, very slightly, the way a deck tilts.

"He told you about me."

"He told me everything about you." Voss's voice stayed level, but something underneath it had shifted, the way water shifts under ice. "How you kept the accounts from the age of ten. How you could tell a bad casting by sound alone. How he used to watch you strike matrices and think that if anyone ever found out, half the guilds in the Baltic would burn their charters out of sheer embarrassment." A pause. "He said you'd hate the pamphlets. Not the cause—the printing of them. He said you'd think the type deserved better than slogans."

Oscar laughed. It came out of her before she could stop it, short and cracked, the first laugh in days, and it frightened her a little, how good it felt, here in a coal cellar with a patrol possibly turning into their street at any moment.

"That," she said, "is exactly what I thought. While I was setting them. Word for word, I thought: my brother knows me better than my own father does, and my father has lived with me twenty-six years."

"Does that anger you?"

"No." She turned her hands over in her lap, palms up, useless in the dark. "It's the loneliest thing I've ever been told, and I'm grateful for it. Both at once. I didn't know a person could be both."

Above them, her father's boots stopped.

They both went still. For a long moment there was nothing—no footsteps, no creak, just the absence of him, which was somehow louder. Then the front door latch lifted, and voices came down through the floorboards, muffled to grain but unmistakable in shape: the clipped official cadence, the lantern-word, the word *papers*, and her father's voice rising underneath it, grand and slurred and terribly eager, saying something that contained the phrase *the people's right to speak*.

Voss was already on his feet. Oscar caught his sleeve.

"No." Her whisper came fast and hard. "If you go up, it becomes real. If you stay down, he's a drunk printer arguing with a corporal, and drunk printers argue with corporals every night in this city right now. Let it be small. Let him be foolish. Foolish is survivable."

Voss looked down at her hand on his arm. He didn't pull away.

"You're protecting him," he said. "After everything."

"I'm protecting the press," she said. "He happens to be standing next to it."

The voices went on. Boots crossed the floor overhead—two pairs, three, the measured tread of men who were bored and thorough and not looking for anything in particular—and Oscar counted them the way she counted lines of type, automatically, completely. Four men. One lantern. The scrape of a chair. Her father offering brandy, God help them, offering *brandy*, and the corporal's answer lost in the joists. Then, mercifully, the front door, the latch, the street.

The boots faded. Rain filled the silence back in.

* * *

They sat down again. Neither suggested going up.

"He offered them brandy," Voss said.

"He offers everyone brandy. He offered the tax assessor brandy. He tried to offer the pastor's widow brandy at my mother's funeral." Oscar leaned her head back against the cool brick. "You should understand something about my father, since you're going to be part of this family whether either of us likes it or not. He is not lying to you. When he talks about the new age, about men like him finally mattering—he means it. Every word. That's what frightens me. A liar you can predict. A believer will walk straight through a wall for his faith and call the bleeding proof."

"And you don't believe."

"I believe in letters," she said. "I believe a *k* should be a *k* at any size, and a man should be able to say what he is at any hour of any day, without the sentence costing him the shop. That's the revolution I'd sign. It's smaller than theirs. It would fit on a visiting card." She paused. "But I notice I believe it more fiercely every year. So perhaps I'm a believer too. Perhaps that's the family disease."

Voss was quiet. Then, in the thin light, he reached over and took her right hand—not romantically, not even gently, but with the deliberate care of a craftsman examining another craftsman's work—and turned it palm-down toward the seam of light.

"Ink under the fingernails," he said. "Coal dust over it. You can't scrub either one out. Anders said you'd have ink under your nails. He said he'd know you anywhere in the world by that alone."

Something in Oscar's chest went very still and very warm, like the moment just after a sheet comes off the press and the impression is perfect and you know—before anyone checks, before the count is confirmed—you *know* the run is good.

"Three years," she said. "He let us bury an empty box. He let my father drink himself gray over it. And he wrote to a stranger and not to us."

"He wrote to me because a letter to this address would have been opened. He wrote to me because I could get here and a letter couldn't." Voss released her hand, but slowly. "And there's more. There's a reason he sent me and not just paper. He has something for this family. Something he wants read aloud, and he was specific about it—specific about *who* should read it, and to whom." He hesitated, and in the pause Oscar heard the harbor bell toll the half hour, far off, patient as arithmetic. "He made me swear I'd wait until the port situation settled. Until you knew how many ships. Until the choice was real and not theoretical."

"What choice?" she asked. But her heart had already begun to count, the way it counted everything, and she thought she already knew.

Voss stood, and brushed the coal dust from his knees, and looked at the false wall of sacking that held the buried press the way a man looks at a promise he isn't sure will be kept.

"Tomorrow night," he said. "When your father sleeps. I have a letter from your brother, and he asked me to put it in no hands but yours."
