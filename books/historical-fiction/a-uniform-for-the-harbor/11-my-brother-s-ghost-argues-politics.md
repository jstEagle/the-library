## 11. My Brother's Ghost Argues Politics

The letter came folded inside a bale of sailcloth, wrapped in oilskin against the harbor damp, and it smelled of tar and of the sea that had been trying to kill him for three years.

*To the house of Eriksen,* it began, in a hand that made the floor tilt under everyone who saw it—because dead men are not supposed to write neatly, and Anders had always written neatly, even as a boy, even copying his letters onto slate by the stove while Oscar cut practice serifs beside him. *To my father. To my brother Oscar. If there is anyone else under that roof now, let them hear it too. A dead man cannot be embarrassed.*

Voss stood by the window with his hat in his hands while it was read aloud. He had insisted on that—aloud, all of it, no private reading—and Oscar understood why before the second paragraph. It was not sentiment. It was strategy. A letter read to a whole room becomes a document of record; a letter passed hand to hand becomes a conspiracy. Voss wanted witnesses the way other men wanted alibis.

But none of that was in the letter. What was in the letter was Anders—wholly, unbearably Anders—and it began like this:

* *

*I have started this page eleven times. I know because I count everything now; counting is what keeps a man honest when honesty has become expensive. Eleven beginnings, and every one of them a lie. "Dear Father, I am well." "Dear Oscar, forgive me." Both true, both lies. So let me begin with the truth instead, which is that I did not mean to survive.*

*You will want to throw the page down already. Don't. Or do—I will never know, which is one liberty the sea has granted me.*

*Here is what happened, as briefly as I can make it, because the long version takes four days to tell and includes two funerals for people who were later seen drinking in Rostock. The *Søster Anne* went down off the Skaw in a gale that the pilot swore on his mother's grave would pass north of us. Fourteen men reached the Danish coast in a fishing boat that should have held eight. I was one of the fourteen because I could tie knots, and because a man named Brandt pulled me out of the water by my collar and said, "Not you. You're worth more dry." I did not ask him what he meant. I should have asked him what he meant.*

*For a month I wrote to you. Letters, Father—dozens of them—saying I lived, saying where I was, asking for passage home. Every one of them came back unopened or simply vanished into the machinery of a port that had decided I was dead and preferred me that way. You will tell me you never received them. I believe you. I have learned to believe terrible things about systems rather than people; it is kinder, and usually more accurate. But somewhere between the fourth returned letter and the fifth, I stopped writing to you and started writing for someone else, and that is how a shipwreck turns a man into what I am now.*

* *

*What am I? You will have heard words. Agitator. Smuggler. Traitor—the customs men use that one freely, though I notice they apply it only to men without titles. Let me give you the word I would choose: printer's son. Everything I believe, I learned at your composing stick, Father, before I ever read a line of philosophy. You taught me that letters are small and hard and worthless alone, and that set together in the right order they can build anything—a hymn, an invoice, an indictment. You taught me that ink does not care who dips the pen. You taught me that a man who controls the type controls the argument, because the argument is only ever as good as its setting.*

*You taught me all this, and then you seemed astonished when I used it.*

*Here is what I believe, plainly, so there can be no mistaking me for something nobler than I am. I believe that a harbor belongs to the men who work it. I believe that a price list drawn up in a customs house by men who have never lifted a crate is a kind of theft performed with excellent penmanship. I believe that the King's blockade of our own port—our port, built by our grandfathers' hands—is an admission that the state fears us more than it fears the enemy fleet outside the roadstead. And I believe that when a government seals a harbor, it has already confessed that what is inside the seal frightens it.*

*Do I believe in the republic the committees talk about? Some nights. Other nights I think every revolution is a printing job where the proof comes out wrong and they run it anyway, ten thousand copies, and call the errors destiny. But here is what forty months of exile have taught me that no pamphlet taught me: the errors are still better than what we had. A misprinted freedom beats a correct chains. I have seen men die for this—not dramatically, not with speeches, just badly, in ditches, over pamphlets—and I would rather be wrong alongside them than right the way the customs house is right.*

*That is my politics. Now, because I am my father's son and cannot leave an argument unfinished, here is what is wrong with my politics.*

* *

*Guilt first, since it is oldest.*

*Oscar. Brother. I know what my leaving cost, because I did the arithmetic before I ever stepped aboard, and I told myself the sum was acceptable. You were seventeen. You were going to inherit a trade that the guild would not formally acknowledge you for, and my going made your position stranger and lonelier and heavier, and I knew it, and I went anyway, because the sea paid three crowns a week and the bench paid nothing until Father died. I dressed it up as ambition. Call it what it was: I chose the wage.*

*And then I drowned, in the eyes of the law and the ledgers and every registry in the Baltic, and the weight I had put on your shoulders doubled without my lifting a finger. I have thought about that a great deal. There is a peculiar cruelty in being mourned. The living arrange themselves around your absence like water around a dropped stone, and they do it whether or not the stone ever touched bottom. I have been a shape in other men's lives for three years. I would like, once, before the tide goes out for good, to be a man again instead of a memory with opinions.*

*Father. I owe you a plainer apology than the ones I have drafted. When I left, I said things about your trade—about cutting letters for other men's books, about serving merchants who would never learn your name—that were true and unforgivable, which is the worst combination words come in. I was twenty and certain. I am twenty-four and less certain, but differently: I am no longer certain that contempt is an argument. You built something real in that shop. I sneered at it because building things slowly frightened me. The committees build nothing slowly; they burn quickly and call the heat progress. Do not mistake me for a man who has stopped believing. But understand that I have watched what belief does when it gets hold of a proud man with a grievance and a press, and I write the next part with my hand shaking, which you may verify against the rest of the page:*

*Be careful with Voss.*

*He is not a liar. That is precisely the danger. Liars you can catch; sincere men you can only survive. He believes every word he says, and he recruits by letting other men's beliefs do the talking—he will stand in your shop with rain coming off his coat and say almost nothing, and you will hear your own ambitions in the silence and think they are his offer. I have watched him do it in three ports. He is not wicked. He is a man who has decided that the cause may spend whatever it finds, including people, including families, including fathers, and he sleeps soundly. I have shared billets with him. I have trusted him with my life twice and he kept it both times. And I would not leave him alone in a room with our family's future, because he would leave owning it and both of us would sign the paper thinking it was our idea.*

*He will show you a berth on the last ship. Listen to what he does not say about who fills it.*

* *

*Now the arithmetic, because I promised myself this letter would contain no evasions, and this is the evasion I have circled eleven times.*

*There is one berth. Not one each—one. The *Mercurius* sails on the morning tide at week's end, before the boom closes, and she can carry one passenger the naval inspection will overlook. One. The committee's lists name three candidates for it and I am the least deserving and the best connected, which in revolutionary arithmetic adds up to certain.*

*But the committee does not decide. I have arranged—bought, bribed, begged, I will not detail the means because Father would audit them and Oscar would improve upon them—that the choice passes to this house. To Eriksens. Singular. I could not manage plural; the man holding the manifest would take instructions from one name only, and I gave him yours.*

*Yours, Oscar.*

*Read that sentence again, both of you, because I watched Father read ahead the first time I rehearsed this page, and I saw his face do what it does.*

*Not because I love you more. I refuse to rank us; ranking children is the first crime of every dying household. I choose you because of what the last three years have taught me about who decides well. Father decides like a man lighting fuses—he needs the flash, and lately, from what Voss tells me of his speeches at the meetings, he needs it very badly indeed. Give him the berth and he will spend it on the movement, or on his legend, or on some third thing that flatters him, and he will call all three of them duty. I know him. I am him. We are the same hunger wearing different coats.*

*You decide like a compositor. You look at the whole line before you commit the type. You have spent your entire life weighing what a thing costs against what it is worth, and doing it silently, and being right. I have read your letters—yes, the ones to our cousin in Lübeck that passed through our network's hands; forgive me, espionage is habit-forming—and I know your mind better now than I did when we shared a bed and a stove. You are the steadiest hand in that house. So the choice is yours.*

*Take the berth yourself. I mean it. I will write it so there is no ambiguity: OSCAR, TAKE THE BERTH YOURSELF. You have spent nineteen years being someone else in that town. On the other side of this war there are cities—Hamburg, Basel, places where a skilled cutter with steady hands is worth her—* 

*worth their weight.*

*Worth any weight. Where no one asks what you were called before. Go, and set type under whatever name you please, and send me a specimen sheet someday with a new face cut for the letter O. That would be a life. That would be a life I could defend having ruined mine for.*

*Or give the berth away—to a wounded comrade, to a witness who must reach the mainland alive, to whoever the week makes most desperate—and stay, and I will not pretend to understand that choice, but I will carry it. Or keep it for me, and I will come up the gangway among the stevedores with coal dust in my collar, and I will try to deserve it, and I will fail sometimes, and I will write to you from wherever the tide sets me down.*

*Any of the three is honorable. What is not honorable is what Father will propose, which is that the berth belong to the movement's printer as payment in glory. Glory, Oscar, is the currency that is always minted in other men's blood. Watch the coin.*

* *

*A few smaller matters, before the tide takes this page out with the pilot boat.*

*The chest behind the false panel in the back room—third board, the one that rocks—still holds Mother's rings. I took nothing when I left except the money I had earned and one of her handkerchiefs, which I still have, which has survived salt water and two arrests and smells of nothing now, and which I would trade every pamphlet I ever printed to see folded into your apron drawer again.*

*Father: the punchcutter's mallet on the high shelf is cracked through the handle. I broke it when I was twelve setting Father's own type upside down to see if anyone checked. No one checked. Draw from that whatever lesson you like; I have drawn several, and they argue with each other at night.*

*Oscar: I remember the winter you cut the swash capitals for the almanac order and Father said they were the finest work the shop had produced in twenty years, and he said it to the room, meaning you, and you looked down at your hands as if they belonged to a stranger you were fond of. I have thought about that look for years. I think I finally understand it, and understanding it too late is the tax I pay on having left. Whatever you decide—whatever you decide—you were always the real heir. The trousers were never the inheritance. The hands were.*

*They will tell you I died well or died badly or died conveniently, depending on which story serves the teller. Here is the truth: I died in October of '45, on paper, in a registrar's office in a language I don't speak. Since then I have been a rumor with a rifle. I would like to be a son again for one morning, on a quay, with the tide going out and my family standing in the light where I can see their faces. Whether I sail or watch the ship go without me, grant me that morning. It is the only thing left that I ask for myself.*

*Everything else I ask for you.*

*Your brother in whatever remains of the old alphabet,*

*ANDERS*

*P.S.—Voss will have told you I need the berth for the cause. Voss will have told you many things in his quiet voice while the rain came down. All of them true. None of them complete. Ask him what happens to printers after the ships sail. Ask him slowly, and watch his hands.*

*P.P.S.—Oscar. Whatever you decide, decide as yourself. Not as his son. Not as our father's ghost-in-waiting. As the girl who cut the swash capitals. She was always the best of us.*

The last page lay on the table, and the lamp hissed, and nobody moved.

Then Father said, "Well. The boy always did write a fine hand," and reached for the letter—

and Oscar got there first, sliding it from under the lamp into the shadow of her apron, saying only, "It's mine to keep. He addressed it to me."

Outside, somewhere toward the customs house, a whistle blew twice and stopped—and Voss, who had not spoken through the whole of the reading, turned from the window with an expression Oscar had never seen on that patient face, and said quietly: "They're early. We print tonight, or we don't print at all."
