## 12. The Red Broadside

Henrik Eriksen had set type for forty years and never once felt the letters tremble under his fingers until the night he set his own words.

That was the difference. That was everything. For four decades he had been a servant of other men's sentences — merchants' bills of lading, hymnals for the Lutherans, wedding announcements for families who never learned his name but praised the crispness of his serifs. He had cut matrices until his eyes failed at close work, had pulled the lever of the press ten thousand times, and every sheet that came off the bed belonged to somebody else. His hands made the voice. The voice was never his.

Now it was. He stood in the lamplight at two in the morning with the galley before him and read what he had written — not set, *written*, composed in his own head like a man composing a prayer — and he read it aloud to the empty shop because he could not bear to keep it inside his own skull.

*"The customs house is a mouth. It eats your herring, your timber, your sons' wages. Who staffs the mouth? Men who have never pulled a rope. Men who have never gone hungry an hour. They tax the fish before the fish is dead. Brothers — pull the plug from the harbor. Let the water run free."*

He said it again, quieter, tasting the rhythm. *Pull the plug from the harbor.* Good. Strong. A blacksmith's phrase, a stevedore's phrase. Not a phrase from a book. Voss would have polished it, Voss with his soft educated consonants, but Voss was not here tonight and Henrik had told him so with some satisfaction: *I write my own pamphlets now.* The committee's man had looked at him a long moment — that measuring look, the look of a man pricing goods — and then inclined his head and said, "Yes. Yours is the voice they'll trust."

*They'll trust.* Henrik turned the phrase over as he locked the chase. Forty years invisible, and now men trusted him. Now men needed him.

He inked the forme himself rather than wake Oscar. The boy slept upstairs like the dead; the boy had been strange lately, silent at supper, watching his father with an expression Henrik couldn't be bothered to parse. Grief, maybe. Or fear. Boys were weather; you waited them out. Henrik worked alone through the small hours, and there was a purity in it, pulling the first proof by candlelight, seeing his sentences stand up in black on white like soldiers mustered out of nothing.

Five hundred copies. Then another five hundred. The press ran smooth and warm beneath his hands, and each impression came off cleaner than the last, as though the machine itself had caught his conviction, and when the sky outside the windows went the color of dirty wool he stacked the damp sheets in bundles of fifty and felt something in his chest he had no honest word for. Exaltation came close. So did vindication. So did the feeling he remembered from his one visit to Copenhagen as a young man, standing in the cathedral, understanding suddenly how small he was and — this was the new part — how large. Both at once. A man could be both at once if the age permitted it, and the age permitted it. The age demanded it.

He left two bundles on the table for Oscar with a note: *Distribute to the fish hall before six. Do not be seen. This is your work too.* He wrote *your work too* and paused over it, pen hovering, and added nothing else. The boy should feel included. A father owed his heir inclusion.

Then Henrik Eriksen put on his good coat, tucked a bundle of his own pamphlets under his arm like scripture, and walked down into the city to watch history begin.

* * *

The customs house sat at the harbor's throat like a tumor, gray stone, iron-shuttered windows, and by eight o'clock the square before it had filled the way a drain fills before it backs up. Henrik had expected dozens. There were hundreds. Dockers still wet to the knee, herring girls with knives still in their belts, coal-heavers black-faced, clerks who had slipped their collars, old men, boys too young to have wages to steal. Word traveled faster than print, someone said later, and it was true — the pamphlets had only lit the fuse of talk that was already laid through the whole city like gunpowder in a mine gallery. All winter the talk had run. All winter the hunger had run. Henrik's broadside had simply given the talk a shape men could hold in their fists.

He climbed onto the steps of the chandlery opposite and watched, and the watching was the finest hour of his life up to that point, though he did not yet know it, and would spend the rest of it trying to get back to.

A docker named Brandt — Henrik knew him, or knew his face, from the tavern — stood on a barrel with a copy of the broadside held overhead, reading it aloud in a voice like gravel poured slow. He stumbled once on *arbitrage*, which Henrik noted with private satisfaction: the word had been chosen for exactly such men to stumble on, so that the crowd would laugh and the laughing would bind them. When Brandt reached the end — *pull the plug from the harbor* — he didn't read it. He shouted it. And the square shouted it back, hundreds of throats, and the sound went up off the stone and out over the rooftops toward the blockading ships in the roads, and Henrik Eriksen, standing above them all with his bundle under his arm, felt tears start in his eyes and did not wipe them away because a man weeping openly in a crowd like this was a man marked for leadership.

They moved on the customs house at half past nine. He would remember the sequence forever, in fragments, the way a man remembers a shipwreck.

The shutters first — iron, bolted, and the crowd surging against them like sea against a breakwater, and the sound of it, boots and shoulders and crowbars, a deep rhythmic concussion that Henrik felt through the soles of his shoes. Then a window above giving way, glass raining into the yard, a cheer going up ragged and huge. Then smoke — someone had brought fire, of course someone had brought fire, paper and tar and the customs ledgers themselves dragged out through the broken window and burned in the square, pages lifting on the heat like gulls, forty years of assessed duties and collected tariffs rising as ash over the harbor, and Henrik heard himself shouting with the rest, heard his own voice crack on the word *free* —

And then the soldiers came around the corner by the fish hall, a file of them, thirty perhaps, muskets levelled, and an officer shouting something about dispersal that no one heard and no one obeyed, because a crowd is a creature and a creature cornered does not disperse.

Henrik saw it all clearly and from slightly above, from the chandlery steps, a spectator at his own miracle, and what happened next happened in less time than it takes to tell.

The volley.

Not a warning shot. Not over heads. The officer had lost his nerve or gained his orders, and thirty muskets spoke together, one flat ugly clap like a door slammed on the century, and the front rank of the crowd folded.

Henrik saw Brandt go down. The big docker, the barrel-orator, took the ball high in the chest and simply sat down, the way a tired man sits, backward onto the cobbles, and did not get up, and the blood came out from under him across the stones in a line that followed the slope of the square toward the gutter, thin and fast and utterly red.

Two others. Henrik counted without wanting to. A herring girl near the fountain — she had been laughing a moment before, he was almost sure it was her, knife still in her belt, laughing at the burning ledgers — and a boy, fifteen at most, who had been climbing the customs house wall for no reason anyone could ever reconstruct, and who fell the way green wood falls.

The square did not riot after that. It shattered. Men scattered, women screamed, the soldiers reloaded with the terrible mechanical calm of men doing arithmetic, and Henrik Eriksen stood on the chandlery steps with his bundle of pamphlets still under his arm and discovered that his legs would not carry him either forward or away.

He looked down at the papers. His papers. *Pull the plug from the harbor.* The type stood clean and black and beautiful. Somewhere under his feet Brandt's blood was finding the gutter.

* * *

He got home by back streets without knowing he'd chosen them. His body had done the navigating while his mind sat somewhere behind his eyes, very quiet, very cold, reviewing.

In the shop he hung up his coat. He filled the kettle because his hands wanted occupation. He sat at his own workbench, the bench where he'd cut his first matrix at eleven years old under his own father's fist, and he waited for the horror to arrive.

It arrived at noon, precisely, while he was staring at the cold kettle, and it arrived whole, without warning, the way a wave arrives over a low deck.

Three men. Three names he would learn by evening — Brandt, and the girl was called Mette Iversdatter, and the boy was Jørgen, son of a rope-maker on Smedegate — three people dead in the customs house square because they had read his sentences and believed them. Believed *him*. A fat middle-aged printer who had never in his life stood in a bread line, whose shop smelled of ink and prosperity, whose own belly was soft from forty years of eating regularly while he composed phrases about hunger. *They tax the fish before the fish is dead.* He had written that. Men had died holding it.

He thought of Brandt sitting down. That was the image that broke him open — not the blood, not the boy falling, but the sitting, the ordinary tiredness of it, a workingman's whole life ending in the posture of a man resting between loads.

Henrik put his face in his hands. His fingers came away smelling of ink. Of course they did. They always would now. He was fifty-three years old and he had ink under his skin deeper than soap could reach, ink in the creases of his knuckles, ink in the calluses, and this morning that inheritance had printed death warrants and distributed them free of charge to men who could not read the fine print because there was no fine print because he had written none, because he had wanted it simple, because simple moved crowds —

"Father?"

Oscar stood in the doorway. The boy looked terrible — hollow-eyed, jacket dusty, a smear of soot along one jaw — and Henrik realized with a lurch that the boy had been in the square. Had seen it. Had perhaps been close enough to touch it.

"You're safe," Henrik said. It came out rough. "Thank God. You're safe."

"They're saying a printer wrote the pamphlet." Oscar's voice was flat, careful, a voice walking on ice. "The soldiers are asking door to door on the harbor streets. Lindqvist was seen at the naval office this morning. Talking a long time."

Let him come, some voice in Henrik said, some voice already armored, already rehearsing speeches about martyrdom and the free press. But underneath the armor another voice, older and smaller, the voice of the man who had wept on the chandlery steps, whispered: *they will come here, to my shop, where my child sleeps.*

"I know," Henrik said aloud. "I wrote it. I'm not ashamed."

Something crossed Oscar's face — quickly, gone before Henrik could name it, though later he would lie awake trying to name it and fail. Something between grief and a kind of terrible recognition, as if the boy had looked at his father and seen a stranger wearing his father's coat.

"You're not ashamed." Oscar repeated it without inflection, a compositor checking a proof, setting the sentence in type to see whether it would hold its shape.

"Three people died," Henrik said, and heard how the words sounded even as they left him — recited, a catechism, a debt acknowledged in the correct coin. "I know that. I grieve it. But grief is not shame, boy. Those men — that girl — they died for something. Before yesterday they were dying for nothing, slowly, of hunger and taxes, and nobody counted them. Today they have names. Today the whole city knows their names. I gave them that."

"You gave them that." Again the flat repetition. The boy turned away, began unbuttoning his coat with careful mechanical fingers. "There's blood in the square they haven't washed yet. It runs toward the harbor. I watched it."

"And what did you feel, watching it?" Henrik asked, and heard his own voice rise, defensive, needling. "Tell me honestly. Tell me you felt nothing."

Oscar stopped with the coat half off. For a long moment the boy stood motionless in the gray light from the window, and Henrik had the sudden vertiginous sense that he was looking at his child across a distance no shop and no supper table had ever contained — that the child was weighing something, measuring it, deciding what to do with it, the way a craftsman decides what to do with a flawed piece of stock.

"I felt that the type was beautiful," Oscar said finally. "Whoever cut it. That's all I felt. That's what frightens me."

And the boy went upstairs, and the door closed, and Henrik Eriksen sat alone in his shop with the smell of ink and the memory of Brandt sitting down.

* * *

The horror lasted until dusk.

He catalogued it afterward, because cataloguing was what a printer did — he sorted the day's emotions into cases like sorts in a typecase, upper and lower, and examined each. The horror had been real. He did not deny it. He had sat at his bench from noon till nearly four with his hands over his face, and twice he had risen intending to walk to the naval office and give himself up, and twice he had gotten as far as the door. He had thought of Anders, three years drowned — or whatever he was, wherever he was, the boy the sea had taken, the boy whose absence had hollowed this family and left only silence and drink — and he had thought, *if Anders could see me now,* and could not finish the thought in either direction.

But at four o'clock Voss came.

The committee's man let himself in without knocking — he had taken lately to that familiarity, or Henrik had granted it, the boundary having dissolved sometime during the third night of printing — and he stood dripping in the doorway (it had begun to rain again; the rain never stopped in this city) and looked at Henrik with those patient gray eyes.

"The square," Voss said. "You were there?"

"I was there."

"They're calling it Black Tuesday already. In the taverns. In the yards." Voss removed his hat, turned it in his hands. "Do you know what they're calling the pamphlet?"

"No."

"*The Red Broadside.* It's everywhere. Every wall, every window, half of them copies of copies, hand-lettered by people who can't spell — they copied it anyway, because the words mattered more than the spelling." Voss smiled, and it was not entirely a pleasant smile; it was the smile of a man watching a fire he had banked carefully spread beyond his control, and finding that he did not altogether mind. "Henrik. Three people died today, and tonight there are more radicals in this city than the committee has recruited in two years. The deaths are terrible. The deaths are also fuel. I say that plainly because you deserve plainness: the movement feeds on what happened in that square, and your words lit it, and every man who read your broadside and wept tonight is a man who will act tomorrow. You did that."

"I killed them," Henrik said. It came out smaller than he intended.

"You wrote the truth," Voss said, "in a city starving on lies. What the soldiers did with their muskets belongs to the soldiers and to the government that aims them. You are not their arm, Henrik. You are the mirror they fired into." He stepped closer, and his voice dropped into the register of intimacy, of conspiracy, of a man handing another man something heavy and warm. "But hear me. The authorities will come for the printer. Perhaps tomorrow, perhaps within the week, before the port closes. When they come, everything depends on where you stand. A printer who hides is a criminal. A printer who stands in his doorway and says *yes, I wrote it, and I will write it again* — that printer is a martyr, and martyrs are worth ten thousand pamphlets. The committee will protect your family. The committee will remember. When the new order comes — and it is coming, Henrik, the whole coast is alight, you cannot see it from this harbor but it is coming — the man who owned the press that printed *The Red Broadside* will not be a tradesman. He will be a founder."

*A founder.*

Henrik stood very still in his darkening shop. Through the window the harbor lamps were coming on one by one, and beyond them, far out in the roads, the riding lights of the blockade ships, patient as fixed stars. Somewhere out there was a world being remade. Somewhere in this room was a man who might be remade with it.

He thought of Brandt sitting down. He made himself think of it, made himself look at it squarely one more time — and found, examining the image with a craftsman's exactness, that it had changed since noon. At noon Brandt had been a man he killed. Now, under Voss's steady gaze, in the gathering dark, Brandt tilted and became something else: a man who had *chosen*, a man lifted out of obscurity into meaning, a name that would outlive the flesh because a printer had given it permanence. Was that not a kind of salvation? Did not every religion in the history of the world trade exactly in that currency — death converted into significance? The girl, Mette Iversdatter. The boy, Jørgen. They would be remembered now. Remembered *forever*. He had done that. No priest in this city could promise what Henrik Eriksen had delivered to those three strangers in a single morning.

The horror did not leave him. He understood that. It would sit in his chest for the rest of his life, a cold sort lodged among the warm ones. But a press has many sorts, and the compositor chooses which to raise to the stick, and Henrik Eriksen chose.

"Voss," he said. "How many copies can we run before the harbor closes?"

The gray eyes gleamed. "As many as you have paper for."

"Then bring paper." Henrik was already lighting the lamp over the composing stone, his hands moving with the sureness of forty years, his heart hammering with the urgency of one. "Bring all the paper in the city. Tonight we give them something bigger than a broadside. Tonight we give them a declaration."

Voss smiled his banker's smile and went out into the rain, and Henrik stood a moment alone, listening to the footsteps fade, and allowed himself — just briefly, just privately, the way a man allows himself a drink before witnesses arrive — to imagine his name spoken in the new order of things. *Eriksen. Yes. The printer. The founder.* His father had been a cutter of other men's letters. His son would inherit a legend.

He was reaching for the lamp trimmer when he noticed the paper on the table — the bundles he'd left for Oscar that morning, undistributed, untouched, and beside them a note in his own hand. *This is your work too.* He had forgotten it entirely. And beneath it, in a different hand — the boy's careful, upright, secretly beautiful hand, the hand Henrik had never much examined — a single line of reply:

*I distributed nothing. And Father — the soldiers were at Lindqvist's office until dark. He knows our street.*

Henrik read it twice. Outside, the rain thickened against the windows, and somewhere down the quay a door slammed, and he found he could not decide, even now, even holding the note, whether the feeling rising in him was fear — or appetite.
