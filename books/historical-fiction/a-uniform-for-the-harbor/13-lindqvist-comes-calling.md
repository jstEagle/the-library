## 13. Lindqvist Comes Calling

He came at the hour when the shop smelled most of itself—lamp oil, lead shavings, the sour-sweet ghost of ink worked deep into the grain of the benches—and he came alone, which was itself a kind of message. Harbormaster Lindqvist did not go anywhere alone. He went with clerks, with constables, with the two broad-shouldered men whose job it was to stand behind him and make his silences feel like verdicts.

Today there was only Lindqvist and his stick, and the rain beading on the wool of his shoulders, and a face as gray and patient as the harbor in February.

Oscar saw him first through the front glass, coming up the cobbled rise from the water, and her hands went still over the case before her mind had decided anything. She had set type for this man for eleven years. Bills of lading, manifests, the harbor's annual report with its columns of tonnage so straight they could have been ruled by God. She knew the way he held his hat when he was thinking—brim down, turning it slowly by the crown—and he was holding it that way now, climbing the hill toward a shop that had no legitimate business left to sell him.

She wiped her hands on her apron. The gesture was habit, not need; her hands were always inked somewhere no cloth could reach.

"Harbormaster," she said, opening the door before he could knock. Better to seem unsurprised. "We're closing early today."

"Then I'll be quick." He stepped past her into the warmth, bringing the sea with him—that smell of tar and cold brine that lived in his coat the way ink lived in hers. He looked around the shop with the unhurried eye of a man taking inventory. The press under its canvas where they'd dragged it back from the cellar only two nights ago, still not reassembled, still smelling faintly of the tallow they'd greased its rails with. The composing stone, clean. The type cases, closed. A shop pretending very hard to be asleep.

"You've tidied," he observed.

"End of season."

"End of something." He set his hat on the counter, precisely, brim parallel to the edge, and lowered himself onto the stool that had been Father's since before Oscar could walk. It put him below her eyeline and somehow made him taller. "Your father is out."

It was not a question, but she answered it anyway. "At the committee." Let him chew on that word. If he already knew—and the way his gaze had gone straight to the covered press said he knew—then pretending ignorance would only insult them both.

"Ah." He nodded slowly, as if she'd told him the tide tables. "The committee. And what does the committee print these days? I hear such fine things about their typography."

The lamplight caught the wet on his shoulders. Somewhere across the city, muffled by rain and distance, a bell rang the half hour, and Oscar found herself listening to it the way one listens to a clock in another man's house—counting seconds that were not hers.

"We cut type," she said. "We print what we're paid to print."

"Yes. That was always the beauty of your father's establishment." He drew off one glove, then the other, finger by finger, laying them beside his hat. "Impartial. Indifferent. A press doesn't care what it says any more than a knife cares what it cuts. I've admired that about you Eriksens for years." He paused. "About your family, I should say."

*About you*, he almost said. She felt the correction land like a fingertip laid on a bruise she'd thought hidden under her sleeve. Eleven years of *young Eriksen*, of *your boy's steady hand*, of compliments paid to the son who did not exist—and she had never once wondered whether Lindqvist, who watched harbors for a living, who noticed cargoes and hulls and the small dishonesties of weight marks, might have noticed her too.

"Is there an order?" she asked. "I can take your specifications to my father."

"I don't want type, young Eriksen. I want to talk about ships." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and for a moment the official mask slipped and she saw the old customer underneath—the man who used to bring his wife's condolences and his own pipe tobacco, who'd once stood in this very shop on a winter evening and read aloud, badly, a poem he'd written about the ice going out of the bay. "There's one civilian vessel sailing on the morning tide Friday. You know this."

"Everyone knows it."

"Not everyone knows how the list was drawn. Not everyone knows that names can move up a list, or off one, or onto one." He turned his hat a quarter turn. "Anders Eriksen is not on it."

The name went through her like a bolt through planking. She kept her face still because she had practiced stillness her whole life, but inside, everything tilted—the floor, the lamplight, the eleven years.

"My brother is dead," she said.

"Dead men write very neatly." His eyes were mild. "Neater than living ones, usually. Living men smudge their letters; grief gets in the ink. Your brother's hand has stayed remarkably clean for a drowned man. Remarkably well-inked, too, for someone three years at sea." He let that sit. "Paper has a provenance, young Eriksen. So do pamphlets. The one that went round the customs house last week—the red one, the one that got three men killed—it was set on a Caslon. Old face, worn 'e' with a chipped serif on the lower bowl. There are perhaps four presses in this city with that 'e'." He looked at the covered shape by the wall. "Yours is the only one I couldn't account for."

The rain filled the silence. Oscar became aware of her own heartbeat, of the lamp hissing faintly, of the enormous patience of the man on the stool. He wasn't threatening her. That was the terrible thing—he was being *kind*, in the way of a man offering to help you carry something heavy before he tells you what's inside it.

"If you know all this," she said carefully, "why am I talking to you and not a magistrate?"

"Because magistrates are blunt instruments, and I am a scale." He rose, joints cracking, and walked to the window, watching the rain come down the hill toward the water he governed. "Do you know what happens to a port when it closes, young Eriksen? Everything stops moving except the rot. Cargo spoils on the quay. Sailors drink. Men with lists in their pockets discover that a name on paper is worth more than gold, and men without lists discover the same thing too late." He turned. "I have spent thirty years keeping this harbor honest-ish. Honest enough. There is no honest harbor on this coast—there is only the question of who keeps the accounts. I keep the accounts. And lately—" his mouth twisted, "—lately certain parties in the naval office have taken an interest in my accounts. In my ledgers. In certain fees I've waived for certain shipments, over certain years, in exchange for certain considerations. Smuggling, if we're using ugly words. The kind of smuggling that kept this port alive through two bad winters, and kept my family fed, and kept half the merchants of this city from ruin. They'll call it corruption when they need a scapegoat, and I'll hang for it, or transport for it, while the admirals who took their cut sit behind desks and call themselves patriots."

He came back toward her, slow, the stick tapping once on each flagstone.

"So here is my position. In four days the navy seals this harbor. In four days every ledger I keep becomes evidence, and every favor becomes a crime, and the only thing between me and a cell is either escape or silence." He stopped an arm's length from her. "You people have a berth on Friday's ship. One berth. And I have reason to believe the Eriksen household intends to use it."

"Reason," Oscar echoed.

"A ship needs papers. Sailing orders, clearance, a bill of health—all printed, all signed, all filed through my office. Whoever sails Friday sails with documents that pass through my hands first." He smiled, and there was no warmth in it, only weariness worn smooth as sea glass. "I could lose those papers for a day. I could find discrepancies that require correcting. I could delay, misfile, misplace—there are forty ways to hold a ship at anchor, and I know every one of them, because I invented half. Or—" he spread his hands, "—I could let her sail on the morning tide, clean and quick, before anyone thinks to look twice. Depends entirely on what's in it for me."

"And what would be in it for you?"

"Two things. Either that berth—I'm an old man, young Eriksen, and I'd rather die in Riga than in a penal colony, and I'm not particular about whose seat I take. Or your silence." He picked up his gloves again, working them back on, unhurried. "My ledgers will be seized within the week whether I sail or not. Copies exist. Copies I made, over years, quietly, against exactly this day. If those copies reach the naval office, they bury me. If instead they reach the *right* hands—the committee's hands, the movement's hands—they become something else entirely. Proof of who really profited. Proof that goes all the way up, past me, past the customs men, into offices with admirals' coats hanging outside the doors." He met her eyes. "Your father wants to burn the old order down. Here is dry tinder with a bow on it. All I ask is that it stays out of navy hands. Your silence. Your press's cooperation in seeing it printed and distributed—or simply in seeing it *not handed over*. Either price suits me. Choose whichever rots less in your mouth."

Oscar stood very still. This was the trade, then, laid out plain: a berth bought with betrayal, or safety bought with complicity, and both prices payable in the same coin—other people. She thought of Voss, who bargained in futures; of her father, who bargained in glory; of Anders, three years dead and writing neat letters, asking her to decide. Everyone she knew was standing in a market stall selling pieces of themselves, and the harbor was full of buyers.

"You've thought about this," she said.

"Since Tuesday." For the first time he looked genuinely tired. "Your father came to my office Monday with a petition for printing permits—permits! As if the revolution will honor paperwork. He wanted everything stamped, witnessed, *legitimate*. He spoke of the new age for forty minutes. I signed nothing and wrote everything down." He settled his hat on his head. "I liked your father better when he only drank. Drink makes a man quiet; ideas make him loud, and loud men get ports closed."

"He believes in it," Oscar heard herself say. "What he prints. However it started, he believes now."

"I know. That's what frightens me. A corrupt man can be bought. A believing man can only be burned, and he'll take the shop with him." Lindqvist pulled the door open onto the rain. "You have until Thursday evening. Find me on the quay—at the powder store, sunset. Bring an answer, not an argument. I've had enough arguments this year to float a fleet."

"Harbormaster." Her voice came out lower than she intended. "That poem you wrote. About the ice going out."

He paused on the threshold, rain silvering past him, and something moved behind the gray of his face—briefly, like a fish under ice.

"Set it beautifully," he said. "Whatever else you do with that press of yours—whatever names you sign, whatever lies you set in fourteen-point—when you print something true, set it beautifully. It's the only absolution our trade offers." He tipped the brim of his hat down over his eyes, the thinking-gesture, and descended into the weather, his stick striking the cobbles in a rhythm like a slow countdown.

Oscar stood in the doorway until the rain blurred him into the color of the street. Then she went inside, threw the latch, and let herself shake—one long tremor from the shoulders down, the body settling after a blow it had seen coming for days.

One berth. Lindqvist wanted it, or wanted her silence, and both demands landed on the same pair of shoulders, because Anders had written *let Oscar decide* and her father had heard *let me decide* and Voss had heard *we'll decide together* and none of them had meant the same thing.

Thursday evening. Sunset. The powder store.

She uncovered the press, though there was nothing she meant to print. She just needed to look at it—her mother's dowry, her whole childhood, the machine that had made her and unmade her and was now, apparently, currency. Its iron gleamed dully in the lamplight, patient as ever, indifferent as a knife.

Behind her, the street door opened without a knock. She knew the tread—quick, light, salt-caked.

Voss came in shaking off the rain, and stopped when he saw her face.

"What happened?" he said. "Oscar. What happened?"

She turned from the press, and heard herself say the words that would start everything moving:

"Lindqvist knows. About Anders. About the pamphlets. About all of it—and he's named his price, and we have until Thursday to decide which of us pays it."
