## 14. Which Child Was I

The pamphlets were gone from the shop by morning—bundled, wrapped in oilcloth, and carried out the back door before the light came—but the smell of them remained. It clung to everything: the ink stones, the aprons hanging on their nails, my own hands. I scrubbed at my fingers with lye and pumice until they burned, and still, when I lifted them near my face, there it was. The riot's ink. The customs house ink. Three men dead on the quay, and every sheet of paper that helped put them there had passed under my fingers first.

Father had not come home until well past midnight. I heard him in the yard below my window, singing—actually singing, a tavern song with the words worn off it, just the tune left, hummed through a throat gone thick with brandy and triumph. He had stood in the doorway of his own house swaying like a sapling in wind, and Voss had taken his arm and steered him inside without meeting my eyes, and I had lain awake listening to my father laugh in his sleep.

Now it was afternoon, and he was sober enough to be dangerous, and he had sent for me.

He was in the workshop, standing before the press the way other men stand before altars. He had washed. His good coat was brushed. There was a stillness about him that I distrusted more than the singing, because I knew that stillness—it was the same one that came over him when he was about to do something irreversible. He used to have it before signing contracts. Now he had it before everything.

"You wanted me," I said.

"Oscar." He said my name—the false one, the one that fit me like a coat cut for someone else—and patted the bench beside him. "Sit. Look at this."

I sat. On the bench between us lay the forme from last night's run, locked tight in its chase, the type mirrored and gleaming: THE PEOPLE ARE THE HARBOR, AND THE HARBOR IS THE PEOPLE. My composition. My spacing. I had set it in eleven minutes flat while the cellar filled with men who smelled of tar and secrets, and even then—even with my stomach knotted around what the words would do—I could not make myself set it badly. That is the curse of craft. The hands know what the hands know.

"Look at that kerning," Father said softly. "Look at the evenness of it. A machine couldn't do better." He turned to me, and his eyes were wet, which frightened me worse than anything else he might have done. "My son did this."

* * *

There are moments that arrive already knowing what they are. I sat on that bench with the forme between us and felt one coming, the way you feel a storm coming in your teeth—some pressure behind the eyes, some wrongness in the air. Three days since the riot. Two days since Lindqvist had stood in our doorway smelling of pipe smoke and veiled threats, telling me without telling me that he knew what we printed, that everyone bargains or burns. One night since I had watched my father promise the press to the committee as though it were his to give, as though thirty years of my mother's dowry and my mother's labor and my mother's unmarked grave had not built it plank by plank.

"My son," he said again, and put his hand on my shoulder—the heavy hand, the hand that had taught me to hold a graver when I was six years old, back when I was still allowed to be small and called by no name but hers. "Do you know what I was thinking, up on that hill last night, watching them burn the customs ledgers? I was thinking: Eriksen, you old fool, you spent your whole life bent over a cutting bench, and you thought that was a life. And all the while your boy was becoming something."

"Becoming something," I repeated.

"An heir." He gripped my shoulder harder. "Not just an heir to a shop. An heir to a *cause*. Do you understand what I'm telling you? When this is over—when the harbor belongs to the people who work it—there will be a new order, and printers will be its priests. Every proclamation, every decree, every word the new world speaks will come off presses. Off *this* press. And the man who owns this press—" he stopped, and laughed, and wiped his eye with the back of his wrist. "The man who owns this press will be somebody. Not Eriksen the type-cutter, not Eriksen who mends other men's fonts and takes what coin they toss him. Somebody."

"And you," I said carefully, "will be that man."

"I will be that man." He nodded, solemn as communion. "And after me—"

"After you," I said.

He looked at me then, full-on, and I saw that he meant it, every word, that in whatever country his mind now inhabited I was not his child at all but his succession, his monument, the name carved on the base of his statue. *Oscar Eriksen, son of the printer.* He had invented me at seven years old out of grief and practicality, and I had kept myself invented for eighteen years so that his trade would not die with him, and now he looked at me with wet shining eyes and loved—not me. Something else. Something he had made.

Something I had made him make.

"Father." My voice came out strange. Flat. Far away, as if someone else were speaking through the floorboards of me. "There is something I have to tell you. About Oscar."

* * *

Here is what I had planned to say, in the years I rehearsed it—in the dark, in the privy, in the long grey hour before dawn when lies sit heaviest:

*I am your daughter. I was your daughter the night Mother died and I became your son because you could not lift your head from her bed long enough to notice what you were doing, and because Grandmother Sanna took me by the wrist and said, "Your father will drink himself into the harbor unless we give him a reason to stand up, and a daughter is no reason, so from tonight you are his boy." And you never asked. Eighteen years, and you never once looked closely enough to ask.*

Here is what I had planned to say about the trade: *Every letter you ever sold, I cut half of. Your famous Eriksen lowercase—that's mine, the whole fount, cut when I was fifteen, my hands bleeding for a month. You signed my work with your name and I let you, because letting you was cheaper than losing you.*

Here is what I had planned to say about Anders: *You mourned him for three years and drank to him every Sunday and told strangers your son died a hero of the sea, and you never once noticed that you still had a son sitting across the supper table from you, eating your bread, wearing your dead wife's patience like a borrowed coat.*

I opened my mouth to say any of it. All of it.

"About Oscar," Father said, and smiled, and turned back to the forme, and answered the confession I hadn't made. "Yes. I've been meaning to tell you. I've been meaning to tell you for days, and a man shouldn't wait, not with the times as they are. Sit. Listen."

I listened. God help me, I listened.

"When your mother died," he said, "I was nothing. You don't remember how bad it was—you were too young, thank God. I couldn't cut a straight stem for a month. Couldn't look at a seriffed letter without seeing her handwriting on the shopping lists. And the shop—" he shook his head. "The shop was dying. No one gives work to a drunk. And then one day I looked up and there you were, this little scrap of a thing, standing at my bench with a graver in your fist, holding it right, *holding it right*, nobody taught you—and I thought: the line continues. God has taken my wife but given me a son worth the having."

The words went through me like cold water through a cracked hull. Because it wasn't true. None of it was true. Grandmother Sanna had put the graver in my fist herself, had positioned my fingers on it like a puppeteer, and had stood behind me in the doorway while Father stumbled down from her bed-chamber and saw, and wept, and lived. I knew this because she told me so, once, at the end of her life, gripping my hand with her terrible bird-claw strength: *"He needed a miracle, girl, so I built him one out of you. Don't you ever take it apart."*

But he believed it. He had believed it for eighteen years, and belief that old doesn't want correction. It wants company.

"—and I'll tell you something else," he went on, warming now, pacing along the bench, "something I haven't said out loud. When the new committee takes the harbor—and they will, Lindqvist's lists are already drawn, the whole city knows the navy seals the port at week's end—when that happens, there are men who will want this shop. Want it seized, want it 'held in common,' want it broken up for scrap. And I will stand in front of it and say no. I'll say: this press has an heir. This press passes to my son. Touch it and you touch the succession itself." He stopped pacing. He looked at me with an expression I had never seen on him before, not in eighteen years, and it took me a moment to recognize it because it was so simple and so unbearable:

Pride. Plain, undiluted pride, aimed straight at me.

"Oscar. My boy. Whatever it was you were going to say—" he waved his hand, magnanimous, already past it, "—whatever trouble, whatever doubt, whatever weakness got into you during these hard weeks—set it aside. Sons inherit. That's the whole of it. Everything I build from this day forward is yours, and no committee, no admiral, no king can unmake that, because it isn't law that makes it so. It's blood." He tapped my chest, twice, over the heart. "Blood and type. That's the Eriksen inheritance."

* * *

Afterward I could not have said how long I sat there. The light moved. The kettle on the stove went from murmuring to whistling and no one took it off. Somewhere below us in the street a cart passed, and a gull screamed, and the city went on arranging itself toward the end of the week, toward the blockade, toward the single berth on the morning tide that was even now being counted and argued over in cellars and harbormaster's offices while my father talked about blood.

Because here was the arithmetic I could not stop doing, the dread sum that had been running in the back of my skull for two days now: one berth. Anders expected it—or expected to yield it to me, if the letter could be believed, if my brother's careful smuggled sentences meant what they seemed to mean. Father assumed it was his due; I had watched him angle for it at the committee, watched Voss watch him angling. One berth. Three Eriksens. And a stranger named Voss who held the letters, and a harbormaster named Lindqvist who held the lists, and somewhere in that ledger of claims and debts a number that would not resolve no matter how many times I carried it.

One berth. And I had just swallowed, whole and unchewed, the only truth that might have changed the count.

Because that was the thing I understood, sitting on the bench beside the forme I had set, under the pride I had earned under a name that wasn't mine: if I spoke—if I said *daughter*, if I said *she*—then everything changed shape. The heir became a fraud. The succession he was building his new glory upon became a forgery older and vaster than any signature I might ever fake. He wouldn't hear the rest. He wouldn't hear that the press was half mine by labor, that the berth should be mine by sacrifice, that I had earned every inch of what he was offering me. He would only hear the lie, and the lie would burn down everything the way he now burned things, gladly, righteously, with brandy in his blood.

So I said nothing. Again. For the thousandth time, nothing.

"Father," I said instead, and my voice was steady, and I hated how steady it was, "if it comes to choosing—who goes on that ship—what would you have me do?"

He didn't hesitate. That was the worst of it. He had the answer ready, polished, waiting, as though he'd been carrying it in his coat pocket for days.

"What any son of mine would do," he said. "Stand fast. Hold the shop. A man holds what's his." Then he softened, and cuffed my jaw the way he used to when I was small, rough affection, and added, almost gently: "Besides. If it comes to it, I'll speak to Voss. The cause owes this family. We're owed a berth, boy, and I intend to collect it—for you, if it must be. But not for Anders."

"Not for Anders," I repeated.

"He ran from us once." Father's face did something complicated—grief and grievance tangled together, three years of drinking to a dead son who was apparently not dead enough to stop disappointing him. "Let him run again. You stayed. Stayers inherit."

* * *

That evening I climbed to the loft above the workshop where the paper stock was kept, because it was the only room in the building with a lock, and I needed a lock between myself and the world. Below me I could hear Father moving through the shop, humming again, touching his press, whispering to it. Talking to it. I had heard him do that lately—murmuring to the iron frame like a man courting—and I had told myself it was the drink, but the drink had been gone from the house since morning and the murmuring had not.

Through the floorboards came the smell of lamp oil. Not ink-oil. Lamp oil, the cheap tallowy kind, and a great deal of it, and beneath the oil another smell I knew from the chandlery next door and had never expected to smell in my father's shop: pitch.

I sat in the dark among the reams of paper with my knees drawn up, doing the arithmetic again, and this time the numbers would not stay still. One berth. One press. One father who had just told me that stayers inherit, and who was downstairs at midnight, alone with his god and forty measures of lamp oil, whispering to the iron like a bridegroom.

Stayers inherit, he had said.

I looked at my hands in the gloom—ink in the creases still, after everything, after the lye and the pumice, ink worked so deep into the skin of me it would outlast any fire—and I understood that whatever my father was planning down there, it was not a plan that included heirs.

It was a plan that included ash.
