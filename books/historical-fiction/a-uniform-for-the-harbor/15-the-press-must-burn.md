## 15. The Press Must Burn

The knock came at half past nine, three blows, unhurried, the way a man knocks when he is certain the door will open for him.

Erlend Eriksen had spent forty years learning to read knocks. Customers knocked twice, quick and apologetic, as though asking pardon for interrupting. Debt collectors knocked four times and then pretended they had not. His son — his *son*, his heir, the boy who could set a line of Baskerville blindfolded in a dark room — never knocked at all, just shouldered through with ink to the elbows and that look of quiet judgment Erlend had never learned to bear.

This knock was none of these. This was an official knock.

He set down his glass. It was nearly empty anyway; he had been rationing himself since morning, since the customs men had stopped a cart two streets over and found three hundred pamphlets sewn into a bale of flax. The whole city knew by noon. The whole city always knew by noon.

Through the frosted pane of the shop door he saw the silhouette of a tall man and the smaller shape of a soldier behind him, musket slung, boots planted in the gutter water.

Erlend opened the door with the face he had practiced in the mirror of the back room: mild, bewildered, a tradesman interrupted at honest labor.

"Magistrate Brandt." He said it before the man could introduce himself. There was no better weapon against an official than the appearance of familiarity. "You've caught us between jobs. Come in out of the wet, if you've business."

"I have business." Brandt did not come in. He stood on the threshold letting the rain run off his hat brim onto the step, and he looked past Erlend's shoulder into the shop with the slow, inventorying gaze of a man pricing everything in the room, including its owner. "You are the printer Eriksen."

"I am."

"Then you'll want to hear this inside, I think." A pause, precisely measured. "Or perhaps you won't. Perhaps you'd rather your neighbors heard it too."

Erlend stepped aside.

* * *

Brandt walked the shop like a bishop processing down a nave. He touched nothing — that was the worst of it, the delicacy of it — but his gloved fingers hovered above the cases of type, drifted along the press's iron frame, paused at the composing stone where Oscar — where *the boy* — had left a forme locked up from the night's work. Erlend watched him read it upside down, the way printers' enemies always could, and felt something cold begin behind his breastbone.

"You print broadsides," Brandt said.

"I print what's paid for. Bills of lading. Marriage notices. A hymnal for the garrison chapel, two years back — you may ask the chaplain."

"I have asked everyone worth asking, Eriksen. That is why I'm here." Brandt turned. Behind him the soldier shifted, and rain dripped off the eaves into the street with a rhythm like a leaking faucet in an empty house. "Three hundred copies of a seditious tract were seized this morning. The paper is Relling's stock. Relling sells to four houses in this city. Two of them are shut. One is yours."

"And the fourth?"

"The fourth is ash already." Brandt smiled without warmth. "The owner burned it himself last night and reported a chimney fire. He is being very cooperative. He has given me names."

The cold behind Erlend's breastbone spread downward and became something else, something that was almost — he was ashamed even as he felt it — *exhilaration*. Names. They wanted names. And there was only one name that mattered in this shop, one name that would be dragged into the light if he let them take the press apart plank by plank, and that name was not his own. Let them have him. He had been ready to be taken since the night he first set the word *liberty* in twelve-point and felt it hum under his fingers like a struck bell.

But not the boy. Never the boy.

"I don't know what you expect me to say," Erlend said, and heard how much steadier his voice was than his heart.

"I expect nothing. I am informing you." Brandt drew a folded paper from his coat, did not offer it, merely held it so the seal showed — the double eagle, the harbor chain, the crown. "At week's end the port closes. Martial law follows. There will be a list posted of persons whose premises will be searched under warrant. Your name is on a draft of that list. Whether it survives to the final copy" — he refolded the paper, slowly, creasing each fold with his thumbnail — "depends on what we find when we come. Or rather, on what we do not find."

He let that sit in the damp air between them like a coin on a counter.

"You're offering me a bribe," Erlend said.

"I am offering you arithmetic." Brandt put on his hat. At the door he turned, and for a moment the official mask slipped and something almost human looked out — tiredness, maybe, or envy. "You have a son, I'm told. Clever with his hands. It would be a pity. These things go badly for the whole household, when they go badly."

The door closed. The soldier's boots splashed away. Erlend stood in the middle of his shop, in the smell of ink and oil and forty years of his own labor, and listened to the rain.

They would come. Not maybe. Not perhaps. The fourth printer had burned his stock and given names, and names led to cellars, and cellars led to committees, and committees led to Voss, and Voss — Erlend's stomach turned — Voss had been in and out of this shop like a cat who considered it his. If they took Voss, Voss would trade this address before they'd finished pouring him his first cup of prison coffee. Everyone talked. Erlend had watched brave men all his life, and every one of them talked eventually; it was only a question of what they were made of, and men were made of the same thing as everyone else, which was fear wearing a coat.

They would come, and they would find the press, and the press would hang him — and worse than hanging him, it would condemn the boy. Guilt ran downhill in this world, always had. A father's crime soaked into his children like ink through cheap paper.

Unless there was no press to find.

* * *

The idea came to him the way all his best ideas came: in the third glass, alone, after midnight, with the shop dark below and the rain still working at the roof.

He was standing at the window of his room above the stairs, looking down into the yard, at the coal shed, at the woodpile he had been meaning to split since autumn, at the little iron hatch of the cellar where they'd hidden nothing at all because there had been nothing left to hide — the pamphlets were gone, sold, distributed, loose in the city like sparks off a grinder's wheel. The press sat below him in the dark. Twelve hundred pounds of cast iron and walnut and his father's name engraved on the frame, *ERIKSEN & SÖN*, cut by his own hand twenty-six years ago, the letters deeper now from polish than from the graver.

*Eriksen & Son.* He had built that name out of grief and stubbornness and one long practical lie, and he had told himself it was for the boy, and lately — lately, with the crowds cheering him at the cellar meetings, with men calling him *Master Eriksen* in voices that used to call him *ink-hands* — lately he had begun to believe the name belonged to him, that he had earned it twice over, once as a father and once as a prophet of the new age.

And now Brandt wanted to take it. The magistrate, the crown, the whole rotten order that had kept men like him stooped over a composing stone for forty years while merchants' sons bought commissions and called themselves gentlemen — they meant to take the one thing he had ever made that mattered.

Let them find ashes.

It arrived whole, the thought, complete, beautiful, the way a forme comes off the stone perfect on the first pull. Burn the shop. Burn the press, the type, the papers, the evidence — burn it all, and stand in the street in the light of it, and let the whole harbor see what the crown's justice drove an honest tradesman to. Let them arrest him in front of witnesses. A martyr was worth more than a printer. A martyr's name could not be confiscated. He had seen it in the cellar: the way men's faces changed when someone spoke of those who had fallen. Dead, a man stopped being a man and became a flag.

And the boy? The thought surfaced, small and reasonable, like a bubble in setting type. What does the boy inherit from ashes?

Erlend silenced it. He was good at silencing it; he had been silencing it for years. The boy would understand. The boy had fire in him too — Erlend had seen it, or believed he'd seen it, in the set of those narrow shoulders at the meetings. The boy would understand that some things are worth more than wood and iron. That a cause is a kind of inheritance. That glory is the only estate that can't be seized.

Besides — and here the third glass whispered its sweetest argument — besides, if the shop burned, then there was nothing to sell. No press for Voss's committee to buy, no bargain, no ship, no berth. All that scheming over passage, all the whispering between Voss and the boy that stopped when Erlend entered the room — it would end. Whatever his children were planning without him, whatever bargains were being struck in corners, fire would sweep the table clean. What remained would be his alone: the flame, the crowd, the name written in smoke over the harbor.

He went downstairs in his nightshirt. He did not light a lamp. He knew the shop the way he knew his own mouth.

He stood before the press a long time, his hand flat on the iron, feeling the cold of it climb into his palm. His father had helped cast the screw. His mother had kept the accounts in a hand so fine the bank clerks had accused her of engraving. He had pulled the lever of this press perhaps a million times, and every sheet that came off it had carried a little of the family into the world — bills and ballads and obituaries and marriage notices, the whole ordinary chronicle of a harbor town, and lately, yes, the pamphlets, the red ones, the ones that made his heart go like a drum.

Three men died at the customs house. He had heard it said aloud today in the market, said casually, the way one says *it rained*. Three men, and somewhere in the city three women were laying out clothes for burial, and the ink that had moved the crowd to stones and torches had come off his press, out of his hands, and he had felt — God forgive him, or don't, he no longer knew which — he had felt *used*, the way an instrument feels used. Exalted and horrified in the same breath, and then exalted again, because the horror passed and the exaltation stayed, the way pleasure stays and pain goes.

Fire would finish the work. Fire was the final edition. You could confiscate a press but you could not confiscate a conflagration; it printed itself on the sky for everyone in the city to read.

There was lamp oil in the back room. There were the barrels of sizing spirits. There was the old straw packing in the loft, dry as tinder after all these months. It would take no more than an hour to lay it right — and it must be laid right, he was a craftsman, even his arson would be well-set type — the oil poured low, the draught opened, the stairway left so the flames would climb into the tarred roof and stand up over the harbor like a sentence in letters ten feet high.

He worked until the sky went from black to the color of wet slate, moving quietly, thoroughly, a compositor locking up his final forme. Oil in the seams of the floorboards. Spirits in the type cases, so the letters themselves would melt. Papers stacked in the center like a keel. When it was done he stood in the doorway of his own shop and looked at what he had made, and it was the finest thing he had ever composed. Every other piece of work he'd set would outlive him only as waste paper. This would be remembered.

He allowed himself one moment of doubt, standing there with the matches in his pocket, dry and small as bones.

One moment, in which he saw the boy's face — not accusing, which he could have borne, but *disappointed*, which he could not — and saw the empty case where the boy kept the matrices their grandfather had cut, and understood in one cold flash that fire did not distinguish between a traitor's papers and a family's memory, that fire was not a flag, fire was just fire, and it ate whatever was nearest.

Then he remembered Brandt's thumbnail creasing the paper. Then he remembered the three dead men, and the crowd roaring his name outside the cellar door — *Eriksen! Master Eriksen!* — and how it had sounded, how it had *finally* sounded like his name and not his father's.

Dawn was perhaps two hours off. He would wait for full dark's end, for the hour when the watchmen changed and the streets were emptiest. He would send the boy away first — some errand, anything, to the wharf, to Relling's — so that no one could say the son fired the shop. The father alone. The father bearing it all. That was the story, and it was even true, and truth made the best propaganda; he had learned that from his own pamphlets.

He climbed the stairs. Below him the shop lay soaked in oil and silence, twelve hundred pounds of iron waiting to become a headline.

In the room across the landing he could hear the boy breathing in sleep, quick and light, the way the boy had breathed since childhood — since always, Erlend thought, since the beginning, that strange quiet vigilance, as if the child slept with one ear open for a world that might turn on him. Good. Let him sleep. Tomorrow the whole harbor would know the name Eriksen, and the boy would wake to a father made of fire and glory, and whatever grief came after, grief washed out, but a name —

A name was forever.

Erlend lay down in his clothes, the matches in his pocket pressed against his hip like a second heartbeat, and stared at the ceiling, listening to the rain thin toward dawn.

He did not hear the floorboard below the boy's door, an hour later, take one careful weight and give it back.

He did not hear the shop door open, soft as a page turning.
