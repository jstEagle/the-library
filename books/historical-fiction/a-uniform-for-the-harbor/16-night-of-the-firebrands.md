## 16. Night of the Firebrands

**Oscar**

She smelled it before she understood it.

Lamp oil — not the clean, thin smell of the shop's own oil, but the heavy, tallowish reek of ship's stores, the kind sold by the drum at the chandlery near the customs house. It hung in the stairwell like a guest who had arrived without knocking and refused to leave.

Oscar set down her graver and listened. Below her, in the shop, her father was moving furniture.

Not printing. Not drinking, or not only drinking. The sounds were wrong for both: the scrape of the composing stone being dragged, the knock of type cases being stacked, the groan of wood on wood arranged with a deliberateness that had nothing to do with work. A man who cleaned did not clean at two in the morning with this much care. A man who organized did not organize his own ruin into neat piles.

She came down the stairs without lighting a lamp. She had learned to navigate the shop blind years ago; she could have walked it asleep, could have found every case and quoin by the map of it printed on the inside of her skull. In the dark she stopped at the bottom step and watched her father kneel by the hearth with a drum of lamp oil between his knees, working the tap loose with hands that shook — from drink, she thought at first, and then saw they were shaking from something else. Something almost like reverence.

He had soaked the floor beneath the press already. She could see the dark stain spreading across the boards, catching what little light came through the window from the harbor lanterns, shining there like a second, blacker sea.

"Papa." Her voice came out flat. In the dark, in this house, she had never once called him anything but Papa — not Father, as he now demanded of the men at committee meetings; not *comrade*, as Voss half-mocked, half-hoped. "What are you doing."

He looked up. His face in the gloom was a stranger's face, or rather it was the face she had been pretending not to see for weeks — flushed with purpose, lit from some fire inside him that had nothing yet to do with the one he was preparing below.

"Oscar. Good. You should be awake for this." He rose, joints cracking, and gestured at the press with an arm that embraced it like a bridegroom embracing a bride he intended to burn. "They're coming. Tomorrow. Maybe the day after — Lindqvist can hold them off no longer than that. And when they come, they will find nothing. Nothing but ash and a story." He smiled, and the smile was terrible because it was sincere. "They will write about us, boy. They will write about the printer of the harbor who would not surrender his press to tyrants. Ink is nothing. *Legend* is everything."

"You're going to burn the shop."

"I'm going to burn the *evidence*," he said, as if correcting a child's arithmetic. "The pamphlets. The lists. The correspondence. Everything that could hang a single man of the faithful. The press—" he laid one hand flat on its iron frame, tenderly, obscenely, "—the press goes with it, so that they cannot say we bargained. So that no one can ever claim the Eriksens sold their principles."

"The Eriksens," Oscar said slowly, "sold their principles three weeks ago. You printed them onto four hundred broadsides and handed them out at the fish market. Burning paper doesn't unprint it."

He turned on her then, and for a moment she saw the man from before — the grieving widower, the craftsman, the father who had taught her to hold a graver when her hands were too small for it — flickering behind the new face like a reflection in moving water. Then it went under.

"Go back upstairs," he said quietly. "Or stay and help me stack the cases. Either way, son, don't stand there talking treason against your own blood."

She stood there. She stood there until he turned back to his oil drum, and then she went out the side door into the rain and ran.

* * *

**Voss**

Voss had taken a room above the rope-walk, four flights up in a building that swayed when the wind came off the water, and he slept the way he had learned to sleep in ten years of conspiracy: dressed, booted, with a satchel packed and a window mapped. So when the pounding came at his door at half past two, he was awake before the third blow and had a hand on the knife under his pillow before he recognized the rhythm — quick, hard, unmistakably a woman's fist, though only one person alive knew to knock it.

He opened the door. Oscar Eriksen stood in the corridor with rain in her hair and no coat, breathing hard, and her eyes had a light in them he had never seen there before. He had catalogued her eyes carefully over these weeks — the way they lowered when Lindqvist spoke to her, the way they went still and watchful when her father held forth, the way they never, ever gave anything away. Now they gave away everything. Fear, and fury, and underneath both something that looked terribly like grief.

"He's going to burn it," she said. "Tonight. Before dawn. The shop, the press, all of it — he says the movement needs a martyr more than it needs a press."

Voss felt the words land in his chest like stones dropped down a well. He thought fast, the way he'd been trained to think, laying out the board: if the shop burned, the papers burned, and the names in those papers — including his own, including three men in Rostock, including a woman in Copenhagen whose arrest would unravel a network from the Baltic to the North Sea — those names survived. If the shop burned, the Eriksen press, which he had spent a small fortune and a larger portion of nerve acquiring for the cause, became smoke. If the shop burned, the harbormaster's careful arrangement collapsed, and whatever fragile thing had been negotiated between Oscar and Lindqvist collapsed with it.

And if the shop burned, Oscar Eriksen lost the only inheritance she had ever been permitted to touch — the one she had bled for, disguised herself for, buried her own name for — and Voss found that this last consideration was not fourth on the list in his mind at all, however much he told himself it was.

"Get your coat," he said. "We can be across the harbor by four, on the Swedish packet if—"

"No."

"No?"

"I'm not running." She pushed past him into the room, dripping on his floor, and stood facing him with her chin up in a way that reminded him, absurdly, of her brother — Anders had that same stubborn angle of jaw, that same refusal to be moved by weather or argument. "You want me gone. My father wants himself a legend. Anders wants his berth. Everyone has decided what I am worth and where I belong, and not one of you has asked me." Her voice cracked, just once, and she mastered it. "The press does not burn. Whatever happens after — the press does not burn. Help me stop him or get out of my way, Herr Voss, but do not tell me to run."

He looked at her a long moment. Rain ticked against the window. Somewhere below, a dog was barking at the harbor bells.

"You understand," he said at last, quietly, "that stopping him may cost you everything else. If the press stands when they come, there will be questions you cannot forge your way through. There will be a trial, maybe. There will be—" He stopped himself from saying *there will be a moment when you must choose between the truth and the lie you've lived*, because he suspected she already knew, and had known longer than he had.

"I have been choosing between truths since I was nine years old," Oscar said. "I'm practiced at it. Come on."

* * *

**Oscar**

They went along the quay road, keeping to the shadow of the warehouses, and the rain did them one kindness: it covered the sound of their running, though it also soaked them through a second time. The town lay strange and hollow at this hour — shuttered, lamplit only at the corners where the watch made their rounds, and everywhere the smell of tar and cold iron and the sea waiting beyond the mole like a held breath. Three days now since the navy's proclamation had gone up on the customs house door. Three days until the harbor closed. One civilian vessel permitted on the morning tide, and lists drawn up in Lindqvist's careful hand, and every family in the port doing arithmetic with their children's names.

The shop's windows glowed.

That was the first wrongness, worse than the oil smell, worse than the scraping in the night: her father had lit every lamp in the place, front room and back, so that the whole building stood lit up against the dark like a stage set, like something meant to be seen burning. Through the glass she could see him moving among the type cases, stacking them around the press in a kind of altar, and she saw too that he had dragged the pamphlet stock out of the cellar — the reams of cheap grey paper, hundreds of unsold sheets — and built them into a wall against the far shelving. Fuel, laid with a craftsman's patience. He had even, she realized with a lurch, opened the flour store and the pitch kegs from the boatyard next door. He had been planning this for days. Since the riot, perhaps. Since the three men died at the customs house and he had stood on the steps with ink on his cuffs and glory in his eyes, and something in him had concluded that a living printer was a liability but a dead one was a flag.

Voss tried the side door. Locked. He looked at her.

"Back way," she said. "Through the cellar. The hatch sticks — lift and shove left, it drops its latch."

They went down into the cellar where the paper was stored, where the smell of size and damp had been the smell of her entire childhood, and Oscar put her shoulder to the inner door and found it barred from above. Of course it was barred. He had barred it. Her father, who had taught her every lock and trick of the shop, had finally used his knowledge against his own household.

Above them, faintly, he began to sing.

It was a hymn, or had been once — some old psalm about the word abiding forever, the word of our God standing firm — and he sang it low and off-key and with tremendous satisfaction, punctuated by the slosh and gurgle of oil being poured. The word abides. The letters endure. She pressed her forehead against the cool wood of the door and felt, with horrible clarity, that he believed every note of it. That in his ruined arithmetic, this was not destruction but publication. The fire was his final edition, and the whole harbor was his readership.

"We go through the front," Voss said behind her. "Window. I'll break it, we go in over the cases—"

"And wake the street? The watch will come, and they'll find us climbing into a locked print shop drenched in lamp oil at three in the morning." She turned. "No. We let him open the door."

"How, in God's name?"

"By giving him what he wants." She heard herself say it and marveled at how calm her voice sounded, how much like her father's when he quoted scripture at committee meetings. "He wants a witness. He wants his legend to have an audience. He wants someone to see him do it, so that someone will tell everyone else." She took a breath. "So I'll knock on the front door, and I'll tell him I've come to help stack the type, and while he's preaching I'll take the key off the hook by the door, and you'll come in behind me and we'll put out what we can and carry the rest."

Voss stared at her. Rain dripped off his chin. "And if he decides to add us to the legend?"

"Then you'll finally get your martyrdom, Herr Voss, and I'll get mine, and neither of us will have to fill out any more lists." But her hand, she noticed, was not steady on the cellar rail, and her heart was hammering somewhere up around her throat, and the lamps above threw their light down through the floorboards in long golden stripes that looked, from below, exactly like bars.

They climbed the outside stairs to the street. The rain had slackened to a drifting mist. Down the quay, the masts of the blockading squadron sat on the horizon like a row of needles waiting to sew the harbor shut.

Oscar stood before her father's door — the door with ERICKSEN & SON painted on its transom in gold leaf her mother had chosen, in another life, twenty years ago — and raised her hand to knock.

Behind the glass, the singing stopped.

* * *

**Voss**

Later — in the years after, in the trials and the testimonies and the long slow telling of it — Voss would remember these minutes as the strangest of the whole affair: standing in the mist outside a condemned print shop, listening through the door as a madman and his daughter performed tenderness at each other across a floorboard of lamp oil.

Because that was what he heard. Oscar knocked, and Eriksen unbolted the door as though he had been expecting her — perhaps he had; the man's instincts were animal where his children were concerned, whatever else had rotted — and Voss, pressed into the doorway's shadow, heard the old man say, warm as brandy: "I hoped you'd come, boy. I hoped you'd understand."

"I understand, Papa."

"You always did. Your mother's head on your shoulders." A wet slap of a hand on a shoulder. "Help me carry the last of the cases to the middle. We'll want it hot and fast when I light it — no smoldering, no half-measures. When the harbor wakes, there will be a column of smoke they can read from the fleet."

"And the press, Papa?"

A silence. In it, Voss heard the creak of a floorboard, the clink of the oil drum being set down, and he eased forward through the gap of the door into the lamp-blasted room, low and quiet, one step, two, toward the hook by the jamb where the cellar key hung — and stopped, because Eriksen had turned, and his eyes, swimming and clear at once, went past his child directly to Voss as if the darkness had been a formality all along.

"Ah," said the printer of the harbor. "The agent. Come to save your precious press, are you? Or come to save your precious names?" He lifted the oil drum and hugged it to his chest like an infant. "Too late for either. It's done, it's laid, it's blessed. All that remains is the match, and the match is mine."

"Papa." Oscar's voice, level and low, in the space between them. "Look at the floor. Really look at it."

Eriksen frowned — and Voss saw it happen, saw the drunk purpose in his face snag on something, because the puddles were wrong. Too shallow. Spreading in the wrong directions. Because while the old man had preached, Voss had not only gone for the key; he had gone first, silently, to the sand bucket by the hearth and the water butt by the sink, and while Oscar's voice had held her father's eyes, his hands had done their work. Half the oil was already cut with sand, drowned, spread into useless grey paste across the boards.

"What have you—" Eriksen's voice climbed. "What have you *done*—"

"What you raised me to do," Oscar said, and stepped between her father and the hearth, between her father and the matches lying on the composing stone, and Voss saw her square her shoulders in the lamplight with the press at her back like a fortress wall. "I learned from the best hand in the harbor. You said so yourself, a hundred times. Well, Papa. Watch the best hand in the harbor work."

Eriksen lunged. Voss moved to catch him —

— and the old man, stronger than any of them had reckoned, drunker and more desperate, spun free with the oil drum still in his arms and hurled it, not at the hearth but at the lamp itself, at the nearest flame —

— and Oscar dove, and caught the drum's rim on her forearms as it came down, and the oil leapt out in a black sheet across her sleeves and the floorboards, and for one suspended second the whole room waited on the distance between the spilled fuel and the flame, and Voss's heart stopped dead in his chest —

— and the lamp guttered. Draught from the broken seal of the door, a breath of harbor air, and the flame bent sideways and shrank to a blue bead, and died.

Darkness. Rain on glass. Three people breathing hard in a black room that should have been an inferno.

Then, from the doorway behind them — a dry, unhurried voice that belonged to none of the three:

"Well," said Harbormaster Lindqvist, striking a match and holding it high so its light fell on his face and on the two constables at his back, and on the warrant paper in his other hand. "It seems I've arrived just in time for the ending. Or," and his gaze traveled from the soaked floor to the oil-blackened daughter to the trembling father to the cornered agent, and settled at last on the press, gleaming in the matchlight like a verdict, "perhaps for the beginning."
