## 17. The Forger's Daughter

The candle had burned down to a thumb's width when Oscar finally understood what she had to do.

She sat at the workbench in the loft above the shop, the one she had occupied since she was nine years old, and she looked at the papers spread before her the way a surgeon looks at a wound: without flinching, without mercy, with only the cold arithmetic of what must be cut. The bill of sale. The transfer of assets. The witness lines waiting empty as graves.

Below her, through the floorboards, she could hear her father moving. He thought she was asleep. He moved the way men move when they are carrying something heavy and holy—slowly, reverently, with the oil can from the press room and the long tapers they kept for lighting the stove. She had watched him all evening rehearsing his martyrdom: the set of his shoulders as he stood at the window looking toward the harbor, the way he touched the frame of the great press like a priest touching an altar he intended to burn.

He was going to fire the shop. She had known it since the moment she'd seen him counting the lamp oil—not the ink, not the paper, but the oil—and setting aside three full tins behind the flour barrel where her mother used to keep the winter stores.

*The evidence must not fall into their hands,* he would say. *Better ashes than chains.* He would say it with his chin lifted and his eyes wet, and he would believe every word, because that was the terrible thing about her father: he never lied to himself. He simply chose, each morning, which truth to worship.

Oscar dipped her pen and did not write anything yet. First she studied.

Her father's signature was not difficult. That was the tragedy of it—the man who had taught her that a letterform must be true down to its smallest serif could not sign his own name twice the same way. His hand was a drunkard's hand, bold and wavering, the capital E of *Eriksen* always too large, like a man shouting his own name at a door that would not open. The crossbar of the T wandered. The final N collapsed into a scrawl that might have been surrender or exhaustion or both.

She had been imitating hands since she was eleven years old.

It had started as a game, then a necessity, then a craft so private she had never once spoken it aloud. When the excise letters came and her father was too deep in his cups to answer them, she answered them—in his hand, with his flourishes, sealing them with his ring while he slept. When the guild demanded confirmation of apprenticeship papers for "his son," she wrote the confirmations herself, describing her own seven years of labor in her father's voice, praising her own steady hand in words she would have blushed to hear spoken. For fifteen years she had been her father's ghost writer, his double, his echo made ink. He had never known. Or perhaps—he had never wanted to know. There were mercies in willful blindness, and her father had taken every one of them.

Now she would take the skill back. All of it. Every forged letter, every disguised year, every hour spent being a boy on paper so she could be a girl nowhere at all—it had all been practice. It had all been leading here, to this table, this candle, this night.

She practiced the signature eleven times on scrap paper torn from the edge of a spoiled broadside. On the twelfth attempt she stopped trying to make it perfect, because perfect was wrong; her father's signature was never perfect. She let her wrist go loose, let the wine-tremor into it, let the E swell arrogant and the tail of the final N die mid-stroke as though the man signing had grown tired of himself halfway through his own name.

It was beautiful. It was damning. It was exactly what he would have written if he had ever once, in his life, done the sensible thing.

* * *

Voss came at the second bell after midnight, knocking the pattern they had agreed on—two quick, one slow, like a heart deciding. She let him up the outside stair, and he came in smelling of rain and river tar, his coat dark with wet, his eyes going immediately to the papers on the bench.

"You found a notary," he said. Not a question. He had spent the evening finding one himself—a gray, frightened man in the Neustadt who owed Voss's network more than he owed the crown, and who would stamp whatever was put in front of him and ask nothing, because asking had become dangerous in this city and silence had become currency.

"I found something better than a notary." She turned the bill of sale toward him. "I found my father."

Voss read it the way he read everything—fast, then again slowly, his finger tracing the line of the signature. When he reached it he went very still, the way he had gone still the first day in the shop, when she had recognized Anders's handwriting in his letters and said nothing, and he had watched her say nothing and understood, somehow, everything.

"That's his hand," Voss said quietly.

"It's better than his hand. His hand shakes too much after the third glass, and he's had six." She kept her voice level, but her own hand, she noticed, was not shaking at all, and that frightened her more than anything else about the night. "He signed a contract with the customs broker in March. I kept a copy. And the guild ledger has his mark from the autumn assize. Between them I have every habit of his pen—the way he loops, the way he presses harder coming out of a curve than going in. A man's signature is a door he thinks is locked. I've had the key since I was a child."

"Oscar." Voss set the paper down carefully, as if it might go off. "Do you understand what you're doing? If this is discovered—if anyone compares this against a true sample—"

"They'll hang me for forgery instead of sedition. One crime instead of two. I've done the arithmetic." She almost smiled. "I've been doing arithmetic my whole life, Voss. How many years can a lie hold? How much of myself fits inside the word *son*? This—" she touched the signature, still faintly tacky, "—this is the first honest dishonest thing I've ever done. Everything else I forged to hide. This I forge to save."

"The press moves tonight?"

"Before dawn. You said your people have the warehouse on the Fischergasse, the one with the barge access." She was already rolling the drawings—the press diagrams, the type inventory, the crate lists she had compiled in her head over three sleepless nights. "Twelve crates. The frame comes apart at four bolts; I marked them in chalk. The type goes in the apple boxes from the cellar—there's straw in them, pack it tight, type cracks against type. And the matrices travel separately, in the false bottom of the flour chest. If they stop you on the street, you're moving a printer's estate. Grief-stricken son settling accounts before fleeing the city. Here." She handed him a letter, folded and sealed. "A letter of instruction, in my father's hand, authorizing the removal. Dated three days ago. Grief makes men do strange things with property."

Voss took it and looked at her for a long moment. Rain ticked against the loft's small window. Somewhere below, her father coughed—the deep, wet cough that had been getting worse all winter, the cough of a man burning himself down like one of his own tapers.

"You could come with us," Voss said. "There's room on the barge. There's—" he stopped, and she saw him choose honesty over comfort, which was the thing about him she trusted most. "There's nothing waiting for you here but a trial."

"There's my name waiting for me here." She began stacking the crates by the trapdoor, working as she talked, because if she stopped working her hands would start remembering they were afraid. "Anders gets the ship. I told you that in the cellar and I haven't changed. But I'm not running, Voss. Someone printed those pamphlets. Someone set every letter of every slogan my father shouted at the mob. The authorities will want a printer. They'll find ash and a drunken old man who will weep beautifully and confess to everything and nothing. And they'll find me." She paused, a crate in her arms, its weight settling against her chest. "They'll find Oscar Eriksen. Not his son. Me. And I will tell them the truth, all of it, and some of it will even help me."

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then I'll have been myself for a few weeks at the end. That's more than most people get."

Voss said nothing to that. He simply picked up the other end of the crate, and together they lowered it through the trapdoor into the dark shop below, where the press waited under its dust sheet like a body under a shroud.

* * *

They worked until the sky behind the eastern roofs turned the color of dirty pewter. Four hours to strip a press her father had sworn would take a day, because she knew it the way she knew her own skeleton—which bolt sang when it turned, which joint needed the wooden mallet and which the iron, which corner of the frame would catch on the stairwell unless it went down tilted ten degrees to the left. Voss's men came in ones and twos, stevedores' faces, dockworkers' hands, saying nothing, taking the crates out the loading door into a cart that left the yard empty and returned empty and left again. Each time the cart rolled away Oscar felt lighter and sicker at once, as though the press were being carried out of her ribcage plank by plank.

By the fourth trip only the stone plinth remained, and the oil stains shaped like a country no map had ever named, and the smell of ink that would live in those walls longer than any of them would live anywhere.

Her father's door stayed shut. She had listened for it all night—the creak, the stumble, the voice slurring her brother's name, her dead name, the name she was killing tonight—and it had not come. Either he slept the sleep of the righteous, or he lay awake listening to his life's work being carried away and could not imagine what it meant, because it had never once occurred to him that his children might act without his permission. That, too, was a kind of blindness he had earned.

At half past four, with the last crate gone and the cart gone and Voss standing in the doorway of the emptied shop, hat in his hands like a mourner, Oscar did the last thing.

She sat down at the composing stick one final time—not the great press, that was already bolted to the barge deck somewhere in the fog, but the little proofing frame she had kept back, hidden in the flour chest with the matrices. She set a single line, twelve-point, in the old Garamond her grandfather had cut, the face that had built the family and would now outlive its ruin:

*Sold, with all appurtenances, freely and lawfully, this day.*

She pulled one proof on scrap paper, looked at it, and burned it in the stove. Then she put the type back in its case, every letter home, and closed the lid.

"It's done," she said to Voss. "Legally, as of the notary's stamp at midnight, the press belongs to the network. My father owns a plinth and a bad reputation. When he comes down with his oil cans and his matches, he'll be burning an empty church."

Voss nodded slowly. Then he did something she did not expect. He took her hand—the right one, the cutting hand, the forging hand—and pressed it briefly between both of his, and said, "Your brother wrote to me once that his sister had the steadiest hand in the Baltic. He didn't know how right he was."

"He knew," Oscar said. Her throat was tight. "He always knew. That was the trouble with Anders. He saw everything and stayed anyway."

The bells of St. Petri rang five. The fog was lifting off the harbor in rags. Somewhere out on the water, invisible, the ship that would carry her brother rode at anchor with one berth empty and the tide turning within the hour.

And somewhere upstairs, a floorboard creaked.

Oscar's head came up. The sound came again—deliberate, heavy, awake—and then footsteps crossing the room above, and the scrape of the window latch, and her father's voice drifting down through the floorboards, thick with sleep and something else, something colder underneath:

"So. The rats are moving the grain."

Voss went for the door. Oscar stood very still in the middle of her emptied inheritance, her ink-stained fingers curled at her sides, and listened to her father begin to descend the stairs.
