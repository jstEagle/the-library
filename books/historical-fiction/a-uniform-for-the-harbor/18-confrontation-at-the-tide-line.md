## 18. Confrontation at the Tide Line

Her father came down the stairs the way a storm comes off the water—slowly, so you could watch it arrive.

He had not dressed. He wore his nightshirt and his boots and nothing between them but fury, and his hair stood up in grey wings that made him look, for one absurd moment, like the man who used to carry her on his shoulders along the breakwater before her mother died, before the lie hardened into law. Then he saw the crates by the door—six of them, straw-packed, roped, stenciled with Voss's network's mark—and the resemblance drowned.

"Sixty pounds of type," he said. His voice was very quiet, which was worse than shouting. "My matrices. My molds. My *name* over the door."

"The name comes down tonight anyway," Voss said. He had positioned himself between Eriksen and the crates with the loose-hipped ease of a man who had been in worse rooms than this. "You were going to burn it."

"I was going to *free* it."

"You were going to burn it," Voss repeated, without heat, and Oscar understood suddenly why Anders trusted this man: he did not flinch, and he did not perform. He simply said the true thing twice and let it stand there bleeding.

Her father looked past him to her. "You signed something."

It was not a question. Somewhere in the small hours, while she bent over the bill of sale with the lamp turned low and Voss watching the street through the gap in the shutters, some animal sense in him had woken—the same sense, she thought wildly, that had once let him pick out her cut of an *a* from among ten thousand others, that had made him say *the boy has my hand* with such stupid pride. He had felt the forgery being made two streets away, the way a man feels weather in an old wound.

"I sold the press," Oscar said. "To the committee. Properly. Papers drawn, witnessed, stamped by Notary Halvorsen before he closed his shutters for good." She heard her own voice as if from across the room—steady, level, a voice that had learned steadiness from years of saying difficult things quietly to drunk men. "The press is theirs now. You can't burn what you don't own."

For a moment nothing in the shop moved except the lamp flame, leaning toward the draft under the door.

Then her father laughed.

It was not a large laugh. It was a single syllable, dry as a snapped sort, and it frightened her more than any rage could have, because it meant he had already passed through rage into something beyond it, some cold country where men make decisions about fires and daughters.

"*Sold* it," he said. "With what authority? The shop is mine. I built it out of nothing—I cut the first punches when you—" and here he stopped, because the sentence he had been about to finish was *when you were a child*, and children were not part of the grammar he had chosen for her.

"When I was your son," Oscar finished for him.

Voss glanced at her. She kept her eyes on her father.

"You'll come to the harbor," her father said, "and you'll tell Halvorsen—or whoever holds those papers—that you signed under coercion. That the stranger forced you. That it means nothing." He took a step forward, and the floorboards spoke under his boot heels, each one a small verdict. "You'll say it because you are an Eriksen, and an Eriksen does not hand his father's life's work to men who print sedition in cellars."

"An Eriksen," Oscar said.

Something in her chest unlocked then—not gently; it went like a crate lid pried open after years of nailing, taking splinters of her with it—and she heard herself continue before any prudent part of her could intervene:

"Yes. Let's talk about what an Eriksen is."

* *

They walked to the harbor because her father made it a procession and she let it be one. He would not stay in the shop—he could not bear to stand among the empty racks where the type had been—and so he strode out into the blue-black hour before dawn with his nightshirt tucked into his trousers like a man too important for costume, and Oscar followed, and Voss followed her, and behind them came the two stevedores Voss had hired, trundling the last crate on a handbarrow, its wheels complaining against the cobbles all the way down Kirkegade like a slow witness.

The town was awake in the wrong way. Windows glowed at hours when no honest window should. There were soldiers at the customs house corner, six of them, stamping their feet against the cold, and their breath rose in white columns and their bayonets caught the first grey light coming up over the water like needles being threaded. Somewhere a drum was beating, unhurried, the navy's pulse.

The tide line lay along the quay like a rule drawn by God: wet stone below, dry stone above, and the single ship riding at anchor inside the boom—the last civilian vessel, her canvas furled, her rail already crowded with shapes that might have been passengers or might have been shadows. Lindqvist stood at the head of the gangway with his ledger open and his clerk beside him, checking names by lantern light, and when he saw Eriksen coming he did not move, but something moved *in* him, a settling, like a man shifting weight before bad news.

Oscar had imagined this moment a hundred ways since the ink dried on the forged signature. In none of them had she imagined feeling this calm. It was the calm of the composing stick, she thought—the calm that comes when every letter is finally in its case and the line is set and all that remains is to pull the lever.

Her father stopped three paces from Lindqvist and produced, from somewhere, a folded copy of the harbor proclamation, holding it up as though it were a writ.

"This man," he said, indicating Voss with a jerk of his chin, "has defrauded me. Papers were signed in my name without my knowledge. My son will confirm it. Won't you, boy."

The word *boy* fell between them on the wet stone.

And Oscar found that she was done.

Not brave—she would not have called it brave; it felt more like a joint finally dislocating after years of strain, painful and inevitable and almost a relief—but done. Done carrying it up and down these stairs. Done cutting her voice to fit the case. Done being the son who would inherit everything except himself.

"Father," she said.

He turned. Around them the quay had begun, without anyone deciding it, to gather—an audience of stevedores pausing with their loads, of sailors on the ship's rail, of women with bundles who had been waiting since midnight for names to be called. She saw Lindqvist close his ledger slowly, the way a priest closes a book.

"My name is not Oscar," she said. "Well. It is Oscar. But it is also—"

Her throat closed. Three years of a dead brother's name worn daily like a borrowed coat, twenty-two years of binding, of lowered voice, of standing to piss at the harbor wall with her heart hammering, of *my son*, *my son*, *my heir*—all of it pressed up against the back of her teeth at once, and she thought absurdly of the letterform *Æ*, the ligature her mother had loved, two letters joined into one sound, neither erased, both present.

"It is also Astrid," she said, and her voice carried farther than she expected, out over the water, and the drum seemed to falter on it. "Astrid Eriksen. Your daughter. I am your daughter, Father, and I have been your son since Mama died, because you needed an heir more than you needed to grieve, and I let you need it. I cut your type. I set your lines. I signed my brother's name to my own work until I forgot which hand was mine."

She pulled the cap from her hair. It had been pinned that morning in the dark of the shop, quickly, the way she had pinned it ten thousand mornings, and it came away and her hair fell down her back in the sea wind, red as her mother's had been, unmistakable, undeniable.

The quay made a sound. Not loud—a collective indrawing, the sound of a hundred people revising a story they had believed for years. A woman near the fish stalls crossed herself. One of the soldiers shifted his musket and said nothing, because whatever this was, it was not yet treason.

Her father stared at her. And here was the thing she had not prepared for: he did not look shocked. He looked *found*. As if some part of him had known—had always known, in the cellar of himself where men keep what they cannot afford to think—and had spent twenty-two years not-knowing with tremendous discipline, and now the discipline had failed all at once and the knowledge came up like floodwater.

"Astrid," he said. He said it the way a man says the name of a town he fled long ago.

"You knew," she whispered. "God help me—you knew."

"I knew I had a son," he said, and his voice cracked down the middle, and for one heartbeat he was only a tired old craftsman on a cold quay, "because a son could keep the trade alive. That is all I allowed myself to know." His eyes went to the crates on the barrow, to the ship, to the soldiers, back to her, moving fast now, cornered-animal fast. "And this—this confession, this *costume*-tearing—is that meant to save you? To put yourself outside the papers? Do you think they care what's under your shirt when they ask who signed?"

"They won't ask," said a new voice, and Harbormaster Lindqvist stepped forward, closing his ledger with a finality like a coffin lid. "Because I witnessed the signing. Alongside Notary Halvorsen, whose clerk has the duplicate. The sale is lawful, Eriksen. The press belongs to the buyer as of yesterday evening." He paused, and his old eyes—eyes that had weighed cargo and men for forty years—moved from the father to the daughter and back. "Signed, if I recall the document correctly, by one O. Eriksen, in a hand any fool can see is yours, girl. Neat. Confident. Your father's *r* exactly, but the *d*—" he almost smiled— "the *d* has your mother's loop. I set type beside her, once. Before you were born."

The wind came off the Baltic and pushed the smell of tar and cold iron up the quay. On the ship, someone began calling names.

"You forged your father's hand," her father said slowly. He was looking at her as though seeing her for the first time—as though the disguise had worked so well that even now, with her hair down and her name spoken, some part of him searched the quay for the boy who ought to be standing there. "Twenty-two years I taught that hand. Fed it. Clothed it. Believed it was mine to command." Something moved through his face that was not hatred, or not only hatred—something older and more terrible, which was pride. "And it was better than mine all along."

"Father—"

"Do you know what they do to forgers?" he asked, and now the cold thing was back, and it was aimed at her like a compass needle finding north. "Do you know what they do to girls who sell their fathers' property out from under them? You've unmade us both, Astrid. You've handed them everything—your name, your crime, your confession, spoken before witnesses on an open quay." He turned to Lindqvist, arms spreading, nightshirt billowing, suddenly magnificent, suddenly the martyr again, the man from the pamphlets, the father of the new age. "Arrest her, then. Arrest the forger. But know that the press is *gone* from her hands too—sold to radicals, spirited onto that ship—and that when the harbor burns, and it *will* burn, no paper of sale will save any of us."

"No one is arresting anyone," Lindqvist said flatly, "before that ship sails. Those are my orders and my inclination both." But he looked at Oscar—Astrid—with something that had shifted in it, something between pity and appraisal, the look of a man re-pricing goods he thought he'd valued correctly.

And then, over the water, the drum changed.

Faster now. And beneath it, another sound: boots. Many boots, on the far quay, coming down toward the tide line in column, and the grey light lifting enough to show them—naval landing party, thirty strong, moving with the unhurried certainty of men who have done this before, and behind them, at the customs house, a signal flag going up the mast, yellow and black, the color of closed ports and confiscated presses.

Lindqvist swore under his breath and opened his ledger again. "Names," he called to the ship. "We call names *now*. Anyone not aboard when the landing party reaches the gangway doesn't sail. That's not my rule anymore."

Voss appeared at Astrid's shoulder. She had not seen him cross the quay. "One berth," he said quietly. "Anders isn't here yet. He was supposed to be among the stevedores by now." A pause, and when he spoke again his voice had dropped to nearly nothing, meant for her alone. "If he's late—if they take him—it will be because someone talked. Someone always talks. And the list they're working from—" he glanced at the soldiers, at the flag, at her father standing tall and doomed in his nightshirt— "will have an Eriksen name on it. Which one, I wonder."

Her father heard none of it. He was watching the landing party come, and his face had gone luminous, transfigured, the face of a man who believes history has finally arrived to collect him personally.

Astrid stood at the tide line with her hair streaming and her father's name still warm on her tongue and her brother's berth empty, and watched the boots come closer, and counted.
