## 19. One Berth, Given

The tide would turn at half past four, and the harbor had already begun to hold its breath.

I stood at the window of the sailmaker's loft above the quay, watching the stevedores come down the ramp in the gray hour before dawn. They moved in their usual shuffling column, shoulders hunched against the wind off the water, each man a shape of canvas and rope and salt-stiffened wool. Somewhere in that column was my brother.

Voss stood beside me at the glass, his breath fogging it in small, impatient clouds. "Third from the end," he said. "The one who walks like he's counting his steps."

"He always counted his steps." My voice came out strange, flattened, as though someone else were speaking through me. "When we were children. He said if you knew how many steps it was to anywhere, you could never be lost."

"And now?"

"Now I suppose he's proving himself wrong."

Below, the *Søster Anna* lay against the quay with her holds half full of timber and her papers stamped by Lindqvist's own hand—the one civilian vessel permitted to sail on the morning tide before the navy closed its fist around the port. Her captain smoked a pipe at the gangway, patient as furniture. The customs men had already been and gone, bored, cold, checking nothing but the manifest. The revolution had not yet reached this stretch of stone. In an hour it would be too late for anyone to reach it at all.

"Oscar." Voss turned from the window. In the lamplight his face looked older than it had three weeks ago, when he'd come dripping through our door carrying letters written in a dead man's hand. "The berth is yours. Anders wrote it plainly. He asked you to decide, and you've decided—so let's not stand here performing the decision a second time."

"I'm not performing anything."

"You're standing very still and looking at the sea. That's a performance. I've seen men do it on the eve of battles."

I almost smiled. Almost. "Go down and fetch him, then. Before I change into someone who runs."

He hesitated—one heartbeat, two—then did something I did not expect. He took my hand, pressed it briefly between both of his, calloused palm against ink-stained knuckles, and said nothing at all. Then he was gone down the stairs, and I was alone with the window and the column of stevedores and the ship that could carry only one of us away.

* * *

You should understand what I had decided, and why, because for most of my life I had decided nothing so large as this.

For nineteen years I had been my father's son. I had bound myself flat every morning since I was eleven years old, had learned to walk with my weight forward and my voice held low, had cut type by lamplight with hands that grew strong and sure while the girl I might have been thinned to a rumor behind the boy everyone saw. I had done it because the trade passed to sons and not daughters, because my father's grief after my mother died had no room in it for a second loss, because a lie told once out of love becomes a law told twice out of habit and a hundred times out of fear. I had resented it. I had also, in some crooked corner of myself, been proud of it—the craft of the disguise, the daily forgery of my own body, the skill of it. I had thought that if I survived long enough to inherit the shop, the truth would keep until it didn't matter anymore.

Then the press burned—or rather, my father had decided it should burn, with all our livelihood inside it, so that he could die a martyr to a cause that had never asked him for anything but paper. And in the last dark hours before dawn I had sat at my father's desk with his ledger open before me and forged his signature so cleanly that even he, when he discovered it on the quay, had stared at it as though the letters themselves had betrayed him. The press belonged now to Voss's network, legally, irrevocably. It waited in a warehouse across the harbor, packed in straw, alive.

And I had stood on the stones and told my father what I was. Told him in front of Voss, in front of God and the gulls and the coming light: *Your son is your daughter, and she has a better hand than you ever did.* I had watched his face do something I had no name for. Then Anders's whistle had sounded from the shadow of the customs house, and there had been no time for anything but arithmetic.

One berth. Two Eriksens. A harbor closing like a hand.

* * *

They came up the loft stairs together, Voss first and then the stevedore who walked like he was counting his steps, and when the man pulled the wool cap from his head I saw my brother's face for the first time in three years and felt the floor tilt exactly as it had tilted the night Voss first laid those letters on our counter.

Anders was thinner. His beard was wrong—patchy, hastily grown, the disguise of a man who had spent years being no one in particular. There was a scar through one eyebrow that hadn't been there when the sea took his ship. But his eyes were our mother's eyes, brown and level and unhurried, and when they found me they went soft with something that broke me open more thoroughly than any argument ever could.

"Sister," he said. Just that. Not *brother*, not the careful word we'd have needed in any other room. *Sister*, spoken aloud, in a voice roughened by salt and smoke, as though the word had been waiting in his mouth for three years and he'd finally found the door to let it out.

"Anders."

We stood there. Neither of us moved. I think we were both afraid that if we touched each other the whole edifice—the disguise, the voyage, the morning itself—would collapse into the ordinary grief of two orphans in a rented room.

It was Anders who broke it, the way he'd broken everything as a boy, by laughing. "You look exactly like Father," he said. "That's the cruelest joke I've ever heard."

"You look exactly like Mother."

"So we've traded." He came forward then, and we held each other, and he was real, solid, smelling of tar and wet wool and underneath it faintly of the printing ink he'd been living among these past years, and I thought: *he is alive, he is alive, the sea lied.*

Voss turned tactfully to the window, though I saw his shoulders ease, and understood that he had feared this meeting too—that some part of him had believed Anders would not come, or would come changed beyond reaching, or that I would not be able to let go once I'd gotten hold of him.

"How much time?" Anders asked against my hair.

"The tide turns at half past four. You should be aboard within the hour, before the customs men make their second round."

"Then say what you've come to say quickly, because I can see you've rehearsed it and I'd rather hear it than watch you swallow it."

I stepped back. I had rehearsed it—I'd built the speech plank by plank through the sleepless night, and now, looking at his face, I dismantled it again and spoke plainly instead.

"There is one berth. Father believed it was his due; the committee believed it; perhaps you believed it. Voss says your letter left the choice to me. So here is my choice." I drew a breath that hurt going in. "You take it. You sail on the *Søster Anna* this morning, and you don't look back at this harbor, and you live. I stay."

"No." He said it immediately, without heat, the way one refuses a bad price. "No, Oscar. The letter was clear. If you wanted passage, it was yours. You've carried that shop on your back since you were a child—you've earned the escape more than either of us."

"Earned." I tasted the word. "Do you know what I did three nights ago? I forged Father's name to save the press. Do you know what I did this week before that? I set seditious type with these hands"—I held them up, the ink still shadowing the cuticles, ground in past washing—"beautiful type, Anders, the best work I've ever done, for pamphlets that put three men in the ground at the customs house. The authorities will want names for those pamphlets. They will want the printer's family. Father will give them a fire and a sermon, and when the fire is out they will come asking who else stood at the composing stick."

"Then you sail and let them ask an empty room."

"And let them write down that the Eriksen son fled like a criminal? Let them hang the whole story on a boy who never existed?" I shook my head. "Listen to me. All my life I have answered to a name that wasn't mine. Every document, every transaction, every handshake—signed with a lie. I am very good at lies, Anders; it's the family trade, along with type. But yesterday, on the quay, I said my true name out loud for the first time since Mother died, and I am telling you it fit my mouth better than any word I have ever spoken. I'm not giving that back. Not to flee from it."

"They'll arrest you."

"Perhaps."

"They could transport you. Worse."

"Perhaps." I smiled, and was surprised to find the smile steady. "But I'd rather stand trial as Oscar Eriksen, daughter of Henrik, type-cutter, than vanish forever as a boy who was never born. One of us has to be real in the record, brother. Let it be me."

* * *

He argued. Of course he argued—he was our father's son in his stubbornness if in nothing else, and for a quarter hour the little loft rang with it, low and fierce, two voices pitched under the wind. He offered to stay himself and send me; I reminded him that a woman traveling alone with forged papers would be stopped at the gangway before her foot touched the deck. He offered to give the berth to neither of us and let it lapse; I told him that would spend our mother's eyes for nothing, and he flinched, because he knew I was right. He invoked the cause, the movement, the future; I pointed out that the cause had already cost him a shipwreck and me a childhood, and that I intended to stop paying interest on other people's convictions.

Voss said almost nothing through all of it. He stood at the window with his back to us, watching the light come up over the water, and only once did he turn and say, quietly, "She's already decided, Anders. She decided it the night she signed her father's name. Everything since has just been the rest of us catching up."

Anders looked at me for a long moment then. And I saw it happen—the moment the argument went out of him and something else arrived in its place, something older and sadder and finer. He crossed the room and took my face in his scarred hands the way he had when we were small and I'd skinned my knees, and he said, "You were always the brave one. Everyone thought it was me because I was loud."

"You were loud," I agreed. My voice cracked on it. "Someone had to be quiet and competent."

"Take care of yourself." His thumbs brushed my cheekbones. "And when they ask you your name—and they will ask—say it the way you said it on the quay. Full voice. Make them write it down properly."

"With a straight descender on the k," I said. "I insist on good penmanship even from my enemies."

He laughed, and it turned into something else halfway through, and we held each other again while the light strengthened and the tide began, imperceptibly, to turn.

* * *

We went down together, the three of us, into the waking noise of the quay. The stevedores' column had dissolved into the day's labor; carts rattled; fishwives arranged their silver heaps with the indifference of women who had seen empires come and go between one herring and the next. At the foot of the gangway, the captain of the *Søster Anna* knocked out his pipe and consulted the sky as though the sky owed him money.

I had expected the parting to be hard. What I had not expected was how simple it became, once chosen. Anders embraced Voss—there was history there I only partly knew, debts and loyalties accumulated in cellars I'd never entered—and then he embraced me a final time, brief and fierce, and whispered against my ear: "Mother would have liked who you became. Both of who you became."

Then he picked up the bundle that held everything he owned in the world, squared his shoulders, and walked up the gangway like a man counting his steps, and at the top he turned once—not to wave, but simply to look at me, to fix me, I think, the way one fixes a page in memory before the book closes—and then he was through the rail and gone into the business of departure, and the ship that held my whole remaining family began, plank by creaking plank, to belong to the water.

I stood on the quay and watched the lines come off. I watched the sails wake. I watched the *Søster Anna* lean into the ebb and gather herself toward the harbor mouth, where the navy's ships sat black against the brightening east like punctuation at the end of a sentence, and I felt the strangest thing, which was not grief, or not only grief—a kind of terrible lightness, as though I had set down a weight I had carried so long I'd mistaken it for my spine.

"She'll make the open water," Voss said beside me. "Lindqvist's papers are clean, and Lindqvist owes us more than he'd like admitted."

"Yes."

"You could still have gone."

"No," I said. "I couldn't. Not and remained anyone."

He nodded slowly. Then, because he was Voss, and practical as a composing stick: "The warehouse by eight. There are forty cases of type to inventory, and the network wants a broadside ready by Friday—not slogans this time. Fair accounts. Names, dates, what actually happened at the customs house. Someone has to set it, and it has to be set beautifully, because the beautiful things are the ones people believe."

I looked at him, and understood that he was offering me not shelter but work—my work, the only inheritance I had ever truly wanted—and that he was offering it without conditions, without questions about what I was, because he had known for weeks and had simply, quietly, never made it matter.

"By eight," I said.

Down the quay, near the customs house, a knot of uniformed men had gathered around a notice board, and one of them was nailing up a fresh sheet. Even at this distance I recognized the official hand, the heavy bureaucratic blackletter, and beneath it, smaller, the words I could not yet read but already knew: a summons, an inquiry, a list of persons wanted for questioning in the matter of the seditious publications.

The wind carried the sound of the hammer, blow after blow after blow, like a press starting its run.

"Oscar," Voss said, using my name deliberately, testing its weight in the open air. "We should go."

"Yes," I said, and turned my back on the ship, and the sea, and my brother, and walked toward the notice board to read my own name in the government's type—and to see whether they had spelled it correctly.
