## 20. What the Ash Kept

I had meant it to be beautiful.

That is the confession I will carry into whatever cell they put me in, and the one I could not say aloud on the quay, with the soldiers' hands on my arms and my daughter standing in front of me like something cut from steel: that when I poured the lamp oil along the floorboards of the shop, between the composing stones and under the press's iron feet, I believed I was performing an act of terrible beauty. A printer burning his own press. A father immolating the instrument of his family's name so that no clerk of the Crown could ever set its letters to their own purposes. I had read enough pamphlets—God knows, I had printed enough of them—to know how the scene should look. The flames climbing the window glass. The crowd gathering in the rain. The martyr's silhouette against his own ruin.

I had even rehearsed a sentence for the neighbors. *Let them see what a free man does rather than surrender his tools.*

I want it written down, somewhere, by some honest hand, that I did not think of Oscar at all as I struck the match. Not of Anders either. That is not true—I thought of them constantly, but only as figures in the scene I was composing, the way a compositor thinks of letters: as types, locked into a line, serving the sense he has already decided upon. My son who had fled across the water. My son who stood at the tide line watching the ship take him. I arranged them in my mind like a broadside. *ERIKSEN FATHER AND SONS, PERSECUTED FOR THE CAUSE.* It was a good line. It set well. It was also a lie, and the lie burned faster than the wood did.

The fire went up quicker than I expected. Old pine, forty years of ink soaked into the floors, the oil, the wind coming off the harbor—it took the shop in minutes, and for a little while I stood in the alley behind the customs shed and watched it, and I will not pretend I felt nothing. I felt exalted. That is the word. Exalted, the way I had felt at the committee meetings, the way I had felt setting the first red pamphlet, the way I had felt every time Father Lindqvist's world—the harbormaster's world, the magistrate's world, the whole greased machinery of men whose grandfathers had owned mine—trembled at something that came out of my press. The fire was the largest thing I had ever printed. The whole sky was my sheet.

Then the roof beams went, and the press went with them.

I heard it before I understood it—a groan deeper than timber, a sound like a whale dying—and I knew, with a clarity that arrived all at once and never left, that the iron had come down. Forty years of my life. The type my wife's brother had helped me buy secondhand in Lübeck. The chases my father had fitted. The letterforms my child had learned to cut before she—before he—had learned long division. All of it folding into the fire like a body kneeling.

And the strange thing, the thing I have turned over ten thousand times since in the dark of this cell, is that the exaltation did not return. I waited for it. I stood in the rain with my sleeves singed and my lungs full of smoke, waiting to feel like a free man, a martyred man, a man of the new age—and instead I felt exactly what I was. An old drunkard standing in an alley, watching forty years go up because he could not bear to watch them taken.

* * *

They took me quietly, which offended me more than violence would have. Two constables and a naval lieutenant who never raised his voice. He asked my name and I gave it proudly, loudly, the whole harbor hearing it—*Eriksen! The printer!*—expecting the crowd to murmur, expecting someone to shout the slogan from my own pamphlets back at me. *The word belongs to the people.* I had written it myself. I had set it myself, letter by letter, in the small hours while my child slept above the shop.

No one shouted it. A fishwife looked at me the way one looks at a dog that has fouled a doorstep. A boy picked up a scrap of charred paper from the cobbles—one of my broadsides, half-burned, the ink still legible—and read it, frowned, and dropped it in a puddle.

"Come along, Eriksen," the lieutenant said. "There are questions about the customs house."

Three men. I knew it was three men before he said it; I had known it since the night of the riot, since I stood at the window of the Weisse Taube and heard the news come up the street, and felt—for one black and shining moment—that my words had moved the world. Three men dead because I wrote that the customs house was a cage and cages must be broken. I had felt horrified for perhaps the length of a prayer. Then I had felt proud again. Then I had gone home and drunk to my own eloquence.

Walking between the constables past the burning shop, I made the mistake of looking back through the doorway—or where the doorway had been; the door itself was already ash—and I saw, in the heart of the fire, the frame of the press glowing like a ribcage. And inside that ribcage, in my mind's eye, I saw the type. Every letter we owned. The founts my child had cut with her own hands—smaller than any foundry's, finer than anything I could afford, letters so true that compositors in three cities had asked who cut them, and I had said *my son* each time, and each time the word had tasted of nothing, because I had said it so long I had stopped hearing what it cost.

My son. My daughter.

She stood on the quay that morning and told a lieutenant of the Crown, in a voice that did not shake, that her name was Oscar Eriksen, daughter of Henrik Eriksen, and that the pamphlets were printed on her press, sold by her signature, and that if the authorities wanted a printer they had one. I heard her say it. I was being held two arm-lengths away, and I heard every word, and the strange part was not the *daughter*. I will be honest, even here, even now: the daughter surprised me less than it should have. There had been years—her mother's funeral, the boy's trousers let down and let down again until there was nothing left to let down, the voice that never quite broke the way the other boys' voices broke—there had been years when some cellar-door in me had known and would not open. What undid me was not *daughter*.

It was *her*.

Her press. Her signature. Her name, offered up like bread. I had spent three years telling anyone who would listen that the Eriksen name belonged to the cause, that my iron and my ink were the movement's property in all but law, and my child had gone out in the dawn and forged my hand on a bill of sale—not to save herself, she could have run, Voss had a berth, there was room for two—and given the berth to her brother, and kept the ruin for herself. She had signed her own arrest warrant in a fair, clear hand, and she stood unbowed while they wrote it down, and I understood, watching her, that everything I had claimed about power was true and that she had understood it better than I had. Power is not the torch. Power is the hand that signs.

* * *

The cell is cold and the straw smells of the last man's sickness, and I am glad of both. I have earned cold straw. But the warders leave a lamp burning outside the bars, and lamplight has been the whole of my life—lamplight and type metal—and so I sit close to the bars at night and I do what I have always done. I compose.

Not pamphlets. There is no press in here, and God forgive me, I hope there is never again a press with my name on it anywhere in the world. No. I compose the scene on the quay, over and over, correcting it, the way one corrects a galley proof, and each correction costs me blood.

In the first setting, the false one, the one I composed that first night, I am noble. I have burned the press to keep it from tyrants. My children are scattered by fate, innocent casualties. The line reads well. It sets tight. It is a lie from ascender to descender.

In the second setting, truer, I am a fool. A vain old man electrified by flattery, who mistook a smuggler's courtesy for history's invitation, and burned his house down rather than admit the movement had used him and discarded him. Voss came to us for a press, and I believed he had come for *me*. That line sets true, and it shames me, and shame is a kind of clarity, so I keep it.

But it is the third setting that keeps me awake, the one I cannot lock up properly, the one where the type keeps shifting in the chase no matter how I tighten the quoins. In the third setting, I see them both clearly, and seeing them clearly is the punishment.

Anders, going up the gangway in a stevedore's coat, turning once at the rail. He looked at me—I know now that he looked at me, Oscar told the lieutenant so, she volunteered it, she gave him away to the truth as thoroughly as she gave herself—and what was in his face was not hatred. That would have been bearable. Hatred is a debt you can acknowledge. What was in his face was pity, and beneath the pity, faintly, the ghost of the boy who used to stand on a crate beside me at the case and sort type into the wrong compartments while I pretended not to notice. He pitied me. His sister had bought his life with a forged name and a surrendered future, and he sailed away carrying his ideals and her sacrifice like two bundles he did not know how to hold, and the last thing he saw of his father was a man being folded into a constable's grip while his shop burned behind him—a man who had chosen fire over fatherhood, slogans over children, the loud red word over the small true ones.

And Oscar. Standing on the wet stones with her cropped hair plastered by the rain, in the coat she had worn as my son for fifteen years, answering questions in a voice level as a justified line. They took her statement down and she read it before signing, every word, and I watched her lips move slightly as she checked it, checking for errors of type, and I realized with a force that nearly put me on my knees that she had inherited everything—every craft, every discipline, every stubbornness—and that I had spent those same fifteen years teaching her only one lesson, the wrong one: that a self is something you hide. She unlearned it in a single morning on the quay. It took me fifty-eight years and a cell to learn the opposite.

The lamp outside the bars gutters. Somewhere below, a ship's bell counts the hour, and I think of the harbor closing its hand, and then opening again, seasons turning, tides keeping their appointments whether or not any Eriksen stands on the quay to watch them.

They tell me the trial will come with the spring assizes. They tell me a woman named Eriksen is to be tried alongside me—accomplice, they call her, though the lieutenant's clerks cannot agree on whether the press was hers or mine, because the bill of sale bears my signature and my signature, they have discovered, is worth nothing, since the man whose hand it imitates cannot even imitate honesty.

I have decided what I will say. I have been composing it nightly by this borrowed lamp, tightening it, cutting away the ornament, leaving only the true letters. When they ask me who printed the pamphlets, I will say: my child did. When they ask me who owned the press, I will say: my child did. When they ask me what I contributed, I will say—and this line has cost me more than all the others, and it is finally set, finally true—

*I contributed two children to this harbor. One I lost to the sea's politics. One I lost to my own. Whatever justice does with the remainder, let it be recorded that the finest work ever set in that shop was not set by me.*

Footsteps in the corridor. Keys. The warder's lantern swings its light across the straw, and with it comes a scrap of paper passed through the bars by a turnkey who will not meet my eye—a prisoner's privilege, a note from the women's wing, written in a hand I know better than any typeface ever cast, because I watched it being formed letter by letter over fifteen years of lamplight.

Four words only, in that fair, upright, unmistakable script:

*Stand up straight, Father.*

I hold the note to the light of the lamp beyond the bars, and for the first time since the fire, since the quay, since the riot and the red ink and the oily shine of my own exaltation—I laugh. It hurts. It sounds like rust. It is the first honest sound I have made in years.

Tomorrow they march us to the assizes together, father and daughter, chained in the same line like two sorts of the same fount. Let the court see us side by side. Let it judge which of us the fire refined.
