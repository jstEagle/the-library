## 21. Setting Type in a New Season

The press came to me in a cart, the way a bride once came to a husband—under cover, at an hour when no respectable person was watching, with only two men and a boy to witness it.

It was March when they wheeled it through the doors of the old sailmaker's loft on Fiskargatan, seven months after the fire, and the loft still smelled of hemp and tar and the sea's long patience. The floorboards groaned under the weight of it. I put my palm flat against the iron frame and felt the cold of the whole winter stored in the metal, and I thought: *you and I are both things that were carried out of a burning house.*

"Careful with the near-side leg," I said. "She's cracked there. She's always been cracked."

The carter looked at me as if a press might have legs one could know intimately, and perhaps he was right to look so. A man does not speak of a machine's cracked leg unless he has spent twenty years listening to it walk.

I had signed the lease three weeks earlier with my own hand—*Oscar Eriksen*—and the clerk at the land office had copied the name into his ledger without looking up, without any flicker of surprise or suspicion, and something in my chest had loosened then that I had not known was tied. It is a strange thing, to commit forgery against yourself for years and call it survival. It is a stranger thing to stop.

The name on the door went up two days later. I painted it myself, because I would trust no other hand with it: **O. ERIKSEN — PRINTER**. Not *Eriksen & Son*, which had been my father's proud lie for nineteen years. Not even *Widow Eriksen's*, which the neighbors might have found more digestible. Just the initial and the name, plain as a loaf of bread on a table. People would ask what the O stood for. Let them ask. I had spent a lifetime composing answers; I could compose one more, and this time it would be true all the way down.

My mother's name had begun with an O too. Odile. I did not tell anyone this. Some inheritances you keep in a drawer.

* * *

The first commission came from Lindqvist himself, which surprised no one who knew him and astonished everyone who didn't.

He arrived on a Tuesday morning while I was still sweeping shavings from the corners—not type shavings yet, only sawdust from the shelving I'd built—and he stood in the doorway with his hat in his hands like a man visiting a grave rather than a business.

"You're thinner," he said.

"You're older."

"We're both accurate." He set his hat on my new workbench, which offended me so much I nearly laughed. He looked around the loft the way he must have looked at ships in distress: measuring, tallying, deciding what could be saved. "I testified for you," he said. "You never thanked me properly."

"I sent a letter."

"A letter." He sniffed. "A letter is ink. I want to see what the ink buys." And he laid a document on the bench between us, weighted down with a lump of lead type I kept for just such purposes—a habit so deep in me I did it before thinking, the way another woman might reach for a shawl.

It was a contract. The harbor office wanted a printed register—arrivals, departures, cargoes, tonnages—issued weekly, in a fair hand, available to any merchant or captain who paid a small subscription. No slogans. No broadsides. No red ink shouting about the rights of man.

"The committee will say it's collaboration," Lindqvist warned. "The authorities will say it's radical bookkeeping. You'll please no one."

"Good," I said. "I've pleased everyone for nineteen years and it nearly killed me twice."

Something moved behind his eyes—approval, maybe, or the memory of the night he'd stood in the magistrate's chamber and sworn that the Eriksen press had been seized and sold by force, that the girl standing accused had acted under duress, that her father alone had composed the seditious matter. All of it true except the duress, which I had let stand because a small false mercy in a testimony is like a kerning error in a beautiful page: invisible to most, and forgivable if the line holds.

He left the contract. He took back his hat. At the door he paused and said, without turning around, "Your father asks after you. In the prison infirmary. He asks whether the shop keeps."

I said nothing for a long moment. The tar smell of the loft seemed to thicken.

"Tell him," I said finally, "that the shop keeps."

It was not a lie. The shop did keep. Whether it kept *me*—whether I kept it—that was the work of every day since, and would be the work of every day to come.

* * *

Setting the first register took eleven hours, and I loved every one of them with a love that frightened me a little, because I remembered what loving this trade had cost.

There is a particular silence that falls over a compositor at work, and I fell into it the way you fall into cold water—all at once and then completely. The stick grew heavy in my hand, satisfyingly heavy, each line a small argument between meaning and space. I set the shipping news in a face my father had cut the year I was six—the old Eriksen text, sturdy and slightly narrow-waisted, the letters like people standing close together in winter. My father's letters. My grandfather's proportions. Hands I would never meet had decided how these curves should open, and here they were again, opening onto lists of herring barrels and timber loads, and there was no politics in it anywhere, no cause, no banner. Just the harbor telling itself the truth in weekly installments.

By midnight I had locked the forme. By two I had the first proof pulled, and I stood holding it up to the lamp, reading my own name in the colophon—*Printed by Oscar Eriksen, Fiskargatan*—and I confess I read it four times. Each time the letters held. Each time they were mine.

A knock came near three. I nearly dropped the proof.

But it was only Halvard, the sailmaker's grandson, twelve years old and unable to sleep in a loft building where someone was hammering type at all hours. He appeared in the stair doorway in his nightshirt, blinking at the lamp like a small owl.

"You're the printer," he said.

"I'm the printer."

"My grandda says printers go to prison now."

"Some do." I turned the proof toward him. "This goes into ledgers. Ledgers have never imprisoned anyone. Come and look, if your grandda won't beat us both."

He came. He looked. And children being what they are—mercilessly curious—he asked the question within a minute: "Why do you hold the stick like that? With your thumb all crooked?"

"Because that's how you hold a composing stick if you mean to set ten thousand lines without your hand failing."

"It looks like a girl's way of holding it."

I looked at him. He looked back, unbothered, a child stating weather.

"Halvard," I said, "there is no girl's way of holding it. There is only the right way, and every wrong way, and the wrong ways all have names of men attached."

He frowned at that, working it through, and then—this is why I tell the story—he held out his hand. "Show me."

So at three in the morning in March, in a sailmaker's loft smelling of tar, I put a composing stick into a boy's hands and showed him where the thumb goes, and he botched it beautifully, and I corrected him, and somewhere in the middle of that correction I understood that my life had changed shape and I had not noticed the moment it happened. For nineteen years I had guarded a secret the way you guard a flame in wind. Now I was handing the craft out through a doorway in a nightshirt. The secret was dead. The craft was alive. It seemed a fair trade, made without my signature, though God knows I would have signed anything to make it binding.

* * *

Spring came to the harbor the way spring comes to northern ports: reluctantly, then all at once, then with a kind of apology. Ice went out of the basin. The first Swedish brig came in flying flags she hadn't flown last autumn. Men who had shouted at each other across barricades in October now nodded carefully at each other on the quay, the way dogs do after a fight neither can explain.

The register ran every week. Subscription lists grew slowly, then less slowly. Merchants discovered that accurate shipping news was worth more than any manifesto—they always discover this, in every age, in every port—and by May I could pay Halvard's grandfather rent on time and keep a boy named Jussi, a Baltic German refugee with ink-stained cuffs and a genius for the imposing stone, as my first journeyman.

Jussi asked no questions about the fire. Everyone in the city asked questions about the fire; it had been the spectacle of the year, the mad old printer torching his own shop as the soldiers came up the hill, the daughter—*the daughter*, the word had run through the town like brandy through a poor household—standing on the quay in the light of it. They had wanted me to weep for the crowd. I had not wept. I had watched my father's great act of martyrdom consume an empty building, because the press was already gone, already sold on papers bearing his forged hand, already rolling east in Voss's carts before the first match was struck. Let the flames have their theater. I had taken the plot.

Voss wrote sometimes. Short letters, unsigned, routed through three cities. The network had carried the press to Riga, then further; Anders's name appeared in none of them, which was either good news or careful news, and after a year of this life I had learned to live inside that ambiguity the way sailors live inside fog. Once, folded into a letter, came a woodcut of the press in its new room—a big room, windows, young people around it—and beneath it, in handwriting I did not know but could not mistake for anyone's but its author's intention: *She runs truer than ever. She misses your hand.*

"She," I said aloud to the empty loft, and laughed until I had to sit down.

* * *

In June the magistrate's final accounting reached me—acquittal affirmed, costs awarded against the crown, a paragraph in the official gazette so dry it crumbled in the reading. Lindqvist's testimony had held through every appeal. The authorities, it turned out, preferred believing a harbormaster to believing their own raid reports, because believing him required no one to resign.

That evening I walked out along the breakwater, as I did most evenings now, and stood where the tide line runs, and let the wind have its way with me. Out in the roads a Danish steamer lay at anchor, waiting for the morning tide, and I watched her riding lights come up one by one in the blue dusk, and I thought about berths. About how much of a life turns on who steps aboard and who stands on the stones. I had given away the berth and kept the harbor, and some mornings I still tested the choice like a tongue testing a broken tooth. Most mornings it held. This was one of the holding ones.

Behind me, up the hill, the loft window glowed. Jussi was working late on the fortnight's accounts. Somewhere below him, in the town, a hundred lamps, a hundred suppers, a hundred lives being lived in ignorance of type metal and just as well. The revolution had come to our city and mostly missed it, the way a storm misses a harbor by ten miles, leaving everyone to argue afterward about whether it had truly been a storm. Three men died at the customs house. My father's pamphlets had called them martyrs; my register, in its small gray way, had printed the coroner's findings, the names, the ages, the widow's petition. Fair accounts. It was not much. It was not nothing. I had come to believe those were the only two categories that mattered.

I stayed until full dark. On the walk back I passed the corner where the burned shop had stood—there is a new brick façade there now, a chandler's, the scars only visible if you know to look at the color of the mortar—and I touched the wall the way you touch a scar, briefly, without ceremony, and went home.

* * *

It was Halvard who brought the letter up, near the end of July, running up the stairs two at a time with the particular thunder of a boy carrying news.

"Post boat came early! There's one for you—from abroad!" He presented it like a fish he'd caught. "It smells of the sea."

Everything smells of the sea here. But he was right; this smelled of farther sea than ours.

I turned it over. Foreign postmarks, layered like scales. No return address. And the hand on the front—

The world did its old trick, the floor tilting, the lamplight swimming. But I was steadier now. Nineteen years of disguise had taught me to recognize a hand the way a mother recognizes breathing through a wall, and this hand—larger than it had been, surer, the descenders cut short as if the writer had learned to save space—was the hand I had known since before I could read it. The hand from the letters Voss had carried through the rain. The hand declared dead by the underwriters of this port three years ago and alive on every page since in my memory.

*Miss Oscar Eriksen*, it said. *Printer.* Miss. He had written *Miss*. My brother had addressed me as the world now addressed me, and my eyes stung over four letters of ink.

Halvard was watching my face with the frank appetite of the young. "Bad news?"

"No," I managed. "No. The opposite. Possibly the opposite."

I broke the seal with my thumb—I had no letter knife yet; the shop was still becoming itself—and unfolded pages written close and fast, and the first line rose up to meet me the way a quay rises to meet a hull coming home:

*Sister—if I may begin with the true word and spend the rest of the letter earning it—*

Anders was alive. Anders was writing. And Anders, by the weight of the packet in my hands—four pages, five, sealed with care, carried across a border and a blockade and half the Baltic to reach a sailmaker's loft where a boy in a nightshirt had once learned to hold a composing stick—had a very great deal to earn it with.

I sat down at my own workbench, among my own type, under my own name, and began to read.
