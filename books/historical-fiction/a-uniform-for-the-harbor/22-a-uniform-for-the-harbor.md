## 22. A Uniform for the Harbor

The new shop smelled of pine shavings and cold glue, and Oscar Eriksen loved it the way one loves a room that has never heard an argument.

It stood on Sillmakers' Lane, two streets back from the water, above a cooper's yard where the hammers started at seven and stopped at six and never once, in all the months she had been there, tried to burn anything down. The press had come through the fire by a day and a signature — her signature, wearing her father's hand like a borrowed coat — and it had crossed the harbor on a lighter at dawn while smoke stood over the old shop in a column so straight it might have been drawn with a rule. She had watched it go from the quay, Anders already aboard the *Meridian* with his berth and his false papers, Voss beside her saying nothing, because there was nothing left to say that the tide was not already saying better.

That had been spring. Now it was October again, three years on, and the light came into the workroom low and gold through windows that had never been blacked out, and four apprentices sat at her benches cutting steel.

Three of them were boys from the trade, sons of compositors and bookbinders who had heard that the Eriksen press paid honest wages and taught honest letters. The fourth was Marta Lindqvist, fourteen years old, daughter of the harbormaster, and she held a graver the way her father held a manifest — as though the whole world were accountable to her for its accuracy.

"You're bearing too hard on the curve," Oscar said, stopping behind her chair. "A counterform isn't dug out. It's persuaded."

"It's resisting," Marta said.

"Everything worth cutting resists. That's how you know it's worth cutting."

Marta adjusted her grip. Under the lamp her jaw was set in a way that made Oscar's chest ache with recognition — she had worn that exact jaw herself, thirty years ago or five, depending on how you counted time, bent over her father's bench pretending to be somebody else's child.

She went back to her own station. On it lay a letter.

It had come that morning on the coastal packet, postmarked Copenhagen, addressed in handwriting she would have known blindfolded, upside down, dissolved in drink or drowned in salt water: *Anders Eriksen*, the ascenders tall and impatient, the descenders trailing off like a man always half-turned toward the next sentence. Three years of letters had crossed between them now. This one was thicker than usual, and the seal had been broken and re-stuck carelessly, which meant he had read it himself before sending it and found something in it he wanted her to see.

She could have read it alone. She almost did. Her thumb was under the flap when the bell over the door rang and Marta looked up, and then Henrik and the two brothers looked up, and the whole room had that end-of-day looseness that makes people brave.

"Is that from your brother?" Henrik asked. "The one who was dead?"

"The one who was dead," Oscar confirmed. "He objects strongly to the past tense."

Henrik grinned. The boys had grown up on the story the way the city had grown up on it — the printer's son who came back from the sea, the fire, the trial where old Lindqvist had stood in the witness box with his cap in his hands and testified, in a voice pitched for the back benches of the courtroom, that he had known for years what the Eriksens printed and had seen no harm in it that the customs house didn't do worse daily. The acquittal had taken eleven minutes. The story had taken considerably longer, and had improved with every telling until even Oscar sometimes doubted her own version, in which nobody had behaved heroically and everybody had bargained with something rotten.

"Read it," said Marta. It was not a request. It was a summons, issued in exactly the tone her father used when he wanted a ship searched.

Oscar weighed the letter in her hand. Outside, a gull argued with the cooper's hammers. Somewhere down the lane a cart load of apples rattled past, and the smell of them came in through the open window, sweet and bruised and autumnal, and she thought: *I am going to be forty-one this winter, and I am standing in my own shop under my own name, and I am about to read my brother's letter aloud to children.*

"I'll read it," she said, "but you'll all keep working. Hands busy, ears open. That's how type gets learned anyway — half through the fingers, half through whatever nonsense is going on in the room."

They bent to their gravers. She broke the seal properly this time and unfolded the pages.

*Dear Oscar,*

*I have been trying for a week to write you something about what happened here last month, and every draft comes out either a sermon or a boast, both of which you'd strike with a red pencil, so let me try plain speech, which is the one style they never taught us and the only one you ever trusted.*

*You will have read the newspapers, which lie by emphasis rather than invention, so here is the truth of it. The demonstration on the ninth was peaceful until it wasn't. We marched for the franchise — the vote, Oscar, men like me standing in line like customers to be counted — and for six hours it was banners and bad poetry, and I thought, this is what it was supposed to feel like, this is what Father died believing in, this ordinary enormous thing. Then the cavalry came, and the poetry stopped being useful, and a boy beside me — nineteen, a typesetter's apprentice himself, can you imagine the odds against that — took a sabre across the shoulder because he wouldn't drop the banner. He'll live. He asked me, while they stitched him, whether I thought the banner was worth the arm, and I told him the truth, which is that I don't know yet, and that anyone who claims to know yet is selling something.*

*Here is what I do know. I know that when I lay on the cobblestones with the crowd running over me, the name I shouted — not called, shouted, the way you shout a thing you're afraid to lose — was yours. Not the cause's name. Yours. I have thought about that a great deal since. A man spends three years learning that history is made by crowds and committees and great winds blowing, and then he finds himself flat on the ground discovering that history, for him, has a face and a name and a pair of inkstained hands, and that those hands once set a line of type so beautiful he wept over it in a cellar at sixteen without knowing why.*

*I kept the banner. It's folded in my trunk. It's ugly — red bunting, someone's mistress sewed it — but it took a sabre and didn't tear, and I find I've become sentimental about things that hold together.*

Now give me the family news, because I have reread your last three letters so often the creases are failing, and I refuse to write another one until you tell me about the girl. Not Marta Lindqvist — I mean the other one, the one who came to you in June asking to learn the trade, whose father the cooper says beat her for wanting it. You wrote one line about her and then changed the subject to paper prices, which is what you do when something matters too much. Tell me her name. Tell me if her hands are healing. Tell me whether you sat her down the first evening and showed her, as you showed me, that a lowercase e is just an o that has decided to speak.*

Oscar lowered the page.

The room had gone quiet in the way rooms do when everyone is working very loudly at not listening. Henrik's graver had stopped. One of the brothers was staring at the ceiling with heroic concentration. Marta was looking directly at her, unembarrassed, the way only the young and the recently rescued can look at a person.

"There's a girl," Oscar said. "Her name is Ingeborg. She came in June with a split lip and a bag with everything she owned in it, and she asked if I took girls." She turned the letter over in her hands. "I told her the truth. I said I hadn't always taken girls. I said there had been a period of some decades during which this shop had exactly one girl in it, and she was disguised as a boy, and the disguise worked so well that even she forgot it was a disguise."

"And now?" Marta asked.

"Now she sleeps in the loft and cuts a mean lowercase g and writes home to her mother, openly, under her own name, and her mother has started writing back." Oscar picked up the letter again. "Which brings us to the rest of it. Because my brother, having nearly lost an afternoon to a cavalry charge, has decided to get philosophical, and philosophy runs in our family like a fever."

She read:

*You once asked me, in the letter after the trial, whether I regretted any of it — the pamphlets, the berth, letting you stay behind to answer for what we printed. You said I shouldn't trouble myself to reassure you, that you wanted the true accounting, and I have been drafting that accounting for three years, which should tell you something about how long a true accounting takes.*

*Here is the draft I believe. I do not regret the berth. I regret that it was singular — that the world handed us one door and two people and called that a choice, as if arithmetic were ever justice. I regret that Father chose the fire instead of the truth, and I understand him more each year, which frightens me. I regret that you spent twenty years answering to a name that wasn't yours so that a man who couldn't see you could inherit through you. But I do not regret that you stayed, Oscar. I have thought about this from every angle on every ship and dockside on two coasts, and I keep arriving at the same place: the woman who walked into that courtroom and gave her name — her real name, the whole of it, spoken clearly enough that the clerk had to ask her twice — that woman is the reason I can stand in front of crowds now and say what I am without my voice shaking. Somebody had to show me it could be done. You did it at the worst possible moment, at cost of everything, and you did it not for the revolution, which you distrusted rightly, but because the lie had finally gotten smaller than the truth. That is the bravest arithmetic I know.*

*I used to think history happened in capitals, in parliaments, on barricades — big rooms, loud rooms. Then I came home to a harbor town and understood that history had been happening to us the entire time, quietly, at a workbench, in a lie told by a grieving man and maintained by a loyal child, and that the barricade was only history raising its voice. It was always here. It was always us. The uniform the harbor wears — the one you titled your letters with, the gray wool of ordinary days — turns out to be the very cloth the century is cut from.*

Hmm. He's right about the title, Oscar thought. She had written to him for years signing her news *from the harbor in its uniform* — gray weather, gray stone, gray ships — meaning nothing by it except that the town kept itself buttoned up. And here was Anders, three hundred miles south, telling her she had been writing the century's epitaph all along without knowing it, the way she had once set beautiful type for causes she distrusted, the way she had once been the truest son her father ever had while being no son at all.

She cleared her throat and read the last of it.

*I'll be north at midwinter if the ice permits. Don't put me up — I know your loft is full of girls and gravers — but let me work a week at the bench beside you, the way we never got to, and let me watch you teach. They say in Copenhagen that you're training women openly now, that the Eriksen shop turns out cutters with girls' names on their indenture papers, and when I tell people I say: yes, and you should have seen her do it when it was illegal, when it was impossible, when the only tool she had was a forged name and a steady hand. Set me a case, sister. Give me a composing stick and a corner of your bench. I want to spend one week being nobody's messenger, nobody's martyr, nobody's ghost — just a man setting type in his sister's shop, in a harbor that knows our names and uses them correctly.*

*Your brother,*
*Anders*

*P.S. — Tell Marta Lindqvist her father keeps a copy of my first pamphlet in his desk drawer. He showed it to me on the quay the morning I sailed. He said, "Evidence," and winked, and I have decided to believe that harbormasters are permitted exactly one wink per lifetime and he spent his well.*

Silence, then the sound of the cooper's hammers filling it up, and Henrik laughing first, the way boys laugh when a story confirms the world is stranger than advertised, and then the brothers, and then Marta — not giggling but satisfied, the way she was satisfied by a clean counterform, a thing coming out right.

"Evidence," she repeated. "My father winked?"

"Once," Oscar said. "Per lifetime. Spend yours carefully."

Marta went back to her graver, and the room resumed its industry, and Oscar stood a moment with the letter in her hands, feeling its weight redistribute — the weight of small letters, she thought, remembering a lamplit bench and a drinking father and a girl who believed revolutions happened elsewhere, in capitals, to other people.

But they hadn't. They had happened here, in this harbor, to her — had happened in the slow compound interest of a lie told out of love, a press sold by forgery, a name surrendered and reclaimed; had happened in a cellar full of pamphlets and a courtroom with a cap in its hands and a berth given away on a dawn tide. History had never once knocked on her door wearing a red sash. It had come in rain-soaked and ordinary, holding letters in her dead brother's hand, and it had waited, patient as type in a case, for her to compose it.

She hung her apron on its nail. Down the lane the lamps were being lit one by one, the harbor putting on its uniform for the evening, gray turning to gold at the edges.

"Ingeborg," she said to the loft stairs, "come down and bring the proof of the almanac pages. And the rest of you — benches cleared, stones wiped. Tomorrow we start the new alphabet."

Marta looked up. "What new alphabet?"

Oscar smiled, and reached for the lamp to trim its wick, and the flame stood up straight as a mast.

"The one," she said, "with all our names in it."
