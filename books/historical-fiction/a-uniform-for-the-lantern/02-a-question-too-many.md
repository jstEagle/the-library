## 2. A Question Too Many

The master clock and I had reached an understanding by the third day: I would respect its age, it would forgive my thumbs. That is the whole of clockmaking, if you boil it down — negotiation. You do not conquer a mechanism. You court it.

I was elbow-deep in the great case, my cheek against the cold pine of the frame, when the door at the top of the tower stair opened without a knock. Doors that open without knocks belong to men who have never once been made to wait, and I have distrusted them ever since.

"Apprentice Hartwell?" said a voice like a filing cabinet being closed politely.

I backed out of the clock on my knees, which is not the dignified way to meet an inspector of anything, and looked up into the face of Mr. Corven. He was a narrow man, gray as ledger paper, with a mustache trimmed to the width of a rule and eyes that did not so much look at you as *enter* you. He carried a leather satchel the way other men carry Bibles — close to the chest, both hands, as though the nation's secrets might spill.

"Census verification," he said. "Vellamouth district, fourth enumeration. You are employed here?"

"Apprenticed," I said. "To the observatory's timekeeper, through Master Aldous Fenwick of the Guild. I'm repairing the master clock."

"So you've said." He had not heard me say it. I understood this later to be his method: he stated what he already knew and waited to see whether your version matched. It was like watching a man lay out cards face-down and study your eyes while he turned each one.

"The clock is failing?" he asked, glancing up at the great dial as if the hour itself were a suspect.

"The escapement's worn. The chime train drags. By winter it would have been losing four minutes a day, and four minutes a day," I said, "is how cities end up missing their tides."

Something flickered across his face — not warmth, but the memory of warmth, filed away somewhere deep. "Precision," he said, "is a form of honesty."

"I'd have put it the other way round," I said, "but yes."

He set his satchel on the workbench without asking, which told me everything about him, and began laying out papers in a grid so exact it could have been machined. Census schedules. Household returns. A map of Vellamouth marked in colored pins, and I will confess now what I would not confess then: my stomach dropped when I saw the pins, because no one pins a map for pleasure.

* * *

*Entry, 14 October 1911. Subject: H., T., apprentice, b. 1893. Employed at Observatory under Fenwick, A. (Guild). Demeanor: cooperative. Hands stained (work, consistent with claim). Answers precise re: mechanisms, slower re: persons. Note: slowness itself may be data.*

*Verification schedule: households adjacent to harbor, prior employers of subject H.*

*Observation: subject glanced twice at map before being invited to. Record.*

* * *

"You'll understand," Corven said, "that the fourth enumeration includes retrospective verification. The Emergency Registration Act requires us to confirm not only where persons are, but where they *were*. Histories, Mr. Hartwell. The Act is very interested in histories."

"Clocks too," I said. "Everyone's after the past lately."

He almost smiled. I want to be fair to him here, because fairness is the one thing I refused him at the time and have owed him ever since. He was not a cruel man. He was worse, or better, depending on where you stood: he was a *thorough* one, and he believed — I am certain of this — that every name he entered correctly was a small mercy performed against chaos. Order as kindness. Paper as protection. I have met men who enjoyed cruelty; Corven only enjoyed correctness, which frightens me more, because cruelty can be shamed and correctness cannot.

"Now." He drew a single sheet from the middle of the grid, and I knew — the way you know a gear is cut one tooth short before the clock ever stutters — what name would be on it. "You were previously employed in domestic service. The household of—" he consulted nothing, which meant he had it by heart— "the lighthouse station at Gullspit Point. Keeper Elias Marchetti."

"Yes."

"For how long?"

"Two years. I was fourteen when I went. I kept his clocks and the fog-signal timer and did the books."

"And your duties ended when?"

"When the light was automated. Two years ago, come spring." This much was easy, because it was true, and truth told quickly sounds quick. It was the next part that slowed me, and he felt it slow, I could see him feel it, the way you feel a mainspring bind.

"You were fond of him."

"He taught me the stars," I said, which was true and also a wall, and walls are only useful if people believe they're just architecture. "A keeper has hours to fill."

"Fond enough to correspond?"

"We exchange a card at Christmas. He's not much of a writer."

"No," Corven said softly, and made a small mark. "He is not much of anything on paper, Mr. Hartwell. That is precisely the difficulty."

* * *

Here is what I did not know yet, that October morning, and what the reader must be told plainly: there had been a new statute passed in the summer, three pages long, with a title like a fence — *Emergency Registration of Alien Nationals* — and inside those three pages lived a word that had been waiting all century for its appointment. *Enemy.* Not enemy by act, mind, nor by word, nor by deed. Enemy by ink. A man born under a wrong flag, who had kept a light burning faithfully over English water for thirty years, woke one morning to find himself a category. And categories, unlike men, cannot be questioned; they can only be processed.

I knew none of this at the top of the tower. What I knew was that my hands wanted something to do, so I picked up a brass wheel from the bench and turned it over, and Corven watched my hands the way other men watch faces.

"You visited him," Corven said, "in June. The second Sunday."

Had I? Yes. Yes, I had — a walk out the coast road with bread and a borrowed bicycle, the kind of errand that makes no shadow. But he had said it like a fact pulled from my own pocket, and facts pulled from your own pocket always feel stolen.

"People visit keepers," I said. "There's nothing out there but gulls and the man."

"Precisely," said Corven, and wrote something down.

*Entry, continued. Subject denies significance of June visit. Denial unprompted — question had concerned correspondence, not visits. Note pattern: subject volunteers defenses against charges not yet made. Either guilty conscience or honest habit. To be determined.*

*Ration office query: Marchetti, E., keeper's allowance discontinued May last. Who has since drawn against household stores at Gullspit? Cross-reference grocers within 4 miles of point. Also: lighthouse board records show structure "closed pending automation" since March — yet lamp room reported cleaned by person unknown, August. A closed house does not clean itself.*

*Note to self: the boy lies badly, which is either virtue or inexperience. Both are correctable.*

* * *

"Do you know," Corven said conversationally, closing the June folder, "how many foreign-born persons reside in Vellamouth?"

"No."

"Neither do I. That is my occupation's whole tragedy." He stood and walked to the slit window, where you can see the whole harbor laid out below like a diagram of itself — the fishing fleet, the customs sheds, the gray water going silver where the morning came off it. "Four hundred and six registered. Perhaps two hundred unregistered. And of the registered, some dozens whose paperwork predates the war scare and is therefore considered... provisional. The government wishes to distinguish friend from foe. A reasonable wish. Wars are lost to ambiguity."

"There isn't a war," I said. "There's talk of one."

"There is always talk of one," Corven agreed pleasantly. "And then suddenly there is one, and the men who prepared the lists are heroes, and the men who laughed at the lists are dead or embarrassed. I prefer useful embarrassment to heroic grief. Now — Marchetti."

"What about him?"

"Where is he?"

And there it was. No circling, no ceremony. The clock ticked above us with the enormous patience of machinery that has never had to lie, and I felt myself standing on the edge of a question the way you stand on river ice, hearing it speak before it breaks.

"I couldn't say," I said.

"Could not, or do not know?"

"They're different questions."

"They're the same question with different sentences attached," said Corven. "Answer the first and we remain colleagues. Answer the second and we become something else, and I should dislike that. I dislike paperwork more than you'd credit, Mr. Hartwell. Every page I don't have to file is a small holiday."

I looked at my hands. There was clock oil in the creases, the permanent stain of my trade, and I thought absurdly of Elias's hands, which smelled of brass polish and lamp oil and salt, and of the promise I had made at fourteen with my father's grip still warm on my wrist — a debt of salt water, owed in peacetime, to be paid when peace asked its price. I did not yet know the price. I only knew that the question hanging in the tower room had teeth.

"He'll be at the light," I said. "Where else would a keeper be?"

"The light is dark, the house is chalked with a census number, and the keeper's name appears on a schedule I am not authorized to show you but will describe as *pending*." He said it without triumph, without even emphasis — a man reading a weather report about a storm he regretted. "So you see, 'at the light' is an answer that costs you nothing and buys me nothing, which makes it the most expensive kind of answer there is. I'll take it anyway." He gathered his papers back into their grid, back into the satchel, each sheet squared to its neighbors. "Thank you for your time. The clock — when will she strike true again?"

"A week. Maybe less."

"Good. A city should be able to trust its noon." At the door he paused, one hand on the frame, and did not turn around. "One more thing, and then I'm only a tired clerk again. When you answer slowly, Mr. Hartwell — as you did today, three times — remember that paper is patient. Paper waits years if it must. People are less patient than paper, and far less kind. Whatever it is you're keeping, ask yourself whether it keeps you."

Then the door shut behind him, soft as a page turning, and I stood alone in the tower with the greatest clock in Vellamouth ticking above my head like a heart that belonged to somebody else.

* * *

I did not go back to the escapement. I sat on the workbench stool and listened to the tick, and thought about how a man can be watched. I had imagined, in whatever imagination I had for such things, that surveillance meant boots and lanterns, a hand on the collar. It had never occurred to me that a man could be followed by *ink* — that a quiet clerk could pin your life to a map in colored dots and let the dots do the chasing, so that when the knock finally comes it feels less like violence than like arithmetic finishing itself.

Corven had known about June. About the second Sunday. Somebody had seen a boy with a bicycle on the coast road and thought it worth remembering, and that somebody was not wicked, merely diligent, and diligence is the most terrifying thing in the world because it never sleeps and never doubts.

Above me the hands crawled toward eleven. Below me, four miles down the coast, a dark lamp room stood over a shuttered house with a number chalked on its door — and somewhere in my chest, rising the way drowned things rise, slow and inevitable and bloated with time, came the knowledge of what I would have to do about it.

I took up my pliers. I went back to the escapement. My hands were steady, which frightened me more than shaking would have — because a craftsman's hands, I understood even then, will carry out any instruction given to them clearly enough, and mine had already begun working out, all on their own, exactly how many minutes a chime can be made to hold its tongue.

It was two days later, with Corven's questions still ringing in my ears like a bell struck slightly flat, that I walked out the coast road to Gullspit Point to see for myself what a closed house looks like — and found the chalked number on the door, and beneath it, half washed away by rain, a mark I recognized with a sickness I still carry:

a tide-table symbol, drawn in Elias's hand, pointing under the hill.
