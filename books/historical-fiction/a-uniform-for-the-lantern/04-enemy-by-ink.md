## 4. Enemy by Ink

The notice was pasted to the guild hall door on a Monday, which felt appropriate. Mondays are the day governments choose for their cruelties, I think, because everyone else is still recovering from Sunday and hasn't the energy to argue.

I had gone in to collect my week's wages — eleven shillings and a lecture about lateness, standard terms — and there it was, at eye level, printed in that official typeface that manages to look both urgent and bored:

*By order of the Emergency Powers Act: all subjects of nations now in a state of diplomatic hostility residing within the municipal bounds of Vellamouth are hereby declared enemy nationals. Such persons must present themselves for registration. Failure to register constitutes a felony against the Crown. Deportation vessels depart weekly from the East Quay.*

Enemy nationals. Four syllables of ink, and suddenly a man who had kept a light burning over the harbor mouth for thirty years — who had pulled drowning boys out of black water without asking them their fathers' birthplaces — was an enemy.

I stood there long enough that old Prewitt, the guild porter, nudged me with his broom handle. "You'll catch flies, Hartwell."

"Who's it for?" I asked, though I knew. "The registrations. Who's actually been taken?"

Prewitt glanced up the street, then down it, in the manner of men who have learned that walls in Vellamouth have recently grown ears. "Baker on Cordwain Lane. The Zeller brothers at the chandlery. Old Frau Aldermann." He shrugged. "And the lighthouse fellow. Marchetti."

The word went through me like a punch through wet paper — no resistance at all, just sudden cold everywhere at once.

"They took him?"

"Nobody took him anywhere. He didn't come register. That's the thing." Prewitt leaned on his broom, lowering his voice to something just above the wind off the harbor. "Man walks past the registry office every day of his life to buy his bread. Doesn't go in. Now they say he's fled, and fleeing's as good as confessing, under the new statutes." He tapped the notice with the broom's end, gently, almost apologetically. "Paper's a funny kind of rope, lad. Ties a man up without ever touching him."

I walked home the long way, along the harbor wall, telling myself a great many sensible things. That Elias Marchetti was a grown man who had survived forty years of winter gales on an iron staircase above the sea. That he would know how to look after himself. That one apprentice clockmaker with eleven shillings in his pocket and grease permanently worked into the knuckles was precisely no use to anyone in trouble.

Sensible things have never once stopped me from doing anything. It is a flaw in my construction, like a mainspring cut a hair too strong.

* * *

The tide-tunnel beneath the observatory is not a secret, exactly, which is why nobody remembers it. It was cut two hundred years ago so that stones for the observatory foundations could be barged in from the quarries without hauling them up the cliff road. When the work finished, they bricked the seaward end halfway, leaving a gap wide enough for water — and, historically speaking, for smugglers, lovers, and boys daring each other to prove their courage. The observatory side ends in a maintenance stair behind the boiler room, which any clockmaker sent to service the tower clocks would know, because half our trade runs through the building's bones like marrow.

I told myself I was only checking. Checking is a respectable activity. Checking requires no decisions.

I brought a lantern, a stub of candle, and — because I am my father's son and cannot help it — a wrench, in case something down there wanted fixing. The tide was going out, which left the tunnel floor glistening with weed and old rope and things I chose not to examine by lantern-light. The smell was salt and coal smoke and rot, the smell every child in Vellamouth carries in their lungs whether they want to or not.

Forty yards in, where the brickwork closes to a crawl and the sea end lets in a gray blade of evening light, I heard breathing.

Not the breathing of the sea. Sea-breathing is even, patient, indifferent. This was shallow and guarded, the breathing of a man listening back.

"Elias," I said.

The silence that followed had texture. Then, from the dark beyond the light's reach: "Theo Hartwell." His voice was rougher than memory kept it, sanded down by disuse. "They send a boy now. With a lamp. Very modern."

"I'm twenty-three."

"You were twelve yesterday." He came forward into the edge of the light, and my chest did something painful, because twelve years will do to a man what twelve years do. Elias Marchetti had been broad as a bollard when he hauled me out of the harbor; now the coat hung on him like laundry left out in rain. His beard had gone the color of sea foam. But the eyes were the same — pale gray, unblinking, the eyes of a man accustomed to staring down weather.

"Half the city says you've fled," I said.

"I have moved lodgings. There's a difference. Fleeing implies a destination."

"You didn't register."

"No." He said it flatly, the way he'd once reported the barometer falling. A fact about pressure.

"Why not? You'd have been left alone. Registered men get work permits. Rations. They stay."

Something crossed his face then — not anger, but its exhausted cousin. He sat down slowly on a bollard of old foundation stone, and I saw his hands, and I stopped wanting to argue, because the hands told the whole story. Knuckles cracked and dirty. Nails broken. The hands of a lighthouse keeper who had been living in a drain for weeks, eating God knew what.

"Theo," he said. "Do you know what happens in the registry office? A man gives his name, and a clerk writes it down, and the clerk asks his nation, and the man says 'I am a keeper of the Vellamouth light,' and the clerk writes 'hostile national, uncooperative.'" He looked at me. "The paper decides what you are before your mouth finishes talking. If I walk in there, I become what they've already written. If I don't—" he spread those ruined hands, "—then I'm still just a man sitting in a tunnel, and the law has to come find me itself. I owe the law that much effort."

"That's not courage, Elias, that's arithmetic with the wrong numbers. They've got ships sailing every week. They've got inspectors. They've got—"

"Ink," he said. "They've got ink. And ink drowns easier than I do."

I crouched down across from him. My trousers soaked through immediately; I barely noticed. "My father made me swear," I said, and my voice came out lower than I intended. "The night he died. He held my hand and made me swear that what you did for me, I'd pay back. Debt of honor. His words. Peacetime, he said, when it costs something. Well." I gestured at the tunnel, the notice in my head, the whole gray machinery of the city above us. "It's costing something now."

Elias was quiet a long moment. Somewhere behind the half-wall of bricks, the sea worked patiently at the mortar, as it had for two centuries, keeping better time than any of us.

"Your father," he said finally, "was the only man who ever thanked me properly for that night. Everyone else wanted to make it a story. Hero pulls boy from harbor. Stories let people stop thinking about it. Your father understood it was a debt, and debts keep a thing alive." He rubbed his face with one hand. "But listen to me, boy, because I'll only say this once. You cannot pay this debt. Whatever you're building in your head — some scheme with ropes and boats and dramatic timing — put it down. I'm an old man. I'm tired. And if they take me, they take me to a camp, maybe, or a ship, and I survive it the way I've survived everything, by standing still and letting the weather pass. Running turns a mistake into a crime. Running confirms guilt. I won't hand them that."

"And if the weather doesn't pass?" I asked. "If it just gets worse? You've read the notices same as me. Every month another statute. First they count you, then they list you, then they move you. That's the order of operations, Elias. I repair clocks. I know how mechanisms run. This one only runs one direction."

He laughed — a short, cracked sound, genuine despite everything. "God help me, the boy argues like a gear train. Everything meshes, everything drives, nothing slips." The laugh died. He stared at his hands. "You think I haven't counted the strokes myself? Out here, nights, listening to your master clock up there through the rock? Eleven, twelve, one o'clock. Counting hours is all a man can do in a hole. Of course I've thought about running." His voice dropped. "I ran a light for thirty years, Theo. Thirty years, I was the fixed point other people steered by. You don't know what it does to a man, being the fixed point. You start believing you deserve it. Sitting still isn't pride, boy. It's the last shape of myself I recognize."

I should tell you, since we've come this far together, what I thought in that moment. I thought: he's right and he's wrong and both halves will kill him. I thought: I am an apprentice with eleven shillings, a wrench, and a talent for small precise movements, and none of that is enough. And underneath all of it, steady as a escapement, I thought: *he pulled me out of the water. He held my head above the harbor while my lungs decided whether to be a drowned boy's or a live one's. There is no version of me, in any year, that walks away from this tunnel.*

"All right," I said. "You won't run. Noted. Entered into the record." I stood, joints protesting the cold. "But I'm coming back tomorrow. And the day after. Because somebody has to bring food to a man who isn't hiding, since he's merely *moved lodgings*, and lodgings apparently don't include supper."

"That's unnecessary."

"It's a debt of honor. Unnecessary is rather the point of them."

He shook his head, but I caught it — the smallest movement at the corner of his mouth, the ghost of the man who used to slip me boiled sweets on the pier and pretend he hadn't. "Candles," he said, as I turned to go. "If you're coming back regardless. Candles, and bread that isn't green, and — " he hesitated, and the hesitation cost him more than anything else he'd said — "and news. What the light's doing. Whether they've got anyone tending it, or if she's dark."

"She's dark," I said. "Been dark three weeks. Harbor board says budget."

He closed his eyes. For a moment he looked less like a fugitive than like a widower, and I understood that for Elias Marchetti, the two amounts of grief were exactly equal.

The boy left, and his footsteps faded up the maintenance stair, and the tunnel settled back into its old sounds: water working the bricks, the far-off heartbeat of the town, and overhead — faint, arriving through stone like a thought through sleep — the master clock striking the quarter hour.

I had been counting that clock for nineteen days. Nineteen days since I climbed down through the gap in the seaward bricks with a sack of bread and a lantern and the last of my pride, and let the tide-tunnel close around me like a fist deciding, slowly, whether to hold or crush.

Nineteen days is not so long. I once kept a light through a November storm for six days without proper sleep. But a storm has an ending you can hear coming. Statutes don't.

Here is what I have learned about becoming an enemy: it requires no effort on your part. That's the elegant part of it. I woke one morning still loving this harbor, still knowing every rock under the water from here to the point, still able to name the fishing boats by the sound of their engines — and by evening I was a category. *Hostile national.* As though hostility were a thing I carried, like a knife, instead of a thing they issued, like a uniform. I never declared war on Vellamouth. Vellamouth declared war on me while I was trimming the wick.

And yet — and here is the shameful arithmetic I do in the dark — part of me understands the machine. That is the worst of it. I understand Corven's ledgers and the neat columns and the need to know who is in the harbor and why, because I spent thirty years needing to know what was on the water and why. Order is a kind of light too. The difference is that a lighthouse exists to save whatever comes near it, and a ledger exists to sort, and sorting always ends with a pile marked *away*. I reported a smuggling run once, years ago, because the law of the sea seemed clearer then than the law of men, and I have been paying for that clarity ever since, from both directions, men in offices and men in boats alike holding it against me. Doing the right thing once, I've come to believe, buys you a lifetime subscription to finding out what everyone really thinks of right things.

So I sat still. Sitting still was my argument, my dignity, my last possession. If they came for me, they would have to bend down into the dark and take me, and somewhere in the taking there might remain one inch of ground on which I was still Elias Marchetti, keeper, and not Enemy, by ink.

Then the boy came back. Theo Hartwell, soaked to the knee, arguing gear trains at me, promising bread and candles and news of my own light with the fierce embarrassed tenderness of a boy trying not to cry in front of an old man.

And I recognized the danger of him instantly, because I recognized it twelve years ago in the water: this is a person who will not calculate the cost before acting. Those people drown young, usually. Unless someone pulls them out.

I sat in the dark after he left and did what I do. I counted the clock. Quarter past. Half past. And I admitted, to the brickwork, to the tide, to nobody: nineteen days of stillness, and the first thing in all that time that felt like rescue was a boy refusing to leave me alone.

That is either hope or the beginning of it, and both are more dangerous than hunger.

Because upstairs, in the town that had renamed me, the deportation ship was loading at East Quay, and somewhere a clerk was sharpening a pencil, and the tide — patient, indifferent, exact — was already turning toward morning.

* * *

I came back the next day as promised, and the day after, and on the third day I brought candles and bread and a folded newspaper, and found the tunnel empty except for the bollard, the blanket, and — arranged neatly on the stone, deliberate as a signature — Elias's brass lantern, still burning.

Beside it, a scrap of paper, written in a keeper's careful hand:

*They came to the harbor end at low water. Two men, measuring the gap. Go to Fenwick. Tell him the favor he owes me has a name, and the name is the night of the eleventh of March.*

I stood in the dripping dark with the little flame trembling between us — the man and me — and understood that the weather Elias had been standing so still against had finally started to move.
