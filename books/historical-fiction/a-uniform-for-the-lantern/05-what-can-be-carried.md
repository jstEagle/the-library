## 5. What Can Be Carried

There is a particular arithmetic a man learns when he has spent his life inside small machines: everything weighs something, and everything that weighs something must be paid for. I had known it first in gears — brass costs more than steel, steel more than iron, and every ounce you add to an escapement is an ounce the mainspring must lift four thousand times an hour. Later I learned it in bread. Now, crouched in the dark of the tide-tunnel with Elias Marchetti's whole worldly estate spread out on my coat like a pawnbroker's shame, I learned it a third time.

"This is it?" I asked, and immediately hated myself for it.

Elias didn't take offense. He had never once in twelve years taken offense at anything I said, which I had come to understand was not patience but a kind of professional discipline — the habit of a man who spends his nights alone with a lamp and a sea and cannot afford to quarrel with the few voices he gets.

"It's it," he said. He said it the way he'd have read it off a log: *wind north-northeast, visibility poor, cargo one life.*

I looked at what he had. A ship's lantern, the glass smoked on one side where it had faced the wick for twenty years. A logbook bound in oilcloth gone soft as chamois, its corners worn round as river stones. And a photograph in a pasteboard frame, no bigger than my palm, of a woman I did not know standing in front of a house I did not know, squinting into a sun that had stopped shining before the war fever started.

"That's Lucia," he said, seeing me look. "My sister. Naples. She thinks I'm still keeping the light."

"Are you going to tell her?"

"I'm going to *be* somewhere," he said, "and she is going to assume the rest." He turned the photograph face-down on the coat with great care, the way you close a door on a room you mean to come back to.

I want to be honest here, because honesty is the only tool I've ever owned outright: my first thought was not *how do we save this man*. My first thought was *how do we move this man*, and the difference between those two questions is the difference between grief and engineering. Grief was a luxury. Engineering was Tuesday.

"Show me the coat," I said.

* * *

You would be astonished — or perhaps you wouldn't; you may be reading this after everything, in which case you already know — how much of a man's survival can be reduced to inventory. Fenwick used to say a clockmaker's whole art is deciding what to leave out. The same, it turns out, is true of flight.

Elias's coat was a lighthouse keeper's coat, heavy wool, double-breasted, built to shed spray and hold warmth against a wind that never stops asking questions. It had pockets the size of hymnals. We laid things out and argued about them the way other men argue about God.

The lantern went back in first. I said it was dead weight. He said it wasn't weight, it was *proof* — proof he'd been a keeper and not a smuggler, if it ever came to a question. I pointed out that if it came to a question, a lantern would be evidence, not alibi. He put it in anyway. There are objects a man will not surrender even to save himself, and I have since learned that these objects are the actual cargo, and everything else is ballast.

The logbook we fought over properly. Twenty years of weather, written in a hand so steady it looked printed. *Fog thick. Light lit 6:41. Fishing boat *Gannet* passed without signaling — logged.* Pages and pages of *logged*. Including, though neither of us said it aloud yet, the pages from nine years ago that had made certain people in Vellamouth very unhappy. That book was both his character and his indictment, and there was no pocket big enough for either.

"They can hang me with it or clear me with it," Elias said, weighing it in his hand.

"They're not going to do either," I said. "They're going to ship you across the water and lose the paperwork. That's the new way. It's cheaper than hanging."

He looked at me then — really looked, the way he had looked at me from the water twelve years ago, assessing whether I'd sink or swim — and he put the logbook in the left breast pocket, over the heart, where a book can stop a knife or start one.

The photograph rode in his waistcoat, against his skin. Some things don't get discussed.

What remained was mine to solve: tools, papers, hours.

Tools first, because tools were the language I spoke. A clockmaker on the run needs surprisingly little — a pair of pliers fine enough to hold a hairspring, a screwdriver ground down to fit slots nobody else's hand has ever touched, a file, a loupe. I could carry my whole profession in a tobacco tin, and I have often thought since that this is the secret dignity of the trade. A barrister needs his library. A surgeon needs his knives and other men's consent. A clockmaker needs a tin and ten fingers and a problem.

"You'll need your hands free," Elias said. "Whatever we do. Hands free and eyes up."

"I know."

"Do you? Because you've got the look of a boy planning a clever thing. Clever things eat clever boys."

"I'm nineteen."

"I know," he said, and something crossed his face that might have been memory or arithmetic. "Your father was thirty-one when the harbor took him. I counted his years on the pier while they carried you past. Thirty-one. You've got twelve years on him and none of his sense."

That was the closest either of us came, that night, to saying what we were actually doing. We talked instead about pockets.

Papers were Maren Fenwick's territory — she'd been re-inking a shipping clerk's forged passes for her father's crews for years, half as a joke, the way daughters learn their fathers' sins — but I'll come to Maren later, because she deserves her own chapter and possibly several apologies. What mattered that night was that papers weigh nothing and cost everything, and we had almost none.

Hours were the worst. Hours you cannot carry at all. You can only spend them, and the exchange rate is fixed by other people. The deportation ships sailed Thursdays on the morning tide. Today was Saturday. That gave us five days, minus whatever Corven needed to connect a chalked census number on a shuttered lighthouse to a missing keeper to an observatory apprentice who had been seen, by witnesses I was only beginning to count, coming and going from the tide-tunnel door with a satchel.

Five days. I did the sums the way I did wheel counts, over and over, hoping a different total would fall out. It never does.

"Elias," I said, finally, because somebody had to say it, "we have to run. Tonight, tomorrow — soon. Running isn't guilt. Staying is just a slower kind of it."

He was quiet a long moment. Out beyond the tunnel mouth the tide was turning; you could hear the change the way you hear a clock tick over the hour, a subtle rearrangement of the same sound.

"When I took this post," he said at last, "the Admiralty man told me: a light doesn't decide which ships deserve saving. It burns, and it burns, and it burns, and it lets God and the pilot sort them out. Twenty-two years, Theo. If I run, then everyone who ever said a foreigner's loyalty is a rented thing — they win the argument. Not the case. The *argument*."

"The argument doesn't matter if you're on a ship to nowhere."

"The argument is all that's left of a man when they've taken everything else," he said. Then he picked up the lantern and held it, not lighting it, just holding it, thumb moving over the smoked glass like a man reading Braille. "But I promised your father I'd see you grown. So. Show me your clever plan, boy, and if it's clever enough, I'll let it insult me."

I didn't have a plan yet. I had a tin of tools and a talent for mechanisms and one fact I hadn't told him: that three days earlier, alone in the observatory dome with the master clock open to its bones, I had discovered that the great chime — the voice of Vellamouth, the bell that told six hundred thousand pounds of shipping and forty thousand souls what hour it was — could be made to lie. Not broken. *Stretched.* Held. Made to ring longer than the truth, if a man knew which lever to cut and had the stomach to cut it.

I kept that in my chest where I keep all my worst and best ideas, and I said instead:

"Let me measure you."

"What?"

"For the coat. Sit still." I took out my rule — a clockmaker is never without his rule — and measured him like stock. Shoulders. Sleeve. Inseam. He laughed at me, a real laugh, rusty at the hinges, and the sound went echoing down the tunnel toward the sea like a coin dropped down a well.

"What in God's name are you doing?"

"Determining what fits," I said. "It's the whole trade, Elias. Everything else is decoration."

* * *

Inspector Corven did not sleep badly. I want that understood, because I spent that winter hating him and it took me years to admit the truth, which is that Corven slept the way a well-regulated escapement sleeps — evenly, in exact increments, releasing nothing it couldn't account for in the morning. He was not a cruel man. He was something more efficient than cruelty: a man who believed the world was a ledger and that every sorrow in it was an entry someone had failed to balance.

On Sunday evening, by lamplight in the census office above the customs house, Corven balanced.

The file was thin. Thin files interested him; thick files were merely loud. This one concerned one E. Marchetti, enemy national, former keeper, Light Station Four, scheduled Thursday tide. Against it he had begun — out of habit rather than suspicion, he would have told you, and he would have been lying, though only in the way a clock lies when it runs two minutes fast: technically, and with good intentions — a small cross-reference.

Item: the master clock at the Observatory, repaired the eleventh by one T. Hartwell, apprentice, age nineteen, of Hartwell & Sons (deceased), Clockmakers, Quay Lane.

Item: Hartwell, T., apprenticed under Fenwick, Master, Guildhall. Prior household service, ages seven to fourteen, domestic and general, the household of — and here Corven's pen paused, because the ink had faded and he had to tilt the page to the lamp — Marchetti, E., Keeper.

A boy who winds clocks for a living had wound the clocks, and swept the floors, and presumably eaten the bread, of a man now catalogued under the emergency statutes as a subtraction from the population.

Corven did not leap at this. Leaping was for constables. He wrote the connection into his day-book in his small ruled hand, and beneath it he wrote a second line, which I did not learn until much later and which I reproduce here from memory because I have never managed to forget it:

*Repaired clock keeps perfect time. Question is whether the repairer does.*

Then he sat back and considered the window, where rain was beginning to write its own illegible entries on the glass, and he thought — not unkindly, that is the horror of it, not unkindly at all — that a debt between a boy and a rescuer was a debt the state had never figured out how to tax, and therefore never figured out how to forgive.

He drew a line beneath the entry. Closed the book. And in the morning he sent a clerk to Quay Lane to ask, politely, for the observatory repair ledger, and to ask, still more politely, whether young Mr. Hartwell had been seen lately, and where, and with whom.

* * *

The clerk reached the guildhall at half past nine. I learned of it at half past ten, from Maren, who learned of it from the guild's porter, who learned of it because porters learn everything — it is the one law older than gravity.

By noon I was back in the tunnel with my heart going like a cheap alarm clock, telling Elias that our five days had become, by my reckoning, two, and watching him take the logbook out of his breast pocket and put it back in again, twice, like a man testing whether his own resolve had any spring left in it.

"Clever things," he said quietly, "eat clever boys."

"Yes," I said. "So we'd better be quick."

And below us, faint through the stone, the tide began to turn.
