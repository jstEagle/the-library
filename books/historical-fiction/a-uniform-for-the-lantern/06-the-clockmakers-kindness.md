## 6. The Clockmakers' Kindness

The Guildhall of the Worshipful Company of Clockmakers of Vellamouth stood on Gilders' Row like a man who had once been handsome and now lived entirely off the memory of it. Its façade was honeyed limestone gone soft at the corners, its crest — an escapement wheel crossed by two files — worn nearly to abstraction. Inside, it smelled of beeswax, pipe smoke, and the particular melancholy of institutions that measure everything except their own decline.

I had walked past that door four hundred times in my apprenticeship and never once entered as anything but a delivery boy. Now I stood before it with Elias Marchetti's face fixed behind my eyes like an image held under a loupe, and my hands, which could regulate a train of wheels to within a second a week, would not stop shaking.

*You are going to ask an old man to commit a crime*, I told myself. *Do it the way you'd ask him to check your work. Politely. As though there were nothing to it.*

Master Aldous Fenwick received me in his study on the third floor, a room I had heard described all my life and never seen: low-ceilinged, crowded, warm. Every horizontal surface bore clocks. Not fine ones — working ones. Kitchen regulators with scoured cases, ship's chronometers with their gimbal rings polished bright by decades of anxious thumbs, a tavern clock missing its dial so that it was nothing but movement and honesty. They ticked out of unison with one another, dozens of small hearts disagreeing about the hour, and Fenwick sat among them like a man who had made peace long ago with the fact that no two things keep the same time.

He did not look up when I came in. He was working — always working — a loupe screwed into one eye, adjusting something no larger than a thumbnail.

"Hartwell," he said. "You're late."

"I wasn't aware I was expected."

"No one is ever aware they're expected. That's what expectation is for." He set down his tweezers, removed the loupe, and looked at me properly. Fenwick's eyes were the pale grey of pewter left out in weather, and they had a way of taking inventory of a man without appearing to glance at him at all. I felt counted, weighed, and found — provisionally — admissible.

"Sit," he said. "You have the look of a boy who has been carrying something heavier than he should, and I don't mean the toolbox."

I sat. A carriage clock on the shelf beside me struck the quarter-hour with a sound like a polite cough.

"I've come about a repair," I said.

"At this hour? The guild closes its benches at six."

"Not a clock, sir."

Fenwick's hands went still. It was a very small stillness, the kind only another craftsman would notice — the cessation of motion in hands that were never truly idle. He folded them on the desk and regarded me over the tops of his spectacles.

"Well," he said. "Then perhaps you'd better tell me what kind of work you imagine this house does."

And there it was: the door, opened exactly one inch, waiting to see whether I had the courage or the foolishness to push.

I should explain what every apprentice learns in his first year and is then instructed, formally and informally, to forget.

The guild keeps tunnels.

Not secret ones, precisely — nothing in Vellamouth is ever precisely secret; the city simply agrees not to look at certain things, the way a family agrees not to mention Uncle Bertie's drinking. When the observatory was built eighty years ago, its clockwork needed servicing from men who could not be seen hauling tools up the public stair at all hours, disturbing the astronomers. So the builders cut maintenance ways — crawl-galleries and drop-shafts threaded through the rock beneath the hill, linking the observatory's foundations to the tide-cellar of a warehouse on Quay Lane, and thence, by older and less documented passages, to half the cellars of the old town. Smugglers had used parts of the network before the guild existed. The guild, being respectable, used them for nothing worse than toolcases and, once or twice a generation, for men.

That was the part we were instructed to forget. There had been the famine years, when a guild master named Hollis moved eleven printers through the tunnels ahead of a magistrate who wanted them for sedition. There had been the fire of '89, when the network carried three families out of a burning tenement. Each time, afterward, the council passed a resolution affirming the guild's strict political neutrality, and each time the resolution was minuted in the same hand — Fenwick's — in the same careful copperplate, and everyone understood that the copperplate and the tunnel keys were kept in the same drawer.

I had learned this from Maren, actually, years ago, in the way children learn forbidden geography: as a game. *Show me where the ghost road goes,* I'd said, aged twelve, and she'd led me through the coal cellar of the Fenwick house to a hatch she was absolutely not supposed to know about, and we had crawled forty feet by candlelight laughing ourselves sick, until her father found us and went white as bone-dust and didn't speak to either of us for a week. I hadn't thought of it since. Now I thought of little else.

But knowing a door exists and asking a man to open it are different trades entirely.

*

"Tell me plainly," Fenwick said, once I had laid it out — not all of it, not the lighthouse and the chalk number and the debt sworn over my father's deathbed, but enough. A man was hidden in the tide-tunnel. His name was on a deportation list. He had once pulled a drowning boy out of the harbor, and the boy intended to move him through the maintenance ways to a boat, and he needed the ways unlocked, because the locks were guild property and the keys lived in the drawer under the resolutions of neutrality.

Fenwick listened the way he read a mechanism: completely, without interrupting, letting the whole thing run through its train before offering judgment.

When I finished, he was quiet for a long moment. Somewhere below us the great regulator in the council chamber struck seven, and a dozen lesser clocks answered it raggedly, like a choir coming in late.

"You understand what you're asking," he said at last. "Not morally. Practically. Say it back to me."

"The tunnels."

"The tunnels are the least of it. Tunnels are stone; stone doesn't hang anyone. What you're asking is that I put the guild's name next to yours. If Corven traces that man through our ways — and he will trace him, Hartwell, that man cross-references for pleasure, I've watched him do it at inquests — then it isn't one apprentice in the dock. It's the charter. It's sixty workshops, four hundred licenses, every man on Gilders' Row who feeds a family by keeping time for people who would happily see us all broken if we inconvenience them." He tapped the desk. "The guild survives because it belongs to no one. Neutrality isn't cowardice, boy. It's load-bearing."

"And if neutrality means handing Elias Marchetti to a ship?" I said. "Is the wall still standing after that?"

Fenwick looked at me for a long time. Then he rose, went to the window, and stood with his back to me, watching the lamps come on along the Row.

"Do you know what Corven asked me last month?" he said. "Not summoned me — asked. Came here with his hat in his hand, courteous as a Sunday visitor, and requested the guild's register of outside repairs for the past year. Which men serviced which public clocks, in which quarters, with whose sanction." He turned. "I gave it to him. Because refusing would have told him there was something in it worth finding, and because the register contains nothing — names, dates, bench numbers. Nothing. That is what neutrality buys, Hartwell. It buys being harmless. And a harmless man can stand in front of a wolf and be passed over, so long as he stands alone."

"I'm not asking you to stand in front of him."

"No," Fenwick said. "You're asking me to lend you my legs while I stand aside. It's the oldest request in the world and the hardest to refuse, because it lets an old man pretend his conscience costs him nothing."

He came back to the desk and sat, and for a moment he was only tired — an old craftsman with ink-stained fingers and a bad shoulder, in a room full of clocks that would go on ticking after him without gratitude.

"The answer is no," he said.

I had known it would be. Knowing it did not stop it landing like a dropped weight.

"Sir—"

"No. And I'll tell you why, and then you'll go home, and you will not come back, and you will not write anything down, and you will forget my face in connection with whatever it is you intend to do anyway, because I can see by your jaw that you intend to do it regardless." He took off his spectacles and pinched the bridge of his nose. "I am seventy-one years old. I buried my wife in the spring. My daughter thinks I'm fragile, and she is mostly right. And I have spent forty years keeping this company alive by the simple expedient of never, ever betting its existence on my own virtue. Virtue is cheap, Hartwell. Ask any man who's never been tested. It's other people's livelihoods that are dear."

I stood. There was nothing else to do. I thanked him — I remember that; I actually thanked him, wretched, formal thing that I am — and got as far as the door.

"The favor," Fenwick said, behind me.

I stopped.

"You haven't claimed it yet," he said. "Your father's favor. You're saving it. I can tell, because you argued law at me instead of memory, and a man with a debt in hand leads with the memory." He turned around slowly. "Elias Marchetti. Your father. Go on. I'm listening now, and I'd rather hear it sitting down."

So I sat back down, and I told him the rest. The pier. The cold. Twelve years old, showing off on the lower ladder-rungs in March, the weed-slick wood, the harbor taking me the way the harbor takes everything — without malice, without hurry. Elias coming down the lighthouse stair two at a time in his shirtsleeves in winter, because he'd seen a boy's cap floating and hadn't waited even to find a coat. The rope. The heaving. His breath in my ear — *breathe, lad, breathe, the water's done its worst* — and then my father dying that same autumn and making me swear, with hands too weak to grip, that whatever Elias Marchetti ever asked of me, in peacetime, I would do it, and count myself paid in advance for a life I hadn't earned.

"He never asked," I said. "Twelve years. He never once called the debt. And now the state has asked for him instead, and I mean to answer on his behalf."

Fenwick was silent. One of the carriage clocks ticked slightly fast, and I caught myself, absurdly, wanting to open it and correct it just to have somewhere to put my hands.

"There was a night in the winter of '02," Fenwick said quietly. "A hard one — ice in the rigging, the kind of cold that finds flaws. A pilot cutter came in with her rudder gear frozen solid, and aboard her a hold full of families off the northern boats, and the harbor master wouldn't risk a tug against the ice, and the tide was turning. Your father was the guild's duty man that night. He went down into the tide-cellar in that cold — a younger man than I am now couldn't have — and he freed the sluice gears by hand, bare-handed, to flood the inner basin and float the cutter clear. He lost two fingers' use to it. Never told the council why he was in the cellar without orders. Never claimed the hazard pay." Fenwick's voice was level, but his thumb was moving over the edge of the desk in a slow circle, the way a man strokes a wound. "I signed the resolution thanking him for unspecified services. Unspecified. I've regretted the word for nine years."

"That's why he trusted you."

"That's why I owe him." He looked up, and the pewter eyes were wet and furious about being wet. "You should have led with that, boy. I told you — a man with a debt in hand leads with the memory."

"Would it have changed your answer?"

"It would have changed how long I pretended otherwise." He drew a ring of keys from his waistcoat — not the great guild bunch, but a smaller, older ring, brass gone dark with handling — and set them on the desk between us with a click that seemed louder than any chime in the room.

"Tide-tunnel gate opens with the short key. The gallery under the observatory hill is yours end to end — mind the third drop-shaft, the ladder rungs are rotten and I've petitioned the council for repairs twice and been refused twice, which will now strike you as convenient rather than negligent. The Quay Lane exit is the warehouse cellar; the warehouseman is a man named Pellowe who owes the guild money and discretion in equal amounts, and you may tell him Fenwick said the account was current." He pushed the keys across. "You will take them tonight. You will return them clean. And if Corven comes to this door asking questions, I will look him in his meticulous eye and tell him that Aldous Fenwick has served the guild for fifty-three years without one stain on the register, which will even be true right up until the moment it isn't."

"Sir—" My voice cracked on it, which was mortifying, and I pressed on regardless. "If this goes wrong—"

"It will go wrong," Fenwick said comfortably. "Plans built in a fortnight by amateurs always go wrong. The question is only how much of the wrong lands on whom. Arrange it so most of it lands on you. That's what mastership is, Hartwell — I'd have taught you that eventually, had you given me the years." He picked his tweezers back up, and just like that I was dismissed, an audience concluded, a mechanism returned to its case.

At the door I stopped once more.

"Why?" I asked. "Truly. After all of it — the resolutions, the neutrality—"

Fenwick didn't look up from his work. But his mouth did something at the corner that was not quite a smile.

"Because I've spent fifty-three years keeping time for other men's ships," he said, "and a man gets curious, near the end, whether he can still make the clock say something true. Go home, Hartwell. Wind your lantern. And pray the storm holds off — you'll want weather on your side, and Vellamouth owes nobody weather."

Maren was waiting for me at the foot of Gilders' Row, under the lamp outside the chandler's, pretending to examine a display of tarred twine with an absorption no human being has ever brought to tarred twine.

"Well?" she said.

"He said no. And then he said yes."

"That sounds like Father." She fell into step beside me, and neither of us touched the other, because Gilders' Row at dusk had more eyes than the harbor had gulls. But her sleeve brushed mine at every second stride, and I confess I timed my steps to make sure of it. "The keys?"

"In my pocket."

"Then you'll need the rest of the map, and you won't get it from him, because he'll insist on drawing it from memory and it will be accurate and illegible and three feet long. Come to the shop tomorrow. Tell Mother it's about the regulator you're mending for us. She'll believe it. She believes everything about regulators; it's how she survives being married to him." She paused at the corner where our ways divided, and in the lamplight her face was grave and young and utterly resolved. "Theo. The tunnels run under the customs house for forty yards. If Corven is watching anywhere, it's there. We go in the dark hours, and we go the long way round, and we do not hurry, because hurrying is how good plans die."

"When did you become the careful one?"

"The day I met you." She said it dryly, but her hand found mine for exactly one second in the shadow of the doorway — one second, measured out like everything in this city, like everything in this life — and then she was gone up the hill, and I stood a moment longer with my palm still warm.

Behind me, far off across the rooftops, the observatory clock began to strike the hour. Nine strokes, steady, indifferent, the same nine it had struck over smugglers and printers and drowned boys and honest men. I put my hand in my pocket and closed it around the brass keys, and started for home the long way, past the dark mouth of Quay Lane, rehearsing in my head the first turn of the first lock.

I did not yet know that Inspector Corven had already requested the guild's register of outside repairs — or that Fenwick, courteous as a Sunday visitor, had handed it over with the page bearing my name left exactly where it was, legible as print, for any man patient enough to read it twice.

Someone, that week, was patient enough.
