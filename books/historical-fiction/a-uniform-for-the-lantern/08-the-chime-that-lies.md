## 8. The Chime That Lies

There is a rule in the clockmakers' guild, carved into the lintel of the hall on Cooper's Lane where no apprentice can pretend he hasn't read it: *No hand shall alter a mechanism without sanction of the masters.* It sits above the door like a saint's face, stern and gilded, and for four years I had walked under it the way you walk under weather — aware of it, untroubled by it. Rules were to clocks what case plates were to movements. They kept the dirt out. They kept the work honest.

I broke that rule at eleven minutes past two in the morning, with my sleeves rolled to the elbow and the observatory's master clock opened up before me like a patient who had agreed, against all advice, to be experimented upon.

Let me tell you first what I was not doing, because I spent a good deal of time telling myself the same thing and it helped not at all. I was not sabotaging the clock. Sabotage is what enemies do. I was *adjusting* it — the word matters, or I needed it to matter — adjusting the striking train so that when the hour came round, the chime would run long. Not broken-long, not the drunken stagger of a mechanism in need of repair, but long the way a held note is long. Eight minutes' worth of long.

Because in four days, if the tides and the paperwork and God's notoriously short attention span allowed it, a fishing boat would slip out past the breakwater carrying Elias Marchetti, and an engine makes noise, and noise at dawn carries across water like gossip across a congregation.

* * *

The master clock at Vellamouth Observatory was built before anyone now living was born, by a man named Aldous Crane whose portrait hung in the stairwell looking perpetually disappointed in everyone who climbed past it. It was a great gravity-fed monster, nine feet of oak and brass, with a pendulum that swung with the patience of a man who has outlived all his arguments. Its chime rang the hours across the harbor, and every ship's boy, fishwife, and customs clerk in Vellamouth set their day by it without ever once thinking about it, which is the highest compliment a clock can earn.

To make it lie, then — to make the most trusted voice in the city say something untrue — felt less like theft than blasphemy. My hands knew this. My hands shook for the first quarter hour, and I stood there in the lamplight listening to them rattle against the brass like a nervous apprentice, which is precisely what I was, and hated myself for it.

Then I thought of Elias in the tide-tunnel below, sitting on a crate in the dark with his lighthouse logbook on his knees, waiting for me to decide whether the promise I'd made as a drowning boy was worth more than the trade I'd bled four years to earn. And my hands stopped shaking. Fear is a poor fuel but a reliable one; you simply have to feed it something better than yourself.

Here is the problem, stated plainly, because problems deserve plainness even when their solutions don't permit it: a clock's striking train is governed by its count wheel and its locking plate. The count wheel decides how many strokes the bell receives; the locking plate decides when the whole business stops. To stretch eight strokes into eight minutes, you cannot merely slow the escapement — the timekeeping would suffer, and a clock that tells wrong hours is worse than useless, it is *suspicious*, and suspicion was the one commodity Corven traded in. No. The chime must be separated from the keeping. The clock must go on telling the truth about the hour while lying magnificently about everything else.

So I did what any craftsman does when the sanctioned path doesn't exist: I invented one, alone, at night, illegally.

* * *

Three nights running I came up through the maintenance tunnel — Fenwick's old route, the one the guild pretended not to remember — carrying my tools wrapped in sacking so they wouldn't sing against each other on the stairs. The first night I only watched. I sat on the floor beneath the great case with my knees drawn up and let the mechanism teach me its habits, because every machine has habits, the way every man does, and you cannot deceive a thing until you know what it expects.

The count wheel turned once per stroke. Between strokes, the fly fan spun down and the locking detent waited, patient as a spider, for the pin that would release the next blow. The interval between strokes was fixed by the weight of the hammer's fall and the drag of the fan. Simple physics. Simple, and utterly insufficient, because even stretched to its laziest, the chime ran perhaps forty seconds past the hour.

Forty seconds buys you nothing across open water.

The second night I brought paper and chalk and did arithmetic by lamplight like a schoolboy cramming for examinations, except my sums, if discovered, would hang me. A stroke every ninety seconds for eight strokes gave seven and a half minutes plus the final decay of the bell itself — close enough to eight if the wind was kind. Ninety seconds between strokes meant the fly fan needed three times its present drag, or the release needed delaying by some intermediate motion. An intermediate motion meant new work. New work meant cutting a gear.

You cannot cut a gear in the observatory. Gear-cutting sings. Anyone within fifty yards knows a file is moving metal, and Inspector Corven, I had learned, could hear a ration card going missing from three streets off. So I cut the gear at Fenwick's bench, after hours, from a blank of hard brass I paid for out of the tin where I kept my rent money — which meant I also spent the week eating bread and dripping and telling my landlady my appetite had gone off, and she looked at me with the flat pity of a woman who has heard every excuse poverty invents and believes none of them.

Maren found the shavings. Of course she did. She found everything; her father said she'd have made a better inspector than Corven, though never say so above a whisper. She came into the workshop while the blank was still chucked in the lathe, stood behind me reading the profile of the teeth over my shoulder, and said nothing for a long moment.

"That's not a repair," she said finally.

"It's an improvement."

"Whose clock?"

"A clock."

"Theo." She said my name the way you set down something heavy, carefully, so it doesn't bang. "My father's been summoned before the council twice since you started coming here at night. Neutrality, Theo. That's the whole religion of this guild. We mend things for whoever pays and ask nothing about what the mending is *for*. You're asking him to stop being neutral without saying so."

"I'm not asking him anything. This is mine. The risk is mine."

She picked up a brass shaving, turned it in her fingers, let it fall. "Nothing is only yours once other people know about it. That's how knowing works." And she left me with the lathe humming and those words sitting in my chest like a swallowed stone, because she was right, and being right is the least comfortable thing a person can do to you.

But she didn't tell her father. Remember that. Whatever else happened, Maren saw the teeth of that gear and understood exactly what they were for, and she carried it silently, which is its own kind of oath.

* * *

The third night was the practice.

Elias came up the tunnel with me — against every argument I could mount, because the man had spent twelve years climbing lighthouse stairs and had developed a contempt for my opinions regarding stairs, ladders, and reasonable caution generally. He moved quietly for a big man; you'd expect a keeper to be loud, all wind and salt, but Elias had the stillness of someone who has spent thousands of nights listening to the sea and learned that silence is a tool like any other.

He stood in the corner of the clock room with his arms folded while I fitted the new gear into the striking train, and I will confess that having him there steadied me more than any amount of breathing exercises. There is a particular courage that comes from working in front of someone who once pulled you out of the harbor, and it is not bravery exactly. It is the desire not to look small.

"Now?" he asked.

"Not yet. If I engage it now and it goes wrong, we've spent our one rehearsal and the clock strikes six o'clock sounding like a drunk falling downstairs."

"So we wait for?"

"Two o'clock. Twenty minutes. Then I throw the changeover lever, the two o'clock chime runs long, and we hear whether my arithmetic survives contact with reality."

Elias lowered himself onto the floor, back against the wall, and looked up at the great case. "You know what I keep thinking," he said. "Twelve years I kept the light. Every night, winding the lens machinery, logging the burn. And the whole time there was this—" he nodded at the clock— "this thing counting over my head. I never once wondered what was inside it. Now my life depends on the inside of it, and it's your hands doing it." He shook his head slowly. "Funny trade, trust. You never need it until you need all of it."

"You trusted the light to warn ships."

"The light doesn't choose which ships to warn. That's the difference. That's why it was easy." He looked at me then, and in the lamplight his face had the gray patience of driftwood. "Yours chooses, boy. Tonight it chooses. That's why yours is hard."

I had no answer for that, so I did what craftsmen do with questions too large for the bench: I went back to work.

At two o'clock precisely, I threw the lever.

The count wheel lifted. The hammer drew back. And then — instead of the crisp stroke I'd heard ten thousand times across the harbor — the mechanism hesitated, caught, turned through my new gear with a whisper of meshing brass, and struck. One. Then the long wait, the fan spinning down through ninety seconds of silence that felt like drowning in slow motion, and Two. Three. Four.

By the fifth stroke I had stopped watching the mechanism and started listening like Corven would listen — like a man hearing a bell he'd trusted all his life suddenly speak in a strange accent. And here is the terrible thing, the thing I have never told anyone until this page: it sounded *beautiful*. The long intervals gave each stroke room to decay fully, so the bronze sang its whole song instead of being interrupted halfway through. The notes overlapped and braided. It was, objectively, the finest the Vellamouth chime had sounded in sixty years.

It was also unmistakably, catastrophically *wrong* to anyone who knew it.

Eight strokes. Seven minutes and forty seconds. My arithmetic had survived. I sat back on my heels with sweat cooling on my neck and felt the two halves of myself pull apart — the craftsman, who wanted to weep at how well it worked, and the conspirator, who understood that beauty was the flaw. A broken clock sounds broken. A *lying* clock should sound ordinary.

"We shorten the intervals," I heard myself say. "Sixty seconds between strokes. Less music, more cover. Nobody counts gaps at five in the morning; they count the bell still ringing when it shouldn't be."

Elias was standing now, one hand flat against the great case as if taking its pulse. "It'll do?" he asked. "It'll cover the engine?"

"At the breakwater? With the wind southeast? Yes." And then, because lying to him was impossible and lying to myself had gotten tiresome: "If everything else holds. Papers, boat, tide, Corven. The clock is the smallest piece of this, Elias. It's the only piece I can control, so it feels like the biggest. That's just fear talking."

"Fear talks sense sometimes." He squeezed my shoulder — his hand still heavy as an anchor stock, even half-starved. "Your father would have—"

"Don't."

"He'd have done exactly this," Elias said anyway, because twelve years of keeping a light teaches a man to hold to his course regardless of weather. "And he'd have told you the same thing I will: whatever happens Friday, you wind this clock back to true afterward. Whatever it costs. A keeper leaves the light burning right, even the night he's leaving."

* * *

I disassembled the alteration before dawn and carried the gear home wrapped in my shirt, and I want to report that I slept. What I actually did was lie in my narrow bed staring at the ceiling, replaying the chime stroke by stroke, adjusting intervals in my head, and somewhere around four in the morning I admitted the truth I'd been circling for three nights:

I was no longer an apprentice who had stumbled into trouble. I was a conspirator with a specialty. The guild rule above the door hadn't cracked; it had shattered, and I'd swept the pieces under my own conscience, and the worst part — the part that kept me awake — was that I felt *more honest* now than I had in years. Every sanctioned job I'd ever done had mended somebody's mantelpiece. This illegal one might save a man's life. Somewhere along the way, the measure of exact work had stopped being tolerances and started being consequences.

Four days. The ship sailed in four days, and I had a gear in my shirt that could buy eight minutes, and eight minutes was either everything or nothing depending entirely on things I couldn't touch — papers, weather, a bored harbormaster's clerk, and the patience of a census inspector who counted things for a living.

I got up, lit the lamp, and unwrapped the gear to check the teeth one more time. Perfectionism, my father used to say, is fear wearing a clean apron. He'd been right about most things. He'd have been right about this.

What I could not have known — what no arithmetic of mine could reach — was that three miles across the city, in a cold office above the customs house, Inspector Corven was at that same hour drawing a line through a column of figures and writing, in his small precise hand, a single question beside the observatory's repair ledger:

*Why was the chime heard striking long on the night of the 14th?*

He had been walking home late. He had heard it too.
