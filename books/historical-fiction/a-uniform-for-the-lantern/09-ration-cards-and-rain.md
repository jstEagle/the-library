## 9. Ration Cards and Rain

The rain came in off the Vellamouth estuary the way bad news always did — sideways, patient, and without any intention of stopping.

I had learned by then that a supply run was a small machine of its own, with as many parts as a watch and just as many chances for one tooth to catch wrong. There was the ration card — Fenwick's third, borrowed against his name like a loan he'd never see repaid. There was the route: down Cooper's Lane, through the fish market, out past the customs sheds where the constables stood under oilcloth and got bored, and bored constables, I had been told, were either harmless or dangerous, depending entirely on which way their boredom tipped that morning. And there was Elias, hooded, hunched, walking with the particular stoop of a man trying to be smaller than the law allowed.

"You walk like a guilty man," I'd told him at the tunnel mouth.

"I *am* a guilty man," he'd said. "I'm guilty of existing in the wrong column of somebody's ledger." He'd pulled the hood lower. "But I'll try to exist quietly."

That was the trouble with Elias Marchetti. He joked exactly when joking was least advisable. It was, I suspect, how he'd survived thirty years on a rock in the sea — laughing at the weather until the weather gave up.

The market was loud enough to hide us and crowded enough to betray us, which is the bargain every crowd offers. Gulls screamed over the fish stalls. A woman argued about the price of herring with the conviction of a barrister. The smell was salt and diesel and wet wool, and underneath it all, the faint iron tang of the harbor, which I had hated since the day it nearly took me and loved since the day Elias pulled me out of it.

We bought what could be carried. Bread, cheese wrapped in wax paper, a tin of lamp oil, tea — real tea, hoarded from some corner of Fenwick's stock because he said a man running for his life deserved better than chicory. Elias carried the basket. I carried the money and the papers, sewn into my coat lining the way Maren had shown me, and thought about her hands doing the sewing, quick and certain, and made myself stop thinking about it.

It was going well. That should have been my first warning. In my experience, things go well right up until they don't, like a clock running beautifully for forty years before some microscopic flaw in the mainspring brings the whole works to a stop at an inconvenient hour.

We were passing the cheese stall — Elias leaning over to sniff a wheel of something hard and yellow, because hunger makes even fugitives sentimental — when the voice came.

"That's a Vellamouth accent you've got there, friend."

I turned. The constable was young, red-faced from the damp, with the cheerful menace of a man who'd been given authority before he'd been given judgment. He was looking at Elias. Not at me. At Elias.

"Or maybe not Vellamouth," the constable went on, ambling closer. "There's something under it. Something... southern."

Elias straightened up slowly. I watched him do the arithmetic I'd watched him do a hundred times in the tunnel: the odds, the exits, the weight of the basket versus the weight of a cell.

"Been away," Elias said. "Sailor most of my life. Accents get mixed like cargo."

"Sailor." The constable smiled. "What ship?"

And here is where I learned something about bluffing that no book teaches: you don't win a bluff by lying better. You win it by making the truth so tedious the other man loses interest.

"The *Meridian*," I said, stepping between them with the basket of my own irritation held out like a lantern. "Coastal freighter, registered out of Harrow Quay. My uncle's been crewing her eleven years and she's never once arrived anywhere interesting. He's home on medical leave — fell down a hold and cracked three ribs. If you'd like, I can fetch the doctor's note. It's a very dull document. It has a diagram."

The constable blinked. Men with authority are rarely prepared for enthusiasm deployed against them.

"Medical leave," he repeated.

"Three ribs," I said. "He snores now. It's ruined the whole family's sleep, if you want the honest cost of maritime labor." I turned to Elias. "Show him the scar, Uncle. He won't believe the ribs otherwise."

Elias — bless him, bless him forever — lifted his shirt hem two inches to reveal a long white seam of scar tissue along his side, genuine, earned years ago on the lighthouse rocks, and said, in perfect wounded-Vellamouth-uncle, "The doctor drew arrows on it. Arrows, boy. Like I'm a chart."

The constable laughed. That was the whole battle, decided in one laugh. He waved us on with a "carry on, then" and turned back to his oilcloth and his boredom, and we walked away at exactly the speed of innocent men — not too fast, never too slow, the pace of people thinking about cheese.

It wasn't until we were three streets gone, under the awning of a shuttered chandlery, that Elias stopped and put a hand against the wall.

"The scar," he said quietly. "You knew about it."

"You pulled me out of the water twelve years ago with your shirt off. I was delirious, not blind."

He looked at me for a moment, and there was something in his face that hadn't been there before — not gratitude, which he already wore like an old coat, but something more fragile. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man realizing the boy he saved has grown into someone who might save him back.

"You lied well," he said.

"I didn't lie. I told the truth tediously. There's a difference, and it's the only kind of armor our family owns."

But we both knew the truth of it: the constable had heard something. And worse — as we came out onto Quay Street, I glanced back and saw him talking to another man, older, dry under an umbrella, taking notes in a small book. The constable gestured. The older man wrote.

A witness. Not a charge, not yet. Just a line in somebody's ledger, waiting for other lines to come and meet it.

* * *

The rain found the papers before we did.

They were in my coat lining, as I said — sewn there by careful hands — but coats are not waterproof, and neither are careful hands, and by the time we reached Fenwick's workshop through the back alley entrance that smelled of oil and old brass, the ink had begun its slow betrayal. Elias's transit pass, the forgery that named him *Thomas Merrick, engine fitter, Harrow Quay*, had gone soft at the edges. The date had bled into a gray bloom. The stamp — Maren's finest work, a thing of real beauty — had wept itself into a bruise.

Fenwick took one look and made the sound all craftsmen make at ruined work: a short exhale through the nose, the sound of a man pricing grief.

"Maren," he called.

She came down the stairs wiping brass filings from her hands, and when she saw the papers her face did something complicated — annoyance and fear sharing a room without speaking.

"The tide-tunnel leaked into your coat?" she asked.

"The sky leaked into my coat."

"Well. At least it's honest work." She held the pass up to the lamp, tilting it. "The stamp's salvageable. The date's gone. The name held — I used the good ink there, iron gall, it bites into the paper. But this—" She touched the blurred figure of the issuing clerk's signature. "This is dead. It'll need re-inking, and it'll need drying properly this time, which means heat, which means time, which means—"

"How much time?" Elias asked.

"A night," she said. "Maybe two, if the paper warps."

Elias sat down heavily on Fenwick's work stool, and for a moment the lighthouse keeper vanished and there was only a tired man in a wet coat, fifty-one years old, holding a basket of bread and cheese like it was the last solid thing in the world.

"Two nights," he said. "In this room. Listening to the rain."

"Better than listening to a cell door," Fenwick said gruffly, and busied himself with the lathe, which was his way of ending conversations he couldn't bear to have.

* * *

Maren worked at the bench by the window, and I worked beside her, and the rain kept its rhythm against the glass like a metronome set by someone indifferent to our troubles.

Re-inking a forged document, it turns out, is delicate work. You cannot simply write over the ghost of the old ink; the lines must sit in the same grooves, follow the same tremors of the original hand. Maren had studied the clerk's signature from a sample Fenwick had obtained through channels I preferred not to ask about, and she reproduced it stroke by stroke, breath held, the way I cut gear teeth.

"Hold this flat," she murmured. "Corner. Don't breathe on it."

"I'm not breathing at all. I've given it up for the duration."

Her mouth twitched. She finished the final flourish of the *k* in *Merrick* and sat back, and we looked at the paper together under the lamp, this small rectangle of lies, and I felt the strangest pride — the pride of craft applied to crime, or possibly crime elevated to craft; by then I'd stopped distinguishing.

"It's good work," I said.

"It's forgery."

"It's *good* forgery."

She laughed — a low laugh, quickly caught, glancing toward the stairs where her father worked — and in the lamplight I saw that her hair had come loose from its pin on one side and that there was ink on her left cheekbone, a small dark comma, and I understood suddenly and completely why poets make such idiots of themselves.

"You've got ink," I said, and touched my own cheekbone, meaning to indicate hers, achieving instead nothing whatsoever.

"Do I?" She wiped the wrong cheek. Then she laughed again, and took my wrist, and moved my hand to the right place on her own face, and for a moment my thumb rested at the edge of the small dark mark, and neither of us moved, and the rain filled the silence the way water fills a lock — steadily, from below, until something has to give.

"Elias told me about the pier," she said quietly. "Twelve years ago. He doesn't talk much, but tonight he talked. About pulling a boy out of the water who weighed nothing and swore like a stevedore."

"I was fourteen and half-drowned. My vocabulary was broad."

"He says you promised your father you'd repay it."

"My father made me promise. There's a difference. Fathers draft the contracts; sons just sign."

"And have you? Repaid it?"

I looked at the pass drying on its board, at the name *Thomas Merrick* gleaming wetly in fresh ink, and said, "I'm working on the final installment."

Something passed across her face then — not disapproval. I'd braced for disapproval and it never came. It was more like recognition, the same expression Elias had worn in the alley: the look of someone measuring another person against a standard and finding the measurement acceptable. More than acceptable. She picked up the blotting paper, pressed it gently over the signature, and said:

"When this is done — when he's away — what happens to you?"

"Corven happens to me, probably. Eventually."

"And you'd still do it."

"There's no version of the arithmetic where I don't."

She nodded slowly, and pressed the blotter down once more, harder, as if sealing something other than ink. "Then we'd better make sure the papers are perfect," she said. "Because imperfect men are going to be carrying them."

* * *

We dried the documents over the warm throat of the workshop stove, pinned to a line like strange laundry, and Elias watched them turn and steam and slowly stiffen back into credibility. Fenwick brought tea. Outside, the storm settled in for the night, and somewhere across the black city the observatory clock struck ten, muffled by rain, and I counted the strokes out of pure habit and found them correct — ten strokes, honest ones — and felt obscurely comforted, as though somewhere in the world a mechanism was still telling the truth.

Elias spoke from his chair, eyes closed, half asleep. "Your father. The promise. Tell me the exact words sometime."

"They weren't elegant."

"Promises never are. That's how you know they're real."

I banked the stove. Maren had gone up an hour before, and the workshop smelled of ink and oil and wet wool, and I lay on the cot beneath the bench with the rain hammering the roof and thought about witnesses. The young constable. The older man with the notebook. Somewhere in the city, a line was being written that would eventually meet another line, the way Corven's question about the chime had waited under the lamplight for its answer.

Luck, I thought, is not a substance. It's a balance wheel, swinging one way and then the other, and ours was swinging, and there was nothing to do but keep the escapement clean and wait.

I was nearly asleep when boots sounded on the alley stair — quick, deliberate, wrong-for-the-hour — and Fenwick's shadow crossed the frosted pane of the door, and his knuckles rapped twice, softly, the signal we'd agreed meant one thing and one thing only:

Someone was asking questions at the front of the shop.
