## 12. Maren's Bargain

Maren Fenwick kept her father's accounts the way she did everything else: in a hand so small and even it looked typeset, in ledgers ruled by her own patience, with no line wasted and no figure left to charity. I had seen those ledgers on the counter of the guild workshop a hundred times without ever once thinking about the hands that made them. That was the trouble with me. I could spend an hour admiring a fusee cut and never notice the woman cutting it.

I noticed now.

She was at the workbench past midnight, re-inking our ruined papers by lamplight, her sleeves rolled to the elbow, a drop of iron-gall ink on her wrist like a beauty mark some crueler century might have admired. The rain still came down on the skylight in long silver threads. Somewhere below us the tide-tunnel breathed its slow salt breath, and Elias Marchetti slept down there among the gears like a man buried alive who had agreed to it.

"You're staring," she said, without looking up.

"I'm thinking."

"Dangerous trade for you. Stick to wheels."

I sat down across from her, careful not to disturb the papers drying in rows like small drowned things brought back to life. "Maren. I need a boat."

The pen stopped.

She didn't look up immediately. She finished the stroke she was making — the tail of a *g*, I remember that absurdly well, the tail of a *g* — then set the pen in its rest with a click that sounded louder than any chime I'd ever built.

"Say that again," she said, "in the tone of someone asking."

"I need a boat," I said. "Please."

"And there it is." She leaned back and looked at me, and I saw that she had known already — had perhaps known since the ration cards soaked through in the rain, since the constable in the market squinted at Elias's accent and I lied badly enough to make a good liar of myself by comparison. Maren Fenwick did not miss things. She filed them. "You want my father's boat."

"No," I said. "Yours."

That landed differently, and I watched it land. Her father's boat was the guild's business, insured, logged, above suspicion. Her own boat — the little clinker-built thing her mother had left her, the one she took out alone on Sunday mornings when the harbor fog made the world honest again — that was hers, unregistered in any way that mattered, small enough to be forgotten by every list but the tide tables.

"The *Kittiwake*," she said quietly.

"The *Kittiwake*."

"She's twenty years old and leaks when she sulks."

"So do I."

"That's true." And there — there it was, the ghost of a smile, gone as fast as it came, because this was not a night for smiles and we both knew the arithmetic. "Theo. Do you understand what you're asking? Not what it costs you. You've already spent yourself, I can see it in your face; you look like a man balancing books he knows won't close. What it costs *him*. My father. If that boat is found carrying an enemy national out of Vellamouth harbor—"

"They won't trace it to him."

"They'll trace everything. That is the entire profession of Inspector Corven, tracing. He is a man who reads a ledger the way you read a movement, gear by gear, and he has already put three facts beside each other that only fit one way." She stood, walked to the window, looked out at rain she couldn't see in the dark. "He stripped his mastership from a man who has served this guild for forty years, or he will. He'll do it smiling, because the council will call it prudence. And my father will let them. Because he loves you, you great ungrateful clockwork fool, and because he decided somewhere between the first refusal and the old favor that some debts are paid in standing."

I had nothing to say to that, because it was all true, and true things have a way of sitting in a room like lead weights until you either carry them or admit they're too heavy.

So instead I said the other true thing.

"One man," I said. "It's one man against the whole machinery of it. Corven's machinery, the statutes, the manifests, the deportation ships sailing every week like a scheduled service. One boat, one keeper, one apprentice who isn't even a journeyman yet. Against all of it. It's mad, Maren. If you told me plainly, if you laid it out on the bench like a broken movement, I would say the repair costs more than the clock is worth. Walk away. Buy a new clock."

"But?"

"But I pulled a drowning man out of arithmetic once," I said. "And he wasn't worth saving either, by any ledger. He was a twelve-year-old boy who fell off a pier because he was showing off. Every sensible account said let the sea have him, it's cheaper. Elias didn't consult the accounts."

Maren turned from the window.

"You think I don't know the argument?" she said. "I make it to myself every hour. One man. There are hundreds going out on those ships, Theo, hundreds, and they go whether we save Elias or not, and saving him changes nothing for any of them. The machine doesn't even notice. That's what a machine is — a thing that doesn't notice. So why spend the *Kittiwake*, why spend Father, why spend you and possibly me, on one man whom the machine will not even register as lost?"

"Because the machine counts wrong," I said.

"How?"

"It counts people as entries. Elias taught me to count them as hours." I heard how it sounded even as I said it, and pressed on anyway, because a craftsman learns you can't take back a cut halfway through. "When I was twelve and half-dead and coughing harbor water into his coat, he held me and he counted. Out loud. My breaths. He said later — I remembered it years after — he said, *there, lad, sixty to the minute, that's a heart that means to stay.* He didn't ask what I was worth. He counted what I was, and what I was was alive, and that was enough to row for. That's all I'm asking of you. Not that it balances. Just that you row."

The lamp flickered. Rain worked the glass. Down in the tunnel, faintly, Elias coughed in his sleep, and both of us went still until it stopped.

Maren sat back down. She didn't touch the pen. She put her hands flat on the bench the way her father did when weighing a job — palms down, fingers spread, taking the measure of the wood.

"If I say yes," she said, "you should know what I'm saying it *to*. Not to you. To the morning Corven stands in my father's doorway with his little notebook and asks where his daughter's boat keeps itself on Tuesday nights. To the council table. To the possibility — not the chance, Theo, the *possibility* — that I watch my father's name read out like a sentence in a hall where he taught men their trade." Her voice didn't break. It narrowed, which was worse. "And I'm saying yes anyway. Do you know why? Not because of your speech about hearts. Though it was a fine speech. You should write speeches when your hands give out."

"Then why?"

"Because last week you asked me what could be carried." She tapped the forged papers, dry now, neat as scripture. "And I've been doing inventory ever since. That's what I am, Theo — I'm the inventory. And I find that what I cannot carry is the version of myself that knew where Elias Marchetti was hiding and let the tide take him without lifting an oar. She'd live comfortably, that version. Comfortably and small. And every clock she ever mended would run a minute slow and she'd know why."

Neither of us spoke for a while. The rain filled the silence the way oil fills a bearing — completely, without argument.

"The storm window," I said finally. "Thursday, maybe Friday. No captain wants it. That's our cover and our coffin both. First light, out past the breakwater before the patrol cutter thinks anyone sane would move."

"You'll be at the observatory."

"I'll be at the clock." I told her then — all of it, the altered striking train, the eight minutes, the chime that would lie beautifully over the water while a leaking fishing boat crept through weather no sane sailor loved. I told her because she'd earned it, and because if it went wrong she'd need to know exactly which of us had done what, to keep her father clear.

She listened the way she inked documents: completely, missing nothing.

"Eight minutes," she said. "Can you truly hold it eight minutes?"

"No," I admitted. "I can build it to try. After that it's in God's hands and the escapement's, and I honestly am not certain which of those is less merciful."

"*Kittiwake* sails Thursday," she said. "First light. I'll take her out myself. I know the channel better than any hired hand, and a hired hand talks."

"You shouldn't be on that boat."

"You shouldn't be at that clock." She gathered the dried papers, squared them, tied them with the same plain string she used for everything. "We are neither of us sensible people tonight, Theo Hartwell. Let's at least be foolish in company."

I wanted to say something then. I felt it rising in me like water in a lock — something with weight to it, something that had no business being said on a night when tomorrow was a rumor and Thursday was a cliff edge. Something that would need naming, and naming things fixes them, and fixed things can be taken from you. I'd learned that at twelve, in the water, watching my father's promise sink with the pier.

What I said instead was: "Wear oilskins. The *Kittiwake* leaks when she sulks."

"What I said," Maren replied, "was that she takes after her owner."

She blew out the lamp between us, but not the one at her end, and we worked on — she at the papers, I at the sketch of the striking train — until the sky behind the rain went the color of unpainted brass, and neither of us mentioned that our shoulders had come to rest against each other somewhere around the third hour, or that neither of us moved away.

* * *

Fenwick met me at the top of the tide-tunnel stairs at dawn. He looked like a man who had slept in his coat, which meant he hadn't slept at all, which meant Maren had told him.

"Mastership," he said, without greeting. "Forty years. My name on the guild roll next to names that go back to the charter kings. And yesterday I stood in front of the council and said nothing, and let them think what they liked, because every second they spent suspecting me was a second they didn't spend looking at you." He held out his hand, palm up. In it lay a small brass key, worn smooth. "Boat shed. Third slip past the customs wall. She's fueled, provisioned, and the bilge pump works, which is more than I can say for my heart."

"Master Fenwick—"

"Don't." His voice cracked like a dropping weight. "Don't thank me and don't apologize and don't tell me it will be all right, because I'm too old for comfort and too proud for lies, and I raised a daughter who inherits both failings. Go down and tell Marchetti the tide turns at seven and he'd best be ready to argue with it."

I took the key. Our hands stayed on it a moment together, two craftsmen's hands, rough at the same places.

"He knows what it costs," I said.

"He does. That's what makes it a gift and not a payment." He turned away, then stopped, his back to me, and said the last of it to the stairwell rather than to me: "My father kept fugitives in this tunnel during the last war, boy. I swore I'd be neutral. Neutrality is just fear wearing a good coat. Tell Elias—" He didn't finish. He went up the stairs instead, and the door closed soft as a striking train settling.

Down in the tunnel, Elias sat sharpening a knife that needed no sharpening, and when I gave him the news — the *Kittiwake*, Thursday, first light, Maren at the tiller — he looked at me a long time and then at the lantern that had been his life's one constant, and he said:

"A girl is risking her father's name for mine."

"Yes."

"Then I had better be worth it," he said, "or die trying to be."

And that was when the harbormaster's clerk's boy came pelting down the alley above, hammering the signal we'd agreed on — three knocks, pause, three more — the signal that meant the manifest had been held, the inspection moved forward, dawn coming a day early, and no time left in the world but what we stole.
