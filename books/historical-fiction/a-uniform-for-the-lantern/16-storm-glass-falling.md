## 16. Storm Glass Falling

The storm glass had been falling since Tuesday, and by Thursday morning it had given up entirely — the camphor in the little sealed tube settling into white cloud like a man lying down to die. Maren's father kept one at the harbor office, and Fenwick kept one on the guild mantel, and I had learned long ago that a clockmaker trusts barometers the way priests trust weather: with hope, and a private suspicion that the whole business is rigged.

By Thursday afternoon it was not a suspicion.

The wind came off the sea in long gray shoves, bending the lamp-posts along the Quay like men ducking under low beams, and the rain came sideways, in ropes, in sheets, in a fine spiteful mist that found every seam in every coat in Vellamouth simultaneously. The gutters ran brown. The gulls gave up flying altogether and sat huddled on the breakwater looking personally insulted.

And the *Kestrel's Grace* — Maren's family boat, twenty-two feet of honest oak and borrowed courage — could not sail in that. Not out of the harbor mouth, not into open water, not with a man aboard who could not swim a stroke and papers that would dissolve if you sneezed on them twice.

Which meant we had one window left, and it was narrow as a coin slot.

"The tide turns at first light," Maren said. She stood at the window of Fenwick's back room with her arms crossed, watching the harbor try to climb its own walls. "Friday. If the storm breaks tonight — and the glass says it will, they always lie about breaking but this one means it — there'll be maybe two hours of calm before the next front comes down from the north. Two hours to get past the breakwater, round the point, and out of sight of anything Corven owns."

"And if it doesn't break?" Elias asked from the corner.

He'd been sitting in that corner for three days now, in the same chair, in the same posture — upright, hands flat on his knees, like a man waiting for an appointment he dreaded. He did most of his talking to the floor. He was eating again, which I counted as progress, though the way he ate — quickly, apologetically, as though food might be reclaimed from him — told me things about the last month that I didn't ask after.

"Then there is no Friday," Maren said simply. "There's only Saturday, and Saturday Corven boards every hull in the harbor with a manifest held open and a list in his pocket. You understand that."

"I understand everything," Elias said. "That's my trouble."

I wanted to laugh at that. It came out more like a cough.

* * *

The storm broke around nine that night, all at once, the way a debt collector arrives — no warning you didn't already have, and total commitment when it came. The rain stopped being weather and became opinion. Somewhere up the hill a chimney lost its argument with the wind and came down with a crash that made us all flinch, and then, remarkably, the sky began to tear itself open along the eastern seam, showing patches of hard black starlight between the racing clouds.

Maren went out into it to see her father about the boat, and Fenwick had been called to the guild — summoned, he said, in a voice that told me he knew precisely what for — so for some hours it was only Elias and me in that back room with a single oil lamp and the sound of Vellamouth being taken apart plank by plank outside.

I mended a watch. It seemed important, somehow, to be doing the work. There was a lady's fob-watch on the bench with a broken balance spring, and I sat and fitted the new spring with tweezers while the storm shouted at the shutters, and I will tell you honestly that I have never concentrated harder on anything in my life, because the alternative was conversation, and conversation had become a thing with edges.

It was Elias who broke first. Of course it was.

"Theo."

"Mm." I did not look up. Looking up was surrender.

"If I go out on that water tomorrow," he said, "and she goes down — and boats do go down, boy, I've watched them go down from a lighthouse for thirty years, I've *counted* them — then I die as what they say I am. A fugitive. An enemy. They'll fish me out of the bay and put me in a box with a number on it and nobody will ever argue with that number, because dead men don't appeal."

I set the tweezers down. Very carefully. The way you set down something sharp when your hand has started to shake.

"But if I walk into the customs house tomorrow morning," he went on, "and give myself up — say, *here, here is Marchetti, come quietly* — then I'm a prisoner. Fed. Counted. Alive. There are rules about prisoners. Even now. Even for—" he made a small gesture that took in his own face, his own name, the entire apparatus of the emergency statutes, "—even for my kind. Two years maybe. Five. And then perhaps they let an old man stand at a rail in the daylight and look at the sea without anyone shooting at him."

"That's the bargain?" I said. "Surrender and live?"

"It's not a bargain. It's arithmetic." He said it tiredly, the way you correct a child. "You of all people should respect arithmetic, Theo. You count things for a living."

"Yes," I said. "And I'll tell you what my counting says. My counting says Inspector Corven has spent three weeks building a file on you with the patience of a man winding a very slow clock. My counting says he doesn't want you fed and counted. He wants you *processed*. My counting says the deportation ships don't carry prisoners, Elias, they carry cargo, and cargo doesn't get five years and a rail to stand at. Cargo gets unloaded somewhere and forgotten, and the paperwork about where is very carefully mislaid."

"You don't know that."

"No," I admitted. "I know it the way you know a gear is going to slip before it slips. Thirty years at a lighthouse, you said. Watching boats go down. So you tell me — how many times did a man take the safe channel and it saved him? And how many times did the safe channel just have a different name for the rocks?"

Elias looked at me for a long moment. Then he laughed — one dry bark, surprised out of him, gone as fast as it came.

"You've gotten clever," he said. "You used to be a boy who fell off piers."

"I still am," I said. "Cleverness is just falling off piers with better notes afterward."

* * *

He got up then, and went to the window, and stood looking out at what could be seen of the harbor through the streaming glass. The lamp at the end of the breakwater was fighting for its life, a yellow smear behind the rain. Beyond it, nothing. The sea had swallowed its own horizon.

"Do you remember," Elias said, "the night you went in?"

"I remember all of it except the part people usually mean," I said. "I remember the pier ladder having rusted through and nobody fixing it. I remember thinking, quite calmly, *this is going to be expensive*, and then the water hitting me like a door. What I don't remember is the drowning part. Apparently I wasn't invited."

"I remember the drowning part," Elias said quietly. "Thirty years, Theo. You learn what drowning looks like from above. It looks like nothing. That's the horror of it. A head goes under, comes up, goes under — and from the tower it's just another piece of the water moving. If I hadn't already been coming down the stair because I'd seen a boy climbing a ladder that was hanging by one bolt—" He stopped. Started again. "I nearly didn't see you at all. You understand? The whole of it, everything since, hinges on a rusted ladder and a keeper who happened to be looking down instead of out."

"Then the sea owed you one," I said. "And it's paying up."

"No." He turned from the window. In the lamplight his face was older than it had been a month ago, older than it had any right to get in a month, and there was something in it I recognized with a lurch: it was the face my father used to make near the end, when the illness had burned through the anger and left only the accounting. "No, boy. That's not how it works and you know it. Debts don't pay themselves. Someone holds them, and someone carries them, and someone drowns under them. Your father made me swear I'd look after you, and I swore it over his deathbed because dying men extract promises the way the tide extracts sand, and I have carried that promise twelve years like a stone in my boot. And now you're carrying one for me, and it's costing you your trade, and it may yet cost Maren her father's name, and old Fenwick his mastership, and for what? For one man. One old keeper who reported a smuggling run twenty years ago and has been paying for doing right ever since." His voice cracked, just slightly, on *right*. "The arithmetic doesn't favor us, Theo. It never did."

"No," I said. "It doesn't."

"Then why?"

And here is the thing. I had an answer ready. I'd been carrying it for weeks, polished smooth, ready to deploy: something about honor, something about promises, something suitably quotable that a man could put in a book someday. And I opened my mouth to say it, and it all fell apart, because none of it was true. Or rather, all of it was true and none of it was the truth.

"Because I saw you eat," I said.

He stared at me. "What?"

"Just now. These past days. You eat like a man apologizing. Like food might be taken back. And I thought — this is what Corven does. This is what the statutes do. They don't kill you first. They teach you to apologize for existing, and *then* they kill you, and by then you've signed the apology yourself." I picked the tweezers back up because my hands needed employment. "My father died owing rent and proud of every other thing about himself. You taught me the difference between those two conditions, Elias, though neither of us ever said it out loud. You're not cargo. You're not a prisoner. You're the man who pulled a stupid boy out of the harbor and then stood on the pier dripping and told him the ladder needed reporting, which is the most *you* sentence I have ever heard. And I promised. Not the way Father extracted it from you — the other way. Freely. In peacetime, when it cost nothing. Well." I gestured at the window, at the storm, at the entire collapsing machinery of 1911. "Now it costs something. That's how you know it was real."

The lamp guttered. Outside, the wind found a new register, lower, almost conversational, the way a big storm talks when it's decided to stay a while.

Elias Marchetti stood in that lamplight and cried. Not much. Not the way younger men cry. Just enough that he had to turn his face away and press the heel of his hand against his eyes for a moment, and when he turned back he was the keeper again, the tower man, the one who counted boats.

"All right," he said. "First light."

"All right," I said.

"But Theo." He pointed one finger at me, steady as a bearing light. "If the boat goes down, you swim. Whatever happens, whatever I do, you swim. I have already pulled you out of that water once and I am too old to do it twice."

"Deal," I said, and we shook on it, and his grip was still strong, and I chose to believe that meant something.

* * *

Maren came back soaked to the skin and triumphant, which is a combination only fishermen and lunatics can manage. Her father would bring the *Kestrel's Grace* round to the old coal-staithe below the tide-tunnel at half four; the engine had been serviced; the fuel was aboard. She stripped off her oilskins, wrung out her hair over the bucket, and delivered the rest of the news in the flat voice she reserved for the parts of plans she disliked.

"There's a complication. Harbormaster's clerk sent word up — Corven's moved the boarding forward. Not Saturday dawn. *Saturday midnight.* He wants every vessel mustered and dark before the tide even thinks about turning."

We looked at each other across the lamplight. I felt the plan shift under me like ice.

"Midnight," Elias said slowly. "But the tide—"

"Doesn't matter what the tide does if every boat in the harbor is nailed to its berth with soldiers on it," Maren said. "Which means either we go at first light tomorrow and pray the storm's tail gives us our two hours — or we don't go at all."

Outside, far off across the water, the observatory bell began the hour. Nine strokes, clear and unhurried, threading through the noise of the storm like a needle through cloth.

Nine strokes. Four hours until the tide turned. Seven until first light.

Somewhere across the city, I knew, Inspector Corven was also awake, also listening to that same bell, also counting. The question — the only question left in the world — was whether his clock or mine struck first.

I reached for the storm glass on the shelf, and held it up to the lamp, and watched the last of the cloud begin, very slowly, to clear.

"We go at first light," I said. "God help us, we go at first light."
