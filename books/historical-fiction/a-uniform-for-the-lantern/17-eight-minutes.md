## 17. Eight Minutes

There is a particular kind of silence that comes just before you do the worst thing of your life. It isn't peace. It's more like the pause between the fourth and fifth stroke of a bell — the mechanism has committed, the hammer is falling, and nothing in heaven or Vellamouth can call it back.

I stood in the clock room at ten minutes to five on the morning of the ninth of November, with my hand on the release lever I had spent eleven nights rebuilding, and I listened to that silence the way a man listens to his own heart when the doctor's ear is against his chest.

Below me, the harbor was a smear of gray under a sky the color of wet slate. The storm had blown itself hoarse overnight and left behind only a long, tired swell, the kind of sea that has argued all night and lost interest. Out past the breakwater, riding her mooring like a horse pretending to be calm, sat Maren's father's fishing boat — the *Kestrel*, twenty-two feet of honest oak and one dishonest cargo.

Elias was aboard. That was the part I kept having to re-verify, the way you check a locked door twice because your hands don't trust your memory. He'd gone down the tide-tunnel steps an hour before dawn, gray-faced, wrapped in Maren's oilskins three sizes too big for him, carrying everything left of his life in a coat that bulged like a stolen goose. At the bottom of the stairs he had stopped and looked back at me.

"You'll wind it properly," he said. Not a question about the boat. About the clock.

"Every day," I said. "Even Sundays."

He nodded once, the way he used to nod from the gallery of the lighthouse when I was a boy waving up at him from the pier — a nod that meant *enough words*, and meant, underneath the enough words, something that would have taken a sermon to say. Then he went down into the dark, and I went up into the tower, and we each climbed toward our own kind of light.

* * *

Let me tell you what eleven nights of criminal engineering looks like, because it looks, God help me, beautiful.

A striking train is an honest thing. It counts the hours on a wheel called the count-wheel, lets down a weight through a governor that keeps the strokes even, and delivers them to the bell at a pace no man could argue with. The observatory's master clock had done this for sixty years without once lying to anybody. It was the most trustworthy citizen in Vellamouth, which is perhaps why nobody suspected it of treason.

My alteration lived in the governor. A governor is a pair of flyweights on hinged arms; spin them fast and they swing out, catching air, dragging the train to a dignified tempo. I had built a second set of arms — smaller, brass, cut from scrap Fenwick had smuggled to me rolled inside a delivery of clock oil — and rigged them to engage only after the third stroke. For the first three strokes, the chime would be perfect. Unimpeachable. Any inspector standing beneath the tower would hear exactly what sixty years of habit told him to expect.

Then the secondary weights would swing out, and the train would slow — not stall, never stall, a stalled train is a broken clock and broken clocks get climbed into — but slow, gently, by degrees, so that each remaining stroke came later than the last, stretching eight minutes of sound across what should have been ninety seconds. To anyone not counting, it would simply be a long chime. Bells are allowed to be long. Bells are allowed to be anything except wrong.

The trick — the whole trick, the trick my neck now hung upon — was that Corven counted.

I had learned that about him the way you learn the grain of wood: slowly, against the direction of ease. In three months of being watched by the man I had come to understand that Inspector Corven did not distrust people; he distrusted approximations. He kept his ledger because paper remembered better than men. And if the observatory bell rang its noon chime in four minutes instead of ninety seconds, he would not think *how strange*. He would take out his watch, and he would count the intervals, and he would find the lie hiding in the arithmetic like a worm in a beam.

Which is why the plan was madness, and why it was also the only plan left.

* * *

You'll want the whole shape of it, so here it is, laid out as plainly as I can while my hands still remember shaking.

Corven had held the morning-tide manifest open. Every vessel leaving Vellamouth harbor at dawn would be boarded and inspected — every vessel, no exceptions, the harbormaster's clerk told me for the price of two weeks' wages and a guilty conscience. The *Kestrel* could not slip out among the fishing fleet, because there would be no fishing fleet; Corven's order had grounded them all until inspection cleared.

But Corven's order had one seam in it, and the seam was time. His inspectors were men, and men cannot be in two places, and the boarding parties were scheduled to begin at first light — at the moment, precisely, when the observatory master clock struck five and the harbor bells took it up, because in Vellamouth the master clock was law and every lesser bell obeyed.

If the chime rang true, the inspectors would move on the stroke, boarding the *Kestrel* within minutes, finding Elias Marchetti folded into the fish hold behind a false bulkhead Maren's brother had built, and that would be the end of the story, and not the good end.

If the chime ran long — if the master clock itself, the city's own voice, stretched five o'clock across eight minutes of slow bronze thunder — then the inspectors would stand about in the rain waiting for the signal that governed their orders, arguing about whether to proceed, consulting watches, deferring to the clock because men always defer to the clock. Eight minutes. Maren had timed the run from the fish quay to the breakwater gap at low throttle: seven minutes, forty seconds, in weather kinder than this.

Eight minutes for ninety seconds' worth of bell. It was arithmetic as desperation, and I loved it the way you love anything that might kill you.

* * *

At four minutes to five, I heard the skiff's oars.

I couldn't see her from the clock room — the tower windows faced the wrong way, of course they did, the universe arranges these things — but I heard the knock of wood on the *Kestrel*'s hull, and then Maren's voice, pitched low across the water, saying something I couldn't catch and didn't need to. She had rowed Elias out from the tunnel mouth herself rather than let him handle a boat on legs that hadn't been fed properly in a month. She would bring the *Kestrel* around to the fish quay under pretense of checking her moorings — a woman tending her family's boat at dawn was unremarkable, or had been unremarkable, before November — and wait for the chime.

Wait for my chime. My forgery. My lie cast in bronze and brass.

There is a moment in every craft when the work leaves your hands and becomes either true or false, and no amount of wishing changes it afterward. Fenwick taught me that with my first escapement: *the file doesn't argue, boy. You file true or you file twice.* I thought of him then — disgraced, silent, stripped of the mastership he'd held forty years, sitting somewhere in the dark this morning knowing what I was doing and unable to lift a finger to help, having already lifted everything he had.

I put my eye to the regulator dial. The pendulum swung its patient arc. Somewhere below, the going train gathered the weight of the night and offered it up to the strike.

I pulled the lever.

* * *

The first stroke went out over the water like a door opening.

I mean that literally — I have stood in that clock room a thousand mornings and felt the bell as much as heard it, a pressure in the chest, a softening of the air, and this morning it sounded to me like every door in Vellamouth swinging wide at once. Five o'clock. Time to rise, time to light the fires, time for the inspectors on the fish quay to button their coats and step aboard the first hull in line.

Stroke two. Stroke three. Perfect, all three, spaced like scripture.

And then the secondary weights swung out, and the great machine began to lie.

I watched it happen with my hand flat against the frame, feeling the tempo change come up through the metal like a change in a pulse. The interval before the fourth stroke stretched — a half-second, a full second, a second and a half — and the note itself seemed to grow in the silence around it, the way a lamp seems brighter in a darkening room. By the sixth stroke the gaps were long enough that a listener might wonder. By the seventh, the harbor below had gone quiet in a way I had never heard it quiet — no boots on the quay, no calls between boats, just the bell and the water and several hundred people collectively deciding that the clock knew something they didn't.

That was the thing I hadn't planned for and will never forget: they *obeyed* it. Not the inspectors only — everyone. Dockhands stood with their hands in their armpits, faces turned up to the tower. A gull hung on the wind above the breakwater like a punctuation mark nobody dared remove. The whole port of Vellamouth held its breath inside a lie, because the lie wore the voice of the master clock, and the master clock had never lied to them before, and faith, it turns out, is just habit that hasn't been tested.

Stroke eight came so late that the dawn had visibly moved — the east gone from slate to the color of pewter left out in rain — and the sound of it rolled out past the breakwater and away.

And in that last long interval, through the tower window, I saw the *Kestrel* move.

She came off the fish quay without a light, without a sail, her little engine muttering under the last of the bell like a child talking during a sermon. Maren stood at the tiller with her hood back — she'd said the hood made her look furtive, and she was probably right, Maren was right about most practical things — and I could see the white smudge of her face turned once toward the tower, once, quickly, and then forward again toward the gap in the breakwater where the gray water stood open all the way to the gray horizon.

Seven minutes, forty seconds. The eighth stroke was still dying in the iron of the bell when she passed the outer beacon.

* * *

Now. Corven.

I did not see him that morning, and yet I will swear to my grave I saw him anyway, the way you see a character in a book you've read too many times. Because I know where he stood. I know it the way I know the count-wheel's teeth: by deduction, by craft, by three months of learning how the man's mind ticked.

He stood at the head of the fish quay, under the lamp that hadn't been put out yet, with his ledger in its oilcloth and his watch open in his palm. He had come early — of course he had come early; the man treated dawn the way misers treat coin — and when the first stroke fell he would have nodded, satisfied, the machine of the morning engaging on schedule.

When the fourth stroke failed to fall on time, he would have frowned.

When the fifth came a full two seconds late, he would have raised the watch to eye level.

And then — this is the part I know, the way you know a thing you were not present for, the way Elias knew the sea was turning by the taste of the wind — he began to count.

Not the strokes. Anyone counts the strokes. Corven counted the *intervals*. I could reconstruct it afterward from his own ledger, which I saw, later, at cost: columns of figures in that small ruthless handwriting, the spacing of nine strokes measured against the spacing the clock's rating certificate promised, the deviation plotted, the total duration written out in ink that did not tremble though I would stake my life the hand holding the pen wanted to.

*Chime commenced 05:00:00. Expected duration 01:31. Observed duration 08:12. Deviation non-random. Deviation progressive. Deviation engineered.*

Engineered. He wrote that word, or one like it, standing on the quay while a fishing boat with a fugitive in her hold slid through the breakwater gap under cover of the most beautiful lie the city's clocks ever told. And here is what I believe — what I have chosen to believe, since belief is all I was issued at the end of it — he hesitated.

Not from doubt about the facts. Corven never doubted facts; facts were his religion and his comfort. He hesitated because the bell sounded *beautiful*. Because whatever he had expected to catch this morning — a smuggler, a coward, a crack in the world he could mend with procedure — what he got instead was eight minutes of bronze thunder rolling out over a storm-washed harbor, stretching time itself like dough, and somewhere in the middle of it he understood that the sound was a message, and the message was addressed to him, and it said: *you are not the only one who can build a machine.*

He counted the strokes himself. I know he did, because he told me so, later, in the room with the high window, in the tone of a man confessing something he couldn't fit in any ledger: "Nine strokes. There should have been eight. I stood there counting, Hartwell, and the ninth stroke came so late I had already decided it wouldn't come at all, and when it did—" He stopped. He looked at his hands. "When it did, the boat was gone."

Nine strokes. I have lain awake over that a hundred times. Nine. My count-wheel delivered eight; the altered train, running slow past its designed stop, must have tripped the warning piece and given one stroke more than the hour asked for. An error. A flaw in my work, the kind Fenwick would have filed out and I missed, the kind that fails apprentices.

It saved us. The ninth stroke — the impossible stroke, the stroke that broke the hour's own arithmetic — was what finally told Corven, beyond any column of figures, that the clock had been tampered with by a human hand. And by the time he lowered his watch, the *Kestrel* was a smudge on the horizon heading for open water, and the patrol cutter at anchor in the roads had heard nothing but a long, strange, lovely chime, and the morning tide was carrying Elias Marchetti out of the country that had renamed him *enemy* with the same pen it used for fish quotas.

* * *

They say the last stroke was still humming in the tower iron when Corven turned from the quay and looked up at the observatory hill.

I was still in the clock room. I should have run — Fenwick's plan, Maren's plan, every sane plan, had me down the tide-tunnel steps and into the guild's warren before the bell finished speaking. But I found I couldn't leave. The altered train was still winding down, the secondary weights folding themselves away like wings, and someone had to stay and let the great clock finish what I had made it do. It seemed — I know how this sounds — it seemed rude otherwise. You don't make a machine lie for you and then walk out before it's done.

So I stood there with my hand on the warm brass of the frame, watching the pendulum settle back into truth, stroke by stroke, second by second, the old honest rhythm returning like a tide, and I thought: *whatever happens next, this morning the clock and I agreed on something.*

Then I heard boots on the tower stair. Measured ones. Unhurried. The boots of a man who had counted every step of the climb and knew exactly how many remained.

I did not turn around. I watched the pendulum swing, and I listened to the footsteps come up through the floor, and somewhere far out past the breakwater a small boat with a lighthouse keeper in her hold was making for the horizon at seven minutes, forty seconds' worth of speed.

The door opened behind me.

"Well, Hartwell," said Inspector Corven, and closed it softly, as though the room were a church. "Shall we discuss the ninth stroke?"
