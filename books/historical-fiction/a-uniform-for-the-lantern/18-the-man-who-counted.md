## 18. The Man Who Counted

"The ninth stroke," I said. "You heard it."

"I count everything, Hartwell." He crossed the room the way he did everything — by the shortest path, disturbing nothing. His coat was soaked through at the shoulders; he had walked up from the harbor in the rain without hurrying, and I understood then that he had known what he would find before he ever left the quay. "The master clock of Vellamouth Observatory has struck noon in eleven seconds since 1874. This morning it struck for eight minutes and change. I stood on the pier with my watch open and my hand on a signal flag, and I listened to a bell lie to me."

"Did you like the sound?"

He looked at me as if I had asked whether he liked breathing. "It was the most beautiful thing this city has produced in years," he said. "That is why I am here. Beautiful things are usually evidence."

The bells were still settling above us, the great hammers coming to rest one by one, each with its small dying shiver. The room smelled of oil and rain and hot metal. Somewhere below us, under the floor, through stone and tide-dark brick, a boat was carrying Elias Marchetti out past the last buoy, and every second that Corven stood here talking about bells was a second spent buying distance.

I did the arithmetic without meaning to. It is an occupational disease.

* * *

Corven took off his hat and set it on the workbench, which was either courtesy or theater, and with him I could never entirely rule out theater. He was not a large man. He had the build of a filing cabinet and the face of one too, if a cabinet could be disappointed. Rain dripped off his coat hem onto Fenwick's old floorboards, and he watched it pool there as though even the water should have registered itself somewhere.

"Let us save time," he said. "Time being, apparently, your specialty." He produced his notebook. "At seven minutes past twelve I requested the patrol cutter *Kestrel* hold position off the breakwater pending inspection of a vessel leaving harbor during a declared storm window. The request requires my countersignature within fifteen minutes of transmission. The harbormaster's clerk who transmitted it is named Pellowe, and Pellowe's mother has been ill since March, and her medicine costs more than Pellowe earns. I discovered all of this yesterday evening. I let him transmit anyway, because I wanted to see where the wire led." He turned a page. "It led to you."

"You're very thorough."

"I am very tired," said Corven, "and thoroughness is what tired men do instead of sleeping. Now. Where is Marchetti?"

"Gone."

"Gone where?"

I looked at him. He looked back, and for a moment neither of us performed anything at all, and I will say this for Inspector Corven: he could bear a silence better than any man I've met before or since. Most people fill silence the way a bad clockmaker fills a gap in the train — with whatever gear comes to hand, whether it fits or not. Corven simply let the silence run, and counted it.

"Out," I said finally. "Past recall. If the cutter sails now, it might still catch her. That's your decision to make, and I'm not going to beg you, because begging would insult us both. But I'll tell you how it was done, and who did it, and I'll put it in writing if you like. Every name. Mine first. Fenwick knew only enough to refuse me properly, which he did, and then helped me anyway, which is worse for him, so include him. Maren Fenwick lent the boat without knowing the whole of it — she thought she was running supplies to a sick friend, mostly true. Pellowe delayed the manifest. And the mechanism —" I touched the striking train behind me, still warm, still ticking down its long invented noon — "the mechanism is mine alone. Eight minutes of chime built by hand over four nights. No accomplice. No sanction. A guild offense, a statute offense, and, I'm given to understand, a moral one, though the statute doesn't list morals among its schedules."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you'll find it all anyway. You just said so. You're the sort of man who finds things." I made myself keep looking at him. "And because if the boat is going to get clear, it gets clearer while you stand here listening to a clockmaker confess. So take your time. Count something."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. It might have been distaste. With Corven it was hard to distinguish distaste from respect, possibly because he had never felt both separately.

* * *

He walked to the great dial and stood beneath it, hands clasped behind him, looking up at the works as though they were a witness he intended to question gently.

"Do you know what I did before the census?" he asked. Not turning around.

"No."

"I kept lighthouse records. Eleven years. Wicks, oil consumption, fog signals, the hours each keeper stood his watch. Do you know what a good record looks like, Hartwell? It looks like nothing. It looks like a column of identical numbers running down years. Nothing happens. Nobody dies. The light burns and the ships go where they're going and the ledger is dull as dishwater, and dullness, in my trade, is the whole of mercy." He turned. "Marchetti's records were perfect. Twelve years of perfect columns. Then the new statutes came, and a perfect record became suspicious, because a loyal subject complains, files grievances, drinks, quarrels — leaves marks. Perfection read as concealment. I wrote the memorandum myself recommending his classification." His voice did not change at all. "I have reread it four times since. It is well argued."

"He reported a smuggling run," I said. "Six years ago. You know."

"I know everything relevant to my files. Yes. He reported the *Cormorant*'s run, and the run was protected by men who now write statutes, and the cargo — which the newspapers never learned — included forty people in the hold, and eleven of them drowned when she was chased aground on the Shallows. Marchetti's report is why she was chased. He did the correct thing, by the law, and eleven people died of it, and the men who owned her were embarrassed, and embarrassment, in this city, is the only crime anyone punishes." He paused. "So no, Hartwell, I do not require a lecture on whether Marchetti deserves his fate. I have read his fate. I filed it."

"Then why—"

"Because the law does not ask me what I feel about it." He said it flatly, like reading a tariff. "That is what the law is *for*. It is a machine. Machines are merciful precisely because they do not care. When men begin deciding case by case whom the law should fit, you get favoritism, and then bribery, and then the strong decide the weak are enemies, and we have already tried that direction, thank you. Order is the only impartial kindness available to a man in my position. I believe that. I want you to understand that I believe it, so that you grasp fully what I am about to fail to do."

The pendulum swung. I realized I had been holding my breath and let it go slowly, through my nose, the way you learn to when your hands are inside a movement and shaking would cost you a pivot.

* * *

He took out his watch. Opened it. Looked at it longer than a look requires.

"The *Kestrel* can make the horizon in twenty minutes at full steam," he said. "In this sea, perhaps thirty. Your boat — whose boat? A fishing smack, Maren Fenwick's, I'd guess, the *Gannet*, eighteen feet, shallow draft, engine older than I am. She cleared the breakwater at—" he consulted the notebook — "seven minutes past twelve, under cover of your bell. It is now—" he looked up at the master clock's own dial, which had finally, obediently, resumed telling the truth — "twelve twenty-two. Fifteen minutes gone. At the *Gannet*'s best speed in that chop, she is nine miles out and opening. The cutter would need the luck of a cathedral to find her in rain and squall, and Captain Reyes of the *Kestrel* has the luck of a tax auditor." He closed the watch. "But that is not the point, is it. The point is that the order exists until I countersign or cancel it. Uncancelled, it stands. Someone else signs it tomorrow. The file stays open. Marchetti spends his life as a line in someone's ledger, hunted by men doing exactly what I do."

"Yes," I said.

"And if I cancel it — if I write *vessel inspected, papers in order, no action required* — then I have falsified a government document to defeat a deportation I personally recommended, and the only person in Vellamouth who will ever know is standing in front of me with grease on his cuffs, waiting to be arrested."

"Yes," I said again. My voice cracked on it, which I hated.

He studied me. "Why did you alter the chime instead of simply stopping it? Silence would have served as well. Better. A stopped clock draws one constable. Eight minutes of lying beauty drew me across the city in the rain."

I hadn't expected the question, and I answered it before I could make the answer presentable.

"Because stopping it would have been theft," I said. "Taking the sound away. Building it longer — giving the city eight extra minutes of its own bell, free, nobody hurt by it, everybody richer by it — that felt like the only way to break a thing and leave it whole. I don't know if that makes sense. My father said a repair should never leave a scar. I couldn't stop the clock. But I could make it generous."

For a long moment Corven said nothing. Rain ran down the tower windows. Below us the whole observatory ticked, ten thousand small honest parts keeping faith with each other, and I thought: this is the machine he loves. Not the statutes. This. He had been a lighthouse-records man. He had spent eleven years worshipping columns of identical numbers, and I had shown him a column that lied beautifully, and some part of him had stood on that pier with his watch open and his heart going like a hammer escapement, and he had called it beautiful, and he had come here to arrest the beauty or forgive it, and I do not think he knew which until this moment.

Neither did I.

* * *

He opened the notebook. Took out his pencil. And wrote.

I watched the pencil move. I am good at watching small mechanisms perform their function; it is my trade. I could not read the words upside down, but I could read the shape of them, the length of the lines, and I saw him pause once, strike out a word, continue. When he finished he tore the page out — tore it cleanly, along the margin, a man who keeps his files in order even when destroying them — folded it twice, and put it in his inner breast pocket, next to whatever heart he kept there.

"*Observation report,*" he said, in the tone of a man dictating to himself. "*Attended observatory re: irregular chime interval. Found master clock suffering from a fault in the striking train, since corrected. Cause attributed to wear and unauthorized maintenance. Clockmaker Hartwell questioned. No evidence of conspiracy. Recommend repair costs assessed against guild funds.*" He snapped the notebook shut. "Pellowe's wire will receive a cancellation citing rough seas and a captain's discretion. The *Gannet* will be logged as a storm-delayed fishing vessel returning late, papers in order. Marchetti's file—" and here his voice did change, very slightly, the way a bearing changes when it takes a load it wasn't rated for — "will note that the subject could not be located for service of the order, and that the matter is referred to ports unknown. Files die quietly when nobody feeds them. I have starved larger ones."

"Why?" It came out of me raw. "You believe in the machine. You told me. You *wrote the memorandum*."

"I did." He picked up his hat and held it, didn't put it on. "Eleven years ago a keeper with a perfect ledger flagged a ship to me, by lamp code, at three in the morning, because he believed the law should touch everyone equally. Including his friends. Including himself. I sat in this office building — different building, same chair — and I entered his report, and eleven people drowned because the machinery worked exactly as designed, impartially, without mercy, and I signed the transcript." He put on the hat at last, and settled it, precise as a gnomon. "I have been counting strokes ever since, Hartwell. Waiting for the number that balances. This morning a bell rang for eight minutes to save one man, and it was wrong, and unlawful, and it was the first stroke in nineteen years that sounded to me like the number might exist."

He walked to the door. Stopped with his hand on it.

"The guild council meets Thursday. There is a complaint against Fenwick already — anonymous, poorly typed, the usual cowardice. He will lose his mastership whatever I do; I cannot prevent that and would not insult him by trying. But I can ensure the inquiry concerns *him*, and only him. See that it does. And Hartwell —" he still hadn't turned around — "when they come for you, and they will come for you, because a cancellation is not an absolution and I am not a miracle worker, only a clerk — when they come, confess. All of it. Quickly. Take the weight yourself and give them nothing to pull on, because a clean confession closes faster than any cleverness, and speed, in sentencing, is the closest thing to mercy the courts sell."

"And you? What happens to you?"

"Nothing happens to me." For the first time all day, he smiled, and it was terrible. "Nothing has happened to me in nineteen years. That is the sentence I am serving." He opened the door. "The ninth stroke, incidentally. It was a half-tone flat. Your replacement hammer spring is two grams light. Even miracles, Hartwell, should be checked against the standard weights."

Then he was gone, and his footsteps went down the stairs counting themselves, and I stood alone in the ringing quiet with the truth of what he'd said landing on me one piece at a time: *they will come for you.*

Through the rain-blurred windows, far out past the breakwater, the sea lay gray and empty all the way to the edge of the world — empty, as far as any eye in Vellamouth could prove, and I pressed my hand flat against the cold glass as though I could feel an engine beating through it, nine miles out and opening, and I whispered the only prayer my father ever taught me:

*Keep good time.*

By Thursday, they came.
