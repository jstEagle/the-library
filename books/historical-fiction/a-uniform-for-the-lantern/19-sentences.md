## 19. Sentences

They tried me on a Tuesday, because Tuesdays were the day the magistrate's court cleared its docket of anything that could not be hanged.

I learned this from the turnkey, a broad man named Pell who had the habit of telling you things in the tone of a man discussing weather. He walked me down the corridor of the Assize annex at half past eight, my wrists cuffed in front of me — a courtesy, he said, since I was "a trade lad" and might need to gesture. I had not known that gestures were permitted at one's own trial. I resolved to use them sparingly.

The courtroom smelled of dust and lamp oil and the particular sourness of men who had been awake since dawn deciding other men's futures. The gallery was half full. A clerk read the charges in a voice like a man reading a laundry list: *unauthorized alteration of civic timekeeping apparatus; obstruction of an officer of the Crown; aiding the unlawful departure of a declared enemy national.* Three charges. It sounded almost respectable when said aloud, like something that might appear in a guild syllabus.

*Unauthorized alteration.* As though I had graffitied it. As though the striking train had not been rebuilt with more care than anything I had ever touched before or since. If they were going to convict me, I thought, they might at least have charged me with doing it *well.*

Inspector Corven sat at the prosecution table with his ledger squared to the edge of the desk. He did not look at me. That was the worst of it, in those first minutes — not his accusation but his indifference, as if I had already been entered into his book, categorized, and closed. I searched his face for the man who had stood at the harbor with the wind pulling at his coat, who had counted eleven strokes of a bell that should have struck four, and who had watched a fishing boat dwindle into rain without raising his hand to the patrol cutter.

He was there somewhere. But in the courtroom he was only paper wearing a man's body.

"Prisoner at the bar," said the magistrate, a dry stick named Aldous Wray whose spectacles sat so low on his nose he seemed to be addressing them rather than me, "you have declined counsel."

"I have," I said.

"You understand that this is unwise."

"I understand that any man who would defend me would have to explain why I did it, and I'd rather explain it myself. Badly, perhaps. But myself."

A rustle went through the gallery, the sound of people deciding whether they'd been offered entertainment. Wray peered over his spectacles at me properly for the first time.

"Then explain it."

And here is what I have learned about courts: they do not want the truth. They want a truth shaped like their forms. I gave them mine anyway, standing there with my cuffs and my apprentice's hands still blackened at the creases with brass polish that no amount of scrubbing would fully lift.

"I made a promise to a man who pulled me out of the harbor when I was nine years old," I said. "My father made me swear it on his deathbed. In peacetime, a debt of honor. Then your statutes arrived and turned him into ink — a category, a number chalked on his door — and I found that the promise hadn't shrunk to fit. So I kept it. At the size it was made."

"By tampering with the master clock."

"By making the master clock tell a lie for eight minutes." I looked at Corven then. He was writing. Of course he was writing. "It was the finest work I've ever done. I'd do it again tomorrow, and slower, so it rang longer."

Wray removed his spectacles entirely, which I gathered was his version of throwing up his hands.

"Young man, do you comprehend what you are saying? You are confessing to the court with pride."

"Yes, sir. It seemed dishonest to bring anything else."

There was a laugh somewhere in the gallery, quickly strangled. Corven's pen did not stop moving.

The trial took two hours and forty minutes, which even by Vellamouth standards was brisk. The evidence was overwhelming and largely supplied by me. A constable testified to finding me in the bell chamber with the striking train disassembled around me like the carcass of some great brass animal I'd been caught butchering. The harbormaster's clerk — bribed, terrified, weeping — confirmed that he had warned me of the dawn inspection. Maren Fenwick was called, and I watched her walk to the witness box with her chin up and her father's storm-gray eyes daring anyone in the room to look at her sideways.

She told the truth, mostly. She had lent her family's boat. She had sailed it herself through weather no sane woman would cross a millpond in. She had done it, she said, "because Elias Marchetti logged my father's first apprenticeship passage when Father was fourteen and seasick, and never once mentioned it after." She paused. "We keep accounts differently in this city than the Crown does."

Wray asked her whether she understood she might face charges herself. She said she understood, and that she would answer for it gladly, and that she hoped the court would hurry, because there were nets to mend and the tide didn't wait on magistrates.

They let her go. Whether out of mercy or because prosecuting a fisherman's daughter in front of a gallery full of fishermen struck someone upstairs as poor arithmetic, I never learned.

Then came the sentence, delivered by Wray in the tone of a man reciting a recipe he disliked.

The apprenticeship: revoked. My indenture papers, seven years of my life folded into a document with a wax seal, declared void. Master Hollis — my own master, who had sent me alone to the observatory that first day with a bag of files and a pat on the shoulder — did not attend. I don't know which hurt worse, his absence or my understanding of it. A man with a shop and three other apprentices cannot afford to be seen loving a traitor. I have forgiven him. It took some years and required several attempts.

In place of the trade I was barred from practicing, the court granted me labor: two years' service in the workhouse ward, repairing the public clocks of the parish — the ones above the post office, the market hall, the gaol itself, all the dull municipal faces that told the city when to pay its rates and when its prisoners ate. Two years of keeping time for the very machinery that had ground Elias up and very nearly me.

"The court notes," Wray added, setting down his papers, "the prisoner's evident skill. It is wasted here. But waste, Mr. Hartwell, is frequently the price of principle. You may consider yourself fortunate the price was not higher."

"Thank you, sir," I said. And because I apparently could not leave well enough alone: "For the record, it wasn't wasted. The clock rang beautifully."

They took me down while the gallery murmured. On the way out, passing the rail, I caught Corven's eye at last. He looked tired. He looked, I thought, like a man who had gotten everything he asked for and found the ledger wouldn't balance regardless. He inclined his head a fraction — a bow so small it might have been a trick of the light, might have been nothing, might have been the closest thing to absolution a man built of paper could issue.

Then the stairs, and the cell, and the beginning of two years.

* * *

The workhouse ward was not so bad as its reputation, which I credit entirely to the clocks.

They put me in a long room on the third floor with north light — good light, honest light, the kind a craftsman will forgive a great deal for — and a bench, and a crate of tools confiscated from better days. My keeper was a warder named Brack, a taciturn man with a walrus mustache and a key ring that announced him from thirty paces. For the first week he watched me the way one watches a dog of uncertain temper. By the second week he was bringing me his mother's mantel clock, which struck thirteen and had done so for a decade, driving the whole street quietly mad.

"It's not haunted," he said, setting it on my bench with the gravity of a man presenting evidence.

"No," I agreed, opening the case. "It's just been lied to. Someone replaced the escape wheel with one from a different movement and never told the rest of the gears. Now every hour it tries to make sense of a story that doesn't add up." I looked up at him. "Most things that seem haunted are just badly maintained."

He grunted. From Brack, a sonnet.

The public clocks came to me in pieces, carted up from their towers by men who handled them like relics. The post office clock ran slow in damp weather because its pendulum rod was plain steel, and I fitted a compensated gridiron from salvaged rods and felt, for the first time in weeks, the old pleasure settle back into my hands — that click when a mechanism comes right, the small domestic thunder of a thing agreeing with itself. I mended the market hall clock. I mended the gaol clock, which struck me as a private joke the universe had arranged at my expense, and I mended it *well*, because whatever else they took from me, they would not take the standard of my work.

That was my rebellion now. Small, precise, invisible. Every clock I set true was a word in a language the court couldn't prosecute.

Other prisoners began to find me. First cautiously, then in a queue. A pickpocket with a dead father's pocket watch. An old debtors' man with a carriage clock worth more than his freedom had ever been. They brought me broken heirlooms wrapped in handkerchiefs, and I mended them, and something passed between us in the mending that none of us named — the sense that a thing broken by time could be reconciled with it, which was a doctrine none of us could afford to believe about ourselves and so believed about watches instead.

But I'm ahead of my story. All of that came later, in its proper chapter. What came first was Maren, and then the file-blade.

* * *

Visits were Thursdays, through wire glass, in a room that smelled of boiled cabbage and disinfectant locked in a losing war with both. The wire was embedded in the glass like a net frozen mid-catch, and it divided Maren's face into small diamonds, so that looking at her was like looking at sunlight through a fishing net — appropriate, I supposed, and cruel.

"You look terrible," she said, sitting down.

"You look wonderful. It's deeply unfair."

"They feed you badly?"

"They feed me *regularly*, which is worse. Regularity without quality. Like being married to a metronome."

She laughed, and the sound came through whatever intercom arrangement served in place of open air flattened and tinny, and I hated the glass for cheapening it. Her hair was pinned back with a bit of netting. There was a smear of tar or pitch on her wrist that she hadn't bothered to wash off, or had washed off insufficiently, and I loved her for it with an ache that surprised me in its size.

"The boat's fine," she said. "Netted and re-caulked. Father's — " she stopped, corrected course. "Father sends his regards."

"How is he?"

"Sitting in his shop with his mastership revoked, drinking tea, and calling it retirement." She said it lightly, but the lightness had ballast in it. Fenwick had taken the council's censure without a word of defense — had, I learned later, refused the chance to name me, refused it with a silence so complete that even the men who voted to strip him left the chamber unsettled. His disgrace was my inheritance. He had paid it forward like a debt, the way debts were paid in our trade: to the next pair of hands.

"Tell him—" I started, and found the sentence had no end that fit through wire glass. *Tell him thank you* was too small. *Tell him I'll repay him* was impossible. *Tell him the clock rang beautifully* was true but inadequate, the way most true things are.

"He knows," Maren said. "He watches your gaol clock from the corner by the chandlery every day at noon. Says it's the best-kept clock in the parish and that everyone assumes it's civic pride." She smiled. "He knows exactly whose pride it is."

We talked of small things, because the large things would not fit through the glass: the weather, the price of brass, a rumor that the emergency statutes were already being whispered about in the papers as *excessive*, too late for Elias and for hundreds whose names I'd never know. She told me the lighthouse had gone dark, its lamp auctioned, its logbook — Elias's logbook, the one he'd carried out in his coat — mourned by no official record whatsoever.

"He'd have liked that," I said. "Going unmourned officially. It means the officials finally got something wrong about him completely."

When the warder called time, Maren pressed her palm flat against the glass. I matched it. The wire diamonds cut our hands into fragments, but the pressure came through whole, and I carried it back down the corridor like contraband.

"I'll be here every Thursday," she said, standing. "Don't make it interesting by dying or anything."

"No promises. I work with springs under tension."

"That's what worries me."

* * *

The file-blade came in October, six weeks in, wrapped in cloth and addressed in handwriting I knew better than my own.

Brack brought it to the bench with visible suspicion. "Says here it's a tool for your trade. Ward regulations permit trade tools to approved laborers." He squinted at the slip. "You're approved. Barely. Don't make me regret the paperwork."

It was a flat file, eight inches, single-cut — the kind used for truing escapement teeth, the kind a master keeps for forty years until the teeth of the file themselves wear soft and it becomes less a tool than a companion. The cloth wrapping was gray wool, good wool, the kind of coat a man might sacrifice a lining to. Inside, along the spine of the file, someone had stamped a mark so small I nearly missed it: the guild's old cipher, the crossed wheels, defaced with a single chisel stroke. Struck off the rolls. A master's mark, cancelled.

Fenwick had sent me his own file, and with it his own mark, broken.

I sat with it a long time. Around me the workshop clattered and muttered — the ward clock ticking its patient count, another prisoner whittling something pointless and necessary, rain starting against the high windows. I unwound the wool and held the file to the light and thought about what it meant, and what it cost, and whether gratitude was a heavy enough word for a gift that said *you are still one of us, whatever they call you.*

Then I noticed the second thing, the thing he must have known I'd find, because Fenwick never taught a lesson without hiding it where only the diligent would bleed for it.

Wrapped within the wool, folded to the size of a postage stamp, was a scrap of paper. On it, in pencil, in the tight drafting hand of a man who lettered gear diagrams:

*The lantern-clock pattern is in your head. Nothing revokes that. Build it.*

Below it, a diagram after all — small, precise, the going train of a lantern-clock, the oldest and humblest form of our craft, the clock that hangs in kitchens and lighthouse galleries and keeps time for people who cannot afford grandeur. And beside the escape wheel, a note in the margin: *Set it to 4–8–12. Lighthouse watch changes. He'll know the rhythm wherever he is. So will you.*

I put the paper in my mouth—no. I didn't. I wanted to; some animal part of me wanted to destroy it instantly, swallow the evidence. Instead I did what a craftsman does with dangerous knowledge: I memorized it, line by line, tooth by tooth, and then I fed it to the stove in the corner and watched the flame take it, and felt nothing but calm.

Because Fenwick was right. The pattern was in my head. Seven years of indenture, a trial, a sentence, a stripped mastership — none of it reached that far. They could revoke my papers, but not my hands' memory. They could give me two years of municipal drudgery, and I would spend them, gear by salvaged gear, building a small brass clock set to the rhythm of a man's night watches, a clock that would strike eight strokes at the top of every evening shift change for as long as brass remembers how.

That night, lying in my bunk in the long ward, listening to forty men breathe and the gaol clock below striking ten — true, now, thanks to me — I turned the plan over in the dark the way I'd once turned a difficult wheel, feeling for the flaw.

Salvage: the ward had bins of broken movements. Brass enough. Wire enough.

Time: evenings, after labor hours, if Brack could be managed. Brack, whose mother's clock I had healed, and who owed me the way quiet men owe — heavily and forever.

Risk: everything, always, but I was learning that risk was simply the interest charged on love, and I had decided to carry the account.

I fell asleep composing the frame in my head, upright pillars and a domed top like a little house for time, and if my last waking thought was of Elias standing some unknown watch on some unknown sea, hearing — impossibly, across all that water — eight strokes and knowing, then I was not the first craftsman to fall asleep believing a clock could carry a voice further than the law could follow.

The next morning I asked Brack, very casually, what the ward did with its bin of dead clock movements.

He looked at me for a long moment over the walrus mustache. Then he jerked his thumb toward the corner, where a crate sat gathering dust, full of the parish's broken time.

"Rubbish," he said. "Take what you like."
