## 20. The Lantern Clock

The workhouse ward clock ran four minutes slow, and I was the only man in Vellamouth who knew it and could do nothing about it.

That is a particular species of torment, gentlemen — knowing a thing to the second and being forbidden to touch it. They gave me the tools to repair it. That was the sentence, after all: two years' labor mending the public clocks of the workhouse ward, under supervision, for the edification of the magistrates who believed that a man corrected by machinery would learn to correct himself. What they did not understand — what no magistrate has ever understood — is that you cannot give a clockmaker a bench and expect him to stay within the borders of your paperwork.

But I am ahead of myself. Let me describe my kingdom first.

It was a room at the back of the ward workshops, twelve feet by ten, with one north-facing window that admitted a gray light perfect for judging metal and useless for everything else. A bench scarred by forty years of other men's vices. A vice, in fact, bolted to it. A small coal stove that smoked when the wind came from the east, which in Vellamouth is most of the time. And on the wall above the bench, because the wardens permitted it and never asked why, a printed card with the lighthouse shift rotations of 1911 — salvaged from the harbor office during my first week, when they were clearing out old papers to make room for new ones about enemy nationals.

I had asked the warden if I might keep it. He looked at me the way men look at things they have decided not to think about.

"Sentimental value," I said.

"It's a duty roster."

"Everything sentimental is a duty roster, sir. That's rather the point of sentiment."

He let me keep it. Wardens are practical men, and practical men mistake stubbornness for harmlessness, which is the most useful confusion available to a prisoner.

* * *

The first month I did exactly as instructed. I oiled the ward clock's escapement. I trued its pendulum. I brought it from four minutes slow to two seconds fast, which is where a public clock ought to live — slightly eager, like an apprentice. The wardens were pleased. Pleased wardens are careless wardens. Within three weeks I had catalogued every scrap of brass in the scrap bin: broken carriage-clock plates, a smashed alarm movement, the gutted remains of a mantel clock someone had dropped down a stairwell with more enthusiasm than aim.

From these orphans I began, at night, after hours, by the light of a candle stub I was technically stealing, to build a lantern clock.

You know the form, perhaps — those early English clocks shaped like a house with wings, brass posts at the corners, a bell on top like a hat worn at a jaunty angle. Three hundred years old as a design and still honest. No hidden works, no fancy dials. Everything visible, everything plain, everything doing exactly what it appears to do. In a world that had recently taught me otherwise, there was comfort in a machine that could not lie about its own construction.

I built it small. Six inches tall if it stood up straight, and it would stand up straight, because I would see to it or die trying. The frame came from the carriage-clock plates, filed and joined at the corners with pins drawn from wire I annealed over the stove. The going train came from the alarm clock — rough work, the wheels cut coarse, but I re-cut the teeth file by file until they meshed like a whispered secret. For the escapement I used the anchor from the stairwell mantel clock, polished on a scrap of chamois until it gleamed like something rescued rather than salvaged, which is what all salvage is, when you come down to it.

The bell took longest. A lantern clock wants a proper bell, and the workhouse offered me nothing but cracked ones. Then one gray Tuesday the laundry matron appeared at my door holding a small handbell by its dead handle.

"This hasn't rung since the old queen died," she said. "Can you do anything?"

I turned it over. Good bronze, thick-walled, cracked along one flank — which meant it would not ring true, but it would ring *low*, with a darkness in the voice that no foundry could manufacture. Some bells are ruined by cracking into something better than they were. I have known men the same way.

"I can do something," I said. "I can't promise it'll be pretty."

"Nothing here needs to be pretty," she said, and looked around my cell of a workshop at the candle stub and the stolen warmth and the card of lighthouse shifts on the wall, and added, quieter: "Some things just need to go on."

She left the bell. I hung it on top of the frame like a crown on a very small, very patient king.

Now — the part that matters. Any fool can build a clock that keeps Greenwich time. Greenwich time was not what I wanted. I wanted *lighthouse time*, which is not a time at all but a rhythm: Elias Marchetti's shifts, kept for nineteen years at the Vellamouth Light. On at dusk. Off at midnight for his supper and a pipe. On again till dawn, with the lamp wound at the top of every third hour, and the great Fresnel lens turned by its clockwork drive — yes, the light itself was clockwork; people forget that — wound and released, wound and released, night upon night, so that ships that had never heard of him lived because of his regularity.

So I set the striking train to count not the hours but his watch-bells. Eight strokes at the turn of his long watch. Four at supper. Two at the winding of the lamp. A clock that struck a schedule only one living man had ever kept, in a city that had erased him from its ledgers with a stroke of ink and called it law.

When it was done — eleven weeks of nights — I set it running and listened to it strike eight for the first time, low and dark and slightly cracked, and I put my head down on my arms on the cold bench and stayed there until the stove went out.

The warden found me like that in the morning.

"Sleeping on duty?"

"Listening, sir."

"To what?"

"A clock that keeps the right time," I said, "for once in this city."

He confiscated nothing. Practical men. God preserve us from them, and God provide us with them.

* * *

Word travels through a workhouse the way water travels through chalk: slowly, invisibly, and arriving everywhere at once. Within a fortnight of the lantern clock striking its first eight, the offerings began.

A bootblack named Cobb appeared first, shuffling, holding out a pocket watch wrapped in a rag as though it were a sleeping bird. "My father's. Stopped the day he went into the infirmary. They say you fix things."

"They say correctly."

"How much?"

"Nothing. Bring me the story instead. Why does it matter that it runs?"

He looked at me as though I'd asked him why he breathed. "It was ticking the day I was born. I want it ticking the day I get out."

"That will do," I said, and meant it.

After Cobb came the others. A debtor with his grandmother's carriage clock, its case dented where, he swore, it had stopped a falling poker during an argument he declined to detail. A poacher with a marine chronometer of frankly criminal provenance, which I repaired without asking questions, on the principle that a chronometer is innocent even when its owner is not. An old woman from the women's side sent across a hair locket whose tiny hinge had failed, containing a curl of hair from a son who had, the note said, gone to sea. She did not say which sea, or whether the sea had given him back. I fixed the hinge and did not ask. There is a whole discipline of mercy that consists entirely of not asking.

Here is what I learned in that workshop, and I set it down now because it took me two years to learn it and I would spare you the tuition:

Craft is endurance. Not endurance *of* craft — I mean the thing itself. When a man cannot change his circumstances, cannot change his sentence, cannot change the fact that the world has decided what he is with a signature and a stamp — he can still change a wheel's center distance by half a millimeter and hear the difference. The bench is the one country where effort and result still speak to each other honestly. Every other institution in my life at that moment — the courts, the guild, the newspapers full of war fever — operated on the principle that words could remake reality. The escapement disagreed. The escapement cared only for truth. It was the last honest conversation available to me, and I had it daily, and it kept me sane, or in the neighborhood of sane, which in a workhouse passes for the same thing.

And memory is mechanism. I do not mean that prettily. I mean that a memory, properly maintained, functions: it drives something. Elias's shift-bells drove my little clock. My father's dying voice drove the promise that had cost me everything. Maren's face — but I will come to Maren. The point is that grief, unmechanized, is merely weight. Grief fitted to a train of gears becomes motion. You wind it every morning and it moves you forward, and the striking of it tells anyone within earshot: something happened here that mattered, and it is still counting.

* * *

In the spring of the second year, the file-blade arrived.

Fenwick sent no letter. Masters of the Clockmakers' Guild, even stripped ones, do not write letters to convicts; it would be noted. But a parcel came through the ordinary channels — cloth-wrapped, addressed in a clerk's indifferent hand to "T. Hartwell, laborer" — and inside the cloth lay a single file-blade, six-inch, Swiss cut, the finest tool I had ever held and the cheapest thing Fenwick could have posted without explanation.

I understood it completely. A file-blade says: *you are still a craftsman.* Not a prisoner, not a traitor, not a name in a magistrate's ledger. A man who files metal. It also said, in the language between master and apprentice that requires no grammar: *I have not disowned you. I was silent when silence was the price of your life, and I would be silent again.*

I have that blade still. It is worn to half its width. Some inheritances are houses; mine fit in an envelope.

Cobb watched me wrap it back in its cloth with the reverence of a man handling a relic. "Who sent it?"

"A man who lost his mastership so I could keep mine."

"That's a bad trade."

"It's the oldest trade there is," I said. "One generation pays, the next one works."

* * *

Maren visited through the wire glass on the first Thursday of every month, rain or shine, and in Vellamouth mostly rain. Forty minutes, a telephone receiver apiece, her voice coming through the grille slightly flattened, as though the state had decided even love should pass through a filter.

She never cried. That was our agreement, unspoken but iron. Crying through wire glass is a currency the wardens collect, and we refused to mint it.

Instead she told me the news of the shop she was keeping alive above the tide-tunnel entrance — my shop, ours now, though the deed still bore complications she was fighting with a solicitor's patience and a fisherman's vocabulary. She told me which customers had stayed loyal and which had developed sudden religious objections to convicted felons. She told me Fenwick's health, and the guild council's small-mindedness, and the price of coal, and once — looking directly at me through the glass with an expression I have spent years failing to find adequate words for — she told me about a letter.

"No address," she said. "Postmarked Rotterdam, or somewhere pretending to be Rotterdam. Inside, a slip of paper. Nothing written on it but a number."

"What number?"

"Eight."

I sat very still inside my own body. Through the grille her face waited, patient as a pendulum.

"Elias," I said. Not a question. A man does not question the striking of a clock he built himself.

"He's alive," Maren said. "And he remembers."

Eight strokes. The turn of the long watch. Somewhere beyond the reach of Inspector Corven and his immaculate ledgers, beyond the statutes and the deportation ships and the whole paper machinery that had eaten two years of my life, a man I had once promised to save was telling me, in the only cipher we shared, that he was keeping his own time still — lighthouse time, watch-bell time — and that he had not forgotten who wound the clock for him.

I went back to my bench that afternoon and worked until lights-out, and the little lantern clock on its shelf struck eight into the dark of the workhouse, low and cracked and true, and eleven men in their cots heard it and did not know what it meant, and one man did, and that was enough.

Then, the following Thursday, Maren leaned close to the wire glass, and her voice dropped to almost nothing, and she said five words that pulled the floor out from under the next two years of my sentence:

"There's been another letter. Theo —"

The bell rang, ending visits, before she could finish.

I stood there with the receiver pressed to my ear, listening to the dial tone where her voice should have been, and thought: whatever she has to tell me, it is either wonderful or terrible, and in my experience the two arrive wearing each other's coats.

I had eleven months left to serve. Suddenly it felt like a life sentence handed to a man standing on a platform, watching a train leave without knowing who was aboard.

The lantern clock ticked behind me, keeping Elias's hours. Whatever came next, it would come on time.

Things always did.
