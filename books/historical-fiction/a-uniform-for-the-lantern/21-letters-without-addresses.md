## 21. Letters Without Addresses

They released me on a Tuesday in March, which I have always considered a small mercy. A Monday release would have meant walking out of the workhouse gate and straight into the week's obligations, and a man who has spent two years winding other people's clocks for the price of his bread deserves at least one afternoon of doing absolutely nothing measurable.

The gate itself was unremarkable — iron bars, a ledger, a clerk who handed me my old coat and asked me to sign for my own name as though I might dispute it. The coat smelled of storage and camphor and, faintly, of the workshop where I had last worn it. I put it on and found in the pocket a single brass screw I did not remember leaving there. I kept it. You would be surprised what a man can build from one screw, given enough years.

Maren was waiting outside.

Not dramatically — no running across cobbles, no weeping. She stood by the wall with her arms folded against the wind, wearing a coat I didn't recognize and a expression I did: the particular patience of a woman who has been waiting somewhere inconvenient for two years and has decided to be gracious about it for exactly four more seconds.

"You're late," she said.

"Two years," I said. "I know."

"I mean you came out the wrong gate. The clerk said the east gate."

"The east gate is for men going back in." I looked at her, and all the speeches I had composed through the wire glass — and there had been many, revised and polished like gear trains — all of them fell apart at once. What came out instead was: "You cut your hair."

"You went to prison," she said. "We'll compare notes inside."

She took my arm as though it were the most ordinary thing in the world, and we walked down toward the harbor, and I discovered that after two years of measuring time in bells and ration counts and the slow erosion of a file-blade's edge, the simplest unit of time was her shoulder against mine. Everything else could be derived from that.

* * *

The shop sat above the tide-tunnel entrance, which sounds romantic until you live above it and learn that twice a day the sea exhales through the arch below your floorboards with a sound like a giant clearing his throat before making a complaint. Fenwick had arranged the lease before his fall — before, I should say, he was summoned before the guild council and said nothing, and said nothing, and said nothing until they took his mastership from him like pulling a tooth from a jaw that wouldn't open. He never told them my name. He never told them anything. When I visited him afterward, he waved off my gratitude with the air of a man declining a second helping.

"Don't thank me," he said. "Thanking implies it cost me something worth counting. Now hand me that file. No — the *good* file. Yes. That one. That's the only thing of value either of us owns now, and I intend to die owning it."

He sent it to me anyway, later. Wrapped in cloth, in a box with no note. The file-blade from the workhouse years, worn thin at the tip from two years of my hands. I mounted it above the shop bench where I could see it, the way some men hang a saint's picture. Mine had teeth.

The shop itself was modest in the way that things are modest when you build them from salvage and stubbornness. A bench under the window. A rack of pliers sorted by grip-wear rather than size, because that is how Maren organized them and because she was right. A sign painted by a signwriter who charged me double and spelled *Hartwell* correctly, so we were even. And beneath the floor, through a trapdoor I kept locked and Maren kept pretending not to notice, the mouth of the old tide-tunnel, breathing its twice-daily breath.

Customers came slowly at first, then not slowly. Word travels in Vellamouth the way water finds a low place. A widow brought her dead husband's carriage clock. A fisherman brought a barometer that had survived three groundings. An old woman brought a watch her son had carried to a war I preferred not to ask about, and when I opened it I found the movement full of salt corrosion and a curl of her hair tucked behind the dial, and I worked on it for six hours without charging her, because some repairs are not about the mechanism.

I became known, gradually, as the clockmaker who asked questions. Not many. Just enough. How long since it ran? Did it stop suddenly or slow? Who wound it last? People thought this was craft. It was partly craft. It was also partly a man learning, very carefully, how much time can be carried in an object, and whether any of it could be sent across an ocean in an envelope.

Because the letters began.

* * *

The first one came eleven months after my release. Postmarked Lisbon — or stamped with a Lisbon mark, at any rate; I learned early not to trust postmarks further than I could throw the post office. No return address. Inside, a plain sheet, and on it, in a hand I knew the way you know a voice through a wall:

**VIII**

That was all. Eight strokes.

I stood in the shop holding it for a long while. Outside, a gull made its opinion known. Below the floorboards, the tide exhaled.

Eight strokes. The chime I had held open on the morning tide — stretched, bent, made to lie beautifully for eight minutes while a fishing boat slipped past the breakwater with Elias Marchetti aboard. Eight minutes. Eight strokes. He was telling me he remembered. That he was alive, and moving, and that somewhere in whatever port the letter had truly come from, a man who once kept a lighthouse was keeping count.

I wrote back, of course. To Lisbon. I addressed it care of the harbor master, then tore it up, because a letter to a harbor master is a letter to whoever reads over the harbor master's shoulder. There was nowhere to send anything. That was the design of it, and I came to admire the design the way I admire any mechanism built well by someone with limited materials: no address, no name, no route to trace. Just a number, arriving like light off a lamp too far out to see the tower.

The letters came perhaps three times a year. Never the same port twice in a row — Genoa, then a stamp I couldn't identify, then Alexandria, then Buenos Aires, which made me laugh out loud alone in the shop, because Elias had once told me, hauling me half-drowned out of the harbor twelve years before any of this, that if he ever left Vellamouth it would be somewhere the sun got honest work to do. Buenos Aires. He'd done it.

Each contained only the count. **VIII.** Always eight. Once — only once — **VII**, and I did not sleep that night, and Maren found me at the bench at dawn re-reading it, and she put her hand flat on the paper and said, "Seven strokes means the ship was late, Theo. Or the pen slipped. Or he was being careful. It does not mean what your face says it means." She was right, of course. The next letter was eight again, and I framed my relief as indifference, and she let me get away with it, which is its own kind of love.

* * *

We married in the spring, small and damp, in the chapel behind the fish market, because Maren refused to marry anywhere the smell of money might reach us. Fenwick attended — old now, moving like a clock with a worn pallet, but present, upright, wearing his old mastership chain under his coat where nobody could see it except everybody, which was entirely the point. He gave us a gift wrapped in newspaper: a small anvil, jeweler's size, for staking balance wheels.

"For the shop," he said. "And for whatever else gets built on top of it."

Maren ran the accounts and, within a year, effectively ran the shop, though we maintained the fiction otherwise for the sake of my pride, which she described as "a movement that runs fine but loses two minutes a day." She learned to true a hairspring. She learned to hear a bad escapement the way sailors hear weather. Customers began asking for "the missus" by name, and I pretended to mind, and she pretended to believe me, and between the two pretenses we built something neither of us had expected: a life with load-bearing walls.

Some nights, when the tide breathed under the floor and the lamps were low, she'd find me at the bench working on nothing in particular — a practice escapement, a broken alarm clock bought purely for the pleasure of fixing it — and she'd say, "Which port this time?" without looking up from her ledger, because she always knew when a letter had come. I never hid them from her. I kept them in the drawer where I kept my father's knife and the debt-paper I'd signed as a boy, and she understood that the drawer was not a hiding place but an archive, which is different.

"Do you think he reads our answers?" she asked once. "If we ever managed to send any?"

"He doesn't need answers," I said. "A lighthouse doesn't need the ships to write back. It just needs to know they're passing."

"That's the loneliest thing you've ever said, and you once said 'I'm fine' for two years straight."

* * *

The workhouse ward had taught me patience with broken things, but the years taught me patience with whole ones — with days that passed without incident, with prosperity that arrived slowly and honestly, with the slow settling of a reputation like sediment in still water. Men brought their fathers' watches. Mothers brought clocks that had marked christenings and funerals and everything between. Once a man brought a ship's chronometer that had belonged to a captain lost off the Skerries, and when I opened it I found the maker's mark of a house in Geneva and thought: somewhere, some apprentice in that house, seventy years ago, fitted this pallet stone without knowing whose life would one day depend on his exactness. That is the whole trade in a sentence. You work for people you will never meet, on moments you will never see.

I thought of that every time a letter came. Somewhere Elias was living inside my eight strokes the way I lived inside his — a shared mechanism, no address required, both of us winding it daily from opposite ends of the world.

Once, a customer — a young merchant with quick eyes and a foreign accent he'd sanded smooth — lingered over the counter after collecting his repaired watch, and said, quietly, "There's a man in the coffeehouses near the docks who tells a story about a bell that rang too long. Eight minutes. He swears he heard it himself, from the water." He met my eyes. "He says the clockmaker must have loved somebody very much."

"What do you tell him?" I asked.

"Nothing. I listen. Stories like that keep better than watches." He tipped his hat and left, and I stood there a long time with my hands on the bench, understanding that the escape had become legend, worn smooth by retelling, and that somewhere in the telling Elias was safe precisely because he was unbelievable.

Maren found the drawer letters eventually — no, I showed her, deliberately, the winter our daughter was born. I had decided that a household should contain its whole truth, even the parts kept in drawers. She read each one in order, slowly, the way she read everything, and when she finished she said, "Buenos Aires. Good for him." Then, after a moment: "He'll want to know about her."

"He knows what matters. He's alive. He's moving. He remembers."

"Yes," she said. "But Theo — someday he might want more than eight strokes. Someday he might want a name."

I didn't answer then. But I began, that night, a habit I kept for years: after each letter, I set our daughter's small lantern-clock — the one I built from salvage during the long nights of her infancy — to run a minute fast, and then corrected it the next morning. A private signal, meaningless to anyone else. Time, adjusted by hope.

* * *

Years compress the way coal compresses: under weight, into something that burns longer. The shop prospered modestly. My hair went gray at the temples, which Maren claimed improved me and I chose to believe. The tide-tunnel below kept breathing. Fenwick died in the autumn of the year the new breakwater opened, and we buried him with the good file's twin — he'd owned two, naturally, and had opinions about which one deserved the honor — and at the graveside I said nothing, which he would have called the finest eulogy available.

And then, on an ordinary gray morning in a year I will not trouble you with yet, the eighth letter came — and it was different.

Same paper stock as always. Same firm hand. But heavier. Thicker, when I weighed it on the bench scale out of pure habit. And when I turned it over, I saw why: pressed flat against the inside, protected by a sleeve of oilcloth folded with a sailor's economy, was a small object.

A brass wheel. A strike-train wheel, by its shape and tooth count — sixty-four teeth, lifted-wheel, the pattern used in tower clocks of a certain generation. The observatory's generation. Its edge was worn, its pivot hole slightly ovalized from decades of turning. Someone had taken it from a dismantled movement and kept it, and kept it for years, and mailed it across the world without a word of explanation beyond the usual inked figure:

**VIII**

I held it up to the window light. And there, stamped faintly near the rim, almost effaced by wear, I saw the maker's proof mark — the little anchor-and-crown that the Vellamouth foundry struck into every wheel it cast for the observatory works.

It was a piece of the master clock. Our master clock. The one I had rebuilt in the dark with terror in my hands. Which meant someone had taken the great clock apart — or was taking it apart — somewhere far from here, and had chosen this single toothed ring of brass to send home to me, like a man sending a splinter of a ship he means to board again.

Below the floorboards, the tide drew breath to speak. Above the bench, the good file hung in its place. And in my chest, a mechanism I had kept oiled for years without admitting its purpose began, unmistakably, to turn.
