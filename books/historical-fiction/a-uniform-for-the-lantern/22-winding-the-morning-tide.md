## 22. Winding the Morning Tide

The lantern-clock wakes before I do. It always has. There is a particular quality to a clock that has been wound faithfully for thirty years — a kind of settled patience in its ticking, like an old man who no longer needs to prove he is right — and mine sits on the mantel of my shop above the tide-tunnel entrance and ticks at Elias Marchetti's old lighthouse rhythm: eight strokes on the hour, every hour, day and night, whether anyone is listening or not.

I wind it each morning at dawn, which is to say I wind it each morning badly, because dawn in Vellamouth arrives grey and grudging and my hands are seventy-one years old and have opinions about cold brass.

"Careful," I tell myself, the way Fenwick used to tell me. "A mainspring forgives nothing."

Fenwick has been dead nineteen years. He never once said that sentence to me about this clock, but he said it about everything else, so I lend it to him freely. The dead are generous that way. They let you quote them.

The shop below — Hartwell & Co., though the "Co." is a fiction Maren insists on to keep the tax man confused — opens at seven. Above it, our rooms smell of tea and oil and the faint salt ghost that comes up through the floorboards from the tunnel, no matter how many times we seal it. We stopped sealing it eventually. Some doors you keep open on principle.

Maren is still asleep. She sleeps later now, and I have stopped pretending I mind. A woman who gave her father's boat to a fugitive and then waited two years for the man who broke his own life against the machinery of the state — she has earned her mornings.

I turn the key. Eight half-turns, no more. The spring takes up its tension with a sound like a held breath being slowly released, and the escapement settles into its long patient argument with gravity, and somewhere inside all that brass a man who cannot come home keeps time anyway.

That is the whole religion of it, if you want to know. That is the entire church.

* * *

My apprentice is called Pip Weller, which is not his name but became it within a week of his arrival, when he dropped a pallet of pinions down the stairwell and stood in the wreckage looking like a small bird considering flight. He is fourteen, all elbows and questions, and he came to me by way of the workhouse ward, where I spent two years repairing public clocks under supervision and where the wardens, in their infinite bureaucratic wisdom, decided that the boy who kept stealing into the workshop to watch was a nuisance rather than a candidate.

I have never thanked them. Perhaps I should. Bureaucracy occasionally stumbles into grace without noticing.

"Master Hartwell." Pip arrived at six-forty, breathless, which meant he had run from the lodging house again. "You're winding it already. You always wind it before I get here."

"That," I said, "is because you are late."

"I'm early. Shop doesn't open till seven."

"The clock opens at dawn. Everything else is negotiable." I set the key down and nodded at the mantel. "Tell me what you see."

He squinted at it the way he always does, one eye shut, as if the clock might be hiding something on one side. "Brass case. Lantern shape. Four pillars. Chapter ring's hand-painted — your wife's work?"

"Maren painted the numerals the winter I was in the ward. She couldn't visit more than once a month, wire glass between us, forty minutes by the warder's pocket watch. So she painted my clock instead." I did not say this dramatically. After thirty years it has worn smooth, like a stair tread. "What else."

"The striking train's wrong." He leaned close. "It strikes eight on every hour. A proper clock strikes the hour. One o'clock, one stroke. This one's — " he hunted for the word, found it, looked almost offended — "sentimental."

"It is sentimental," I agreed. "It is also correct. Sit down, Weller, and I'll tell you why, since you've asked me four times now and I've put you off three."

He sat. He is a good boy. He knows when a story is coming and has learned not to spoil it by asking for it directly.

"This clock keeps lighthouse time," I said. "There was a keeper on the Vellamouth light named Elias Marchetti, and his shifts ran in watches of four hours, and the bell above him struck eight times at the change of every watch, all night, all his working life. Twenty-two years. When they took the light away from him — " I paused, because after thirty years the pause still costs me something — "when they took everything away from him, there was no bell left to strike for him. So I built one."

Pip turned this over. "You built a lie."

"I built a memory with a mainspring in it. Those are different things, though I grant the difference is subtle." I poured the tea Maren had left warming. "Now. The story. And I want you to listen properly, because there is an examination at the end, and the examination is the rest of your life."

* * *

I told him about 1911, the way I always tell it — beginning with the observatory clock, because that is where I began, a nineteen-year-old apprentice sent alone up the hill with a tool roll and too much pride and no idea that the master clock's chime could be silenced by hand, a detail I noticed without ceremony and carried like a coal in my coat for months before it caught fire.

I told him about Inspector Corven, whose ledger I would come to respect even as it tried to destroy us — a man who believed order was mercy, who counted the strokes of a lying bell with his own finger in the air, and who watched an empty horizon one storm-washed morning and chose, deliberately, with full knowledge, not to signal the cutter. I do not know if that made him good. I know it made him tired, and that tired men sometimes do the one right thing left to them, and that I have lit a candle for him every year since he died, which surprised everyone including me.

I told him about the tide-tunnel, and Fenwick's silence before the guild council — how the old man absorbed the blame like a bearing absorbs load, saying nothing, stripped of his mastership, and sent me afterward a single file-blade wrapped in cloth. No note. The file-blade was the note. It hangs over my bench now; I have worn through four others but never that one.

I told him about the ration cards ruined by rain, and Maren re-inking them by lamplight with her tongue between her teeth, and the market constable with the good ear, and the coal cellar with the floorboards inches above a breathing man, and the night Corven canvassed my own lodging house question by question while I sat above a fugitive and discovered that a held breath can become a prayer if you hold it long enough.

"And the harbor," Pip said quietly. He had heard fragments before, from other boys, garbled. Every apprentice in Vellamouth has heard some version of the harbor.

"The harbor," I said. "Eight minutes. I rebuilt the master clock's striking train so the noon chime would run eight minutes long — enough to cover a fishing boat's engine slipping past the breakwater in weather no captain wanted. Every gear had to be perfect. There was no second attempt. And when it was done — when the bells were still ringing their beautiful wrong song and Corven came up the tower stairs to arrest me — I confessed everything and asked him to let the boat go beyond recall."

"Did he?"

"He watched the water for a long time. Then he closed his notebook." I drank my tea. It had gone lukewarm, which is its natural state around me. "Then he wrote down my name very carefully, the way he wrote down everyone's name, and I understood that he was keeping me the only way he knew how — precisely, completely, on the record. Some men forgive with their hands. Corven forgave with his ledger."

"What happened to Elias?" Pip asked. "After."

"After," I said, "he could not come home. That is the part no one wants to hear, boy, so listen. We saved his life and lost his country in the same morning. He sailed from port to port for years. He worked lights in places whose names I couldn't spell. He wrote letters with no addresses — just postmarks, and inside each one, nothing but a number written in a careful keeper's hand."

"Eight," Pip said.

"Eight. Every letter. Alive, moving, remembering. That was the whole message, and it was enough. It was more than enough. My father made me swear a debt of honor to that man when I was nine years old and half-drowned, and I paid it, and here is the lesson, Weller, so mark it: the debt was not discharged. Debts of that kind don't discharge. They convert. They stop being something you owe and become something you carry. Like a mainspring. You don't pay a mainspring back. You wind it. Every morning. Forever."

He was quiet for a moment. Then, because he is fourteen and constitutionally incapable of leaving a mechanism unexamined: "And Mrs. Hartwell? In the story? Did you — "

"We argued," I said. "About whether saving one man mattered against the machinery devouring many. We argued beautifully. And then we chose each other's company over certainty, and we have been arguing ever since, and I recommend the arrangement without reservation."

From upstairs came the unmistakable sound of Maren dropping something heavy in a manner that suggested she had heard every word, which she probably had, the floorboards being thin and her hearing being selective but excellent.

* * *

The morning went on. Pip cleaned pivots; I supervised and pretended not to nap. A widow brought in a carriage clock that had belonged to her husband and wept a little when it ticked again, and I let her weep, because that is half the trade — people bring you their dead in small brass boxes and ask you to make them move again. The other half is knowing that you can't fix anything except the moving.

At noon the shop clock struck eight, as it always does, and a customer new to the street frowned and asked if it was broken.

"No," I told him. "It's keeping someone's time."

He looked at me the way people look at clockmakers who say things like that, which is to say with polite alarm, and bought a watch chain, and left.

Maren came down at two with bread and cheese and the particular expression she wears when she has decided to be fond of me in public. Thirty years, and she still inspects my bench like a harbormaster inspecting a suspect vessel.

"You told him the whole story," she said.

"He asked four times."

"You told him the part about the letters?"

"All of it."

She set the plate down and looked at the lantern-clock on the mantel, ticking its faithful wrong count. Her hair is grey now, and she is more beautiful than she was at twenty, which I maintain is a scandal of physics that no one has investigated properly.

"Do you think he's alive?" she asked. Not for the first time. Never for the last time.

"I think the letters came for eleven years," I said. "I think the last one came from a port called — whatever it was called — and I think a man who stops writing either dies or decides the debt is paid, and Elias Marchetti never in his life decided a debt was paid. He reported a smuggling run once because it was right, knowing it would cost him everything, and it did, and he'd have done it again. Men like that don't stop writing. They just run out of ports."

"Or out of strength."

"Or out of strength," I allowed. "But strength runs out slower than ports."

She squeezed my shoulder — her version of a sonnet — and went back upstairs, and the afternoon wore down toward evening the way afternoons do when you have survived enough of them to stop resenting the wear.

* * *

I was closing up. That is the hour I like best — shutters half-drawn, lamps low, the day's work squared away, the lantern-clock on the mantel gathering itself toward the hour. Pip had gone home. The street outside was doing its evening arithmetic: gulls, cart wheels, a foghorn out past the breakwater counting its slow patient eights, because the new automated light kept the old rhythm and nobody quite remembers why, except that some rhythms outlive their reasons.

I locked the till. I straightened the file-blade on its nail. I looked at the clock.

Five minutes to the hour.

And then — I want to be precise about this, because precision is the only honesty I have ever fully mastered — I saw the shadow in the doorway before I saw the man. A stillness in the frame of the open door, the particular quality of a person standing in a threshold deciding whether to cross it. Thirty years of watching movements and escapements teaches you to read hesitation the way other men read weather.

He was grey. All of him. Grey hair, grey coat, a face the sea had worked on for decades the way water works on stone — patiently, thoroughly, without malice. He stood with his cap in his hands, the way a man stands in a church or a courtroom, and he looked at the little brass lantern-clock on my mantel as if it were the only object in the world.

I did not speak. Some mechanisms you do not disturb mid-strike.

The minute hand climbed. The gathering click of the warning wheel. The lift of the hammer.

The clock struck eight.

One. Two. Three. Each stroke falling into the quiet shop like stones into deep water. The man in the doorway closed his eyes at the fourth, and I saw his lips move, counting — counting, after all these years, out of habit, out of devotion, out of whatever it is that keeps a man writing numbers in letters with no addresses.

Seven. Eight.

The last stroke faded. The foghorn out past the breakwater answered, distant, eight for the change of watch, and for a moment the shop held both sounds the way a bell holds its note — complete, brief, unwilling to end.

Elias Marchetti opened his eyes and looked at me across thirty years and one small room.

"Hartwell," he said. His voice was gravel and salt and home. "You're winding it wrong. Half a turn short. You've been doing it for years."

I am an old man and a craftsman and I have faced inspectors, councils, tribunals, and wire glass, and I did not cry at any of them.

I cried at that.

"You're late," I managed, when I could.

"Aye," said the keeper of the light, stepping over my threshold at last, into the tick and glow of the shop above the tide-tunnel, where every door we ever sealed had been left open on principle. "But the clock opens at dawn. Everything else is negotiable."
