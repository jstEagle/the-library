## 1. The Weight of Water

The sea does not care what you owe it. That was the first thing my father taught me, and the last thing he proved.

I learned to dive before I learned to write. This is not an unusual order of education in Saltmere Bay, where a girl's hands are worth more calloused than lettered, but it is the fact I return to on mornings like this one, when the tide is low and the light comes down through the water in long grey columns and the oyster beds lie below me like a country I am not a citizen of. My father used to say the bay had two populations: the ones who breathed air and the ones who didn't, and the second group was more honest. He said it with his mouth full of sea, treading water, laughing at whatever joke the morning had made. Then one morning the sea stopped laughing back, and took him, and kept him, because that is what the sea does with things it decides belong to it.

I was eleven. I have been keeping accounts ever since — first of grief, which does not balance, and then of pearls, which do.

* * *

By seven o'clock I had brought up forty-one shell from the eastern beds, of which perhaps nine would hold anything worth weighing. The Meridian Trading Company does not pay for hope; it pays for nacre by the grain, and it pays at rates set in a city I will never see, by men who have never held their breath past the point where the body begins to argue. I know this because I have read their correspondence. Filing other people's letters is the other half of my indenture, and between the two halves — the diving and the writing — the company owns most of my waking hours and, per the contract, all of them until my twenty-first year.

Four more years. The number sits in my chest some mornings like a stone I swallowed.

"Blackwood." Old Tammett, the bed-master, hailing me from the punt. "You're down long again. Your lungs will file a complaint."

"My lungs can't afford the fee," I called back, and surfaced, and spat the bay out of my mouth, and handed up the basket.

It was a joke. It was also arithmetic. Everything in Saltmere is arithmetic: the grain-weights, the boat-rentals, the medicine that costs a week's diving and the funeral that costs a month's. When my father drowned, the company deducted the cost of recovering his body from the balance of his own account, as though he were a crate dropped overboard, and presented my mother with the remainder. She signed for it. Her signature looked, she said afterward, like a bird that had flown into a window. She died two winters later, of the cough, of the damp, of the ordinary cruelties, and her account was closed by the same clerk who had closed his, in the same brown ink, and I remember thinking — I was fourteen, and already keeping the ledgers myself by then — that the page did not even wrinkle where her name went down.

That is when I understood it, or began to. History is not what happens. History is what gets written, and what gets written gets written by the people who own the ink. Kings and admirals and factors in far offices write the war and the treaty and the glorious death of Captain Aldous Renn, and the rest of us go into the ledger as weights and balances: *one pearl diver, lost; one widow, settled; forty-one shell, eastern beds.* We are entered, never asked. We are the column, not the sentence.

I want you to understand that I believed this completely, that Tuesday morning. I believed it the way you believe the tide — totally, without thinking about it, right up until the day it goes out further than it has any right to and shows you everything it has been hiding.

* * *

The weigh-house smelled of brine and lamp oil and the particular sourness of men who count money they don't own. I carried the day's baskets in at half past noon, changed my wet sarking for a dry shirt in the passage behind the scales, and climbed the stairs to the counting room, where my desk sat under the one window that faced the harbor rather than the sea — the company's small joke of architecture, or perhaps its honest one. From that window you could see the ships and the cranes and the flag with its meridian line stitched down the middle, north to south, cutting the world into owned and unowned.

The counting room held four desks. Mine was the smallest, and the only one without a lock, because a bonded diver could hardly steal from herself. On it waited the morning's correspondence, sorted and waiting: invoices from the cooperage, a customs notice, and a circular from Head Office regarding the war effort, requesting continued "patriotic economy in stationery expenditures."

We were at peace by then, or nearly. The armistice was being negotiated in the capital; everyone said it would be signed within the season. But the circulars still came stamped with the war office seal, and the requisition forms still asked after gunpowder storage capacity in a port that had no garrison, and I filed these things under M for Miscellaneous because there was no drawer for questions.

That was another thing I knew how to do: put things in drawers where they fit and stop looking at them. It is a marketable skill. It is also, I have since decided, a kind of drowning done slowly, at a desk, fully clothed.

At three o'clock Factor Corvus Hale came through on his rounds. You would know Hale anywhere by the sound before the sight: boot heels with the exact cadence of someone who has never once been late and never once been early, arriving precisely when he intends to. He was a tall man built like a mast — all verticality and no give — with grey going to silver at the temples in a way that suggested he had ordered it. His eyes were pale and patient, the eyes of a man who reads ledgers the way priests read scripture: not for pleasure but for authority over it.

"Blackwood."

"Sir."

"The Renn memorial correspondence. Has it been archived?"

"It's archived, sir. Cross-indexed under R, with duplicates under H for Heroic Loss, per Head Office instruction."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not a smile. A smile's accountant. "Head Office does love its categories." He set one finger on my desk, lightly, the way a man tests whether a thing will bear weight. "There will be visitors soon. Peace delegation. Commissioners, historians, whatever they are. The town will make itself ridiculous with bunting." He looked out the window at the harbor, at the cranes, at the flag. "When they come, this office will be spotless and its records will be exemplary. Do you understand?"

"Exemplary, sir," I said, which meant nothing, which is why it was safe.

He nodded once and went, and his heels went with him, and I sat there feeling the way I always felt after Hale spoke to me: as though I had been weighed and found to be exactly what I appeared, and the weight had been entered, and the entry closed.

* * *

Here is what my afternoon actually contained, before it became the afternoon everything else came out of. Invoices, thirty-one. A letter from a widow in the fishing rows asking — politely, heartbreakingly, in a hand that gripped the pen the way drowning men grip spars — whether the company might waive the last quarter's boat-rental against her husband's years of service. There was no provision for waiver. There was a provision for deduction, and a form for it, and the form had been designed by someone who understood that if you make the paper humiliating enough, most people won't fill it out. I filled it out for her. I wrote in the margin, in my smallest hand, a note recommending consideration, knowing the note would be read by a clerk in Head Office who processed mercy in batches, quarterly, at whatever rate the year's profits permitted.

Then I copied shipping manifests. Then I sharpened the pen. Then the light went long and gold across the harbor, and the bell rang the hour, and I did what I did every evening at that hour, which was open the bottom drawer and take out my father's diving logs.

They were not company property, though the company would have claimed them if it had known they existed, because my father kept a second accounting — not of pearls, but of the beds themselves. Depths, currents, temperatures, the knot-marks he used to log the seabed's moods. Twenty-two years of a man's mornings, written in grease pencil on waterproofed slate and transferred to paper at night, in a script so small and even it looked typeset. I read a page every evening. I don't know how else to explain it except that it was a conversation I refused to let end, and I did most of the talking.

*Third of the Flood Month. East beds cold. Current from the northeast, strong. Found a shelf nobody's worked. Marked it. Kept it quiet. Some things the company doesn't need to know.*

He'd written that about a shelf of oysters. I used to laugh at it, quietly, alone. My father, smuggler of latitude and longitude. Keeper of small secrets from a company that owned his lungs on a contract. I loved him for it the way you love a bird for flying inside a room: futilely, entirely.

I put the logs away at dusk, locked nothing because there was nothing to lock, and went down through the weigh-house as the lamps were being lit.

* * *

You should know something about me, since I am apparently telling you everything now: I am not brave. People have said it since — that I must be, to have done what I did — and it flatters me and it isn't true. What I am is accurate. I spent six years learning to see a ledger whole, to notice the entry that doesn't match the invoice, the sum that's off by a single grain-weight, the ship listed as carrying dye-stuff that draws too much water for dye-stuff. Accuracy is a habit of mind, and habits of mind are stronger than courage, and cheaper, and they keep better.

So when I say what happened next happened fast, understand that the fast part was the man falling. The slow part — the part that mattered — was already in me, waiting, the way a loaded ledger waits for its mismatched line.

He came through the weigh-house doors at the changing of the watch, when the floor was crowded with baskets and scales and men knocking off, and he came in wrong. Everyone comes into a weigh-house with intent: to sell, to buy, to argue, to steal. This man came in like weather. Grey-faced, salt-stiff jacket, one shoulder riding high and dark where the cloth stuck to him, walking on legs that were obeying orders from a headquarters that had already fallen. He crossed the floor past the great brass scales — past Tammett, who turned to look, past the lamplighter, past everyone — and reached my table by the door, where I stood with my coat half on.

And he put a leather satchel into my hands.

Not offered. Put. Pressed down onto my forearms with both of his hands, which were shaking, and which left wet prints — and I knew even then, in the half-second I had, that the wet was not seawater. Seawater dries cool. This dried warm.

"Keep it," he said. His voice was wrecked, sand and rope. "Don't read it. Don't give it to anyone in a uniform. Don't—" And then the company guards came around the corner of the cooperage wall with their lanterns, because of course they did; the watch had been alerted to a man off the evening boat who hadn't paid his landing fee, or who had, or who looked wrong, and the reasons hardly matter now. He straightened up like a marionette whose operator remembered him. "Forgive me," he said, louder, to no one, to everyone, a man performing being drunk, badly, while bleeding. "Evening, all. Fine scales. Very fine scales—"

They took him at the door. I watched it happen with the satchel pressed flat against my stomach under my coat, and here is the part I have gone over a thousand times since: I did not decide. There was no moment where I weighed the risk and chose the danger, no drumbeat of heroism, none of it. The guards asked if anyone had seen where the man boarded, and six people answered at once, and I heard my own voice join them saying *no, nothing, I was just closing up* — a lie, my first, delivered in the same clear tone I used for reading sums aloud — and the satchel against my ribs seemed to beat like a second heart, faster than mine.

They dragged him out toward the watch-house. The doors swung. The lamps hissed. Somewhere out on the water a ship's bell counted eight, unhurried, indifferent, the sea keeping its own books.

Tammett appeared at my elbow. "You look like you've seen a drowned man walk," he said.

"Just tired," I said, which was true, and a lie, and I was beginning to learn these could be the same sentence.

* * *

I walked home the long way, along the sea wall, because the long way was empty and the short way had neighbors. Under my coat the satchel knocked against my hip with every step, patient as cargo. I told myself I would turn it over to the factor in the morning. Of course I would. An unregistered parcel, found in the weigh-house, connected to a bleeding stranger taken by the guards — the correct procedure was obvious, was practically printed on the thing. I was a bonded clerk of the Meridian Trading Company. Correct procedure was the uniform I wore underneath my other uniform.

I got home to the room above the chandlery that the company housed me in, deducting rent, and lit the candle, and sat down at the table, and did not turn it over.

Here is what I noticed first, before I even opened it — before I knew whose hands had packed it or what it would cost me, though I think some part of me had begun to suspect, the way you suspect a sum is wrong before you find the error. I noticed that the satchel was heavy. Not with papers. With purpose. Things packed in a hurry feel a certain way; things packed to survive feel another way entirely, and this was packed to survive — wrapped in oilcloth, corners folded like a sailor's knot, built to endure salt water and rough handling and, presumably, discovery.

Inside: letters. Perhaps twenty of them, bundled and bound with tarred twine. And beneath the letters, wrapped separately, a slim book in grease-proof covers that I recognized instantly and completely, and that made my breath do something I have never quite forgiven it for.

But I'm ahead of myself. That discovery belongs to later tonight, and later tonight belongs to another chapter of this, though I didn't know it yet. What I knew, standing in my rented room with the candle guttering, was simpler and worse:

For twenty-two years my father had written down what the company didn't need to know, and the company had never once read it, because the company did not believe people like us wrote history. We were entries. We were columns. We were *forty-one shell, eastern beds.*

And now I was standing in the dark holding something that had been carried through a guarded port by a bleeding man who had chosen *me* — the clerk with the unlocked desk, the diver's daughter, the margin-note writer — out of everyone in that crowd.

Outside, the watch patrol passed below my window, boots on cobbles, lantern-light sliding across the ceiling like water. I blew out the candle and sat in the dark and listened to them fade, and the satchel sat on the table beside my father's diving logs, and neither of them made a sound, and both of them were waiting.

In the morning, Factor Hale would ask his questions, and I would lie again, better. In the morning the harbor would bring in its boats and its lies and its ordinary arithmetic. But that night I unwrapped the oilcloth by the relit candle, and compared one impossible handwriting against another, and the world tilted on its axis like a scale coming out of balance —

— because the corpse was writing letters, and one of them was addressed, in cipher, to my father.
