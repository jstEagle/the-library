## 4. Elias

### Yara

The cooperage smelled of oak shavings and old brine, which is to say it smelled like every building in Saltmere, including the one where I slept. But the loft above it had one advantage no other building could claim: nobody from the Meridian Trading Company had ever thought it worth entering. The coopers were paid by the barrel, not by the hour, and they went home at dusk to wives who counted their wages before they counted their kisses. A loft full of dust and staves was as good as a fortress.

I climbed the ladder at half past nine, a covered basket on my arm, my heart going like a gull in a storm.

He was awake. I knew it before I saw him, because the breathing in the dark had changed — the shallow, careful rhythm of a man pretending to sleep rather than the honest slog of a man actually doing it. I set the basket down and lit the stub of candle I'd brought, cupping the flame until it steadied.

He was propped against the wall where I'd left him, one shoulder bound in linen gone rusty at the center, his coat folded under his head like a pillow he didn't trust. Twenty-five, maybe. Hollow-cheeked. The kind of face that had been handsome once and had decided, somewhere along the way, that beauty was a liability.

"You came back," he said. His voice was gravel and rust.

"I brought bread and a poultice. Don't make it sound noble."

"Didn't." He watched me kneel. "Most people don't come back."

"Most people aren't fools."

"No," he agreed. "They aren't."

I unwrapped the poultice — comfrey and salt water, my mother's recipe for divers' bruises, though she'd have wept to see it wasted on a landman's shoulder. He flinched when I pressed it to the wound but made no sound, which told me two things: he'd been hurt worse before, and he'd learned early that crying out cost more than it bought.

"What's your name?" I asked.

A long silence. Long enough that I looked up from the bandage and found him studying me the way I studied a ledger page — looking for the entry that didn't balance.

"Elias," he said finally. "Elias Vane."

"And you're running from someone."

"I'm running toward something. It just happens to be behind me."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only kind I can afford."

I tied off the bandage harder than strictly necessary. He didn't complain, which annoyed me further. I had spent twenty-two years being the girl who balanced the books, who never misspoke, who lied to no one because lying required a self you could afford to lose. And here was this stranger bleeding through my good linen, rationing out truth like it was coin he expected to need later.

"The letters," I said. "The ones in the satchel. They're signed by Captain Aldous Renn."

Something moved behind his eyes — not surprise. Confirmation. As if he'd been waiting to see whether I'd say it aloud, and now that I had, some decision had been made without his permission.

"You know the handwriting," he said.

"I keep the company's correspondence archive. I've filed four hundred pages of Renn's official letters. Victory dispatches. Requisitions. Condolences to widows written in a hand so steady you'd think grief was a form of arithmetic." I sat back on my heels. "The dead man writes a better letter than most living men."

"He always did." Elias almost smiled. It didn't survive the trip to his mouth. "He used to dictate signals to me and then rewrite them himself because he said my phrasing lacked *moral urgency*. He was right. I was nineteen and mostly concerned with supper."

"You were his signalman."

The almost-smile died entirely. "You've been reading my satchel."

"I've been reading paper that collapsed into my arms while its owner bled on the scales. Ownership seems ambiguous from where I stand."

For a moment I thought he'd be angry. Instead he laughed — a short, cracked sound, quickly suppressed, as if laughter too was rationed. "Fair," he said. "That's fair."

Outside, the tide was turning; I could hear it in the pilings, that long slow exhale the harbor makes when it gives up on the day. I trimmed the candle and waited. I was good at waiting. Waiting was most of what a ledger keeper did — between entries, between tides, between the life you had and the life you'd been promised by no one.

"They name ships," Elias said at last, quietly, as if the walls might be paid informants. "The letters. Ships, dates, harbors, buyers. Three years of them."

"Three years of what?"

He looked at me for a long time. Then: "Arms. Company ships carrying guns to both sides of the war, after the armistice talks began. After the embargo. Renn found the manifests two years ago and reported them up the chain, and the chain swallowed them, and then the chain tried to swallow him. So he let the sea report his death instead, and started writing it all down."

I felt the floor tilt — or imagined I did, the way a diver feels the bottom shift beneath her fins. "The *Meridian* armed both sides."

"The Meridian armed both sides, sold to both sides, insured both sides, and will sell the peace treaty's commemorative medals to whichever side survives the signing." His voice was flat, but flatness, I was learning, was its own kind of fury. "Renn has proof. Names. Dates. Buyers who sit in Parliament and buyers who sit in company boardrooms, and in at least three cases those are the same chairs."

"Why hasn't he taken it to the peace delegation?"

"Because the delegation arrives in eleven days, and Commissioner Marsh's historians will write the official record of this war, and the record will be whatever the company lets it be — unless the proof lands in the right hands at the right moment, complete and undeniable. Renn is a dead man, Miss Blackwood. Dead men make poor witnesses. So he sent me ahead with the letters, and the company sent men after me, and here we are." He gestured at the loft, the dust, the candle, the general indignity of it all. "Here we are."

"How do you know my name?"

"You shouted it at the guards. 'Yara Blackwood, ledger clerk, this man is my responsibility.' Very formal. Very stupid." He paused. "Thank you."

I stood, because sitting felt suddenly too intimate. "It wasn't stupidity. It was bookkeeping. You were an unrecorded asset on company property. Someone had to enter you into the record."

"That," said Elias Vane, "is the strangest act of mercy I've ever heard described."

### Elias

She left, and I lay in the dark listening to the cooperage settle around me like an old man into a chair, and I took inventory.

This is what signalmen do. We take inventory. Flags, lamps, distances, wind. What do I have, what does the enemy have, what can I see that they can't. For six years my whole world was Aldous Renn's flag deck — three flags up meant convoy sighted, two meant trouble, one meant *come quick, bring brandy, the admiral is being insufferable*. Simple language. Honest language. A flag says exactly what it means or it says nothing at all.

People, I had learned, were not flags.

The girl — Blackwood — was not a flag. She'd come back with bread and a poultice and questions filed in order of importance, and she'd looked at Renn's handwriting the way a priest looks at a forged relic: not with shock, but with professional disappointment that anyone thought she wouldn't notice.

I should have run. That was the inventory's cold conclusion. Run at dawn, satchel and all, find another hiding place she couldn't trace, trust no one — that was the rule, the only rule that had kept me alive from Harrow Quay to here. Trust no one, especially not people who are kind, because kindness is the bait and the trap is always the same shape.

But.

But she'd lied to the guards. Fluently. *He's my cousin's man, drunk, fell on the scales.* Delivered it with the bored precision of someone reading shipping rates aloud. And then she'd walked me — staggering, dripping blood down her sleeve — through the barrel yard, past two coopers, past the weighmaster's window, chattering about tonnage the entire way, and not one soul had looked twice.

You don't learn that in a day. You learn that over a lifetime of being overlooked, and then one morning you decide to use it.

So I lay in the dark and revised my inventory. Asset: one ledger clerk, indentured, observant, frightened, brave in the specific way of people who have never been allowed to be anything else. Risk: total. If she broke, if Hale squeezed her even once, everything Renn had bled for would be in Corvus Hale's soft white hands within the hour.

And still, when her ladder creaked again the next evening, I felt something in my chest loosen that I hadn't known was clenched.

### Yara

He was sitting up the second night, color back in his face and opinions back in his mouth, which I gathered was his natural condition.

"Your father was a diver," he said, before I'd even set the basket down.

"My father was many things. Diver, primarily. Ledger-adjacent never."

"There are diving logs in the satchel. Copies. Renn collected them — said the answer to the whole war was floating in fishermen's notebooks." He said it lightly, watching me. Watching too closely.

"Give them here."

He hesitated. Then he reached into the satchel — my satchel, our satchel, the satchel whose ownership we had both silently agreed not to litigate — and passed me a bundle wrapped in oilcloth. Inside: perhaps thirty pages, copied in various hands, some neat, some crabbed. Depth markings. Knot patterns. Tide tables. My father's shorthand stared up at me from the third page and my hands went cold.

I knew these marks. Of course I knew them. Every diver's child knows them — the little knot-and-depth code the divers used to log their grounds without logging them, so the company factors couldn't tax what they couldn't read. Papa had taught me the alphabet before I could write my own name. *Three knots at the left margin means the bed shifts with the storm-tide.* *A crossed loop means danger, stay off.* It was a language built by people who needed to keep something of their own from the people who owned them.

"Why does Captain Renn have copies of divers' logs?" I asked.

"Because he said—" Elias stopped. Recalibrated. "Because he told me, the last night before I sailed, that the company's secrets weren't in the company's books. They were in the margins of everyone else's. He'd spent two years learning to read things the company never bothered to read. Fishermen's logs. Dockworkers' tallies. Divers' codes." He looked at me sideways. "He said the empire keeps its history in ledgers, but the truth keeps itself in notebooks."

My father drowned eight years ago, off the Gullet Shoals, on a company contract, in water the company had certified safe. There had been no inquiry. There had been a condolence payment of eleven crowns, entered in the ledger under *Miscellaneous Losses*, and I had been the one to make the entry, aged fourteen, because my mother's hands shook too badly to hold a pen.

I had believed, all my life, that history belonged to capitals and admirals — that it happened elsewhere, to other people, and arrived in Saltmere only as prices. Sitting in a dusty loft holding my father's own secret alphabet in a dead hero's correspondence, I understood for the first time that this belief had been installed in me like a rivet. Installed by whom? By the same people who filed my father under *Miscellaneous*.

"These letters," I said. My voice came out strange. "The cipher work in the margins. Some of it's in this code."

"So Renn said. He couldn't crack all of it. Said he needed someone raised in it."

The candle guttered. Somewhere below us a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

"If I help you," I said slowly, "if I decode these — what happens to me? Not rhetorically. Concretely. When Hale finds out, and he will find out, what happens to an indentured clerk who aids a fugitive?"

Elias was quiet for a moment. "I won't lie to you. Badly. Possibly fatally. Hale doesn't leave loose threads."

"Charming."

"But the alternative," he said, "is that in eleven days the armistice gets signed, the record gets written, and the company that armed both sides becomes the respectable hero of the peace. Forever. Your father stays a miscellaneous loss. Everyone who ever bled for their secrets stays a footnote, or less than a footnote — an unentered line, a blank space in a column." He leaned forward, wincing at his shoulder. "I'm not asking you to be brave, Miss Blackwood. I'm asking you what your ledgers are *for*."

I looked down at the diving logs. At the crossed loop that meant *danger, stay off*, drawn in my father's hand, in a dead captain's files, in a war's unwritten history.

"For recording what's true," I said. "That's what they're for."

"Then we understand each other."

We did. And understanding each other, I was discovering, was far more dangerous than any lie I'd yet told.

### Elias

On the fourth night she fell asleep over the decoding table, cheek on her forearm, one hand still resting on her father's handwriting, and I sat very still and did not allow myself to think anything inadvisable.

The trouble with trusting no one, I reflected, is that it works beautifully right up until it doesn't, and then it works against you. Because trust withheld is a debt, and debts accrue, and hers had accrued for three nights of bread and poultices and lies delivered flawlessly to company guards, and tonight she'd decoded a passage that named the *Kestrel's Pride* and a buyer in the Admiralty, and she'd looked up at me with her father's stubbornness and her mother's grief and said, "Keep going," and I had understood that I was no longer deciding whether to trust her.

The decision had been made. Probably the first night. Probably by the poultice.

Below us, boots on the quay. Two sets, unhurried, stopping near the cooperage door. I was on my feet with the satchel strap in my fist before my shoulder finished screaming, and Yara was awake instantly — awake and silent, finger to her lips, already moving the candle to the far side of the loft where its light wouldn't reach the stair.

The boots moved on. Harbor watch, making rounds. Ordinary. Nothing.

But Yara stayed crouched by the wall a moment longer, and when she turned back to me her face in the candlelight had changed. Not afraid, exactly. Decided.

"Hale posted extra watch tonight," she whispered. "Two men on the weighing house, one on the customs stairs. He's tightening the net, and he hasn't found us, which means he's going to start searching places he trusts people not to be." She met my eyes. "How much time do you think we have?"

I did the inventory honestly, for once.

"Less than we need," I said. "More than nothing."

She nodded, once, like a factor confirming a figure, and reached for the next page of her father's logs — and that was when I saw, in the margin of the topmost sheet, a mark I recognized. Not from the divers' code.

From Renn's private signals. One flag, flown alone.

*Come quick. Bring brandy.*

Except this wasn't brandy. This was a rendezvous mark, inked fresh, dated tomorrow, low tide — and it pointed, if I read the depth figures right, out past the Gullet Shoals.

To a place where a dead man, apparently, was waiting to be found.
