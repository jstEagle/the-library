## 6. The Diver's Cipher

I had spent eleven years learning to read water and three years learning to read paper, and I want it recorded — somewhere, anywhere — that the second was supposed to be the safer occupation.

Paper doesn't hold its breath when you descend into it. Paper doesn't go cold at your ankles and then your knees and then your chest. Paper just sits there in the lamplight of the cooperage loft, patient as a debt, while you turn its pages and tell yourself you're only looking.

Elias slept on the far side of the loft, propped against a grain sack with his bandaged shoulder rising and falling in a rhythm that had stopped worrying me around midnight. The wound had gone from angry to merely sulky, which Tam Cooper's wife said was the best you could hope for from a musket ball that had passed through meat instead of bone. He'd given me the letters before his fever took him under — "Look at them," he'd said, gripping my wrist with a strength that surprised us both. "Renn said if anything happened to me, find someone who reads everything. You read *everything*, don't you? The ledgers, the manifests, the margin notes nobody else bothers with."

I do. It's an occupational deformity. When your whole life is entered in someone else's book, you learn to read books the way a prisoner learns footsteps in a corridor.

So I looked.

The letters were seven, written on different paper across what must have been two years, all in the same hand — the hand I'd already confirmed against the archive copies of Captain Aldous Renn's official correspondence, the celebrated hero whose memorial portrait hung in the weighing house and whose corpse, apparently, had taken up letter-writing as a hobby. The prose was exactly what you'd expect from a company man educated at somebody's expense: crisp, formal, faintly apologetic about the urgency of its own contents. *The manifests are enclosed. The buyers are named. If I am dead, trust no one who wears the uniform.*

But it wasn't the prose I kept coming back to. It was the margins.

There were marks in them. Small ones, inked in a different hand-pressure than the body text, clustered near the fold of each page like barnacles on a hull. At first I took them for shorthand — clerks are forever inventing private shorthand and then dying without leaving a key, which is how history loses half of itself. A loop here. Two short strokes there. A figure that might have been a number crossed through, or a fishhook drawn by someone who'd never actually fished.

Then, somewhere past the third cup of chicory I didn't remember brewing, one of the loops resolved itself into something I knew better than my own signature.

A figure-eight knot, drawn open.

My father used to draw knots in the margins of everything. His diving logs — four volumes of them, salt-swollen, kept wrapped in oilcloth under my cot since the day they fished him out of the shallows with his lungs full of the Meridian Company's oyster beds — were less records than knot-catalogues interrupted occasionally by weather. Anchor bend. Carrick. Bowline on a bight. And always, always, beside every entry: depth. Not in fathoms the way the company measured, but in the divers' way — a system of strokes and dots that meant nothing to the factors because it meant everything to the men and women who went down. Three strokes and a dot: three arm-spans and a breath-hold past the drop-off. Two dots below the third stroke: the ledge where the big pearls sat and the current ran cold enough to stop your heart if you lingered.

It wasn't a cipher, exactly. That's what I told myself for the better part of an hour, hunched over Renn's letters with my father's logs open beside them, telling myself I was seeing ghosts. The divers' markings weren't secret because anyone had sworn us to secrecy. They were secret the way a language is secret — secret because the people who mattered had never bothered to learn it, and the people who spoke it had nothing worth stealing except each other's lives.

Except someone had bothered to learn it.

I laid the fourth letter flat. In its margin, beside a paragraph about a ship called the *Charity's Wage* loading crates at a pier that officially didn't exist, the same hand that wrote the paragraph had inked: a bowline, two strokes, a dot, a stroke, and then — this was the part that made my hands go strange — a figure-eight drawn *closed*.

In my father's logs, a closed figure-eight meant one thing only. It was the last mark he made on every page before surfacing. *Found it,* it meant. *This is the spot.* Divers' shorthand, passed mouth to mouth along the whole coast: the closed eight marked a cache. A hoard. A place where something good was hidden and could be found again by whoever knew how to read the sign.

Captain Aldous Renn, hero of the Breakwater Action, dead these two years and mourned in bronze, had been writing to himself — or to someone — in a drowned pearl diver's code.

I set down the letter very carefully, because my hands had developed opinions of their own.

Here is what I should have felt, according to every sensible ledger of the heart: fear. The letters named ships and dates and buyers; Elias had said so before the fever pulled him under, and the fragments I'd allowed myself to skim confirmed it in a clerk's nightmare of specificity. Armistice-breaking arms, moving both directions through a port that had never once made history. Anyone who understood those margins was already an accessory to whatever Captain Renn had died — hadn't died — to expose.

Instead, sitting in the dark of the loft with my father's logs breathing their old salt smell up at me, what I felt was closer to vertigo. The pleasant kind. The kind you get at the top of a mast when the harbor suddenly arranges itself into a map and you realize how much of it there is, how much you've been walking through blind.

My father had been dead six years, and he had just spoken.

Not literally — I'm a ledger-keeper, not a medium, and grief has never once done me the courtesy of arranging itself into convenient messages. But the code was his. Ours. Learned on the deck of a diving boat from a man who measured wealth in breaths, and now marching through the margins of a war hero's treasonous correspondence like it owned the place. Which, in a way, it did. Renn had learned it from someone. From divers. From my father's people, or people enough like them that the strokes matched stroke for stroke.

Why would a company captain learn the divers' code?

Unless he needed to write things the company couldn't read.

Unless he needed to hide something where the company would never think to look — in the margins of its own celebrated correspondence, tucked behind the ornamental prose, invisible to every factor and adjutant and archive-clerk in the Meridian chain. Invisible to everyone except the one kind of person the company trained to be literate in nothing but obedience.

Everyone except, say, a diver's daughter who kept the ledgers.

I worked until the lamp gutted. Letter by letter, log beside letter, I built the key on a scrap of sailcloth: the knots that opened each sequence, the strokes for distance, the dots for depth, the closed eights that said *found it*. Most of the marginal strings were coordinates — and not sea-coordinates either, but land-marks rendered in a diver's grammar. Distances walked instead of swum. Depths measured downward from a fixed point instead of upward from a seabed. *From the leaning post, three spans down, one breath past, the ledge where—*

No. Not a ledge. Underneath the depth-marking on the fifth letter, pressed hard enough to emboss the next page, Renn had written one word in plain script, small as a confession:

*Cooperage.*

I looked across the loft at the sleeping man who had been carried into this exact building bleeding, who had begged shelter of the one trade — coopers, divers' cousins, salt-people all — whose lofts stood on pilings above the tide-flats. Who had, when I asked him why here, why me, said only: *"Renn's instructions. He said the letters would know their own way home."*

The letters knew their own way home because their author had buried a cache beneath the floor of the room they were destined to reach.

Or beneath something near it. Renn's marks gave distances from a "leaning post," and the cooperage yard contained exactly one leaning post — the old mooring spar out back, listing seaward like a tired sentry, that every child in the quarter had swung from and every adult ignored for thirty years. Three spans south. One span down. Below the low-tide line, in the wet sand where nothing stayed buried unless it was wrapped in tarred canvas and weighted — the way divers cached tools, the way my father cached his emergency kit under the third rock of the north reef, because some habits outlive the bodies that held them.

The tide would be low again at dawn. Low tides come twice a day, but the morning one came lower, and I lay in the dark after that doing arithmetic I didn't want to finish: dawn was three hours off; Elias couldn't walk to the yard gate unaided, let alone dig; and I could not dig alone because whatever Renn had buried, he'd buried it deep enough to need two backs and a lever, and because — I admitted this to the ceiling with some irritation — I did not intend to let a wounded deserter out of my sight until I understood exactly what kind of trouble I had adopted.

Grief, I discovered that night, is not a stone you carry. It's a current. For six years I had been swimming against it, exhausting myself, calling the exhaustion duty. Now the current had turned, and it was pulling me toward something, and the terrible exhilarating truth was that I wanted to go. My father's hands were in these letters. My father's sea was under this floor. Whatever Aldous Renn had stolen from the margins of history, he had stored it in my mother tongue, and I intended to have it back.

I did not sleep. I copied the decoded sequences onto the sailcloth, folded it into my bodice, and watched the window go from black to slate to the color of old pearl.

At first light, I shook Elias's good shoulder and said the first sentence of my new life:

"Get up. We're going to rob Captain Renn's grave, and I'll need you to carry the shovel."

* * *

Fever is a country you visit, and I'd been living there for three days. Coming back was like surfacing too fast — everything bright and ringing and slightly wrong-sized.

The girl was shaking my shoulder. Yara. The ledger-keeper with the diver's eyes who hid satchels on instinct and lied to Corvus Hale's face — I'd heard about that second feat from Tam's wife, delivered in the awed whisper usually reserved for miracles and price-gouging. Yara didn't look like she lied well. She looked like she'd been born mid-apology. She also looked like she hadn't slept, and her voice had a wire in it that hadn't been there yesterday.

"Get up," she said. "We're going to rob Captain Renn's grave, and I'll need you to carry the shovel."

Which is how I learned she'd cracked the margins.

I'll be honest: my first feeling was not gratitude. It was the cold drop you get when a plan you thought had three locks turns out to have none. Those letters were the only thing keeping me alive — proof, ransom, purpose, all three — and I'd left them in reach of a stranger while a fever picked my pockets. But she handed them back unasked, all seven, corners aligned, and then held up a scrap of sailcloth covered in her father's diving-code translated into clerk's script, neat as a hymnal.

"You could have run," I said. "Sold them to Hale. Bought out your indenture twice over." Hale's men had been asking after a stranger since the weighing house; the whole quarter knew it; the whole quarter also knew that the company paid in silver for silver's worth of silence.

"I considered it," she said, in the tone of a woman reviewing a menu she'd already decided against. "The problem with selling a truth is that you have to stay bought. I've been sold since I was nine. I find I've lost the taste for it." She said it lightly, the way people say the worst things about themselves when they mean them most. Then, businesslike, folding the sailcloth away: "The cache is under the yard. Under the leaning post. We have perhaps two hours before the tide turns and the watch changes. Can you walk?"

"Define walking."

"Upright. Forward. Quietly."

"That's ambitious walking."

She got me upright anyway. Tam's wife fed us bread and a look that said she knew exactly what we were about and expected, in return, to keep knowing nothing — the oldest contract on the coast, and the only one the company had never managed to rewrite.

The yard at dawn was grey and empty, gulls arguing over something dead by the water gate. The leaning post leaned exactly as advertised, worn smooth at the swing-height by thirty years of children, and I thought — watching Yara pace off the spans with her lips moving, counting in her father's units, her shadow long and certain on the sand — that Renn must have loved her father a little, or feared him, or both. Men don't learn a dead man's private language for convenience. They learn it because the dead man taught them something worth keeping.

Three spans south. One span down.

The digging took forty minutes and cost me more sweat than blood, though the wound filed its complaints. Yara dug like she read — completely, furiously, sand flying in sheets — and about the length of a coffin down our spade rang on tarred canvas. We hauled it up between us, heavy as sin, and cut the binding with her ledger-knife, and inside, wrapped in oilcloth against the wet the way divers wrap the things they mean to come back for—

Manifests. Dozens of them. Bills of lading in triplicate, company-stamped, in Hale's own countersignature: gun-crates listed as salt pork, powder as dyewood, rifles as machinery, shipped east to one army and west to the other, dated across two years of a war the company publicly mourned and privately fed. Names of buyers. Names of escorts. Dates that mapped the whole rotten cargo-route like veins under skin.

And beneath them, a sealed letter addressed in that famous heroic hand: *To whoever the margins choose.*

Yara read the top manifest twice. I watched her face do something complicated — rage and relief fighting for the same ground — and then she looked up at me across the open cache with the sun coming up behind her, and she wasn't the frightened ledger-girl from the weighing house anymore. Grief had gone through her like a tide and left the bedrock showing.

"They armed both sides," she said. Flat. Certain. "My father drowned in their oyster beds while they sold the bullets. This goes in the record. All of it. Every page."

"It goes in the record," I agreed, "the moment we find someone the record can't refuse."

Which was when the gulls went up off the water gate all at once, screaming, and a harbor watchman stepped out of the fog with a lantern still lit against the dawn, looking directly at the hole in the ground, the two of us, and the company stamp on the manifest in Yara's hands.
