## 7. Low Tide, High Stakes

The tide goes out like a man leaving a debt unpaid — slowly, grudgingly, and with the certain knowledge that he'll be back to collect.

I'd learned that from Yara. Everything I knew about this harbor's moods, I'd learned from Yara Blackwood in four days of convalescence and whispered arithmetic in a cooperage loft that smelled of oak shavings and old tar. She could read water the way I read signal flags: at a glance, without thinking, the knowledge sitting so deep it had become something closer to instinct than education. Her father's diving logs were full of it. *Slack water at the third bell after dawn. Ebb runs hard past the breakwater pilings.* A whole language of tides, drowned along with the man who spoke it.

I lay on the loft floor with my ribs strapped and my pride in worse condition, watching the moon through the gap where two roof boards had warped apart, and did the arithmetic she'd taught me without meaning to.

Low tide: just past midnight. The cache was under the third piling of the old fish quay, which meant three hours of exposed seabed, which meant a window. The letters had said so in Renn's hand — *when the sea gives back what it borrowed* — and beneath them, in knots and depth-marks only a diver's child would recognize, the exact spot. Yara had translated them for me twice, tracing her father's notation with one finger, her voice going strange and quiet each time, as though she were reading aloud in a dead language at his grave.

"You shouldn't go alone," she'd said.

"I shouldn't go at all," I'd said. "We're past *shouldn't*. Now it's only *must*, and *must* doesn't wait for company."

She hadn't liked that answer. She hadn't argued either. That was the thing about Yara Blackwood I was coming to understand: she didn't waste words on battles she'd already decided to win some other way.

*

Getting out of the cooperage was the work of a minute and cost me an hour. My ribs had opinions about ladders. The town between midnight and low tide belonged to cats and the harbor watch, and I moved through it like a man walking on a frozen river — weight distributed, no sudden sounds, every step a negotiation. The watch made their rounds on the half-bell. I counted bells the way Renn had taught me to count them aboard the *Meridian Rose*: not by the hour but by the gap, because gaps tell you when someone isn't looking.

Renn. Two years dead, officially. Two years of a name carved into a memorial stone in the capital, two years of the company printing his face on commemorative ledgers they gave to favored clients — I'd seen one in Hale's office, gold-stamped, obscene. And all that time he'd been writing letters in cipher, moving through the sea lanes like a ghost with excellent penmanship, building a case against the men who thought they'd buried him.

I still didn't fully believe it. That was the honest truth of it. I'd carried the satchel through three checkpoints and one near-drowning, and I believed in the satchel because I'd bled for it. But Renn alive? Some part of me had attended the funeral. Some part of me had stood in formation on the deck while they rang the bell and read the citation, and grief is a kind of belief, and you don't unbelieve it easily. You carry your dead. You don't expect them to send you mail.

But the letters were real. The cipher was real. And if there was even a chance the cache existed — manifests, originals, proof — then a wounded signalman with cracked ribs owed it to a dead captain to go look.

*

The harbor at low tide was a country I'd never visited. The water had withdrawn past the breakwater, leaving behind a plain of wet sand and weed and the skeletons of things the sea usually kept private: a collapsed skiff, a mattress gone to rags, crab pots furred with barnacles, the ribcage of something I chose not to identify. Mud sucked at my boots. The smell was enormous — salt and rot and iodine and the mineral breath of a seabed remembering sunlight — and above it all hung the town, dark and indifferent, its windows like closed eyes.

Third piling of the old fish quay. I found it by counting, then found it again by checking, because a man who digs in the wrong hole at low tide drowns in the right one at high tide.

The base of the piling was wrapped in old netting, crusted over, invisible unless you knew to see it — and I only knew because Yara had described her father's caches, the divers' habit of hiding their true takings from company weighmasters who skimmed every catch. Of course Renn had used their method. He'd spent years running signals through occupied waters; he'd learned the divers' code from the same logs Yara grew up on. The powerful steal from people like us so routinely that we've built entire secret languages around hiding things from them. It had never once occurred to me that those languages might someday save a war hero.

I dug with my hands. The mud gave up its secrets reluctantly, cold climbing my wrists, my ribs screaming every time I leaned my weight wrong. Twice I heard the half-bell and froze, forehead against the piling, breathing through my mouth until the footsteps faded. Once something in the mud moved and I flinched hard enough to tear stitches I didn't have to spare.

Then my fingers hit canvas.

Oilcloth, triple-wrapped, tied with the kind of knot a sailor ties when he intends it to hold for years — and inside, dry as the day it was buried, a tin box. Inside the box: oilskin packets. Inside the packets: paper.

I knelt there in the sucking mud with the tide's absence all around me and opened the first packet with hands that would not stay steady, and I read.

Manifests. Originals, with signatures, with company chops, with dates. Cargo listings for ships I knew by reputation — the *Charity's Reward*, the *Sable Ann*, the *Meridian Rose* herself, God help me, my own ship — listed as carrying "agricultural implements" and "textile goods" to ports that everyone in the fleet knew were arms markets, everyone except the historians and the commissioners and the memorial committees. Rifles going to one side. Powder going to the other. The Meridian Trading Company feeding both mouths of the war and booking the profit under the names of farm tools, and here it was, itemized, signed, stamped, *true copies attested* in the neat hand of some clerk who probably went home to his family every night believing he worked for a respectable firm.

I laughed. I couldn't help it. It came out of me in the dark like something torn, and it echoed off the pilings, and I clapped a muddy hand over my own mouth like a madman.

Triumph is a strange animal. It doesn't feel like the songs say. It feels like vertigo — like standing on a height and realizing the drop has been there all along and you simply never looked down. Every number on those pages was a body. Every "implements" was a widow. And I was holding the proof, the actual proof, the thing men had died to keep quiet, and it weighed less than a loaf of bread.

I rewrapped everything exactly as I'd found it — habit, discipline, Renn's voice in my head saying *a courier protects the cargo before himself* — and stowed the tin inside my coat, against my chest, where it sat cold as a stolen heart.

That's when the lantern came on.

*

Not a searchlight. Not a patrol beam sweeping the flats. Just a small, ordinary lantern, unshuttered, held high by a figure standing at the head of the quay stairs, maybe forty yards away, close enough that I could see him clearly and know that he could see me.

A harbor watchman. Old coat, official badge catching the light, a truncheon hanging off his belt with the casual weight of long ownership. He stood very still, the way men do when they've seen something they need a moment to decide about.

My mind did what it was trained to do — ran the options like flags up a halyard. Run: forty yards of open mud, bad ribs, no cover, and the sound of my boots would carry like gunshots. Talk: a night fisherman, a drunk, a man looking for lost property — lies require details, details take time, and time was the tide. Fight: I had a knife, cracked ribs, and the arithmetic of age and health all pointing the wrong way.

So I did the only thing left. I stood up slowly, mud to my knees, holding nothing, and I smiled the smile I'd worn through three checkpoints — the bland, apologetic, slightly stupid smile of a man with nothing worth finding.

"Evening," I called, keeping my voice easy. "Lost my way back from the tavern. Tide caught me out."

The watchman said nothing. That was the worst part. A shout I could work with. An accusation I could parry. Silence meant he was doing sums of his own — a man knee-deep in mud at midnight, at the third piling, at the exact spot where, twenty years ago, divers had been known to hide their takings. Maybe he was just a tired old man wanting his bed. Maybe he'd already marked me and was waiting for reinforcements.

"Come up from the stairs," he said finally. Voice like gravel in a bucket. "Slow."

And here is the thing I will never put in any report, if I live to write reports again: for one full second, I considered it. Considered walking up those stairs, showing my honest face, telling my honest lie, and trusting to the ordinary decency of a tired watchman on an ordinary night. Because that's the trick the world plays on people like me — it makes the rules look safe right up until the moment they aren't.

Then I remembered the watchman's rounds. Half-bell. And this man was standing at the quay stairs at low water, alone, unshuttered lantern, which meant either he'd broken his own routine or he'd finished early — and either way, when I didn't report to whoever he reported to, he would remember my face. My face was already in his lantern light. Running wouldn't undo that. Nothing would undo that.

I turned and ran anyway.

The mud fought me like a living thing. Every step sank to the shin, then the knee, and my ribs announced themselves with every jarring stride until my vision went white at the edges. Behind me — boots on the stairs, then a whistle, two short and one long, the harbor watch's signal for assistance, and God, I knew every signal in the book and that one landed in my chest like ice water. He wasn't chasing me himself. He was calling men who would.

I cut toward the breakwater instead of the town, because the town had streets and streets had corners and corners had watchmen, but the breakwater had shadow and shadow had the sea. The ebb channel ran deep along its base even at low water — Yara's father's logs again, *the channel holds, trust the channel* — and I went over the algae-slick stones on my backside more than climbing, taking skin off my palms, clutching the tin to my chest with one arm like a mother with a child.

The water took me at the bottom. Cold enough to stop breath, stop thought, stop everything but *swim*, and I swam the way wounded men swim, badly and stubbornly, thirty yards along the breakwater's seaward face to the ladder by the old customs door that Yara swore rusted shut but whose rungs held. I came out of the water like something born, gasping, streaming, one hand locked around the tin through the soaked coat.

They found my boot prints later, I'm sure. They found the flattened grass by the third piling, the disturbed netting, the story written in mud that anyone could read. What they didn't find was me, because I crawled through the customs yard on hands and knees, climbed a drainage wall, crossed two rooftops belonging to buildings I cannot now name for fear of naming them wrongly, and dropped into the cooperage yard behind the barrel stacks as the first answering whistles sounded across the harbor — two short, one long, repeated, spreading outward like ripples from a stone.

By the time I dragged myself up the loft ladder, the whole town's watch was awake and organized, and somewhere in it was a watchman describing a tall man with a limp and a naval bearing and mud to the waist.

Yara was waiting at the top of the ladder with a candle and a face like the end of the world. She'd heard the whistles. She'd done her own arithmetic. When I crested the floor and collapsed onto the boards, she didn't ask if I'd found it. She looked at the shape of the tin under my soaked coat, and then she looked at my face, and whatever she saw there made her blow out the candle.

"We have it," I whispered. "Manifests. Signed. Both sides of the war, itemized. It's real, Yara. It's all real."

Her hand found my shoulder in the dark, gripped once, hard. For a moment neither of us said anything, and the whistles kept sounding outside, patient and methodical, men doing their jobs, closing a net around a town that had never once made history and was about to make quite a lot.

"They'll come asking," she said finally. "Door to door. He saw you, Elias. Whoever he was — he *saw* you."

"I know."

"And tomorrow morning, Corvus Hale will hear about a stranger in the mud at the third piling, and he'll do that arithmetic faster than any watchman." Her voice stayed level. That frightened me more than shouting would have. Level voices are what you get from people who have stopped hoping to be rescued and started planning. "Which means we have days. Maybe less."

Outside, a lantern swept the cooperage yard below us, slow and thorough, and we both went flat against the floorboards and stayed there while the light crawled across the ceiling seams like something hunting.

Triumph, I thought, lying in the dark with a stolen heart of paper against my chest and salt drying in my wounds. It curdles fast.

Above the harbor, the tide had already begun to turn.
