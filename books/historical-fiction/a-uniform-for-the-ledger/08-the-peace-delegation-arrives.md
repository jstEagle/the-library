## 8. The Peace Delegation Arrives

I had lived in Meridian's harbor town my whole life without once seeing a gun salute, and then on the morning the peace delegation arrived I saw eleven of them before breakfast.

The first one knocked the gulls off the customs roof in a single offended cloud. The second made old Mrs. Tallow drop her bread into the street, where it was claimed by a dog who would dine well for years on the reputation of that moment alone. By the fourth or fifth, the town had learned to count down between shots, and there was something almost liturgical about it — the whole waterfront holding its breath, then the boom, then the exhale, smoke drifting out over water that had spent six years swallowing things far worse than powder smoke.

"They're saluting," said Pem, the youngest tally-clerk, standing beside me at the weighing house window with his mouth open like a fish at market. "Who are they saluting? We haven't done anything."

"We exist," I said. "That's apparently enough."

He looked at me as though this were the most beautiful thing he'd ever heard, which told me everything about why Pem would die content and I never would.

The bunting had gone up three days earlier — red and white and that particular shade of blue that costs more per yard than a diver makes in a season. Company men had strung it from the weighing house to the chandlery to the cooperage, where it hung over Elias Vane's hiding place like a festive flag planted atop a secret. I had watched them work from my desk with a ledger open under my hands and my heart going like a bilge pump, because every hammer-stroke of those decorations was a reminder of what was coming: the people who would write the war's official history were arriving in my town, and I was sitting on documents that would either correct that history or hang everyone who helped me keep them.

Both, possibly. The two outcomes were not mutually exclusive. That seemed to be how history worked.

* * *

Commissioner Adela Marsh came ashore at half past ten aboard a white-sided steamer that looked less like a ship than like a very large, very disapproving document. She descended the gangway in a grey traveling coat with no ornament whatsoever, which I recognized instantly as its own kind of ornament — the uniform of a woman who wanted you to notice she didn't wear uniforms. Behind her came four clerks, two soldiers, and a man carrying a lacquered case that he held the way priests hold relics, which is to say: carefully, and slightly afraid of it.

Corvus Hale stood at the foot of the gangway in his best coat, and I will grant the man this: he could perform welcome the way other men performed grief at funerals, convincingly enough that you almost forgot to check whether anything underneath was alive.

"Commissioner Marsh. Meridian Trading Company is honored beyond expression."

"I'm sure," said Marsh, in a voice like a ledger line — flat, precise, leaving no room for sentiment to accrue interest. She shook his hand once, briefly, the way you'd handle something you intended to audit later. Her eyes went past him immediately to the town itself: the cranes, the warehouses, the bunting. Reading us. I knew that look. It was my own look, bent over a page of figures, deciding what was true and what merely tallied.

Then her gaze crossed mine — just for a moment, just a woman in a company apron standing among company clerks — and moved on without stopping.

I felt the strangest sensation. Not relief. Something adjacent to it but colder. *She didn't see me*, I thought, and then, with a lurch: *No one sees us until we're numbers.* Six years of war, and this whole town — the divers, the coopers, the tally-boys, the widows of the drowned — all of it would pass beneath that grey gaze like ballast, necessary for the voyage, invisible in the record.

Unless. Unless someone put themselves where the pen would have to go around them.

I was not yet brave enough to finish that thought. I only held it, the way you hold a hot coal passed hand to hand, knowing you'll have to do something with it eventually.

* * *

Hale gave the Commissioner a tour, naturally. He gave everyone tours; it was his native art form. And because the archive sat between the weighing house and the factor's offices, the tour came through my domain.

I heard them before I saw them — Hale's voice doing its polished thing, Marsh's voice cutting it into shorter lengths — and I had perhaps forty seconds to decide how a careful indentured clerk behaves when history walks into her archive.

Carefully, as it turned out. I stood, I inclined my head, I kept one hand flat on an open ledger as if touching it grounded me. Which, honestly, it did.

"This is our records office," Hale said. "Every cargo, every contract, every entry since the company's founding. Yara Blackwood keeps it. She has an exceptional memory and an inconvenient habit of accuracy."

"Inconvenient," Marsh repeated.

"A joke, Commissioner."

"Mm." She had stopped in front of the war-years ledgers, the ones I had entered myself, row upon row of ships and tonnage and destination codes. She did not touch them. Her hands stayed clasped behind her back, which somehow made it worse — a woman refusing even the temptation of paper, because she already knew what paper was worth.

"You lost men in the war," she said. It wasn't quite a question.

"The company lost ships, Commissioner. Men are harder to enter."

I nearly dropped my pen. Hale said it smoothly, sadly, with exactly the right weight of grief, and Marsh looked at him for a long moment, and I understood I was watching a fencing match conducted entirely in the passive voice.

"And your records," Marsh said finally. "They'll be available to my historians. The armistice commission requires full access to commercial shipping archives. All signatories agreed to it."

"Of course." Hale smiled. "We have nothing to hide."

*Everyone* says that, I thought. It's practically the motto of hiding things.

Marsh turned to leave, and then — I still don't know why, whether some instinct or simply that my face was the nearest unpolished surface — she paused beside me.

"You keep these yourself? All of them?"

"Yes, ma'am. Since I was fifteen."

"Fifteen." She glanced at the shelves, the sheer tonnage of it. "Do you ever find errors?"

"Only when other people make the entries," I said.

Something happened at the corner of her mouth. Not a smile. A filing. "Keep it so," she said, and walked out after Hale, and I stood there with my heart hammering against my ribs like a signal drum, aware that I had just spoken to the most powerful person ever to set foot in this town, and that I had told her a small true thing dressed as a joke, and that I had no idea whether it had been courage or suicide.

Elias would want to know. Elias would say it changed everything, or nothing, depending entirely on which way I decided to move next.

* * *

That evening the town celebrated a peace it hadn't been asked to sign. Lanterns along the quay, fiddles in the Anchor & Chain, free beer bought by the company — the company being generous, the company being visible, the company wanting very much to be remembered fondly by whatever historians were currently drinking its beer.

From the cooperage loft window, Elias and I watched the lights below.

"She's the real thing, isn't she," he said. Not a question. He'd seen the steamer come in; he'd kept flat under the loft boards while the salute shook dust from the rafters, and I'd felt him trembling through the floorboards and couldn't tell if it was fear or fury.

"Marsh? She frightens me more than Hale does."

"That's because Hale wants something from you. Marsh doesn't need to want anything. She just writes, and it becomes true." He picked at a knot in the floorboard. "In the signals corps we had a saying. *The wire doesn't care what happened. The wire cares what was sent.*"

"And whoever holds the wire—"

"Holds the war." He said it tiredly, like a man quoting his own scar tissue. "Your Commissioner Marsh, she'll have historians with her. Young ones, clever ones, writing up the official account. And the official account will say the war ended because wise men signed a wise paper, and nobody will write about the arms that kept both sides shooting, because—" he gestured at my satchel, tucked under the eaves where the bunting's shadow fell across it like a blessing, "—nobody official knows yet. Or worse. Somebody official knows already, and that's the version getting written tonight, in nice ink, in a nice room, while they dance down there."

Below us, the fiddlers struck up something fast and major-keyed. Someone laughed. A bottle broke. Peace sounded, from a distance, like any other night.

"Elias." I hadn't planned to say it. "When the historians write the record. Will there be a place in it for — " I gestured vaguely, at the loft, the harbor, myself, my father's drowned bones somewhere out past the shoal. "For anyone like us?"

He was quiet long enough that I regretted asking.

"There's a place for witnesses," he said at last. "Usually a footnote."

"A footnote."

"If you're lucky. If you're unlucky, you're a rounding error." He looked at me then, and his face in the lantern-light through the window was younger than his voice. "Yara. Your father dove for pearls no one wears. You keep accounts no one reads. Renn spent nine years keeping this company's signals and they'll carve his name on a cenotaph for a death he didn't die. None of us is in the story. We're the cost of the story. That's how it works."

"It doesn't have to."

"Everything has to, until someone makes it not."

I looked down at the celebration — Hale below on the quay, silver-tongued, steering two of Marsh's young historians toward the best table, pouring their wine himself, laughing at the right moments. Courting them. And I understood, with the cold clarity of deep water, exactly what I was watching. Hale could not destroy the manifests I'd hidden. But he didn't need to. He needed only to be the friendliest voice in the room when the official version got drafted — to supply the archives, to assist the historians, to shape the record so gently no one would feel the hand.

Whoever controlled the paper controlled the peace. Hale had known it his whole life. Marsh had built a career on it. And the only person in this town positioned to interfere was a nineteen-year-old indentured clerk whom the Commissioner had filed away in a half-second glance and forgotten.

A footnote. Perhaps.

Or perhaps footnotes were where the dangerous things lived. My father's diving logs had taught me that much: the margins were where the truth got written when the body of the page belonged to somebody else.

Down on the quay, Marsh stepped out of the tavern alone for a breath of air, grey coat, grey eyes, looking out at the black water. And I saw the moment — plain as a signal flag — when Corvus Hale detached himself from the party and followed her out, and stood too close, and began to speak with his hands moving in that smooth explanatory way, and I watched Adela Marsh listen to him with her face giving away absolutely nothing at all.

Whatever he was telling her, he was telling it first. Before the archives opened. Before the historians asked a single question. Before I could get anywhere near her with a single page of the truth.

"He's poisoning the well," I whispered.

Elias followed my gaze down. "Tonight?"

"Now. This minute." I was already moving, grabbing my shawl, my head filling with the worst plan I'd ever had, which was saying something, given the competition. "She sails with the delegation's schedule — she won't be here long, and every hour Hale talks to her alone is an hour the record gets written without us in it."

"Yara." Elias caught my wrist. "What are you going to do?"

I looked at him. I looked at the satchel under the eaves. I looked at the grey figure on the quay, listening to the enemy, deciding the shape of six years of dead.

"I'm going to introduce myself," I said, "to the woman who writes history."
