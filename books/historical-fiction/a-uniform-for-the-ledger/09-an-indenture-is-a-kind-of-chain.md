## 9. An Indenture Is a Kind of Chain

Corvus Hale did not summon people. He *requested their presence*, in a hand so immaculate it looked machine-pressed, and the request arrived on my desk at half past nine in the morning, folded once, sealed with wax the color of dried blood.

*Miss Blackwood — my office, eleven o'clock. There is a matter of your contract we should discuss.*

Your contract. Not *a matter of company business*. Not *a question regarding the ledgers*. He might as well have written *I know*, in letters a foot high, and lit it with lamps.

I read it four times while the morning traffic of the weighing house clattered past my window — carts, crates, a quartermaster shouting about tarred rope as though tarred rope were the fate of nations. Perhaps, I thought, it was. I had spent two years learning that everything in this building was connected by an invisible thread to everything else, and that the thread was always paper.

Elias was upstairs in the cooperage loft, three days mended and already restless, rewrapping the satchel's contents in oilcloth because sitting still made him itch. When I brought him the note he read it twice and went very still in the way of a man who had learned that stillness was sometimes the only weapon left.

"He knows."

"He suspects," I said. "There's a difference. Suspecting is what men like Hale do instead of thinking. He'll want me to confirm it for him. He'll want me to walk in there frightened."

"Are you frightened?"

I considered lying to him, then decided I'd done enough of that lately to want a holiday from it. "Yes. But I'm also curious, which is worse. Fear at least keeps you alive. Curiosity is how people end up in sea caves."

He almost smiled. Then he didn't. "Yara. If he offers you something—"

"He won't offer me anything. He'll offer my *contract* something." I tucked the note into my apron. "There's a difference there too, and I intend to keep hold of it."

* * *

Hale's office occupied the corner of the third floor where the light came in off the harbor, and he had arranged his furniture so that any visitor must stand with the glare behind them, squinting, while he sat coolly in shadow. It was a small trick. I had catalogued a hundred of Hale's small tricks in two years of entering his correspondence into the record, and knowing them all made them no less effective. A trick only needs to work; it doesn't need to be clever.

"Miss Blackwood." He gestured to the chair across from his desk and did not look up from whatever he was reading until I was seated, which was another trick — the suggestion that you were worth less than the page in his hand. "You've been with us six years now, haven't you."

"Seven in the spring."

"Seven." He set down the page at last. He was a spare man, Corvus Hale, gray at the temples in a way that seemed less like age than like administration, as if the gray itself had been audited and approved. "And your father before you. A diver, wasn't he. Pearl fleet."

"The pearl fleet," I agreed.

"Poor business, diving. Short careers. Shorter lives." He said it without malice, which was somehow more offensive than saying it with any. "The Company took you in when he drowned. Educated you. Fed you. Gave you a trade no one could take from you." He let that sit. "Numbers are honest work, Miss Blackwood. I've always said so. A ledger doesn't care whose name is on the entry. It cares that the entry is true."

It took genuine effort not to laugh. I converted it into a cough.

"You wanted to discuss my contract," I said.

"I did." From the drawer beside his knee — not the desk drawers, the knee drawer, the one he kept for things he wanted close to his body — he produced a single sheet and laid it face-down between us, like a card player who has already seen your hand. "Do you know what your indenture is worth?"

"It's printed on the document. Eleven pounds outstanding against training and upkeep, amortized over the remaining term."

"Eleven pounds." He nodded, as though I'd recited scripture correctly. "Eleven pounds, and four years of your labor, priced like a barrel of nails. That's what the law says you're worth. The law is often wrong in both directions." He turned the sheet over.

It was a bill of sale. My own name at the top in his immaculate hand. *Yara Blackwood, indenture purchased and discharged in full, consideration paid.* Below it, a figure. Three hundred pounds.

Three hundred pounds was what the Company paid for a seaworthy coastal brig. Three hundred pounds was eleven years of my father's best seasons, and my father had died owing eight. I stared at the number and felt it do exactly what it was designed to do: reach into my chest with cold fingers and begin, gently, patiently, to turn.

"In consideration," Hale said, "of certain documents currently unaccounted for in the archive."

"There have been discrepancies," I said carefully. "The inventory audit flagged several items."

"Don't." It was the first time his voice changed register — not louder, just lower, the way water goes quiet over deep places. "Miss Blackwood. I am going to be frank with you, because frankness is cheaper than the alternative, and I am a man who watches costs. Two years ago Captain Aldous Renn died at sea, and the Company mourned him, and the Crown decorated him posthumously, and everyone concerned was satisfied. Recently, papers have begun circulating in this port bearing his handwriting. Papers describing shipments that, if true, would embarrass the Company considerably during a week in which embarrassment could cost us the peace itself." He folded his hands. "A stranger arrived here carrying those papers. A stranger who was treated in the cooperage loft by persons unknown, and who has not been seen since. And the junior archivist responsible for the shipping correspondence — the one archivist in this building with both the access and the literacy to know what she was looking at — has been observed leaving the archive after hours."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere below, a winch groaned through its cargo.

"Who observed me?" I asked, and immediately hated myself for it, because it was a confession wearing a question's clothes.

Hale smiled faintly, the way a man smiles at a fish that has taken the hook and is now explaining why it isn't caught. "Does it matter? Here is what matters, Miss Blackwood, and I will say it once. The Company is prepared to discharge your indenture entirely. No debt, no term, no obligation. Free paper. You could walk out of this building today owning yourself for the first time in your life. Every girl in this port dreams of that arithmetic." He tapped the bill of sale. "All it costs is the return of stolen property and your silence regarding its contents. Which, I remind you, may not even be true. Forged papers are common in wartime ports. A dead man's handwriting? Sensationalism sells, but it doesn't survive cross-examination."

"And if I don't?"

The faint smile remained, but the warmth left it entirely, the way heat leaves a room when a door opens onto winter. "Then the archivist who falsified her attendance records, removed documents from a restricted archive, and harbored a fugitive from Company justice will find that eleven pounds buys a great deal of magistrate's attention. Indentured laborers who steal from their masters go to the workhouse, Miss Blackwood. Or the hulks. The hulks, I'm told, are damp." He rose, crossed to the window, and stood looking out at the harbor with his hands clasped behind him, a man surveying property. "I am not your enemy. I want you to understand that. I have read your entries. Your columns balance to the farthing. In seven years you have never once been late or wrong. Do you know what that makes you, in my estimation?"

"A tool that works," I said.

That earned me a real look — quick, appraising, almost respectful. "An asset. Assets are rare. I protect my assets." He came back around the desk and slid the bill of sale toward me, along with a pen. "You have until tomorrow evening. Think about your father. Think about what eleven pounds bought him, in the end. Salt water and a pauper's plot." His voice softened into something almost kind, which was the most frightening register it had reached all morning. "Don't let sentiment sink you the same way."

* * *

I walked back to the archive the long way, through the weighing house floor, because I needed the noise. Crates thumped. Scales clanked. A clerk argued about the tare weight of hemp, passionately, as though hemp were his mother. Ordinary life, going on around the fact that a man had just offered me three hundred pounds to become the kind of person I had spent seven years quietly refusing to be.

Here is what nobody tells you about temptation: it doesn't arrive looking like temptation. It arrives looking like relief. It arrives wearing your own face and speaking in your own voice, reasonable as rain. *Take it,* said the voice, walking beside me in my own shoes. *Three hundred pounds. Your own name on your own paper. Elias can run — he's a signalman, he knows ships, he'll be gone within the week regardless, and Renn is either alive or he isn't, and neither outcome is improved by you drowning for him. Give Hale the satchel. Keep the copies. You're clever enough to use them later, carefully, when the moment serves. This isn't surrender. This is strategy.*

And the truly humiliating thing — the thing I have never told anyone until this page — is that I stood in front of the pearl-fleet manifest for a full minute and let the voice talk. Because it wasn't wrong. That was the horror of it. Nothing Hale offered was a lie. Free paper. Safety. An honest trade. Every word true, and every word together a lie, the way a forged ledger balances perfectly and is still a forgery.

My father used to say the sea tests you twice: once with storms, which anyone can survive by holding on, and once with calm, which kills the ones who stop rowing. Hale was offering me calm. Beautiful, windless, paid-in-full calm.

I thought about the watchman's report Hale hadn't needed to make, because someone had made it for him. I thought about the way he'd said *the alternative*, cheaply, the way he'd say *freight charges*. I thought about Elias in the loft, wrapping oilcloth with a deserter's patience, trusting me with the only thing he had left because I balanced my columns to the farthing and had never once been late or wrong.

An asset. He'd called me an asset. And the worst part, the part I turned over all afternoon while my pen moved through other people's freight totals, was how much of me had spent seven years trying to *be* one — trying to be so useful, so accurate, so indispensable that the Company would someday forget I was also a purchase. Seven years of making the cage comfortable and calling it competence.

Well. The factor had shown me the price tag. Three hundred pounds, minus eleven owed, minus four years, minus one father, minus one name.

I finished the day's entries. I closed the ledger square with the desk edge, the way I always did, because some habits are mine and not theirs. And then I climbed the cooperage stairs in the blue dark and pushed open the loft door, and Elias looked up from the candle with that wariness he wore like a second uniform, and I understood that whatever I said next would decide what kind of person the rest of this story belonged to.

"He knows," I told him. "He offered me my freedom for the satchel. I have until tomorrow evening."

Elias was silent a long moment, the candle flame bending in the draft from the door. Then he said, "And what did you tell him?"

"Nothing yet," I said. "But I've decided what freedom costs, and it isn't eleven pounds."

Outside, far down the harbor, a search lantern began to sweep the waterfront — slow, deliberate, methodical, working its way toward the cooperage like a finger walking down a column of figures, looking for the line that didn't balance.
