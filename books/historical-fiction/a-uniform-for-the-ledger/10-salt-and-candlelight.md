## 10. Salt and Candlelight

### Yara

The candle was a crime.

That's what I kept telling myself as I carried it up the cooperage ladder, cupping the flame against drafts like something newborn and illegal, which in the eyes of the Meridian Trading Company it very nearly was. Unauthorized light after bell-down. Unauthorized company above an unauthorized man. I had committed perhaps forty distinct offenses since Elias Vane collapsed into my life, and I found I could now recite them the way I once recited shipping tariffs — fluently, and with a certain pride.

"You're late," he said from the loft, not looking up from the letter he was copying by feel, because I hadn't dared bring him more than one candle's worth of illumination at a time. "I thought they'd taken you."

"They took my evening," I said. "Hale inventories everything twice now. He counts the spoons." I set the candle on a barrel head. "He counted me today. Stood there while I tallied the salt cod receipts and watched my hands."

"What did your hands do?"

"What hands do when they've been taught manners. Nothing." I sat down across from him, the barrel between us like a table in a very small, very flammable tavern. "But I'm learning that stillness is its own kind of lying. I used to think lying was a thing you did with your mouth."

"And now?"

"Now I know it's a whole-body discipline. Like diving. You hold your breath and go down and try not to panic at what you find."

Elias set down his pen. In candlelight he looked less like a fugitive and more like a man someone had once loved properly — the hollows under his eyes softened to shadow instead of wound. "Yara Blackwood," he said, "you are the most alarming person I have ever met."

"I've been called worse by better-dressed men."

"Hale?"

"Hale calls me *the asset*." I pulled my boots off, one at a time, because my feet ached from standing on the counting-house floor for eleven hours pretending to be furniture. "Corvus Hale does not call people by their names. Names imply you might die and need to be missed."

Elias laughed — short, surprised out of him, the way a cork comes free. And then we were both quiet, because the laugh had done something to the air between us that neither of us knew how to undo.

The loft smelled of oak shavings and old brine. Through the gaps in the boards we could hear the harbor working its night shift: water slapping pilings, a gull arguing with nothing, far off the watch-bell marking the half hour. Somewhere below us, barrels full of legitimate commerce slept the sleep of the innocent.

"Tell me about your father," Elias said.

I looked up. "Why?"

"Because you talk about his logs like scripture, but you've never told me about the man who wrote them." He turned his pen over in his fingers. "Renn always said you can't read a code until you know the hand behind it. The cipher is just the hand, slowed down."

That was the most romantic thing anyone had ever said to me, which tells you either a great deal about me or about the men I'd known before him.

So I told him. Not all at once — I rationed it, the way you ration breath — but the candle was generous and the night was long and he listened the way few men listen, without waiting for his turn.

"My father dove for pearls fourteen years," I said. "Before the war made pearls vulgar and steel profitable. He could hold his breath four minutes. He claimed five, but my mother said he counted like a merchant, generously and in his own favor." I turned my hands over, palm up, where the old calluses had long since softened into ledger-calluses, a different trade's scars. "He taught me the knot-marks before he taught me letters. Depth, current, bottom-conditions — a diver can't shout underwater, so we wrote to each other in rope. He'd come up and I'd read his line and know exactly what the sea had told him."

"And Renn learned it."

"He must have. From the divers, during the blockade years, when the company ran boats through waters no honest chart would admit existed. The divers saw everything. They always see everything — they're the only people who look at the sea from underneath." I traced a knot-pattern on the barrel lid without thinking: three loops, a cross, a drop-line. *Deep water, clear, danger below.* "My father died in those waters. Officially. A cramp, a cold current, the usual mercies of the deep. His boat came back; he didn't. The company paid out two months' wages as condolence and docked a third for the lost gear."

Elias was very still. "You don't believe it was an accident."

"I believe the sea is a convenient graveyard for men who see things from underneath." I heard my own voice come out flat and found I didn't mind. Flat was honest. "I was twelve. I asked the factor — not Hale, his predecessor, a softer man with the same eyes — whether anyone would dive to recover the body. He said the company doesn't spend good money on bad news. Then he handed me my father's indenture, transferred to my name, and told me grief was deductible at source."

The candle flame leaned and righted itself. Elias reached across the barrel — not for me, exactly; for my hand, which happened to be in the way, which is how men describe it afterward — and held it. His palm was rough with rope-burn and gun-salt.

"That's why the letters matter so much to you," he said quietly. "Not the arms. Not the scandal. The handwriting of a man the company erased."

"My father was never even written down enough to be erased. That takes importance." I let my hand stay where it was. "Renn at least got a monument. There's a plaque in the weigh-house. My father got a deduction."

We sat with that. Some silences are empty and some are load-bearing, and this one held us both up.

Then Elias said, "Tell me about desertion."

I blinked. "Yours?"

"If you like. But I meant—" He stopped, started again, and I understood he was paying me back in kind, coin for coin. "Renn didn't fake his death alone. Someone had to report it. Someone had to swear the affidavit, identify the body — such as it was, which was mostly uniform and seawater — sign the papers that made a living man officially dead." He looked at the candle. "That someone was me."

The harbor bell struck. Neither of us moved to count it.

"He asked me," Elias went on, "three days before. Sat me down in his cabin and laid it all out — the manifests, the buyers, the names of the men on both sides of the lines taking company silver to keep the war warm. He said, *Vane, if I disappear, they'll stop looking in six months. If I resign, they'll start looking immediately.* Death is the only resignation the Meridian honors." His mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "And he said he needed a witness who could be trusted to lie. He said the signalman was the only man aboard whose whole profession was making true things legible at a distance."

"Flags," I said.

"Flags." He nodded. "I spent six years sending messages I didn't write, for purposes I wasn't told, hoisted up poles where everyone could read them and no one could ask them questions. Renn said it was time I sent one message of my own choosing." He exhaled. "So I swore my captain dead. I wept at the memorial service — genuinely, as it happens, though not for the reason printed in the program. And then I waited two years while the letters accumulated, and when Hale's auditors started pulling the divers' records — pulling *your father's* records, Yara, that's how I found your name, in the requisition lists for the archive — I ran."

"With a satchel."

"With a satchel." He looked at me across the candle. "Toward a weighing house, into the arms of a stranger, on the strength of a name in a requisition book. Renn would call that poor tradecraft."

"Renn isn't here."

"No," Elias said softly. "It's just us."

And the sentence landed between us like a dropped anchor, and after that the conversation changed registers entirely, the way conversations do when both parties finally admit what the silence has been saying for chapters.

I want to record honestly what happened next, because I have spent my life around documents that lie by omission, and I refuse to become one.

Nothing happened next. That is the truth and also the entire story.

He came around the barrel — or I went around it; the geometry is disputed and will remain disputed — and we stood close enough that I could smell salt and tallow on him, and he raised his hand toward my face as if to move a strand of hair from my cheek, an operation requiring, apparently, considerable deliberation, because his hand hovered there like a flag awaiting wind.

"Elias," I said.

"Yes."

"The lanterns."

"What lanterns—"

The lanterns.

Light swept through the gap in the boards — a slow white blade, moving along the quay below, accompanied by the particular rhythm of boots that belongs to men being paid to find something rather than men merely walking somewhere. Two voices, low. A third answering from the water side.

Elias killed the candle with his bare fingers. I felt rather than saw him fold down beside me behind the stack of staves, and we pressed ourselves into the dark like two signatures pressed into wax, and the light climbed the cooperage wall and slid across the gap in the boards and lay there, trembling, a finger's width from discovery.

A voice below — I knew it, God help me, I knew it: Dree, one of Hale's porters, a man whose wedding I had attended and whose child I had once paid the doctor's fee for — said, "Cooperage looks quiet."

"Check it anyway. Hale wants everywhere checked twice. He says the girl's clever."

My heart performed a maneuver my father would have admired: four minutes' suspension of all function.

"*Clever,*" Dree repeated, with a laugh that contained, I chose to believe, a note of defense. "She's a ledger clerk, not a smuggler."

"Clever's worse. Smugglers run. Clever ones wait." Boots crossed the yard. The door below rattled — locked, thank the drowned gods, because I had locked it, because some habits survive even moral collapse. "Locked. Nobody's home."

"Move on, then. Tide'll be turning."

The light withdrew. The boots withdrew. For a long time — I would estimate ninety years — neither Elias nor I breathed.

Then, in the black, his hand found mine again, and this time there was no pretense of stray hairs. He held on as if I were a line and he were at depth.

"They're not searching randomly anymore," he whispered. "They named you. *The girl.* Hale knows it's you."

"I know," I whispered back. "I've known since the spoons."

"Yara." His voice was very close and very unsteady, and I realized with a vertigo entirely different from the archive-night kind that the unsteadiness was not fear for himself. "When they take me — and they're going to try —"

"Don't finish sentences like that in the dark. It's how rumors get entered into the record."

"— I want it noted," he went on, stubborn as a signalman, "for the record, since you're the keeper of it. That I—"

The bell rang the hour. Three bells. And beneath it, faint but unmistakable, from the direction of the factor's house: more bells, quick ones, the alarm pattern, the one that means *turn out the watch.*

Elias's grip tightened. "That's not for us. That's too fast. Something else—"

Somewhere out on the dark harbor, a whistle blew, high and urgent, and then another, and then the sound I knew best in all the world besides my own heartbeat: running feet on quay stones, many of them, coming toward the water.

Toward the tide line. Toward the flats where, at low tide, a certain cache lay hidden under a diver's knot-mark — and where a certain watchman had lately been seen talking to a certain fugitive, and had lately stopped appearing at his post.

"Oh no," Elias breathed, and I felt him understand it a half-second before I did, or I a half-second before he did; history is unclear on the point, and I am the historian. "The watchman. The one who saw me at the flats. If Hale got to him first—if the body's still—"

He was already moving for the ladder, feeling for his boots in the dark.

"Elias—"

"They'll drag the flats at dawn. If anything's down there that shouldn't be, it needs to not be down there tonight." He paused at the ladder's top, a silhouette against the gray square of the window, and whatever he had been about to say to me hung in the air between us, unwitnessed, unsigned, real anyway. "Hold the thought," he said. "I'll come back and finish the entry."

And then he was gone down into the dark, and I was alone with a dead candle and a live alarm, listening to the bells ring out over a town that had never once made history — knowing, with the certainty of a woman who reads other people's handwriting for a living, that by morning it would have.

### Elias

Later — much later, soaked to the ribs, with lungs full of estuary and hands shaking in a way I would blame on the cold for exactly as long as anyone was polite enough to believe me — I would replay that moment at the top of the ladder and curse myself for every syllable of restraint in it.

*Hold the thought.* As if thoughts were goods in bond, safe in warehouse until the duties were paid. As if there would be time.

There is never time. That is the first lesson of signals: you send the message when the flag is up, because the flag comes down.

But I get ahead of myself, and the tide does not.

I went down the ladder faster than sense permitted, out through the cooperage yard, over the fence where a board had been loose since before the war, and onto the flats with the alarm bells still ringing behind me and the watch running ahead of me, their lanterns strung along the quay like a lit fuse burning toward the waterline.

The tide was out. The flats stretched black and gleaming to the channel, pocked with pools that held pieces of the sky, and somewhere out there, under a foot of sand marked by a loop-and-cross-and-drop-line that a dead man's friend had copied from a dead diver's log, lay the proof that the Meridian Trading Company had armed both sides of a war that had eaten eleven years and a generation and my captain's name.

And possibly, much nearer, lay a watchman named Tam Brell, who had seen me at these same flats four nights ago and had been missing from his post since yesterday noon.

I found the answer to that question before I found the cache, and I wish to God the order had reversed.

Tam Brell was not drowned. Not yet. He was sitting against a mooring post in the shallows, which is a strange place for a healthy man to sit at midnight, and when my lantern-light — I'd brought the cooperage storm-lantern, shuttered to a slit — touched his face, I saw why he was sitting.

His wrists were lashed to the post with tarred twine. His mouth was gagged. The tide, which had been out when I arrived, was beginning to consider coming back.

Breath. Four minutes, my father claimed. I didn't claim anything; I just worked. Knife out, twine cut — tarred twine, chosen by someone who wanted the job done slowly and visibly, which told me things about Hale I already knew — and Tam Brell came off that post sobbing through the gag, and when I pulled it free the first thing he said, hiccuping, half-drowned in relief, was:

"They said they'd let the tide do it. Said it'd look like accident. Said—" He grabbed my coat with both hands, a man clutching the rail of a ship he thought he'd boarded in hell. "Said the girl was next. Said if I talked, they couldn't protect her."

The world went very narrow and very loud.

"Who?" I said. "*Say the name.*"

"Hale's man. Big fellow, scarred ear. They're watching her lodging house *right now*, mate, that's the alarm, they're turning her out—"

I did not run to the cache. Let the record show that. The arms manifests — the whole case, the whole war's secret, the thing we had bled and lied and nearly hanged for — sat under its little knot-marked stone fifty yards out on the flats, and I left it there, because some entries cannot wait for the official form.

I ran for the lodging houses.

Behind me the bells kept ringing, and ahead of me the lanterns of Hale's men were already moving up the street toward the door where Yara Blackwood slept — and I ran through the shining pools with the sky breaking apart under my feet, understanding at last, in the way that only matters at a dead sprint, precisely how short the time was.

Not just for the evidence.

For everything.
