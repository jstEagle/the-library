## 11. The Watchman's Price

The watchman's name was Tam Brellery, and I knew it because I'd spent two nights memorizing it.

That's the thing about being a signalman — you learn to hold names the way other men hold knives. Names are coordinates. *Tam Brellery, harbor watch, third shift, lantern habitually trimmed low, drinks at the Anchor's Tooth on his half-nights.* I had written him into my head the way you'd mark a reef on a chart: here be the man who saw me hauling a oilskin-wrapped bundle off the mudflats at low tide, and who had the good sense or the bad luck to remember my face.

I'd been careful after that night. Careful the way you're careful when you know the current has already got a hand on your ankle. I stopped going out before full dark. Yara started bringing the food up to the loft herself instead of leaving it with the cooper's boy. We spoke in the divers' cipher when we could, little knots of meaning tied in pencil strokes along the margins of her practice ledgers, and if anyone found them they'd read like arithmetic gone mad.

For six days it worked.

On the seventh day, the sea gave Tam Brellery back.

* * *

I heard about it the way you hear everything in a port town — sideways, from someone who wanted to be the one telling it. The cooper's boy came up the ladder two steps at a time, breathless with the particular joy children take in catastrophe that isn't theirs.

"They pulled a watchman out by the pilings," he said. "Drowned. Fell in drunk, they reckon."

"Fell in," I said. "Drunk."

"That's what they reckon."

Children repeat what they're told. That's why you never say anything true in front of one. I tipped him a coin for the news and sat very still on the edge of the cot until his footsteps faded down the stairs, and then I did something I hadn't done since the war: I put my head between my knees and breathed like a man counting the seconds between shellfire.

Because Tam Brellery didn't drink. Not properly. He drank at the Anchor's Tooth the way a man waters a horse — enough to keep up appearances, never enough to lose his footing on wet planks he walked every night of his working life. A watchman who fell off the harbor wall was a watchman who'd failed at the only skill his job required, which was standing up.

And a watchman who'd seen a stranger pull arms manifests out of the mud at low tide — a watchman who might have mentioned this to someone, or been overheard mentioning it, or simply been *known* to have seen something — that watchman was not an accident. That watchman was a loose thread, and somebody with a needle had come along and snipped him clean off.

I knew whose needle. You always know. The question is whether knowing does you any good at all.

* * *

Yara came up at dusk with bread and a look on her face that told me she already knew. She set the plate down without taking off her coat, which she never did — the coat came off first, always, a ritual as fixed as prayer, because the loft was the one place in Meridian Harbor where she wasn't indentured property.

"You heard," she said.

"The boy told me. Drowned. Drunk."

"Corvus Hale posted a notice offering a reward for information regarding 'a suspicious person seen near the tidelands.'" She recited it flatly, the way she recited ledger entries, which is its own kind of fury. "It describes you. Badly. They think you're taller."

"Everyone thinks I'm taller. It's the posture." I meant it as a joke and it landed somewhere near one, and then neither of us laughed, because there was a dead man between us now, a man with a name and a family and probably a coat with his name stitched inside the collar, and jokes were just another way of drowning.

She sat down on the crate across from me. In the candlelight her face had the stillness I'd learned to read in her — not calm, never calm, but *organized*, every feeling filed and cross-referenced before it was allowed above decks.

"I keep thinking," she said, "that if I hadn't taken the satchel. If I'd let the guards have him that day at the weighing house—"

"Then he'd be dead anyway, and so would we. Hale doesn't leave witnesses. You saw the manifests. You've *memorized* them, God help you, you copied them into the margins of grain ledgers like a woman hiding scripture in a hymnal." I leaned forward. "Yara. Listen to me, because I've run from these people before and I'm better at it than you'll ever need to be. There is no version of the last three weeks where you walk away clean. There's only versions where you walk away at all."

"That's supposed to comfort me?"

"It's supposed to orient you. Comfort's for afterward, if there is an afterward."

She looked at me for a long moment. Outside, the harbor bells rang the change of tide, and somewhere below us the cooper's dog barked at nothing, at everything, at the general condition of the world.

"Tam Brellery has a daughter," she said finally. "Eight years old. She comes to the weighing house sometimes with pennies for candied ginger. I've sold her the ginger myself, twice." Her voice didn't break. It did something worse — it went quieter, flatter, the voice of a woman entering a figure into a column that would never balance. "Someone will tell her it was the drink. And she'll grow up ashamed of her father for a thing he never did, and that shame will be the official record, because nobody writes down the corrections."

And there it was. The whole of her, in one sentence. I'd known soldiers who could face a charge across open ground and I'd never once seen one of them say anything half so dangerous. Grief makes most people smaller. It was making her larger, and it terrified me more than Hale ever could, because Hale only wanted us dead and Yara wanted something that couldn't be killed by any means I knew.

"We move," I said. "Tonight. There's a fishing smack going down the coast on the morning tide, the master owes Renn a favor older than the war—"

"No."

"*No?*"

"Not yet." She stood, and took the folded paper from inside her coat — the decoded page, the one with the ship names and dates and the buyer's marks, the page that proved the Meridian Trading Company had armed both sides of a war it claimed to mourn. She held it the way other people hold infants. "Commissioner Adela Marsh arrives within the week. Her historians will write the record of how this war ended. If we run, we're two fugitives with a story nobody prints. If we stay—" 

"If we stay, we're two corpses with a story nobody prints either. Yara, they killed a watchman. A *watchman*. Do you understand what that costs? Killing a company employee isn't cheap — it takes arrangement, it takes silence, it takes men willing to do it and other men willing to file it as misadventure. Hale didn't spend that money because Brellery *might* talk. He spent it because the net is closing and he can afford to cut away anything that moves. Including us. *Especially* us."

"I know." She said it softly, and that was the worst part. She wasn't naive. She'd done the sums; she'd always done the sums faster than me. "I know exactly what it costs, Elias. I've been keeping accounts since I was nine years old. I'm telling you the price is worth paying."

I wanted to argue. I want that noted, somewhere, by someone — that I argued, that the sensible half of me, the half that had survived Renn's fall and the retreat at Corrin Sound and two years of running, stood up on its hind legs and said *run, you fool, run while your legs still answer*. But I looked at her standing there in the candle-smoke with a dead watchman's daughter somewhere asleep across town, and I understood that I had stopped making decisions about survival somewhere around the time a bleeding stranger collapsed among the cargo scales and a pearl diver's daughter lied to a factor's face for him.

You don't get to choose the moment your life changes hands. You only find out afterward, doing the audit, exactly when the entry was made.

"When Marsh's ship comes in," I said at last, "Hale will be watching the crowd. So will half the company guard."

"Yes."

"And we'll be in it."

"Yes."

I exhaled slowly. "Then we'd better decide which of us she sees first, because we will not get two introductions."

* * *

I didn't sleep. I went to the loft window instead and watched the harbor do its nighttime work, lanterns bobbing, the tide sliding black and patient under the pier heads. Somewhere out there was the piling where they'd found Tam Brellery, snagged by his own belt, they said, though a belt that catches you is a strange kindness from the sea.

Here is what I know about drowning, because I trained men in signaling and part of the training is what happens when the signal goes wrong and you end up in the water: drowning men don't shout. The films and the ballads lie. A drowning man is busy, quietly, desperately busy, and the last thing he does is go under without a sound, and the people on the shore never even turn their heads.

Tam Brellery had seen something he shouldn't have, and he'd probably done the ordinary decent thing — mentioned it to a colleague, maybe, or failed to mention it loudly enough, or simply existed as a fact in someone's report. And the sea had closed over him without a sound, and the town had turned its head, and by morning the whole harbor agreed he'd been drunk, because a story everyone repeats is the closest thing to true that most people ever need.

That was the moment the cold arrived. Not fear exactly — fear is hot, fear is animal, fear wants your legs to move. This was colder and older than fear. It was the understanding that we had crossed some line on a chart I hadn't known was there. Three weeks ago we were hiding evidence. Evidence is a thing you protect, a thing with weight and value, a thing you smuggle and conceal and bargain with. But you don't hide evidence anymore when the other side starts killing people. When they start killing people, you understand that the evidence was never the point — *you* were the point, all along, the satchel was just the excuse, and the game has changed from smuggling to surviving.

We were no longer couriers. We were quarry.

And quarry, if they're lucky, if they're quick, if they're willing to spend themselves utterly — quarry can still choose the ground.

Below me, a lantern moved along the quay. Just one. Just a watchman making rounds, probably, some honest tired man walking his shift, and I watched him until my eyes watered and told myself that most lanterns are only lanterns.

But I counted his pace. I noted his route. I marked him on the chart in my head, the way you mark a reef: *here be the place where the water hides its teeth.*

The bells rang the turn of the tide. Two days, maybe three, until the delegation's guns saluted the peace and Commissioner Adela Marsh stepped onto a gangway into a town where a man had drowned for looking too closely.

Two days to decide how much of ourselves we were willing to spend to make the record true.

I went back to the cot and lay down in my clothes, and if I slept at all, it was the way sailors sleep in weather — lightly, with one hand closed around something that matters, listening for the sound the sea makes just before it takes what it came for.
