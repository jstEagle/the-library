## 12. Commissioner Marsh's Ear

The delegation had been in town four days before I understood that Commissioner Adela Marsh took her tea the same way my father took his last breath: slowly, with great attention, and in a manner that suggested she knew exactly how it would end.

I learned this by watching. Watching is what I was made for. A diver learns to read water from above — the dark seams where pearls hide, the treacherous shimmer of current — and I had learned to read ledgers the same way, finding the places where numbers swam out of formation. So while Hale fawned and the town hung bunting crooked from every eave, I watched Commissioner Marsh.

She arrived at the weighing house each morning at half past eight precisely, escorted by two clerks who carried her papers like relics. She wore grey. Not the grey of mourning or modesty, but the grey of a woman who had decided color was a distraction from argument. She listened to Hale's tour with her hands folded and her face arranged into an expression I can only describe as *patiently unconvinced*, and when he gestured at the harbor and spoke of the company's loyal service to the war effort, she nodded in a way that meant nothing whatsoever, which is a skill I recognized immediately, because I had spent two years nodding like that at Corvus Hale.

She was writing the official history of the war. That was her commission — sealed, salaried, signed by men whose names appeared on documents I filed daily. Every battle, every blockade, every heroic death would pass through her hands and come out the other side as permanent fact. And Captain Aldous Renn, dead hero, would be in it. Already was, probably, in draft. A fine chapter. A closed book.

Except he wasn't dead, and the book was a lie, and I held forty pages of proof in cipher that said so.

The problem — and I want to be honest about this, because honesty became expensive for me around this time — the problem was not courage. The problem was procedure.

* * *

Elias thought it was madness. He said so in the cooperage loft on the third night, in the low voice he used for things that frightened him, which was a very short list and apparently now included me.

"You walk up to the one person in this town who can have you arrested with a word," he said, "carrying stolen company documents, decoded with a dead man's cipher, based on testimony from a deserter." He counted these off on his fingers like entries in a ledger. "Yara. In the navy we called that a firing squad with extra steps."

"I'm not going to walk up to her," I said. "I'm going to leave something where she finds it."

"That's worse."

"It's better. Anonymous means deniable. Deniable means alive."

He sat on the edge of the cot with his bandaged shoulder hunched forward, and for a moment he looked so young that my chest hurt. Then his jaw set in that signalman's way, all business, and he said, "If you do this, she'll know someone local decoded it. The cipher isn't navy standard. She'll start asking who in this port reads divers' marks."

"Then I'll make sure what she finds tells her exactly what I want her to think."

"And what's that?"

I had thought about this for three days, turning it over during inventory, during supper, during the long grey hour after the lamps went out. "That Renn is alive, and that the truth is worth more to her than the signing is."

Elias laughed — one dry note without any humor in it. "Commissioners don't work that way."

"Then I'll learn how they do work," I said, "the same way I learned everything else. By watching."

He looked at me for a long time then, and whatever he saw in my face made him stop arguing, which I suspected even then would prove to be a tactical error on his part. He only said, "One page. Not the manifests. One page, and nothing that names the cache."

"One page," I agreed. "And nothing that names the cache."

* * *

Choosing the page took longer than choosing the plan.

It couldn't be the arms manifests themselves — too explosive, too traceable, and if Marsh confiscated them I'd have surrendered everything for nothing. It couldn't name Elias or the loft. It couldn't mention Hale directly, because an accusation against the company factor, unsigned, would be dismissed as grudge or lunacy before the ink dried.

What it could be was arithmetic.

I copied out a single decoded passage: dates, ship names, tonnage. Three shipments over fourteen months, grain ships outward bound, returning light. The discrepancy between declared and actual cargo, laid side by side with the company's own published manifests — which I had filed myself, in triplicate — left exactly one conclusion, and the conclusion pointed at both sides of the war buying from the same neutral-flagged holds. Armistice-breaking arms, sold to enemies simultaneously, under the flag of the celebrated Meridian Trading Company.

I wrote no accusations. Accusations are opinions. I wrote sums. Sums are facts, and facts are the one thing a historian cannot ignore, however much she might wish to.

I copied it in a plain mercantile hand — not my ledger hand, which Hale could identify across a room; I'd practiced a slanted, careless script on discarded wrapping paper until it looked like the penmanship of a tired clerk with something to hide. Which, I reflected, it now essentially was.

* * *

Getting the page to her was almost insultingly easy, which should have warned me.

Marsh took her morning walk along the seawall at seven o'clock, alone, before the clerks arrived. I learned this by watching, as I say, and on the fifth morning I went down before dawn with my page folded inside a merchant's invoice — genuine, lifted from the weighing house discard pile — weighted with a stone, and set it on the seawall bench where she always paused to look at the water.

I did not linger. Lingering is how people get caught, and besides, I wanted to watch her find it, and watching required distance and the patience of a diver hanging weightless over a pearl bed.

She came at seven o'clock precisely. She sat. She looked at the water for perhaps a minute — and I swear she looked at it the way my father looked at the sea, not with love but with assessment, measuring what it owed him — and then she picked up the invoice, unfolded it, and read.

I was thirty yards away, mending a net I had no intention of finishing, and I watched Commissioner Adela Marsh's face do something extraordinary, which was nothing at all. No flicker, no widening of the eyes, none of the theater of surprise. She simply read the page twice, folded it along its original creases with a precision I found deeply unsettling, and put it inside her coat.

Then she stood, brushed off her grey skirt, and walked back toward the delegation house without once looking around.

That night I told Elias she'd taken it. He asked what she'd done with it. I told him she'd folded it. He frowned and said that meant nothing to him, and I said, "It means everything. People who intend to dismiss a document crumple it. People who intend to destroy it burn it. People who fold it are keeping it."

"Keeping it for what?"

"For evidence," I said, and then heard myself, and laughed, because I had just described the commissioner's behavior in the language of my own trade. "For *evidence*, Elias. God help me, I've become one of them."

"No," he said quietly. "You've become the opposite of them. They write history first and let the truth catch up. You're doing it backwards."

I didn't tell him that being called the opposite of them felt, just then, less like praise than like a diagnosis.

* * *

She summoned me two days later, which I had not anticipated, and which nearly ended everything before it began.

Not me specifically — that was the genius and horror of it. A notice went up at the weighing house, posted among the tariff announcements: *All persons holding information material to the historical record of the war may present written statements to the Commission's office, hours nine to eleven, until the signing.* An open door. A bureaucratic invitation, printed in tidy type, offering exactly what I'd risked everything to arrange — and thereby making anonymous contact impossible, because now anyone walking through that door would be *logged*. Name, trade, statement, entered into the record.

Hale read that notice aloud at morning assembly with something oily in his voice. "The Commission welcomes cooperation," he said, eyes moving across the assembled staff like a man counting coins. "The company encourages all employees to assist the Commissioner in her important work. Loyalty to the record, after all, is loyalty to the peace."

Which meant, translated from Hale: *anyone who walks through that door will be reported to me within the hour.*

So I did the thing that still amazes me when I look back on it. I waited until eleven o'clock, when the office closed, and I went anyway.

* * *

The delegation house had been some shipping magnate's town residence before the war, and it still smelled of other people's money — beeswax and cold marble. A clerk at the door informed me the hours were over. I told him I had worked until the bell rang and come straight away, which was true, which I had learned was the most efficient kind of lie: built entirely of true parts.

He looked at my company uniform — I was still wearing it, deliberately, the drab grey-blue of Meridian indentured staff — and hesitated. And that was when the voice came from inside, level and unhurried:

"Let her in, Pell. Hours are for people with something to hide from their employers."

I walked into a room lined with document cases and found Commissioner Marsh at a desk beneath the window, and she looked at me the way she'd looked at the sea from the seawall: assessment, measurement, the calculation of what I owed her.

"You're the diver's daughter," she said. "Blackwood. You keep the ledgers at the weighing house."

My blood went cold and stayed cold, which was useful; panic makes a poor clerk. "Yes, Commissioner."

"Hale mentioned you. Called you the best filing mind he has." She turned a paper on her desk so that it faced me. It was my page. My careful, anonymous, deniable page, sitting in the open like a confession. "This was left on a public bench. Unsigned. But whoever wrote it knows company manifests intimately — knows them better than the company does, I'd wager, since the errors are invisible in the originals and glaring beside them. There are perhaps six people in this port capable of producing it. You're one."

I said nothing. Silence is a diver's first skill: go quiet, go still, and let the water decide.

"You're wearing your uniform," she continued. "You came after hours, knowing you'd be seen and reported. Either you're extraordinarily foolish, or you wanted me to know exactly who you were, and to understand that you came anyway." She leaned back. "Which is it?"

And there it was — the trap and the door in a single question. I could deny, and she would believe the denial the way she believed Hale's harbor tour, patiently and completely unconvincingly, and the page would die in a drawer. Or I could step through.

"The company armed both sides of the war," I said. "I can prove it. Not all of it — not yet. Enough."

Something moved behind her eyes then, brief as fish-shadow. "Do you understand what you're saying? Do you understand what happens to the armistice if this comes out mid-negotiation? Both delegations learn their counterparts were armed by the same neutral concern. Trust collapses. The signing fails. The war resumes." She said it flatly, reciting consequences, not threatening — or claiming not to threaten, which in a commissioner is the same posture with different tailoring. "There are men at that table who want this peace more than they want justice. Justice can wait a year. Another winter of that war cannot."

"Justice can wait," I agreed. "But records don't. Records get altered. I've seen it happen." I hadn't, yet — not with my own eyes — but I knew it the way you know weather coming. "Every day between now and the signing, pages go missing, entries get corrected, witnesses grow uncertain. If your history waits for the peace to be comfortable, Commissioner, it will be written by whoever is most comfortable when the waiting ends. And I promise you that is not me, and it is not the truth."

She was quiet for a long moment. Outside, gulls argued over the harbor refuse, and somewhere below a winch groaned through its cargo.

"You want it entered in the record before the signing," she said at last. Not a question.

"I want it entered in *any* record. Yours would be the one that survives."

"And if entering it costs the peace?"

"Then the peace was built on the smuggling," I said, "and it deserved to cost more."

She studied me for what felt like the length of a full tide, and I had the distinct, vertiginous sense of being weighed — properly weighed, not the company's dishonest scales but true ones, the kind my father trusted with his life twice a day.

"Here is what will happen," she said finally, and her voice changed, became the voice of a woman reading terms aloud. "You will bring me the complete documentation — manifests, ciphers, testimony — through channels we establish. Quietly. If it proves what this page suggests, I will consider entering a sealed annex alongside the armistice: the record preserved, the scandal deferred, the peace intact. Everyone's interests served."

"*Deferred*," I repeated.

"History is patient, Miss Blackwood. It's people who aren't." She rose, signaling the audience over, and added, as though it were an afterthought, "Corvus Hale dines with our senior negotiators tomorrow evening. He has already offered the Commission the company's full cooperation and its complete archives. Complete, he assures me. Nothing missing."

She let that sit between us like a stone dropped into still water.

"So you see the difficulty," she said. "Two versions of completeness, arriving on my desk. I will require yours to be better."

I walked back to the weighing house through streets strung with bunting, past windows full of celebration preparations, carrying the strangest cargo of my life: hope. Hope with conditions attached, hope wrapped in red tape and deferrals and sealed annexes — bureaucratic hope, hope that moved at the speed of committees.

But it was hope. For the first time since the satchel landed in my arms among the cargo scales, someone with power had held the truth in her hands and folded it instead of burning it.

I was halfway home, rehearsing how I'd tell Elias, when I saw the lanterns.

Company lanterns, six of them, moving fast along the quay toward the cooperage district. And running ahead of them, doubling through the fish-market alleys with the desperate economy of a man who had been a runner in a war where runners died first — a figure I knew by the set of his shoulders.

They had followed me. Of course they had; Hale's notice had been a net and I had walked through it on schedule. And whatever Marsh and I had built in that marble room, it would not survive the night if Elias Vane was dragged out of the cooperage loft with the satchel still under his floorboard.

I turned and ran the other way — toward Hale's office, toward the one thing I could still steal that might buy his freedom — and behind me the lanterns swung wider, closing on the water like a hand.
