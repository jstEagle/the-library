## 13. Renn Lives

### Yara

The message came on a Tuesday, which felt insulting. Catastrophes should arrive on Sundays, when a person has had time to prepare spiritually.

It arrived the way most dangerous things in Saltmere did — folded inside something boring. A fishmonger's boy pressed a twist of oilcloth into my hand outside the weighing house and said it was "the change owed from last week," which was nonsense, because I hadn't bought fish, because I couldn't afford fish, because the Meridian Trading Company paid its indentured clerks in a currency that could be exchanged for nothing except more indenture. But I took the oilcloth, thanked him gravely for his accounting error, and did not open it until I was alone in the archive with three ledgers of plausible lies spread out around me like a defensive fortification.

Inside was a strip of paper covered in knots.

Not rope knots — drawn ones, small circles with tails and crossings, arranged in rows of five. My father's diving logs had been full of them. For years I'd assumed they were tally marks, some diver's private arithmetic of breaths held and pearls found. It wasn't until Elias sat across my father's old table with the letters spread between us, both of us ink-stained to the wrist like a pair of counterfeit saints, that we understood what they actually were: depth markers. Each knot a fathom, each crossing a turn, each tail a direction. The divers had kept their own record of the harbor floor for two generations — where the company said the beds were barren and where the divers knew oysters clustered thick as gossip. The company never learned the code. Why would they? The code belonged to people whose knowledge had never been worth writing down.

Captain Aldous Renn had learned it.

The message decoded to nine words, and I read them four times before the meaning stopped sliding off me like wet rope:

*The Gullet cave. Tomorrow's low tide. Come armed with truth.*

"Armed with truth," Elias repeated when I showed him, and made a sound that was half laugh, half something worse. "He always talked like that. Signal officers spend their whole careers compressing enormous things into tiny messages. Then retirement rots them and they start writing like funeral orations."

"You'd know. You deserted him."

"I was reassigned," Elias said, with the precision of a man who had rehearsed the word *deserted* until he could survive hearing it. He looked at the paper again. "If this is a trap, Yara—"

"It's in his hand."

"It's in a *code only he knows*. That's better and worse." He rubbed his eyes. "Better because Hale can't forge it. Worse because if Renn wrote it, Renn is alive, and everything we've done for two months — every risk, every lie, your father's logs dragged out of a sea chest — all of it just became real instead of theoretical. Theoretical gets you hanged slowly. Real gets you hanged Tuesday."

"Today is Tuesday."

"Then we're ahead of schedule."

I want to record, honestly, what I felt that night, because memoirs lie about feelings even more than ledgers lie about cargo. I did not feel brave. I felt the specific vertigo of a woman who has spent her life believing dead men stay dead, discovering that the world is less orderly than that — and more possible. Somewhere under the water my father had swum, a man the whole coast mourned was alive and writing in the language of pearl divers, and I could not decide whether I wanted to kiss the messenger or be sick over the tide tables.

I settled for neither. I checked the moon, marked the low tide at 4:17 in the morning, and told Elias to bring the manifests and a knife.

He asked why I needed the knife.

"For cutting rope," I said. "Obviously."

Neither of us believed me, and neither of us said so. That, I have since learned, is the truest form of intimacy available to people in our situation.

### Elias

Low tide at the Gullet is a lesson in how the sea keeps secrets. At high water it's a black slot in the cliff that swallows swell and swimmers alike; at slack ebb you can walk in upright, dripping ceiling, floor ribbed with stone, and forty feet back — past the point where daylight gives up pretending — a shelf of dry rock where smugglers have been leaving offerings since before the war made smuggling treasonous instead of merely profitable.

We went separately, as agreed. Yara by the cliff path with a storm lantern shuttered to a coin of light; me along the shingle with the satchel strapped flat against my ribs, walking like a man with no interests whatsoever, which after eleven weeks of practice I nearly managed.

The cave announced Renn before I saw him. A smell — woodsmoke long cold, tar, and under it the sweetish reek of a wound tended too long without proper dressing. Then a voice from the dark, low and level, the voice of a man who had spent years making himself heard over guns:

"That's far enough, Vane. Show me your hands and say something only an idiot would know."

My throat went strange. "You called me 'Signalman' for eight months because you couldn't be bothered learning my name."

A pause. Then, roughened: "I knew your name. I was waiting for you to earn the right to hear me use it." And Captain Aldous Renn stepped out of the dark, holding a lantern, and I finally understood what two years of being officially dead does to a man.

He was thinner than the portrait on the recruitment posters — thinner than the man who'd stood on the *Meridian Star*'s quarterdeck and lied beautifully to admirals while passing me coded orders under the flag locker. His beard had gone half grey in places no thirty-eight-year-old's beard should go grey. His left arm hung in a sling made from, God help us, a piece of signal bunting, faded ceremonial blue, knotted with a diver's hitch. And his eyes were the worst part. They were still intelligent. They were still absolutely awake. Everything else had been spent, but the eyes were hoarding something, and I recognized the expression because I'd seen it on survivors of the bombardment at Cape Ferren: the look of a man who has decided to stay alive specifically out of spite toward people who would prefer otherwise.

"Captain," I said.

"Don't." He said it fast, like stopping a bleed. "Not yet. First — show me."

I unstrapped the satchel. He took the manifests and read them standing, one-handed, and I watched the reading move through him like weather. When he reached the page listing the *Cormorant's Grace* — the ship that carried Meridian rifles to the northern militia eleven days before the massacre at Harrow Field — his jaw worked once, twice, and he set the papers down on the stone shelf with exaggerated care, the way men do when the alternative is throwing them.

"Dates correct," he said quietly. "Buyers correct. That's the third copy I've seen and the only one I believe, because you two stole it rather than copied it. Copiers make improvements." He looked up. "Who read the logs?"

Yara's light came around the bend of the cave then, and I watched the captain see her — really see her, the way he'd once assessed a shoreline for landing. Small woman, diver's shoulders, ink on her fingers, lantern steady.

"I did," she said. "They were my father's."

"Tam Blackwood's daughter." Something moved through his face. "He taught me the knot-code. Did you know that?"

She went very still. "No."

"Three winters ago. He was diving the deep beds off the Star's anchorage — company contract, company rates, which is to say robbery — and I caught him marking positions in knots on his float-line tags. I thought it was superstition. He offered to teach me for the price of a bottle, and I paid it, and I have thought about that bottle every day since, because it's the only honest transaction I was ever part of in that company's service." He picked the manifests up again. "Your father's code is the only reason these exist, Miss Blackwood. When I decided to disappear, I wrote my evidence in knots because I knew no company clerk could read them. I didn't know his daughter could."

"He drowned," Yara said, in the voice of someone setting down something heavy very carefully, "two years ago. The same season you died."

"The same month," Renn said. "I know. I've had time to learn things, down here. Tam didn't drown diving. He was coming back from the deep beds with samples — proof of what the company was pulling out of waters they'd sworn to the Admiralty were empty. Proof they were selling salvage rights to both sides. He drowned the week after he told me what he'd found." The lantern flame bent in the cave draft. "I don't have proof it was murder. I want to be precise about that, because precision is all we'll have when they come for us. But I have the pattern, and the pattern is Corvus Hale's signature: loose threads cut cleanly, accidents arranged tidily, and the ledger balanced."

Yara didn't cry. I want that recorded too. She stood in a sea cave at four in the morning, twenty feet from a dead man, being told her father's death might be a line item, and she did not cry. She took out her notebook — the cheap one, salt-warped, that she'd been carrying since the cooperage — and she said:

"Then give me the pattern. All of it. Dates. Ships. Names. If it goes in the record, it goes in complete."

Renn looked at her for a long moment. Then at me. And whatever he saw in my face made him laugh — short, cracked, genuine.

"You've recruited well, Vane."

"She recruited herself. I mostly bled on her floor."

"Efficient." He lowered himself onto the stone shelf with the care of a man negotiating with his own body, and I saw him favor his left side, and I saw, beneath the sling, the ruin of what had been a shoulder. "Sit. Both of you. What follows takes an hour and you will write every word, Miss Blackwood, because I may not live to repeat it, and unlike the company, we're going to keep our books honest."

### Yara

Here is what I learned in the Gullet cave, in the hour before dawn, from the mouth of a man the nation had buried with honors:

That in the winter of the war's second year, the Meridian Trading Company began running arms through the archipelago under bills of lading marked as salt cod. That the operation was authorized by no more than five names, Corvus Hale's among them, and structured so that any single name could deny any single shipment. That the buyers included the northern militia *and* two southern privateer fleets — the company arming both banks of the same river of blood, because war was profitable and peace, to men like Hale, was a market correction. That Captain Aldous Renn discovered this not through heroism but through bookkeeping: as signals officer he handled the convoy clearances, and clearances, like all paper, eventually contradict themselves. That when he began asking questions, he was invited to dinner, promoted, praised, and scheduled — he used the word *scheduled*, and let it sit there — for a reconnaissance run everyone privately expected to lose him. That he chose instead to die publicly and live obscurely, because a dead man can watch, and a celebrated corpse makes poor bait.

"And the wound?" Elias asked, near the end.

"Cape Ferren. After my death, I stayed close enough to watch the shipments. Got careless in March. Company soldier, not a soldier's aim." Renn shifted the ruined arm in its bunting sling. "It's mending. Slowly. The sea's good medicine if you can afford the room rates, and the Gullet doesn't charge."

He dictated. I wrote. Dates, hulls, masters' names, the codewords used between Hale's office and the fleet — including one, *graywater*, that I had personally entered forty times into the company's own ledgers without ever once being told what it purchased. Forty entries. I had written the crime myself, in a fair hand, with the date and the page number, because the company valued neatness above all things.

When he finished, the cave was full of the sound of the tide beginning to return, that deep swallowed breathing in the rock behind us.

"There's a last thing," Renn said. He drew something from inside his coat: a folded paper, sealed with plain wax, no mark. "My full confession. Signed, witnessed by no one but God and an oystercatcher. If anything happens to me, it must reach Commissioner Marsh — Marsh, mind, not her secretaries, not Hale's friends on her staff. She's a bureaucrat, which means she's a coward with a conscience, and cowards with consciences can be steered if you get to them before the signing." He put it in my hands, not Elias's. I noted that. So did Elias. "You're the one who understands what paper costs. You've already paid more than I have."

"Hardly," I said.

"More honestly, then." He looked at me with those hoarded eyes. "Miss Blackwood. Your father believed the sea should belong to the people who worked it. I believe the same about history. Between us we've produced a great deal of paperwork to that effect. Try not to drown carrying it."

Elias laughed, and I almost did, and then the tide breathed again behind us, closer, and Renn said, "Go. Separate ways. And Vane—" 

"Captain?"

"Use the east path. There's a watchman on the west stairs who owes Hale money." He paused. "There's always a watchman who owes Hale money. It's how he builds his walls. Out of other men's debts."

We climbed out as the sky went the color of dishwater, and I remember thinking, absurdly, that I had just taken dictation from a ghost in a cave, and that my father would have found this hilarious, and that I would tell him someday, somehow, in whatever ledger the drowned keep.

What I did not know — what none of us knew, standing in that grey morning feeling, for the first time in two months, like the trap had become the hunters — was that the watchman on the west stairs had already earned his evening's pay. That he had seen a woman's lantern moving along the cliff path at four in the morning, and known whose route that was. And that by the time I reached the weighing house to begin my shift, Corvus Hale was already standing at my desk, turning my father's diving log over in his clean white hands, reading the knots.

He looked up at me and smiled the smile of a man balancing his books.

"Good morning, Miss Blackwood," he said. "I wonder if you could help me with a translation."
