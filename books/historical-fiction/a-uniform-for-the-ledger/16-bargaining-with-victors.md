## 16. Bargaining with Victors

The pen was still wet when I turned around.

Two people stood in the doorway of the archive, and the candle I'd carried threw their shadows long across the shelves, so that for a moment they looked like four people, then six, then a congregation. The one who had spoken was a woman of perhaps fifty, dressed in delegation grey with a commissioner's chain at her throat, and she held a lantern that made her face look carved rather than lived-in. Behind her stood a soldier with the bored posture of a man who had been woken twice already tonight and expected to be woken once more.

"Commissioner Marsh," I said, because there was no sense pretending otherwise. My voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect. "You told your man you knew I'd come."

"I know ledgers," Adela Marsh said, stepping into the aisle. She did not reach for me. She did not call for the guard. She looked past my shoulder at the open ledger on Hale's desk — at Hale's half-finished line of ink, and beneath it, where I had set down my own hand before his key found its lock — and something moved behind her eyes that might have been appetite.

"May I?" she said, and lifted the book without waiting for permission, turning it to the light.

For a long moment she read. The archive was very quiet. Somewhere above us the building ticked and settled, the way old buildings do, like animals dreaming. I thought absurdly of my father's hands, of the way he used to hold a shell up to the light before opening it, as if asking it first.

"You copied arms manifests into shipping columns," she said at last. It wasn't a question. "Dates, hull numbers, buyers. In the margins. In a cipher I don't recognize but which is, I'm told, consistent throughout." She closed the ledger gently, the way one closes a door on a sickroom. "Do you know what this is, Miss Blackwood?"

"Falsified history," I said. "Being written by Corvus Hale, an hour ago, by candlelight. I watched him do it."

"Yes. And what you've done is worse."

I confess my stomach dropped. "Worse?"

"Hale falsifies records because he believes no one will ever check them. That's a sin of arrogance. But you—" she tapped the cover of the ledger once, sharply— "you have hidden true entries inside false ones, inside *company* records, in a private code, and then come here alone at night to move them into the official archive. Do you understand how that looks? To a tribunal? To a historian?" She smiled without warmth. "It looks like a clerk who has been altering books and has now been caught mid-alteration. Hale will have you hanged with your own handwriting, and he will be *thanked* for it."

The candle guttered between us. I became aware, distantly, of how cold the archive was, and of how far the door was, and of the fact that Elias had been taken eleven days ago and no one had said his name aloud to me since.

"You came here tonight," I said slowly, "knowing I would."

"I've known about you since the morning after the cooperage raid. A diver's daughter who requests the same ledgers three times, whose ink purchases doubled in a fortnight, who asked the harbormaster's clerk — very casually, very badly — which ships kept logs from the autumn of the blockade." She set the ledger back on the desk, precisely square with its edge. "You are the worst conspirator I have ever encountered, and I say that with genuine admiration, because you are also the only person in this town who has spent two years touching the truth every single day and writing it down correctly. Do you know how rare that is? Everyone else I meet either doesn't know the truth or has already sold it."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you came here to bargain, Miss Blackwood. With Hale, I assume — or against him; the distinction blurs. You meant to trade something. Your silence, your testimony, your father's logs, yourself." Her lantern rose slightly, illuminating my face, and I understood that she had catalogued me already: my value, my weaknesses, my price. "I am simply informing you that I am the better counterparty. I always am."

* * *

We sat at Hale's own desk, which I imagine would have pleased him if he could have seen it — the victor's furniture repurposed for the victor's unmaking. The soldier withdrew to the door. Marsh poured nothing, offered nothing, folded her hands.

"Terms first," she said. "Mine, so you may reject them properly. Elias Vane is in the company lockhouse under a charge of desertion that predates anything he did in this town. That charge belongs to the Admiralty, not to me, and the Admiralty does not negotiate. However." She let the word hang. "A deserter who can be shown to have acted under orders — Captain Renn's orders, specifically, in service of exposing armistice-breaking traffic — becomes not a deserter but a witness. Witnesses are remanded to the peace commission. I can take custody of him tomorrow. He walks out of the lockhouse and into my escort, and whether he ever stands trial depends entirely on whether Renn's amnesty holds."

"And Renn?"

"Aldous Renn is officially dead. Resurrecting him formally means reopening the inquiry into the *Meridian*'s last voyage, which implicates naval officers, company directors, and two men who will be signing the armistice on Thursday." Her mouth thinned. "I can give him amnesty in practice — protection, passage, a name that isn't his. What I cannot give him is his name back. Not now. Perhaps not ever. If you need a hero restored to his uniform, go elsewhere. If you need him alive and free, I'm your only buyer."

"And what do you want in exchange?"

"Everything you were going to sell anyway. Your testimony, sworn and public, entered into the record before the signatures dry. Your father's diving logs — the originals, not your copies — as corroborating material evidence. The cipher, explained, so my archivists can verify every entry independently. And your name." She said it plainly, no cruelty in it, which somehow made it worse. "You will sign the deposition as Yara Blackwood, formerly indentured clerk of the Meridian Trading Company. Your name goes into the official record beside the manifests. Which means that if this goes wrong — if Hale's lawyers find a seam, if the Admiralty digs in its heels, if any part of this collapses — your name is the one attached to the wreckage. Not mine. Not the commission's. Yours."

"That's not a bargain," I said. "That's a shield."

"It's both," she agreed. "Most things are. Ask any uniform."

I looked down at my hands. They were steady, which surprised me; I felt as though they belonged to someone else, some other Yara who had walked into the archive certain she was trading herself to a monster and had instead been out-negotiated by a different kind of monster entirely, one with excellent manners and a commission seal.

"Say the rest," I said. "The part you're not saying."

Marsh regarded me for a moment, and I saw something flicker behind the carving — fatigue, maybe, or respect.

"The rest is this: I may use you. I probably will. When the histories are written, the commission will require the story to be *shaped*, Miss Blackwood. Shaped stories survive; true ones get lost. You may find that your role in Thursday's events is larger than you'd hoped or smaller than you deserve, and you will have no recourse, because you will have traded your name into my keeping. That is the honest price. Most sellers never hear it stated. I'm stating it because I want you to understand that whatever happens afterward, you chose it with your eyes open." She paused. "And because a witness who understands the machinery makes a better witness."

Outside, far off, a bell marked the third quarter of the hour. Somewhere across the harbor, in a cell that smelled of tar and salt rot, Elias was asleep or not asleep, and didn't know that his freedom was being priced in a dead man's office.

"My father's logs," I said. "You get the originals. But the cipher stays mine. I teach it to your archivists myself, page by page, and no one else copies it. It's a diver's code, Commissioner. It belongs to divers."

"Accepted."

"If Vane is released, he's released to me until Thursday. Not to your escort. To me."

Something almost like amusement crossed her face. "He'll chafe at being guarded by a pearl diver's daughter."

"He'll chafe at being guarded at all. That's why it should be someone he trusts."

"Accepted," she said again, and then, standing, gathering her lantern: "One more thing. Hale knows you were in this room tonight. His man saw the candlelight from the yard before we arrived — that's how I found you, incidentally; you led me straight to him. By morning he'll know what you took, or believe he does. Whatever you imagined the next three days would look like—" she glanced at the ledger, at Hale's interrupted line of falsification, and her voice dropped to something almost gentle— "imagine faster."

She left. The soldier left. The candle burned on over the desk where a factor had been rewriting the war by hand, and I sat down again in his chair, and I put my head in my hands, and I laughed — once, quietly, horribly — because I had gotten everything I came for and I had never felt less safe in my life.

Then I finished the entry. In the official hand, in the official ink, in the space Hale had left blank for a lie, I wrote the truth in plain characters where anyone could read it, signed my name beneath, and dated it.

Let him find it. Let him choke on it.

* * *

**Elias**

They moved me on the fourth day, which I took at first for a prelude to hanging.

The lockhouse cell had a window the width of a brick, through which I had learned the harbor's moods by smell alone — low tide, fish offload, rain coming off the hills. On the fourth day the door opened and instead of Hale's sergeant there were two soldiers in delegation grey, and a paper was read at me in a flat bureaucratic voice containing more Latin than mercy, and I was escorted — not dragged, *escorted*, which frightened me more than dragging would have — through the yard and into a covered cart.

They drove me not to the courthouse but to a merchant house on the hill with bunting going up along its rail. Delegation quarters. A clerk took my name, my rank, my former vessel, and my statement — "which statement," he said, "has already been provided, Mr. Vane, by another party, and verified against material evidence."

"Whose statement?"

"Miss Blackwood's."

I sat with that for the length of the corridor. Eleven days I had assumed she'd cut loose — the sensible play, the play any survivor makes, and I'd have respected it even as it gutted me. Instead she had gone to the commission. She had traded *herself*. I knew her well enough by then to know exactly what that trade had cost and exactly what she'd been promised in return, and exactly how much of the promise would keep.

They gave me a room with a bed and a barred window and a guard outside, and on the desk, weighted under a stone inkwell, lay my satchel — empty, contents inventoried — and a note in a neat, upright hand:

*Your captain is alive and provisioned. Your testimony is scheduled. Your advocate is a pearl diver. Try to be worth the price. — A.M.*

I read it four times. Then I sat on the bed and put my face in my hands the way I hadn't let myself do in eleven days, and what came out of me wasn't quite laughter either.

On the second evening, the door opened without a knock and Yara came in carrying a basket of bread, a lantern, and an expression I recognized from the weighing-house floor the night she'd caught me: fear, sharpened into purpose, worn like armor two sizes too small.

"They're letting you visit me," I said.

"They're letting me *depose* me," she said. "The difference is a table. Sit down, Vane. We have three days, and Commissioner Marsh has kindly permitted me to prepare my witness." She set the basket down and looked at me across it, and her voice cracked just enough to show the armor's seams. "Elias. There's something you need to hear before Thursday, and I need you to hear it from me and not from a lawyer. About what I traded. All of it."

So I sat, and she told me — her voice level, her hands not — and when she reached the part about her name, about the shield she'd agreed to become, I reached across the table and took her hands, and she let me.

Below us, in the town, the bells began practicing the armistice peal. Above us, on the roof, boots crossed and recrossed, slow and patient, like a man counting the hours.

And at the harbor gate, though neither of us knew it yet, a launch was putting out into the dark with no lights burning, carrying a passenger the commission believed safely exiled and Corvus Hale believed safely drowned — a man in a borrowed coat who had decided, in defiance of every promise, every amnesty, and every ounce of sense, that he would not watch the signing from the water.

Captain Aldous Renn was coming ashore.
