## 17. Armistice Eve

There is a particular kind of silence that comes before bells, the way the air holds itself, and I had been living inside that silence for three days — in a drainage culvert outside the town walls, then under a tarpaulin in Renn's cousin's fish shed, then walking by night along the salt flats where nothing grows and nothing watches except gulls with no loyalty to anyone. Now, at dusk on armistice eve, we crouched behind a stack of ceremonial timber at the edge of the signing square, and I understood that the silence was over. The bells were coming. Everything after them would be noise.

Renn counted soldiers. It was what he did instead of breathing, I think. His lips moved without sound, his ruined hand ticking against his knee like a metronome that had survived its own shipwreck.

"Twelve on the square," he murmured. "Four at the hall doors. Two on the roof of the weighhouse." He glanced at me. "They're not guarding the peace, Vane. They're guarding the version of it."

"They don't know we're here."

"No," he agreed. "But Hale knows someone's coming, or he wouldn't have doubled the watch. A man doesn't fortify against ghosts unless he's heard one knocking." His mouth did something that on a healthier face might have been a smile. "You knocked loudly, signalman."

I had, hadn't I. Freed from the cooperage cell by Marsh's lawyers — a release signed so fast and so quietly it smelled like leverage changing hands — I'd spent forty-eight hours being hunted through my own town by men who'd stopped pretending to be harbor watchmen. They weren't looking for a deserter anymore. Deserter was paperwork. What they wanted now required a boat and weights, and everyone involved knew it.

"You could still walk away," Renn said, and it wasn't kindness; it was arithmetic. "The delegation sails at dawn. If you're on it, you're a witness under commission protection. If you're caught here after dark—"

"If I'm caught here after dark, Yara walks into that hall tomorrow alone."

He didn't argue. He'd read the letters too, all those months in hiding, decoding her father's knots and depths. He knew exactly what she'd waded into, and I think some part of him — the part that had faked his own death rather than sign his name to the company's cargo lists — recognized the shape of her choice as a mirror.

"Bells at eight," he said. "The delegation processes at nine. Signing at ten. Which means whatever happens, happens between the eighth bell and the tenth."

* * *

We moved through the back lanes while the town dressed itself for peace. Bunting went up on the chandlery. Lanterns were hung along the quay in strings like captive constellations. Children chalked doves on the flagstones, and shopkeepers who had profited from either side of the war — or both, thanks to certain manifests I could recite in my sleep now, ship names and dates and buyers arranged like a psalm of shame — hung out flags whose allegiances changed depending on which window you viewed them from.

It should have felt obscene. Mostly it felt tired. Wars end the way storms end: somebody always claims they saw it coming.

At the cooperage corner I nearly broke cover. There was a notice nailed there, fresh paper, fresh ink, and my name on it — VANE, ELIAS — with the word DESERTER beneath in letters sized for reading from horseback, and beneath that, smaller, the company seal doing its best impression of lawful authority.

Below mine, another notice. BLACKWOOD, YARA.

Not deserter. Worse. *Person of interest in matters touching the security of the Company.* The phrasing was Corvus Hale all over: precise, deniable, and oily enough to slide off a magistrate's bench if challenged. But the intent needed no lawyer's gloss. She was to be taken before she could be sworn. Held somewhere quiet until the ink dried on the armistice and her testimony became the ranting of an indentured girl with a grudge — or simply never became anything at all, because girls in company custody have historically been very good at not surviving their interrogations.

"She copies everything," I said. My voice came out strange. "The manifests. Into ordinary ledgers. Margins, flyleaves — I told her it was madness, keeping originals when copies would do, and she said—" 

"What did she say?" Renn asked, though I suspect he knew.

"That a copy can be called a forgery. That the original pages, entered in the official ledger, in the official hand, witnessed and sealed — that's the only version Hale can't burn." I stared at the notice with her name on it. "She's going to try to enter it herself. Tomorrow. In the chamber. Before the signing."

Renn was quiet a long moment. Somewhere down the lane, a hammer rang against a stage being built for the commissioners.

"Then we know where the battle is," he said finally. "And we know she'll be early. People who intend to change history are always terribly early." He pulled his hood lower over the scar tissue that had passed, for two years, for a dead man's face. "Which means Hale will be earlier."

* * *

By the seventh bell we'd reached the signing hall's shadow — a customs house once, grandly renamed for the occasion, its pillars draped in white cloth that did its best to hide the tar stains and the anchor scars of a building that had weighed, taxed, and occasionally condemned most of the cargo that ever made this town matter to anyone but itself.

That was when we found the cart.

It stood in the alley behind the hall, unremarkable except that it was a company cart, and company carts at this hour meant one thing. Two men leaned against its side, talking low. One held a lantern; the other held, loosely, contemptuously, a coil of rope.

I've been a signalman. I know codes. The code of a rope and an empty cart at dusk behind a hall where a girl was due to be sworn as witness in the morning was not a difficult cipher.

"They're collecting her tonight," I breathed. "Before the bells even finish. Get her out of her room, get her in the cart, and by morning she's 'fled,' she's 'absconded,' she's an indenture broken, a thief of company property—" My hands had become fists without consulting me. "Hale isn't going to let her stand in that chamber. He's going to make sure the only story told about her is one he wrote."

Renn watched the cart with the flat patience of a man who had once ordered artillery and never fully gotten the habit out of his blood.

"Then here's what we do," he said. "We don't fight two men in an alley. We're not heroes tonight; heroes die before the bell." He turned to me, and in the lantern spill his face was all angles and old damage. "Signalman. How long since you ran messages?"

"Years."

"And you remember every shortcut in this town because a runner who forgets a shortcut is a dead runner?"

"Yes."

"Then run." He pressed something into my palm — his ring, the Renn signet, the one the company had declared buried with him. "Get to her first. Tell her the cart exists, tell her the hour, tell her—" He hesitated, and for a moment the captain fell away and there was just a tired man who had spent two years writing letters to strangers because living men had stopped listening. "Tell her the man who taught you the divers' cipher says history doesn't belong to whoever writes it down. It belongs to whoever refuses to let it be unwritten."

"The bells," I said.

"When the eighth bell rings, be inside walls. Any walls. After that, Hale's men stop arresting people and start disappearing them." He was already moving toward the cart, toward the two men and their rope, moving with the unhurried certainty of someone about to cause a distraction of historical proportions. "And Vane—"

"Yes, Captain?"

"If she asks whether I'm really alive—" He smiled, properly this time, and it was terrible and wonderful. "Tell her dead men make excellent witnesses. No one can hang us twice."

* * *

I ran.

I ran the way I hadn't run since the war, through lanes that remembered my feet, cutting behind the ropewalk, over the tide-slick stones below the granary, my lungs full of salt and terror and something that wasn't quite hope but lived next door to it and borrowed its light. Above me the sky went the color of cooling iron, and the town below me glittered with its festival lanterns, all of it lit up for peace, for paper, for the great sealing of the record — and none of it knowing that the record was a battlefield, that right now, in an alley behind the hall, a captain officially two years drowned was stepping out of the shadows to negotiate with a length of rope.

Her lodging was above the sailmaker's, three doors from the weighing house where I'd once collapsed bleeding among the scales with a satchel full of impossible letters clutched to my chest. Full circle, if circles could bruise.

I took the stairs two at a time and hammered on her door, and when it opened — when *she* opened it, candlelit, already dressed, already carrying a leather folder of ledger pages strapped across her chest like armor — relief hit me so hard I nearly forgot every word I'd carried up those stairs.

"Elias." Her eyes went past me to the dark landing, quick and professional, cataloguing threats. "You shouldn't be here. There are notices with your face—"

"There's a cart." I got it out between breaths. "Behind the hall. Company cart, rope, two men. They're coming for you tonight, before the bells. Hale means to have you in custody — or worse — before you ever reach the chamber."

I watched the fear arrive in her face and watched her master it, the way she must have mastered it every day of an indentured life, filing it away with all the other things a careful girl learns to survive. Then she looked down at the folder against her chest.

"Then we're out of time," she said.

Below us, faint but unmistakable, rolling in off the harbor like a tide that kept its appointments — the first stroke of the eighth bell.

Yara blew out her candle, shouldered the folder, and said, "Then we go now — and Elias, whatever happens between here and that chamber, you stay behind me. Dead men can't testify. But neither can arrested ones."

Somewhere in the dark behind the signing hall, a cart began to move.
