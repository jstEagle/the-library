## 20. Free Paper

The papers came to me the way most official things did in Saltmere: late, damp, and smelling faintly of someone else's authority.

Commissioner Adela Marsh sent them by clerk — a nervous young man with ink-stained cuffs who stood in the doorway of my room above the cooperage and held out a leather folio as though it might bite him. "From the Commissioner's office," he said. "With her compliments. And, ah—" he consulted a card, "—her assurance that all matters arising from your testimony are now considered settled."

Settled. As though I were an account. Which, I supposed, I had been.

I took the folio. He left at a speed that suggested he'd been instructed to leave quickly, and I sat down on the edge of my narrow bed with the thing on my knees and did not open it for a long moment. Outside, the harbor was doing what the harbor always did — arguing with itself, gulls against tide, tide against hull — and somewhere below me Elias was mending nets with the cooper's boys because a man waiting to be tried for desertion may as well be useful while he waits.

I broke the seal.

Inside: three documents, each heavier than its paper had any right to be. The first was a certificate of settlement, all flourishes and clauses, in which the Meridian Trading Company, through its legal representatives, agreed to dissolve the indenture of one Yara Blackwood, pearl diver's daughter, entered into service at the age of nine years upon the death of her father, said indenture being hereby rendered null, void, and of no further effect, in consideration of services rendered to the peace process.

Services rendered. That was what they called it. Not: for reading the ledgers no one wanted read. Not: for standing in a signing chamber with shaking hands and reading aloud the names of ships that had armed both sides of a war while this town slept soundly, believing itself beneath history's notice. Services rendered, like a delivery of rope.

The second document was a letter from Marsh herself, short and cold and, in its way, honest. *Your name will appear in the commission's annex,* she wrote. *Not in the main record. The main record must remain legible to men who read only headlines.* She was telling me, in the language of our trade, that I would be a footnote. She was also telling me she knew exactly how much that would cost me, and that she was paying it anyway, and that we both understood the arithmetic of power well enough not to pretend otherwise.

I almost admired her. I want that recorded somewhere — though where, I wonder? Perhaps I shall have to start my own ledger.

The third document was the smallest. A single page, headed with the company's crest — the same crest I had stamped ten thousand times, the two-headed meridian bird that looked equally smug facing either direction — and beneath it, in a clerk's careful hand:

*Be it known that Yara Blackwood, formerly indentured to the Meridian Trading Company, is hereby freed of all obligation, debt, and claim. She owes nothing. She is owed nothing. Her person and her labor are her own.*

Her labor is her own.

I read that line perhaps forty times. Then I set the papers down on the bed beside me and looked at my hands, which had spent seventeen years belonging to something else — first to the sea, then to the Company, then, briefly and gloriously, to the truth — and I found that they did not feel different. They felt like my hands. They had always felt like my hands. That, apparently, had been the trick all along: everything had been mine the whole time, and the papers were only admitting what had never been true otherwise.

You would think freedom would have more weight to it. It didn't. The folio weighed less than a ledger. Less than a diving log. I could have posted it through a keyhole. All those years of counted hours, of wage entries that never quite cleared the debt column, of Corvus Hale's voice saying *your time is the Company's time, Blackwood* — and here it all was, dissolved by three pages lighter than a gull's feather.

I laughed. I'll admit it. Alone in my room, holding my own emancipation, I laughed until it turned into something else, and then I sat there wiping my eyes with the back of my hand like a fool, and the harbor went on arguing below, indifferent.

*

By afternoon I had composed myself enough to do what any sensible freedwoman does with her first hours of liberty.

I went to work.

Old habits. Or perhaps new ones — I genuinely could not tell anymore which impulses were the cage and which were the bird. Marsh had offered me a position, of sorts: *the commission will require clerks familiar with the regional records during the wind-down,* her letter had said, *at standard rates, if you are interested.* Interested. As though I might decline. As though there were some version of me that, handed her freedom, would walk away from paper entirely.

But the commission's offices were not due to receive me until the morrow, and so instead I did something I had been putting off since the signing chamber: I went down to the archive of the Meridian Trading Company itself, which still stood, still smelled of dust and lamp oil, and still employed a skeleton staff to wind down affairs that would take years to unwind. Hale's office stood empty behind glass. They had let him go, the lawyers having performed their customary miracle; word was he'd sailed east on a company ship with his severance and his reputation intact among people who valued discretion over decency. But his candle was gone from the archive, and his alterations — well. Those were mine to find now.

The young clerk on duty — a boy named Pellowe who had once been terrified of me and now regarded me with the wide-eyed reverence usually reserved for saints and arsonists — waved me through without a word.

I went to the shelf where my father's diving logs had lived for seventeen years, catalogued under *Miscellaneous Maritime, Non-Commercial*, which was the Company's way of saying *worthless*, which was the Company's way of saying *not ours, therefore nothing*. I pulled them down. I held them. And I understood, finally, why the emancipation papers felt so light.

Because the weight had never been in the papers. It had been in the belief that the papers were the whole of the world. That the ledger was the truth. That a girl's life could be summed in a debt column and closed with a stamp. I had spent my whole existence inside an accounting system, and when they handed me the receipt saying *paid in full*, I had expected to feel larger, and instead I felt unmoored — because the system was gone, and I was still here, and nobody had told me what I was for now.

My father had known. I opened his log — the third volume, water-swollen at the corners, the knot-marks running down the margins like a diver's lifeline — and read his handwriting for the thousandth time. *Depth 40. Visibility poor. Bottom rich. Kept faith.* Kept faith. With what? With the sea? With us? With the idea that a man's observations, honestly kept, mattered even when no one above the surface would ever read them?

He had written history every day of his life, my father. It had simply drowned with him, until Renn found the code and Elias carried the satchel and I dragged it all up into the light by its hair.

*

Elias found me at dusk, still sitting cross-legged between the shelves with my father's logs around me like tidewrack.

"I thought you'd be celebrating," he said. He lowered himself down beside me with the careful economy of a man whose ribs still remembered Hale's soldiers. "Freedwomen drink something, don't they? I've heard rumors."

"Freedwomen," I said, "appear to sit in archives." I showed him the papers without being asked. He read them slowly, the way he read everything, lips moving slightly, a signalman's habit — every message confirmed twice before acted upon.

"Light, aren't they," he said at last.

"You noticed."

"Renn warned me about this." He folded the folio closed and handed it back with a strange gentleness. "After the Kestrel ran. He said the worst day of his life wasn't the court-martial or the faked drowning or any of it. It was the morning after, when he woke up and realized no one was telling him what to do. He said a man can survive anything except the sudden absence of orders." He looked at me sideways. "He also said it passes."

"Did it?"

"For him?" Elias smiled — the crooked, half-apologetic smile that I had already begun building entire evenings around. "He became a smuggler of the truth instead. So draw your own conclusions."

We sat there in the lamplit aisle, two people surrounded by other people's records, and after a while he said, quietly: "What will you do? Now. Actually do."

And I found — this is the honest part, the part I would not say aloud for months afterward — that I did not know. Seventeen years I had known precisely what I was: property, with excellent penmanship. Now I was a person, and a person, it turns out, is a much vaguer entry.

"I know what I won't do," I said finally. "I won't keep books for anyone who thinks a life is a line item. And I won't write only what powerful men agree to remember." I touched my father's log. "Beyond that, the page is blank. Apparently I'm meant to find that liberating."

"Apparently," Elias agreed. Then, because he was Elias, and because the lanterns outside had stopped sweeping past the windows an hour ago, and because the archive was quiet in the way places are quiet when they have decided to allow things: he reached over and laced his fingers through mine.

His hands were rougher than mine. Diver's daughter, signalman's son — we had both been shaped by rope and salt. His thumb moved once across my knuckles, a small semaphore, and I understood it without translation: *here. still here.*

"Tomorrow," I said, "Marsh's clerks will offer me a desk. In a week they'll thank me politely and forget my name. In a month the armistice will be old news and the annex will be misfiled, and that will be that." I said it evenly, because I had learned in the last weeks that even the truest things go down easier delivered flat. "And yet I keep thinking about Pellowe. The archive boy. He asked me yesterday — actually asked me — whether it was true that a ledger could be forced open. Whether ordinary people could get into the record." I shook my head. "He asked it like I'd done a magic trick. Like it had never occurred to him that the door was a door."

"Wasn't it locked?"

"It was always locked," I said. "That's rather the point. But locks are just arguments made of iron, and arguments can be lost."

Elias laughed softly. "You should write that down."

"Don't tempt me. I've just discovered I'm free. God knows what I'd write."

Outside, the harbor bells rang the evening watch, and somewhere out beyond the breakwater the sea kept whatever it keeps — my father, the watchman, the ships, the war, the version of Captain Aldous Renn that the world had agreed to believe dead. Tomorrow I would take a desk in a commission office and enter testimonies at standard rates. Tonight I sat on the floor of the company that had owned me, holding the receipt for my own soul, feeling lighter than I had ever felt and emptier too, like a hold after cargo's gone — all that space, suddenly, and no instructions for filling it.

Except.

Except that as we gathered the logs and rose to leave, Pellowe came hurrying down the aisle with a slip of paper, breathless, apologetic, eyes shining.

"Beg pardon, Miss Blackwood — it came on the evening packet, no sender, but the clerk said it was definitely for you, and—" he hesitated, caught between discretion and curiosity, curiosity winning as it always does in the young, "—it's odd, miss. The address isn't written. It's tied on. In knots."

I took it from him.

Small cord, sailor's work, knots spaced along its length in a pattern I knew better than my own signature — depth-markings, my father's shorthand, the divers' code that a supposedly dead captain had learned well enough to speak.

I untied the first one with fingers that had gone very steady, very calm, the way they do in deep water.

The second knot said: *still alive.*

The third said: *coming south.*

And the fourth — the fourth was dated.
