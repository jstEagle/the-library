## 21. What the Sea Keeps

**Yara**

The tide was going out, and with it, by order of Commissioner Adela Marsh and the full weight of the peace delegation's escort, so was Elias Vane.

I had argued with Marsh for three days — in her rooms, in the archive antechamber, once memorably on a staircase where she stopped mid-descent and looked at me the way an auditor looks at a column that won't sum. She had kept her word. Elias walked free of the company brig with papers signed in her own hand. But freedom, it turned out, came with a heading attached: he would sail with the delegation's escort vessel as far as the capital, there to give his deposition before the war tribunal, after which his status as deserter from His Majesty's navy would be *reviewed*.

*Reviewed.* What a soft little word for a thing that could end in a rope.

"You could refuse," I said. We stood at the harbor mouth where the cobbles gave up and became shingle, and the wind came off the water with that flat, honest smell of weed and salt that my father used to say was the only truth a coast ever tells you. The escort ship rode at anchor beyond the mole — *Vigilant*, thirty guns, white ensign, bunting still up from the armistice celebrations, looking like a butcher dressed for a wedding.

"And be tried at home instead of deposed here," Elias said. "They'd hang me in the capital square and put it in the papers as discipline restored. At least on the *Vigilant* I'm evidence. Nobody hangs evidence until they've read it."

"You've been spending too much time around me."

"Impossible. There isn't enough time." He said it lightly, and then heard what he'd said, and neither of us laughed.

The morning of the signing felt like a hundred years ago instead of six days. I remembered standing in that chamber with manifests in my hands and my voice shaking, reading ships' names aloud — the *Cormorant*, the *Sable Ann*, the *Meridian Star* itself — while Corvus Hale's face went through the stages of a man watching his fortune drain out of a hole in the hull. Hale had lost everything he controlled. He had not lost everything he owned; lawyers saw to that, scuttling between chambers like crabs. And Renn —

Renn was gone. That was chapter nineteen, if history were being written in chapters: the hero who refused to be one, slipping out of the harbor two nights after the signing on a fishing ketch with a false name and half his old wounds weeping salt into new ones. He left no letter. He left a knot tied in a length of tarred cord hanging on the cooperage door, and I am not ashamed to say I sat down on the step and held it like a hand.

Now the sea wanted Elias too.

"I'll write," he said.

"To where? I don't have a where anymore." It came out sharper than I meant. My emancipation papers were folded in my coat pocket, and I had carried them for a week now like a coin I kept checking for, and every check reminded me that a name on paper is not the same as a life. "Marsh's settlement dissolved my indenture. It didn't give me a house or a trade or a town. It gave me myself. Which is more than I had. But it doesn't give you an address."

"The delegation's records office," he said. "Care of Commissioner Marsh. You're famous enough now that letters will find you. Infamous, maybe."

"There's a difference?"

"With governments? A spelling."

Down the shingle, a bosun's whistle shrilled twice. The longboat crew began gathering at the waterline, stamping their feet against the cold, pretending not to watch us, which is the universal sailor's method of watching someone completely.

Elias turned to face me fully. He had his collar up and his kit over one shoulder and the wind was doing what it liked with his hair, which was roughly what it always did. Two years of running, a week of hiding in a cooperage loft among the smell of oak staves and candle smoke, four days of a cell — and somehow he looked less worn than he had the night he bled out among the weighing scales. As though telling the truth had taken weight off him that lying had put on.

"Yara Blackwood," he said, formal, like a man opening proceedings.

"Don't you dare make a speech."

"It's not a speech. It's an entry." He reached into his coat and brought out a strip of canvas, sailcloth, hemmed on both sides — cut, I realized, from the loft's old patch, the one we'd sat under during the lantern searches. On it, in tar and chalk, was a line of markings. Knots drawn in ink. Numbers in a small sure hand. Depth figures descending, then holding, then ascending.

My father's diving log notation. Renn's borrowed cipher. Our cipher now — mine and his, since the night in the loft when I'd taught him the depth-marks and he'd taught me the signal-flag substitutions, trading codes across a candle like children swapping marbles.

I read it without meaning to, the way you read your own name.

*Low water, third day of the ebb, spring tide. The cairn below the old pilot's beacon. Bring the red buoy line. I will be under the water where the ledger can't follow.*

"That's nonsense," I said, because my voice needed something to do besides break. "That's — you can't dive. You told me you sink like an honest man at a factor's dinner."

"I've had lessons." A ghost of the grin. "From a girl who talked about her father's breathing method for four nights straight and thought I wasn't listening."

"You weren't listening. You were bleeding."

"I multitask." Then the lightness went out of him the way it did, all at once, like a cloud off the sun, and what was underneath was just a man of twenty-six who had run from one war into another kind and was about to step into a boat. "Third day of the ebb. Spring tide. If the tribunal releases me, I'll be back by then. If it doesn't — if they keep me, or worse—" He stopped. Started again, plainer. "If I don't come, don't wait past the season. Don't turn yourself into a woman standing on a beach."

"I'm already a woman standing on a beach. You're looking at her."

"Yara."

"Elias." I took the canvas strip from him. My hands were steady. I noted this with the detached professional interest of a bookkeeper: *subject's hands steady; cause unknown; recommend investigation.* "You know what my father said about the sea? Everyone quotes him the pearl part — *the sea keeps what it keeps*. But there's a second line, in his last log. *And gives back what it's paid.*"

"He believed the sea settles its accounts?"

"He believed everything settles its accounts eventually. He was a diver. You learn patience at forty fathoms or you learn nothing." I folded the sailcloth small and put it inside my coat, against the emancipation papers, so the two documents could get acquainted. "Third day of the ebb. I'll bring the red line. I'll bring two lines, because you'll have forgotten yours."

"I won't forget anything," he said. "That's the problem with me. I remember everything. Ships' names, dates, buyers, the exact weight of every lie Hale ever told me. And the sound of you reading manifests out loud in a room full of men who wanted you dead. That one especially. I intend to keep it."

The whistle shrilled again, longer this time, impatient. The longboat crew shifted. Somewhere behind us the town bells were ringing the hour — ten o'clock, ordinary as bread — and above the mole the gulls were conducting their usual audit of everything, finding most of it wanting.

He kissed me.

I want it entered into the record — since entering things into records is apparently my vocation now, since I have learned that a thing unwritten is a thing the world is free to deny — that he kissed me first, on the shingle, in front of six sailors and two gulls and whatever Commissioner Marsh's aide thought he was doing loitering by the pilings. It was not a long kiss. It was not a short one either. It was exactly as long as it needed to be, which is a sentence I would never allow in a ledger but will allow in my memory, where I keep the only accounts that have never once balanced.

Then he stepped back, shouldered his kit, and walked to the boat, because that was the whole of the arithmetic. Love against duty is how the ballads put it, and the ballads are liars with good instincts: it wasn't love against duty. It was love *with* duty, two currents in the same channel, and you don't fight a current, you read it, and you set your rendezvous, and you breathe the way divers breathe — slow, and low, and trusting the surface to still be there.

He turned in the boat, once, when they were past the mole. Not a wave. Just a look, held a beat too long, a man committing coordinates to memory. Then the oars bit, and the *Vigilant*'s shadow took him, and I stood on my beach with my father's cipher against my heart and counted the days until the ebb.

Eleven. Eleven days. Even a bookkeeper can count that high.

* * *

**Elias**

Here is what nobody tells you about ships of the line: after a week ashore, they smell wrong. Tar and bilge and a hundred men's cooking, and underneath it all the particular institutional reek of a floating bureaucracy — because that's what the *Vigilant* was now, make no mistake. Half her guns were unloaded and her main deck had been converted to offices. Desks where the carronades had been. Clerks where the gun crews had been. Peace arrives on warships or it doesn't arrive at all; somebody has to carry the paper.

They gave me a bunk in the warrant officers' berthing and a table and a lamp and eleven days of deposition, and I gave them everything. Names. Dates. Tonnages. The buyers in the capital, the brokers in the free ports, the convoy schedules Renn had copied in his own blood-faint hand. Lieutenant Pryce, the tribunal's recording officer, wrote until his fingers cramped and then looked at me over the spectacles he wore for intimidation purposes only.

"You understand," he said on the fourth day, "that a deserter's testimony carries weight only insofar as he returns to answer charges."

"I understand that a dead witness carries none at all," I said, "which is why the company preferred me drowned. You're welcome to continue their policy if you like. It has a track record."

Pryce did not smile. Pryce was constitutionally incapable of smiling; I suspected the navy had it surgically removed along with the humor. But he wrote down what I'd said, which was the whole trick, the only trick I knew. Get it into the record. Once it's in the record, even enemies have to walk around it like furniture.

At night I stood at the stern rail and watched the coast fall away astern — the harbor light, the old pilot's beacon, the black seam of the town that had never made history until a clerk's daughter made it stand still and listen. Somewhere under that water was a cairn my own hands had built in the dark, three nights before the raid on the cooperage, when I'd gone down with a stone in each fist and Renn's cache instructions burning in my head. Divers' work, stacking it. I'd nearly drowned twice and thought of her father's log both times — *the sea keeps what it keeps* — and come up coughing with the job done.

The tribunal sat on me for nine days. On the tenth, Pryce came to the berth with a document and an expression that on any other face might have been called apologetic.

"Desertion in wartime," he said. "It stands. But in consideration of services rendered to the commission, the sentence is commuted to service aboard this vessel for the duration of the delegation's return voyage, after which — " he consulted the page, hating every word — "discharge, dishonorable, effective at port. You'll be a civilian, Vane. Free to go where you like."

"Where I like," I repeated.

"Their majesties' mercy is expansive."

Their majesties' mercy was a leash with generous slack, and I knew it, and I took it anyway, because slack is slack. I signed. Watching my own name go onto a discharge paper felt like watching someone else sign for me — Elias Vane, late signalman, late deserter, late evidence, currently a man with eleven days of sea ahead of him and a strip of sailcloth in a woman's coat pocket on a shore falling away behind us.

Eleven days. Spring tide on the third day of the ebb. I had built the arithmetic into the cipher deliberately, because she'd catch it — she'd caught everything else — and because I wanted the promise to be the kind you can audit. No poetry. Poetry is for people whose promises aren't checked. Give me a tide table and a beacon and a red buoy line, and I'll show you a love letter.

On the eleventh day the weather turned. Nothing dramatic — no storm, no drama worth a painting. Just a swell out of the southeast and a headwind that slowed us to three knots, and the sailing master shaking his head at the glass and saying we'd lose a day, maybe two, maybe three if it backed around.

Three days.

I stood at the rail with my hands gripping the wood and did the sums again, and again, and got the same bad answer every time, because that is what sums are for. Third day of the ebb, spring tide, and the tide does not care about tribunals. The tide keeps its books better than any company ever did. It would open the cairn's window for one morning and close it, and if I wasn't there —

She'd wait past the window. I knew it the way you know a current under calm water. She'd wait a week, a month, standing on her beach with two buoy lines, and I had told her not to and she would do it anyway, because that was Yara Blackwood all over: obedient to the record, disobedient to every instruction about her own heart.

Unless she didn't. Unless she read the delay in the wind and did the sums herself and concluded the entry had failed to post, and filed me under losses.

The swell rolled under us, patient, enormous, indifferent. Somewhere behind us, shrinking by three knots an hour, a woman was counting days.

I went and found the sailing master, and asked him, very politely, what he'd take to risk the topmast in a quartering breeze.

* * *

**Yara**

The third day of the ebb came in gray and glassy, the sea lying flat as spilled ink, the spring tide pulling the water off the rocks below the pilot's beacon in long silver sheets. I was there before dawn with two buoy lines coiled on my shoulder and my father's old diving belt buckled around my waist — his belt, his lead weights, the ones the company had let me keep because they were worthless on any manifest. Worthless. I have opinions about that word now.

I waited through the slack. I waited through the turn.

The water stayed empty.

By noon I had done the sums so many times they'd worn smooth: a headwind out of the southeast, the harbormaster said; the escort would be delayed, maybe a day, maybe more. Maybe. Maybe is not a figure I trust. Maybes are how ledgers die and how people do.

By evening I had stopped waiting and started diving.

Not deep — I hadn't his lungs, and grief makes divers stupid — just down along the cairn in the failing light, checking it, touching the stones he'd stacked, telling myself I was verifying the site rather than keeping vigil over a pile of rocks like a widow at a grave with no body in it. The cold climbed my arms. Above me the surface went from silver to pewter to dark.

And then, faintly, distorted, coming down through the water the way sound comes to divers — everything comes down to us eventually, my father said, that's why we listen — I heard bells.

The harbor bells. Ringing fast, ringing wrong, ringing the way they ring when a ship makes an unexpected landfall and the town turns out to look.

I surfaced, gasping, salt in my eyes, and looked toward the harbor mouth, where a lantern was swinging at the top of the molehead and a boat was coming through the fairway under a single lugsail, running in on the last of the flood with more nerve than canvas —

and standing in the bow, one hand on the stay, hatless, soaked, grinning like a man who has beaten both the tide and His Majesty's navy in the same afternoon, was Elias Vane.

I stood waist-deep beside the cairn with a buoy line in my fist and did the only sensible thing.

I dove, and swam out to meet him halfway, because some entries you don't wait to have recorded. Some you swim out and enter yourself.

* * *

**Yara and Elias**

We met in the shallows below the beacon, where the water came to our ribs and the last light was going copper on the swell. He waded the final yards hauling the lugsail boat's painter behind him, and when he was close enough he didn't say anything at all, and neither did I, and the sea kept what it kept and gave back what it was paid.

"You're late," I said finally, against his shoulder.

"Two days. I have it in writing. Dishonorable discharge, effective at port, signed and witnessed." He pulled the folded paper from inside his shirt, sopping but legible, and held it up like a trophy. "I'm a civilian, Yara. Free to go where I like."

"Where you like," I said.

"Wherever the record-keeper is." He looked past me at the cairn breaking the surf, at the beacon above it black against the sky. "I owe that pile of stones an apology. I nearly missed our appointment."

"The tide wouldn't have. Tide keeps better books than you do."

"So I've learned." He took the buoy line from my hand — the red one, the spare, the one I'd brought because I'd known he'd forget his — and wound it once around both our wrists, absurd, binding, a knot with no practical purpose whatsoever, which made it the first truly useless thing either of us had done in months and therefore the most important. "Third day of the ebb next moon," he said. "Same station. And the one after. Standing rendezvous, entered into perpetuity."

"Perpetuity is a strong term for a tide table."

"All my best terms are strong. Ask the tribunal."

Above us the pilot's beacon caught the first star. Behind us the town bells were still ringing, the whole gray port turning out to see what the sea had sent home. And in my coat pocket, dry against my skin, a strip of sailcloth waited for its next entry — because we were not finished writing, either of us, and the sea, whatever it keeps, has learned by now to leave the margins alone.

What we didn't know — what the bells couldn't tell us yet — was that a letter was already moving toward the harbor, carried in a courier's oilskin wallet, written in a handwriting I would know anywhere, from a captain officially dead and defiantly otherwise.

But that is the next entry. This one closes here, at the waterline, two names, one knot, tide turning fair.

Signed and witnessed.
