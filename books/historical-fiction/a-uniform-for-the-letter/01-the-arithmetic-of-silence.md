## 1. The Arithmetic of Silence

There is an arithmetic to keeping a light, and I have had seven years to learn it by heart. One wick trimmed twice a day. Four hundred turns of the clockwork every hour and a half, so that the lens might spin its slow, indifferent circle over the water. Two hundred and six steps from my bed to the lamp room if I take them briskly, two hundred and nine if I pause on the landing where the paint has blistered into a shape that looks, on grey evenings, remarkably like my wife's profile. I do not take those three extra steps often. But I know they are there.

Grief, you see, is not an ocean. Everyone says it is — vast, fathomless, etcetera — but the people who say it have plainly never lived beside a real one. The ocean does not keep accounts. Grief keeps nothing *but* accounts. It is a ledger with one entry, entered daily: *she is not here; she is not here; she is not here.* And the only way to survive a ledger like that is to balance it with other figures. Oil consumed. Glass polished. Fog signal tested at noon. A man can drown in arithmetic just as thoroughly as in water, but at least the drowning is tidy.

My name is Ivo Okafor. For seven years I have been keeper of the Carrick Vean light, alone, which the Board considered eccentric until the first winter proved me competent, after which they considered it economical and stopped asking questions. Before that I was many things — a clerk in Falmouth, briefly; before that, a boy on my father's boat out of Freetown, learning that the sea is the same employer everywhere and pays the same wages. And for eleven months of that earlier life, I was a signalman's assistant aboard HMS *Vigilant*, which is where I learned the most important sum of all:

A secret kept costs one life. A secret told may cost several.

I have been paying the first instalment since 1886.

* * *

The morning the letter came began as any other, which is to say the wind came up from the southwest with the manners of a debt collector and the supply boat *Kittiwake* made her monthly attempt to murder our landing stage.

I watched her come in from the gallery, rolling like a drunk leaving chapel, and I went down to receive her cargo with the resignation of long practice. Young Tregose skippered her now — he'd inherited the run from his father, along with his father's conviction that every landing at Carrick Vean should be accompanied by commentary.

"Rough enough for you?" he shouted over the engine, as he did every month, as though the sea's temper were a personal courtesy he'd arranged for my benefit.

"Adequate," I said, catching the first crate.

"Post's wet. Sorry about that. Left it on top and a wave found it anyway." He handed down a canvas mail bag, and I felt through the sacking the particular soft weight of paper gone damp — the sea reaching even into His Majesty's mails, which struck me as fitting. The sea considers all correspondence addressed to itself eventually.

I carried the bag up to the kitchen, set the kettle on, and opened the mail the way I always do: bills first (the Board's coal invoice), then the circulars (a seed merchant who has never grasped that I grow nothing here but condensation), then the personal letters, of which there are rarely more than two. One from Mrs. Pascoe in the village, who writes to me monthly though we have exchanged perhaps forty sentences in person, God bless her stubborn charity. And one more.

I set the kettle whistling without noticing I had done so. That is the sort of thing grief trains into you — the body continuing its offices while the mind stands elsewhere, staring.

The envelope was grey, salt-stained along one edge, addressed in a hand I knew better than my own late wife's. Better than my own, if I am honest. A tall, impatient hand, the letters leaning forward as though the writer were perpetually about to stand up and leave the room. The capital E had a flourish that crossed back through itself like a signature on a doorpost.

*Elias Vane* had written that hand. Elias Vane had written thousands of pages of it — signals, log extracts, letters passed across a wardroom table, once a note tucked into my kitbag reading *You count too carefully, Okafor. Learn to be lucky instead.*

Elias Vane had been dead for seven years. I had watched him die. I had, in fact, held the piece of him that died — held his wrist while the pulse went out of it, in a ship's boat off a burning coast, with the smell of cordite and orange blossom fighting each other in the dark. I had zipped his effects into a canvas bag and given them to the Navy and kept one item back, and I had attended the memorial service at Portsmouth and stood at attention while they read his name among the honoured dead, and I had said nothing, then or ever, about what else had been in that boat besides a dying man.

And now his handwriting sat on my table, steaming gently beside the kettle, addressed to Mr. I. Okafor, Keeper, Carrick Vean Light.

I did not open it. I want that recorded somewhere, in whatever ledger governs such things: I did not open it for four hours. I trimmed the wick. I wound the clockwork. I polished the storm panes until I could see my own face in them looking like a man who has seen a ghost through glass. I descended two hundred and six steps and ascended them again. The letter sat on the kitchen table the whole while, patient as tide.

At last, at noon, when the fog gun had barked its daily bark and there was nothing left between me and it but honesty, I sat down and broke the seal.

* * *

The letter is short. I will set it down here as nearly as memory serves, because I have since burned it, and burning a thing does not burn the need to repeat it.

*Okafor —*

*You will be surprised, and then you will be angry, and then — knowing you — you will begin counting. Count quickly. I have need of your arithmetic.*

*I am alive. I know how that must sit with you, and I am sorry for it in ways you cannot yet imagine. I will explain everything when I see you, which will be soon — the next dark moon, weather permitting, by the old route. I ask shelter. Nothing more than shelter, and silence, both of which you have already provided these seven years, though you did not know the second was being spent.*

*They are saying I betrayed the convoy. You know what I gave you. You know what it proves. There are men coming to your coast asking questions — polite men, in good coats — and one of them is Edmund. Be kind to him. He is hunting me with love, which is the worst instrument there is.*

*Burn this. Keep the packet. Trust no one who compliments your tidiness.*

*— E.V.*

*P.S. Tell me she rests well. I never dared ask before, and now I find I have run out of time to be delicate.*

I sat with that last line for a long while. Outside, the gale worried at the tower like a dog with a bone, and below, far below, the Atlantic performed its ancient indifference.

She. Margaret. Who had written to me for two years while I served, whose letters I had waited for the way other men wait for pay, whom I had married in a registry office with Vane standing witness because neither of us had family near and he had volunteered with that grin of his, saying, *Someone ought to hold the book, Okafor, and I'm the only one here who can read it upside down.* She had died in '89, in the fever year, and I had buried her on the mainland and rowed back out to my rock and climbed two hundred and nine steps and lit the lamp that same night, because the light does not mourn, and someone must do the arithmetic.

Vane had sent condolences — one letter, formal, correct, signed *E.V.* with the flourish crossing through itself. He had never spoken of her again. Neither had I. That is what men of our training do with unbearable things: we file them.

Now he asked after her rest, casually, as a postscript, as though seven years of death between us were a formality to be got past before the real correspondence could resume.

* * *

That evening I did something I had not done in seven years.

Beneath the lamp room floor there is a space — every lighthouse has them, voids and cavities where the ironwork forgets itself — and in that space, wrapped in oilskin and sealed with wax, there has lain a packet since 1886. Documents. Papers Vane pressed into my hands in a boat while his blood soaked through my sleeve, saying words I have repeated to myself so many times they have worn smooth as sea glass: *Keep these. Don't look at them. If anyone asks, you're holding them for the Admiralty. If no one asks — if I don't come back — keep them anyway, and keep them dark. Promise me, Ivo. Promise me and live a boring life.*

I promised. I have kept the promise the way I keep everything — on schedule, in order, unexamined. In seven years I have taken the packet out perhaps five times, only to check the oilskin, only to confirm the wax, never to read. Not reading was part of the ritual. Not reading kept it clean. As long as I did not know what was inside, I was only a keeper of packages, and a man cannot hang for storing luggage.

But a dead man had written to me, and asked for shelter, and warned me that polite men in good coats were coming to my coast. And I understood, sitting in the lamplight with the wind screaming past the glass like something trying to get in, that the arithmetic had changed. The cost of silence had been steady for seven years — one life, paid in instalments, mine. Now the price was rising.

So I went up, past my bedroom, past the blistered paint that looks like her, up to the lamp room, and I knelt on the iron plates with a pry bar and took up the floor.

The packet lay where it has always lain, dry and dark and heavier than paper has any right to be. I brought it into the light and set it on the chart table, and I looked at it for a long time — the wax seal intact, the oilskin stiff with age, the whole shape of it exactly as a dying man's hands had left it.

Then I turned it over. And turned it back. And finally, with the discipline of a man stepping off a known height into unknown water, I left it sealed — but I moved it. I moved it from its hiding place to the locked drawer beneath the chart table, closer to my hand, closer to the night.

Because the dark moon was eleven days off. And somewhere out beyond the reef, in the black water where I had last seen him die, a living man was counting on my silence — and I intended, God help me, to spend it one more time.

Eleven days. Two hundred and six steps. One promise, come due at last.

I lit the lamp, and set the lens turning, and began to count.
