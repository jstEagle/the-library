## 2. What the Tide Brings

I did not sleep. This will surprise no one who has kept a light through a winter; sleep is a thing we ration like oil, and I had spent my allowance staring at a dead man's handwriting until the wick of the candle burned down to its brass collar.

The letter sat on my table where I'd left it, face down, as if it were a card in a game I was losing. I had read it eleven times by lamplight and once more by the first grey coming through the eastern pane, which is the honest light, the one that shows you what you actually think.

It was written in cipher — or rather, it was written in the cipher Elias Vane invented during the long dull months of convoy escort, when two officers with too much education and too little to do amused themselves with substitution tables scratched into a chart-table's underside. He called it the Gull Code, because he said any system that could be broken by a seagull walking on the key was no system at all. It was never broken by a seagull. It was broken, eventually, by boredom, and then forgotten, which amounts to the same thing.

I still had the key. That is the sort of man I am: I keep things. Keys, promises, packets under floorboards, griefs folded small enough to fit in a breast pocket.

Deciphered, the letter said this:

*Okafor — If you are reading this without your heart stopping, you are a better man than I gave you credit for. I am alive. The particulars are long and tedious and mostly involve lying very still. I will come ashore at the next dark moon, weather permitting, which off your headland means never. Come out to me beyond the point in whatever boat you have not yet let rot. Burn this. Trust no one whose boots shine. — E.V.*

And beneath that, smaller, pressed hard enough to tear the paper:

*I know what I asked of you was too much. I ask something worse now. Forgive me.*

I set the letter down and looked out at the sea, which was doing its usual impression of indifference, and I laughed — one bark of it, ugly, alone, the laugh of a man who has just been told the ghost in his house intends to visit.

Seven years. Seven years since they pulled a shattered hull out of these waters and called it the *Vespid*, seven years since I knelt on a rock shelf with a dying captain's hand in mine, and all that time the man himself had been — what? Lying still, he says. Living under another name, growing a beard, letting the world bury him while I stood in his grave-mud every day of my life, keeping his secret like a relic, keeping his documents like a debt.

I should have burned the letter. He told me to burn the letter.

Instead I fed it to the stove piece by piece and watched each fragment curl, and felt nothing whatsoever like relief.

* * *

You will want to know about the night he died, or didn't. Everyone wants that story eventually, though for seven years nobody has known to ask.

It was October of '86. I was then second keeper under Vane's command aboard HMS *Meridian*, or nominally under his command; in practice Elias Vane commanded nothing so much as his own appetite for trouble dressed as duty. We had served together three years by then, and friendship between us had grown the way barnacles grow — slowly, silently, and to a strength neither of us would have guessed at from the outside.

He was the only officer aboard who treated me as though my colour were merely a fact about me, like my height, rather than a question requiring an answer every hour of every day. "Okafor," he said once, watching me take a noon sight, "you are the only man on this ship who can find our position to within a cable's length, and yet half the wardroom cannot find their own arses with both hands. I intend to exploit this shamelessly." It was not a compliment, exactly. It was better. It was recognition, which is rarer.

That night in October we ran into a gale off the French coast — this same coast, give or take fifty miles of drowning water — and took aboard survivors from a packet steamer going down. Among them was a woman with an infant. There was an order waiting for us by signal lamp: proceed, do not linger, the convoy comes first. I watched Elias read that order twice. Then he turned the ship anyway, and we spent four hours pulling people out of the sea while the convoy went on without us, and the woman with the infant lived, and eleven other people lived, and somewhere in an Admiralty ledger a clerk wrote down that Captain E. Vane had disobeyed a direct order under fire-adjacent conditions, and drew a line under it that would one day become the word *traitor*.

But that came later. On the night itself, the cost was paid differently. A boat went over in the swell with Elias in it. When they hauled him back aboard he was cold as ballast and bleeding inside somewhere we couldn't reach, and the surgeon shook his head in the way surgeons do when they want to be excused from the conversation.

I sat with him through the last of it. He was conscious in patches, and in one of those patches he gripped my wrist — a captain's grip, the grip of a man used to being obeyed — and said: "The papers. In my cabin, the oilskin roll. Whatever happens, Okafor, whatever they say about me after — those papers are true, and I need them kept true. Keep them. Keep them hidden. And keep quiet." And then, because he was Elias, even dying: "You're the only man alive boring enough to manage it."

"I'll tell your wife you were brave," I said, to make him angry enough to stay awake.

"Don't you dare," he said, and smiled, and closed his eyes.

They buried him at sea two days later — a shroud, a shell, a prayer read by a chaplain who had never spoken to him. Margaret Vane received a letter in a fine copperplate hand expressing Their Lordships' regret. I received, some months afterward, a posting to a lighthouse station at the end of England, which everyone understood to be an honour and nobody understood to be exile except me, and possibly whoever arranged it.

I kept the papers. I kept them hidden. I kept quiet.

I have often wondered, in the honest hours, whether I kept them out of loyalty or out of cowardice — whether silence was the shape my courage took or simply the shape my fear left in me. Seven years is a long time to hold a question like that. You get to know it intimately. It stops being a question and becomes furniture.

* * *

The dark moon would come on the night of the twenty-third. Nine days away.

I made a list, because lists are how I pray. Wood for the oil store, which had stood empty since the paraffin conversion and smelled of rust and old sheep. Bread, salt meat, tea — the tea especially; whatever else Elias Vane had become in seven years of hiding, he had been, at minimum, a man with opinions about tea. Lamp oil beyond my allocation, which meant a word with the supply boat's master, which meant a lie, which meant practising the lie until it sat in my mouth like a filling.

I told the supply boat master I'd taken up curing fish and needed extra salt. He looked at me — a lighthouse keeper, sixty years old, suddenly a curer of fish — with the expression Cornishmen reserve for Englishmen's hobbies, and sold me the salt, and I added this small dishonesty to my account without ceremony. One accumulates such debts quickly, like sand in a pocket.

By day I trimmed the wicks, wound the clockwork, polished the lens until it threw its nineteen white flashes a minute across the water, faithful as breathing. By night I lay in my bunk listening to the tower hum in the wind and rehearsed conversations with a dead man.

In the rehearsals I was magnificent. I demanded explanations. I enumerated grievances: the seven years, the grave, the widow — God forgive me, the widow, Margaret, who had died believing herself childless and brotherless-in-law and married to a traitor, who had written me letters full of dignity and dread right up until the fever took her, letters I still kept in a tin because I could not burn anything, apparently, that bore her hand. *You are the only person who writes to me as though he knew him,* she wrote once, near the end. *Tell me something true about him. Anything. I am so tired of the official version.*

And I had answered with something safe and useless, because the truth was sealed in oilskin under the lamp room floor and I had promised a dying man.

In the rehearsals I told him all of it. In the mornings, sweeping the gallery, I understood that when the actual man came rowing out of the dark I would say none of it, and be glad simply to see his face above the gunwale, and hate myself for the gladness.

Because that was the shameful thing — the thing I circled for nine days and never landed on. Underneath the dread, underneath the arithmetic of risk (Harrow's boots, Edmund's questions, the gallows they'd built for Vane's name), there was a flicker of something warm and disgraceful: *hope*. Hope that the story wasn't finished. Hope that the promise had been a door and not a tomb. Seven years I had kept faith with a corpse, and corpses require nothing of you but consistency. A living man requires forgiveness, argument, truth — all the expensive things.

I caught myself, on the fifth evening, whistling while I filled the salt barrels. Whistling. Like a boy. I stopped at once and glared out at the empty horizon as though it had caught me at something.

* * *

On the ninth day the sky lowered and the barometer fell like a confession, and I thought: of course. Of course he would choose this. Elias Vane had never once in his life arrived anywhere by convenient means.

At dusk I walked down to the boat skerry below the station and looked at my dinghy — sixteen feet of clinker-built stubbornness, tarred every spring, oars sound, thole pins new. Beyond the point, the sea was already working itself into the long grey ridges it makes before it decides to become serious. No dark moon would be visible tonight regardless; the cloud sat on the water like a lid. Which was, I supposed, the entire idea.

I checked the oil store one last time: straw laid down, blanket, the lamp I'd brought down from the storeroom, bread wrapped in cloth, and — after some internal argument I lost — a bottle of brandy bought three Christmases ago for an occasion that never arrived.

Then I climbed back up and lit the lamp and set the mechanism turning, and stood a moment in the warm glass-smelling circle of the lens as it swept past, again and again, nineteen white flashes to the minute, saying to the darkness: *here, here, here.*

"Trust no one whose boots shine," I said aloud, to nobody, and went down to the boat.

The tide was making. Somewhere out past the point, in all that black water, a dinghy was coming toward me carrying either salvation or catastrophe, and I pushed off from the skerry into the swell and bent my back, and I could not have told you — not honestly, not even to myself — which of the two I was rowing out to meet.
