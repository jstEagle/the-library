## 3. A Man Called Grey

The supply boat had come on a Tuesday; the Admiralty came on a Thursday, which told me something about how the Navy ranked its own comforts against its suspicions.

I saw them first as two specks in a gig rounding the eastern point, and I did what any keeper does with visitors: I put the kettle on, swept the step, and hid everything I didn't want explained. It is a short list, ordinarily. That week it was longer than usual.

Lieutenant Harrow came up the path like a man who had rehearsed the climb and found it wanting — boots too clean for Cornwall, coat too good for weather, eyes already cataloguing my paintwork. Behind him came a second man, younger, softer at the edges, carrying himself the way people carry grief they haven't decided where to set down.

"Keeper Okafor." Harrow extended a hand gloved in grey kid. "Lieutenant Harrow, Admiralty. We're inspecting storm damage along the coast. A formality."

"A formality," I said, "in February."

"The sea doesn't keep a calendar."

"No," I agreed. "It keeps accounts."

I shook his hand and then the other man's, and it was the other man's hand that troubled me. He held on a half-second too long, and his eyes went over my face the way one reads a letter twice to be sure of the handwriting.

"This is Mr. Grey," Harrow said, "of the Board of Trade. Civilian oversight. You'll find him less tiresome than myself, I hope."

*Grey.* The name sat wrong, the way a borrowed coat sits wrong — fine across the shoulders and false everywhere else. But I had kept secrets long enough to know that a lie offered plainly is often best taken plainly.

"Tea?" I said.

"Kind of you," said Grey, and his voice cracked slightly on *kind*, which was the first true thing either of them had brought ashore.

* * *

Edmund Vane had not intended to use his sister's married name, and within four minutes of landing he had nearly given it away twice.

The keeper — Okafor — was not what he had expected. He had built the man from Margaret's letters: solemn, precise, a little stern, a church of a person with the lamp always lit. The man who poured tea in the lighthouse kitchen was all of that, but underneath it ran something drier, an irony worn smooth like beach glass, and Edmund found himself liking him before he could stop.

Which was precisely the problem. Margaret had liked him too. And Margaret's husband was dead, or was not dead, depending on which of the Admiralty's sentences you believed, and one of these two men at the table knew which.

"You'll have lost shutters in the last blow," Harrow was saying, notebook open, pencil moving. "And your landing stage?"

"Split down the middle. The sea took the near end as a souvenir."

"And your stores are sound? Oil?"

"Counted weekly. You're welcome to count them again."

"Oh, I will," said Harrow pleasantly, and wrote something that Edmund was fairly sure had nothing to do with oil.

Edmund turned his cup a quarter-turn on its saucer. "My sister corresponded with this station for years," he said. "Before her death. She spoke of the keeper here as though he were family."

Ivo Okafor looked at him then — directly, without hurry — and Edmund felt the peculiar sensation of being measured by a lighthouse.

"She was family," Okafor said. "As near as makes no difference."

"Then you knew Captain Vane as well. Through her letters."

"I knew of him." The keeper rose to fetch more tea, his back to them both. "A name on an envelope. Husbands usually are, in other people's marriages."

It was beautifully done. Edmund almost applauded. Almost — because he had spent three months reading those same letters after the funeral, and he had noticed what the Admiralty's investigators, hurrying toward their conclusions, had missed entirely: that pages were gone. Cut neatly from their folds. Dates that skipped like a stone over water. And in the last letter Margaret ever sent, a line that had haunted him since: *Ivo carries more than we ask of him. Pray God it does not sink him.*

You did not write that about a man who only knew husbands as names on envelopes.

"More tea, Mr. Grey?" Okafor asked, and Edmund realized he had been staring into his cup like a fortune-teller.

"Thank you, no." He smiled. "Tell me — do you keep the old letters? Correspondence, out here. It must be half a life's company."

For the first time, the keeper hesitated. Not visibly — a stranger would have sworn there was no pause at all — but Edmund had spent his career as an actuary, and his whole trade was noticing when numbers declined to behave.

"Burn them, mostly," Okafor said. "Paper rots in sea air. Memory keeps better."

"What a pity," said Edmund gently. "Memory keeps so badly, too. It edits. It leaves things out." He met the keeper's eyes. "Like a careful hand with scissors."

The kettle ticked on the stove. Outside, a gull screamed once and thought better of it.

"I'll bear that in mind," said Ivo Okafor, "if ever I take up editing."

* * *

After they left — Harrow to measure the landing stage, Grey to "stretch his legs" along the cliff path, both directions being, I noted, toward my station rather than away from it — I stood in the kitchen and admitted the truth I'd been refusing since the gig rounded the point.

Margaret's brother. It had to be. Same grey eyes, same way of listening as though the answer might be smuggled past him if he blinked. She'd shown me his miniature once, laughing at the stiff collar. *Edmund counts everything, Ivo. Even affection. He can't help it. It's how he loves.*

Well. Then he loved me very thoroughly just now, and every ounce of it was arithmetic aimed at my throat.

I checked the floor plate in the lamp room — nothing disturbed, the packet cold under its boards — and then I walked down to the disused oil store, where a gaunt man lay on a cot in the dark pretending to sleep, and failing at it, the way he failed at most forms of rest.

"They've come for you," I said through the door. Or for me. Or for the truth. In February the distinctions blur.

"How many?" Vane's voice, roughened by seven years and a beard.

"Two. One in uniform, one wearing a name that doesn't fit."

Silence, and then, bitterly: "Grey. He always was economical with himself." A pause. "Is Edmund among them?"

I should have lied. Lying to Elias Vane had become a habit of mine, like breathing, and habits are strongest exactly where they do the most harm.

"Yes," I said.

The cot creaked. When he spoke again his voice had changed — stripped of anger, stripped of everything but a terrible, naked want.

"Does he mourn me?"

"He's here looking for you," I said. "What would you call it?"

Another silence, longer. Through the wall I heard the sea working at the rocks below, patient as an editor.

"Ivo," he said at last, "when they search — and they will search — there's a loose board behind the salt store. I broke it getting in. Nail it down. If they find signs of habitation before we've chosen our moment—"

"I know my house, Elias."

"You knew it seven years ago, too," he said quietly, "and still you left me on that deck."

I stood in the dark with my hand flat against the door between us, and I did not answer, because the answer was *yes*, and because somewhere above us, on the cliff path, a soft-footed civilian was walking back toward my station with his sister's letters folded in his memory like knives.

I nailed the board down before dusk. And when I climbed the tower for the lighting, I found on the mat inside the door a small card, left without ceremony:

*Mr. Grey regrets missing you at the house. He will call again tomorrow — earlier, if the weather permits. He hopes to see the whole of the station, top to bottom. — E.G.*

*Top to bottom.*

Beneath the lamp room floor, the packet lay where it had lain for seven years, patient as a debt. Tomorrow, I thought, trimming the wick, watching the flame stand up straight and obedient in its glass, tomorrow he may stand exactly above it, thanking me for my hospitality, while the truth of a dead captain breathes through the boards an inch beneath his boot heels.

I lit the light. I kept the light. And all night, in the back of my mind, I heard Margaret's brother counting.
