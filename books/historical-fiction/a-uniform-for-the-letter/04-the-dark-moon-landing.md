## 4. The Dark Moon Landing

The dark moon came on the fourteenth of November, and with it the fog — a fog so complete it seemed the sea had climbed ashore to smother the land. I had kept lights through eleven such nights in my time at Gullwrack, and I will tell you what no manual admits: a lighthouse is only useful when someone can see it. In fog we sounded the bell instead, blind percussion against blindness, a drum for an audience of nothing.

That night I was glad of it. A man who must strike a bell every fifteen seconds has no leisure to think.

I had spent seven days thinking anyway. Seven days since the letter, and I had done everything a careful man does: burned nothing, moved nothing, changed nothing — because changing things is how guilty men are caught. I had simply gone about my rounds as though the words *I am coming* were not folded into my breast pocket like a splinter working its way toward the heart. The packet beneath the lamp room floor stayed where it had lain for seven years, patient as a seed. I did not dig it up. I told myself that if I dug it up, it would mean I believed him alive, and believing him alive meant believing I had spent seven years mourning a lie, and mourning a lie meant —

You see how the arithmetic runs. Grief is a ledger, and I have always been tidy with figures.

At half past nine I killed the lamp room's seaward glimmer, banked the main light to its fog duty, and went down the ninety-one steps to the water door with a lantern shuttered to a coin of light and the boat's painter coiled over my shoulder like something I intended to hang myself with.

*One flash at the quarter hour, green, then dark,* the letter had said. *If you come, row to the third rock past the narrows and wait. If you do not come, I will understand.*

He would understand. That was the cruelty of Elias Vane — he always gave you the exit first, knowing you'd refuse it out of sheer stubbornness. It was how he'd talked me into keeping his papers in the first place. *You needn't say yes, Okafor. Only don't say no.* Seven years ago. A beach in another country, blood going black in the sand, and me holding an oilskin packet while he held his side, saying, *Don't tell them where it is. Promise me. Swear it on her name* — because he knew about Ruth, everyone who ever mattered knew about Ruth, and I swore, God forgive me, I swore on a living woman's name to a dying man.

Except neither of them died. And now one of them was waiting off the third rock past the narrows, and the other was buried under a cairn I visited every Sunday.

The sea was running a heavy swell, the kind that comes three days after a storm has formally ended, like a guest who won't take the hint. I rowed into it with the fog pressing on my shoulders and thought, not for the first time, that a sensible man would have written back: *Come to Falmouth. Come anywhere but here.* But Vane had asked for here, and here was the only place on this coast where I controlled the comings and goings, the light, the bell, the very weather report that went to the Admiralty in my own neat hand. Here, I could lie with infrastructure.

I found him by sound before sight — the knock of oar against gunwale, arrhythmic, exhausted. Then the fog thinned for a moment the way it does, grudgingly, like a curtain twitched aside by someone who resents you, and there was the dinghy, and in it a shape.

Not the shape I remembered. The man I'd known aboard the *Meridian* had been broad as a door, bearded like a tsar, loud enough to be heard over gunfire without a speaking trumpet. The shape in the dinghy was gaunt as a gaff, wrapped in oilskins two sizes wrong, with a beard gone grey at the jaw and a face above it I knew the way you know your own hands — badly, until you look, and then completely.

"Elias," I said. His name came out of me like a tooth.

"Keeper," he said.

Not *Ivo*. Not *old friend*. *Keeper*. As though seven years were a shift change and I merely the current tenant of his acquaintance.

"I brought blankets," I said, because I am a fool, and grief makes us all housekeepers. "And brandy."

"You brought yourself." He looked past me at the fog, at the tower invisible behind it, its bell tolling somewhere above like a patient heart. "I half thought you wouldn't."

"I wrote back."

"You wrote back *carefully*. Four lines. No name. Even your handwriting was hiding." He shipped his oars and let his boat bump mine, and in the close dark I caught the smell of him — salt and coal smoke and something underneath, sickness maybe, or just years. "I lay in a French loft for three weeks reading that letter over until the ink wore soft. Deciding whether you'd sold me."

The word went through me colder than the spray. "Sold you."

"People do. People did." He said it flatly, without accusation, which was worse. "Harrow's kind offer men pensions for smaller betrayals than mine."

"I mourned you." My voice cracked on it, and I hated that, hated the crack, hated the swell knocking our boats together like conspirators. "Seven years, Elias. I stood at a graveside with your hat in my hands because they sent me your hat and told me it was all that was left."

"And you kept my papers under your floor the whole time and never once opened them."

"I promised."

"You promised a dead man." He leaned forward, and even starved, even bent, there was the old captain in the set of his shoulders, the man who had once held a convoy together with swearing and arithmetic. "A dead man can't release you from anything. That's the trick of promising corpses, Okafor. You're never off the hook."

"I know," I said. "Believe me. I have kept books longer than some marriages."

Something moved in his face then — not quite a smile, but the scar tissue where a smile used to live. "Ruth would have laughed at that."

"Don't," I said.

"She'd have laughed, and then she'd have told you to burn the damn papers years ago—"

"*Don't*." Louder than I meant. The fog took it and gave back nothing. We sat with our boats grinding softly together, two men pretending the silence wasn't an argument.

Because that was the thing I could not say, out there on the black water with the bell tolling its idiot patience above us: that I had wanted to open the packet. That on the worst nights — the anniversary nights, the nights when the wind found the one loose slate and worried it like a tongue finding a broken tooth — I had sat on the lamp room floor with a pry bar in my lap and the floorboard's edge under my fingers, and stopped, every time, at the same threshold. Not from loyalty alone. From fear. Because the papers were the last piece of him I possessed unopened, and opening them meant he was finished, closed, a matter for archives. Sealed, he could still, in some corner of my mind where widowers keep their impossible accounts, be alive.

Well. He was alive. And it turned out that was worse.

"Third rock's no place to talk," I said finally. "Take my painter. There's swell coming up the narrows and your boat rides high and empty — she'll capsize in the turn."

"My boat stays. If anyone finds a strange dinghy at your door, we're both finished."

"If anyone finds a strange dinghy at my door, we were both finished an hour ago." I got the line across anyway. He resisted, then yielded, the way he always did — making you feel the concession was his idea. "There's a disused oil store below the old watch platform. Stone walls, iron door, dry as a sermon. Nobody's been in it since the Board switched to paraffin tanks. I keep it locked; the key lives on my ring, which lives on my belt, which lives on my body, which does not leave this station."

"You've thought about this."

"I've thought about everything," I said, "for seven years. Mostly at three in the morning. You'd be astonished what a man can build out of insomnia and guilt."

He hauled himself over the gunwale into my boat and the whole craft dipped, taking his weight, and for a moment our faces were close in the shuttered lantern light, and I saw what the fog had spared me: the hollows under his cheekbones, the new white in the beard, the eyes that had been warm brown and were now the colour of tea brewed too long and forgotten. A man aged thirty years in seven. Treason will do that, apparently, or whatever it was they'd made of his name.

"They say you died at Cape Serrat," I said, pulling for home. "They gave me your hat, Elias. I want to know, before we go one step further onto my rock, what actually happened that night. All of it. Because I rowed away from that beach with your papers and your blood on my cuffs, and I have built a life on top of that night like a house on a sinkhole, and if the truth is that I abandoned you—"

"You didn't abandon me." He said it quickly, and that quickness frightened me more than any accusation. "You did what I ordered. The order stands. But—" and here he looked down at his own hands, those capable ruined hands, "—you were the last face I saw before they took me, and I called your name, and you didn't turn around. I know why. I know I told you not to. Knowing a thing and surviving a thing are different animals, Keeper. One of them bites."

The bell tolled. I counted strokes without meaning to — old habit, old arithmetic — and rowed.

* * *

The oil store smelled of cold iron and old paraffin, a smell like the inside of a struck match. I lit the storm lantern, showed him the cot I'd smuggled in over three nights disguised as "spare canvas," the water can, the brazier with its flue through the old vent, and he looked at it all with the expression of a man being shown his own coffin, nicely appointed.

"It'll do," he said, which from Elias Vane was a eulogy.

"There's bread and cheese in the tin. Eat it all; you look like a rumour of a man. I'll bring the rest tomorrow after the supply boat clears. Harrow's lot inspect the seawall Tuesday — I'll keep you locked and silent, and you'll hear them walking overhead and you'll do nothing. Can you do nothing? It was never your strong suit."

"I once did nothing for three years," he said. "It nearly killed me. Doing nothing is how a man dies standing up."

I got to the door. My hand was on the iron. Behind me he said my name — properly, this time, *Ivo*, and it landed like a stone dropped down a well, a long time falling.

"The letter you kept. Ruth's letters. Do you still have them?"

"Every one," I said. "Why?"

"Because Edmund Vane is coming to your rock," he said, "and he reads her handwriting better than either of us ever did."

I turned. "How do you know that?"

He settled onto the cot with the care of a man whose bones had opinions, and pulled the blanket up like a flag of surrender. "Because I wrote to him too," said Elias Vane. "Three weeks ago. From Falmouth. Telling him I was alive — and asking him not to believe anything I'd told you."
