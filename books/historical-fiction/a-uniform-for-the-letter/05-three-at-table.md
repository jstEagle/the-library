## 5. Three at Table

The oil store smells of whale fat and rust, and I have been dead in it for two days.

I say "dead" advisedly. I have had practice. Seven years of it, in fact, and I had grown quite good at the condition — the way one grows good at a limp, or a lie about one's age. Dead men require so little. No breakfast, no correspondence, no opinions at dinner. It was the dinner that undid me.

But I am ahead of myself.

The store sits twenty paces from the keepers' cottage, a low stone shed with a door that groans like a man confessing, so Ivo has propped it with a wedge and greased the hinges with tallow until it swings silent as a thought. Through the gap where the window glass once lived and now lives only as a jagged memory, I can see the cottage door, the path to the tower, and a slice of grey sea that changes its mind hourly. It is from this slice of sea that I take my weather, my time, and — I am not ashamed to admit it — my hope, in whatever small ration the tide allows.

Ivo brings me food after dark: bread, cold mutton, tea strong enough to stand a spoon in. He says little. He has always said little, which is precisely why the things he does say carry the weight of notarized documents. On the first night he stood in the doorway with the lamp held low, and I saw him decide, visibly, not to ask me the question he had been carrying since the beach — *why did you let me believe it?* — and I loved him a little for the mercy, and hated myself a great deal for needing it.

"You look terrible," was all he said, which from Ivo constitutes a eulogy and a welcome home in one breath.

"I've been dead," I told him. "Dead men don't trouble themselves with tailors."

He set down the plate. "Dead men don't eat mutton either. Eat."

So I ate. And that is how the living resumed, in a shed that smelled of whale, while three hundred yards away the Admiralty's finest dined on Ivo's mutton and asked him, I later learned, the most exquisite questions.

* * *

I heard the dinner before I understood what I was hearing. Sound carries strangely on a headland; the wind that flattens voices on the path will lift them clean over stone walls and deliver them, intact, to anyone crouched in a shed pretending to inventory oil cans. The cottage windows were lit, and the voices came to me in pieces, like a concert heard through a wall in another city.

There was Harrow's voice first — Lieutenant Harrow, though "lieutenant" seems an insufficient word for a man whose voice sounds like it has already been typed, duplicated, and filed. Smooth, courteous, and entirely without warmth, the voice of a man who says "I wonder if you might help me" when he means "I will have it from you by Thursday." He was saying something about storm damage, about the lantern gallery, about the admirable state of the apparatus, and every compliment was a small hook baited with sugar.

And then — and here my hands went cold around the oil can I was pretending to examine — a third voice. Younger. Warmer. A voice with laughter buried in it the way a beach has shells: you don't see them until the tide turns.

I knew that voice. I had last heard it arguing with me about the correct way to eat a mango, on a deck in the Indian Ocean, in another life, under my own name.

Edmund. Margaret's Edmund. Her little brother, whom I had last seen as a boy of seventeen with ink on his fingers and a genius for asking the one question you didn't want asked. He was in the cottage. He was at the table. He was — I pressed my eye to the gap in the glass and saw him by the light spilling from the window — laughing at something Ivo had said, with his sister's exact laugh, and the sound of it went through me like a boarding pike.

They had told him I was dead. Of course they had. Everyone had. That had been the entire architecture of the arrangement: the court of inquiry, the finding of "lost with all hands," the letter Margaret had received that she read aloud to me once in a dream I still refuse to call a dream. And now here he sat, the boy grown into a man with his sister's laugh, eating mutton in a lighthouse cottage, mourning me across a table I could not see — while I stood in a shed smelling of whale fat, alive, a traitor, listening.

I sat down on an oil can and put my head in my hands, which is not a posture I recommend to anyone hoping to keep his self-respect.

* * *

The wind shifted after the plates were cleared, and the voices came clearer, and I understood that I had been granted not a mercy but a sentence: I was to hear all of it.

Edmund was talking about me. About Elias Vane, that is — about the man I had been, or the man the Admiralty had decided I had been, which is a different creature entirely, the way a portrait is different from a face. "I don't believe it," he was saying, and his voice had gone quiet in the way that quiet is worse than shouting. "The court found what it found. But I knew him. I *knew* him. He used to write to Margaret — I read the letters over her shoulder, I was fourteen and shameless — and no man who describes a sunset in the Bay of Biscay for two full pages is a man who abandons a convoy to save his own skin."

"Perhaps he was a good writer," said Harrow.

"Perhaps he was," Edmund said, "and a good liar. That's the horror of it, isn't it, Lieutenant? That I can't tell anymore. That the man I knew and the man in the finding can't both be true, and I have no way of choosing." A pause. The fire ticked. "Margaret chose. She believed him to the end. She said — I have her letter somewhere — she said, *Elias would never. Whatever they say, Elias would never.*"

I made a sound. I will not record what sound. There was no one to hear it but the whale oil, and whale oil keeps confidences.

*Elias would never.* She had written it, and it had outlived her, and it had crossed an ocean and a war and seven years of silence to arrive at a dinner table where I was not invited, and it had landed on the table like a coin placed down by a dead woman's hand, and it said: *he pays his debts.*

And then Ivo spoke, and this is the part I have turned over perhaps four hundred times since.

"Captain Vane was my friend," Ivo said. His voice, as always, was level as a spirit gauge. "I saw the ship go down. I told the court what I saw. I have nothing to add."

Every word true. Every word a wall. I have since tried to decide whether what I felt was gratitude or its opposite, and I have concluded that the two are the same feeling viewed from different ends of a telescope. Because it was loyalty — of course it was loyalty; Ivo Okafor does not know how to be disloyal, the way other men do not know how to be ambidextrous. And it was also cowardice, or something wearing cowardice's coat: because the truth was in his keeping, folded in oilskin under the lamp room floor, and he could have said *the finding is wrong* with six words, and he said *I have nothing to add* instead, and Edmund — Edmund thanked him. I heard it. Edmund said, "Thank you, Mr. Okafor. I know how hard it must be, having seen it," and the poor boy thanked him, thanked him for the wall, and I sat in the dark with my dead man's beard in my fists and thought: *this is what my silence has purchased. A good man lying, kindly, to a grieving brother, and being thanked for it.*

Is that justice? I had come ashore, I told myself, for justice. For the packet, for the truth, for the clearing of my name — for Margaret's name, too, which had been dragged through the mud of "wife of a traitor" until she died under it. That was the story I had told myself across seven years and three false names: *I go back for justice.* But justice, I have learned, is a remarkably convenient flag to sail under. It covers a multitude of cargoes, and some of them are vanity. Some of them are the unbearable thought that the world's last word on Elias Vane will be written by men who never ate a mango with him. Some of it — I will be honest, here in the whale-smelling dark, where honesty is the only currency accepted — some of it was simply the selfish, animal refusal to stay dead while my accusers lived warm and promoted.

And I had known, had I not, that coming meant this? That my resurrection would be paid for in other men's peace? Ivo had kept my secret for seven years at the cost of — what? I had never asked. I had never once asked what my silence had cost *him*, because asking would have required admitting the silence was mine to impose. A dying man makes his requests; the living are obliged to honor them. But I had not died, and the requests had not expired, and I had let them stand anyway because it was easier than the alternative. That is not loyalty on either side of it. That is two men standing back to back with a secret between them, each pretending the arrangement was the other's idea.

* * *

Near midnight the cottage door opened and boots came down the path — not to the tower, but toward the store. I was on my feet with the oil can in both hands before my brain caught up, which would have made an absurd final image: the traitor Vane, drowned at court, beaten to death by a gallon of sperm oil.

It was Ivo. He set down the lamp, and in its low light his face was the face of a man carrying something heavy in a place no one can see.

"You heard," he said.

It was not a question. The headland had told him; the headland tells everything.

"Every word," I said. "Your mutton was praised. Your light is in admirable order. And a dead man was mourned at your table while you passed the salt."

"Yes." He set the lamp on a shelf. For a long moment he looked at me, and I looked back, and the question he had been carrying since the beach finally came out of him, quiet as tide: "Why did you come, Elias? Truly. Not the packet. Not the name. Why does a man come back from the dead to a place where his hunters are sleeping?"

And I opened my mouth to give him the answer I had rehearsed across seven years — justice, the truth, Margaret's name, all the respectable flags — and what came out instead, in the dark, smelling of whale, was:

"Because I couldn't bear for you to be the only one who knew I was innocent. Because a secret shared by one man is a coffin. I needed a witness, Ivo. I came to make my coffin smaller."

He didn't flinch. He absorbed it the way the tower absorbs weather, and then he said the thing that turned the whole night over:

"Then you should know. Harrow's man searched the oil store this afternoon. He found the wedge, and the fresh tallow on the hinges. He said nothing at dinner. He smiled at me over the mutton and asked for a second helping."

The lamp flame stood very still between us.

"So," Ivo said, "whatever we are going to do — we are going to do it before morning."
