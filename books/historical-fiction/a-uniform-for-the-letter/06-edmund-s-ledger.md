## 6. Edmund's Ledger

I have kept ledgers most of my life. Cornish granite and Cornish tin both run on them, and my father taught me before I could write my own name that a ledger never lies — it can only be *made* to lie, and the making always leaves a mark. A gap in the folios. A hand that changes mid-page. A sum that balances too neatly, the way a man's coat sits too neatly when he has borrowed it.

So when Margaret's letters to Ivo Okafor arrived at the station in their oilcloth bundle — sent up from Penzance by our solicitor, who believed, as everyone believes, that he was being helpful — I did not read them as a brother. I read them as an accountant.

It was a foul thing to do. I want that recorded somewhere, even if only here, in the privacy of my own skull: I sat down in the guest room of a lighthouse station, in a chair that smelled of salt and paraffin, and I audited my dead sister's love letters while her widower cooked supper for me two floors below.

Margaret wrote the way she lived — in bursts, with margins full of afterthoughts. Her letters from the courtship years were giddy, overfull, the ink blotched where she'd written too fast for the nib. Then Cornwall, then marriage, then the slow settling of her voice into something warmer but quieter, like a fire burned down to coals. And Ivo had kept every one of them. That alone should have settled the matter. What sort of conspirator keeps his accomplice's sister's correspondence in date order, tied with string?

But there was the letter of March 1887, in which she wrote, in her looping hand, about "the captain" — Elias Vane, though she didn't name him; she'd have said *the captain* and assumed I'd know which one, because in her world there was only ever one — about how he'd dined with them in Falmouth and argued politics with Ivo until the candles guttered, and how she'd gone to bed leaving the two of them still talking "like boys who have found a secret door in the world." Four pages. The fourth page ended mid-sentence at the bottom, and the fifth page — the next sheet in the bundle — began three weeks later, discussing marmalade.

There is no marmalade so interesting that it follows naturally from a secret door in the world.

I laid the pages side by side on the bedspread and went through the whole bundle twice more, and once you see the method you cannot unsee it. Not many pages removed — perhaps half a dozen across seven years — and never torn out crudely. Cut, with a blade, close along the spine, and the remaining stubs pressed flat so the eye slides past them. Dates matched around the excisions. Whatever had been taken had been taken carefully, by someone who understood paper the way I understood ore samples.

A ledger never lies. It can only be made to lie. And the making always leaves a mark.

The question, which I turned over like a coin of doubtful mint, was not *who* had cut the pages. There was only one pair of hands in this story steady enough, patient enough, and grieving enough. The question was *why*. A man removes evidence of his own guilt, or a man removes evidence of another's innocence, and those are very different crimes wearing the same scissors.

And underneath that question, unwelcome as a stone in a boot: what if he removed them because they implicated *me*? Because Vane had been married to my sister for six years and I had spent those six years telling anyone who would listen that the man was a glory-hunting fool who'd get a convoy sunk. What if Margaret had written something kind about him, something prophetic, and Ivo had cut it out to spare me? Or worse — to protect me from myself?

You begin to see the difficulty. Suspicion is a solvent. It eats through everything you thought was solid, including your own reasons for suspecting.

* * *

Supper that evening was mutton again — Harrow had requested it specifically, which told me the Admiralty's idea of hospitality research extended exactly as far as the previous night's menu — and I watched the table the way I had learned to watch it. Harrow performing the genial inspector, asking Ivo questions shaped like small talk: *How long does a supply of oil last you in a bad month, Mr. Okafor? Do you find the winters trying, alone up here?* Each question a little sounding-line dropped into dark water, Harrow reading the depth off the line when it came back up.

Ivo answered everything evenly. Too evenly, maybe. But I had spent two days in this man's company and I could no longer establish a baseline for him, because grief had flattened his voice years before any investigator arrived. That is the genius of mourning, if you want to hide something inside it: nobody can tell where the sorrow ends and the concealment begins. Perhaps not even the mourner.

Afterward, while Harrow retired to make his notes — I heard his pencil scratching through the floorboards, industrious as a mouse in a wall — I found Ivo in the lamp room, trimming the wick with those careful hands of his. Up there the wind spoke its whole vocabulary at once, and we had to stand close to be heard.

"I've been reading Margaret's letters," I said.

His hands did not stop moving. I will grant him that. "She wrote beautifully."

"She did. She wrote four pages in March of '87 about Captain Vane dining with you in Falmouth. The fourth page ends mid-sentence." I let that sit between us like a dropped tool. "The next page is about marmalade."

The wick trimmer continued its small precise arc. "Paper spoils up here," he said. "Salt air. Damp. Pages crumble."

"Pages crumble," I agreed. "They don't crumble *along the spine*, in straight lines, in six places across seven years, with the dates matched round them."

Now he stopped. He set the trimmer down on the brass tray with a click that the wind almost took, and he looked at me, and this is the part I keep returning to, the part that makes this whole business unbearable: he looked at me with such plain, unhurried kindness that I felt like a burglar caught in a church. Not angry. Not cornered. *Sorry for me.*

"You've been sitting upstairs all afternoon doing arithmetic on your sister," he said.

"Somebody has to do the sums."

"And what do they come to?"

"That's rather the point of arithmetic, Mr. Okafor. You don't get to choose the answer." I took out the March letter, because I had brought it up with me the way a man carries a warrant he hopes he won't have to serve. "I'm not Harrow. I don't care about your oil stores or your tallow or whatever it is he smiles about over the mutton. I care about one question, and I'll ask it once, man to man, without any Admiralty listening. Did Elias Vane die on the rocks at Gwennap Head in October of '86?"

The lamp roared softly above us. Somewhere below, Harrow's pencil scratched on, keeping its own ledger.

"No," Ivo said.

The word went through me like cold water through a boot. Not because I hadn't suspected it — I had carried the suspicion for two days now like a hot coal passed hand to hand — but because hearing it aloud changed its weight entirely. Suspicions can be put down. Answers cannot.

"Then where—"

"No." He raised one hand, gentle as a man stopping traffic. "That's not a refusal, Edmund. It's an instalment. You asked one question and I've paid it. Come back tomorrow and ask the next one, and I'll pay that too. But not tonight, and not up here, where every wall has ears and one of the ears writes things down." He nodded toward the floorboards, toward the scratching pencil beneath us. "Your friend downstairs searched the oil store today. Did he mention it?"

"He mentioned nothing to me."

"He smiled at me over the mutton and asked for a second helping."

I stood there holding my sister's letter, feeling the full absurdity of my position. I had come armed with an audit and a confrontation, prepared — I admit it — to offer friendship as leverage, to say *I like you, Ivo, and liking you is precisely why you owe me the truth*, which is the most respectable form of blackmail ever devised. And instead the blackmailer's quarry had handed me a confession in instalments and warned me about the weather.

"Why tell me anything?" I asked. "You must know I'll write it down. I'm a Vane. Whatever's left of that family, I'm it."

"Exactly," he said, and trimmed his wick, and turned back to the light as though I had asked him about the tide tables. "Because whatever is left of that family deserves better than to learn it from a naval lieutenant's report."

I went down the stairs slowly, one hand on the cold iron rail, my sister's letter folded against my chest like something contraband. In the passage below, Harrow's door opened just as I passed it — pure coincidence, I'm sure, the way his yawns always coincided with interesting conversations — and he gave me his pleasant grey smile.

"A fine night coming," he said. "Storm by morning, I shouldn't wonder. These towers do suffer in storms."

"Do they?" I said.

"Oh, yes." He looked past me, up the stairwell, toward the light. "Everything hidden in a high place eventually comes down."

I lay awake a long while after that, listening to the wind rise, doing sums in the dark. And near dawn, as the first gusts began to shake the glass, I heard a sound I could not account for: the scrape of the boat below, oars shipped quietly, and low voices on the rocks where no voice had any honest business being.

Two voices. One of them hoarse, and unfamiliar, and alive.
