## 8. Confession at Low Water

*IVO*

The tide goes out further in March than any other month, as though the sea, having spent the winter throwing itself at our rocks, finally steps back to see what damage it has done. Tonight it withdrew past the third ledge — a thing I have witnessed perhaps six times in seven years — and laid bare the causeway that only a liar and a fool would cross.

I was both.

Vane went first. Of course he did. Seven years dead and still giving orders like a man who expects them obeyed. He picked his way down over the weed-slick granite in his borrowed oilskins, moving like a heron, all elbows and patience, and I followed with the packet inside my coat, pressed against my chest where my heart ought to have been doing more useful work.

Above us, the tower stood black against a sky the colour of wet slate. Harrow's watchman sat in the lee of the fuel store, wrapped in a blanket, pretending with great commitment to be awake. We had watched him settle for an hour through the slit of the oil store door, and Ivo Okafor, keeper of the light, keeper of the arithmetic of silence, had made his calculation: two men could cross the ledges below the watchman's sightline if they went now, while the tide was out, and returned before it turned.

If we did not return, the sea would keep its mouth shut. The sea has always been better at discretion than I am.

"Mind the kelp," Vane said, without looking back. "It holds your foot an extra second before it lets go. Like guilt."

"You would know."

"I would." He stopped on the second ledge, where the water had carved a basin deep enough to hold a drowned man comfortably, and turned to face me. In the grey half-light he looked less like Elias Vane than like the idea of him, worn thin — the beard gone iron-grey, the cheekbones standing up like ribs. "Sit down, Ivo."

"I'll stand."

"You always stood when you meant to stay. Sit down." And because there is no arguing with that voice — I had tried, across a quarter-deck and a deathbed and seven years of silence — I sat on the cold stone, and he sat opposite me, and between us lay four feet of wet rock and everything we had not said since 1886.

For a long time neither of us spoke. Below us the tide pooled and shifted, filling the gullies one by one, patient as an auditor.

"The night I pulled you from the water," I said at last. "You were dying. I held you and you told me to take the packet and say nothing, and then you stopped breathing against my shoulder. I felt it stop. You don't do that to a man and then turn up alive in his oil store seven years later expecting tea."

"I didn't expect tea."

"You got tea."

"You make terrible tea," he said. "That was the worst part. Dying was easier than drinking it."

I laughed before I could stop myself, and hated him a little for making me laugh, and loved him a little too, and neither of those things helped anyone.

"I was dead," he said quietly. "Ivo, I mean it. For three days, by every measure that matters. They fished me off that raft half-drowned and raving, and the Admiralty's own surgeon wrote *deceased*, and some clerk with a good hand copied it into the ledger, and that clerk's error is the only reason I'm sitting here. By the time I woke in a civilian hospital under a name not mine, I was a traitor in every newspaper and a ghost in every ledger, and I understood — slowly, the way you understand frostbite — that being alive was going to be the harder sentence." He looked down at his hands. "I let you mourn me. I knew you would. I counted on it."

"Yes," I said. "You did."

"Say what you've been not saying for a week."

"What I've been not saying," I said, "is that I kept your secret so well I built a life inside it. A whole lighthouse of silence, room by room. And I told myself it was loyalty. And it was. But it was also easier than the truth, because the truth would have cost me this light, this uniform, the last shape she gave my life—" I stopped. His eyes had gone still at the word *she*, and I realised I had opened a door I had kept locked even from myself.

"Say her name," Vane said.

"Clara," I said. It came out rough. "Her name was Clara."

"I know. Edmund talks about her the way other men talk about saints. I've had to sit behind a wall listening to him do it, Ivo, knowing I'm the reason his sister married a man who'd end up choosing a dead friend over a living family—"

"Don't." My voice cracked on it. "Don't you dare put that on me tonight. She died of fever, Elias. Fever doesn't consult the Admiralty."

"No. But she waited for letters you never sent, because you were busy keeping mine." He leaned forward. "Tell me about the night I 'died.' All of it. Because I've had seven years of my version, and yours has been locked in your face ever since, and I can't gamble both our lives on half a confession."

So I told him. The fog, the guns, the convoy scattered like spilled beads. The order he'd refused — I had heard the shouting through the bulkhead, the captain's voice going up and up until it broke. The boat going down. The packet pressed into my hands with hands already cold: *Keep it. Don't open it. Don't give it to anyone who comes asking politely.* Him going still against me. Me swimming for the rocks with a dead man's secret and a living man's duty, and choosing, without ever admitting I'd chosen, to be silent about both.

"And the part you haven't said," Vane said, when I finished, "is whether you believed me innocent."

I looked at him. The tide was coming back; I could hear it finding the lower gullies, one by one, like fingers closing.

"I read the papers," I said. "They said you abandoned a convoy to save a hospital ship full of civilians. And then they called it treason. And I thought: either the newspapers are wrong, or the man who died in my arms was wrong, and I had already decided which of those I could live with." I took out the packet. It was smaller than memory had made it — seven years of worship shrunk to a foot of oiled canvas and string. "But I never opened it. That was the coward's portion. I kept faith without paying for it."

"Open it now."

"I've read it. Last night, after Harrow found the oil store disturbed."

Something moved through his face — not anger. Relief, maybe, which is worse, because relief means you expected to be betrayed.

"Then you know it isn't just my innocence in there. It's the convoy logs. It's the order itself, signed, dated, countermanded. It's names — men who are admirals now, whose careers sit on top of my grave like a headstone. If we hand that to the Admiralty, it goes into a room and dies there. If we hand it to the newspapers, I trade one kind of ruin for another. But Edmund—" He exhaled. "Edmund Vane is the only man alive who wants the truth and can survive having it."

"He's also the man hunting us."

"He's the man mourning me," Vane said. "There's a difference. Grief looks everywhere; suspicion only looks where it's pointed." He stood, joints cracking like the old boat davits. "Tomorrow night. The storm's coming — you can smell it, don't pretend you can't. When a storm hits, everyone's courage gets reassigned to survival. We give him the packet while the wind screams, when no report can leave the station and no decision can be wired away. Make him read it by lantern light with the sea trying to drown us all. A man reads differently in a storm, Ivo. Slower. Truer."

"And if he chooses the Admiralty after all? If family love loses to institutional loyalty?"

Vane looked out at the darkening water, and for a moment the old captain stood in him straight as a mast.

"Then we're traitors together," he said, "and you finally get the exile you've been too honest to earn. Come on. The tide's turning, and I refuse to die twice in the same century."

We crossed back over the ledges with the sea climbing our heels, and above us, unseen, the light began its rotation, sweeping its white arm across the water like a man patting the dark, saying *still here, still here, still here* — and I thought: not for much longer, perhaps. Not for much longer.

* * *

*VANE*

There is a particular torture reserved for hidden men, and it is dinner.

I heard it through two feet of stone wall: the clink of Ivo's good spoons, the scrape of a chair, and Edmund Vane's voice — my wife's brother's voice, my brother's voice, because Margaret made him mine the day she married me and nobody ever filed the paperwork to undo it — saying, *"More potatoes, Mr. Okafor? Clara always said you ate like a gull."*

Clara. He carried her into every room like a lamp.

I sat on a crate of rope in the disused oil store with my knees against my chest like a stoker in a sinking ship, and I listened to my own funeral continuing at volume, and I will record honestly that I nearly gave myself away twice. Once at the laugh — Edmund laughs exactly as he did at nineteen, a bark followed by immediate apology — and once when Harrow spoke. The lieutenant has a dry, unhurried voice, the voice of a man who has never once been surprised and intends to keep his record. He asked Ivo about tide tables. About supply schedules. About, with exquisite casualness, *whether the station had seen any small craft lately, what with the weather.*

And Ivo — God help me, Ivo lied like a lighthouse shines: steadily, mechanically, with no visible effort, which is somehow more frightening than lying well.

*"Nothing but wreckage and the usual gulls, Lieutenant."*

Afterward, at low water, on the ledges, I made him say the rest of it. I have written in this account that I demanded his confession, but the truth is I demanded my own absolution, and he was stubborn enough to make me earn it. Good. I'd have trusted him less if he'd forgiven me easily. Forgiveness given cheap is just pity wearing its Sunday clothes.

Now I stand at the oil store's high slit window, watching the barometer fall the way a condemned man watches the clock. The glass has dropped a full inch since morning. The gulls have gone inland, which is the sea's oldest warning: *we are leaving; you should consider it.* The swell is already changing its mind out there, coming round to the south-west in long, deliberate swells, each one bigger than the last, like a debt collector counting up.

Tomorrow night, or the night after, the season's last great storm will come ashore here, and everything will happen at once, because that is how storms and truths prefer it.

I think about what I will say to Edmund. I have drafted it forty times and every draft is a lie of one kind or another — *Elias, I'm sorry I let you believe* — no. There is no gentle version. There is only the packet in Ivo's coat and the lantern in Edmund's hand and whatever is left of my name at the bottom of the arithmetic.

Below me, Ivo walks the gallery, checking the storm shutters, moving through his rituals like a priest who suspects the church is about to burn. He does not know I watch him. He thinks his grief is private. It has never once been private; it has been the most legible thing about him since the day I met him, a man keeping a light for a woman already dead and a friend officially drowned, tending flames for ghosts, wearing a uniform for a letter that never comes.

Well. Tomorrow a letter comes.

The wind arrives ahead of schedule, just past midnight — a single long breath from the south-west that makes the whole tower hum like a struck wire — and I put my palm flat against the cold stone and feel it shudder, and I think:

Hold together, old girl. One more night. Then you can have us all.
