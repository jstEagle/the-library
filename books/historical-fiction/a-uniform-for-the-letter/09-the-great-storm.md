## 9. The Great Storm

The barometer had been falling for two days the way a man falls downstairs — slowly at first, then all at once — and by the evening of the ninth of March I had stopped pretending I didn't know what was coming.

I have kept lights through gales that would make a bishop swear, but this one arrived differently. It came out of the northwest without any of the usual courtesies: no long swell to announce it, no gradual darkening, just a wall of weather that seemed to have taken personal offence at our headland. By eight o'clock the sea was coming over the lantern gallery in sheets, and the light itself — my light, my one faithful companion these seven years — was burning steady in its glass room while the world outside tried to tear it down.

Vane stood at the foot of the stairs watching me check the reserve lamps. He had been in the oil store eleven days, and eleven days of hiding had given him back something of his old look: a kind of hungry alertness, like a hawk deciding whether the mouse is worth the dive.

"You could still send me away tonight," he said. "Before it gets bad."

"It's already bad. And no." I wound the clockwork that turned the lens. "You'll stay where I can see you. If the sea wants you, Elias, it'll have to get past me first, and I'm notoriously difficult."

He almost smiled. It was the closest we had come in seven years, and I counted it.

The plan — Edmund's plan, really, though he did not yet know it was his own — was simple enough to survive a storm. In the morning, when Harrow's boat went back to Falmouth for supplies, Edmund would come down to the rocks alone. We would give him the packet. Not Harrow; never Harrow, who had spent a fortnight being courteous at us the way a cat is courteous to a bird. Edmund, who mourned his sister and his friend with an honesty that made him dangerous only to liars.

That was the morning. This was the night, and the night had other appointments.

* * *

The light failed at twenty minutes past eleven.

I want to be precise about that, because precision is all I had left. One moment the beam was sweeping its great slow arc across the water, and the next there was a sound from the lamp room like a door slamming in God's house, and the arc died mid-swing. Darkness fell over the station so completely that I could not see my own hands.

"The mantle," Vane said, already moving. "Or the oil feed."

"Both possible. Neither matters. What matters is that there are ships out there right now steering by nothing." I was climbing. "Get below. Stay inside the tower — do you hear me? Inside."

I heard him say he heard me. I heard him mean to obey it. And then I was up in the lantern, hands working blind, and the wind hit me like a debt collector.

Here is what nobody tells you about relighting a lamp in a gale: it is not courage, it is arithmetic. Match, valve, pressure, patience. My fingers were shaking and I let them shake and worked anyway, because the shaking doesn't matter if the sequence holds. The glass above me flexed and boomed. Somewhere below, a door banged that should not have been open.

I got the spare mantle lit at half past midnight. The beam swung out again, trembling, imperfect, alive — and in its first full sweep I saw the harbour, and saw that the boat moorings were tearing loose, and saw a figure down among them hauling line against the sea.

Not a figure. A coat I knew, because I had mended its shoulder myself with sail thread and a curse.

"Vane!" I shouted it into a wind that ate words whole. "Inside! Inside, you beautiful idiot!"

He turned once — and the beam swept past him, and in that half-second of illumination I saw what else he had seen: a lantern bobbing on the quay path. Two men in oilskins. One of them built like Harrow, holding the light high, looking not at the boats but at the man securing them.

* * *

**Vane**

There is a particular clarity that comes when you stop caring whether you live. I had felt it once before, seven years ago, in the water off Cape Corvo, and I felt it now, ankle-deep in freezing spray, with the *Providence*'s dinghy tearing at its painter like a horse that smelled fire.

If I lost the dinghy, the station lost its last seaworthy escape. If I kept hold of the line, the sea might decide to take me as payment. These were the terms. I found I preferred them to the ones I'd lived under for seven years — the terms of being a dead man, which required nothing of me but silence.

The line went taut. I wrapped it twice around the bollard and threw my weight backward, and the sea hit me broadside and knocked me flat, and I held on anyway, because that was the whole of my character now: a man holding a rope in the dark.

A lantern came toward me along the quay. I knew the walk before I knew the face. Harrow. Of course. The man had spent two weeks finding nothing, and here was the universe handing him everything on a night when no one could plausibly claim it was anyone else.

"I can see you're busy," Harrow called, with his terrible politeness. "Perhaps you'd introduce yourself?"

I looked at the rope. I looked at the lantern. Behind Harrow, smaller, hurrying, came Edmund — and Edmund was not looking at me. Edmund was looking at the bundle under Harrow's arm, the oilskin packet, sodden and torn open, papers fanning loose in the wind like a flock of frightened birds.

It had been under the floor of the lamp room. Which meant Harrow had gone up while Ivo climbed, or while Ivo fought the sea, and taken it. Seven years of my life, blowing across a Cornish quay.

"Captain," I said, because lying was finished, "the dinghy needs another hand or she'll smash herself to matchwood. Help me or watch. But choose quickly."

* * *

**Edmund**

My sister used to say that grief is a ledger, and that the cruellest entries are the ones you can't verify. She would have appreciated the irony: her brother standing on a quay in a hurricane, reading pages she had never seen, torn from letters she had written, while the author of her widowhood hauled on a rope ten feet away.

I had watched Harrow take the packet from beneath the lamp room floor. I had watched him do it with a warrant he hadn't shown and a satisfaction he couldn't hide, and I had followed him down here intending — I don't fully know what. Argument. Arrest. Something official.

Then the wind took the papers.

They went everywhere. Across the stones, into the foam, one sheet plastered against my chest like a hand. I caught it, pinned it under my coat, and by lantern light — Harrow's lantern, held shaking against the gale — I read four lines in a naval clerk's hand that turned seven years upside down.

An order refused. Civilians aboard a hospital transport. Vane's signature declining to convoy a ship he believed would draw fire onto them. The order countermanded by someone else's authority, the refusal reported upward twisted, the report bearing a name that was not Vane's beside the word *insubordinate*.

"He refused to murder civilians," I said. The wind took most of it. I said it again, louder, directly into Harrow's face. "This is the treason. This is the whole of it."

"The interpretation of classified documents is not—"

"Give me the rest of them."

Behind him, Vane shouted something about the dinghy, and the dinghy answered by leaping off the crest of a wave and coming down on the bollard hard enough to crack stone. The painter parted. Vane went with it — one instant upright, the next swallowed, the rope whipping free across the wet rock like a living thing.

I have thought since about how fast the mind moves in catastrophe. In the space between one wave and the next I understood three things entirely: that Elias Vane was innocent; that Harrow had known it for years and built his career on the lie; and that I was going into the water after my sister's husband whether the sea approved or not.

I dropped the lantern. It went out. Good — darkness favours the desperate.

* * *

**Ivo**

I came down the tower stairs faster than any keeper should and out into a night that had stopped being weather and become an argument between heaven and sea. No lantern. The beam overhead swept past every few seconds, giving me the world in flashes: the empty bollard, the parted rope, the black water, and — flash — two heads in it, close together, one dark-haired and one fair.

Edmund had gone in after him. Of course he had. That family never learned to stay ashore.

I got the heaving line from the boat shed, paid it out, and went down the rocks to the low shelf where the sea came at me in shoulders of green glass. Flash: Vane surfacing, gasping, dragging Edmund by the collar. Flash: a wave erasing them both. Flash: Harrow on the quay, and I will grant the man this — whatever else he was, in that moment he was coiling the fallen rope's end and throwing it, badly, again and again, into the dark.

"IVO!" Vane's voice, cracked with salt and seven years. "IVO!"

"On the line!" I had tied it around my own waist. Sentimental, perhaps. Practical, certainly — if the sea took me, it would at least take me somewhere I could argue with.

Flash: their hands closing on the rope. Then weight, real weight, the honest dead weight of drowning men, and I leaned back against it with my heels wedged in rock and my shoulders doing what they had done for thirty years, which was hold.

We came up the shelf together in pieces. Vane first, coughing the Atlantic out of himself. Then Edmund, white as the foam, gripping my forearm and laughing — actually laughing, a wild, salt-broken sound — with a soaked page crushed in his fist.

"I've read it," he shouted at me over the wind. "Ivo — I've read all of it. He refused the order. They lied. They let us bury an empty coffin—" His voice broke clean through. "Seven years. You knew. All this time you knew and you kept him, you kept him safe—"

"I kept a letter," I said. "The keeping was his."

Harrow stood above us on the quay, lantern dead in his hand, rain streaming off him, and looked at the three of us — the keeper, the corpse, the mourner — tangled together on the rocks like survivors of the same wreck, which is precisely what we were. He opened his mouth. Whatever verdict the Admiralty kept in him, the storm blew it away unread.

Above us, my light swept on, steady as a heartbeat, lighting the way home for ships that would never know how nearly it hadn't.

And in the lee of the boat shed, shivering, drowned, exposed, cleared, Elias Vane put his hand on my shoulder and said, quietly enough that only I heard it:

"Well, Keeper. I suppose you'll want your uniform back."

"No," I said, and found I meant it. "I find I've outgrown it."

The storm was not done with us. Far out in the dark, a ship's distress rocket went up — red starburst, falling, dying — and the sea turned its attention from us to something newer and began, with a kind of terrible appetite, to reach for it.
