## 10. A Uniform for the Letter

They gave me a medal. That is the part I still find funny, when I find any of it funny at all. A man spends seven years keeping a secret in an oilskin packet under the lamp room floor, lies to an officer of the Crown across his own dinner table, harbours a dead man in a shed that smelled of whale oil and regret — and the Admiralty's response, after all the shouting was done, was to send him a medal in a box lined with velvet.

I have not opened the box. It sits on my kitchen table at the station, next to the salt cellar, and every morning I look at it the way one looks at a relative one does not entirely trust. Edmund says I should open it. Edmund says a great many things with the serene confidence of a man who has spent his life being right in rooms where being right costs nothing.

It is April now. The storms have gone off to bully some other coast, and the sea has gone that pale, apologetic green it wears in spring, like a brawler pretending he was never in the fight. The gorse on the headland has come up yellow and shameless. Seven springs I have watched it bloom from the gallery rail, and this will be the last, and I find I am not so much sad about it as I am surprised — surprised the way you are surprised when a tooth that has ached for years finally comes out and the jaw feels strange and empty and, God help me, better.

* * *

The clearing of Elias Vane's name took four months, which tells you roughly how much the Admiralty valued the truth against the convenience of its own paperwork. There were hearings in Plymouth. There were men in good coats saying *the record shows* and *the convoy order was clear* and *one hesitates to impugn*, and then there was the packet — my packet, Vane's packet, the one I had kept beneath the lamp for seven years like a stone in the shoe of my conscience — and the record showed, when it was finally made to show, that Captain Elias Vane had been ordered to hold a convoy position that would have put him broadside to a French squadron with three hundred civilian evacuees in his holds, and had refused, and had saved every one of them, and had been called a traitor for his trouble by men who had never smelled salt water except off a club sandwich.

Harrow was reassigned. *Reassigned* — the word doing the work of a dozen more honest ones. I am told he is now inspecting coaling depots in Malta, where the only storms are administrative. I do not hate him. That surprised me too. He was a man doing a job the way a hammer does a job, and a hammer cannot be blamed for the carpenter's hand. Though I will confess — privately, here, in this letter, where I confess everything now — that when the order came through, I read his name twice, and both times I smiled like a man who has found a shilling in the street and knows he shouldn't keep it but keeps it anyway.

* * *

Edmund came to the station in March, on a day so mild it felt like an apology. He stood in the doorway of my kitchen holding his hat, which he had done on every visit, as though the room might at any moment require him to leave at speed, and he said, "Ivo, I've spoken to the Board. There's a position for you. Shore-based. Signal station at Falmouth — light duties, good pay, a house with a garden. And there's the cottage at Penzance besides, mine, half-empty, yours whenever you want it. Margaret would have—" He stopped, the way he always stopped at her name, and looked out the window at the sea, and finished: "You've kept enough. Let someone keep you, for once."

And there it was. The whole of it, offered on a plate with a garden attached. A uniform for the letter, as it were — the institution, having taken everything else, offering to give me back a smaller, tidier version of myself, with a pension and a rank and a place in the order of things. Belonging, issued by the yard, in regulation cloth.

I made him tea while I thought about it, which is what I do when I am thinking, and Edmund, who had learned me over those months as well as any man has, drank his tea and waited.

"No," I said at last.

He didn't argue. That is the thing about Edmund that his sister's death taught him and nothing else could have: that some refusals are not rejections but compass readings. He only said, "Falmouth, though. You'd be good at Falmouth."

"I'd be good at Falmouth," I agreed. "That's the trouble. I'd be good at anything they put in front of me, and I'd be nobody's. I've spent seven years being the keeper of a light because I could not be the keeper of anything else. The tower didn't choose me, Edmund. I hid in it. And a man who leaves a hiding place ought not to walk straight into the next one, even if the next one has a garden."

He turned his hat in his hands. "Then what?"

"Then I'm going to see what I am when nothing is keeping me." I heard how it sounded and laughed. "That's a young man's sentence. I'm fifty-one. But I find the sentence doesn't check your age at the door."

* * *

Vane left before I did. That was right, though it took a fortnight of the two of us circling each other like dogs who have decided against fighting but haven't settled on friendship. He came to the gallery on his last evening — his beard trimmed now, his face still gaunt but with the look of a man eating regularly — and stood looking at the sea with his hands behind his back in the old way, and said, "You could come with me. There's shipping out of Falmouth. A man with your hands could always find work aboard."

"No," I said again, and this time it was easier. "I've done my time on water. I want to be somewhere I can see the end of a street."

He laughed — the first real laugh I'd had out of him since the night he came ashore gaunt and furious in the fog — and then he was quiet a long while, and then he said, "I blamed you. That night. For leaving me on the deck of the *Sparrowhawk*. For rowing away." He looked at me. "I was wrong. You rowed away because there was nothing to row *to*. I've had seven years to learn the difference between a man who abandons you and a man who carries you the only way he can. You carried me under a floorboard for seven years, Ivo. That's not nothing. That's the heaviest way there is."

I said nothing, because there was nothing, because some sentences you just have to stand inside for a while like weather.

He shook my hand at the top of the cliff path the next morning, and he did not say goodbye, because men like us don't, and he went down to the coach road with his kit on his shoulder, and I watched him until the gorse took him, and then I went up and trimmed the wick, because the light does not care what you are feeling.

* * *

The new keeper arrives Thursday. A young man, Cornish, earnest, with the particular terror of a man about to take charge of his first station. I have spent the last week teaching him the rhythms — the clockwork, the mercury, the moods of the fog signal, the way the sea lies to you in an easterly. Yesterday he asked me, shyly, how long I'd kept the light, and I said seven years, and he said, "That's a long time to be alone," and I said, "I wasn't alone. I was keeping company with a letter," and he looked at me the way the young look at the old, politely certain I was mad, and I let him.

Tomorrow I leave. The boat comes at nine. I have packed very little: clothes, the medal box (unopened — I have decided it will make a fine container for sewing needles, should I ever take up sewing, which I will not), and one letter.

* * *

The letter is to my wife.

Adaeze has been dead seven years, and I have written to her perhaps a hundred times in my head and never once on paper, because paper makes a thing real and I was not ready for any more real things. But tonight the tower is clean, the lamp is trimmed, the packet's hiding place under the floorboards holds nothing but a ring of dust in the shape of the truth, and I am sitting at my kitchen table with a pen and a candle and the whole dark headland for a witness, and I am writing to her at last.

*My love. You have been gone seven years and I have been faithful to you in the way of a man guarding a tomb, which is to say I have kept everything and lived nothing. Here is the truth, all of it, because you always said I would tell you everything eventually and you were right, as you were right about the hens and wrong about the marmalade.*

*I kept a dead man's secret under our floor. He was not dead. I lied for him, and lied for him, and nearly lost everything, and in the losing found out what I was keeping all along — which was not his secret but my own: that I was afraid. Afraid that if I stopped keeping things — the light, the packet, the grief — there would be nothing left of me. You would have laughed at that. You would have said, Ivo, a man is not a lighthouse, he does not go dark when the keeper leaves. And then you would have made me tea and told me to go and see the world I kept telling the ships about.*

*I am going, Adaeze. Not to Falmouth, not to a uniform, not to any post with a number on it. Just going. Edmund has a cottage; I have a shilling and a medal I intend to use for needles. I am told there is a world. I mean to find out if it keeps.*

*The light goes on tonight without me. That is the whole of what I have learned: it was never mine. It was only ever lent, like everything — like you, my love, lent to me for eleven years, and the keeping of you was the one posting I held with honour, and I lay down my commission now with a full report.*

*Yours entirely, and at last aloud,*

*Ivo*

I sealed it, and I did not address it, because there is no address for where she is, and because Edmund taught me — no, Vane taught me, or the sea did, or the seven years did — that a letter's work is in the writing. But I shall post it anyway. There is a box at the crossroads below the cliff path, and tomorrow, walking down to the boat with my kit on my shoulder like a man going to see a dead friend off on a coach, I will drop it through the slot and hear it land among the other letters, all of them going somewhere, all of them carried.

And then I will turn and walk to the boat, and I will not look back at the tower, because looking back is a keeper's habit and I have resigned.

Though I will tell you — you, letter, you dark page, you last confession — that as the boat pulls away tomorrow morning, and the tower stands up behind me white and patient against the spring sky, with the new keeper's face small and pale in the gallery, I already know what I will do.

I will raise my hand.

Not in farewell. In the old signalman's salute, the one that says *all's well, keep your watch, I am relieved and the relief is good.*

And if the young man raises his back — and he will, they always do, it is the first thing the tower teaches you — then I will turn to face the open water, and the open road past it, and whatever is keeping me next.
