## 1. What the Water Keeps

The tide went out at half past five that morning, and with it went the island's patience.

I learned to tell time by water before I ever learned to read it off a clock. The pearl beds gave us four working windows a day, and the Verrone Trading Company expected every one of them filled with bent backs and open hands. By six the divers were wading out past the marker stakes, their loincloths pale as moth wings in the grey light, and I was already seated on my upturned crate at the sorting table with a slate tally and a stub of pencil worn down to the width of a thumbnail.

Sixteen years old, and my whole kingdom was four feet of driftwood planking laid across two barrels.

"Almeida girl." That was Mateus, the bed foreman, who had a voice like gravel poured slowly and a heart he kept, so far as anyone could tell, in a locked drawer somewhere behind his ribs. "Count's light yesterday."

"The count is never light," I said. "The count is exactly what comes out of the water. If you want more pearls, complain to the oysters."

Mateus looked at me the way he always did — a long, tired blink, like a man watching weather come in — and moved on. He had been foreman since before my father drowned, and in all those years I don't believe I'd ever won an argument with him outright. But I'd learned you could win sideways. You could be so precisely, politely correct that a man gave up just to stop hearing his own echo.

It was a skill, I decided early on, that would serve me better than swimming.

* * *

Let me tell you what I carried, because everything about me — everything that mattered — could be weighed in two hands.

First: a knife. Three inches of blade, bone handle cracked and mended twice with wire, given to me by my father the year he died. Not for fighting. For cutting rope, for paring fruit, for the hundred small surgeries a life on the water requires. It hung at my hip on a strap Mother had stitched from sailcloth, and I slept with it under the edge of my mat the way other girls slept with dolls or prayers.

Second: a ledger stub. Half a page of company paper, torn from a ruined book Brandt had ordered pulped, on which I kept — in letters small enough to hide under a thumbprint — my private arithmetic. Not the company's numbers. Mine.

And third: a name. Joaquim Almeida. Diver, second class. Drowned in the season of the red wind, eight years past, when I was eight years old myself. The water took him between one dive and the next, and when they pulled him up his hands were still curved around nothing, reaching for a shell that had turned out not to exist.

That was the thing about my father's death nobody liked to say aloud: he had gone down chasing a ghost pearl. The divers called it a *sirena* — a flash of nacre in deep water that looks like fortune and is really only moonlight playing tricks on a hungry man. The company paid out nothing for a sirena death. Dying for a shell that wasn't there was, in the ledgers, dying of your own stupidity, and stupidity voided the widow's claim.

So my mother signed me over instead. Indenture, seven years' service against the debt of Father's funeral — a funeral we paid for ourselves, mind you, since the company wouldn't cover a sirena — plus the outstanding balance on our hut, plus interest compounded at a rate I have since calculated would embarrass a moneylender in any port from here to Lisbon.

Seven years. I have served six. One remains.

I know the figure to the copper. Fourteen pesos, eleven reales, and what the book calls *miscellaneous charges* — chalk, lamp oil, the annual fee for the privilege of living in a house the company owned on land the company owned above beds the company owned, in which the company owned, in every way but the paper sense, also me.

Some people keep saints' cards in their shirts. I kept sums.

* * *

The first basket of oysters came up at seven, hauled dripping onto the sand by girls younger than me who dove deeper than was sensible and surfaced with noses bleeding into the salt spray. I opened the shells with a short blade and a practiced wrist — twist, pop, sweep the fingers through the mantle, feel for the hard little secret inside.

Most oysters held nothing. That was the ocean's oldest joke, and it never got less funny.

"Big one!" crowed Lurdes, who was twelve and built like a knot in rope and had no fear of anything except her aunt's slipper. She came pelting up the beach holding a shell the size of her two fists together. "Astrid, look, look —"

"I'm looking."

"It's going to be a big one. I can feel it. It has a *weight* to it."

"Lurdes. Every shell has weight. That is what makes it a shell and not a rumor."

She pried it open right there in front of me, tongue between her teeth, and inside was — meat. Meat and mud and one small blister pearl, cloudy as a fish eye, worth perhaps half a real.

"That's a sirena pearl," I told her gently, handing it back. "Pretty, worthless. Keep it. Sew it into your hem and in ten years it'll still be pretty and worthless, which is more than most things manage."

Lurdes laughed and bit the pearl anyway — she was forever testing whether pearls were edible; they are not, and she was forever surprised — and ran back down to the water. I watched her go and felt the old familiar ache settle behind my breastbone, the one that lived where grief and arithmetic met.

Because here is what the sorting table taught me, lesson after lesson, year after year: the sea gives and the company takes, and the difference between those two motions is written down, in my handwriting, in books I am not permitted to correct.

* * *

At midmorning the wind swung around and brought the smell of rain that wasn't coming yet — the monsoon was weeks off, but you could smell it gathering itself out beyond the strait like a dog circling a door. The bridge across to the mainland stood low and brown against the horizon, its pilings dark with old salt. Twice a year it opened for carts and travelers and the company's wagons of pearls going north. The rest of the time it might as well have been painted on the sky.

I had crossed it once, when I was small enough to ride on Father's shoulders. I remembered almost nothing of it except that the far side smelled different. Greener. Like somewhere else.

"Astrid." Mateus again, appearing beside my table with the suddenness of a man who had spent thirty years surprising divers. "Brandt wants the morning tallies by noon. Clean copy."

"Clean copy goes to the countinghouse," I said. "The countinghouse clerk is Senhor Ferro, and Senhor Ferro says noon means noon and not eleven fifty-five, because last time I was early he had to stop eating his lunch to sign for them, and a man interrupted at lunch makes errors, and errors in the countinghouse are — "

"— are charged against the compound. Yes." Mateus rubbed his face with both hands. "You've made that speech before."

"It's a good speech. I stand behind it."

He almost smiled. With Mateus this consisted of one eyebrow rising approximately a millimeter, but I counted it. "Noon, then. And Almeida — the girl Lurdes. Her mother's arrears came due this week. Two pesos short."

I knew. Of course I knew. Lurdes's mother, Serafina, widowed the same red-wind season as mine, three children, a lung that whistled like a kettle. Her account was page nine of my private stub, and I could recite it forward and backward: principal, interest, the medicine charge from two years ago that the dispensary had entered twice by "error," the shoe tax, the roof tax, the tax for the repair of a fence neither of us had ever seen anyone lean on.

"What happens if she's short?" I asked, though I knew.

"Company holds the eldest child's labor another season."

The eldest child. Twelve years old, built like a knot in rope, currently at the bottom of the bay pretending pearls were food.

"Noon," said Mateus, and walked away before I could answer, which was his way of saying he hated it too and had stopped having opinions about it sometime around the previous century.

I looked down at my tally sheet. Forty-one shells opened, four market pearls, one baroque misfit the graders would call *ornamental* and pay for as *flawed*. I picked up my pencil stub and wrote the numbers true, because that was the one rebellion available to a sixteen-year-old clerk that cost nothing: I wrote down exactly what happened. Somewhere between the beds and the countinghouse, the numbers would grow fatter for the company and thinner for the divers, but on my sheet — on *my* sheet — the truth would sit in neat columns, patient as a stone under water, waiting.

Everything waits, if you write it small enough. That is the first thing a ledger teaches you, and the last thing it forgives.

* * *

I ate my midday rice on the seawall with my back to the compound, knees up, knife across my lap where it belonged. From there you could see the whole architecture of our captivity arranged like a table setting: the drying sheds, the grading house, the countinghouse with its shuttered windows, and beyond them the overseer's quarters — two stories, whitewashed, the only building on the island with glass in it. Brandt's flag flew from the pole beside it, some northern colors gone pink at the edges from the salt.

Mother found me there, as she always did, carrying a gourd of water and wearing the expression she wore when she wanted to talk about something without talking about it.

"You're squinting at the bridge again," she said, sitting down with a grunt.

"I'm squinting at the horizon. The bridge happens to be in it."

"Mm." She drank, passed me the gourd. Her hands were earth-brown from the orchard, nails permanently crescented with soil no amount of scrubbing fully evicted. She tended the company's mango and lime trees, and she did it with the tenderness of a woman who had made her peace with feeding the hand that held her family down — or so it appeared. My mother appeared many things. "Dry season soon. The tollhouse opens."

"Tomás says this year the line will stretch to the chapel."

"Tomás says a great many things."

Tomás. Seventeen, my older brother by fourteen months and by roughly the distance between the earth and one particular star of his choosing. He worked the tollhouse gate in dry season, lifting the barrier arm for carts, and had discovered — with the unshockable pragmatism of a boy raised on debts — that a man who controls a gate controls a great deal else. Letters passed through that tollhouse that no company inspector ever saw. Petitions. Complaints. Love notes from divers to cousins on the mainland. Tomás charged nothing for it, which in his economy meant he charged everything: favors, silences, the delicious currency of being indispensable.

"He'll get himself flogged one day," Mother said, but she said it the way she said *the rain will come*, as a fact about weather rather than a fear.

"He'll get himself a boat ticket one day, more likely. Everyone owes Tomás something now."

"Everyone owing your brother something is not the same as anyone being free." She said it mildly, looking out at the water, and I felt it land the way her mild statements always landed — soft-footed, then heavy. "Your father used to say the same about the company, you know. Everyone owes Verrone. And the water keeps the difference."

The water keeps the difference. He had said it about tides, originally — the sea borrows and returns, minus whatever it decides to keep — and then he had gone and become part of the difference himself, and the phrase changed shape in our house forever.

I finished the rice. I wiped my hands on my skirt. And because the day was hot and the truth was heavier than usual, I said the thing I had been not-saying for six years:

"Mother. When my indenture ends — next dry season — I want you to come north with me. Across the bridge. Both of us. We'll find a town where nobody's name is in anybody's book."

She didn't laugh, which I appreciated. She considered it, the way she considered weather and grafting cuts and whether to spend a precious egg.

"And the debt?" she asked.

"There is no debt. There's a number someone wrote down, and numbers can be checked. When I'm free I'll check ours. Line by line. Interest, charges, all of it. If the total is honest I'll pay what's honest. But Mother—" I turned to look at her straight on, and I saw something flicker across her face, quick as a fish turning in shallow water, there and gone before I could name it. "If it isn't honest?"

For a moment she was quiet. A kingfisher hit the shallows below the wall and came up flashing.

"Then you'll want a bigger pencil," my mother said, and stood, and brushed the dust from her skirt, and told me the orchard limes needed picking before the rains, and left me sitting there with my heart going strangely, the way it did whenever she said something ordinary that wasn't.

* * *

The afternoon count came in heavy — a good bed, a lucky bed — and by the time I closed the tally the light had gone the color of weak tea. I carried my sheets to the countinghouse, waited outside Senhor Ferro's door until the church bell struck the hour exactly (he required it; punctuality was, he informed me once, the only virtue a clerk could practice without talent), and handed the tallies through the gap.

Ferro flipped through them, lips moving. Then he paused at the third sheet, and his brow folded.

"This diver's count. Ribeiro, J. Thirty-one shells?"

"Thirty-one."

"The basket log says twenty-eight."

I looked at the basket log, which lay open on his desk in another clerk's hand. Twenty-eight. I had watched Ribeiro's baskets come up myself. I had counted them into the shed myself, with Lurdes hauling and grinning beside me.

"Perhaps the basket log lost three," I said.

"Logs don't lose."

"No," I agreed pleasantly. "They don't. Someone loses for them."

Ferro looked up at me then — a long, measuring look, the kind you give a scale that has begun to surprise you — and made a small correction in the margin, and said nothing, and I took my leave.

But walking back along the seawall in the last light, I did what I always did: I opened the private stub against my thigh, wet my pencil stub, and wrote, in letters fine as hair, the discrepancy. Date, name, number, witness. The third such entry that month. The seventh that season.

Shells vanishing between the beds and the books. Pearls growing lighter as they traveled toward the company's coffers, like birds losing weight in flight. Small thefts, deniable thefts — or the visible edge of a theft so large nobody had thought to look underneath it.

I sewed the stub flat into the hem of my sleeve where it always lived, and I sat on the warm stones, and I listened to the water keeping its difference below me.

Somewhere out past the strait, the monsoon was assembling itself. Somewhere north, the bridge waited to swing open. And somewhere on this island — I could not yet say where, could not yet prove it, could only feel it the way you feel a storm in an old scar — something in the company's arithmetic was rotten all the way down to the pilings.

I sharpened my pencil stub on my father's knife and went home in the dark, counting.
