## 2. The Bridge Opens Twice a Year

The bridge opened on the first windless morning of the dry season, and the whole island heard it before anyone saw it — a groan from the far pilings that traveled across the water like an old man clearing his throat after a long sleep.

Astrid was at the pearl beds when it came, knee-deep in the shallows with a sorting tray balanced on her hip, and she felt the sound in her chest before her ears caught up to it. Around her, the divers straightened one by one out of the water like a row of question marks, all of them listening, all of them smiling in that careful way people smile when they are trying not to hope too loudly.

"There it is," said old Marisol, wringing out her hair. "The island's stomach growling."

"It's not the island," said her grandson Pio, who was eleven and knew everything. "It's the chains cooling off. Heat makes metal expand, so in dry season they—"

"Pio." Marisol held out her hand. "Give me your share of the morning's shells and expand somewhere else."

Astrid laughed despite herself, and then, because laughing cost nothing but was still somehow a currency she tried not to spend carelessly, she bent back over her tray. But her hands had gone slow. They always did, on bridge day.

The Verrone Trading Company Bridge was the only way off the island that didn't involve paying a boatman who drank half his fee before the second sandbar. Twice a year — once when the rains ended, once when they threatened to begin — the company lowered the toll and opened the span, and for three days the island poured itself toward the mainland and the mainland poured itself back: cloth merchants and salt carts and letter-carriers and one unforgettable year a man with a gramophone who set it up at the tollhouse and played the same waltz until even the gulls looked offended.

Astrid had crossed it four times in her life. Each time she had stood in the middle, where the planks hummed underfoot, and looked down at the green water sliding past, and thought: *elsewhere is right there. Elsewhere is under my feet.* And each time she had walked back onto the island because her indenture papers said she must, because the company held her father's debt the way the sea held her father himself — completely, and without any intention of giving him up.

She measured her world in carryable things. That was a habit she'd stopped apologizing for. A knife small enough to hide in a sleeve. A ledger stub light enough to memorize whole. A name — *João Almeida, drowned 1911, debt outstanding* — that weighed nothing at all and more than everything.

Today, though, today there was the bridge, and Tomás worked the tollhouse, and Tomás always saved her the best gossip the way other brothers saved sweets.

* * *

The tollhouse sat at the island end of the span, a wooden box on stilts with a window cut for collecting coins and complaints in equal measure. Tomás Almeida, nineteen years old and built like a well-fed heron, leaned in that window with his chin on his fists, watching the first crowd come down the ramp.

"Astrid!" He spotted her before she'd even reached the queue forming behind the rope. "Come to pay your toll or come to admire me? Be honest. I've been admiring myself all morning and I could use the competition."

"I've come to see if the rumors are true," Astrid said, "that they let anyone work the toll window now, even boys who failed arithmetic."

"I did not fail arithmetic. Arithmetic failed to be interesting." He reached through the window and tugged her braid, which was as close as he came to affection in public. Behind him, inside the tollhouse, the iron cash box sat open, and Astrid's eye — the eye that kept ledgers even in its sleep — counted the coins in it automatically. Forty-one reales and a button. Someone had paid with a button. She would have bet her knife it was Pio.

The crowd was thick already. Women with baskets of dried fish going across to sell; men coming back the other way with news folded into their shirts; children running the planks while their mothers shouted the names of saints after them like thrown nets. The air smelled of rope and frying oil and, faintly, of the mainland itself — a smell Astrid couldn't have described if you paid her, except that it was the smell of *not here*, and she breathed it in like medicine.

Tomás dropped his voice as she leaned against the window frame. "Letters came through last night. Eleven of them. I'll need your help with the distribution."

"Eleven?" Her eyebrows went up. In the wet season, when the bridge shut and the boats grew scarce, letters piled up like driftwood. Families on this island hadn't heard from sons and daughters sold north to the rubber camps in years — not because the letters weren't written, but because carrying them cost money the families didn't have, and because the company post charged by weight and read what it carried besides.

"The cobbler's wife got one from her boy in Colombo," Tomás went on, warming up. "Three pages. I skimmed — professionally, don't look at me like that, someone has to check for trouble — he's apprenticed proper, he's eating twice a day, and he's grown a mustache he describes in detail for a full paragraph. A full paragraph, Astrid. On a mustache."

"What does it say about it?"

"'It has begun to assert itself.'" 

Astrid put her hand over her mouth. "He wrote that to his mother?"

"He wrote that to his mother," Tomás confirmed solemnly, "and his mother will weep with pride, and it will be worth every real I didn't charge her." He shrugged, and for a moment the joke fell away from his face the way a shirt slips off one shoulder, showing something underneath. "That's the whole trade, isn't it. People pay the company to move their words and the company skims them like cream. Somebody ought to move them clean."

Somebody did. It was the island's worst-kept secret and best-kept arrangement: Tomás carried letters for the indentured families free of charge, tucked into the false bottom of the tollhouse cash box, sealed inside wax cylinders that had once held pearl samples. Nobody spoke of it above a whisper, and everybody spoke of it below one.

"You're a criminal," Astrid said fondly.

"I'm a public service." He glanced past her shoulder, and his voice changed — flattened, smoothed over, the way water flattens when a shadow crosses it. "Company."

She didn't turn around fast. She made herself not turn around fast. Then she turned, and there he was, coming down the ramp with two company clerks trailing him like pilot fish.

Overseer Brandt was not a large man, which everyone agreed was the most alarming thing about him. He had the neatness of a man who ironed his own soul. His boots never scuffed, his hat brim never bent, and when he smiled — which he did often, generously, at everyone — it never once reached the part of his face where opinions were kept. He had come to the island six years ago to manage accounts, and he had done so well at managing accounts that now he managed everything else too: the docks, the divers' rations, the weigh-scales, the debts, and, though no document said so, the bridge itself.

"Miss Almeida." Brandt stopped in front of the tollhouse window and inclined his hat with genuine courtesy. "Helping your brother count the crowd?"

"Counting everything, sir," Astrid said. "It's a habit."

"It's a virtue." He smiled his generous smile. "The company values habits. Especially in its clerks." His eyes moved over her — not lingering, not improper, just *inventorying*, the way he inventoried crates and men — and moved on. "Tell your mother the orchard rows look fine this year. Fine as ever. She has a steady hand."

"She'll be glad it was noticed, sir."

"Oh, everything is noticed." He said it lightly, the way a man says *good morning*, and walked on down the ramp, boots crisp on the planks, pilot fish bobbing after.

Astrid watched him go and found her hands had gone cold around the window frame.

"He gives me the shivers," Tomás said quietly, once the man was out of easy earshot. "Like a fish you can't tell is rotten because it's been packed in ice."

"That's exactly what he is."

"Mm. Well." Tomás brightened with visible effort. "Rotten fish aside — there's other news. Real news. You want it?"

"I always want it."

He beckoned her closer, close enough that the words could pass between them without passing the air between them. Down the ramp, the crowd noise swelled — a cart wheel squealing, somebody's rooster objecting to travel — good cover.

"One of the mainland carriers drinks with the military mailmen," Tomás said. "You know how it goes. Drink loosens envelopes. Anyway." He glanced left, glanced right, a man performing caution badly enough that it was clearly habit rather than fear. "There's talk of conscription lists. Navy lists. Every man of age on this coast, written down — names, trades, whose debt he owes. For when they start taking men for the war work. Dredging, loading, whatever they need bodies for."

Astrid felt something settle in her stomach, cold and smooth as a stone. "Taking them whether they want to go?"

"Whether they've got a choice, you mean? No such animal on this island." Tomás tapped the windowsill. "But here's the part. The carrier says the lists aren't being drawn up by the navy. They're being drawn up *here*. Company paper. Company hands. Our names get written down in our own tollhouse, practically, and then sent north like invoices."

"Invoices," Astrid repeated.

"Well, what else would they be?" Tomás spread his hands, and his grin came back, crooked and defiant, the grin he wore like armor. "We're not people to them, Astrid. We're stock. Stock gets listed. Stock gets sold." He knocked twice on the wood, superstitious, breaking his own spell. "Anyway. Rumor. Salt it heavily. But I thought you should know, since you're the one with the ledger hands."

Since you're the one with the ledger hands. She turned the phrase over as she walked back along the ramp, through the crowd, past the rooster and the squealing cart, past the gramophone man's empty spot where everyone hoped he might return and equally hoped he might not. Ledger hands. Mother said it like a compliment. Tomás said it like a warning. Both of them were right.

Because here was the thing about Astrid Almeida, the thing she told no one: numbers talked to her. Not in a mystical way — she'd seen the diver women read the water's moods and she knew better than to claim magic — but in the honest way that some people hear music and some people hear lies. A column that added true sounded like a struck bell. A column that didn't sounded like a bell with a crack in it, and she could not stop hearing the crack.

And lately — these past few months, since they'd moved her from tally-girl to copy clerk, since they'd put the master books under her ink-stained fingers — she had been hearing cracks.

Men listed as sold north who had never gone north. Men whose debts were marked *discharged* in one book and *outstanding* in another. Men who, if the rumor was true, now existed on a piece of company paper as available bodies, ready for the navy's taking, while their families believed them free.

She climbed the path toward the orchard, where her mother would be working the rows and pretending not to watch the bridge crowds, and she thought about elsewhere. Elsewhere was under her feet this morning, humming in the planks. Elsewhere was a mustache asserting itself in Colombo. Elsewhere was a war happening on a continent so far away it might have been the moon, except that the moon apparently wanted to buy divers.

At the top of the path she stopped and looked back. The bridge stretched out over the strait, loaded with people going both directions, and the water beneath it ran green and patient, keeping what it kept.

*Twice a year,* she thought. *The world opens twice a year, and the rest of the time we live in the gap.*

Then she went home to her mother's orchard, carrying the rumor the way she carried everything — lightly, carefully, and where no one could see it.

She did not yet know that within the month she would hold the proof of it in her own hands, in her own handwriting, in another man's ink. She did not know that the list had her brother's name on it already, written in a room she swept every Thursday.

All she knew was that the bridge had opened, and that for three days the island got to breathe.

She meant to enjoy it.
