## 3. Ledger Hands

The promotion came on a Tuesday, which was fitting, because Tuesdays were the day the company pretended to be generous.

"You," said Mrs. Ferreira, the head clerk's wife who functioned as the office's unofficial herald, "have been elevated." She said *elevated* the way other people said *afflicted*, but her eyes were kind. "Brandt wants you copying in the main room. Clean hand, he says. He says your figures are pretty."

Astrid had been keeping the pearl-bed tallies for two years — small work, done at a desk by the drying sheds where the smell of oyster and salt got into everything, including, eventually, her dreams. The main room was different. The main room was where the real ledgers lived: conscription rolls, debt schedules, the shipping manifests that decided who ate and who didn't, who left and who stayed.

"Pretty figures," she repeated.

"Don't let it go to your head." Mrs. Ferreira lowered her voice. "And don't let it go anywhere else either. A girl with a clean hand is useful. A girl with opinions is replaceable."

"I have no opinions," Astrid said. "I have a knife, a ledger stub, and my father's name. Opinions would be greedy."

Mrs. Ferreira laughed despite herself, then remembered she was delivering good news and composed her face into something more solemn. "Thursday you start. Wear something without oyster on it."

* * *

The main room of the Verrone Trading Company smelled of ink, camphor, and the particular staleness of paper kept too long in a humid country — a sweetness underneath, like fruit deciding whether to rot. Nine desks. Eight occupied by men who looked up when Astrid walked in and then, finding her neither interesting nor threatening, looked down again. The ninth was hers, wedged against the window where the afternoon light came in slanted and gold, catching the dust motes so they drifted like tiny pearls.

She liked it immediately. She would have liked it even if the chair hadn't been better than the one she'd had for two years. It was a swivel chair. Nobody on the island had any business owning a swivel chair. She resolved to spin in it exactly once, when no one was looking, purely for scientific purposes.

Her work was simple: copy. Brandt or one of the senior clerks would slide a ledger across and indicate pages, and she would reproduce them into fresh volumes in her best hand — the small, upright script the nuns at the mission school had drilled into her before the fees drowned along with her father. Columns of names. Columns of sums. Men sold north as contract labor; men conscripted; men whose debts were transferred, bought, forgiven, or converted from money owed into years of body owed.

It was arithmetic wearing a uniform. She had always found arithmetic restful.

For eleven days it stayed restful.

On the twelfth day, the columns stopped adding up.

* * *

It began, as these things do, not with a thunderclap but with an itch. A number that felt wrong the way a stone feels wrong inside a shoe — not painful, just present, insisting on being noticed.

The page in question listed divers transferred north under naval conscription: fourteen names, fourteen dates, fourteen signatures of receipt from a broker in Port Salina whom Astrid had never seen and could not picture. She copied the fourteenth signature, set down her pen to dip it again, and paused.

Because she knew Joaquim Reyes.

Everyone knew Joaquim Reyes. He was forty-one years old, built like a mangrove stump, and he dove the eastern beds every single morning, including this morning, because Astrid had passed him on the path at dawn and he'd complained — as he did every dawn — about the water temperature, his wife's cooking, and the government of Portugal, in ascending order of passion.

Joaquim Reyes was listed as having departed the island on the fourteenth of March.

Joaquim Reyes was currently, at this very moment, underwater, insulting the government of Portugal to a fish.

Astrid sat very still. Outside the window, a drongo made its rusty-hinge sound. Inside the window, eight men scratched their pens and thought their own thoughts.

She dipped her pen. She continued copying. Her hand did not shake, which surprised her, because everything else in her was shaking like a palm in a squall.

Perhaps, she told herself reasonably, it was an old list. Perhaps Joaquim had gone north, served, come back. But the date was March of this year — she checked twice — and besides, there were others. Rui Batista, listed as sold north in February, who had repaired her mother's roof tiles in April and charged her an amount Rosa still complained about with religious fervor. Anselmo Costa. The twin brothers Duarte, who between them possessed half the island's opinions and all of its laughter, both listed as departed, both present at Sunday market haggling over mackerel with the intensity of lawyers.

Fourteen names. She counted the ones she knew were lies and arrived at nine. Nine men sold north on paper who had never gone north at all.

Which meant somebody was being paid for nine men who still lived here, diving, breathing, complaining about the water temperature.

Or — and this was the thought that arrived uninvited and settled in her chest like a swallowed coal — which meant somebody was selling the same men twice.

* * *

That evening at supper, Tomás talked with his mouth full about the tollhouse, about a merchant who'd tried to bribe him with a hat, about the bridge crowds thinning now that dry season was aging toward its close. Rosa listened, corrected his elbows off the table three times, and asked Astrid nothing, because Rosa had a genius for knowing when asking would be worse than waiting.

But after supper, when Tomás went out to check the snares he wasn't supposed to have, Rosa sat across from her daughter and peeled a mango in one unbroken spiral, which was her way of thinking.

"New work suits you," Rosa said finally.

"It's only copying."

"Everything is only something until it isn't." The peel came away whole, a long yellow ribbon. "Your father used to say that about pearls. Only a stone until it isn't."

"Did he say anything else? About the company?"

Rosa's knife paused for perhaps half a second. Half a second is nothing, unless you're watching for it, and Astrid was watching for it the way she watched columns — automatically, helplessly, trained by years of ledgers to notice what didn't move right.

"He said the water keeps things," Rosa said. "He said people think drowning is the worst thing the sea does, but it isn't. The worst thing is how much it remembers for you." She laid the peeled mango on a leaf, cut a slice, offered it. "Eat. You've gone thin from sitting at a desk. Paper is heavier than oysters. No one warns you."

Astrid took the slice. It was sweet and cold and perfect, and she ate it while looking at her mother, and her mother met her eyes with the flat, calm expression of a woman who had buried a husband and raised two children on an island owned by a trading company and had run out of expressions to waste on ordinary occasions.

"Mother. If a man was on a list as gone, and he wasn't gone—"

"Then someone wrote his name where it shouldn't be." Rosa gathered the peel into her palm. "And a name written wrong is a dangerous thing to know about. More dangerous than a knife. Knives only cut the person in front of you." She stood, kissed the top of Astrid's head — she had to reach up slightly now, which pleased them both and neither mentioned — and carried the peel out to the compost.

Astrid sat alone with the lamp and understood that her mother had answered the question without answering it, and that this too was inheritance.

* * *

Thursday was the day she swept Brandt's office. She had swept it every Thursday since she was fourteen, back when she was small enough to be beneath notice entirely, and she had never once looked at anything except the floor, because the floor was what she was paid — technically, in the currency of reduced debt — to look at.

This Thursday she looked at the desk.

Not obviously. Obviously was death. She swept, and she moved the inkstand to sweep beneath it, and she noted — the way she noted everything now, the way the coal in her chest had turned her whole self into a ledger — that the second drawer locked. That the key lived on the ring at Brandt's belt, the heavy ring he jingled absently when he counted. That the duplicate shipping manifests, the ones countersigned by the Port Salina broker, were filed not in the general cabinet where she copied from but in that drawer, because she had once spent an entire afternoon hunting a page Brandt finally retrieved himself, from his belt, with a little flourish, like a magician.

Nine men sold north who never went north. And the proof filed in a locked drawer worn against a fat man's hip.

She finished sweeping. She emptied the dustpan. She thanked the universe, quietly and without irony, that Brandt was the sort of man who believed servants were furniture, because furniture doesn't count.

* * *

The copying itself took three more weeks. That was the terrible patience of it. She couldn't rush; rushing was a signature, and signatures got read. So she waited for the pages to come to her, sliding across the desks of senior clerks like slow fish, and when the conscription roll came — the master roll, the one from which copies were made — she copied it in her pretty hand, all forty-six names, and her pen never trembled, and her face never changed, and inside she was screaming in a language she didn't know she spoke.

Forty-six names. She had begun, in the evenings, a private tally. Not on paper — never on paper — but in her head, in the small vault behind her eyes where she kept debts: who owed the company what, who had paid, who never could. Now the vault held a second ledger. Names listed as departed. Names known to be present. The overlap. The gap.

Eleven men. By the time the master roll came through her hands, the count was eleven, and she suspected it would grow.

Here is what she understood, sitting at her window desk in the slanted gold light while the drongo complained and the monsoon clouds assembled themselves far out over the strait like an army deciding: knowledge was a weight. Everyone said knowledge was power, but everyone who said that had never held any. Knowledge was weight. It pressed on the lungs. It made the swivel chair feel like a dock piling. And the only way to make a weight bearable was to divide it — to write it down, to give part of it away, to make it carryable.

She was, after all, a girl who measured her world in carryable things.

* * *

She chose a Thursday. Brandt was on the western beds inspecting the new rafts; the senior clerks took their midday meal together at the tavern, a ritual as fixed as tide; the main room emptied the way it always did between noon and one, and she was the only person on the island who noticed that the empty hour existed, because she was the only person who used it — to eat her rice, to spin the chair once (scientific purposes), to stand at the window and watch the water.

This Thursday she did not eat. She took a sheet of the cheap grey wrapping paper from the supply shelf — the kind used to bundle oyster shipments, the kind no one counts — folded it to the size of her palm, and copied.

Nine names first. Then, working from memory, sharpened by three weeks of evening rehearsal behind closed eyelids, the dates, the broker's name, the pattern of the sums — because it wasn't the names alone, it was the arithmetic around the names, the way each phantom departure generated a payment that appeared nowhere in the company's own revenue columns. Sold north, paid by navy. Sold again, paid by labor broker. One man, two prices, zero departures. The mathematics of it was almost beautiful, if you could divorce it from the men, and she could not, because one of the names on her scrap of grey paper was Anselmo Costa, who had taught Tomás to swim, and another was young Felipe Duarte, who was seventeen and laughed like a door falling off its hinges.

Her handwriting on the scrap was smaller than her copying hand, cramped and quick. It didn't matter. It only had to be legible to one reader.

When she finished, she folded the scrap into eighths, and then, because folding wasn't enough, because folded things can be unfolded, she sewed it — three stitches, coarse thread, the kind of repair any girl's clothing might need — into the hem of her skirt, at the side seam, where the fabric already hung heavy.

Then she swept up the thread clippings, ate her rice standing at the window, spun the chair once, and went back to work.

* * *

Nothing happened for six days, which was somehow worse than if something had happened. She caught herself watching Brandt — the jingle of keys, the small flourishes, the way he licked his thumb before turning pages, a habit so gross it circled back to fascinating — and forced herself to stop, because watching was also a signature, and she had learned to sign nothing.

On the seventh day, Tomás came home late with salt in his hair and something folded tight in his fist, and he was grinning the grin he grinned when the world had handed him a joke with teeth in it.

"You'll want to hear this," he said.

Rosa looked up from her mending. Astrid felt the coal in her chest shift, the way coals do just before the fire takes.

"A boat came through at dusk," Tomás said. "Military courier, going north. And the tollhouse order that came with it—" he flattened the paper against the table, though he didn't actually show it, just pressed his palm flat on the wood as if the news needed pinning — "says the bridge closes early this year. War schedule. A month early, maybe more."

The lamp flame leaned in the draft from the door. Somewhere out in the dark, the orchard leaves moved, and moved, and did not stop moving.

"And there's more," Tomás said, and his grin faded into something else, something younger. "The courier was talking at the tavern. Loose talk, drunk talk. He said the company here has been selling names north. Conscription lists." He looked at Astrid, and then at Rosa, and then back at Astrid, and his voice dropped. "He said somebody on this island has been selling the same men twice."

The lamp leaned. The orchard breathed. Astrid sat very still with eleven names sewn into her hem and her face arranged into polite, sisterly surprise, and understood — with the cold, clear pleasure of a column finally balancing — that the water was not the only thing on this island that keeps.

She had beaten the rumor to her own kitchen by seven days.

She said, "How awful," and reached for the mending basket, and began, stitch by stitch, to plan.
