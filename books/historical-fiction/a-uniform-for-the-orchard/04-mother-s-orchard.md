## 4. Mother's Orchard

Rosa Almeida had learned to count in secrets the way other women counted in coins, and she had learned it young enough that she could no longer remember which came first — the hiding or the wanting.

The orchard began where the compound's gravel ended: forty mango trees, a dozen guava, two sad jackfruit that produced more ambition than fruit, and a row of limes along the wall that Brandt liked to point at when company men visited, as though a row of limes were proof of his generosity. *Look what grows here,* he'd say. *Look how we provide.* Rosa had stood behind him on six such occasions with her pruning knife in her apron pocket and had thought, each time, with great calm: *I planted those. You bought them from a broker who stole them from a widow in the south. But yes. Look.*

She worked the orchard alone now. In the old days there had been three of them — herself, old Marta who talked to the trees as if they were slow children, and a boy named Ferro who was afraid of bees and therefore useless in guava season. Then Marta's knees gave out and Ferro was listed north, and after Ferro went, the company decided one woman could manage forty-two trees if she managed them humbly enough.

Rosa managed them humbly. She also managed something else.

It had started the first dry season after her husband drowned, when grief made her reckless and hunger made her practical. She had buried a clay pot of rice beneath the roots of the oldest mango — the big one at the orchard's heart, the one whose branches threw shade wide enough for a family and its arguments both. A pot of rice was nothing. A pot of rice was a woman being foolish. But the foolishness felt so good in her hands, pressing the earth down flat over it, sweeping leaves across the scar of disturbed soil, that the next month she did it again, and the month after that, until there were four pots under four trees and Rosa understood that she had built herself a small country underground, population one, laws: *never dig the same tree twice in one season; never go at noon; never let your shadow fall where your hands have been.*

The rice wasn't for her. That was the strange part she never said aloud, not even to the trees, and she talked to the trees plenty. The rice was insurance against a day she refused to imagine too precisely — the day the company took back its small mercies, or the day the rains failed, or the day a warrant came with a name on it. On that day, whoever was left standing would need to eat while they ran. Rice keeps. Rice forgives you for forgetting about it. Rice waits under the roots of a mango tree with more patience than any saint Rosa had ever been introduced to.

This morning she dug up the pot beneath the third guava — rotation mattered; even a secret needs variety or it becomes a habit, and habits get noticed — and checked it. Dry. Sealed tight. Good. She added a handful from her apron, taken grain by grain over weeks, a theft so slow and scattered that no ledger in the world could catch it. Somewhere Astrid would have appreciated the mathematics of it. Her daughter thought in columns. Rosa thought in handfuls. Between them they covered most of life.

She pressed the earth back down and sat on her heels a moment in the shade, listening. The island's sounds sorted themselves for her the way beads sort by color: the pearl beds' far-off clatter of shell and shout; the compound bell marking the hour nobody free needed marked; and closer, under everything, the sea doing its long dull arithmetic against the shore. Her husband used to say the sea kept better books than the company. He'd said it laughing. He had not known, then, how true a thing he'd said, or what it would cost him to be right.

Rosa did not think about her husband before noon. This was a rule like the others — same tree twice, no digging at noon, no shadow over fresh earth. Grief was a crop too, and it rotted if you forced it early in the day.

* * *

At half past ten she carried a basket of windfall mangoes toward the clerks' quarters, because the overseer's cook expected them and because the walk took her past the schoolroom window, and because a woman was allowed to walk slowly past a window if her arms were full of fruit.

Astrid sat at the second desk from the left. She always sat at the second desk from the left, and Rosa knew this the way she knew the weight of rain coming off the water — without needing to see it. Through the glass her daughter bent over a page, pen moving, spine straight, lips moving faintly around numbers the way other girls moved them around songs. Sister Beatriz stood at the board explaining interest, of all the cruel jokes, teaching an indentured girl the mathematics of debt as though debt were neutral weather and not a rope with the company's name braided into it.

But Astrid loved it. That was the thing that undid Rosa every single time. The child — sixteen now, though the word *child* stuck in Rosa's throat — loved the ledgers the way Tomás loved the bridge crowds, hungrily, shamelessly, leaning into them as into a strong current. When Astrid came home at night she recited sums under her breath like prayers, and once, half-asleep, she had murmured *"the column doesn't balance"* with an expression of such pure distress that Rosa had laughed until she cried, and then simply cried, quietly, into her hands, where the dark could keep it.

Here is the bargain, and Rosa held it the way she held the pruning knife — firmly, by the handle, knowing what the blade end could do.

Three years ago, when the company proposed taking Astrid full-time into the counting house and letting her schooling lapse, Rosa had gone to Brandt's office. She had stood on the worn square of floor before his desk with her hands folded and her voice level, and she had said: *My daughter counts better than your clerk Duarte, who cannot find Tuesday in a calendar. Educate her properly and I will keep my eyes down and my mouth shut, and I will keep the orchard producing, and neither I nor mine will ever ask you a question about your business.*

Brandt had looked at her for a long moment with those pale, unhurried eyes, and Rosa had understood that she was being weighed — not as a person but as a risk, the way he weighed everything. Then he had smiled the smile he used on visitors, the one that showed teeth but never warmth, and said, "Educated hands make expensive mistakes, Senhora Almeida."

"They also keep accurate books," Rosa said. "You seem a man who likes accurate books."

He had liked that. God forgive her, she had found the exact coin this man valued and paid him in it, and the bargain had held for three years: silence for schooling, obedience for opportunity, her eyes down for Astrid's eyes open. And every month the arrangement cost her something new she hadn't priced in advance. Last year it was pretending not to hear when the overseers joked about the divers' rations. This year it was turning her face away from the road when men were marched past toward the boats, men she recognized, men whose mothers brought her guavas. Silence, she had learned, is not a single payment. It is rent, due forever, and the landlord raises it whenever he pleases.

She watched the schoolroom window a moment longer. Astrid looked up — some instinct, some daughter-sense — and their eyes met through the glass. Rosa lifted the basket slightly. Astrid's mouth twitched, the smallest smile, there and gone, a signal between conspirators: *I see you. Keep walking. Nothing happened here.*

Nothing ever happened here. That was the whole architecture of their safety, and Rosa had built it plank by plank, and some nights she lay awake admiring it and some nights she lay awake wondering whether a fortress made of looking away deserved the name.

* * *

In the afternoon the heat came down like a lid and Rosa retreated to the tool shed to sharpen blades, which was honest work and good cover, since a woman sharpening knives hears everything and sees nothing. Sure enough, within the hour, voices drifted over from the path between the drying sheds — Brandt and the naval man who visited monthly, Lieutenant Corvera, a thin man with a thin voice who smelled of the mainland and its importance.

"—the draft rolls again," Corvera was saying. "Headquarters wants the final tally before the bridge closes. Names, ages, fitness. The full accounting."

"You'll have it," Brandt said comfortably. "My clerk produces clean copies."

"Duarte?"

"The girl." A pause Rosa could feel like weather. "Almeida's daughter. She doesn't make errors. It's almost unsettling."

Corvera laughed, a sound like paper tearing. "And the families? Any trouble when the lists go up? There's talk on the mainland about islands hiding men. Deserters swimming ashore. The navy pays well for—"

"This island hides nothing," Brandt said, "because this island owns everything. A man who owes me his boat, his bed, and next year's wages does not hide a deserter in it. Fear is a better watchman than any sentry, Lieutenant, and I employ it at half the cost."

Rosa's whetstone moved in slow circles. *Stone, stone, stone.* Her face arranged itself into the mild blankness of a woman thinking about blades.

Deserters. Lists. Final tallies before the bridge closed. Each word went into her apron beside the pruning knife, to be laid out later on the table of her mind and examined.

Because here was the other part of the bargain, the part she had never spoken to anyone, the part that lived deeper than the rice pots: Rosa Almeida had not always had her eyes down. There had been a time — she measured it now in tree-rings, seven seasons ago — when her husband had come home from the tollhouse white as cut shell, saying he'd seen a page he shouldn't have, saying names were written twice, saying *Rosa, if anything happens to me, look at the lists, don't mourn me, look at the lists.* And then the sea had taken him, gently, conveniently, in sight of forty witnesses none of whom saw anything, and Rosa had gone to the company office to inquire after his effects and had been received with such smooth, warm courtesy that she understood, in her bones, in the place where mothers understand things, exactly what the courtesy was for.

So she had made her counter-bargain, alone, in the dark, with no one's signature: *They think I'm blind. Let them keep thinking it. Blindness will keep my children fed and my daughter in a schoolroom, and one day — one day when the water gives something back — the blindness ends.*

She didn't know yet what form the ending would take. She only knew that lately the air had changed, the way it changes before the monsoon comes out of season. Brandt's voice had a new silkiness in it. The naval man visited twice this month instead of once. And yesterday, Tomás had come home from the tollhouse wearing the particular grin he wore when he was carrying a joke too heavy to put down.

The whetstone whispered. Out in the rows, the mangoes hung heavy and patient, and beneath them, in the cool dark, four pots of rice waited with a patience older than fear.

Rosa finished the last blade, wrapped it, and stepped out into the light — a broad, sun-browned woman with dirt on her knees and nothing in her face at all, which was, if she said so herself, the finest uniform a mother could wear.
