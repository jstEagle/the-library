## 5. The Man Who Fell From the Boat

The tide gave up its secrets grudgingly that morning, and I was there to receive them.

Low water at the beds meant the oyster racks stood exposed like a congregation of drowned men, and the divers' morning tally had to be taken before Brandt's assistant came down with his slate and his sneer. I knelt on the mudflat with my counting board balanced on my knees, entering weights into the stub of ledger I kept tied to my wrist — a habit Father taught me before the sea took him: *write it small, write it twice, keep one where no one thinks to look.* He meant it for pearl counts. He could not have known how useful the habit would become.

Forty-one racks. Eleven hundred and six shells, give or take the ones the crabs had argued over. The divers worked the shallows in their loose lines — old Mateo who sang to the oysters as though they might be shamed into opening; the twins, Inês and Joana, who dove in tandem so that neither could be accused of slacking; young Ruy, whose mother had sold three years of his labor to cover a debt of eleven rupees that had somehow grown to forty.

I wrote their names. That was my work, mostly, now — names and numbers, names and numbers. The Verrone Trading Company did not think of these people as people. It thought of them as entries, and I had learned to think of them as entries too, because an entry can be copied, carried, hidden, or — if you were brave enough, and I was still deciding — altered.

The mud sucked at my knees. A crab the size of my thumbnail waved its claw at me with what I chose to interpret as solidarity.

That was when Inês started shouting.

She was standing hip-deep at the edge of the channel, pointing at something dark rolling in the current — a shape that came around the bend of the channel with the particular boneless helplessness of a thing that has stopped caring whether it lives. At first I took it for driftwood. Then it turned over, and driftwood does not have arms, and driftwood certainly does not have a face the color of spent lime.

"He's breathing," Inês called, when we'd all waded out and hauled him onto the flat. "Look — he's breathing. Barely."

He was perhaps twenty-five, maybe older — hard to tell with a face that bloated and split. Salt crusted his lips white. His shirt had once been a uniform; I could tell by the buttons, even stripped of insignia, even rotted at the seams. There was a stain along his left side that was not salt and not seawater and not going to wash out.

"Naval," said Mateo, crouched beside him, prodding the buttons with the delicacy he usually reserved for pearls. "Or he robbed a naval man. Either way, not our trouble."

"Then why are you kneeling next to him?" asked Joana.

Mateo had no answer for that, which is how most moral questions die on our island — quietly, in the presence of witnesses.

I should explain what a deserter means here, because on the mainland they have speeches about honor and duty, grand words that fit nicely in newspapers. On our island a deserter is simpler. A deserter is a man the boats will hang if found, which makes him a danger to everyone who has ever looked at him, which makes him, by the arithmetic the company prefers, already dead. And anyone who touches him is dead too, just on a slightly delayed schedule.

So of course we stood around him in a circle, eleven people in the rain-light, doing nothing, magnificently.

"We report him," said Ruy, finally, because someone always says it. "Brandt pays a bounty for runaways. Two rupees."

"Two rupees," repeated Inês. "You'd sell a man's life for two rupees."

"Three, if he's got teeth." Ruy shrugged. "It's two rupees more than I've seen since Christmas."

And there it was — the whole island in one exchange. Not cruelty. Arithmetic. Everyone on this mudflat owed the company money, and everyone knew exactly how many rupees stood between their family and the next bad season, and two rupees was not nothing. It was never nothing. That was how the company held us: not with chains but with sums.

I looked down at the man. His chest rose in shallow, uneven jerks, like a bellows with a split seam. His eyes were half-open, fixed on the gray sky, and I want to say he saw nothing in it, but I am a ledger-keeper and I notice things, and what I noticed was this: his lips were moving. Very faintly. Around a word.

I leaned close enough to smell brine and blood.

"Please," he was saying. Or possibly "priest." With drowned men the vocabulary runs narrow.

Now. Here is the part of the story where I am supposed to tell you I felt the hand of destiny on my shoulder, some great turning of fate, the moment everything changed. What actually happened was smaller and stupider. I looked at his face, and his face reminded me — absurdly, unforgivably — of a boy who had been in my father's diving crew, a boy called Anselmo who used to bring me sea-glass and pretend it was treasure, and who had been listed in the ledgers as *sold north* two years ago, though I had watched the north-bound boat leave empty of any diver named Anselmo. I had written the entry myself. My own hand. *Anselmo Ferreira, transferred, per order of Overseer Brandt.*

The company had sold a living boy on paper and pocketed him twice, and I had inked the lie without knowing it was a lie, and this dying stranger's face wore the same stupid hopeful expression Anselmo used to wear when he offered me green glass instead of blue because he couldn't tell the difference.

Fear said: walk away. Fear said, quite reasonably, *Astrid Almeida, you are sixteen years old, you weigh less than a sack of rice, your entire worldly power consists of a knife you use for trimming ledger pages and a memory your mother calls a curse.* Fear had an excellent case. Fear had won every previous argument of my life.

But instinct — that other voice, the one that sounds suspiciously like my father saying *keep one copy where no one thinks to look* — instinct put its hand flat on the ledger stub tied to my wrist and said nothing at all, which was somehow louder.

"Ruy," I said, standing, brushing mud from my skirt. "You're needed at the drying sheds. Mateo sent for you an hour ago."

"I wasn't—"

"Mateo. Drying sheds. Now." I turned to the twins. "Inês, Joana — the morning tally needs carrying up to the storehouse. Both of you. It's heavy."

It was not heavy. It was a slip of paper. But the twins exchanged a glance — the twin glance, the one that conducts entire parliamentary sessions in half a second — and Joana said, "Heavy indeed," and they went. Because that is the other way the island survives: everyone pretending not to see, together, in formation.

Which left me alone on a mudflat with a drowning soldier and a decision.

* * *

Tomás found us an hour later, and I will record honestly that his first words were not heroic.

"You've killed me," he said. "You've actually killed me. I come down to check the tollhouse stores and my sister is sitting in the open like a church statue, next to a naval corpse, in front of God and the tide schedule."

"He's not a corpse."

"He's a corpse with opinions. Look, he's trying to talk." Tomás crouched, studied the man with the frank appraisal he usually gave smuggled goods — weight, condition, likelihood of being seized at the bridge. "Uniform's been stripped. Buttons say navy. Wound's a blade, low on the ribs, gone bad. He didn't fall off a patrol boat, Astrid. Somebody put him off one."

"Then somebody wanted him dead, and the sea disagreed."

"The sea agrees with nobody. The sea just delivers." Tomás rubbed his jaw, and I watched my brother — seventeen, quick-tongued, keeper of the tollhouse and its steady trickle of contraband letters — do the same arithmetic Ruy had done, and arrive at a different total. Not because Tomás was braver than Ruy. Because Tomás had spent two years carrying other families' secrets through a checkpoint, and a man's life weighed differently once you'd gotten used to the feel of it in your pockets.

"If Brandt hears a breath of this," Tomás said, "we're finished. All of us. Mother included."

"Mother included," I agreed. "So we don't let him hear a breath."

"That's your plan? *We don't*?"

"It's a beginning. Plans grow."

Tomás laughed — the short, disbelieving laugh that meant he had already lost the argument and knew it. Then he bent, hooked the man's arm over his shoulders, and hauled him upright, and the man groaned in a register I felt in my back teeth.

"Help me get him to the mangrove path," Tomás grunted. "There's a fisherman's hut past the second bend — old Duarte's, before he ran off to the mainland and abandoned it to the rats. Rats won't talk."

"And after the hut?"

"After the hut," said my brother, staggering under his burden, "after the hut is a problem for a version of us that isn't currently carrying a bleeding sailor through the company's own pearl concession. One catastrophe at a time. It's how I stay so calm."

"You are the least calm person I know."

"Calmly catastrophizing," he corrected. "Walk."

* * *

Old Duarte's hut smelled of rat and rusted fish hooks, but the roof held, and the floorboards were sound, and there was even a hammock sling strung between two posts that had outlived its owner's desertion of it. We laid the man down. Rosa arrived twenty minutes later with a basket she had told the compound cook was for collecting crabs, and inside the basket were boiled rags, a clay jar of the bitter green salve she brewed from orchard leaves, a needle, thread, and a bottle of cane spirit whose provenance I elected not to investigate.

My mother knelt beside the stranger, cut his ruined shirt away with three efficient strokes, and looked at the wound for a long moment without touching it. Her face did the thing her face does — went smooth and closed, like a shuttered window.

"Fever's set," she said. "The flesh is angry. If the poison reaches the blood proper, no rag in this basket matters."

"Can you draw it?" I asked.

"I can try. Trying costs needle and thread and spirit. Failing costs the same." She uncorked the spirit bottle, poured a measure into the wound itself, and the man woke up entirely — arched, gasped, swore in a language that was definitely not ours and possibly not anyone's.

"There he is," said Tomás cheerfully. "Good evening to you too."

The man's eyes found each of us in turn — my mother, my brother, me — and settled somewhere in the middle distance, on whatever vision of hanging or firing squad he was composing for us. "You shouldn't—" he managed. His voice was gravel soaked in seawater. "Report me. You should report me. They'll — the bounty—"

"Two rupees," said Tomás. "Three with teeth. You'd best hope yours survive the fever, friend, because you're currently worth more intact."

The man almost smiled. It cost him enormously. Then Rosa's hands went back to work and he stopped smiling and started making a sound like a rope under strain, and my mother murmured to him in Portuguese, the soft nonsense women murmur — *there now, breathe, the worst is the worst, it cannot get worse* — while I held his shoulders down and thought about ledgers.

Because here was my problem, and I turned it over during all that noise the way you turn a shell looking for the pearl inside.

Every person who comes onto this island comes onto it in the books. That is the first law of the Verrone Trading Company, older than the bridge, older than the orchard: *nothing enters that is not entered.* Fishermen log their catch. Boats log their cargo. Indentured workers log their hours and their debts and their children, because children are debts too, waiting to ripen. There is a daily ledger at the beds, and a weekly summary, and both pass across my desk, and at week's end both go up to Brandt, who reads them the way a spider reads a web.

Somewhere in those pages, tomorrow or the day after, would go a line recording the morning's events at the beds. And the divers would be asked — casually, over the weighing scales — whether anything unusual washed in with the tide. And eleven people would have to lie in unison, and eleven people lying in unison is a choir, and choirs are audible.

Unless the line they read made the question unnecessary. Unless the ledger itself said: *nothing of note.*

I could write it. I was the copy clerk. My hand was trusted; my columns balanced; my figures had never once been challenged. A single omission, a single blank space where a truth should sit — the smallest crime in the world, invisible unless you already knew to look for it. That was the trap of it. To falsify the ledger well, you had to understand that a lie's power lies in what it leaves out.

And if I was caught — and fear, which had lost its argument on the mudflat, kept filing appeals — if I was caught, it would not be two rupees of trouble. It would be my indenture voided into something worse. It would be questions asked about my brother's tollhouse. It would be Mother's schooling bargain torn up and every year of her silence spent for nothing.

The man on the hammock groaned. Rosa stitched. Tomás paced the width of the hut, four steps and turn, four steps and turn, wearing a groove into Duarte's floorboards on behalf of all of us.

"Astrid." He stopped pacing. "Whatever you're calculating over there, stop it. You have your thinking face. Your thinking face gets people arrested."

"Somebody has to enter today in the beds ledger," I said. "And somebody has to decide what today contained."

"And what did it contain?"

I looked at the stranger. Sweat had broken across his forehead; the fever was climbing exactly as my mother had said it would, and underneath the bloat and the salt his face was ordinary — a workingman's face, a face built for weather. His lips moved again, that same word or another one, and this time I caught it clearly.

"*Not yet,*" he whispered. To the ceiling. To the fever. To whoever keeps the final ledger of a man's days. "*Not yet.*"

Everyone on this island measures themselves against the water. Father went into it and stayed. Anselmo went onto a boat that sailed light. This man had been thrown in and had refused — had swum, had crawled, had clung to a channel current he couldn't possibly have survived sober, all on the strength of two words and a stubbornness I recognized, because I kept the same stubbornness in the same place, folded small behind the true figures.

"No," I said aloud. Everyone looked at me. "Today contained nothing. Rain, a poor haul on the eastern racks, and a crab bite on Ruy's ankle. I'll write it myself tonight."

Tomás exhaled slowly through his nose. "You're sure."

"I'm sure that the company taught me bookkeeping and never once asked what I'd use it for." I untied the ledger stub from my wrist, checked the day's column, and found it clean — a page with room left in it for the shape of a silence. "One line missing, Tomás. That's all a lie ever is. One line missing, written neatly."

Rosa looked up from her stitching then, and for a moment her eyes met mine over the wounded stranger's body, and something passed between us that I didn't yet have a name for — something that felt less like approval than recognition, as if she were seeing a door open in a wall she had long ago wallpapered over.

"Go home," she said quietly. "Both of you. Before the evening count. I'll stay till the fever breaks or doesn't. I collect crabs until dark."

"Mother—"

"*Crabs,*" she said, in the tone that ends discussions, and Tomás and I went.

* * *

We walked home along the mangrove path with the light going amber and the tide coming in behind us to erase our footprints, which felt, under the circumstances, like the sea taking notes.

"You know what you've done," Tomás said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I've omitted a line."

"You've committed a crime against the Verrone Trading Company, in writing, in your own hand, in a document that goes to Brandt's desk every seventh day." He kicked a fallen mangrove pod into the water. "Congratulations. You're a criminal now. We'll get you a badge. It'll say *balanced to the cent.*"

"Does it frighten you?" I asked. "What I did?"

He was quiet for ten steps. Fifteen. Long enough that I counted.

"Yes," he said finally. "But I've been frightened since I was fourteen and it turns out it's not a stopping place, it's just weather. You dress for it and keep walking." He glanced sideways at me. "What I want to know is what he said to you. Down on the flats. Before I got there. You looked at him like — like he was a figure that wouldn't add up."

I hadn't told him about Anselmo. I filed that away, one more entry in the private ledger I was only beginning to understand I kept.

"He said please," I said.

Tomás nodded slowly, accepting this, the way he accepted most things — provisionally, with an eye toward resale value. Ahead of us the compound lamps were being lit, and somewhere up the slope the supper bell rang, and the ordinary evening of the island assembled itself around us, oblivious, orderly, and — as of one blank space in one column — built on a crack.

At the compound gate a company runner was waiting, holding a sealed paper. "For the clerk Almeida," he said. "From the overseer's office. He wants the weekly summaries early this month. Tomorrow morning, first light."

Tomás and I looked at each other.

First light. Which meant tonight. Which meant the line I had promised to leave blank would be written — or unwritten — under Brandt's reading eye within twelve hours, before I'd even learned whether the man in the hut would live to make my crime worth committing.

"Well," said Tomás, as I took the paper. "One catastrophe at a time."
