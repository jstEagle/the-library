## 6. Rain Out of Season

The fever came with the rain.

I had been on the island four days when it found me — four days in a room above the drying sheds that smelled of salt fish and turmeric, listening to rain fall where rain had no business falling yet. The dry season was supposed to hold until the bridge opened. Instead the sky had split open like a rotten sack, and I lay there under a roof that was not mine, in a house belonging to people who should have turned me over to Brandt's men by now, and I understood that I had brought the weather with me. Bad luck travels. It rides in the blood like a stowaway.

"You're burning," said the girl.

Astrid Almeida stood in the doorway with a basin of water and an expression I would learn to know well: the look of someone doing arithmetic against her will. She was sixteen and carried herself like the ledgers she copied, every line ruled straight, nothing wasted. She set the basin down beside me with the care of someone who resents caring.

"I'm fine," I said.

"You are lying in my mother's good bed, sweating through her good sheets, and your leg is the color of a storm cloud. You are the opposite of fine." She dipped the cloth, wrung it, laid it across my forehead before I could object. Her hands were quick and impersonal, a clerk's hands. "My mother says you're to drink this." A cup appeared. It smelled like something that had died fighting something else.

"What is it?"

"Medicine."

"Made of what?"

"Things that were alive this morning." She almost smiled. Almost. "Drink it, or I'll write in my ledger that you did, and then it will be true whether you drank it or not."

I drank it. It tasted the way a harbor smells at low tide. Somewhere below us, the pearl beds hissed under the unseasonal rain, and I thought about how far I had run from a war everyone on this island seemed determined to pretend was happening somewhere else, on some other planet, to other people.

* * *

Her name was Rosa, the mother. She came up the stairs in the evenings after the orchard work was done, and she treated me the way she must have treated trees — firmly, without sentiment, but with a deep and unsentimental tenderness underneath. She washed the wound in my thigh with boiled water and a brown soap that stung like honesty. She packed it with a poultice of crushed leaves whose name she refused to tell me, on the grounds that I would only worry about whether they were poisonous.

"They're not," she said.

"How do you know?"

"Because if they were, I would be rid of you by morning, and I'd have my bed back."

She said it dryly, without looking up from her work, and I laughed, and the laughing hurt, which she took as evidence of progress.

But at night the fever talked, and the fever told the truth. I dreamed of the transport boat, the one I'd jumped from, the rail cold under my hands, the water black as ink below. I dreamed of Sergeant Voss's face when he saw me go — not angry, worse than angry, *understanding*, the way you understand a man who steps off a cliff because standing still has become unbearable. And I dreamed of the others. The divers. The names.

It was the names that made me sick worse than the wound.

On the third night of the fever I woke shouting them. I didn't know I was shouting until Astrid was in the room holding a lamp, and Rosa behind her with a rifle — an old fowling piece, held like she knew what to do with it, which frightened me more than anything the war had shown me so far.

"The lists," I said. My mouth was full of cotton and salt. "They're selling the names twice."

Rosa lowered the rifle slowly. She looked at me for a long moment, and then at her daughter, and something passed between them that I could not read and would spend many nights trying to.

"Sleep," Rosa said. "Tomorrow you'll tell us properly. Tomorrow you'll tell it once, all the way through, and we'll decide what it costs us to have heard it."

* * *

So the next day, propped against the wall with my leg out straight like a compass needle pointing at trouble, I told them.

I told them I had been a paymaster's clerk with the coastal garrison, third class, a man who handled paper the way Astrid did — except that the paper I handled was war. Requisitions. Muster rolls. Conscription schedules. I had thought myself a small man in a large machine, a gear too little to matter, and I had been comfortable in that thinking the way a man is comfortable in wet boots because the alternative is admitting how long he's been standing in the water.

"And then?" Astrid sat across from me with a slate on her knees. She wrote things down as I spoke. Not for me. For herself. I recognized the habit; it was mine too, once. Writing things down is how you convince yourself you might someday do something about them.

"Then I was transferred to help audit the labor levies." I looked at my hands. They were still a clerk's hands, soft at the fingertips, though the last weeks had begun to roughen them. "The Verrone Company supplies divers to the naval stations. Pearl men, strong swimmers, men already bound by debt. Every season Brandt sends north a conscription schedule — so many names, so many bodies, requisitioned against the company's contracts. The navy pays a head fee. You've seen the boats take them?"

"We've seen," Rosa said quietly.

"Well. The navy pays per name delivered. And the labor brokers inland pay per name *not* delivered — for the same names. Men who can pass a physical and disappear into a plantation or a quarry, their papers sold clean, their debts transferred. Brandt sells each man twice. Once to the navy as a body, once to a broker as a blank. The navy gets a name on a roll that never has to be filled by the man himself — some poor devil further down the ledger goes instead, or the berth is marked 'lost at sea,' which the navy accepts because accepting it is cheaper than investigating it. And the broker gets a healthy diver who owes him everything and can never complain to anyone, because on paper that man drowned two seasons ago."

The rain ticked against the shutters. In the yard, a chicken complained about the weather with real feeling.

"That's not possible," Astrid said. But she said it the way I had once said it, in the audit office, running the columns a fourth time, a fifth time, hoping the numbers would behave. "The ledgers would show—"

"The ledgers show exactly what they're meant to show. That's the whole art of it. I audited three seasons of Verrone shipping manifests before I saw it, and I only saw it because a broker's clerk got drunk in Colombo and boasted. Forty-one names, over two years. Maybe more now. Men written dead who are digging coal. Men written shipped who never left the island." I swallowed. My throat felt full of the sea I'd crossed. "And the ones the navy does take — the real ones — their families are told the debt dies with them. Except it doesn't. The debt transfers. To widows. To children. To anyone the company decides inherited it. Your father—"

I stopped. Too late. The name was already in the room, the way names are, filling it.

Rosa had gone very still. Astrid's slate pencil had stopped moving.

"Drowned men don't transfer debts," Astrid said at last, very evenly. "That's the law."

"The law is a ledger," I said. "And somebody has to hold the pen."

* * *

Rosa changed my dressing that evening and said nothing for most of it. Then, winding the last of the linen, she asked, without looking at me: "Why did you jump? If you were safe in your office with your columns adding up."

The question deserved a better answer than the one I had ready.

"A man came through the garrison," I said. "An old diver, half-deaf, escorted north for conscription. He'd been counted 'shipped' two years ago — I had the manifest myself, I'd stamped it. He was standing in front of me, alive, being shipped again, and he asked me — he leaned close, because of his ears, and he asked me if I knew what happened to his sons. Both boys, both taken in different seasons. He asked me like I was the kind of man who would know. Like I was the kind of man who would *tell* him."

"And were you?"

"I knew," I said. "I'd seen the duplicate entries. I'd told myself they were clerical errors. Errors, forty-one times, always in the same direction, always toward money." I heard my own voice go thin. "I told him the truth. All of it. There in the transit yard, with the escort waiting. And then I ran, because staying meant becoming the kind of man who knows and ships them anyway, and I discovered — late, at the rail of a boat, in the dark — that I would rather drown than be that. So I jumped. And the sea, which is generous with fools, spat me out here."

Rosa tied off the bandage and sat back on her heels. Outside, the rain went on falling out of season, drumming on the orchard leaves, drowning the dry-season dust.

"My husband found the lists first," she said.

I looked up. Astrid had gone white.

"He worked the tollhouse accounts, before Tomás. He found a column that didn't add, and he was the sort of man who couldn't sleep next to a wrong number." Her voice was flat and careful, a floor bearing weight. "He was going to carry it across the bridge. He drowned in the shallows between here and the tollhouse, in water he'd waded since he was a boy. The company called it misadventure. The company calls everything misadventure. It saves ink."

"Mother," Astrid whispered.

"Eat your supper," Rosa said, to both of us, in a voice that ended the conversation the way a stamp ends a document. But as she gathered the basin and rose to go, she paused at the door, her back to me, and said: "You'll get well, Mr. Elias. Not for your sake. A witness who dies of a bad leg is worth nothing to anyone, and I have buried enough worthless deaths in this family."

Then she was gone down the stairs, and I lay in the borrowed bed feeling the strange double weight of it — gratitude and guilt tangled together like fishing line, impossible to separate without cutting.

* * *

The fever broke on the sixth day. I woke clear-headed for the first time since the water, and the clarity was its own punishment, because a fever lets you feel sorry for yourself and health does not.

Astrid brought the morning rice and stayed to argue with me, which I think was her way of being kind.

"You should rest until the bridge opens," she said. "Dry season proper. Two months. No one crosses, no one looks for a deserter on an island that's sealed."

"No one looks except Brandt, and Brandt doesn't need the bridge to look. He needs the bridge for everything else." I sat up. The leg ached, but it ached like a living thing now instead of a dying one. "Every day I'm here is a day your family carries me. You've fed a stranger, hidden a deserter, lied in whatever books keep track of strangers — and I haven't even thanked you properly, because thanking you means saying out loud that if Brandt finds me, he doesn't just hang me. He asks why the Almeidas hid me, and he keeps asking until the asking ruins you."

"So don't be found," she said, with the terrible simplicity of the young.

"It's not enough to not be found. Listen to me." I leaned forward, and the room seemed to lean with me, the lamp, the shadows, the rain. "I'm a paymaster's clerk. I can recite those entries from memory — dates, amounts, initials, the broker's marks. I can put Brandt's signature next to forty-one names of men who are either dead on paper or alive in holes, and I can make a commission believe it. That's worth more than my hiding. That's worth everything — but only if it reaches the mainland. Only if it reaches people who aren't paid in Verrone pearls."

Astrid was quiet a moment. Then she said, in the voice of a girl reading aloud a column she wished were wrong:

"There's a rumor at the compound. Conscription schedules being redrawn. New levies. Bigger ones. Tomás heard it at the tollhouse — military order, sealed orders, the bridge closing early this year." She met my eyes. "If they're selling names twice now, and the navy wants more names…"

"Then every family on this island is inventory," I said, "and the war hasn't even come ashore yet."

The rain kept falling, patient as bookkeeping. Below us, somewhere in the orchard, Rosa moved among her trees in the grey light, tending things buried under roots, and I thought about what she had said — that her husband had found the lists first — and I understood that I hadn't washed ashore into a hiding place at all.

I had washed ashore into the middle of something that had been waiting, all these years, for the water to give it back.
