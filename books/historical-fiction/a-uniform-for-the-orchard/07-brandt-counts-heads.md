## 7. Brandt Counts Heads

The morning the tally came, my mother put salt fish on the table and said nothing at all, which was her loudest way of screaming.

I knew before she set the plate down. Rosa Almeida communicated in food the way other people communicated in words: salt fish meant danger, plain rice meant grief, and sweet cassava — rare as a dry sock in monsoon — meant that something had happened that could not be spoken of but must be survived. That morning it was salt fish, and under the fish, folded into the banana leaf like a secret, a single dried chili.

*Count your heads,* the chili said. *Someone is coming to count yours.*

"Company inspection," Tomás said through a mouthful, because Tomás had never once read the language of the table correctly. "Brandt's doing his rounds before the boats go north. Papa saw the notice nailed up at the tollhouse."

"Papa sees notices," Mother said, "the way rocks see rain." She meant it as an insult to the dead man's attention span. It was also, I noted, an instruction: tell no one who might repeat it to a rock.

I ate my fish and did my arithmetic, which by then was less a habit than a heartbeat. Our compound held thirty-one souls when counted honestly. It had held thirty-two since the night Tomás dragged a half-drowned soldier out of the pearl beds and we had all, without discussion or vote, agreed to pretend otherwise.

Elias had been with us nine days. Nine days of fever, of my mother's poultices that smelled like a swamp's apology, of him sleeping in the space behind the rice jars where the wall bowed away from its frame just enough for a thin man lying very still. Nine days of me walking past that wall each evening and listening — not for breathing, exactly, but for the absence of boots.

And now boots were coming officially.

* * *

Overseer Brandt conducted inspections the way a slow tide erodes a cliff — not violently, but with the absolute certainty that everything would eventually be his shape. He was not a cruel man in the way of men who shout. He was cruel the way ledgers are cruel: every line balanced, every loss recorded, nothing forgiven because forgiveness does not fit in a column.

He arrived at our compound at half past eight with four soldiers, a folding table, and a clerk named Pinto whose job was to hold the book and look apologetic about existing. Brandt himself carried only a small black notebook and a pencil sharpened to a point that could have drawn blood if he'd been the sort to waste lead on violence.

"Almeida," he said to my mother, consulting the notebook though he plainly knew her name the way he knew the names of his own teeth. "The orchard looks thin this year."

"The year is thin, sir."

"The year," Brandt said, "is exactly the size the company made it." He turned to me. "You're the ledger girl now."

"I keep copies, sir."

"Copies." He smiled with one corner of his mouth, the corner that did the arithmetic while the rest of the face rested. "Copies are how errors become traditions. You'll want to be careful which errors you copy."

I said yes, sir, in the tone of a girl thinking about laundry, and inside my chest something cold and bright sat up and paid attention. He knew. Or he suspected, which was worse, because suspicion could be fed and grown like a pearl around any grain of grit he chose to insert.

The counting began at the northern barracks. We could hear it across the yard — Pinto's reedy voice reading out names, soldiers' boots shifting, the occasional flat syllable of Brandt correcting a pronunciation no one had gotten wrong. They counted divers first, then dock hands, then the families. Thirty-one names on the company list. Thirty-one people who needed to be standing in the yard when called.

Thirty-two people eating from the company's rice.

I did what I always did when terror arrived: I made a list. This is a habit I recommend without reservation. Fear is a fog; a list is a knife you can cut your way out of it with. My list went:

1. Elias cannot walk yet. Fever broke yesterday; legs unreliable.
2. Drying sheds are behind the racks, smell of fish so strong even dogs complain. Soldiers will hate them. Brandt will send someone anyway because Brandt hates unexamined spaces more than he hates the smell.
3. The shed has the false floor where Father used to hide the good knives from the tax men. Father hid knives there for eleven years and died in open water, so perhaps floors are not destiny, but it is the floor we have.
4. The tally itself. Pinto holds the book. Pinto reads the name. The person answers. What makes a tally true? Not the people. The mark against the name. A mark is a small thing. Small things can be managed.

Tomás found me staring at the drying sheds with the expression, he later told me, of a girl planning a murder with geometry.

"Astrid."

"They'll count us within the hour."

"I know."

"He can't run. His legs are—"

"I know that too." Tomás chewed his thumbnail, a habit Mother had beaten out of him twice and which returned each time like a debt with interest. "So we don't hide him. Hiding is for guilty men. We make him invisible."

"Invisible how?"

"You'll figure it out. You're the one who sees the numbers." He said it lightly, the way he said everything, but underneath it was the thing he actually believed about me, which was more frightening than Brandt: my brother thought I was capable of anything, and he was cheerfully willing to find out.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about committing your first crime: it feels exactly like doing your job.

That is either a horror or a comfort depending on the day, and that morning it was a comfort, and I have never fully made peace with that.

The drying sheds were three long rooms behind the fish racks, built for salted catch, stacked with trays in various stages of becoming unbearable. The false floor ran beneath the third room's back wall — a gap two hand-widths deep, running six feet, originally designed by some clever smuggler whose name the water kept better than any stone did. Father had kept knives there. Now we lowered Elias into it with a folded sailcloth under him and a tray of the oldest, most weaponized fish laid over the boards above, and I will say this for the fever: it had taught him to be still. He looked up at me through the gap between two trays with eyes gone calm the way deep water is calm, and he mouthed one word — *go* — and I went.

Then I walked into the yard and did the second crime, which was smaller and larger.

Pinto stood at the folding table with the master list. Brandt stood behind him. Four soldiers arranged themselves in the loose semicircle of men who are paid whether or not anything happens.

"Almeida household," Pinto read. "Rosa. Tomás. Astrid."

We answered. Mother with her chin level, Tomás with a grin he aimed somewhere over Brandt's shoulder as if the horizon owed him money, me with my eyes down and my hands folded, the posture of a girl who is nowhere at all.

But here is the thing about tallies that I had learned at my father's knee and perfected at the company desk: the count happens twice. Once aloud, once in ink. And between those two counts lives a country where a girl with quick eyes and quicker fingers governs absolutely.

Pinto marked three strokes against our names. Then, because Brandt had noticed a discrepancy in the northern barracks — a diver named Corvo who had answered to two names, drunk, bless his loyal liver — the whole page had to be recopied cleanly before signing. Pinto set the book flat. Pinto turned away to deal with Corvo. Pinto left the pencil on the table beside the open page.

It lay there for eleven seconds. I know because I counted them the way I count everything, and in those eleven seconds I crossed the yard carrying the water jug I had picked up for exactly this purpose, paused at the table's edge exactly as long as a girl pausing to let an overseer pass, and added one stroke to the Almeida line.

One stroke. A hair of graphite, thinner than a diver's fingernail. The smallest lie ever told on this island, and it weighed more than the anchor chain.

"Almeida household shows four," Pinto said when he returned, squinting at the page, because ink and graphite agree with each other more readily than either agrees with memory. "Is that right?"

"My aunt," said my mother, without missing a breath, "visiting from the eastern shore. She leaves tomorrow. She is very old and does not like being counted."

Nobody likes being counted, Brandt almost said. I watched him decide not to say it, because saying it would admit that counting mattered, and admitting things was against his religion. He signed the page. Pinto blotted it. The soldiers moved on toward the orchard rows, and the fourth Almeida — old, imaginary, and currently lying under a tray of fish that could have been declared a weapon in any navy — breathed salt-smelling dark and did not exist.

* * *

That evening Brandt came to the orchard alone.

This was worse than the soldiers. Soldiers were weather; they passed over everyone equally. But Brandt walking alone between the mango rows, stopping at our tree, turning a fallen fruit over in his white fingers as though grading it — that was a man choosing where to stand, and men like Brandt only chose to stand near you when they wanted something or suspected something, and I could not tell which frightened me more.

"Your mother keeps a tidy orchard," he said.

"Yes, sir."

"She learned that from her husband, I suppose."

The fruit turned in his fingers. Somewhere behind his face, I understood suddenly, was a page I had never been allowed to see — a page with my father's name on it, entered in a column I couldn't read. The drowning had been ruled misadventure. The water keeps what the water keeps. But Brandt's voice had done something small when he said *her husband*, some settling of weight, like a ledger closing.

"He kept tidy accounts too," Brandt said. "Until he didn't."

"Sir?"

"Nothing." He set the fruit down precisely where it had lain, aligned with the row, a man who could not abide disorder even in rot. "You copy well, they tell me. Quick hands. Good eyes." He looked at me then, fully, for the first time, and his eyes were the color of ledger paper — blank, ruled, waiting for entries. "A girl like you could go far in the company. Farther than the beds. Farther than the island, even."

Every sentence had a hook in it and I could feel them all, little silver hooks, patient as fishing lines.

"I'm where my family needs me, sir."

"Yes," he said softly. "Family. That's always the difficulty, isn't it. Families need things. Boats need crews. Lists need names." He straightened his cuffs. "Good night, Almeida. Tell your mother her trees are an example to the compound."

He walked back through the rows and the dusk closed behind him like water over a stone, and I stood among the mangoes shaking — not from fear, or not only fear, but from the strange new heat that fear had cooked itself into. Because I had lied to him today. In graphite. In fish. In a fictitious aunt. And the sky had not fallen, the soldiers had not come, the world had simply continued, indifferent, and I had continued inside it, and somewhere between the eleventh second and the twelfth I had discovered that I was good at this.

Terror, I realized, walking back to the house where my mother was already laying out rice — plain rice, grief or gratitude, hard to say — terror is just competence that hasn't found its work yet.

Behind the rice jars, faintly, Elias coughed. One short bark, quickly smothered. Alive. Still ours. Still a name off every list, except the one I was building in my head, in my collar seams, in the growing dark behind my ribs.

And then Tomás came through the door at a run, sand still on his feet, tollhouse lamp oil still on his jacket, wearing the expression of a boy who has read a notice nailed to a post and wishes desperately he hadn't.

"Astrid," he said. "You need to hear this. About the bridge."
