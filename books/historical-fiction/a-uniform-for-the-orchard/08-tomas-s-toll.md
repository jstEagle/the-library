## 8. Tomás's Toll

There were three kinds of days at the tollhouse, Tomás had decided in his four years of working it, and he could tell them apart before the mist finished lifting off the strait.

There were coin days, when the bridge filled with carts and pearl-baskets and pilgrims going to the mainland shrine, and the iron box under his counter grew heavy enough that he had to carry it with both hands and a theatrical groan, so the company clerk who came to collect would think the tolls were lighter than they were. There were rain days, when nobody crossed at all and Tomás passed the hours teaching himself to whistle through his cupped hands — badly, according to everyone who had ever heard him try — and counting the planks of the bridge by sight until he lost his place, which was always somewhere in the forties.

And then there were days like this one, which began as a coin day and ended wearing the face of something else entirely.

It started well enough. The mist burned off early, the water went from pewter to that hard bright green the strait got in dry season, and by seven o'clock there was a queue: two ox carts from the coconut groves, a woman with forty chickens in woven hampers who always paid in small coins counted out one at a time like she was burying each one individually, and old Ferreira the fisherman, who had been crossing this bridge since before it had a tollhouse and considered the toll a personal insult he renewed daily.

"Morning, Ferreira."

"Hmph."

"That'll be one anna."

"Hmph." But the coin appeared between two calloused fingers, as it did every morning, as it would every morning until one of them died, and Tomás dropped it in the box and gave the old man the nod that was their whole friendship, conducted over forty years without either of them wasting a word on it.

The chickens woman took longer. She always did. Tomás leaned on his counter and watched her count, and past her shoulder he watched the bridge itself — the long timber spine of it walking out across the strait on stone piers, the only way off this island that didn't involve paying a boatman or trusting the sea. Twice a year it opened its mouth fully: dry season out, dry season back, and in between the rains came up the channel and made the current run so fast and brown that even Ferreira wouldn't cross it in a boat.

Twice a year the island breathed. Right now it was breathing out. You could feel it in the queue — everybody leaving had that loosened-shoulder look, and everybody arriving had bundles tied tight for staying.

Tomás knew the feeling from the other side of the counter. He'd been standing on this side of it for four years, taking other people's coins to other places, and the whole time a small hot coal had been sitting in his chest that never quite went out. It wasn't even a plan, most days. It was just the knowledge — the way you know there's land beyond the horizon — that somewhere on the far end of this bridge the world got bigger. Bigger than a tollhouse. Bigger than an island where a trading company held your name in a book and could look you up whenever it liked.

He thought about his sister sometimes, standing at those high windows in the counting house with her ink-stained fingers. Astrid had the same coal in her, he suspected. She just kept hers banked down under arithmetic.

* * *

The military man arrived at half past nine.

Tomás saw him first as a figure on the far span, and he knew before the man got within shouting distance, because soldiers walked bridges differently than travelers. Travelers drifted. Soldiers measured. This one walked like the planks were being requisitioned under him.

By the time he reached the tollhouse he resolved into khaki and sweat and a lieutenant's insignia gone salt-faded, with a corporal trailing him carrying a document case like it contained the man's actual spine.

"Morning!" Tomás said, cheerful, because cheerfulness was armor and he wore his daily. "Toll's one anna, but I'll waive it for the uniform if you tell me how the war's going."

"The war," the lieutenant said, "is going fine." He said it the way a man reads a signpost. He looked past Tomás into the tollhouse, at the counter, the stool, the iron box, the little stove, the shelf of ledgers, cataloguing all of it. "This is the only road crossing?"

"On this island, yes, sir. The rest is boats, and the boats are mostly Verrone Company boats, so if you're looking to move men or materiel—"

"I'm not." The lieutenant held out his hand. "Papers first. Your authorization for this post."

Tomás produced it from the drawer where he kept it folded around a stub of pencil and a dried mango skin he'd been meaning to throw away for three weeks. The lieutenant read it without expression, handed it back, and then nodded to the corporal, who opened the document case and laid a single sheet on Tomás's counter, weighting one corner with a coin from his own pocket.

"Military order," the lieutenant said. "Effective immediately. Read it aloud, please. I'm required to confirm comprehension."

Tomás picked up the sheet. It was typed, which already made it worse; things typed had been typed many times, sent many places, decided by someone who had never smelled this strait.

"'By order of the District Military Authority,'" he read, "'the causeway bridge shall close to civilian traffic effective'—" He stopped. Started again. "'Effective the fifteenth of this month.'"

The fifteenth. He did the sum without wanting to. Dry season ran another six weeks past the fifteenth. The bridge had never closed early in living memory — the rains closed it, the rains were the calendar here, older and more reliable than any government.

"That's a month early," Tomás said. "Sir. That's — the fishing families cross to market twice a week. The shrine pilgrims—"

"The bridge will be needed for military transport. Civilian crossings cease on the fifteenth. After that date the strait itself remains open only until the monsoon, at which point—" the lieutenant consulted nothing, because he had memorized it, which meant he had said it before, on other islands, to other men behind other counters — "all maritime traffic ceases by naval order. No vessels in or out. Duration of closure: until further notice."

"Until further notice," Tomás repeated.

"The last authorized civilian departure by sea will be the company steamer *Meridian*, one week from today. Thereafter any vessel attempting the strait will be fired upon." The lieutenant said this with the mildness of a man reciting train times. "Sign here to confirm receipt."

Tomás signed. His signature came out neat, which surprised him; he'd have bet money it would shake.

* * *

The rest of the day, people came to the tollhouse not to pay but to ask, and Tomás discovered what it was to be the bearer of bad news at a chokepoint, which was a job with no dignity in it at all. He told the chicken woman. He told Ferreira, who said "Hmph" in a new key, a lower one, and stood looking at the water for a long time before walking back the way he'd come. He told the priest from the mission, who crossed himself and asked whether the closure applied to sacramental travel, and Tomás said honestly that he didn't think God was exempted anywhere on the form.

And all day, underneath the announcements and the questions, the coal in his chest had stopped being a vague warmth and become a countdown.

*Meridian*. One week. Last boat.

He knew about berths the way he knew about everything that moved on this strait — it was part of the job, and also part of the smuggling, though those two parts had blurred years ago. A berth on the *Meridian* cost more than a diver earned in a season, unless you bought it the way things got bought on this island: in favors, in pooled rupees, in somebody's cousin owing somebody's brother. And there was exactly one berth left. He'd known that for ten days, actually, sitting on the knowledge like a hen on an egg, because Senhora Vasquez's nephew had taken ill and given up his passage, and the village money that had been gathering in a tin under Rosa's floor for two years — gathered against some emergency nobody could name — suddenly had a destination.

Everyone assumed the berth was his. He assumed it himself, most mornings. He was the one with the warrant — that crumpled paper with his name on it that lived folded inside his shirt next to his skin, the conscription notice he'd dodged for eight months, the reason he worked a tollhouse instead of carrying a rifle for a war he wanted no part of. If the military police ever matched the name on that paper to the face behind this counter, the hanging offense wasn't theoretical anymore. Getting off the island wasn't ambition for Tomás Almeida. It was survival with a schedule.

So he joked about it. That was his method, learned young: when the fear got big, make it ridiculous, shrink it down until it fit in your mouth. He spent the afternoon telling the queue that the *Meridian* was haunted, that its cook was a convicted poisoner reformed only by poverty, that the last man who sailed on her came back with a wife and eleven children and a limp. They laughed. He laughed. Underneath, both hands were gripping the counter edge, and neither of them let go until dark.

* * *

His mother found him after closing, which was unusual; Rosa came to the tollhouse perhaps twice a year, and always with a pretext — delivering food he hadn't asked for, checking on a stove he maintained perfectly. Tonight she carried a covered basket and no pretext at all, and she sat on the stool behind his counter like a woman taking possession of a chair she intended to keep.

"You've heard," she said.

"Heard what?" He tried the joke reflex. "That the *Meridian*'s cook—"

"Tomás."

He set down the rag he was wringing. Outside, the last light was going copper on the strait, and the bridge stood empty for the first time all day, its whole long length bare, like a sentence with the words removed.

"A month early," he said. "Military order. And the boat leaves in a week, and there's one berth, and it's mine. It's been mine since Vasquez's boy got sick. The tin under your floor has my name scratched inside the lid, Ma, don't pretend you haven't seen it."

"I've seen it." She opened the basket. Rice cakes, wrapped in banana leaf, still warm — she'd baked them this morning knowing, somehow, before she even knew why. That was Rosa. "And I've seen the way you laugh too much when you're frightened. You get it from your father. He laughed the week before the water took him, told jokes at supper like a man being paid for them."

"Ma—"

"I'm not here to argue the berth." She smoothed the leaf wrapper flat with both hands, an old habit, making neatness where none was required. "I'm here to say: whatever you're planning, plan it properly. Because Brandt doubled the searches yesterday. Two of the drying sheds turned out this morning, and a coracle checked at the north mangroves. He knows the boat is coming. He'll want to know who's on it."

Tomás looked at his mother — small, gray-streaked, flour to the wrists — and felt the familiar vertigo of realizing, again, that she was the toughest person on this island and always had been.

"And there's something else," Rosa said quietly. "Your sister. She's been coming home late. Ink on her cuffs where she doesn't usually ink. She's copying something, or reading something, and whatever it is, she's scared of it and can't put it down. Watch her, Tomás. The quiet ones drown deepest."

He thought of Astrid at the supper table lately, answering questions a beat late, her eyes doing sums behind them that had nothing to do with money. He'd filed it under tiredness. Filing things under tiredness was how you missed them.

"She'll be fine," he said. "She's the smartest person on this island."

"Yes," Rosa said. "That's what worries me. Smart people find true things, and true things are heavy to carry." She stood, tucked the basket under his arm, and paused at the door of the tollhouse, looking out at the darkening bridge. "One berth," she said, almost to herself. "And a family of three, and a stranger sleeping in our shed, and a war closing in from both ends of the water." She shook her head slowly. "Pray it stays simple, meu filho. In my experience it never does."

She went. Tomás sat alone in the tollhouse with the rice cakes cooling and the military order pinned to the wall above his head, and did the only arithmetic that mattered, over and over, hoping a different answer would eventually fall out:

One boat. One berth. One week.

And somewhere out there in the dark, past the third pier where the smugglers' path met the shore, a lantern blinked twice — the signal he'd taught them, the signal that meant *cargo tonight, bring the letters* — and Tomás got up, pocketed the toll box key, and went out onto the empty bridge to earn his keep.

The last boat was coming whether he was ready or not.
