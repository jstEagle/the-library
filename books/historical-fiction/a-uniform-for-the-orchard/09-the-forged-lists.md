## 9. The Forged Lists

The rain came sideways that evening, the way it did when the monsoon was still deciding, and Astrid sat in the copying room with a lamp turned low and a lie turning over in her chest like a stone in a diver's hand.

She had been careful. She had been so careful for three weeks that care had begun to feel like a second skin, worn over the first one, which itself had been worn over whatever she'd been before Elias washed into the pearl beds half-drowned and full of dangerous sentences. *The company is selling their names twice.* She hadn't wanted to believe it. Believing it meant acting on it, and acting on it meant becoming someone her father would have had to squint at to recognize.

But tonight the columns had confessed.

* * *

It started, as most of Brandt's sins did, with arithmetic.

Astrid Almeida could add faster than any clerk the Verrone Trading Company had employed in its forty years on the island, and she knew it the way she knew her own hands — not as vanity but as fact, the way you know you can hold your breath for two minutes or walk the sea wall in the dark without missing a plank. Numbers were honest. That was the whole reason she loved them. A number could be wrong, yes — miswritten, smudged, transposed by a tired hand at the end of a long day — but it could never *lie*. It could only be made to look like something it wasn't. And making numbers look like something they weren't took effort. Effort left fingerprints.

The conscription lists were supposed to balance against the labor rolls. Forty-two divers contracted north for naval service, forty-two names struck from the compound rolls, forty-two families' debts transferred to the navy's account with a small handling fee — Brandt's fee, always Brandt's fee, entered under *administrative costs* in a hand so neat it might have been printed. Astrid had copied those entries twice before without blinking. Handling fees were as much a part of island life as salt air; you didn't resent the tide for being wet.

But tonight, cross-checking the September rolls against the August muster — because there was nothing else to do in the copying room while the rain hammered the shutters and the lamp oil was too cheap to waste on daylight hours — she found Joaquim Reyes.

Joaquim Reyes, listed on the September conscription manifest: *transferred north, naval service, per order.*

Joaquim Reyes, on the October labor roll, three weeks later: *bed four, pearl harvest, wages garnished 30 percent, debt accruing.*

Astrid set down her pen. Picked it up again. Checked the spelling — *Reyes* was sometimes written *Reis*, depending on which clerk was tired, and a tired clerk's error could explain almost anything except what she was looking at now.

Both entries were in the same handwriting. Both were correct. Both were signed with the same small, precise initials at the bottom right corner: **H.B.**

Hendrik Brandt. Overseer of the Verrone Trading Company, keeper of the docks, the debts, the bridge tolls, and — apparently — every diver on the island, sold like a double-strung pearl to two buyers who each believed they alone held him.

Her heart did not race. That surprised her later, when she thought about it. Her heart went very quiet instead, the way the lagoon goes quiet before wind, and she read the page again, and then she did what she had trained herself to do since she was eleven years old and first understood that her father's drowning had been entered in a ledger as *asset lost, debt reassigned*: she memorized it.

Then she looked for more.

* * *

There were eleven.

Eleven men, over five months, appearing on the conscription manifests *and* the active labor rolls, sometimes within days of each other, sometimes weeks apart, always in different hands — no, not different hands, she corrected herself, scanning again — always with slightly different ink, because Brandt was clever enough to vary his pen but not clever enough to vary his initials, his spacing, his habit of crossing sevens with a flat stroke that no other clerk on the island used. Astrid used a curved stroke. Old Mateus in the storehouse used a looped one. The flat cross was Brandt's signature tic, as distinctive as a limp, and it ran through all twenty-two entries like a stitch through cloth.

Each man's name sold north to the navy — fee collected, debt marked *satisfied*.

Each man's name kept home on the labor rolls — wages garnished, debt *accruing*, growing fat with interest while the family dove and hauled and scraped shells for a company that had already been paid for them once.

And the families. God, the families. The Reyes household had been paying on a dead man's debt for four months — no, worse than dead, she thought grimly, *worse than drowned*, because drowning at least ended the account honestly — paying on a debt that had already been settled in full by the navy's transfer payment, plus a bonus Brandt had pocketed under *transportation costs*. The Senas. The Almeida cousins up the hill — not close cousins, but cousins. Eleven households, bleeding monthly installments into a wound that had healed on the other side months ago.

Astrid sat very still in the lamplight and felt something in her chest that was not fear.

She had expected fear. Fear was familiar; fear was the taste of the company's bread. But this was colder and cleaner, and it frightened her precisely because it wasn't frightening. It was the feeling she imagined her knife had, lying in its drawer — patient, sharp, waiting to be useful.

*They are not even hiding well,* she thought, and then, more honestly: *They don't need to hide. No one checks. No one has ever checked.*

That was the true architecture of it, she realized. Not the forgery itself — forgery was easy, forgery was practically a service the company offered. It was the *checking*. Nobody audited an overseer against himself. Brandt wrote both sides of every transaction and answered only to a head office across the water that saw islands as line items and line items as abstractions. The duplicate ledger — the clean copy sent north each quarter — would show exactly what Brandt wished shown. Which meant somewhere in his quarters, in the locked cabinet behind his desk that Tomás had once joked he'd sell a year of his life to open, sat the real book. The working book. The one where the same name appeared twice with two different prices beside it.

Tomás. The thought of her brother arrived with its usual weight, heavier lately, carrying the warrant rumor with it like a stone in a net. If the lists were forged, then the warrants built on them — desertion charges, conscription-evasion charges, the paper chains the soldiers carried — were forged too. Every man hunted on this island was hunted by a lie with a government seal on it.

Including, possibly, Elias.

* * *

She heard Rosa moving below — the particular creak of the third stair, the one that announced Mother rather than intruder, though after the last month Astrid had stopped being sure those were different categories — and she closed the ledger with a soft sound, smoothing her expression into the mild blankness clerks wear like aprons.

Rosa came in with tea she couldn't afford and poured it anyway, because Rosa's answer to catastrophe, grief, war, and monsoon alike was hot sweet liquid administered with a stern face.

"You've been up here four hours," her mother said.

"The rolls needed balancing."

"The rolls," Rosa said, "always need balancing. That is how they keep us." She set the cup down, watched her daughter's face a moment too long, and Astrid watched her mother decide — visibly, the way you watch a woman choose not to ask about rice hidden under tree roots — to let it go. "Drink it while it's hot. Cold tea is just punishment."

When she'd gone, Astrid drank the tea and thought about the locked cabinet.

Not about whether to act — some part of her had already acted, had already copied the eleven names onto a scrap of rice paper in letters smaller than grains, folded it into a shape no bigger than a thumbnail, and sewn it — that afternoon, with steady fingers — into the hem of her sleeve. Her hands had decided while her mind was still deliberating. That was the family way, maybe. Rosa hid rice. Tomás smuggled letters. Astrid, apparently, committed forgery-adjacent crimes in a copying room during rainstorms.

What she thought about was *access*.

Brandt's quarters were in the administrative house, guarded less by locks than by etiquette — no clerk entered uninvited, no clerk *wanted* to enter, and the one time a delivery boy had stumbled in with a crate of lamp oil, he'd come out white-faced and been unable afterward to say why, only that the overseer had smiled at him. Brandt's smile was famous the way storms were famous. But the quarterly reports went north on the supply boat, and the supply boat was due within the month, and a man preparing a clean ledger for the mainland would need to work from the dirty one.

Which meant the dirty one was in that room *now*.

Astrid finished her tea, blew out the lamp, and stood a moment in the dark listening to the rain. Below, faintly, Elias coughed in his corner of the drying shed — healing, walking better each day, carrying his guilt around like everyone else on this island carried their debts.

"Eleven names," she whispered to the dark, testing the weight of it. "Eleven families. One book."

The cold thing in her chest settled into something with edges. Not fury yet — fury was hot, and this had no heat at all. This was inventory. This was a woman counting what was owed and noting, in the flat neat hand she'd learned from the best liars in the territory, that Hendrik Brandt's account was badly, catastrophically overdrawn.

She picked up her pen one more time, and in the margin of her practice sheet, where no auditor would ever look, she drew a single small flat-crossed seven.

Then she crossed it out.

Tomorrow she would find out when Brandt slept. Tomorrow she would find out who cleaned his rooms. Tomorrow, somehow, she would get into that cabinet — and she already knew, with the calm certainty of a woman reading figures that finally added up, that she would not stop at looking.

Because a ledger, once opened, is very hard to close again. And somewhere across the water, in a city that thought this island was a line item, there was going to be an audit.

She just had to carry it there herself.
