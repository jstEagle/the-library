## 10. Orchard Roots

The mangoes came in early that year, which Rosa Almeida took as a personal insult. A tree she had coaxed through three seasons of salt wind and company neglect, and it chose to ripen its fruit while the rains were still deciding whether to arrive — as if the orchard itself had grown impatient with waiting.

"Greedy," she told the tree, standing under it with her basket on her hip. "You'll be the death of me."

She said this to everything. To the chickens, who laid their eggs behind the lime press where no one could reach them without a prayer and a rope. To the rain barrel, which grew a skin of green so thick she could have cut it into cloth. To God, occasionally, though with Him she kept her tone more respectful. But the truth was that Rosa liked talking to things that couldn't answer back, because the things that could answer had a way of asking questions she wasn't ready to give.

Her daughter was one of those things.

Astrid had been coming to the orchard every evening that week, after her hours at the counting house, and she did not come for fruit. Sixteen years old and she walked the rows like an auditor, hands folded, eyes moving from tree to tree the way they moved down a ledger column — quick, exact, missing nothing. Rosa recognized the walk because she had invented it. She had worn it herself for twenty years, walking these same rows, counting what could be counted and hiding what could not.

Tonight Astrid arrived later than usual, and there was something in her face that made Rosa put down the basket.

"You've been crying," Rosa said. "Or you've been near smoke. Which is it?"

"Neither." Astrid sat on the low wall by the irrigation channel, which was really just a ditch the company called infrastructure. "Mama. Can I ask you something, and will you answer it truly?"

Rosa picked up a fallen mango and turned it over in her hand, buying time the way poor women always have — with small busy work. "I have never once lied to you," she said, which was true in the way that most comforting statements are true: technically, and only if you defined your terms carefully.

"The divers' names. In the ledgers." Astrid's voice was low, though the nearest neighbor was two rows of papaya away and deaf besides. "Men listed as sold north who never left the island. Men whose families still bring rice to the company store because they think the debt travels with the man. Mama, do you know anything about that?"

The mango in Rosa's hand had a bruise on one side, soft and going brown. She pressed her thumb into it and watched the flesh give.

*Here it is*, she thought. *Twenty years, and here it comes up through the roots like blight.*

"Help me with the trees first," she said.

* * *

They worked in silence for a while, because Rosa had learned long ago that the body must be given something to do before the mouth can be trusted with heavy things. She pruned the deadwood off the guava while Astrid gathered windfalls into the basket, and the evening came down around them the way it always did — gold going to copper going to that particular island purple that made even the company compound look almost beautiful, if you squinted and forgot what it cost.

It was Astrid's father who had planted half these trees. That was the thing nobody at the company remembered, because the company did not remember people, only accounts. Joaquim Almeida had come to this island with nothing but strong shoulders and a laugh too big for his body, and he had put saplings in the ground along the compound wall and told anyone who would listen that a man who planted a tree was making an argument with the future.

"He argued well," Rosa murmured now, touching a branch.

"Mama?"

"Your father." She looked at her daughter across the row. "You asked if I know about the lists. Sit down, Astrid. Not on the wall — here, on the ground, where I can see your face properly."

Astrid sat. She had her mother's patience when it mattered, which was one of the three things she'd inherited from Rosa. The other two were a stubbornness that had been described by three different overseers as insubordination, and a way of keeping secrets so completely that even her own face forgot them.

Rosa sat down beside her, skirts pooling in the dirt, which Brandt would have had opinions about if Brandt had ever once looked at the orchard instead of through it.

"I'm going to tell you about your father," Rosa said. "Not the version I gave you when you were small enough to need a kind one. The real one."

Astrid went very still. Stillness ran in the family too.

"You think he drowned," Rosa began. "Everyone thinks he drowned. The company wrote it down — lost overboard, rough water, regrettable — and they wrote it down so neatly that even the sea believed them."

"It was a storm night. Tomás remembers—"

"Tomás remembers being nine years old and being told his father fell off a boat. Memory is a cooperative animal, my love. It agrees with whoever feeds it." Rosa pulled a weed from between her knees and snapped its stem. "Your father did not fall off any boat. Your father could swim before he could walk. His mother used to say he came out of the womb doing the breaststroke, and I believe her, because I married a man who thought a crossing to the mainland in a leaky coracle was a reasonable Sunday activity."

"Then how—"

"He found the lists."

The words came out of Rosa flat and quiet, the way she'd practiced them for twenty years in the privacy of her own head, all those nights lying awake listening to rain on the roof and rehearsing a confession she'd hoped never to deliver.

Astrid didn't move. Didn't breathe, nearly. Rosa watched her daughter's mind do what it always did — seize the fact, turn it, weigh it, file it.

"The conscription lists," Astrid said finally. "He found them. Before."

"Before you were born. Before Tomás could walk. Nineteen years ago, and the same fraud running the same trick with the same ink, because men like Brandt don't invent evil, Astrid, they inherit it, like a shop. Like a uniform." Rosa heard her own voice sharpening and made herself slow down. "Joaquim worked the pearl beds, but he also ran errands for the company office — carrying messages, hauling boxes, whatever paid a little extra. He was clever with numbers, your father. Everyone forgets that because he laughed so much. But he could look at a column and see what it wanted to hide. It's where you get it, that eye of yours. Not from me. From him."

She stopped. Pressed her thumb again into the bruised mango, though it was long past giving.

"One season he comes home late, twice, three times, smelling of lamp oil and paper instead of brine. Won't say why. Then one night he wakes me — this hour, near enough, the light just like this — and he says, *Rosa, I have found out how they keep us.* And I say, we're indentured, Joaquim, everyone knows how they keep us, it's the debt. And he says no. No, the debt is only the net. He'd found the duplicate ledger. The real one. Every diver's name written twice — sold to the navy as a conscript, sold again to a labor broker on the mainland, and both times the family left holding a debt for a man who was gone, or worse, holding a debt for a man who was listed as gone and was actually diving in someone else's beds under someone else's name while his wages fed the overseer's private account."

Astrid's breath came out slowly. "That's exactly—" She stopped herself. Rosa saw her daughter decide, deliberately, not to say what she knew. Another inheritance.

"Yes," Rosa said gently. "It is exactly what you found. I know that look. I wore that look for twenty years, wondering when you'd wear it too."

"What happened to him?"

The question landed the way such questions do — softly, and then all at once. Rosa let the silence sit between them for the length of three waves against the seawall.

"He went to the priest first," she said. "Father Anselmo, may he rest, though he rests easier than he lived. Joaquim thought the Church would act. The priest told him to be careful, and Joaquim laughed — he always laughed at danger, it was his worst habit and his best quality and I loved him for both and cursed him for both. Then he went to the old harbormaster, a decent man, long dead now, who promised to carry word to the commission on the mainland when the bridge opened. And then—"

"And then the storm night."

"And then the storm night. On a night when the water was flat as a church floor until sundown, and the storm came up sudden, they said. Sudden storms happen, Astrid. But boats don't sink themselves, and men who can swim don't drown in the shallows off their own jetty." Rosa's voice held steady, because it had held steady for twenty years and it was not going to crack now, in front of the girl who needed it straight. "Three days later the harbormaster resigned his post and took ship north and was never heard from again. Two weeks after that, an overseer's clerk — a boy who had helped your father copy pages — was transferred inland and died of fever within the year, or so we were told. And I understood."

"Understood what?"

"That the company doesn't punish. The company *tidies*. It removes the loose thread and smooths the cloth and writes a neat line in the margin — lost overboard, regrettable — and the pattern goes on unbroken." She looked out past the orchard wall, toward the dark line of the sea. "And I understood something else. That if I raged, if I ran to the authorities, if I stood in the compound square and shouted the truth, I would be tidied too. And there would be no one left to raise a girl with sharp eyes and a boy with quick feet, and the lists would go on selling our people twice until the end of the world."

"So you were silent."

"So I was *strategic*," Rosa corrected, and here, despite everything, the ghost of her old humor surfaced. "There is a difference, and the difference is that strategic women get to live, and also get to keep a rice cache under the third guava tree that has fed eleven families through four bad seasons, and also get their daughter into the counting house, which I promise you was no accident. Do you think Verrone took a diver's widow's child as a copy clerk out of charity? I spent six years making myself useful to Mrs. Ferreira and her terrible bridge club and her worse singing. Useful women are expensive, Astrid. Silence is a currency. I have been paying it out coin by coin since before you were born, and tonight I am showing you the vault."

Astrid was quiet a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had gone careful, the way it did when she was handling something fragile. "The page I copied. From the duplicate columns. You already knew it was there."

"I knew it was there the way I know the monsoon is coming — by the smell, by the birds, by twenty years of the same weather." Rosa reached over and took her daughter's hand. It was a working hand already, ink-stained at the fingertips, callused where the pen rested. Her father's hand. "What I did not know was whether you had found it. And I made myself a promise, lying awake all these nights, that if you ever came to me with that look on your face, I would not lie to you. Even if the truth was heavier than you were ready to carry."

"Is that why you hid the rice? All those years?" Astrid frowned. "No. The rice was before. You were hiding rice before you knew I'd find the lists."

"The rice is a different lesson," Rosa said. "But it's the same one. Everything worth saving gets buried first. Trees know this. Seeds know this. Women on this island have known it since before the company had a name." She smiled, tired and real. "You bury the truth deep, you tend the ground above it, and you wait for the season when it can come up without being cut down the day it sprouts."

"And is this the season?"

Rosa looked at her daughter — at the set of her jaw, at the ink stains, at the copied page Rosa would bet her life was sewn somewhere close to that stubborn heart — and felt the old fear rise in her chest, the fear she had nursed for twenty years like a second child, and felt something else rising beside it, stronger.

Something that felt disgracefully like pride.

"I don't know," she admitted. "The bridge closes early this year, they say. There's a war coming closer than the radio admits. Men like Brandt get careless when they're frightened, and dangerous when they're cornered, and he is about to be both." She squeezed Astrid's hand. "So hear me, and hear me fully. If you carry this thing forward — and I can see that you will, because you are your father's daughter and arguing with the future runs in the blood — then you do it the way I taught the orchard. Deep roots. Many hands. Nothing that matters kept in one place, not in one tree, not in one house, not in one heart. Because a truth that lives in one person can be tidied. A truth that lives in a whole village—"

"—has to be dug out root by root," Astrid finished.

"Look at that. Raised her myself and she quotes me back my own gardening." Rosa laughed, and the laugh cracked something loose in her chest that had been locked since a flat-calm night nineteen years ago, and for a moment she let herself lean her shoulder against her daughter's shoulder and simply breathe.

Above them the mango tree shifted in the evening wind, dropping one last ripe fruit into the grass, greedy and early and alive.

* * *

They walked back to the house together in the blue dark, and Rosa lit the lamp and put rice on to soak without either of them discussing it, because some rituals run deeper than conversation. Tomás was out — tollhouse, probably, or wherever quick-footed boys went when they claimed to be at the tollhouse — and the house held the small wet sounds of the island settling into night.

It was while she was skimming the pot that Astrid spoke again, from the doorway, quietly.

"Mama. The man in the drying shed. Elias."

Rosa's hands did not stop moving. Twenty years of practice. "What about him?"

"He says the war is nearer than anyone admits. He says the names are being sold twice right now, this season, in Brandt's own books." A pause. "He's seen it done on other islands. He knows what it looks like."

"Does he." Rosa skimmed the foam from the rice, slow and even. "And does he know what happens to men who see things, on this island especially?"

"He knows what happened to Papa."

The ladle stilled. Rosa turned. In the lamplight her daughter's face was pale and set, and beneath the fear Rosa could see the other thing, the thing she had spent two decades hoping and dreading to see: the argument with the future, taking shape in a sixteen-year-old's jaw.

"Then he knows," Rosa said, "exactly what he's sheltering under this roof, and exactly what it will cost all of us if Brandt counts one head too many." She turned back to the pot, because her hands needed the work, and because if she looked at Astrid a moment longer the girl would see that the fear in her mother's chest had just doubled in size and shown no sign whatsoever of winning.

"Eat," she said. "Both of you. Tomorrow I want to know everything that man knows — every name, every date, every page. If we are going to dig this up at last, my love, we are going to do it properly."

From the doorway, very softly, Astrid said, "Deep roots."

"Many hands," Rosa answered, and stirred the pot, and listened to the rain beginning, out of season, on the roof.
