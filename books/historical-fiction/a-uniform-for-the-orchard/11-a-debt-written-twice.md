## 11. A Debt Written Twice

The rice paper came from the temple box at the back of Mother's cupboard, where she kept the things that mattered more than money: my father's one good shirt, a string of seed pearls too small to sell, and forty sheets of paper thin enough to read a hand through.

"You're certain about this," Rosa said. She laid the sheets on the table the way you lay out a body — flat, straightened, given its dignity first.

"It's what I have." I turned my father's pen in my fingers. It was a cheap thing, bamboo, split at the nib and mended with wax, but he had written his last letter home with it and I trusted it the way other people trust saints. "If I copy it big, it's a book. If I copy it small, it's a secret."

"And if they find a secret sewn into your collar?"

"Then they find a girl who keeps her accounts close." I said it lightly, because that is how we said terrible things in our house — lightly, so they couldn't take root. But Rosa looked at me for a long moment over the lamp, and I saw her decide not to argue, which from my mother was a kind of blessing.

* * *

The problem was not courage. The problem was arithmetic.

I had spent three years learning that everything on this island could be measured in carryable things: a knife, a ledger stub, a name that drowned. Now I had to measure truth itself. There were eleven pages in the duplicate ledger that mattered — the ones where Brandt's beautiful columns sold each diver twice, once north to the navy and once east to the labor broker, two debts collected on one man's back, and neither man paid, and neither man told. Eleven pages of names. I knew them all by heart already; that was the curse of being good at my work. But hearts are searched more often than collars, and ink stays put when memory drowns.

So: eleven pages onto forty sheets of rice paper, each sheet half the size of my palm, each line fine as a hair.

I practiced first on nothing. On the back of an old tally slip I wrote the alphabet smaller than I had ever written it, then smaller again, until my letters were ants and then were fleas, and my eyes watered, and Tomás — who had come in dripping from checking the tollhouse lines and refused, absolutely refused, to explain why he smelled of lamp oil — peered over my shoulder and said:

"That's very impressive and completely unreadable."

"It doesn't need to be readable," I said. "It needs to be *there*."

"It needs to be readable by whoever gets it," he said, and then went quiet, because we both understood who that would be: some clerk on the mainland, some commission man with clean cuffs and no reason to trust a scrap of cloth covered in flea-sized script. "Write me a sample line. Big enough to squint at."

I wrote it. He held it to the lamp, then at arm's length, then upside down, making a performance of it, because Tomás carried fear the way he carried everything — as cargo he joked about so nobody noticed the weight.

"All right," he admitted. "A determined man could read it. Are there any determined men on the mainland?"

"There are men who get paid by results."

"Those are the most determined creatures alive." He set the sample down and, for once, didn't make another joke. He just looked at me, and I felt him wanting to say something large and brotherly, and I felt him deciding against it, and I loved him for both.

* * *

I worked at night, after the compound lamps were doused, with the shutter cracked to a knife of moonlight because even lamplight through a seam could be reported by a neighbor with a grudge or a debt. Elias sat across from me those nights, wrapped in Rosa's shawl, holding pages steady while the ink dried. His wound had gone from angry red to a sullen purple, which Rosa pronounced "the color of healing" with such authority that the wound seemed embarrassed and began doing better.

"You don't have to sit up with me," I told him the second night.

"I can't sleep anyway." He turned a page toward the moonlight. "In the army I stood watch for two years. Four hours of dark, every night, staring at a trench. My body still believes it owes somebody a watch." He smiled without much humor. "So let me stand yours. It's easier, watching someone write down the thing that ruined us instead of waiting for it to arrive."

He held the paper. I wrote. And between us, in the smallest handwriting on the island, the dead men got their names back.

That was the part I hadn't expected — how much it would feel like that. In the company ledgers, the divers were entries. *Joaquim Reyes, account closed.* As if a man were a sum that had been settled. But copying them by hand, syllable by syllable, I had to write each whole name out, and each date of conscription, and each date of the second sale, and somewhere around the fourth page I stopped being a clerk and started being something else. A witness, maybe. Or a debt collector working for the dead.

"Say it while you write," Elias suggested quietly. "My sergeant did that with the roll call. Said a name only stays alive if it passes through a mouth."

So I whispered them. Joaquim Reyes, taken twice, March and April. Anselmo Cruz, taken twice, his fee pocketed both times. The Ferreira brothers, taken together, sold apart. Forty-one men on eleven pages, and I gave every one of them their voice again in our kitchen at midnight, and Elias answered each name softly — *"present"* — like a soldier answering roll, and if that made me cry a little into the rice paper, well, rice paper drinks tears without complaint, and I kept the worst of them off the names themselves.

Rosa slept in snatches and rose in snatches, bringing tea that tasted of burnt sugar and certainty. Once, passing behind me, she stopped and read a line over my shoulder — my mother, who could not read more than market prices, reading numbers the way other people read psalms — and touched the back of my neck once, briefly, and went back to bed. That touch stayed warmer than the tea.

* * *

On the third night, the sewing.

We took my oldest collar, the faded blue one I wore under everything else because it had been Father's preference and I'd never broken the habit. Rosa unpicked the facing with her smallest blade — not my knife; my knife stayed mine, handle worn to the shape of my grip, because a girl on this island learns early which objects are allowed to mean things.

"Turn it," she said, and I turned, and she worked the first folded sheet into the channel between the fabric layers, stitching with thread pulled fine as fishing line. Her hands were quick. Twenty years of hiding rice under orchard roots had taught her hands things her mouth never admitted to.

"Will it show?" I asked. "Stiffness? A bulge?"

"On you?" She snorted. "You wear your clothes like a scarecrow wears rain. Nobody has ever looked at your collar twice in your life."

"Thank you, Mother."

"It's the greatest camouflage God ever devised, a plain girl. The pretty ones get looked at. We get *believed*." She tied off a knot with her teeth. "Hold still. This next one sits at the nerve of your neck and I don't want to stab the only clever child I have."

"You have two children."

"Tomás was stabbed plenty growing up. He deserved most of it."

But her hands were gentle, and the work went slow and sure, page by page, eleven small weights settling along the inside of my collar like a necklace worn backward. When she finished, near dawn, she turned me to the polished tin that served us as a mirror and made me look.

I looked ordinary. That was the whole art of it. A tired girl, sixteen, salt-stiff hair, a blue collar gone soft at the edges. Nothing in the glass said *this throat carries forty-one men*. I turned my head left, right. The paper moved with me and only me.

"How does it feel?" Rosa asked.

I considered honestly. "Like a debt," I said. "One I finally know the amount of."

* * *

There was one page left over. The twelfth — my own copy of the tally I'd falsified for Brandt's inspection, the night I hid Elias in the drying sheds. My first crime, written in my own neat clerk's hand. I had copied it without quite deciding to, the way you swallow before you've thought about swallowing.

"What will you do with that one?" Elias asked. He'd watched me weigh it on my palm for the better part of an hour.

"Burn it, probably."

"Why did you copy it at all?"

I turned it toward the moonlight. There was my signature — *verified, A. Almeida* — sitting under a count I knew to be a lie. "Because it's evidence," I said slowly, working it out as I spoke, "that I'm a liar. If anyone ever checks my true copies against the real ledger, they'll find one page where I certified a falsehood. They'll say I forged the forgeries. That I altered things." I looked up. "This page is my alibi and my confession in the same breath. Whoever reads it will understand exactly how deep the rot goes — because here's a clerk forced to lie in the company's own hand, and here's proof she knew she was lying."

Elias was quiet a long moment. Then he laughed — a short, surprised sound, quickly smothered for the sake of the sleeping house.

"You're going to be dangerous," he said. "Not brave. Brave is common in a war. *Dangerous* — that's rarer. That's someone who understands that paper is the real weapon and everyone else is still fighting with guns."

"My father drowned carrying pearls," I said. "Pearls at least are worth something anywhere. Paper—" I tapped the little folded packet that now lived against my neck, "—paper is worth whatever we say it's worth. So we'd better be very sure of what we say."

* * *

Dawn found me in the orchard, because habit is stronger than insomnia. The trees stood black and dripping in the unseasonal rain, and beneath the oldest mango, where the roots made a room underground, our cache of rice waited out the season the way we waited out the season — patiently, buried, alive.

I didn't dig. I just stood over it a moment, one keeper visiting another.

Eleven pages sewn against my neck. One page of my own guilt folded flat inside my boot. The master knowledge of forty-one stolen men arranged in my head in columns so precise I could recite them under interrogation, if it came to that, if the collar burned and the papers drowned and they left me nothing but the space behind my eyes. Three copies of the truth now: cloth, boot, blood. A debt written twice by Brandt; a debt written three times by me.

"Astrid."

Tomás came down the orchard rows fast, too fast, wet to the knees and trying to hide it. I knew his walk. There was a walk for *nothing's wrong* and a walk for *something's wrong but I'll joke first*, and there was a third walk I had only seen twice in my life, and he was wearing it.

"What," I said.

"So the funny thing about boats," he began, and stopped, and started again. "The funny thing is everyone thinks they're guaranteed one eventually. Like rain. Like death." He wiped his face, which did nothing, since it was raining. "There's a berth, Astrid. On the last boat out. The village pooled for it — old Marques, the fisher families, even the nuns, God keep them. One seat. Before the strait shuts."

"For you," I said. It wasn't a question. He had a warrant with his name drying on it; everyone from here to the tollhouse assumed the pooled money had bought his life back.

"For me," he agreed, and his grin arrived on schedule, right on time, wrong season, and underneath it his eyes were already counting days. "It leaves soon. Before the bridge does. Which means, little sister, that we have a decision to make, and we have to make it *quickly*—"

Down the hill, from the direction of the compound, a bell began to ring. Not the work bell. Not the church bell.

The storm bell.

Tomás and I looked at each other through the rain, and I felt the collar shift against my neck, eleven small weights settling, as if the truth itself had leaned in to listen.
