## 12. The Last Berth

The news came to the tollhouse the way all important news came to the tollhouse: sideways, wrapped in something ordinary, carried by someone who pretended they were there for another reason entirely.

In this case, the something ordinary was a sack of dried fish, and the someone was old Mateus Ferreira, who had not bought dried fish from anyone in eleven years on account of his own nets being, as he liked to say, "perfectly adequate." Tomás weighed the sack anyway, because weighing things was what a toll clerk did, and because Mateus's hands were shaking in a way that had nothing to do with age.

"Bit heavy for fish," Tomás said.

"Good fish," Mateus said. "Fat year. Rain early, fish fat."

"Rain early," Tomás repeated. He set down the weights. "Mateus. What did you hear."

The old man glanced past him at the empty toll queue — it was late afternoon, the bridge traffic thinned to a trickle of orchard hands and one very tired mule — and then leaned across the counter as far as his ribs allowed, which was not far.

"One berth," he said. "On the *Santa Luzia*. Last boat out before the strait seals. The village pooled for it. Everyone gave what they had — Senhora Batista sold her good table, can you imagine, the woman ate off that table at her wedding — and old Duarte gave his boat money, which means Duarte is now a man who owns no boat, which around here is like a priest who owns no God." Mateus paused for effect, being a man who believed every sentence deserved a small audience. "One berth, Tomás. Bought with everyone's money. And everyone knows whose name goes in it."

Tomás kept his face very still, the way you learn to when you work a tollhouse and people hand you letters sewn into hems and expect you to look bored while doing it.

"Whose name," he said carefully, "does everyone think goes in it?"

Mateus looked at him the way you'd look at a man who'd asked whether the sea was wet. "Yours, boy. Yours. You're the one with the paper on your head. Warrant, they call it. Sounds so official, doesn't it? Like an invitation." He snorted. "Brandt's clerks wrote it up pretty and everything."

* * *

Tomás walked home that evening by the long way, along the seawall, because the long way was where a man went when his head needed more room than the road offered.

A berth. A real berth, on a real boat, paid for in tables and boat money and whatever else the village had scraped together when nobody was watching — though of course everybody had been watching, that was the nature of the island, and the pooling had been conducted with the discretion of a carnival.

He tried to feel what he was supposed to feel. Hope, surely. Relief. The mainland meant no warrant, no Brandt, no mornings spent counting heads and wondering which name would be next to grow a red mark beside it in some ledger. The mainland meant dry season that stayed dry, a bed that wasn't behind the tollhouse, a life where the most dangerous thing he did all day was argue about cargo weights.

Instead, mostly, he felt the way he'd felt at twelve years old when Astrid had beaten him to the top of the breadfruit tree: pleased, breathless, and faintly cheated, because she'd taken the ladder route and nobody had said the ladder counted.

He laughed at himself for it, alone on the seawall, and the laugh came out wrong, and he stopped walking and looked at the water for a while.

Here was the arithmetic he couldn't get around. The village had bought the berth for the man with the warrant. That was sound reasoning. That was even generous reasoning — Duarte had literally given up his boat for it. But sound reasoning assumed the situation was simple, and the situation was not simple, because simple situations didn't have little brothers who copied ledgers and came home with their eyes doing that thing where they knew something and were deciding whether to tell you.

Astrid had been doing the eye thing for three weeks now.

* * *

At the house, Rosa had rice on the fire and Elias propped in the corner chair looking less like a drowned thing and more like a man, which after two months was its own kind of miracle. His shoulder had knitted crooked — Rosa said it would always ache before rain, which on this island meant it would ache roughly always — but he could walk to the window and back without holding the wall, and he'd begun doing small useful tasks with his left hand as if daring anyone to comment.

"You're late," Rosa said, not turning from the fire.

"Toll ran long."

"The toll never runs long. The toll runs short, or the toll doesn't run at all." She pointed the spoon at the table. "Sit. There's fish."

"There's always fish."

"And yet you complain."

"I'm not complaining. I'm observing. There's a difference. One is ingratitude and the other is science."

Elias made the low sound he made instead of laughing, having learned somewhere in his soldiering that laughing loudly got men killed, and Tomás sat, and the fish was good, and for a quarter of an hour the house held only ordinary things — the fire, the rain starting against the roof, Elias's spoon clicking, Rosa humming something tuneless that might have been a hymn or might have been her own invention.

It was Astrid who broke it, of course. Astrid, who had inherited their mother's gift for knowing when a house was keeping a secret and refusing to be left outside it.

"Mateus came by the drying sheds today," she said, mild as milk. "He wanted to know if I thought the *Santa Luzia*'s manifest would need checking. Officially."

Tomás put down his spoon. "And what did you tell him?"

"That manifests are Brandt's department and I keep my nose in my own ledger." She turned her cup on the table, once, twice. "Then he told me about the berth anyway, because Mateus has never once respected a professional boundary."

"Astrid—"

"One berth," she said. "Bought by the village. For you. Because of the warrant."

Rosa had gone still by the fire. Even Elias had stopped clicking his spoon. Tomás looked around the table at the three faces waiting on him and understood that this conversation had already happened without him, in pieces, in the sheds and the garden and the corner chair, and that he was the last one invited.

"Well," he said. "Good. That's — good. That's settled, then."

"Settled," Astrid repeated.

"Yes. Settled. The village bought me a ticket off this rock, I take the ticket, everyone claps, very moving, we'll cry at the dock." He picked his spoon back up. "What's unsettled about that?"

"Nothing," Astrid said. "Only that you haven't asked why the boat leaves when it leaves."

"It leaves before the rains. Boats do that."

"It leaves *early*, Tomás." And here her voice changed, dropped the teasing edge, became the voice she used for numbers that didn't add up. "Two weeks early. Military order. The strait closes ahead of schedule this year and nobody will say why, and the *Santa Luzia* sails on the first clear tide after the bridge shuts, and after that there's nothing in or out until dry season. Nothing. No boats, no letters, no bridge." She let that sit. "Whatever happens on this island after it sails, happens without witnesses."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, the rain found its rhythm and committed to it.

"So I should be on it," Tomás said. "That's rather my point. If the island's about to become a locked room, the man with the warrant should be a man on the other side of the door."

"Yes," Astrid said. "That's exactly what I'd say too, if all I knew about was the warrant."

Tomás looked at her. "Astrid."

"If all I knew about," she said again, gently, terribly, "was the warrant."

* * *

He didn't sleep much that night, which was fine, because neither did anyone else in the house; he could tell by the quality of the silence, the way each creak of the roof got answered by a different creak from a different room, four people pretending to sleep at each other.

The trouble with Tomás Almeida — and he'd had years to inventory his troubles, being a man who processed everything by making lists — was that he could not stop doing the math.

The math went like this. The village had bought one berth. The village had bought it for him because he had a warrant, and a warrant meant soldiers, and soldiers meant a rope if they caught him, and everyone agreed that Tomás hanging would be a waste of a perfectly good toll clerk. Sound.

But the math also went like this. Astrid knew things. She'd been copying Brandt's ledgers since spring, and she'd come home from the sheds some evenings with a particular flatness in her face that Tomás had learned, growing up with her, preceded either a confession or a scheme, and lately it preceded both. And whatever she knew, it was big enough that their mother had started standing watch at night, and big enough that Elias — a man who'd crossed open water to escape a war — had stopped talking about leaving, which for Elias was the equivalent of a normal man nailing his boots to the floor.

And the biggest piece of math, the one he kept turning over like a stone in his pocket: if the lists were forged — and he'd known they were forged since before Astrid did, because smuggled letters travel faster than suspicion — then his warrant wasn't a fact. It was a fiction with ink on it. Fictions could die. Fictions died all the time, the moment somebody with standing read them aloud in the right room.

His life was riding on a document that one honest clerk on the mainland could void with a stroke of a pen.

Whereas Elias —

Well. Whereas Elias was a deserter, which was also a rope, but a rope that followed him personally, that didn't reach out and touch the divers, the families, the debt books, the orchard. Elias's danger was his own. Tomás's danger had been manufactured, and manufactured things could be unmade, and the person who knew how to unmake them slept one room away pretending to be asleep.

He got up near dawn and stood at the window, and saw the bridge in the grey light, shut now, its gates closed early, the military order already hung on the tollhouse door in Brandt's tidy hand. Beyond it the strait lay flat and patient, the color of unsold pewter, waiting to be sealed.

* * *

Breakfast was cassava and argument.

"You're going to say it," Tomás said, "so say it."

Astrid buttered nothing, because there was nothing to butter; she simply folded her hands and looked at him across the table with the patience of a woman who had already won. "The berth should carry Elias."

"There it is."

"He can identify the buyer. On the mainland. He told us — the navy man, the labor broker, he's seen both their faces, he's signed papers for both. His testimony voids the lists. Voided lists mean voided conscriptions, voided debts—" she spread her hands, "—voided warrants, Tomás. Your warrant isn't worth the paper if the company's claim collapses, and the company's claim collapses the moment a commission hears him out."

"And until that moment," Tomás said, "he hangs for desertion the second any naval patrol looks at him twice, which they will, because the *Santa Luzia* gets boarded at the mainland pilot station, everyone knows it, they check for exactly one kind of passenger and it's his kind."

"He'll manage it. He managed getting off a warship."

"He managed falling off a warship! There's a difference and it's mostly luck!"

"Lower your voice," Rosa said, without heat, and they both lowered it, twenty-odd years of training operating independently of the argument.

Tomás stood up. Sat back down. Stood up again, because sitting felt like agreeing. "You want to send a half-healed deserter across the strait carrying — what? Whatever it is you've been copying in your collar, yes, don't look surprised, Astrid, you've been wearing that shirt for six weeks straight and sewing at midnight, I'm a toll clerk, I notice cargo — and you want him to walk into a naval inspection with our whole future stitched to his neck, on a bad shoulder, with a face that says 'arrest me' in three languages?"

"He's the proof," Astrid said. "You're the brother. I love you both, but the commission takes proof."

"The commission takes *witnesses who survive the crossing*."

"And so does Mother," said a new voice.

They all turned. Elias had come to the doorway on his bad side, favoring the crooked shoulder, and there was something in his stance that hadn't been there two months ago — not health exactly, but decision, which on this island passed for health often enough.

"No," Tomás said immediately. "No, absolutely not, you don't get a vote, you're a guest."

"I'm the cargo," Elias said. "Cargo votes on routing." He came to the table and sat, slowly, the chair accepting him with a creak. "Listen to me, boy. I fell off that boat because I couldn't make myself go back over the rail toward the guns. I have thought about that rail every day since. Every single day, I lie there and I'm back on it, choosing the water." He looked down at his hands, then up, and his eyes were steady in a way that made Tomás's stomach hurt. "Your sister has been sewing names into her collar for six weeks. Your mother has been lying to Brandt's face for longer than I've been alive here. Your whole family keeps choosing the hard thing, daily, like it's breakfast. I'm not going to sit in this chair and be the reason the one easy choice — send the healthy brother with the paperwork problem — wins out over the right one."

"It's not about winning—"

"Everything's about winning," Elias said. "That's the first thing the army teaches you and the last thing it means."

"Then win later! Win after you can lift your arm above your head! Win on the *next* boat!"

"There is no next boat." Elias said it quietly, and the quiet did what shouting couldn't; it emptied the room of every other sound. "There's this one. Then the strait seals. Then whatever happens here happens in the dark." He glanced at Astrid. "How long until it sails?"

"Thirteen days," she said. "First clear tide after the bridge ceremony. Maybe fewer — the storms are running ahead of the almanac. Mateus says the fish are fat, and fat fish mean early rain."

"Then we have thirteen days," Elias said, "and I intend to spend them becoming the kind of witness who survives a boarding. Which I will. I was good at not being caught before I was bad at staying on ships." Almost, almost, a smile. "And in the meantime, your sister teaches me the names. All of them. If I'm carrying those families across the water, I'll carry them properly, not as paper. As people."

Nobody spoke. Outside, the rain thickened, drumming on the orchard leaves, on the roots that hid things, on the whole green buried economy of the island.

Tomás looked at his sister, at the collar she touched without seeming to touch it, at his mother's face by the fire which had gone into the stillness she saved for decisions already made, and he felt the countdown start ticking somewhere under his ribs, thirteen days and falling, hope with a clock bolted to it.

"All right," he said finally, and hated how easily it came out, and loved everyone at this table too much to take it back. "All right. But I'm negotiating the terms. Nobody puts Elias on that boat until I've bribed the pilot, mapped the boarding routine, and taught him to smile like a man with legitimate business, which currently he cannot do, he smiles like a man confessing."

"I smile fine," Elias said.

"You smile like a funeral."

"That's called gravity."

"It's called a gibbet with a face on it."

Astrid laughed — actually laughed, short and bright, the first one in weeks — and Rosa shooed them all toward the day's work, and the house broke apart into motion, and Tomás stepped out into the wet morning feeling lighter than he had any right to feel, given that he'd just argued himself out of his own rescue.

He was halfway down the orchard path when the light changed.

Not the rain — the rain was constant, had been constant for an hour. It was the wind, coming around hard out of the southwest where the wind didn't come from until monsoon, bending the young trees flat, and down at the shore the fishermen were already hauling boats above the tideline, and Mateus Ferreira was standing at the end of the seawall staring at the sky with the expression of a man doing arithmetic he didn't like.

Tomás counted days in his head. Thirteen.

Looking at that wind, he'd have bet the tollhouse it wasn't thirteen anymore.
