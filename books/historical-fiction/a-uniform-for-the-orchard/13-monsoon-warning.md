## 13. Monsoon Warning

The first sign was the birds.

Astrid had learned to read the island the way she read ledgers — not by the grand entries but by the small ones, the marginalia, the smudged figures that told the true story underneath the tidy one. The pearl beds taught her this. A healthy oyster bed announced itself in a hundred small ways: the clarity of the water at dawn, the way the sand shifted, the particular silence of crabs who had decided somewhere else was better. And on that morning, walking the tideline before her shift, she noticed that every bird on the eastern flats had left.

Not scattered. Left. The egrets, the gulls, even the insolent kites that usually patrolled the fish racks like unpaid overseers — all of them gone inland in one gray river of wings.

"Storm," said Old Ines, who sat on an upturned hull every morning mending nets and pronouncing judgment on the weather, the company, and the moral character of anyone under sixty. "Big one. My knee's been singing since Tuesday."

"Your knee sang in March and we got nothing but drizzle."

"My knee," Ines said with dignity, threading her needle, "has never once lied about anything larger than my age."

By noon the sea had turned the color of pewter, and by late afternoon there was a swell coming in from the southwest that no one could remember seeing so early in the season. The monsoon was supposed to be three weeks away. The bridge was supposed to stay open another month. Every calculation Astrid kept folded inside her head — Tomás's toll shifts, the last boat, the pages sewn into her collar like a second heartbeat — depended on *supposed to*.

She stood at the edge of the drying sheds and watched the water rise into its temper and thought: the ledger is wrong. The whole season is a column that doesn't add up, and someone has already signed off on it without asking us.

* * *

Tomás came home at dusk moving faster than she'd ever seen him, still wearing his tollhouse cap askew, which for Tomás was the equivalent of other men tearing their shirts.

"It's closing," he said, before he'd even cleared the door. "The bridge. Tomorrow morning."

Rosa looked up from the rice pot. "The military order said the end of the month."

"The military order," Tomás said, dropping onto the bench, "is now a piece of paper saying 'effective immediately,' because a gunboat came through at midday flying signals nobody on this island can read except Brandt, and Brandt read them very quickly and then locked himself in his quarters." He laughed, but it was his tollhouse laugh, the one he used on nervous travelers, all surface and no floor. "They're pulling the pontoon sections. Soldiers on both ends. By tomorrow noon you could swim across and they'd shoot you for your trouble."

Astrid felt the arithmetic shift beneath her, cold and fast, like the sand going out from under a wader's feet. "And the boat?"

"That's the part you won't believe."

"I'll believe it. I just won't enjoy it."

"The departure's moved up. Three days. Dawn, day after tomorrow — no, wait." He pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "No. Dawn *tomorrow after next*. They're loading through the night tonight. Captain Ferro says if the strait's not clear of him within four days he loses his hull insurance, and apparently a man's hull insurance matters more than—" He stopped. Looked at his mother. Looked at Astrid. "More than whether anybody on this island gets out at all until dry season."

The kitchen went quiet in the specific way kitchens go quiet when a family is doing sums it doesn't want to do out loud. Rain began against the roof, soft at first, testing, like a clerk running a finger down a column.

"One berth," Rosa said.

"One berth."

"And the whole village pooled for it."

"The whole village pooled for it," Tomás agreed, "and every one of them thinks it's mine. Mrs. Dacosta kissed my cheek at the tollhouse today. Kissed it! I haven't been kissed by Mrs. Dacosta since I was seven and stole her guavas, and even then she followed it with a slipper."

Despite everything — despite the pewter sky and the collapsing calendar — Astrid felt her mouth twitch. "Did you steal her guavas?"

"I redistributed them," Tomás said. "There's a difference. It's a difference she didn't appreciate."

* * *

The compound found out by lamplight, the way it found out everything: door to door, voice to voice, each telling slightly wrong and all of them essentially true. By full dark the panic had taken its familiar shapes. The Dacostas packed everything they owned into baskets and then unpacked half of it when they realized baskets don't float. Senhor Palmeira, who had spent eleven years insisting the mainland was a myth invented by priests and traders, appeared at the Almeidas' door holding a single suitcase and asking, in a voice like a man reading his own eulogy, whether Tomás thought the boat would take passengers with no papers, because his papers had been held as collateral since 1913 and he'd stopped expecting them back around 1914.

Astrid stood in the doorway of her own house and watched the compound come apart in orderly disorder, and what frightened her most was not the noise but the speed. Three days ago there had been a plan with edges. Dry season. The bridge open. Time enough to copy, to sew, to decide. Now the season itself had been called in ahead of schedule, the way Brandt called in debts, the way the navy called in boys, the way everything on this island arrived early except relief.

Time wasn't passing anymore. It was being confiscated.

"You're doing the face," Tomás said beside her. He'd come to stand in the doorway too, shoulder to shoulder, the old childhood arrangement.

"What face?"

"The ledger face. The one where you're adding up something terrible and getting an answer you don't like twice in a row to make sure."

"I'm always doing the ledger face. You once told me I did it in my sleep and recited interest rates."

"You do. It's haunting. But tonight it's worse than usual, so I'll ask plainly, since plain seems to be the only language left this week: what happens to your collar-pages if that boat leaves without us?"

Astrid touched her collar without meaning to. Under her fingertips, through the cloth, the rice paper lay flat and patient, forty-one names in ink fine as eyelash strokes. Her father's name among them, written in her own hand because no one else would ever write it again. "The pages keep," she said. "Pages are patient. It's people who run out of time."

"That's either very wise or a way of not answering me."

"It's both. Most true things are."

Down the lane, Mrs. Dacosta was instructing her sons to bury the good silver under the breadfruit tree, loudly, in front of the entire compound, which defeated the purpose so completely that even the rain seemed to pause out of professional respect. Somewhere a child was crying and someone was singing to it, badly. Lantern light swung between houses like slow lightning. And beyond the roofs, past the orchard where Rosa's rice slept under the roots, the bridge lamps burned in their line across the darkening strait — burning, Astrid knew, for their last night. Tomorrow soldiers would snuff them one by one, west to east, and the island would become a sealed jar.

"We should get Elias inside before the searches start," she said quietly. "Storms make Brandt generous with torches."

* * *

Elias was already in the kitchen when they came in, sitting propped against the wall with his leg stretched out, helping Rosa shell beans in the manner of a man who had clearly never shelled a bean before and was being tactfully supervised. His color was better. The fever had broken two nights ago, and though the wound in his side still pulled at him when he moved, he could sit upright for hours now and had begun, dangerously, to have opinions.

"They're saying the boat leaves in two days," he said, looking up at Astrid. Word traveled even into the drying sheds; the compound's walls were made of gossip and bamboo, mostly gossip. "That's what the wind's carrying, anyway."

"The wind's carrying correctly, for once." Astrid hung her wet shawl on the hook. "Two days. Dawn. One berth."

Elias set down the bean pod he'd been mangling. Something moved behind his eyes — she'd learned to watch for it, the flicker of a man recalculating his own worth — and then Rosa said, briskly, "Well. Then we know the arithmetic," and the flicker was buried alive, and Astrid hated how easily the family buried things, herself included.

"Do we?" Elias asked.

"We know whose warrant is real," Rosa said. "My son's. Everything else is commentary."

Outside, the rain thickened from testing to committed. It came down in ropes now, hammering the palm thatch, filling the barrels, turning the lanes to broth. Astrid listened to it and tried to hear it the way she used to — the sound of the closed season, of rest, of the island folding its hands. Tonight it sounded different. It sounded like a door shutting three weeks early, with the key already thrown into deep water.

Tomás paced. Tomás only paced when he was composing jokes he couldn't say out loud, which meant the jokes were bad and the fear was large. "Here's what I keep thinking," he finally announced. "I keep thinking about the pontoon sections. Somebody has to unbolt those things. That's skilled work. There's maybe six men on this island who know the coupling bolts, and I've sold two of them cigarettes on credit for years, so—"

"No," said Rosa, without looking up from her beans.

"I hadn't said anything yet."

"You were going to suggest unbolting the bridge yourself, or bribing someone to leave it passable, or some other scheme with exactly one flaw that will take us a week to discover and a lifetime to regret. No."

Tomás opened his mouth. Closed it. "It was going to be a *small* scheme," he said, wounded.

"All schemes start small. So do storms." Rosa nodded at the roof, where the rain had reached full argument. "Look what this one did to the calendar."

Astrid caught Elias watching her across the lamplight. He had that stillness he got sometimes, the soldier's stillness, the kind that wasn't calm at all but attention compressed into a small space. When Rosa stepped out to check the rain barrels, he leaned forward, wincing, and lowered his voice.

"Astrid. If it comes down to the berth — and it will, because the world is cruelly simple that way—"

"Don't," she said.

"—then you should know something before anyone makes anyone else's decision for them. There's a thing I haven't told you yet. About why my testimony matters more than you think. About a name I saw, in a place I shouldn't have been, that goes higher than Brandt." He glanced toward the door, toward the rain, toward the bridge lamps invisible now behind the weather. "And I need to tell it while I still have the nerve, because nerve, I've discovered, is like this monsoon. It arrives when it wants and leaves early, and you'd best use it while it's here."

The lamp guttered in a draft. Astrid stood very still, her hand at her collar, her brother pacing his cage of unsaid jokes, the rain erasing the road home hour by hour, and understood that the last of her time had just walked into her kitchen and sat down against the wall.

"Then tell me now," she said. "While the beans are still listening."
