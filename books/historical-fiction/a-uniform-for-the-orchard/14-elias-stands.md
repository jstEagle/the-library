## 14. Elias Stands

The first thing I could do again was sit up. The second thing I could do was regret it.

Three weeks in the drying sheds, or whatever this room behind Rosa's kitchen truly was — I had stopped asking questions after the second week, when Astrid explained that questions were a currency and I was broke. My shoulder had gone from a red ruin to a purple ruin to something the color of old tea, and when I pushed myself upright against the wall, the whole left side of me filed a formal complaint.

"You're sitting," said Tomás, who appeared in doorways the way weather appears over water: suddenly, and usually with bad intentions. "Congratulations. At this rate you'll be walking by Christmas."

"When is Christmas here?"

"Nobody's asked before." He considered it. "We'll invent one. Any festival is an excuse for Rosa to kill a chicken."

He was gone before I could answer, which seemed to be his way. I got the impression he rationed himself around me — two minutes of talk, then off to the tollhouse or the mangroves or wherever it was a boy went to smuggle letters and pretend he wasn't afraid of anything. But those two minutes were the brightest part of my day, and I was ashamed of how much I looked forward to them, the way a man adrift looks forward to gulls. Gulls don't mean land. They just mean the world still exists somewhere.

I stood on the fourth day of sitting. It took eleven minutes, most of which were spent negotiating with my legs like a merchant haggling over spoiled fruit. *You carried a rifle through three provinces,* I told them. *You can cross this room.*

My legs said: *that was another man.*

It was true, though. That was the trouble. The man who had walked onto that transport boat at the naval yard — nineteen years old, boots too big, mother's medal sewn into his collar because she'd made him promise — that man had jumped ship in the strait with shrapnel in his shoulder and no plan beyond *away*. I had spent three weeks being remade by rice porridge and Rosa's terrible singing, and somewhere in the remaking I had become someone who flinched at his own reflection. Not because of the wound. Because of what the wound was *for*.

Or rather, what it wasn't for. A hero gets wounded holding a line. A coward gets wounded falling off a boat.

I made it across the room. Then I opened the door and stepped into the orchard light, which hit me like a debt collector.

* * *

Rosa found me leaning against a mango tree ten minutes later, sweating as if I'd swum the strait instead of crossing thirty feet of packed earth.

"So," she said. "Walking."

"Barely."

"Sit before you fall. There's dignity in choosing your ground." She lowered me back down with hands that were small and hard as boat cleats, and put a cup of something ginger-hot into mine. "Astrid said you'd try today. She said you're stubborn."

"She talks about me?"

"She counts things about you. How many steps. How much you eat. Whether your fever comes back." Rosa settled beside me with the ease of a woman who had been sitting under trees judging men her whole life. "She keeps ledgers, that girl. Even in her head. Even about people."

"I noticed."

"Do you know what she wrote down about you, first week? When you were half dead?" I shook my head. "She wrote: *breathes shallow, lies about pain.* That's all. Two entries. And from those two entries she decided we keep you." Rosa sipped her drink. "In my experience, that's a better character reference than any priest would give you. Priests only see what you confess. Astrid sees what you can't help."

The ginger burned going down and I let it. "What if what I can't help is running away?"

Rosa looked at me for a long moment. Out past the orchard rows, the company's drying racks stood empty in the rain-shadowed light, and somewhere beyond them the pearl beds lay flat and silver under the bruised sky, where men dove until their ears bled so that a company in another country could weigh their lungs on a scale.

"Then you're honest," she said finally. "Honest cowards are more useful than brave liars. I've buried brave liars." She stood, brushing off her skirt. "Supper's at dark. If you're going to be dramatic about your leg, do it before the rice runs out."

* * *

Astrid came at dusk, as she always did, carrying the day's small intelligence like a bird carrying twigs. News moved through that compound the way water moves through cloth — slowly, invisibly, saturating everything — and she was the one who wrung it out.

"The bridge closed early," she said, sitting cross-legged across from me. "Military order. Four weeks ahead of season. Tomás heard it from a corporal who was drunk enough to talk and sober enough to be believed."

"That fast?"

"That fast. Which means the last boat leaves sooner than anyone planned, and there are more people who need to be on it than seats." She said it flatly, the way she said everything, but I had learned by now to read the ledger beneath the words. Her fingers were moving against her knee — counting. Always counting.

"Astrid." I waited until she looked up. "There's something I have to tell you. Both of you. Is Tomás—"

"On his way." As if summoned, the boy dropped through the window rather than using the door, which I suspected was less about stealth than about style. He landed, dusted his hands, and sat. "Reporting for secrets. What did I miss?"

"Nothing yet." My mouth had gone dry. I took the ginger cup Astrid handed me without looking at her, because looking at her while saying this felt like handing over a hostage.

So I told them.

"You know I was on a supply transport out of the northern yard. You know I went into the water. What you don't know — what I haven't said — is why I was on that boat at all." I turned the cup in my hands. "I wasn't crew. I was escort for a passenger. A broker. Civilian clothes, navy papers, a chest he never let out of arm's reach. Everyone assumed gold. It wasn't gold."

Astrid had gone very still. Stillness, on her, was loud.

"He was coming here," I said. "To your island. To buy names. Divers' names — men whose conscription papers the company held. He buys them twice over: sells the same man to the navy press-gang and to the labor brokers upcoast, collects both payments, and the family never sees a coin of either. The man just disappears into one ledger and out of the other, and nobody ever asks where he went, because everyone who might ask has already been paid."

"How do you know this?" Astrid asked. Quiet. Terrible quiet.

"Because I was assigned to guard him, and guarding someone means listening to them, and he liked to talk. He talked to me because I was nineteen and he thought nineteen meant stupid." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "He was right about half of it."

"And the other half?"

"The other half is that I memorized his face, his name — Verhoeven, he called himself, though I'd bet the company's whole warehouse that's not the name on his real papers — and the harbor office on the mainland where he banks. And I could stand in front of any commission, any court, any naval board you care to name, and say: *this is the buyer, this is the account, this is the route.*" I set the cup down because my hands weren't steady enough to hold it anymore. "Testimony like that doesn't embarrass Brandt. Testimony like that voids every list he ever sold. Every forged conscription. Every indenture built on top of them. Including—"

"Including ours," Astrid said softly.

"Including yours."

Tomás whistled, long and low, the way boys whistle when they've seen a wave bigger than they want to admit. "So you're worth more than the cargo chest after all."

"Tomás," Astrid said.

"What? It's a compliment."

But I barely heard him, because the thing underneath everything had surfaced now, and it had to be said or it would drown me quieter things. "There's a problem," I said.

"There's always a problem," said Tomás. "Problems are our chief export."

"If I go to the mainland — if I testify — the first thing they ask is my name. The second thing they ask is my unit. And when I answer honestly, they arrest me before I finish the sentence." I looked at my own hands, at the knuckles that had gripped a rail and then let go. "Desertion. In wartime. They hang men for less than what I did, and what I did was jump."

The room got very quiet. Rain started against the roof, soft at first, then committed.

"You didn't jump because you were lazy," Astrid said. "Or thieving. You jumped because—"

"Because I was afraid." I said it plainly, because I had promised myself that if I ever told this story, I would tell it without decoration. "The transport was going to a station I'd heard about from men coming back. What happened to divers there — to conscripted men sent into cold water until their hearts gave out. I'd guarded men on that deck who were going exactly there, and they knew it, and they were calm about it, calmer than me, and something in watching them be calm made me—" I stopped. Started again. "I couldn't do it. So I didn't. And I've spent every day since telling myself the arithmetic works out. One deserter saved versus however many men I abandoned to their posting. Some nights the math favors me. Most nights it doesn't."

Tomás was uncharacteristically silent. Astrid watched me with those eyes that tallied everything.

"So if I testify," I finished, "they may free your families. And they will certainly hang me. Or shoot me. Whichever the board finds more efficient. And I need you to understand that I'm telling you this anyway, because—" My voice did something embarrassing. "Because I've been a coward for a year and I'm tired of renting rooms inside it."

* * *

Tomás spoke first, which surprised me.

"You know what my father used to say?" He was picking at a splinter in the floorboard, not looking at me. "About fear. He worked the beds, before. He said every man who goes down thinks, right at the moment his head goes under, *maybe I won't come back up.* And the ones who come back aren't the ones who didn't think it. They're the ones who thought it and dove anyway." He glanced up. "You jumped off a boat with a hole in you and swam to a strange island. That's not nothing. That's diving."

"It's different."

"Everything's different when you're the one in the water."

"Your father," Astrid said quietly, and Tomás shut up as if a hand had covered his mouth. Something passed between them — a whole submerged conversation, years deep. I understood suddenly that the drowned father was not a closed account in this house. He was an open one. Nobody had told me how he died, and nobody was going to tonight.

Rosa appeared in the doorway with supper and ended the silence the way she ended everything: by feeding it. We ate rice and dried fish and a pickle so fierce it renegotiated my relationship with my sinuses, and nobody talked about hanging or harbors or the war, and it was the best meal I could remember, which tells you something about the meals I could remember.

Afterward, Astrid stayed behind while the others drifted off. The lamp was trimmed low. Outside, the monsoon rehearsed its opening act.

"I need to ask you something," she said, "and I need the real answer, not the brave one."

"I don't have a brave one."

"Good. Then this should be easy." She folded her hands. "If it came down to it — if the only way your testimony reached the commission was you standing in front of soldiers who had every reason to arrest you on sight — would you do it? Not *could*. Could is easy, everyone can die in theory. Would you walk in?"

I thought about lying. Old habit; I'd gotten good at it; deserters live by it. But she had written *lies about pain* in some inner ledger of hers, and I had a sudden horror of being accurately recorded forever.

"I don't know," I said. "That's the truth. A month ago, no. Today—" I turned my shoulder toward her, the ruined one, the map of everything I'd run from. "Today I think yes. But I've thought yes before and been wrong. Fear doesn't announce itself, Astrid. It shows up dressed as common sense. *Be practical. Be careful. Live to fight another day.* It always sounds reasonable right up until you realize you've reasoned yourself into a corner you'll never leave."

She nodded slowly. "My father used to say the water keeps what you give it. Everything else, you carry." She stood, took the lamp. "Get strong, Elias. Not brave. Strong. Brave is just strength that arrived on time."

She paused at the door.

"One more thing. Tomás heard something at the tollhouse today. The last boat — the one before the strait seals — it has one berth left. Village money bought it. Everyone assumes it's for him, because there's a warrant out." Her voice stayed level, but I saw her thumb pressing against her forefinger, counting something invisible. "One berth. And now, apparently, two passengers' worth of reasons to use it."

"Then it's simple," I said. "It's his. He's the one they'll hang tomorrow. I'm the one they'll hang eventually. Eventually is generous."

"It's not simple," Astrid said. "Nothing on this island has ever been simple. That's the company's whole trick — making everything complicated enough that decent people give up on the arithmetic." She lifted the lamp, and her face went gold and shadowed above the flame. "But we don't give up on the arithmetic in this house. We do it. All of it. Down to the last column."

She left me alone with the rain and my heart hammering out its dishonest rhythm.

I lay awake a long time doing sums I couldn't balance: one berth, one warrant, one witness, three lives, and a commission on the mainland that might never convene unless a ragged deserter walked into it and named a name. Somewhere past midnight, the answer assembled itself out of all the pieces I'd been refusing to look at together, and it was so obvious I almost laughed.

They were going to ask me to take the berth. I could feel it coming like weather off the strait.

And for the first time since I'd gone into the water — since I'd let go of that rail with the shrapnel singing in my shoulder — I found I wanted to be the one who deserved the asking.
