## 15. What Can Be Carried

The rain had been falling for two days without conviction — a thin, gray drizzle that couldn't decide whether to be a monsoon or merely a bad mood — and I spent it the way I spent most weather that kept me indoors: taking stock.

This was an old habit. My father taught it to me before he taught me letters, back when I was small enough to sit on his knee in the pearl boat and he would ask, *Astrid, if the sea took everything tomorrow, what could you carry?* And I would name things. A comb. A mango. Once, memorably, I named the cat, and he laughed so hard he nearly dropped an oar.

He never got to teach me what to do when the sea actually took everything. I had to work that out myself.

So on that gray afternoon, with Tomás out at the tollhouse pretending the bridge still mattered and Rosa in the orchard talking to trees as though they answered, I laid my life out on the sleeping mat and counted it like a ledger.

Item one: the knife.

It was a plain thing — a diver's knife with a bone handle worn smooth by two generations of palms, my father's before mine. The blade had been sharpened so many times it had gone from a broad wedge to something narrow and patient, like a sentence pared down to its last word. I kept it wrapped in oilcloth under the loose brick by the hearth, because a girl who carried a knife openly on Verrone Island got questions, and questions were a currency Brandt collected.

I unwrapped it now just to look at it. It wasn't worth anything. That was the point. It was worth nothing to anyone but me, which made it the only truly safe object I owned.

Item two: the ledger stub.

Not the real ledgers — those lived in the counting house under Brandt's eye, chained to their shelf like prisoners who'd learned to enjoy it. I meant my stubs: the torn-off corners of scrap paper where I practiced columns at night, copying figures from memory until they lived in my head even if the paper burned. Debts owed by families to the company. Days worked. Pearls found and pearls *recorded* as found — two numbers that had stopped agreeing years ago, though only I seemed to have noticed the space growing between them.

I had memorized four hundred and eleven entries. I sometimes recited them to myself when I couldn't sleep, the way other people said prayers. It was not comforting, exactly. But it was true, and truth has its own rough comfort, like a blanket made of nettle cloth.

Item three: my father's drowned name.

You cannot put this on a mat. You cannot weigh it or wrap it in oilcloth. But it was there anyway, sitting among the objects like a stone that looked like all the other stones until you tried to lift it. *Joaquim Almeida.* Lost off the eastern beds eight years ago, recorded in the company's books as an accident of weather, mourned by no column, compensated by nothing. My mother had grieved him the way she did everything — silently, thoroughly, and into the ground, literally, planting a sapling over the spot in the orchard where she said his heart had come to rest. Trees grow better over secrets. Everyone on this island knows that; nobody says it.

* * *

"You're doing the face," Tomás said.

I looked up from my inventory. He had come in dripping, shaking rain off his shoulders like a dog, and was now standing over my carefully arranged life with the expression of a man appraising fish at market.

"What face?"

"The face you make when you're counting things that aren't in front of you." He dropped onto the mat, narrowly missing the knife's oilcloth bundle, and I moved it without comment. "Mother says you do it in your sleep. Says you muttle numbers."

"I do not *muttle*."

"You muttle. Last night you said 'four hundred and eleven' three times and then apologized to someone called Ferreira."

Ferreira. Entry two hundred and six. Owed the company nine months of labor that the ledger said he'd served and the man himself swore he hadn't, because the days had been sold twice — once north to the navy, once west to the broker — and only one of those sales had been crossed off. I apologized to Ferreira in my sleep because I couldn't apologize to him awake. Apologizing awake meant explaining how I knew, and knowing was a thing that, once shared, became a thing that could be taken from you by men with keys.

"Tomás," I said, "how much does the berth cost?"

He went very still, the way he always did when a subject he'd been circling for days finally landed in front of him. My brother was many things — quick, funny, brave in the specific way of people who don't think past the next ten minutes — but subtle was not among them. For a week he'd been wearing hope the way you wear a shirt that doesn't fit yet: constantly adjusting it, tugging it down whenever someone looked.

"The village pool came to thirty-one dollars and forty cents," he said. "Old Marta gave her church tin. The Furtados sold a goat. Don't ask me which goat." He grinned, then made the grin do the work of hiding what was underneath it. "One berth. The *Marisol*, day after tomorrow if the strait lets her, and after that—" He drew a finger across his throat, cheerfully. "Closed till dry season next year. Sealed island. No boats, no bridge, no post, nothing but us and Brandt and the rain."

"And you'll be on it."

"Everyone assumes I'll be on it." He picked up one of my ledger stubs, examined it upside down, set it down. "Warrant with my name on it, sister. There's a soldier at every dock who can read well enough to manage five letters."

"Then you'll be on it."

"That was the plan, yes."

"It's a good plan."

"It's a *great* plan. It's the best plan anyone on this island has had since—" he waved a hand at the general catastrophe of our lives— "ever."

But he said it too fast, and we both heard it, and neither of us said anything else for a while. Outside, the drizzle thickened, gathered an opinion, and began properly raining. Somewhere down the slope, the drying sheds creaked against their ropes. In the drying sheds, wrapped in sacking behind the salt barrels, a man was lying very quiet and running a fever that Rosa measured twice a day with her palm and lied about with her mouth.

Elias. Item four, I supposed. Except item four didn't belong to me. That was the trouble with people — they refused to stay inventoried.

* * *

Rosa came in at dusk smelling of wet leaves and woodsmoke, and without a word she began cooking, because that is what my mother does instead of speaking. Rice, of course — rice from roots I was probably not supposed to know about, though I'd known since I was twelve and had maintained the fiction of ignorance ever since, which I believe is the truest gift children give their parents.

She fed us. She watched us eat with that particular maternal arithmetic — measuring how much worry each of us was carrying by how much food we could get down — and when Tomás left for the night watch at the tollhouse, she sat across from me and folded her hands.

"You've arranged your things again," she said.

"How do you—"

"You do it when the world gets heavy. You line it all up like you're going somewhere." Her eyes went to the mat, to the knife, to the stubs, to the collar of my old work shirt hanging on its peg — the collar I had been sewing in secret for weeks, tiny stitches, rice-paper pages folded to the width of a fingernail pressed flat between fabric layers. Four hundred and eleven entries, copied smaller than ants, riding against my neck like a second pulse.

She knew. Of course she knew. My mother had known things about me before I knew them myself for as long as I could remember. It was her particular genius, along with orchards and silence.

"Astrid." She said my name the way she said it maybe twice a year — fully, with the accent my father had given it, *Ah-STREED*, rolling the middle like water over stones. "There is one boat."

"I know."

"There is one berth."

"I know, Mama."

"And three of you who cannot stay." She held up fingers, unhurried. "Tomás, with his warrant. Elias, with his fever and his uniform hidden in the rice. And you, with whatever it is you've been sewing into your collar since the Feast of Saint Anne." She let the fingers fall. "Three. One berth. I have done sums longer than you have been alive, minha filha, and I am telling you that this sum does not resolve politely."

"Then it resolves impolitely," I said, and heard how young I sounded, and hated it.

My mother looked at me for a long moment. Rain drummed the roof. The lamp guttered and steadied itself.

"Do you know why I stayed on this island after your father drowned?" she asked. "I could have gone to my cousin in Colombo. There was room on a boat. There was money enough, barely. Instead I signed myself deeper into Verrone's debt — a decade of labor in their orchard — in exchange for one thing only." Her voice didn't waver. It never wavered; wavering was for people who could afford it. "They agreed to let you attend the mission school. Paper and slate and half-days free. A bargain: my silence and my back, for your letters."

"I know."

"You know, but you have never asked me what I bought with it. So I will tell you. I bought a daughter who can read a ledger and see the lie inside it. I spent ten years buying exactly you." She reached across and touched my collar, just barely, one fingertip against the stitched seam, feather-light, the way you touch a bird to see if it's alive. "So understand me. Whatever you are carrying in here — I did not spend a decade so that you would carry it onto a sinking argument at a crowded dock. And I did not spend it so you would burn it either. Do you hear me? It must go across. It must reach whoever on that mainland still owns a conscience. But *who carries it* —" she withdrew her hand — "is a sum we have not finished."

* * *

I couldn't sleep, so I did what I always do. I took inventory.

But this time I did it differently. This time I did it the way Brandt would — coldly, in columns, with a price beside every line — because if I was going to solve my mother's impolite sum, I needed to think like the enemy, and thinking like the enemy, I've found, mostly means being honest about weights.

The knife: portable. Divisible from me at no cost to anyone but me. If Tomás needed a blade on the mainland, he could take mine and I would not be less myself. Verdict: transferable.

The ledger stubs: worthless as paper. The value lived in my skull, and skulls travel badly when their owners drown. Verdict: I could copy them, sew them, give them away — the knowledge was already divisible. I had been dividing it for weeks.

The collar of names: divisible by design. That was the whole point of sewing it in pages. Tear it apart and each piece still told part of the truth; assemble them anywhere in the world and the whole truth reassembled itself. It was built to survive being split. It was the most generous object I owned.

My father's drowned name: non-transferable. But also — and here the cold column surprised me — non-essential to the crossing. Grief travels with you or waits for you; it does not need a berth.

Tomás: one warrant. One berth. The village had bled goats and church tins for that berth with his name spoken aloud over the collection plate, and a warrant, unlike grief, *expires* — it dies the moment the company loses the power to enforce it, and the company loses that power the moment Elias stands in front of the commission and says *here are the names, here are the sales, here is the buyer, count them twice.*

Elias: one testimony. Worth more than any single life on this island, because it wasn't one life at all. It was four hundred and eleven entries. It was Ferreira. It was every family in the compound holding a page of dismantled ledger under a floorboard, praying. His body was merely the delivery method.

And me?

I ran the column down to the bottom line and found it waiting for me, patient as arithmetic.

The berth was the only thing in my entire inventory that could not be divided. Not transferred, not copied, not sewn into a collar, not memorized. One plank seat on one boat through one closing strait. Everything else I owned — every true thing, every dangerous thing — could be cut into pieces and scattered and still survive. The berth alone was whole, and singular, and mortal. It would die at dawn on a dock if it wasn't given entirely to one person.

Which meant the question was never *who escapes*. That was the question Tomás joked about and the soldiers would ask. The real question, the one my mother had left on the table between us like an unturned card, was *what does the truth weigh, and who can carry it farthest?*

And the answer sat in a drying shed behind the salt barrels, feverish, afraid, wearing the borrowed shape of my dead father's shirt, holding inside him the only words that could break Brandt's arithmetic forever.

I lay in the dark and felt the choice settle over me — not arriving dramatically, the way it does in the mission school's novels, but the way rain arrives: gradually, then completely, until you look down and realize you're soaked through and have been for some time.

I would send Elias. I would keep the master pages against my ribs and wait out the rains on a sealed island with a man who owned my debts and suspected my family, and I would do it because the math was merciless and the math was right.

Tomorrow, I thought. Tomorrow I would tell Tomás, and he would refuse, and it would be terrible, and I would win, because I had been keeping ledgers since I was nine years old and I had never once lost an argument with a column of numbers.

I was almost asleep when I heard my mother rise quietly in the next room, dress in the dark, and slip out the door toward the overseers' quarter — and I lay very still, listening to her footsteps fade down the wet slope, and understood that while I had been solving my sum, my mother had gone out to begin solving hers.

Where, I did not yet know. What she intended, I could not guess. But I knew the sound Rosa Almeida makes when she goes to pay a debt, and it was that sound, walking away from me into the rain.
