## 16. Rosa's Bargain Renewed

The night before she went to see Brandt, Rosa Almeida did what she always did before a negotiation: she made soup.

Not because Brandt would taste it — he wouldn't, the man had a palate like wet cardboard and considered pepper an act of rebellion — but because her hands needed work that was honest while her head planned something that wasn't. Her mother had taught her that much about bargaining on this island: *never walk into a room with empty hands, even if the hands are only holding soup.* It gave you something to set down. Something to look at instead of his eyes when he lied to you, which left your own face free to lie back.

She peeled taro with the small knife Astrid kept sharpened for her, and she thought about the shape of the lie she meant to sell.

* * *

Rosa had been selling things to the Verrone Trading Company for nineteen years — pearls by proxy, fruit at fixed prices, silence at no price at all but the one she'd named herself. She knew how men like Brandt bought. They didn't want flattery; they wanted confirmation of their own cleverness. They wanted to feel that they'd won something, so you had to leave a little of yourself bleeding on the table or they got suspicious.

And they wanted information. Brandt above all wanted information, the way some men wanted drink — not for any use it could be put to, but for the feeling of having swallowed it.

That was the door she intended to walk through.

Because here was the trouble, laid out plain as laundry on a line: the duplicate ledger was gone from Brandt's quarters, cut into forty-one pieces, and those pieces were sleeping in the flour tins, the hymnals, the roof-thatch, and one memorable instance the hollowed-out figurehead of a decommissioned ferry, of eleven households across the compound. If Brandt searched them all methodically, he would find them. Not all at once — that was the genius of the cutting, Astrid's idea, and Rosa had felt her chest swell with a pride she hadn't been able to show — but in time. And time was the one thing they did not have, because the boat had sailed, the bridge was shut, and Elias's testimony was now worth nothing until the armistice commission actually came, whenever that might be, if ever.

So somebody had to make sure Brandt searched slowly. Carefully. In the wrong places first.

Somebody had to become, in his eyes, a woman who could be bought.

Rosa set down the peeling knife. "Well," she said to the empty kitchen, in the tone she used for announcing unpleasant chores, "it may as well be me."

* * *

Astrid objected, naturally. Rosa had expected the objection the way she expected the monsoon — not gladly, but without surprise.

"You can't," Astrid said. She'd come in from the drying sheds with rain still beaded on her shoulders, and she stood there dripping on the good mat like a righteous accusation. "If he suspects you—"

"He already suspects us. All of us. He's simply too proud to say so." Rosa ladled soup into the covered pot she'd take as her offering. "Suspicion is a fire, my love. Left alone it spreads through everything. But if you feed it something small — a rat, a rumor — it eats that and sleeps."

"And what do you feed him?"

"Me." She said it lightly, the way she said *we're out of salt*, and watched her daughter's face go tight. "Oh, sit down, Astrid, I'm not offering him anything but talk. I'm fifty-one years old and built like a breadfruit tree. The man finds me terrifying; I've seen him cross the road to avoid my opinions on his drainage ditches." That earned her half a laugh, grudging, which was all she'd asked for. "What I offer is this: I tell him I've heard whispers in the orchard. Families talking about hiding things before searches come. And I offer to listen for him. For a price."

"A price."

"An easing of our indenture terms. A reduction of the debt. Small enough to be believable — greedy women ask for believable things — and written down." Rosa tapped the table with one finger. "Paper is the whole trick, Astrid. A man who writes down a bribe has confessed it twice: once to me, once to himself. After that he can't betray me without confessing his own crime. It's the same arithmetic you use in the ledgers. Every entry balances."

Astrid sat down slowly. "He'll test you. He'll feed you false information and watch where it goes."

"Yes." Rosa smiled, and was aware that her smile frightened people who weren't her children. "So I'll move his false information somewhere harmless and let him find it there. He'll think his bait worked. A man whose bait works once believes in his own fishing forever."

For a moment her daughter just looked at her — that long, measuring look Astrid had inherited, God help them both, from her father — and then she said, quietly, "You've done this before. Bargaining with him."

"I married your father, didn't I? Same skill, gentler opponent." Rosa stood and wrapped the pot in cloth. "Go check on Elias's bandage. And Astrid — don't wait up. Negotiations run late when both parties enjoy pretending they're cleverer than they are."

* * *

Brandt received her in his quarters at nine o'clock, under the lamp he kept trimmed too bright because he believed brightness discouraged dishonesty. Rosa had found in nineteen years that brightness did nothing of the kind; it merely showed everyone's pores. His quarters smelled of camphor and ink, and the shelf where the duplicate ledger had lived held a gap so conspicuous it might as well have been draped in black crepe.

"Mrs. Almeida." He gestured at the chair across from his desk without rising. "Soup?"

"For you. Taro and crabapple. My own recipe." She set it down between them, establishing, per maternal doctrine, neutral ground.

"I didn't summon you."

"No. I summoned myself." She folded her hands in her lap and looked at him with the expression she used on hens that had stopped laying: patient, faintly disappointed, willing to wait out any amount of foolishness. "Overseer Brandt, I am going to speak plainly, and you are going to want to throw me out, and then you are going to hear me out, because I know something you need and you know I know it."

His jaw did its little working motion. "You forget yourself."

"I remember myself very well, thank you. That's rather the point." She leaned forward. "There is talk in the compound. Quiet talk — the kind that stops when a cart passes. About families moving things. Hiding things ahead of your searches. And about a boat that left carrying more than its manifest said." She let that last one hang, watching him decide whether to pretend surprise. He decided to pretend surprise, badly. "I have lived on this island longer than your company has owned its docks, Mr. Brandt. I grow the fruit you serve at your dinners. Women tell an orchard-keeper things they'd never tell a man with a ledger, because an orchard-keeper's hands are dirty and her memory is assumed to be poor."

"And you wish to sell these memories."

"I wish to trade them. There's a difference, and the difference is paper." She produced, from her sleeve, the sheet she and Astrid had drafted three times — Astrid insisting on clauses, Rosa insisting on plainness. "My family's debt reduced by one-fifth, entered formally against the company rolls, signed by you, dated today. In exchange I bring you word of movements, meetings, and hidings among the indentured families. Weekly. Beginning now."

Brandt looked at the paper for a long time. Then he looked up, and there it was — the thing she'd come to harvest. Not suspicion. *Relief.* A man drowning in a missing ledger being thrown a rope, and never quite asking who was holding the other end.

"You understand," he said, "that if this is a trap—"

"Mr. Brandt. I am a widow with two children and a debt. Traps are for people who can afford to close them." She let a real tiredness into her voice, which cost her nothing, since she genuinely was tired. "I am buying my children's futures with gossip. Judge me as harshly as you like afterward. Sign first."

He signed. Rosa watched the pen move and thought, absurdly, of her husband, who had once told her over the pearl beds that the company would fall the day someone made its own paperwork testify against it. *You're doing it with his signature instead,* she thought. *I hope that counts.*

It counted. It would count.

* * *

She fed him his first morsel that same night, and she fed it to him exactly as designed.

"There are rice caches under the old casuarina rows, west end of the orchard," she told him, in the tone of a woman betraying neighbors she'd known thirty years. "Three families. The Ferreiras, mostly. Move on them soon, before the rain softens the ground and the roots rot the evidence."

All of which was true, except for the parts that mattered. There *were* caches under the casuarinas — Rosa had put them there herself, four days ago, deliberately shallow, containing nothing more damning than rice, salt fish, and one carefully aged letter of complaint about rations that any family on the island might have written. Let Brandt dig up hunger. Hunger proved nothing except that the island was hungry, which he already knew, and finding contraband rice would satisfy him the way a bone satisfies a dog: thoroughly, and without nourishment.

Two days later she watched from the orchard rows as his men dug up the caches and Brandt stood over them looking satisfied, cataloguing sacks, and Rosa bent to her weeding and allowed herself one long slow breath.

The hook was set. Now came the part she liked less — keeping the line taut week after week, reeling out truth and lie braided together so finely that even she sometimes lost track of which was which. She gave him a meeting that had happened. She withheld a meeting that hadn't. She described the drying sheds' new watch rotation accurately and attributed it to the wrong family, and watched suspicion settle on the Baptiste brothers like a comfortable coat, and hated herself for it, and noted in her heart's private ledger a debt owed to the Baptistes that no company roll would ever record but that she fully intended to pay — in fruit, in silence, in whatever coin the years after this demanded.

Maternal sacrifice, her daughter might have called it. Rosa rejected the phrase. Sacrifice implied loss offered up to heaven. This was none of that. This was strategy wearing an apron. She was not giving anything away that couldn't be manufactured again; she was renting out her reputation, and reputations, unlike daughters, regrew.

Though there were nights — she'd admit this to nobody, least of all the soup pot — when the weight of the performance sat on her chest like the damp. The night she had to drink tea in Brandt's quarters and smile at his theories about "native ingratitude." The night he mentioned, casually, testingly, that a warrant existed for one Tomás Almeida, tollhouse smuggling, and studied her face while saying it.

Rosa's face, trained by nineteen years of company scrutiny, did nothing at all. Inside, something went cold and quick, like a fish turning in deep water.

"A warrant," she repeated, mildly, as though discussing weather. "For Tomás. Whatever for — the boy can't count past his fingers."

"The boy," Brandt said, "has moved more letters than the mainland post office."

"Then the mainland post office should hire him. At least there he'd be paid." She laughed, short and scornful, a mother's laugh defending a son's honor through sheer incredulity, and she saw him file her reaction away as authentic, because it was — every scrap of it true except the fear underneath, which she buried so deep even she couldn't find it until later, alone, in the dark, with her hands pressed flat against her sternum counting her own heartbeats the way Astrid counted debts.

* * *

The weeks turned. Rain came daily now, drumming on the orchard leaves like impatient fingers. Rosa walked her rows, fed her informant's fiction, and waited for the armistice news that Elias swore was coming, that the whole island whispered about, that seemed to recede the way the horizon recedes when you sail toward it.

And then, on a grey morning thick enough to chew, she saw the launch come around the point flying navy colors — not the usual patrol, but a heavier boat, a boat with an officer on deck consulting papers, and behind it, unmistakable even at distance, a pinnace full of soldiers in wet khaki standing up to see the shore better.

Rosa straightened slowly, dirt on her knees, her heart going quiet in that particular way it had learned — not racing, but dropping, sinking to some deep hold below thought where decisions got made before the mind caught up.

They were coming ashore at the compound dock. The officer had a list. She could tell, even from here, that he had a list.

She pulled off her gardening gloves finger by finger, tucked them in her apron, and started walking toward the house — not hurrying, because hurrying tells stories, walking calmly, the walk of a woman with nothing hidden anywhere, thinking all the while, fast and clear as a bell: *Tomás. They've come for Tomás.*
