## 17. Tomás Learns the Truth

Tomás Almeida had a theory about tollhouses: they were the only places on the island where a man could be three people at once before breakfast. By dawn he was the company's clerk of the bridge, stamping passes and counting carts; by midmorning he was the smuggler of letters, tucking folded paper into hollowed bamboo poles and into the linings of fishwives' baskets; and by the time the sun climbed high enough to make the tin roof sing, he was simply Tomás, a young man with a warrant out for his arrest who had learned to whistle while he worked because whistling men looked like men with nothing to hide.

He was whistling — badly, something half-remembered from a mainland steamer's band — when his sister came up the tollhouse steps carrying a basket of guavas she could not possibly have picked herself.

"You're supposed to be mending nets," he said.

"Rosa said I was supposed to stay in the orchard," Astrid corrected, setting the basket on his counting table with a care that made his stomach drop. Astrid handled ordinary things carelessly — dropped spoons, lost sandals, a hat blown off in every wind on the island. She only handled things gently when they mattered.

"That's worse," he said. "Mum sent you out under escort?"

"Mum doesn't know I left." She glanced at old Ferreira dozing in the corner chair, the one duty assigned to him that year because his eyes were going and his hands still weren't. "Ferreira, would you walk to the water tank and see if it wants filling? It always wants filling."

"It does want filling," Ferreira agreed, hauling himself up with the dignity of a man who had been waiting all morning for someone to give him an errand. "Rain's coming. Everything wants filling."

They waited until his footsteps faded down the stairs. Then Astrid did something she had never once done in her life inside a company building: she shut the door.

Tomás put down his pen. "I'm either about to be arrested or hugged," he said. "In this family those look identical."

"Sit down."

"I'm sitting." He was already sitting. He'd been sitting for two hours over a column of cart-tariffs that refused to add up, which he now suspected was the whole island's condition in miniature.

Astrid reached into her collar.

He had seen her do it before — the little gesture, fingers hooking the fabric at her throat as though checking for lice or loose thread. He'd assumed it was a habit, the way their mother crossed herself twice at doorways. But now she unpicked three stitches with a fingernail, and from inside the seam of her collar she drew a strip of rice paper so fine it was nearly translucent, folded down to the size of a postage stamp, and opened it on his tariff ledger like a woman laying a winning hand on a church table.

"What am I looking at?" he asked, and then he read it, and stopped being able to ask questions for a long time.

It was names. Divers' names — he knew half of them, men whose shoulders he'd slapped at the jetty, men whose daughters he'd carried letters for. Beside each name were two columns. One column said NAVY. The other said NORTHERN LABOR BROKER. And beside both columns, in a neat clerk's hand that Tomás recognized with a physical sickness in his gut, were figures. Prices. The same name, sold twice. The same man, paid for by two buyers, delivered to neither, kept diving pearls on an island that officially believed him gone.

"Where did you—" He couldn't finish. His voice had gone somewhere small.

"The duplicate ledgers," Astrid said quietly. "Brandt keeps them in his quarters. I copied what I could. There's more. There are pages and pages, Tomás. Every diver listed as conscripted since '15. Some of them never even knew they were 'sold.' They just kept working because nobody told them they'd been declared dead on paper."

Tomás looked at the strip of paper again. It seemed impossible that so much ruin could fit in something you could roll up and lose in a shirt cuff. He thought of the letters he smuggled — love notes mostly, and complaints about prices, and once a recipe for pepper sauce that had caused a minor sensation in the northern villages — and how proud he'd been of himself, the great smuggler of the tollhouse. This wasn't smuggling. This was dynamite wearing a lace collar.

"This is a hanging offense," he said. "For you. Reading it is one thing. Copying it—"

"I know what it is."

"Do you?" He stood up, which surprised them both; his knees cracked like the tin roof in heat. "Because I've spent four years learning exactly what Brandt does to people who inconvenience him. He doesn't hang them, Astrid. Hanging's quick. He drowns them slowly, in debt, and then writes drowned in his book and sells the name anyway. Our father—"

He stopped. The room got very quiet. Outside, the tide was doing its patient work against the pilings, that endless soft chewing sound the island made in its sleep.

"Our father found these lists first," Astrid said. Not asking. Telling. "Didn't he. That's why the boat went down."

"How do you know that?"

"Because Rosa told me. Last week. In the orchard, over the rice cache, like we were discussing compost." Astrid smiled, and it was not a happy smile; it was the smile of a person who has decided to stop being afraid and hasn't yet figured out what to replace it with. "She's known for years. She traded her silence for my schooling. She let everyone believe he was just unlucky so Brandt wouldn't wonder if there were others who knew."

Tomás sat back down. His legs had opinions about standing. "Rosa," he said, "our mother, the woman who once made me apologize to a crab I stepped on, has been sitting on proof of murder for six years."

"She's been sitting on us," Astrid said. "There's a difference. We're what she's protecting. The lists are just how."

* * *

The trouble with tollhouses, Tomás reflected later, was that they taught you arithmetic whether you wanted it or not. Every day he counted heads crossing the bridge, counted carts, counted coins, counted lies. Numbers were honest work. So when Astrid finished explaining — Elias, the deserter in the drying shed, the testimony he could give, the commission on the mainland, the armistice rumor that might be true or might be smoke — Tomás did what he always did with a problem. He counted.

"One berth," he said.

"One berth."

"On the last boat. Bought with the pooled village money. Which everyone — Ferreira, the fishwives, old Baptiste who can barely walk and contributed anyway, that child who gave you three shells — everyone believes is mine."

"Yes."

"And your plan is that instead of me — the one with the actual warrant, the one they'll drag off the boat by his ears — Elias goes, and I stay here. On the island. Where Brandt is currently conducting searches because someone burned part of his precious tollhouse, an act of arson I had absolutely nothing to do with and will thank you not to look at me like that."

"I'm not looking at you like anything."

"You're looking at me exactly like Rosa looks at the goat when the mangoes go missing."

Astrid folded her hands on the counting table, the way she did when she was about to be insufferably reasonable. "Tomás. If the lists reach the commission, the company's claims are void. All of them. The forged conscriptions, the debts, everything. Including indentures."

"Including yours," he said.

"Including mine. And including any legal basis for your warrant. Your crime only exists if the company exists. You wouldn't be a fugitive anymore. You'd be a man with an interesting story."

"That's a beautiful theory."

"It's not a theory. It's arithmetic."

"Arithmetic," Tomás repeated, and laughed, because if he didn't laugh he was going to shout, and shouting in a tollhouse was how men ended up in ledgers. "You want to talk arithmetic? Fine. Let's count. Item one: the armistice rumor. Rumor. Item two: Elias reaches the mainland, finds the commission — assuming there is one, assuming it's sitting where the last letter said, assuming they listen to a ragged deserter with a hole in him — item three: the paperwork wends its merry way through whichever navy owns whichever channel this month, item four: someone sails back here with a verdict. Best case, that's months. Worst case, that's never. Meanwhile item five: I am on this island with a warrant, a burned tollhouse, and a sister committing treason in her collar. What's my number, Astrid? What column do I get sold into while we wait for the world to be fair?"

"You don't get sold into any column. We hide you in the interior until—"

"The interior!" He spread his arms to indicate the view through the window: rain beginning to stitch itself across the strait, gray on gray, the bridge already emptying as the first squall drove the carters home. "The interior is leeches and wet charcoal and men who haven't seen a mirror since the last dry season. You've met the interior, have you? It eats people, Astrid. Politely, but it eats them."

"Better than the gallows eats them."

"Everything's better than the gallows. That's why it's the gallows."

And then, because he was Tomás and because the fear in him had always come out sideways, dressed as jokes, he grinned at her. "Besides, think of the scandal. The famous smuggler of the tollhouse, fleeing justice at last! They'd write songs. Ferreira would claim he always suspected me. Baptiste would take credit for my entire criminal career."

"Tomás."

"Two verses minimum. A chorus about the bamboo poles."

"*Tomás.*"

He stopped grinning. It cost him more than the shouting would have.

She was looking at him across the counting table with their father's eyes — that steady dark patience that had made the man such a dangerous bookkeeper and such an easy man to love — and she said, very simply, "You're the one who taught me to check the columns. When I was nine. You sat me right where Ferreira's sitting and you said, 'Numbers don't lie, but liars use numbers.' Do you remember?"

"I remember being extremely wise at fourteen."

"You were insufferable at fourteen. But you were right." She tapped the strip of rice paper. "This isn't just my freedom in a collar, Tomás. It's every family on this island. The Costas. The Mendonças. Old Baptiste's sons who 'died' at sea and are probably digging someone else's canal right now. If the berth carries me, I'm a girl running from a warrant, and if they catch me, the truth burns with me. If it carries Elias, it carries a witness — a man who saw the buyer's face on the mainland, who can stand in front of the commission and say *I was sold twice, here are the hands that sold me.* A fugitive runs. A witness ends things."

"I know," Tomás said.

"You know."

"Of course I know. I count heads for a living, Astrid. I've been doing the arithmetic since you walked in with those guavas." He picked one up out of the basket and turned it over in his fingers, green-gold, heavier than it looked. "That's the problem. The math says Elias. The math has said Elias since chapter one. The math is a smug little clerk who sits in my chest and adds everything up and comes out with the same answer every time." He set the guava down. "And I'm telling you the math is wrong, because the math forgot to carry a figure. It forgot you."

"I'm not—"

"You're staying on an island run by a murderer, holding evidence that would get you killed ten different ways, with a mother feeding false movements to the man who drowned our father, during monsoon season, with no boat, no bridge, and no exit." His voice cracked on the last word and he hated it, hated how young it made him sound, sixteen going on sixty. "That's not a plan, that's a eulogy with extra steps."

"Rosa has a plan. I have the families. Forty pages among forty houses—"

"Forty houses that Brandt is already sniffing around, thanks to the fire, thanks to the searches, thanks to a war that gets closer every time a gull flies overhead looking military." Tomás pressed the heels of his hands into his eyes. When he took them away, the tollhouse swam and steadied, the way it always did, and Astrid was still there, still stubborn, still his sister, watching him with the terrible gentleness of someone who has already won and is letting you arrive at it yourself.

"I refuse," he said.

"I know."

"I formally refuse. As head of this family, which I am, by virtue of being the eldest and the tallest."

"You're taller than me by two inches and you got those from slouching."

"As head of this family," he continued over her, "I refuse to sit in a swamp eating charcoal while my little sister walks into Brandt's parlor every morning with treason stitched into her collar. No. Absolutely not. Find another berth. Steal a coracle. Learn to breathe underwater like Father supposedly could—"

"He couldn't breathe underwater, Tomás. Nobody can. That's rather the point of the whole island."

"Well, then swim!"

"Swim where? The strait closes in days. The rains are sealing it as we speak. There is one boat, one berth, and two of us who need it, and only one of us whom history actually requires." She reached across the table and took his hand — her fingers cold, always cold, even in summer, a thing he used to tease her about when teasing was safe. "Listen to me. If they catch you on that boat, you hang, and everything Mum traded, everything Father died for, everything I copied stitch by stitch at night until my eyes ached — it all becomes nothing. A warrant hanged. Case closed. Brandt wins by lunchtime. But if they catch *me*, what do they find? A clerk. A girl. Papers sewn in a collar that they'll tear apart looking for and find nothing, because the master copy isn't on me, Tomás. It's somewhere they'll never think to look, and it stays there whether I'm free or not."

"Where?"

She almost smiled. "Ask me again when we're not in a company building."

"I hate you."

"No, you don't."

"I hate you *specifically*. With precision. Like a tariff."

Outside, the rain thickened from stitching to sheeting, and the strait disappeared entirely, and somewhere down the stairs Ferreira was filling the water tank with the serene industry of a man who had solved one problem today and intended to enjoy it.

Tomás looked at his sister — sixteen years old, soaked hem, ink stain on her jaw where she'd scratched at it with her pen, a strip of paper's worth of dynamite folded back into her collar — and felt the argument go out of him the way air leaves a boat hull: not all at once, but steadily, and with the full knowledge that you are sinking.

"If you stay," he said at last, hoarsely, "and if this goes wrong — if Brandt so much as breathes on you wrong — I will come out of that swamp, Astrid. Leeches, charcoal, mirrors, all of it. I will walk into his office and do something so stupid and so loud that they'll teach it to children. I want that on the record. I want it noted in your ledger."

"Noted," she said softly. "Column one. Non-negotiable."

"Good." He squeezed her hand once, hard, because anything longer and he'd have done something embarrassing like cry, and the smuggler of the tollhouse had a reputation to maintain among exactly zero witnesses. "Now get out of my tollhouse before Ferreira comes back and asks why the guavas are unguarded. And Astrid—"

She paused at the door, hand on the latch, rain-light silvering her hair.

"When this is over," Tomás said, "when the lists are delivered and the company's rotted through and you're walking around legally owning your own name — you're teaching me to write small like that. Rice-paper small. Because I've got four years of letters to every family on this island, and when they're free, every single one of them is getting an apology in handwriting too beautiful to burn."

For a moment she just looked at him, and the look held everything neither of them had said — the father, the fire, the boat, the berth — and then she said, "Your handwriting couldn't fit on rice paper if you ironed it flat," and slipped out into the rain.

Tomás stood alone in the tollhouse with the guavas and the arithmetic and the sound of the strait closing like a mouth.

He was reaching for his pen — there were tariffs, there were always tariffs — when he heard footsteps on the stairs that were neither Astrid's nor Ferreira's. Heavy ones. Two sets. The particular unhurried tread of men whose boots were issued to them and therefore didn't care what they walked through.

Through the rain-blurred window, he saw them clearly: soldiers, uniforms dark with water, and behind them, under an umbrella held by a trembling junior clerk, the broad gray shape of Overseer Brandt, coming up the steps of Tomás's tollhouse out of the storm like weather given a salary.

Tomás slid the strip of rice paper deep into the lining of his coat, picked up his pen, and began to whistle.
