## 18. The Tollhouse Fire

The fire woke the island before it woke the sun.

I was at the kitchen table with a cup of chicory that had gone cold an hour before, copying names onto rice paper by lamplight — the small, careful work that had become as natural to me as breathing, letters no bigger than grains of pepper stitched into the margins of my mind. The light came first: a bruised orange glow over the water, wrong in every way a glow could be wrong, because it sat where the bridge met the shore and there was nothing out there but the tollhouse and Tomás.

Mother was in the doorway before I heard her feet on the floor. She had that trick, my mother. She could be asleep one moment and standing in a doorway fully dressed the next, as if sleep were just another room she passed through on her way to wherever trouble lived.

"The tollhouse," I said.

"I see it." Her voice was flat, which meant she was afraid. When Rosa Almeida raised her voice it was because she wanted you to think she wasn't frightened at all.

We stood together in the doorway like two figures painted on a pot, watching the smoke climb. It was not yet the season of dry timber. Everything on this island dripped. Wood did not burn willingly here; it had to be persuaded, coaxed, fed. Which meant someone had fed it.

"Tomás is on nights this week," Mother said. "Tollhouse rotation."

"I know."

"He'll have run when it started."

"He'll have run *toward* it," I said, and hated how well I knew my brother. Tomás had never once in his life walked away from a fire, a fight, or a girl who looked at him twice. He ran toward things the way other men ran away, and he called it charm.

Mother said nothing to that. She took down her shawl, tied it the way she always did — left corner under the right arm, the knot hidden, the same knot she used for carrying rice out of the orchard — and we went into the wet dark together.

* * *

By the time we reached the causeway road, half the compound was already there, drawn by the light the way everything on this island is eventually drawn to something burning. The Verrone Company's divers stood in their ragged line, arms crossed against the pre-dawn damp, watching the tollhouse's eastern wall go up like a festival nobody had paid for.

And there was Tomás, soot-faced, hauling buckets with the rest of them, shouting orders he had no authority to give and being obeyed anyway, because Tomás could make obedience feel like a party.

My heart did two things at once. It fell with relief — he was alive, he was whole, he was being insufferable — and it clenched with a colder feeling underneath, because I counted heads the way I counted debts, and I noticed what the fire had chosen to eat.

Not the tollhouse records wing. Not Brandt's office with its duplicate ledgers and its locked drawers. No — the fire had started in the western storeroom, the one where the smuggled letters came through, the one where Tomás kept his little post office of the indentured, the bundles of family words sewn into sacking and waiting for the bridge to open.

Someone had burned our mail.

"You're staring," Mother murmured.

"I'm counting," I said.

Brandt arrived last, which was itself a kind of theater. He came down the causeway in his good coat with his lantern held high, and he stopped at the edge of the crowd the way a man stops at the edge of a stage, and he looked at the fire for a long moment with an expression I will describe honestly: he looked *pleased*. Not delighted — Brandt was too careful for delight — but satisfied, the way a fisherman is satisfied when the net comes up heavy.

Then he turned to us all and made his face sorrowful, and it fit him about as well as his coat fit the dockhands.

"A terrible loss," he announced. "A criminal loss. Someone has set fire to company property in wartime, and wartime law has teeth." He let that sit. He was good at letting things sit; it was how he got men to fill silences with confessions. "There will be searches. Every quarter, every shed, every sleeping mat. Beginning now."

He began, naturally, with the crowd nearest him, and the crowd nearest him included my brother, still holding a bucket, still dripping, still wearing the particular grin of a man who has just been very lucky and knows it.

* * *

What actually happened, I learned later from Tomás himself, in the orchard, in whispers, between rows of lime trees, went like this:

He had been in the tollhouse after midnight doing his rounds — his real rounds, the letter rounds — when he smelled smoke that didn't come from his own lamp. He went to the western storeroom and found it already alight, fed from below, oil poured along the sill, and through the window he saw a figure going away fast toward the overseers' quarters. Not running. Walking quickly. A man who knew exactly how fast a man may walk without drawing attention.

Tomás did the arithmetic in about four seconds. If the fire was set to catch the letters, then whoever set it either knew about the letters or suspected them, and either way the next thing that would happen was a search, and the next thing after that would be his name on a warrant that already existed in draft somewhere, waiting only for a reason.

So he did the bravest stupid thing available: he stayed. He fought the fire he should have fled, loudly, publicly, heroically, so that when the searches came he would be the man who'd burned his hands saving company property rather than the man who'd been near it when it started. He blistered both palms doing it. He showed me those blisters in the orchard with genuine pride, holding them up like medals.

"They nearly had me anyway," he admitted. "After. Brandt pulled me aside. Asked me where I was when it started."

"And you said?"

"Said I was doing rounds. Said I smelled it early, that's why I was first with the buckets." He winced, flexing his bandaged hand. "He asked why I do rounds alone at night. I told him the truth, actually. Told him I like the quiet, and the quiet pays better than the day shift because nobody checks the night book."

"That's not the truth."

"It's adjacent to the truth. Astrid, listen." He stepped closer, and the humor drained out of his face, and what was left underneath was older and thinner than my brother usually allowed to show. "The letters are gone. All of them. Whatever was in that storeroom burned — the families' words, the money some of them tucked inside, everything. And whoever set it knew *exactly* which room."

"Brandt?"

"Maybe. Or maybe Brandt's buyer, cleaning up loose ends before the strait closes. Or maybe somebody selling Brandt information, wanting favor." He shrugged, and the shrug cost him something; I saw it. "Doesn't matter which. What matters is Brandt doubled the searches within the hour. They're going through the drying sheds tomorrow. Then the quarters. Then the orchard."

The orchard. Where the rice slept under the roots. Where Elias lay healing in the far shed behind the compost screens, well enough now to walk to the privy and back, not well enough to walk anywhere else.

"They can't search the sheds," I said. "Elias—"

"I know."

"They can't search the *orchard*, Tomás, the roots—"

"I know, Astrid." He said it gently, which was worse than if he'd snapped. "That's why I'm telling you tonight and not at breakfast. We move him. Tomorrow, before the searchers come. There's the old salt cache past the mangrove cut — no. Too exposed. The cistern platform, maybe, the broken one, nobody goes there since the crack. Or—" he rubbed his blistered palms together, thinking, wincing, thinking again, "—or we don't hide him better. We hide him *differently*."

I waited. My brother, for all his noise, occasionally produced an idea worth the wait.

"Everyone hides people in far places," he said. "Far sheds, far caches, far corners. So that's where they look. But nobody hides a man in the middle of a thing. Put him in the drying racks themselves, under the split fish layers, in the compound proper, ten steps from Brandt's own kitchen. Nobody searches what's under their nose; it insults them."

"It's insane."

"It's *adjacent* to sane," he said, grinning again despite the blisters, despite everything, and I understood suddenly that the grin was not carelessness. It was load-bearing. It was the beam that held up the roof of him. Take it away and my brother collapsed into the fear that lived in the cellar, the same fear I carried, the same fear Mother carried under her flat voice, and none of us could afford a collapse this week.

* * *

The searches began at dawn, as promised, and they were thorough in the way that fear makes men thorough.

They went through the divers' quarters first, turning out sleeping mats, poking bayonets into rice jars, confiscating three knives and a tin of foreign matches and a girl's ribbon that belonged to nobody's contraband but pleased the searcher anyway. They went through the drying sheds at midmorning, and I watched from the ledger window, my pen moving steadily across columns while my whole attention hung out that window like laundry.

Because Tomás's mad idea had been adopted overnight — not by vote, but by the simple momentum of having no better one — and Elias was at that moment lying beneath the third rack of split mullet, wrapped in oiled cloth, breathing through a length of hollow reed that Tomás swore was a technique used by pearl divers and therefore unimprovable.

The searchers came within an arm's reach of him. One of them leaned on the very rack above his head, complaining about the smell, and Elias — who could hear every word, who could not cough, could not shift his wounded side, could not even sneeze — endured it. I learned this afterward too, from Elias himself, delivered in his quiet mainland accent with a ghost of a smile: "Your brother's plan works. I recommend it to no one."

But the searchers found nothing in the sheds, because there was nothing in the sheds to find, and moved on to the orchard.

This was the part I had dreaded all night. Mother met them at the gate with her shawl knotted left-under-right and her face arranged into the mild blankness of a woman whose entire personality is fruit. She walked them up and down the rows. She answered questions about yields. When a young soldier drove his bayonet into the soft earth near the lime trees, she said, "Mind the irrigation channel, you'll flood the west row," in the tone of a woman more concerned with drainage than with whatever he might turn up, and the soldier, flustered by competence, probed shallowly and moved along.

The rice held. The roots kept their secret. Mother came back to the kitchen afterward and sat down and drank an entire cup of water in silence, and then said, "He probed the east row badly. Amateur." And that was all, and it was enough, and I loved her so much it was inconvenient.

* * *

It was Brandt himself who came to the ledger house at the end of the day, and I want to record accurately how that felt, because memory smooths these things and I refuse to let it.

It felt like a change in pressure. Like the moment before a storm when your ears fill and the birds go quiet and everything ordinary becomes slightly unreal, slightly lit from the wrong direction. He filled the doorway the way he filled every doorway, which is to say completely, and he smiled at me with the smile he reserved for useful people.

"Astrid Almeida. My best copy clerk, sweating over her columns while the world burns." He glanced at the pages before me — the legitimate ones, the safe ones, the ones I kept ready for exactly this — and his eyes came back to my face and stayed there a moment too long. "You hear anything, working the ledgers? Talk among the divers? Anyone angry enough at the company to burn its property?"

"No one talks to clerks," I said, which was true, and humble, and exactly the answer a clever clerk would give.

"Mm. And your brother? He was remarkably close to the fire when it started. Remarkably quick with the buckets."

"He likes fires," I said, before I could stop myself, and then, hearing how it sounded, added, "as spectacle. He's been like that since he was small. Our mother couldn't keep candles away from him."

Something flickered behind Brandt's eyes. Not belief. Not suspicion, exactly. Something more patient than either — the look of a man adding a grain to a scale and waiting to see which way it tips. "Family trait, is it? Attraction to flames?"

"We're an island family, sir. Everything here burns eventually if you're careless."

He laughed at that, a short bark with no warmth in it, and tapped the doorframe twice with one finger — a habit of his I had catalogued long ago, the tap meaning *this conversation isn't finished, I'm merely leaving it* — and went out into the evening.

I sat very still for a long time after. Then I checked the hidden pages in my collar, all present, all dry, and I wrote in the margin of my own mind the sentence that had been forming since the night of the fire:

Whoever burned the storeroom had known which room. Whoever searched had known where to push. And Brandt's grief had fit him like a borrowed coat — worn correctly, buttoned properly, and smelling faintly of someone else.

The net was tightening. I could feel it around all of us — around Tomás with his blistered hands and his warrant waiting in a drawer, around Mother with her rice and her garden parties, around Elias under the fish racks breathing through a reed, around the families whose letters had gone up in smoke and who would grieve those burned words the way you grieve the drowned, with no body to bury.

But nets have a property that nets-owners prefer not to discuss: pull them tight enough, and you can feel every knot. You can learn the shape of the mesh. You can find, if you are patient, the place where the rope is joined — the splice, the flaw, the one weak binding that holds the whole pattern together.

For Brandt, that splice was his duplicate ledger, locked in his quarters, keeping his double-dealing safe and damning and *findable*, because a man who sells every name twice must keep accounts of both sales, and accountants — even wicked ones — cannot help themselves.

Three days until the boat. Two days until the season-closing dinner, where the overseers would drink and their keys would hang fat and careless on their belts.

I blew out the lamp and sat in the dark, counting knots.

Somewhere across the compound, under the smell of split fish, a deserter breathed through a reed and waited for me to come tell him the news: the net was closing, yes — but so was the window, and only one of us intended to be on the right side of it when it shut.
