## 19. Night in the Drying Sheds

The drying sheds smelled of salt and old fish and, underneath it all, of Astrid Almeida's soap, which she had apparently decided was a strategic necessity. Elias had tried to tell her that a man hiding from a military search did not need to smell of jasmine, but she had looked at him with the particular patience of someone who kept ledgers for a living, and he had understood that the matter was closed.

So now he lay in the dark between racks of hanging fish — silver bodies swaying on their lines like a congregation at prayer — listening to boots on gravel outside, and smelling of jasmine.

*This is what my life has become,* he thought. *A deserter of His Majesty's coastal garrison, perfumed.*

He almost laughed. He caught it behind his teeth just in time, because laughter, like light, carried.

The search had begun an hour after sundown. Lanterns moved along the compound paths in slow pairs, and voices called to each other in the flat, bored tone of men who had done this many times and found nothing many times. Brandt's voice rose above the others once or twice, clipped and precise, giving instructions about the mangroves, the cisterns, the spaces beneath floors. Not the drying sheds. Not yet. But Elias had learned in two years of war that "not yet" was the most dangerous phrase in any language.

Then the shed door opened, quietly, without lantern light, and a small shape slipped inside and pulled it shut.

"You're supposed to be in the house," Elias whispered.

"And you're supposed to be dead in the water somewhere," Astrid whispered back, feeling her way along the racks toward him. "We are both disappointing people tonight."

She sat down beside him in the narrow aisle between the hanging lines, close enough that he could feel the warmth of her shoulder through the dark. She had brought a lamp with a shuttered flame, the kind pearl divers used below decks, and she opened its slide only a finger's width. A coin of light fell on the floorboards between them, no bigger than a communion wafer.

Outside, the boots went on crunching.

"They'll check here eventually," Elias said.

"Eventually," she agreed. "Not yet."

"There's that phrase again."

Her head turned. In the sliver of lamplight he saw one eyebrow lift. "You've been talking to yourself again. Rosa says it's a symptom."

"Rosa says a great deal." He shifted carefully; his wound had healed to the stage where it ached rather than screamed, which he had decided counted as progress. "Your mother has opinions the way the sea has water."

"She does. And she is usually right, which is the irritating part." Astrid settled back against the rack post. For a while neither of them spoke. The fish turned slowly on their lines. Somewhere out in the compound a dog barked twice and was silenced by a kick or a command, and then there was only rain beginning against the roof, soft at first, the way the monsoon always began — politely, before it made demands.

"Do you know why I keep coming out here?" Astrid said at last.

"To check on your investment."

"To breathe." She said it simply, without drama, the way she recorded figures. "The house is full of held breath. Mother holds hers, Tomás holds his, and I hold mine, and none of us will put it down because putting it down might make a sound. Out here the fish don't mind if I exhale."

Elias looked at her. The little coin of light trembled as the shed breathed with wind. He had known officers who talked more in a year than this girl had said in all the weeks he'd lain in her mother's house, and yet everything she said seemed to have been weighed first, like pearls sorted by carat.

"Why do *you* stay alive?" he asked, before he could decide whether it was a kind question or a cruel one.

She didn't answer immediately. Outside, the lantern-pairs passed the shed without stopping, their light sliding across the gaps in the boards, striping the floor, striping them, moving on. Elias held still through each pass with the old soldier's discipline — lungs shallow, hands loose — and noticed that Astrid did the same, naturally, as though she'd been born knowing how to be searched for.

"My father," she said finally. "Everyone thinks I stay alive out of duty to the ledger, or fear of Brandt, or love of Tomás. Those are true. But underneath, it's simpler and stupider than that." The corner of her mouth moved. "My father drowned, and the water took his name off every record but mine. As long as I remember it — Joaquim Almeida, drowned the ninth of March, four years ago, in the west beds, and the company wrote *equipment loss* in the margin — he isn't finished. Staying alive is just remembering out loud. With a pulse."

"*Equipment loss,*" Elias repeated. The words tasted like bilge.

"He was worth eleven rupees more than the rope they lost with him. I checked." Her voice didn't shake. That was somehow worse than shaking. "I was twelve, and I checked, and I have never stopped checking since. So that's my reason. What's yours? And don't say habit, because I've watched you, and you'd have let go of life months ago if it were only habit holding you."

Elias leaned his head back against the post and looked up into the dark where the rafters would be, thinking about the honest answer, which was heavy, and the easy answer, which was a lie, and wondering why lying had become so much harder since he'd washed up half-drowned among strangers.

"When I ran," he said, "I told myself it was sense. Everyone at the front was being spent like small change — sent over ground no one could hold, to take a hill no one wanted, because some major needed a victory to put in a report before his transfer came through. Running was arithmetic. One man deserting costs nothing. One man staying costs everything and buys nothing." He paused. "That was the lie. It wasn't arithmetic. I ran because I was afraid, and I dressed the fear up in numbers so it would look respectable. Fear loves a uniform too."

The rain thickened. On the roof it drummed, and under the drumming the boots outside became harder to track, which was either safety or blindness.

"I stayed alive," he went on, quieter, "because of a boy called Ferro. Nineteen. From a fishing family up the coast — he used to talk about his mother's fish stew the way other men talk about saints. He couldn't swim. Can you believe that? A fishing family, and the sea never forgave him for it, so he joined the garrison to prove something to the water." Elias's jaw tightened. "When our boat was hit, I went into the water because I could swim, and I got to him, and I held him, and the current took him anyway. My arms were around him. I felt it happen. And when I surfaced alone, the first thing I thought — God forgive me — the first thing I thought was *I'm free.*"

Astrid said nothing. She was very good at saying nothing; it was perhaps her finest skill.

"So I ran. And every day since, staying alive has meant two things at once. It means proving Ferro's death bought something. And it means paying a debt to a woman I've never met, whose son I failed to bring home." He turned his head and found her eyes in the thin light. "That's my reason. It's not noble. It's a debt. You'd understand that better than anyone on this island."

"Yes," Astrid said softly. "Debts I understand."

For a long moment the only sound was rain and the creak of hanging fish, and Elias realized that he had not spoken of Ferro aloud to a single living soul until now. Two years of silence, broken in a fish shed that smelled of jasmine, to a sixteen-year-old clerk. There was a sermon in that somewhere, and he was glad no priest was present to deliver it.

"The lists," Astrid said, changing course the way she changed subjects — abruptly, like a boat tacking. "You said the buyer is on the mainland. You said you could name him."

"I can."

"You've never said his name."

"No." Elias flexed his fingers, watching the coin of light waver over them. "Because a name in my mouth dies with me if a bullet finds me, and names written down get found. Your mother taught me that much — paper burns, but it also betrays. Everything on this island gets searched eventually. I've seen the soldiers go through the rice jars."

"Not everything gets searched." Astrid reached up, unpinned her collar, and drew out a folded square of rice paper so fine it crackled like frost. Even in the finger-width of light he could see the columns of tiny figures covering it, dense as ant trails. "Everything gets *worn*, though. Nobody searches a girl's collar. They search her baskets, her hair, her shoes. They pat the seams of her skirt. But a collar is just a collar." She refolded it and sewed it back in with three quick stitches, working by touch, the needle flashing. "I've been carrying your war in my neck for a week."

Elias stared at her. Something moved in his chest that was not quite fear and not quite hope but lived in the neighborhood of both.

"That's the bravest and the maddest thing I've seen since the front," he said.

"It's bookkeeping," she said, pinning the collar closed. "I'm just changing where the ledger is kept."

He laughed — a real laugh, brief and helpless, quickly smothered — and outside a boot paused, and they both froze, shoulder to shoulder, breathing through their teeth, until the boot moved on. When it had gone, Astrid exhaled through her nose, and he felt her laugh too, silent, shaking against his arm. It occurred to him that they were laughing together in a hiding place during a manhunt, and that this was, absurdly, the least lonely he had felt in two years.

"Astrid." He waited until she stilled. "I need to ask you something, and I need you to hear all of it before you answer."

She turned to face him fully.

"When I'm well enough to travel — and I'm nearly there, whatever your mother claims about my ribs — I can't be the one who carries those pages. If they catch me, I'm a deserter. They'll shoot me at the dock and call it weather, and the pages burn with the body. It has to be someone the company has no warrant for. Someone ordinary. Someone they'd never think to stop." He held her gaze. "And on the mainland, the people who can use this — the commission men, the ones gathering testimony before the armistice talks — they're strangers to you. Strangers I know by reputation and half a name. I'd be asking you to carry the truth across the water and hand it to people you've never seen, on nothing but my word."

The rain hammered. The fish turned. Astrid's face in the sliver of light was unreadable, and then, slowly, it wasn't — it was doing sums. He could see the sums happening behind her eyes, columns adding and subtracting, and he understood suddenly that for her this was not a metaphor. Trust, for Astrid Almeida, was an audit.

"Give me the half-name," she said.

"Marek. Runs a chandlery near the customs quay in Porto Verde. Left arm stiff — old injury, he'll tell you it was a crane, it was not a crane. Ask him what he charges for rope and he'll say *depends who's asking*, and if you say *someone who can't pay*, he'll feed you and listen. That's the whole of the password. It's ridiculous. It's worked for six years."

"*Someone who can't pay,*" she repeated, committing it wherever she committed things — that ledger behind her eyes that never lost a line.

"You understand what I'm asking," Elias said. "Not just the crossing. I'm asking you to believe that strangers on the mainland are worth more than your fear of them. Because that's the whole trick of it, Astrid. The company runs on everyone believing every stranger is a threat. Brandt doesn't need soldiers if nobody on this island will trust anybody beyond their own bloodline. The lists survive because the truth stays divided. Trusting a stranger with the truth — that's not naivety. That's the weapon."

Astrid was quiet for a long time. Outside, the search parties were regrouping; he heard Brandt's voice give the order to sweep the shore path again, and heard the frustration in it, thin and cold as wire.

"My father trusted the company," she said at last. "It killed him and billed him for the rope."

"I know."

"My mother trusted the company's bargain. Silence for schooling. Look where silence got her."

"I know."

"But—" She stopped. Started again, slower, testing the sentence like a plank over water. "But Tomás trusts strangers every dry season. Every letter he smuggles through the tollhouse is a stranger trusting another stranger, and it works, it has *worked*, for years, and the bridge opens twice a year on the strength of it." She looked down at her hands. "I keep the ledgers. I know exactly how much good faith moves through this island, because I've seen it in the margins where the company pretends it doesn't exist. I suppose I already knew what you're telling me. I just hadn't written it down anywhere official."

"Is that a yes?"

"It's an *eventually*," she said, and he heard the smile in it. "There's that phrase again."

They sat together in the dark while the search wore itself out, and after a while, because the dark makes confessors of everyone, she told him about the bridge — how it sounded underfoot, how the crowd smelled of dust and frying oil, how she had stood on it once at seven years old and believed the far end was the edge of the world. And he told her about snow, which she refused to believe in until he described it three different ways, and even then she declared it *clerically improbable*, and he laughed again, and had to press his sleeve against his mouth.

Much later, when the last lantern had gone into the overseers' quarters and the rain had settled into its long overnight monologue, Astrid rose to go.

"Elias." She paused at the door, a shadow among shadows. "Ferro's mother. When this is done — when the lists have done their work — write to her. Tell her what her son's death bought. Don't decide for her that she wouldn't want to know. That's the mistake men make. They protect women from the truth and call it love, and meanwhile we're the ones sewing it into our collars."

The door opened and shut on the smell of wet earth, and she was gone.

Elias lay back between the swaying fish and stared into the black, and found, to his considerable surprise, that he was smiling. Somewhere out there, a chandlery keeper with a stiff arm was going to open his door one morning and find a small, precise, jasmine-scented girl on his step saying *someone who can't pay* — and Marek's careful, comfortable, six-year-old anonymity was going to end forever.

He hoped the old man appreciated it.

He was still smiling when he heard, faint across the compound through the rain, the tollhouse bell begin to ring — fast, arrhythmic, wrong — and the first shout go up from the direction of the shore.

Fire. Something was burning by the water, and the whole night was waking up to look at it.
