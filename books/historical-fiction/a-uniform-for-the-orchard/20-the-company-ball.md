## 20. The Company Ball

The invitation to serve at the season-closing dinner came down from the overseers' quarters on a slip of company paper, which meant it was an order wearing a nice shirt.

*Twelve hands required for table service. Report to the kitchen by four o'clock. Clean uniforms provided.*

Clean uniforms provided. Astrid read that line three times. In six years of indenture she had been issued exactly one uniform — a gray smock with the Verrone Company's pearl stitched over the heart like a brand — and she had washed it so many times it had gone soft as an apology. The dinner uniform, when she collected it from the stores clerk, was white. Stiff. It smelled of starch and somebody else's ambitions.

"It makes you look like a ghost of yourself," Tomás said when he saw her, and then ruined the compliment by laughing until he had to hold the doorframe.

"It makes me look like what they want me to be," Astrid said, smoothing the front. "Invisible but present. Like furniture that can hear."

"That's the most frightening thing you've ever said."

"Good. Remember it." She said it lightly, but she meant every word, because tonight she was not going to the dinner as furniture. She was going as a thief.

The plan had assembled itself in her head the way plans do — not all at once, but piece by piece over the past week, each fragment arriving on its own schedule like cargo off a late boat. First fragment: Rosa's observation, delivered while shelling beans, that Mr. Brandt kept his quarters locked but wore his key on his waistcoat chain "the way other men wear their mothers' portraits." Second fragment: the wax. The kitchen used tallow candles for the dinner tables, and tallow, Astrid knew from years of sealing letters for Tomás's smuggled correspondence, takes an impression like a jealous memory. Third fragment: the fact that Brandt drank. Not badly, not stupidly — he drank the way he did everything, with method and restraint — but a man who counts his glasses still drinks them.

And the fourth fragment, the one that made all the others worth the risk: somewhere behind that locked door sat the duplicate ledger, the true accounting of every diver sold twice, and if the duplicate could not come out through the lock, then the lock would simply have to learn some manners.

* * *

The compound's long hall had been transformed for the occasion, or at least dressed up in the way a fishing boat is dressed up for a wedding — same bones, better bunting. Lanterns hung along the rafters in strings. The long tables wore white cloths that reached nearly to the floor, hiding crates underneath. Someone had cut fronds from the orchard palms and pinned them along the walls, and Astrid recognized her mother's pruning work in the neatness of the cuts: Rosa had been drafted too, and had apparently decided that if she must decorate a tyrant's party she would do it beautifully, as a form of contempt.

The guests arrived as the light went copper over the water. Overseers from the out-stations, their wives pink from the climb up from the dock. Two naval officers, young, sunburned, loud. The harbor master. A labor broker named Fenn whose smile Astrid distrusted on general principle and specifically because she had seen his signature in the forged columns — the man bought names by the dozen and never once looked at the faces attached.

And Mr. Brandt, host and centerpiece, in a suit the color of wet slate, moving among his guests with the unhurried satisfaction of a man surveying a harvest he intends to take credit for.

"Miss Almeida," he said, finding her at the sideboard where she was arranging glasses. He always found her. She had stopped believing it was coincidence sometime around the tollhouse fire; now she understood it was surveillance with good manners. "You clean up respectably."

"Thank you, sir. The uniform helps."

"The uniform always does." He picked up a glass, considered it, set it back down half an inch to the left. "You'll see the wine is poured generously tonight. A season closed well deserves generosity. And a season about to open—" here he smiled, and the smile had arithmetic in it, "—deserves anticipation."

"Yes, sir."

He moved on. Astrid let out a breath and counted what he'd given her: *a season about to open.* The boat. He'd said nothing directly, but Brandt never said anything directly; he scattered information the way a sower scatters seed and then watched to see who bent to pick it up. So she straightened her tray and resolved to bend very carefully, in the right direction, where he could see her doing honest invisible work.

* * *

Dinner was served in courses, and the courses were served by Astrid and eleven others moving in a choreography the cook had drilled into them that afternoon with the patience of a woman who had survived forty years of company dinners and intended to survive forty more.

Astrid worked the head table. This was either an honor or a trap, and she had decided to treat it as both, because the head table was where Brandt sat, and where Brandt sat was where the conversation happened.

She learned a great deal between the fish course and the meat course, mostly by carrying things and saying nothing.

"—closing earlier every year, that's the whole of it," the harbor master was saying. "Strait'll be shut tight inside a week. Nothing moves after that, not a coracle, not a gull with a letter tied to its leg."

"And your boat?" one of the naval officers asked. "The mail run?"

"Gone already. Left Tuesday." The harbor master helped himself to more wine from the bottle Astrid was holding, without looking at her, the way men took things from furniture. "Excepting one charter. Special arrangement."

"Ah," said the officer, and glanced at Brandt, and grinned. "The dawn departure. Very military. Nothing says discretion like leaving before God's awake."

Astrid's hands did not shake. She had trained them too well for that; her hands could copy a forged column without a tremor and carry soup across a rolling deck, presumably, if it came to it. But something behind her ribs went very quiet and very cold, the way the water went cold below the warm skin of the pearl beds.

*Dawn.* The village had pooled its money against a noon departure. Tomás had built three days of margin around a noon departure. Every plan in the compound — Elias's route, the pages sewn into collars, the timing of the walk down to the staging cove — was built on noon, and noon had just quietly died between the fish course and the meat course, killed by two drunk officers and a harbor master who liked the sound of his own discretion.

She poured the next glass steadily. She even got the pour right, no drip down the neck of the bottle, which under the circumstances she counted as the finest professional achievement of her life.

"Dawn," Brandt said mildly, "is when the tide cooperates. I don't choose the tides, gentlemen. I merely decline to argue with them." He lifted his glass toward Astrid — not for wine, just a little salute, a host acknowledging good service, or perhaps a spider admiring the quality of the silk. "More of the claret, Miss Almeida."

* * *

The evening wore on the way these evenings do: louder, looser, warmer. The officers told a story involving a goat and a general that Astrid did not follow and did not need to. Mrs. Ferreira from the out-station laughed at everything her husband said with the disciplined enthusiasm of long practice. Fenn the broker grew expansive and began telling anyone within range about opportunities north, opportunities in the new plantations, opportunities that Astrid knew were built on names from a second column in a book she intended to steal before the week was out.

And Brandt drank.

Not much. Three glasses, maybe four, each spaced out like entries in a careful ledger. But Astrid watched him the way she watched anything that mattered, and she noted the moment — somewhere past the ninth hour of the evening, during the toasts — when his laugh came a half-beat easier than usual, when his hand rested on the table instead of hovering near his keys.

The keys were on his waistcoat. They had been there all night. One large iron key for his office, and one smaller brass one, and it was the brass one she wanted, because Rosa had told her — in the orchard, hands deep in the dirt, eyes never lifting from the roots — that the brass key opened the quarters, and the quarters held the books, and the books held everything.

The wax was waiting. She had prepared it herself that afternoon in the kitchen, working beside the cook with the tallow, pretending to trim candle-ends. A small disc of softened wax wrapped in cloth, tucked now in the folds of her serving napkin, riding against her wrist like a second pulse.

Now she only needed proximity and a moment.

The moment, when it came, came disguised as an accident, which was fitting, since the entire evening was an accident waiting to be arranged.

Mrs. Ferreira, reaching for her seventh glass with more enthusiasm than aim, knocked it. Not catastrophically — the wine spread in a lazy crimson tongue across the white cloth and began, with terrible patience, crawling toward the lap of the naval officer beside her. The officer leapt up swearing cheerfully. Mrs. Ferreira stood too, apologizing in two languages. Chairs scraped. Everyone leaned in to help, because spilled wine at a company dinner was the most interesting thing that had happened in weeks.

And Brandt rose too, stepping back from the spreading stain, and turned slightly away to direct the salvage effort — "Salt, salt, the cook will know, get salt before it sets—" — and his hip came level with Astrid's shoulder, and the waistcoat swung free of the table's edge.

Astrid had rehearsed this in her head perhaps four hundred times, and in every rehearsal the difficult part was the same: not the lift, but the return. Anyone can move a hand. The art is in making the hand look like it never left home.

So she did what she had practiced. She reached for the fallen glass with her left hand — the natural motion, the expected motion, the motion of a servant clearing wreckage — and while her left hand performed its public duty, her right hand, low, close to her body, hidden in the geography of her own apron fold, brought the wax disc up against the brass key.

Press. Hold. Two seconds. Three. Her heartbeat sounded enormous to her, a drum in a silent room, though of course the room was not silent, the room was full of shouting about salt.

She withdrew her hand. The wax disc dropped back into the napkin fold, warm, carrying its stolen portrait.

Then she stood, glass in hand, and said clearly, "I'll fetch the salt, sir," and walked toward the kitchen at a servant's pace, neither fast nor slow, a woman entirely composed of errands.

Behind her the cleanup continued. Behind her Brandt was telling the table some anecdote designed to restore dignity to the proceedings. Behind her, on his waistcoat, hung a brass key that now existed in two places at once — which was, she thought as she pushed through the kitchen door, a kind of justice. The company had spent years making people exist twice: once in truth and once in the ledgers. Tonight the trick ran the other direction. Tonight the key was duplicated, and the duplicate belonged to her.

* * *

The kitchen received her in steam and noise. The cook took the salt jar from her without thanks and dispatched her back out with a fresh cloth, and Astrid went, because a servant who vanishes during a crisis is remembered, and a servant who returns with supplies is furniture again.

But on her way back through the corridor — the narrow passage that ran behind the dining hall, lined with shelves of spare glassware — she paused. Just once. Just long enough to draw the wax disc from the napkin and hold it to the lantern light.

The impression was perfect.

There sat the brass key in miniature, teeth and shaft and bow, pressed into the tallow with the fidelity of a confession. She turned it once, twice, studying it the way she studied ledger columns — looking for the flaw, the anomaly, the thing that didn't add up. There was none. Wax, it turned out, was more honest than the company.

She wrapped it back in its cloth and slid it into the seam of her sleeve, where it lay against her forearm, small and warm and heavier than any object its size had any right to be.

When she returned to the hall with the cloth, the stain was being defeated, the officers were resuming their seats, and Brandt was standing at the head of the table raising a final glass.

"To the closing of the season," he said. "And to those who make the company what it is." His eyes traveled the room, and paused — briefly, briefly — on Astrid, refolding her napkin at the sideboard. "Every one of them."

The guests drank. Astrid folded her napkin. Somewhere outside, the rain had begun again, soft and businesslike against the lanterns, the monsoon clearing its throat.

* * *

It was nearly midnight when the last guest wobbled down to the dock and the hall stood empty except for debris and fronds. Astrid helped strip the tablecloths, carried stacks of plates to the kitchen, and finally, finally, was dismissed with a grunt from the cook, who was too tired even to criticize anybody's folding.

Outside, the compound slept the heavy sleep of people who had eaten and drunk too much of someone else's hospitality. Astrid crossed the yard in the rain-light, white uniform gone gray at the hem, and slipped into the orchard, where her mother was waiting exactly where she knew Rosa would be waiting: at the boundary row, under the tree that hid the rice, arms crossed against the damp.

"Well?"

"Dawn." Astrid said it plainly, because Rosa deserved plainness. "The boat goes at dawn, Mother. Not noon. Tomorrow dawn. We have five hours, maybe less, and everyone down at the cove thinks they have a full day."

Rosa closed her eyes. For a moment she looked old — older than the orchard, older than the water — and then, as Astrid watched, she gathered the years back up and put them away, the way she put away tools at the end of a day, neatly, where they wouldn't be lost.

"Tomás first," Rosa said. "Then the cove. Then we wake whoever needs waking. Go change out of that ghost-cloth before you frighten someone."

"One more thing." Astrid drew the wax disc from her sleeve and opened the cloth under the tree's shadow. Rosa leaned close, and by the dim shine of the sky through the leaves she studied the impression, and Astrid saw her mother's face do something complicated — fear and pride fighting for the same ground and agreeing, at last, to share it.

"You stole his key," Rosa said.

"I borrowed its shadow."

Rosa laughed — a short, startled sound, quickly swallowed, the first laugh Astrid had heard from her in weeks. Then she gripped Astrid's wrist, hard, over the sleeve that hid the wax.

"Five hours," she said. "Go."

Astrid went — past the sleeping quarters, past the dark bulk of the drying sheds where Elias waited and did not yet know, toward the room where Tomás slept with his warrant and his jokes and his berth that was no longer a day away but a handful of breaths —

— and behind her, in the overseer's quarters above the empty hall, a lamp flickered on. Brandt, sleepless, pouring himself one last measured glass. And on his desk, the duplicate ledger sat shut and patient and, as of tonight, no longer as safe as its owner believed.

Five hours. The boat at dawn. And a lock that didn't yet know it had already been opened.
