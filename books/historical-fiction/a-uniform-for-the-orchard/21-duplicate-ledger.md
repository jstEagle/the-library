## 21. Duplicate Ledger

The wax impression sat in the bottom of my sewing basket like a small yellow sin.

I had made it three nights before, at the season-closing dinner, pressing Brandt's spare office key between my thumb and a candle stub while he toasted his own generosity across the room. It had come away perfect — every tooth, every nick — and I had spent the following days telling myself that a copy of a key was not yet a crime. A key was only an idea until it turned. I could still put it in the stove and be a girl who had done nothing but handle wax at a party.

But ideas are heavy things once you've carried them a while. They settle into you like ballast.

So on Thursday, when the compound emptied for the rice distribution and even the dogs followed the smell of boiling grain down to the jetty, I took the idea out of the basket, wrapped it in my apron, and walked to the counting house as though my legs were ordinary legs belonging to an ordinary clerk with no plans at all.

* * *

The counting house smelled the way it always did — ink, salt rot, and the ghost of Brandt's cheroots — but today it smelled different to me. Today it smelled like a room that was about to remember me.

I stood in the corridor a full minute, listening. The rain came down on the tin roof in the steady, businesslike way of the early monsoon, which meant nobody could hear anything beyond arm's length, including me. Including anyone who might catch me. The rain cut both ways; it always did on this island.

The lock was the third door on the left. I knelt — a clerk kneeling to pray, if anyone looked through the window, and let them think what they liked about girls who prayed at odd hours — and fitted the wax mold around the blank iron key Tomás had filed down for me from a broken padlock. My hands were shaking so badly the first attempt missed entirely. The second scraped. On the third try the key slid in, caught its teeth one by one like a climber finding handholds, and turned.

The sound it made was very small. It sounded enormous.

"Right," I whispered to the empty corridor, because scolding myself quietly has always been how I manage fear. "You're in. Now don't be stupid about it."

Brandt's private office was exactly what you'd expect from a man who counted other people's lives for a living: immaculate, cold, and arranged so that everything faced him. His chair sat behind his desk like a throne that had given up pretending. The window overlooked the pearl beds, so he could watch men drown for their wages without leaving his seat.

And there, in the iron cabinet beside the desk — the cabinet whose key hung from his belt at dinner, the cabinet I'd watched him open with two fingers, casual as a man scratching his nose — sat the duplicate ledger.

I want to be honest here, since honesty is the whole point of everything that follows: I had planned to photograph nothing. That was the joke Elias and I had made in the drying sheds, back when the plan was elegant. There is no camera on this island, no photographic paper, nothing but rain and rice paper and my own hands, which can copy a column of figures faster than any machine Brandt could order from the mainland. Copy the pages, sew them small, carry them out. A needle's worth of truth at a time. Elegant. Safe, or as safe as anything is.

That was the plan when I opened the cabinet.

The ledger defeated it instantly.

It was enormous. Not ledger-sized — ledger-sized would have been fine, ledger-sized I could have managed under my blouse in sections over a week. This thing was built like a ship's anchor bound in leather. It must have been twelve inches thick, four hundred pages of double-entry duplicating every name Brandt had sold twice, and the paper inside was the good thick stock he reserved for documents meant to survive scrutiny. It weighed more than Tomás. It possibly weighed more than Mother's entire cache of hidden rice.

I stood there holding it — I had lifted it out before my brain finished objecting, which tells you something about where my brain ranked in that moment — and I thought, with a clarity that felt almost like calm: *I cannot copy this. Not in a week. Not in a month. The boat leaves in days, and the bridge is closed, and this book is too big to hide under any collar ever sewn.*

Fear arrived right on schedule. I let it sit down and wait, the way you let an unwanted guest cool his heels, because I had noticed something important about fear over the past year: it is loud, but it is rarely correct. Fear said *put it back.* Fear said *you'll hang.* Fear said *copy just one page, one page is enough.*

But one page was not enough. One page was a wound; the whole book was the confession. If Elias testified on the mainland and the commission sent for proof and the proof turned out to be a single leaf — deniable, dismissable, *clerk's error, clerks make errors, look at her record* — then we had risked everything for nothing. And worse: Brandt would know someone had been in the cabinet, and the search would begin, and the families holding whatever we'd distributed would be picked off one by one like fruit off a low branch.

No. If I was going to commit the crime, I was going to commit the whole crime.

The audacity surprised me more than the fear did. I had thought of myself all my life as a careful person — careful the way divers are careful, measuring breath, measuring depth, never surfacing fast. But standing in that office with a stolen book in my arms, I discovered that somewhere along the way the care had worn through, the way Father's boots wore through, and underneath it was something else. Something that looked at an impossible weight and thought: *well then, we'll simply have to be cleverer than the weight.*

I am not saying I wasn't terrified. I am saying the terror got in line behind the plan, which is the only place terror is useful.

* * *

The first problem was getting out of the building with it.

A ledger the size of a child cannot go under a blouse. I considered wrapping it in my shawl and walking out bent like an old woman, but the compound was not empty enough for theater. The second problem was the cabinet itself: locked again, it would show nothing, but Brandt checked that cabinet the way other men check their purses. I had hours at most. Possibly minutes, if the rice distribution ended early, which it never did, because free grain moves slower than flood water.

I put the ledger back, closed the cabinet, locked it, and went home to think.

Mother saw my face the moment I came through the door and set down her knife mid-onion, which for Mother is the equivalent of dropping everything.

"You went," she said.

"I went."

"And?"

"And the book is too big." I sat down heavily at the table. "Mother, it's enormous. It's obscene. He didn't keep evidence, he kept a monument."

She looked at me for a long moment, onion knife forgotten, and then — and this is the moment I will remember about her until I die — she began to laugh. Not loudly. Rosa Almeida does not laugh loudly. But her shoulders shook and her eyes went bright and she pressed her apron to her mouth, and when she finally spoke she said:

"He counts everything. He counts everything, and he never once counted on us being able to lift it."

"It weighs as much as a goat."

"Then we treat it like a goat." She wiped her eyes. "You don't smuggle a goat in your blouse, Astrid. You take it apart."

* * *

We went back after full dark, Mother and I, in the rain, because rain was our accomplice now and we intended to use it thoroughly.

Tomás met us at the corner of the drying sheds with the coracle and a face that was trying very hard to be stern and failing, the way his faces always fail. "Let me say once," he whispered, "for the record, that this is insane."

"Noted for the record," I whispered back.

"Good. Record's complete. Give me the corner."

Between the three of us we got the cabinet open in half the time — Mother holding the shuttered lantern, Tomás on the lock because his hands were steadier, me doing nothing for one humiliating minute until the book was in my arms again and suddenly everyone needed me. We wrapped it in oilcloth, we lowered it into the coracle, and we poled it through the black flooded lanes behind the counting house while the rain covered every splash we made.

Twice we froze. Once it was a patrol lantern swinging near the jetty; once it was only a heron taking offense at us, which Tomás says is nearly as dangerous. Both times I held the book against my chest and felt its weight as something new. All those years I had memorized debts in my head because I owned nothing that couldn't be taken — a knife, a ledger stub, my father's drowned name. Now I was carrying the opposite of a debt. I was carrying the receipt.

We buried it — temporarily, badly, in the loose earth behind the copra shed, under a layer of husks that fooled nobody who actually looked but fooled everybody who merely passed — and I lay awake the rest of the night doing arithmetic that had nothing to do with numbers: four hundred pages, thirty trusted families, dawn coming whether we were ready or not.

At some point Mother appeared in my doorway with tea, which is how she says everything she doesn't say.

"If we're cutting up a man's whole life," she said, "we should do it properly. Tonight, before he misses it. Bring the scissors."

"The big ones?"

"Astrid." She smiled in the dark. "For this we take the butcher's."

* * *

One last thing, and then I will get to the cutting, because the cutting deserves its own accounting and Rosa will tell it better than I would anyway.

When we lifted the ledger from the ground that final night, wet earth clinging to the oilcloth, Tomás paused with his hands flat on the cover and read aloud the embossed title the way he reads everything — dramatically, unnecessarily, with feeling: *"Verrone Trading Company — Register of Engagements — Property of the Company."*

"Property of the company," he repeated. Then he grinned at me through the rain, that grin that used to mean stolen mangoes and meant something else now. "Funny. It doesn't feel like theirs anymore."

It didn't. It felt like ours — like it had been ours all along, every page of it, every sold name, every diver entered twice, every figure in my father's drowned handwriting that I'd copied without knowing what I was copying. Brandt had kept a monument to what he'd done, and we were carrying it home in pieces of oilcloth, and the strangest part, the part I keep returning to, is how light it felt.

Four hundred pages, heavier than a goat. And lighter than the single stub of ledger I had kept at fifteen.

We got it into the orchard by midnight. By one o'clock the lantern was shaded, the butcher's scissors were out, and Mother had spread the first page flat on the table like a dress she intended to alter.

That was when the dogs started up at the gate.
