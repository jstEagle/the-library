## 22. Cut Into Pieces

The knife I use for the ledger is the same one I use to halve breadfruit, and there is a lesson in that which I did not plan but will accept.

Astrid laid the stolen book on our kitchen table at half past nine, after she had walked it home from the compound sewn into a sack of dried fish, and for a long moment none of us touched it. It sat there breathing its company smell — ink, salt, the particular sourness of paper that has been handled by men who never once washed their hands before supper. Tomás looked at it the way a man looks at a snake he has been told is dead. Elias, propped in the corner chair with his wound draining him of color and patience both, looked at it like a door.

I looked at it like a kitchen problem. That is what forty years on this island teaches you: everything, eventually, becomes a kitchen problem.

"Well," I said, and tied my hair back. "We'll need more thread."

* * *

You should understand something about me before you understand what we did that night. I have spent my whole adult life hiding things — rice under tree roots, letters inside salted fish, grief behind a pleasant face for overseers' wives who thought me simple because I smiled while they complained about their linens. Hiding is a craft, and like any craft it rewards the humble tools. You do not hide a thing by putting it somewhere clever. Clever places are the first places searched, because clever men assume other men are clever too.

You hide a thing by making it ordinary. You make it *boring*.

So when Astrid said, "Mother, how do we keep one search from finding all of it?" I did not answer as a conspirator. I answered as a woman who has fed eleven families through two bad monsoons and one very bad year of locusts.

"You don't," I said. "Not if it stays whole. A whole truth can be confiscated. A truth cut into forty pieces and fed to forty houses — that truth lives where the soldiers don't look, because no soldier on this earth has ever wanted to search forty kitchens."

Tomás laughed — that sharp, disbelieving bark he uses when something frightens him and he refuses to let it win. "You want to give the evidence away."

"I want to *lend* it," I said. "There's a difference, and the difference is that lenders get their property back."

* * *

We began at midnight, when the compound dogs had finished their rounds and the rain had settled into that steady, businesslike drumming that covers all sounds and excuses all lights. Astrid cut first. She insisted, and I understood why without her saying it: her father's name was in those pages, drowned twice over — once in the strait and once in Brandt's tidy columns — and she was not going to let another pair of hands make the first cut.

The knife went through the binding spine with a sound like a small bone breaking. I have heard worse sounds in kitchens, but not many.

Then the work turned tedious, and tedious work is where families either quarrel or become armies, so I made rules early.

Rule one: no page leaves this table until someone can recite where it's going and who holds it. Rule two: nobody says the names of the families out loud more than once; we used the orchard rows instead — the Ferreras are Row Three, the Baptistas are Row Seven, and so on, because a name spoken twice sticks in a frightened memory, but a row number slides off like water off wax. Rule three, which I announced with full authority and which Tomás immediately violated: no jokes about hanging.

"Mother, I'm *allowed* one joke about hanging—"

"You're allowed one joke per hour, and you've used this hour's on the coracle."

"That joke was yesterday."

"Time moves differently in this house tonight."

Elias, from his corner, made a low sound that might have been laughter or might have been his wound objecting to being shaken. He had been trying to help and we had been trying to stop him — a man who cannot stand unassisted should not be folding contraband — but he refused to sit idle, so we gave him the lightest task: reading the pages aloud in a whisper as Astrid sorted them, so we could match each sheet to the right family. He read the names of divers sold north who had never left, the sums collected twice, the neat initials of buyers on both sides of the water. His voice got quieter as the night went on. By the second hour it was barely above the rain.

"These men signed nothing," he said at one point, holding a page up to the lamp. "Half of them can't write. Their marks are forged right here beside the forger's own initials. It isn't even careful forgery. He never thought anyone would count."

"No one counts," Astrid said, cutting steadily. "That's the whole business model."

She said it flatly, without bitterness, and I felt the old cold thing move in my chest — the thing I have carried since the day I learned how her father died, which I will come to, because tonight of all nights it deserves saying. But not yet. First the pages.

* * *

Here is what we built, between midnight and the fourth bell, and I record it plainly because someday someone may need the method:

Forty-one pages. Forty-one hiding places. We did not use cellars or floorboards or any hole in any wall, because holes imply hiddenness and hiddenness invites digging. Instead each family received its pages folded small and sewn into an object so mundane that searching it would be an insult to the searcher.

Senhora Ferrera took hers inside the stuffing of a church altar cushion — not because the church is sacred, though it is, but because no soldier in history has unpicked an old woman's kneeler looking for arithmetic. The Baptista boys' pages went into the lining of fishing boots that stank so profoundly even the dog avoided them. Old Man Correia, who is deaf and delighted about it, got his sewn into the brim of the hat he wears everywhere including bed. The widow Nogueira, who sells sweetmeats at the tollhouse and has survived three inspections by offering the inspectors fritters until they forgot why they came, took hers rolled into the paper tubes of her candied ginger — and I tell you, if Brandt's men find anything in that woman's stall it will be because they ate their way to it, and by then they'll have forgotten what they were looking for anyway.

And ours? Ours stayed closest. Three pages, the ones naming the buyers on the mainland side — the ones Elias needed corroborated, the ones worth the most and therefore the most dangerous — went into the hem of my good shawl, the grey one I wear to the overseers' dinners. Let them pat down the informants first, I say. Nobody suspects the woman carrying the tea.

Astrid kept the master copy separate already, against her ribs, and I did not argue. There is a version of motherhood that consists entirely of knowing which battles your child has already won in her head.

* * *

The deliveries themselves were almost the easiest part, because the island did what islands do: it closed around us like a hand around a coal.

Tomás ran the first rounds under the honest cover of his tollhouse errands — a man carrying boots to be resoled, a hat to be reblocked, a cushion to his aunt, nothing in this world more invisible than a boy doing chores. The rest went out through the orchard itself, in baskets of windfall fruit, in bundles of pruned branches destined for cooking fires, in the bottom of the rice panniers I carry to the drying sheds every morning of my life. Each delivery came with a word in the ear and no paper instructions, because instructions can be found and words can only be remembered, and memory is the one warehouse the company doesn't hold the deed to.

What moved me — and I am an old woman, my tears ration themselves — was not fear. It was readiness. When I pressed the little packet into Senhora Ferrera's floury hands and told her what it was, she did not flinch. She held it up to the lamplight, weighed it like a fish at market, and said:

"Heavy?"

"Lighter than a debt," I said.

"Good." And she put down her bread dough, unpicked three stitches in her kneeler without even sitting, and had the whole thing secured before the tea went cold. Then she asked me, quite seriously, whether the company paid its clerks enough for young Astrid, because a girl doing that kind of work should eat meat.

Community as armor — yes, that is what Astrid called it later, and the phrase is pretty, but the truth is less elegant and more nourishing. The truth is that these people have been robbed so long and so thoroughly that being trusted with the instrument of their own rescue was, for some of them, the first dignity the island had handed them in years. Old Man Correia, deaf as a post, gripped my wrist when I explained it by signs and shouted — he always shouts, bless him — "ABOUT TIME SOMEBODY CUT THE BASTARD'S BOOK UP." Half the street heard. Half the street cheered. I had to remind everyone that shouting about it rather undid the sewing, but privately I agreed with him down to my bones.

* * *

Toward the fourth bell, the house emptied of errands and we four remained — myself, Astrid, Tomás soaked through from his last round, and Elias gray-faced in his corner — and the work turned from labor to something else. Waiting does that. It strips a room down to its people.

Astrid sat among the paper scraps and the trailing threads and suddenly looked sixteen again, which she is, and which I forget, because she has been keeping our accounts since she was eleven. Her fingers had ink to the second knuckle. There was a thread caught in her hair like a pale wire.

"Eat," I said, and set the rice before her, and she ate, because she knows better than to fight me on food.

"Mother." She said it around a mouthful, which would have scandalized her father, which is partly why she did it. "How long have you known? About the lists. Truly."

Ah. So we had arrived there after all. I had wondered if we would. I had hoped, selfishly, that we might not.

I poured myself the last of the weak tea and bought a moment with it.

"Your father found them first," I said. "Nine years ago. He was doing salvage work on a wrecked transport barge and he pulled up a waterlogged strongbox with duplicate manifests inside. Same trick, even then. Names sold north, names sold to brokers, men counted twice so the company could collect twice." I watched her face take it. "He wasn't a fool. He knew what holding that knowledge meant. He wrote to a magistrate on the mainland — quietly, once, through a fish trader he trusted. The letter never arrived. Or it arrived and was buried; we never learned which. Six weeks later he went out on a calm sea in a sound boat and didn't come back."

Tomás had gone still. Elias had stopped pretending not to listen.

"The company called it weather," I said. "The priest called it God's accounting. I called it neither, and I kept my mouth shut, and I tended their orchard, and I raised two children inside their debt, and every year at the bridge opening I smiled at Brandt and asked after his digestion." My voice did something I hadn't authorized. I let it. "So yes, daughter. I have known since before you could write. And I hid it the way I hid everything — badly, in the only place I had, which was inside myself — and I let you grow up memorizing debts because I thought knowledge was a burden I could keep off you if I carried it alone."

"It nearly sank us both," Astrid said. Not cruelly. Just truly.

"Yes," I said. "Concealment is love's worst servant and its best disguise. I'd do half of it again and none of it the same way." I reached across the table and took her ink-stained hand. "But hear the part that matters: your father's letter failed because it traveled alone. One man, one letter, one boat. Tonight we sent out forty pieces through forty doors, and every one of those doors belongs to someone who hates the company exactly as much as we do. Your father died of being singular. We are going to live by being plural."

Nobody spoke for a moment. Outside, the rain kept its patient rhythm, and somewhere down the row a rooster made an idiot of itself hours before dawn, and Tomás — violating rule three, and I let it pass — whispered, "Plural. Mother's been saving that word for a special occasion."

* * *

We burned the scraps at first light. Not in our own stove — too much ash tells tales — but in the orchard fire pit where pruning debris burns daily and nobody examines smoke. Astrid fed the binding, the false margins, the practice sheets, watching them curl. Elias stood at the window on his own two feet for the first time in days, leaning on the frame, watching the smoke rise over the trees.

"They'll be looking for a book," he said. "Brandt will tear the compound apart looking for a book."

"Let him," I said. "He'll find forty kitchens, forty cushions, forty pairs of terrible boots, and one old woman's hat. And by the time he's done insulting all of them, the pages will have grown roots."

It sounded brave. It was mostly true. What I did not say — what I kept in the place where I keep things, though I am trying, at my age, to break the habit — was the arithmetic underneath: forty households now hold pieces of a hanging offense, and it takes only one frightened tongue, one bribed diver, one neighbor turned neighbor to undo the whole garment. Armor made of people protects beautifully and betrays just as easily. I have dressed in it all my life. I know both ways it can fail.

I was still thinking this when Astrid appeared at my elbow, collar high, eyes bright with a sleeplessness she wore like a decoration.

"Mother. The boat moves at dawn tomorrow, not noon. I heard it at the dinner — I lifted Brandt's key while I was there." She said it fast, the way she reports figures. "Which means the searches start tonight. Which means—"

Which means the net comes down today, is what it meant. Which means everything we sewed must hold before anyone tests the seams.

I untied my apron, folded it over the chair, and picked up my market basket.

"Then I'd better call on the overseers' wives," I said, "before they call on us."
