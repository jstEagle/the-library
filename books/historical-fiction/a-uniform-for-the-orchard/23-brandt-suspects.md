## 23. Brandt Suspects

Hendrik Brandt had a theory about theft, and it was this: a man could steal anything from anyone, provided the victim was made of paper.

He had built his career on that principle. Companies were paper. Debts were paper. The navy's conscription orders were paper, and so were the labor broker's receipts, and so was the honest face he wore to church on Sundays when there happened to be a mainland inspector in the congregation worth impressing. Paper did not bleed, did not weep, did not stand up at dinner and call you a thief. Paper only needed to be kept in the right room, under the right lock, and counted twice a week by a man who trusted no one — which was why Brandt slept well, most nights, on an island full of people who owed him their lives in installments.

So when he unlocked the cedar cabinet in his quarters on the morning after the season-closing dinner and found the duplicate ledger gone, his first feeling was not rage.

It was offense.

Someone had taken his arithmetic. His beautiful, private, extremely profitable arithmetic — every diver's name sold twice, once to the navy and once to Ossman's labor brokers on the mainland, the two columns running side by side like a hymn in harmony — and they had simply *lifted* it, the way you might lift a papaya off a neighbor's cart when the neighbor wasn't looking.

He stood there for a long moment with the empty folder in his hands, listening to the rain begin its morning argument against the roof.

Then he sat down at his desk, poured himself the first coffee of what he already understood would be a very long day, and began to think like a bookkeeper, because that was how he thought best.

* * *

The inventory of suspects was short, which was convenient.

First: outsiders. There were none. The island had been sealed for three days; the bridge was shut; the last boat sat at anchor in the strait like a fat patient duck waiting to be told to swim. Whatever had happened to the ledger had happened among his own people.

Second: the servants. He ran through them in his head the way he ran through accounts. The cook, who stole sugar but could not read. The houseboy, who stole nothing because he was too frightened of everything else to add ambition to his list of vices. The washerwoman, who read a little, mostly prayer books, and whose hands shook too badly to turn a small key in a small lock without leaving the whole door swinging.

Third — and here Brandt set down his cup, because the coffee had suddenly developed a taste like wet iron —

Third: the Almeidas.

He turned the name over the way he turned over any figure that refused to balance. Rosa Almeida, orchard keeper, widow, quiet as a held breath. She had been at the dinner. She had served at the long table with her hands folded and her eyes down, and she had laughed — softly, correctly — at the right moments. He remembered thinking at the time that she laughed like a woman who had rehearsed it. That should have pleased him. Compliance, rehearsed, was the backbone of civilization.

And the daughter. Astrid.

Brandt allowed himself, briefly, the pleasure of admiring her. It cost him nothing; admiration was free, unlike loyalty. Sixteen years old and she kept his ledgers better than the clerk he'd imported from Singapore, who drank. She added columns the way other girls skipped rope — effortlessly, rhythmically, with a little flourish at the end. When she brought him the weekly tallies she stood with her chin level and her hands still, and if there was fear in her he had never once been able to find it, and he had looked, because looking was his profession.

A girl who could keep books could also unkeep them.

He remembered something else now, something that had slid past him at the time like a coin dropped in deep water. At the dinner — while he had been charming the overseers' wives and lifting the key from his own belt to show young Ferreira how the new padlocks worked, an educational gesture, a mistake he would be chewing on for weeks — Astrid Almeida had refilled his glass. Twice. And both times she had stood just slightly too close to the desk where he'd left the wax and the spare impression block, because he was a careful man but not, evidently, careful enough, and because no one expects the bookkeeper to pick your pocket while handing you wine.

"Twice," he said aloud to the empty room. "She poured twice."

The rain said nothing. Rain never did. It was one of the few creatures on this island Brandt considered honest.

* * *

By eight o'clock he had dressed in his good grey suit — the one that made the wives say he looked like a bank, which he took as the compliment it was — and walked down through the compound in the rain with an umbrella he declined to open, because a man who feared water on an island of divers would never be trusted with their debts.

The compound woke around him. Divers' families filing toward the sheds, heads down against the weather. Children counting puddles. Somewhere the smell of frying fish and woodsmoke, ordinary as Sunday. He nodded to people and they nodded back with the particular blankness he had spent eleven years cultivating in them, and he found himself searching each face — the old pearl-cutter Vaz, the twins who mended nets, the woman who sold limes — for the specific expression of a person who knew where a ledger was sleeping.

They all wore the same face. That was the trouble with an island of people trained to have no expressions: eventually even guilt went into hiding behind them.

At the drying sheds he stopped and watched the tally being taken. The foreman called names; the men answered; a boy scratched marks onto a slate. Routine. Except that yesterday's count had come back one short in the second shed, corrected within the hour, explained as a man gone to the latrine. Small things. Brandt had built a fortune on small things — a name sold twice, a death recorded as drowning instead of what it was, a bridge closed a month early because a naval captain liked his whisky. Small things were how the world actually worked. Big crimes were just small crimes wearing uniforms.

And someone on this island had committed a very large small thing indeed.

"You." He beckoned the shed foreman, a broad, damp, decent-natured man named Corvo who feared him in the healthy way a dog fears thunder. "The Almeida boy. Tomás. Where is he this morning?"

Corvo blinked. "Tollhouse, sir. Or — well. What's left of it." He rubbed the back of his neck. "Been there since the fire, more or less. Helping clear."

"Has he now." Brandt smiled, and watched Corvo take a half-step backward, which was the correct response to that smile and the only intelligent thing anyone had done all morning. "Diligent family, the Almeidas. Very diligent. Tell me, Corvo — when the tollhouse burned, did anything strike you as odd?"

"Odd, sir?"

"Fire is generally odd, Corvo. It's the details I'm after."

Corvo considered this with the gravity of a philosopher. "It started from the seaward corner," he offered at last. "Which is where Tomás keeps the lamp oil. But—" and here he hesitated, visibly wrestling between honesty and self-preservation, and lost, as honest men usually do, "—the oil store wasn't burnt through, sir. Fire went *past* it. Like it was started somewhere drier."

"Like someone knew exactly which corner to light," Brandt said pleasantly, "and which stores to step around."

Corvo's face performed a small, doomed journey through several emotions before settling on blankness again. "I wouldn't know about that, sir."

"No," Brandt agreed. "You wouldn't. You're not clever enough, which is why I like you." He clapped the man's shoulder and moved on, leaving Corvo standing in the rain looking as though he'd been patted by a shark.

* * *

The tollhouse was a blackened ribcage against the grey water. Half of it had gone — the records room, naturally, because fire had a sense of humor — and the rest stood charred and dripping, with Tomás Almeida hauling timber out of the wreckage in the company of two other boys, all of them soaked to the skin and working with the furious energy of people who would rather lift heavy things than think.

Brandt stood at the edge of the site for a while, umbrella finally opened, watching the boy.

Tomás was nineteen and had his father's shoulders and his mother's stillness, and he was, by every account Brandt had collected over four years, the most cheerful criminal on the island. Tollhouse duty meant letters — everyone knew it, nobody proved it, and the letters that crossed the bridge in dry season in the pockets of pilgrims and traders were the closest thing the indentured families had to a postal service. Brandt had tolerated it the way one tolerates mice in the walls: the mice kept the snakes away, and besides, a man who let the poor pass love notes back and forth could always find them later when he needed a favor or a hostage.

But the ledger changed the arithmetic. A boy who smuggled letters would smuggle pages. A boy who smuggled pages had a mother who grew rice under tree roots — oh yes, Brandt knew about the rice cache, had known for years, had let it be because a woman who hid rice was a woman who could be ruined in one sentence whenever ruin became useful — and a sister who poured wine twice and stood too near the wax.

It fit. It fit the way a forged signature fits: perfectly, and damningly, and with just enough elegance that you almost wanted to frame it rather than act on it.

Almost.

"Tomás Almeida." He raised his voice just enough to carry over the rain.

The boy came down the blackened steps wiping his hands on his trousers. Up close he had ash in his hair and a scrape along one forearm, and he arranged his face into the open, easy expression of a young man with nothing whatsoever in his pockets. "Mr. Brandt, sir. Terrible business, the fire."

"It was. Arson generally is." Brandt studied him. "You weren't hurt?"

"Slept through most of it, sir. Woke up when the roof went." A shrug, artless, boyish. "Bad luck."

"Mm. Your sister — where is she this morning?"

Something crossed the boy's face then, quick as a fish under a boat: not guilt exactly, but its cousin, the flinch of a man hearing a question he'd been braced for. "Ledger room, sir. Where else? She's always there."

"Yes," Brandt said softly. "She is."

He thanked the boy, wished him luck with the timber, and walked back up the compound path feeling the pleasant, cold clarity that came over him when a case closed itself. Suspicion was a debt like any other. It accrued interest. And the Almeida account had just been marked for collection.

* * *

There remained, however, the problem of the boat.

This was the complication that kept Brandt pacing his office through the afternoon while the rain threw itself against the shutters like a creditor. The *Meridian* — the last boat out before the strait sealed — sat at anchor with one purpose above all others, and that purpose was not passengers. In her hold, packed inside crates of dried fish and copra, was his own cargo: four months of skimmed profits in silver and pearls, bound for Ossman's warehouse on the mainland, bound for the account that would one day buy Hendrik Brandt a house in Colombo with a veranda and no monsoon in sight. The armistice rumors were getting louder. Commissions of inquiry were being whispered about in the same breath. A prudent man got his money across the water before the lawyers arrived.

Which meant the *Meridian* had to sail. On schedule. With his cargo intact.

And he could not search her thoroughly without delaying her, and he could not delay her without explaining the delay, and he could not explain the delay without admitting to the naval liaison aboard — a Lieutenant Prasad, young, ambitious, and blessed with the moral flexibility of wet rope — that the company suspected contraband, at which point Prasad would insist on supervising the search personally, at which point the search would become official, at which point whatever was found would be found *in front of witnesses*, and if what was found was a stolen duplicate ledger full of names sold twice, then Hendrik Brandt's next voyage would be to Colombo in irons.

Greed, he reflected, pouring his third coffee, was a marvelous engine and a terrible passenger. It had gotten him everything he had, and now it sat in the hold of his own escape plan, weighing anchor.

No. There was a cleaner way.

He sent for Sergeant Dharmapala of the compound guard — a solid, unimaginative man who could search a hold the way a hound searches luggage, thoroughly and without conversation — and gave him instructions that sounded simple and were anything but.

"The *Meridian* sails at dawn tomorrow. Before she loads the final crates, you will conduct a routine customs inspection. Contraband check. Standard procedure, nothing dramatic." He watched the sergeant nod. "You are looking for weapons, desertion stowaways, untaxed goods. You are *not*, officially, looking for papers. If papers happen to be found — a bundle, a satchel, a collar with something sewn into it — you will bring them to me quietly, before anyone else sees them. Quietly, Sergeant. The word 'quietly' is doing a great deal of work in this conversation."

"And if I find nothing, sir?"

"Then you'll report that the inspection was clean, and the boat sails, and everyone is happy." Brandt smiled. "Especially me."

Because that was the beauty of it. If the ledger was on the boat — sewn into a collar, folded into a satchel, hidden in a crate of his own copra, the little ironies available to fate being endless — then Dharmapala would bring it to him, and the problem would drown quietly in the strait along with everything else the sea was owed. And if the ledger was *not* on the boat —

Then it was still on the island. Still in someone's rafters, someone's rice cache, someone's collar. And the island, thanks to the early monsoon and the closed bridge, was the tightest box in the ocean. No witnesses in. No testimony out. Whatever truth the Almeidas thought they were carrying, they would carry it in circles until the commission — if it ever came — arrived to find a cooperative population, a spotless company, and an overseer in a good grey suit saying *ledger? what ledger?*

Either way, he won. That was the thing people never understood about bookkeeping: done properly, it was prophecy.

* * *

In the evening he permitted himself one indulgence, which was a walk past the ledger room.

The window was lit. Through the rain-blurred glass he could see Astrid Almeida at her desk, lamp low, pen moving, the picture of industry. She must have felt him — women like her always felt him, that was what made them dangerous — because she looked up, met his eyes through the window, and inclined her head. Not a bow. An acknowledgment. One professional to another.

Then she went back to her columns.

Brandt stood in the rain a moment longer than was dignified, and found, to his mild irritation, that he was smiling. Good, he thought. Be confident. Confidence made people careless, and carelessness made them legible, and legible people balanced their accounts eventually, one way or another.

Tomorrow the boat would be searched. Tomorrow evening, if the sea gods were kind and Sergeant Dharmapala was thorough, he would be holding a certain bundle of rice paper in his hands, and the day after that the *Meridian* would carry his silver across the strait and the island would swallow its own secret whole.

He was halfway back to his quarters, already composing the polite note he would send Rosa Almeida inviting her to discuss her daughter's future — promotions made such useful leashes — when a runner caught up with him, breathless, dripping, holding out a slip of naval signal paper.

From the mainland relay station. Priority mark.

Brandt read it twice. The rain drummed on his umbrella. Somewhere behind him, in the lit window, the pen of Astrid Almeida went on writing, steady as a pulse.

The signal was brief. A military police launch had put in at the mainland relay that afternoon carrying warrants. One of the names on the manifest, forwarded for local execution at the company's convenience, was *Almeida, Tomás — tollhouse, deserter correspondence, harbor master's complaint.*

They would arrive with the dawn tide. The same tide that lifted the *Meridian*. The same hour as the search.

Brandt folded the paper into his breast pocket, and for the first time in eleven years on the island, Hendrik Brandt felt the faint, unfamiliar sensation of a column refusing to balance — too many events crowding into one morning, too many moving pieces, and somewhere among them a sixteen-year-old girl who poured wine twice and wrote in a hand too steady for her age.

"Well," he said to the rain, straightening his cuffs. "Let's see who drowns first."
