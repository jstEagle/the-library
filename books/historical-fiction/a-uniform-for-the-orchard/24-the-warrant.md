## 24. The Warrant

The morning the soldiers came, Tomás Almeida was eating a stolen mango and thinking about his own funeral.

Not in a morbid way. In the way of a man who has spent three weeks doing arithmetic with his life and keeps arriving at the same sum. The warrant had been coming since the tollhouse fire — he'd known it the way you know rain is coming, not from any single sign but from the whole weight of the air. Brandt's searches had doubled. The soldiers at the bridge had started checking faces against a list instead of just waving through the company men. And old Ferreira, who sold dried fish and gossip in equal measure, had leaned across his cart two days ago and said, without looking up from his scales, "They printed something with your name on it, boy. I'd eat light and sleep standing."

So Tomás had done what he always did with unbearable things: he made them funny.

"Small funeral," he told the mango, before biting into it. "Good turnout. Mother cries once, efficiently, then feeds everyone. Astrid reads out my debts so people know exactly what they're mourning." He chewed. "Actually, that could take hours. Maybe they read an abridged version."

The mango was excellent. Green-gold, sweet as a bribe, taken that dawn from the eastern rows of the orchard where Rosa's trees grew the best fruit precisely because no one guarded them — everyone knew the company orchard belonged to the company, and the company never ate its own mangoes, which Tomás considered proof that the company deserved to be robbed.

He was sitting on the sea wall behind the drying sheds, feet dangling, when he heard the boots.

That was the thing about soldiers. They never learned to walk quietly. Company men moved soft, like thieves and accountants, but soldiers walked like the ground owed them money. Four sets of boots, maybe five, coming up the compound path at a pace that said *we are in no hurry because there is nowhere for anyone to go.*

Tomás did not panic. He would later be proud of this. He finished the mango, wiped his hands on his trousers with great thoroughness, and dropped the seed into the water below the wall — because if he was going to run for his life, he refused to do it littering.

Then he slid down the far side of the wall and went looking for his mother.

* * *

Rosa Almeida was in the yard behind the house, beating a mat with the kind of violence usually reserved for enemies, when Tomás came around the corner at a crouch, which made him look less like a fugitive and more like a man sneaking up on his own family.

"They've come," he said. "Boots. Five pairs, maybe six. Coming up the path."

Rosa did not stop beating the mat. That was the first miracle of the morning. Her arms kept their rhythm — *whack, whack, whack* — and her face kept its expression, which was the flat, sun-baked look she wore for overseers and other weather.

"How long?"

"Two minutes. Three if they stop to kick the dog, and Sergeant Ossia always kicks the dog."

"The dog bites him back. Two minutes." She stood the mat against the wall and looked at him, and here was the second miracle: there was no fear in her face at all. There was inventory. He could see her taking stock of him the way Astrid took stock of ledgers — height, build, the warrant in some clerk's fist with his name spelled wrong or right, the distance to the mangroves, the tide.

"You can't be seen near this house," she said.

"I know."

"You can't be seen near *anyone*. If they find you in the village, they'll take whoever opened a door for you."

"I know, Mãe."

"And you will not come back here. Not tonight, not tomorrow, not because it rains, not because you're hungry. You'll want to. You'll stand in the cane field at midnight telling yourself one visit won't hurt. It will hurt. It will hurt everyone."

Tomás felt something in his chest fold up small, like paper creased too many times. "I said I know."

"You say you know things all the time. Knowing is cheap." She crossed the yard, took his face in both hands — flour on her palms, he could smell the bread she'd been making, bread for a house that might be searched within the hour — and pressed her forehead to his. For a moment neither of them said anything, and the boots got louder, and the moment was very short and very complete, the way good moments are.

Then she pushed him toward the fence line and became, instantly, a different woman: loud, irritated, ordinary.

"The gate!" she called after him, at full maternal volume. "You were going to fix the gate and you've left the hinge off again! Useless boy! Go on, get the hinge, I'm not paying Ferreira's son to do what you promised!"

It was beautiful. It was, Tomás thought as he vaulted the fence and dropped into the cane, the finest performance he had ever witnessed, and he had watched Astrid lie to Brandt's face over a tally sheet with her pulse steady as a metronome. His mother had turned his escape into a domestic complaint audible to every soldier on the path. By the time they reached the yard, the story of the neighborhood was already fixed: Rosa Almeida, shouting at her worthless son about a gate hinge, son storming off toward the village in a sulk. A boy leaving, loudly, in daylight, in the opposite direction from anywhere he'd actually go.

Except he wasn't going toward the village. He cut hard right through the cane, low, moving the way he'd learned moving contraband letters for six years — never in a straight line, never twice through the same gap, always knowing where the next cover was before you needed it.

Behind him, faintly, he heard the soldiers arrive at the house. Heard Ossia's voice, bored and cruel: "Papers for a Tomás Almeida. Warrant. Where is he?"

And heard his mother say, with magnificent irritation, "Probably stealing something. He does that."

* * *

He lay in the cane for an hour, which was the worst hour of his life up to that point, listening to the search he couldn't see.

Sound carried strangely in the fields. Boots became everywhere at once. A shout by the drying sheds might mean they'd found something or might mean a sergeant had found a lizard and wanted witnesses to his courage. Once, close — too close, ten rows away — a voice said, "Check the cane," and Tomás pressed himself into a furrow with his cheek against wet earth and counted his breaths the way Astrid had taught him when they were children hiding from Father's moods. *In for four. Out for four. Your body lies to you when it panics; make it tell the truth.*

They didn't check the cane. Or they checked it badly. Either way, the boots moved on toward the village, and after a while the compound settled into its afternoon noises — hammers, gulls, someone singing badly at the washing lines — and Tomás allowed himself to move.

He should have gone straight for the mangroves. That was the plan, the plan he'd made three weeks ago with the same arithmetic that had produced the funeral musings: warrant comes, run south, mangroves, coracle, Elias's contacts, gone. Simple. Clean. Heartbreak as logistics, which was the only way heartbreak was survivable.

Instead he went to the tollhouse.

He told himself it was to retrieve the letter cache — the oilskin packet wedged under the third floorboard, four years of other families' words, which could hang him twice over if the soldiers ever thought to look. This was even true. But it was also true that the tollhouse was the only place on the island that had ever felt like his. Six years of dry seasons, six years of watching the bridge crowd pour across, of slipping letters into coat linings, of being — for a few weeks a year — the man who connected this sealed world to every other world. The tollhouse smelled of tar and dust and other people's destinations, and he loved it the way you love a thing that was never really yours.

He got the packet. He was re-seating the floorboard when the door opened behind him and he nearly died of fright, which would have saved everyone a great deal of trouble.

It was Ferreira's boy, Duarte, fourteen years old, all elbows and discretion, holding a basket of fish as a prop.

"Mãe sent me," Duarte said. "She says: don't come home, don't come to the shop, don't come anywhere you're expected. She says your sister knows the rest of the plan and will find you when it's safe. She says—" the boy frowned, reciting, "—'tell him if he eats anything Ferreira sells him he'll die of it before the soldiers get the chance.'"

"That's her all over," Tomás said. His voice cracked on *all*, and he covered it with a laugh. "Anything else?"

Duarte shifted. "She said to tell you she's proud of you. But she said to tell you that part quiet, so." He said the last part quiet.

Then he put the fish basket down on the tollhouse counter — inside it, under the fish, a cloth bundle of bread and dried mango and a knife that Tomás recognized as his mother's good one, the one she'd sworn she'd buried with her husband — and left, elbows first.

Tomás sat on the tollhouse steps with the food in his lap and let himself shake for exactly one minute. He timed it by the church bell, which rang the quarter hour. When the bell stopped, he stopped, tied the bundle to his belt, and went south.

* * *

The mangroves took him in at dusk.

Everyone hated the mangroves — the mud that sucked at your legs like a debt collector, the roots that tripped you, the smell of rot and salt, the mosquitoes in congregations large enough to elect officials. Tomás loved them. The mangroves were the island's honest country: nothing here pretended to be pleasant, and nothing here belonged to the company. The roots held the shoreline together out of pure stubbornness, which he had always found morally instructive.

He worked his way along the channels he knew, following the memory of them more than the sight, because the light was going and the channels changed with every tide anyway. Twice he heard patrols on the shore paths — lantern-light swinging, voices flattened by the water. Once he heard his own name, spoken by a stranger's mouth with a stranger's boredom, and it was the strangest feeling of his life: *Tomás Almeida*, wanted, official, a piece of paper walking around wearing his name while he crouched in the mud being nobody at all.

By full dark he reached the staging cove — the pocket beach where the coracles waited, where Astrid would bring Elias when the night was right, where the last boat would take someone away from all this. He settled into the hollow between two root buttresses that he'd used before, ate half the bread, wrapped the rest away, and began the waiting.

Waiting was a skill. He'd learned it at the tollhouse, where the work was ninety percent watching and ten percent moving fast. So he watched. The cove. The channel. The dark line of the strait where the last boat would come from, eventually, carrying the berth that everyone in the village had bled money to buy for him.

That was the thought that finally caught up with him, out here in the dark with nothing to distract him. The berth. The pooled coins — fisherman's coins, washerwoman's coins, the widow Costa's coins that she'd earned gutting fish with hands ruined by salt. All of it gathered to buy one seat on one boat, and every coin given on the understanding that it bought out *him*, Tomás, the boy with the warrant, the one whose neck was actually at risk.

And somewhere in the last week, without deciding it, he had begun to understand that the math didn't hold anymore.

He'd seen the copied lists. Astrid had shown him — pages small as prayers, sewn where no one would think to look — and he'd understood in one cold flash what Elias was worth. A deserter who could name names. Who could stand in front of a commission and unravel Brandt's whole ledger with his testimony. Whose word could void the conscriptions, the debts, the claims — including the claim the company held on every indentured family on this island, including the claim it held on his sister, who stayed behind, who would stay behind, who had already decided it before anyone asked her.

His warrant died with the company's legitimacy. If the lists surfaced, if the testimony landed, there would be no legitimate authority left to hang him. But if the boat carried him instead of Elias, and Elias rotted in the drying sheds until the rains sealed the strait and the searches closed in —

No. He wouldn't do that arithmetic out loud yet. Saying it made it real, and real things had to be argued with, and he wasn't ready to argue with Astrid, who argued like a ledger closing.

The tide turned. The channel murmured. Somewhere north, in the compound, his mother was sleeping in a house that soldiers had already torn apart once today and would tear apart again, and his sister was keeping books for the man hunting them all, smiling at him across a desk, counting days.

A branch snapped at the edge of the cove.

Tomás was on his feet with his mother's knife out before his mind caught up — and then a shape resolved in the dark, small and sure-footed, picking its way down through the roots with the particular economy of movement he'd have known anywhere, in any light, in any life.

"Astrid?" he whispered. "What are you— you can't be here. If you're seen—"

"I wasn't seen." She stepped into the hollow, mud to her knees, breathing hard, and in the faint starlight her face was set in the expression he privately called *the closing balance* — the one that meant a decision had been made and the books were already updated. "There's a patrol crossing the spit. We have maybe twenty minutes. And Tomás—" she caught his arm, grip fierce, "—we need to talk about the berth. Now. Before you fall in love with dying for something."

He laughed, because it was that or weep. "Too late," he said. "I fell in love with it weeks ago. It's very devoted. Brings me flowers."

"Well," said Astrid, pulling him down into the dark as the lantern-light swung onto the spit, "you're about to have an argument with it."
