## 25. Mangrove Night

The coracle was older than I was and had been patched by people who believed in hope more than in caulking.

Tomás had built it the year he turned twelve, weaving rattan over a frame of bamboo and coating it with tree sap and melted pitch until it looked like a great grey bowl that some careless giant had set down at the edge of the mangroves and forgotten. It had carried firewood. It had carried smuggled letters sewn into fish. It had never, in all its honest life, carried a wounded deserter and the evidence that could unmake a company — but there was no time to build anything better, and the tide was going out, and the soldiers' lanterns were already moving along the shore like a string of low orange stars that had lost their way.

"Get in," I whispered.

Elias looked at the coracle. Then at me. Then at the water, which was black and breathing.

"It floats," I said. "Mostly."

"That's not the word you want before a crossing."

"It's the only word I have. Get in."

He got in, folding himself down with his bad shoulder held stiff, and the coracle settled into the shallows with a groan of wet rattan that sounded, to my strung-tight ears, like it was announcing us to the entire garrison. I slid in after him with the paddle and the oilcloth bundle strapped across my chest, and we pushed off from the root-tangle just as the first lantern came around the point behind us.

I did not look back. Looking back is how boats tip. My father taught me that, or the sea did, on the last day he ever stood on one.

* * *

The mangroves grow along the island's western shore in a green darkness that goes on for two miles, roots rising out of the water like the knuckles of something enormous sleeping just under the mud. In daylight they smell of salt and rot and life. At night they simply smell of dark. The channels between them are narrow enough that a grown man can touch both banks at once, and twisty as a ledger someone else has been cooking, and I knew them the way I knew my columns — because Tomás had made me learn every bend when I was small, paddling me through by lantern-light while he recited the turns like poetry.

*Left at the leaning one. Right where the herons nest. Straight past the drowned boat until the water tastes of open sea.*

I had thought those lessons were a game. Tomás had known, even then, that someday the mangroves would be the only door out of the island that no soldier would think to guard. My brother planned everything twice: once for the life he wanted and once for the life he'd probably get.

The paddle was a broken oar-shaft, half its blade gone. It caught on roots, on mud, on the invisible things the tide drags beneath the surface, and each catch sent a shudder through the coracle that Elias bore with clenched teeth. His wound had opened again during the run from the house — Rosa had bound it with strips of her good sheet and her worse temper ("If you tear that again, boy, I will sew you to the floor"), but blood does what blood does, and I could feel the warmth of it soaking through the cloth between us every time the boat rocked us together.

"I'm sorry," he murmured, for perhaps the tenth time since the spit.

"If you apologize once more," I said, "I will put you out and let you walk."

"You can't put me out. You need my testimony."

"I need quiet more."

That shut him up for nearly four minutes, which I counted against the rhythm of my paddling, and then the lanterns found the shore ahead of us.

There were six of them at least, spread along the beach where the mangroves thinned to sand, and behind them, faint in the rain-smell, boots. The military had come off the gunboat after midnight — Brandt's doing; only Brandt could have talked the navy into lending him men for a manhunt on the eve of the strait closing — and they were doing the thing soldiers do badly, which is search somewhere they don't know, loudly, in a line, as though the world were a field instead of a maze.

"They'll sweep to the point," Elias breathed, "then come back."

"How do you know?"

"Because that's what I'd do. Before." He said *before* the way other people say *another life*, and I understood that on the mainland he had been the lantern on the shore, not the shadow under it, and that this knowledge sat in him like a swallowed stone.

We let the coracle drift. That is the hardest thing I have ever done with my hands — holding them still on a paddle while armed men walked a beach forty yards away, holding my breath until black flowers bloomed behind my eyes, holding still while my shoulders screamed and the current took us sideways toward an open channel and the lanterns. The tide was our accomplice and our traitor both. It pulled us out of their sightline and pulled us, equally patiently, toward the mouth of the channel where the mangroves ended and the strait began, wide and silvered with rain, with nowhere at all to hide.

A voice on the shore called something. Another answered. A dog barked twice and was silenced.

And then the line moved on, boots squelching northward, and the night closed over us again like water over a dropped pearl.

I paddled until my arms stopped being arms and became two aches attached to a stubbornness.

* * *

Somewhere in the middle miles, Elias began to talk. Not loudly — a thread of voice pitched under the rain that had started falling fine and needling out of the clouds. Delirious, maybe. Or maybe just a man who needed to fill the dark so the dark wouldn't fill itself.

"My mother kept hens," he said. "Forty of them. She named every one. She said if she named them they couldn't be taken for granted, and if they couldn't be taken for granted they'd keep laying."

"What happened to the hens?"

"The army happened to the hens." A breath that might have been a laugh. "She wrote to me about them for two years. Then the letters started arriving already opened. Then they stopped arriving at all. That's when I knew which side of the paper I lived on."

I said nothing. Paddling takes everything, and besides, there are things you can say to a stranger in a boat that you cannot say on land, and things you cannot say anywhere, and the space between them is best crossed in silence.

"Astrid Almeida," he said, after a while. "Your mother told me. She talks in her sleep, your brother snores, and your mother talks. Do you know what you're carrying?"

"The truth."

"The truth is heavy. Everyone thinks it's light because it fits in a sentence. But you carry it in your body, and your body notices." He shifted, winced. "My sergeant used to say a lie weighs nothing going in and everything coming out. The truth's the opposite. It weighs everything going in and nothing coming out. You just have to get to the other side of it."

"Is that supposed to comfort me?"

"It's supposed to prepare you. Comfort's for afterward." He paused. "If there is an afterward."

"There's always an afterward. My mother says so. She says it the way she says grace — daring God to disagree."

Another laugh, weaker. Then, quietly, almost lost under the drip of the rain off the leaves: "When we get there — the cove — if it comes down to choosing—"

"Don't," I said. "Not tonight. Tonight nobody chooses anything except which channel."

"But—"

"Which. Channel."

He subsided. The coracle went on. And I will admit here, in the privacy of my own telling, that I said it not because I hadn't thought about it but because I had thought about it every hour for three days, and thinking about it felt like standing at the edge of the pearl beds looking down into water too deep to see the bottom of, and I was not ready to dive. Not yet. There were still miles of mangrove between me and the arithmetic, and I intended to spend every one of them being nothing more complicated than a girl with a paddle.

* * *

The leaning tree came at last — a great tilted giant leaning so far over the channel that its crown brushed the water on the far side, hung with orchids like pale lamps. Left at the leaning one. I steered us under its dripping arch, and the channel beyond bent away from the shore entirely, back into the deep green interior of the swamp where the soldiers would not follow because the soldiers did not know it was passable.

Here the exhaustion caught me properly. It had been circling all night like a gull over a fish, waiting, and now it came down.

My hands would not close on the paddle shaft anymore. I had to wrap my fingers around it one at a time, deliberately, like tying knots. My eyes kept sliding out of focus so that the roots doubled and swam. Once I nodded clean asleep mid-stroke and woke with the coracle spinning slowly in a pool of black glass, Elias watching me with an expression I couldn't read.

"Rest," he said. "Ten minutes. We're hidden. Even the mosquitoes have gone to bed."

"We can't—"

"We can. If you drop, we both drown, and I've read enough ledgers to know drowning isn't in anyone's interest." He held out his good hand. "Give me the paddle. I'll hold us against the current. You sleep."

"You can't paddle with that shoulder."

"I'm not going to paddle. I'm going to anchor. There's a difference, and it's exactly ten minutes wide."

I gave him the paddle because my body mutinied before my pride could stop it, and I curled into the bottom of the coracle with the oilcloth bundle hugged to my ribs, and I slept — truly slept, the heavy dreamless sleep of the completely spent — for what the position of the moon later told me was closer to twenty minutes than ten.

I woke to Elias humming. Very softly, very badly, a tune I didn't know, his head tipped back against the woven rim, rain beading on his face.

"What is that?"

"Hen song. My mother made it up. One verse per hen." His eyes stayed closed. "It has forty verses. You're lucky I only remember nine."

"Sing the ninth."

"'Bessie, Bessie, brownest hen, / laid an egg at half past ten, / half past ten was late, it's true, / but Bessie, Bessie, what would you?'" He opened one eye. "It improves with the other thirty-one."

"Nothing could improve that."

"No," he agreed comfortably. "But it passes the time, and passing the time is ninety percent of surviving. Nobody tells you that. They tell you about courage and sacrifice. What actually keeps you alive is having a song for the ninth hen and a reason to know what comes after the bridge closes." He looked at me then, fully, and the humor drained out of his face and left something steadier underneath. "You have the reason. I've watched you all night. You're not brave, Astrid. Bravery runs out. You're something better. You just keep."

I didn't know what to say to that, so I took the paddle back and kept.

* * *

The last hour was the worst, because the worst hour is always the one where you believe it's over.

We came down the final channel with the sea-salt sharp in our mouths and the trees opening around us onto the staging cove — and heard voices. Real ones, close ones, and the knock of wood on wood, and I froze with the paddle half-raised like a fool about to duel the mangroves.

Then the voices resolved. *"Mind the gunwale, mind it, you great ox—"*

Tomás.

He came wading out between the roots with a lantern shuttered down to a coin of light, and behind him the cove opened up: three fishing boats riding at anchor, black-hulled and patient, and figures moving among them with the practiced quiet of people who had been smuggling things past authorities since before any of us were born. Uncle Ferro's people. The pooled village money had bought more than a berth — it had bought a crew that owed debts of its own.

Tomás reached the coracle, grabbed its rim, and looked at the two of us huddled in it — me gray-lipped and shaking, Elias white-faced with his bandage blooming red — and something crossed his face that was relief and grief holding hands.

"You look terrible," he said.

"You should see the other boat," I managed.

"There is no other boat."

"Precisely."

He laughed — that desperate bright laugh of his, the one he wore like the tollhouse wore its lamp, lit against the dark whether or not anyone came — and hauled the coracle in by hand, wading hip-deep, refusing all help, while Elias clambered out onto the roots and stood swaying like a heron.

And that is when Tomás saw the bundle.

I had meant to explain it gently. On dry land. With sentences I had rehearsed. Instead he simply saw it — the oilcloth shape strapped where I'd carried it all night, the shape too flat and too deliberate for a change of clothes — and his hands went still on the coracle's rim, and the laughter drained out of him the way water drains from a pulled plug, and he looked up at me across three feet of black tidal water with the whole night in his face.

"Astrid," he said slowly. "What did you bring?"

Behind him, at the nearest boat, a man called out in a low urgent voice that the tide was turning and the boat would leave within the hour, and that there was room — the last room — for one.
