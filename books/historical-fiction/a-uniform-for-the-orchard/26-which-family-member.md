## 26. Which Family Member

The cove had no name on any map I'd ever copied, which was precisely why Elias had chosen it. A bite of gray water folded between two shoulders of mangrove, a shelf of sand that appeared and vanished with the tide, and above it all a curtain of rain so thick it seemed less like weather than like a decision the sky had made about us.

We arrived in pieces. That was the only way to arrive anywhere now.

I came first with the coracle, paddling the last stretch with arms that had stopped being arms somewhere around midnight and become two lengths of rope someone else was pulling. Elias sat behind me, too tall for the little boat, one hand trailing in the water because he said — once, hoarsely, early in the crossing when there was still breath for talking — that if he was going to be shot he'd rather it happened with salt water already on him, so his mother's ghost wouldn't know the difference.

Tomás came second, on foot, through the interior, guided by old Serafim who knew paths that didn't exist until you needed them to. He stepped out of the treeline an hour before dawn, soaked to the ribs, grinning the way he grinned when a toll went uncollected or a letter crossed the bridge sewn into a fishmonger's apron.

And the pages came third, distributed through six bodies. Rosa held the spine-pages against her stomach beneath her shawl, where any searching hand would find what it expected to find there and stop looking. Three diver families carried leaves of the duplicate ledger in their fish baskets, their rice jars, the false bottoms of their children's toy boats. I carried the master copy against my ribs, wrapped in oilcloth, sewn into the lining of a vest that had belonged to my father and now carried more of him than his name ever had.

By full light we were eleven people in a cove the size of a chapel, waiting for a boat the size of a coffin lid, and the arithmetic was already sitting among us like a thirteenth passenger nobody would look at directly.

* * *

The boat was called the *Mercê*, which meant mercy, which meant some boatwright had a sense of humor. She was a fishing lugger gone gray with salt, captained by a man named Duarte who owed my mother more favors than he could count and was therefore deeply unhappy to see us. Favors, on this island, were just debts wearing nicer clothes. I knew. I kept the ledgers.

"One berth," Duarte said, not looking at any of us, spitting into the water as though the word itself tasted bad. "One. The navy counts heads at the point, and they count twice since the fire. One person goes out with the catch and doesn't come back, and that's the whole miracle I can work." He wiped his mouth. "I'm sorry, Rosa. I told your husband once I'd carry anyone across this strait for free, and I meant it, but even a dead man can't carry two."

Rosa stood very still, the way she stood when she was deciding whether a storm was worth arguing with. "How long?"

"High tide tomorrow. Maybe the one after, if the rain holds off. After that the strait closes and stays closed, and whatever happens on the mainland happens without us."

He left us then, walking back along the sand to his crew of two, leaving the arithmetic alone with us at last.

Elias sat down on a fallen palm trunk. His wound had healed enough that he could walk, could even run if running were required, but sitting down still looked like a decision he made carefully, the way an old man decides about stairs. He pulled his coat tighter — my father's other coat, actually, given to him three weeks ago by Rosa with the same expression she wore when feeding anyone: defiance disguised as housekeeping.

"So," Tomás said.

Nobody answered him. Rain ticked on the mangrove leaves. Somewhere out past the curtain of gray, the navy launch made its slow circuit of the point, a shape like a knife turning over.

"So," Tomás said again, louder, and this time he looked at me. "Are we going to say it, or are we going to stand here being polite until the tide makes the decision for us? Because I'll tell you something about politeness, Astrid. It's what people do instead of counting."

"Tomás," Rosa said.

"No, Mãe. Let me." He turned in a slow circle, addressing all of us — the three families huddled under an awning of woven palm, old Serafim pretending to mend a net that needed no mending, Elias on his log. "There's one berth. There are two of us who need it. Everyone here has been doing sums since sundown and everyone has been doing them quietly, the way we do everything on this island, quietly, politely, until the company does our arithmetic for us. Well. Not tonight."

"Tomás—" I started.

"You first, then." He pointed at Elias. Not cruelly. Tomás had never been cruel in his life; he'd been reckless, funny, infuriating, generous to the point of self-harm, but never cruel. He pointed at Elias the way a teacher points at a sum on a slate. "You're a deserter from the Third Coastal Regiment. If the navy takes you here, you hang — maybe. If they're feeling legal. If they're feeling efficient, you drown in the channel with your wrists tied, and the report says attempted escape. Either way, you're dead, and the lists stay hidden, and Brandt keeps selling every name on this island twice."

Elias said nothing. His jaw worked once, like a man chewing on something that fought back.

"And me." Tomás spread his hands, showman to the last, though I could see the effort it cost him — the grin was load-bearing, holding up something that wanted very badly to fall down. "I have a warrant. Actual paper, actual seal. They burned half the tollhouse looking for me and got close enough Tuesday night that Serafim's dog is still hoarse from barking. If I stay, I hide in the interior eating yams until the war ends or I don't. Those are the options. Yams or the rope." He smiled wider. "Honestly, some days the rope sounds cleaner. Have you smelled an interior yam pit?"

Nobody laughed. Even Tomás noticed. His smile flickered like a lamp low on oil.

"But here's the part everyone's been too polite to say," he went on, quieter now. "If I get on that boat, I'm just a smuggler with a warrant, running. I reach the mainland, I disappear, I write letters nobody can answer. Fine. Good, even. I live. But if *he* gets on that boat—" and here he did look at Elias, straight on, no performance in it at all— "he can name Brandt's buyer. He sat in the room. He watched the names change hands. His testimony voids every forged conscription list on this island, and every forged list voids every debt the company holds against every family in this compound, including ours, including yours, Mãe, including Astrid's indenture, which that woman-copied ledger says runs another nine years. Nine years. I did the sum. I always do the sum."

The rain filled the silence. Out on the water, the navy knife turned over again, patient as a heron.

"So there it is," Tomás said. "One berth. Him or me. And I want it said out loud, in front of God and the mangroves and old Serafim's terrible mending, so nobody gets to lie about it later and call it fate."

* * *

The families began to murmur. I heard my own name in the murmuring, and Rosa's, and the strange truth that a crowd will do your deciding for you if you let it, gently, with the best intentions, until you wake up one morning owned by everyone's kindness the way you were once owned by one man's ledger.

I let them murmur for exactly as long as it took me to walk to my brother.

Up close I could see what the grin had been hiding: he hadn't slept in four days; there was a scratch down one cheek from the mangroves, crusted black; and his eyes had the flat shine of a man who had already done this arithmetic a hundred times and gotten the same answer every time and hated the answer with his whole body.

"You've decided," I said. It wasn't a question. I'd grown up watching him decide things in the tollhouse — which letters to carry, which guards to bribe, which nights the bridge stayed open ten minutes late. Tomás never agonized in public. He agonized afterward, privately, expensively, the way he paid back every favor double.

"I've decided," he agreed.

"You're wrong."

"Astrid—"

"You're wrong, and you know you're wrong, and you're going to make me say why in front of everyone because you think it'll be easier if it's public." I kept my voice level. Below us, the tide was coming in, eating the sand shelf inch by inch, the cove shrinking around us like a closing fist. "So I'll say it. Your warrant dies with the company. You said it yourself. If those lists surface — if Brandt falls — there's no legitimate authority left to honor that paper. The soldiers tear it up or frame it, whichever amuses them. You don't need the berth, Tomás. You need three weeks and a bit of luck, and you've lived twenty years on three weeks and a bit of luck. It's practically your trade."

"And if the armistice fails?" he said softly. "If the commission never comes, or comes too late, or comes and decides an island of pearl divers isn't worth upsetting a trading company over? Then my warrant outlives the company, and I hang, and you spent your one chance on sentiment." He shook his head. "No. Don't build my escape on maybes. Build it on evidence. Evidence crosses water. Maybes drown."

"Elias can't testify if he's dead," I said. "You said it yourself — the channel, the patrols, the point where they count heads twice. He's the most hunted man on this island wearing the most visible face. What if they search the boat? What if they take him at the point? Then we've spent the berth on nothing, and you're still here, and we've lost both."

"Then we've lost both anyway," Tomás said, "because the alternative is I save myself and watch the only witness to everything rot in the interior until the rains end and Brandt finds him. I'd rather lose properly than win like that."

"That," I said, "is the stupidest noble thing you've ever said, and you once jumped off the bridge on a dare to impress a girl who couldn't swim."

"It worked, though. She married a fisherman with a boat. Impression lasts." But the grin cracked down the middle, and for a moment my brother stood there not grinning at all, and he looked sixteen again, the way he had the day Father drowned, standing on the dock asking me questions neither of us could answer: *what does the water keep, Astrid, does it keep him, will we get him back.*

Behind us, Elias rose from the log.

It took him a while. We both heard the effort in it — the small involuntary sound he made, quickly swallowed. When he spoke, his voice was rough, and he spoke in our language, badly, syllable by syllable, which was itself an argument, because he had learned our words the way a man learns the words of the place he intends to spend his life paying rent to.

"You are both wrong," he said. "Both. Listen." He held up one finger, then a second, a schoolmaster of the obvious. "One: if I go and they take the boat, I hang alone, and the lists are still on this island, safe in your collars and your rice jars, and you can send them later, next season, next year. My death buys nothing if I die before the mainland. Two—" the second finger— "if Tomás goes and hangs nothing, hides in yams, fine, good. But then who moves the lists when I cannot? Who carries them across when the commission sits? Not your mother — they watch her garden now. Not Serafim — he is older than this bridge." He looked at me then, and there was something in his face I hadn't seen before, something that had been forming for weeks underneath the guilt and the gratitude, patient as coral. "Not me. Me, they know by now. My face is on a wall in the garrison with a number under it."

The murmuring stopped. Even the rain seemed to lean in.

"So the lists need a carrier," Elias said, "who is not hunted, not known, not expected. Someone who walks past Brandt every day and smiles at him. Someone whose body has already proven it can carry this island's secrets sewn against its skin." He paused, and the pause was gentle, and it was terrible. "Someone who is not either of us."

"No," Rosa said.

"Mãe—" I said.

"*No.*" She came forward out of the crowd of families, shawl dark with rain, and she planted herself between Elias and me like a tree that had decided, after forty years, to move. "I buried one child to this water and I have fed the rest of my life into keeping the other two out of it. I am not choosing between my son and my daughter. I refuse. Find a third option or I will invent one myself, and my inventions involve Brandt's dinner table and I promise you none of you want to hear them."

But there wasn't a third option. That was the thing about arithmetic — it didn't care how loudly you refused it. One berth. Two candidates. And now, hovering at the edge of the count like a figure at the edge of a photograph, a third possibility that was worse than either, because it wore my face and my mother's fear and it fit.

Tomás looked at Elias. Elias looked at Tomás. And I realized, with the particular cold clarity of a clerk finding the error in her own column, that they had both seen it — seen me — and that each of them had been arguing for the *other* man partly, partly, to keep from saying my name aloud.

"Well," said old Serafim, from behind his unnecessary net, in the tone of a man laying down a winning hand he'd rather not have drawn, "there's the tide coming in, and there's the launch coming round the point, and there's Duarte counting his one berth with both hands. So whichever of you is doing the deciding—" he nodded at the three of us, at Rosa, at the shrinking cove, at the whole wet impossible world— "you might consider doing it before the navy does it for you."

Out past the mangroves, low and getting lower, the navy launch sounded its horn. Once. Patient. Like a toll collector at the end of a bridge.
