## 27. Tomás Decides

The coracle smelled of fish and old rain, and Tomás had never loved a smell more in his life.

He crouched at the edge of the staging cove with his sister on one side and the sea on the other, and for the first time since the soldiers came with his name folded into their pockets, he felt something like luck. The mangroves had swallowed him whole last night — he'd run through black water up to his chest, mud sucking at his ankles like the island itself trying to keep him — and here he was, breathing, laughing even, because Astrid had come for him the way she always did: quietly, precisely, with everything already counted.

"You look terrible," she said.

"I've been living in a swamp."

"You look *at home* in it," she said, and he laughed again, too loud, and Elias shushed him from behind the screen of roots where he sat propped against a fallen trunk, gray as driftwood but upright, upright for the first time in days.

Tomás studied the man. He'd seen him before only in glimpses — carried, hidden, bandaged — a shape in the dark of the drying sheds. In daylight he was younger than Tomás expected, older than Astrid deserved to be worrying about, with hands that had clearly done hard work and eyes that kept going to the water the way a man's eyes do when he's crossed one and isn't sure he'll cross another.

"So this is the famous deserter," Tomás said, keeping his voice light. "My mother risks hanging for you and you can't even manage a proper beard."

"Tomás," Astrid said, in the voice she used when correcting a ledger entry.

"What? If we're all about to die, I'm allowed opinions." He sat down on a root, knees cracking, and looked out at the cove. It was a good cove. Narrow mouth, deep water past the sandbar, overhung enough that a boat could sit there half a day without being seen from the channel. Their cousin Duarte held the lease on a fishing boat that could be anywhere by now, except that Duarte was exactly where Tomás knew he'd be, three coves down, mending nets and pretending not to know anything, which was how Duarte helped with everything.

One berth. That was the arithmetic sitting between them, fat and patient as a toad. One berth on the last boat out, bought with the pooled coins of forty families who had decided — without asking him, which stung less than it should have — that Tomás Almeida was the one whose life was worth buying back.

And now Elias was standing — sitting, anyway — in front of him, alive, walking almost, and saying things in a low careful voice about a man on the mainland. A buyer. A commission. Names, dates, the shape of Brandt's whole crooked machine.

"I can point to him," Elias said. "I can say his name in front of people who have the power to write it down. That's worth more than my life, and my life is currently worth nothing, so it's a very efficient arrangement."

Tomás liked him immediately and resented it thoroughly.

* * *

They argued for two hours.

Not shouting — the mangroves didn't allow for shouting; sound traveled over water like gossip — but the low, circling kind of argument that families are best at, where every position is stated four times in different words until everyone forgets what anyone originally said.

Astrid laid it out first, of course, in ledger language, because that was her native tongue. "The warrant is paper," she said to Tomás. "Paper dies when the company's claim dies. The lists prove the claim is forged. Elias's testimony proves the lists. Therefore the warrant is already dead; it just hasn't been told yet."

"That's beautiful," Tomás said. "I'd frame it, except framing requires walls, and I don't currently have any."

"Tomás."

"No, listen — listen." He stood up, which was a mistake, because standing made his legs remember the swamp, and he had to put a hand on a root and pretend it had been a dramatic gesture. "You're doing sums. You're always doing sums. But here's mine, and I learned it at the tollhouse, counting heads, which is the only school I ever attended." He held up fingers. "One: if they catch me, they hang me. Not maybe. Not after a trial with lawyers and tea. They hang me, and they hang me fast, because Brandt needs an example before the rains end or nobody's scared anymore. Two: if they catch Elias, they flog him and ship him back to a war that's nearly over. That's not nothing, but it's not a rope either. Three—" and here he faltered, because three was the part he hadn't wanted to say aloud, "—three: if the boat goes out empty of testimony, then the berth bought me buys me a rope anyway, just slower. Because without those lists surfacing, the company stands forever, and a man with a warrant and no friends on the mainland lasts about a week."

"He's right," Elias said quietly.

"He's wrong," said Astrid.

"He's doing math," Elias said, "and his math is better than yours, which must hurt."

"It does," Astrid admitted, and Tomás saw it land on her — the small flinch, quickly filed away, of a girl who had never once lost an argument about numbers and was losing one now, to her brother, about her brother.

* * *

Here is what Tomás did not say, because he was saving it, hoarding it like rice under roots:

He was afraid.

Not of the rope — well, yes, of the rope, honestly, mostly of the rope, he thought about the rope approximately nine hundred times a day and had developed strong opinions about the knotwork of the garrison, which were not favorable. But underneath that, quieter, worse: he was afraid of being *carried*. Of being the thing his family poured itself into. His mother had sold silence for sixteen years to buy Astrid's schooling. Astrid had memorized a thousand debts so the family could eat. And now forty families had scraped together a berth for him as if he were a pearl, as if the sum of Tomás Almeida was something worth pooling pennies for — and every coin of it made him smaller, made him a debt instead of a man, and he had spent his whole life watching debts eat people he loved.

If he got on that boat, he would owe everyone forever. And if he drowned, or was taken at sea, or simply failed — if the war swallowed one more Almeida — then all that pooled hope would curdle into grief with interest compounded, and his mother would stand in the orchard another thirty years growing silence.

Whereas if Elias got on the boat—

Elias was already a debt. A deserter, a stranger, a man who owed them his bandages and his rice and his mother's midnight nursing. Nobody would mourn him with interest. Nobody would count him. He was weightless in the way that only a man with nothing left can be weightless, and weightless men make the best cargo.

It wasn't noble. Tomás checked it carefully, turned it over, looked for the nobility hiding in it the way you check a coin for shaving. It wasn't there. It was just math, cold and clean, and the fact that the math happened to also break his heart into convenient pieces was irrelevant to the sum.

* * *

"You'll die here," Astrid said. She had gone still, the way she went still over a column that wouldn't add. "Don't you understand? Even if you're right about the warrant — even if the lists void everything — that takes months. The commission has to come, has to rule, has to send someone. Until then you're a hunted man in a swamp eating raw crab, and the rains will seal us for half a year, and—"

"I've survived worse than a swamp," Tomás said. "I survived Mother's cooking during the year she refused to use salt on principle."

"This isn't funny."

"It's a little funny."

"*Tomás.*"

He crouched down in front of her then, so they were eye to eye, the way they hadn't been since he'd grown past her at fourteen and she'd hated him for it. Up close he could see how tired she was, how the fear had been living in her face so long it had stopped paying rent and started furnishing the place.

"Little sister," he said. "Listen to me once, properly, because I'm about to say the only wise thing I've ever said and I don't want to repeat it." He took a breath. "You found the lists. You copied them onto paper thin as breath and sewed them into your collar, and you've been wearing our freedom around your neck for weeks while serving dinner to the man who forged them. You stole his ledger. You cut it into pieces and hid it in forty houses, and every one of those families is braver today because you trusted them with the truth. You did all that. Me? I collected tolls and told jokes and smuggled letters, which is honorable work but hardly history." He smiled at her. "The world doesn't need me on that boat. The world needs what you're carrying, and what he can say. I'm just the spare brother."

"You're not a spare anything."

"Mother would disagree. She named me after a goat."

"She did not."

"There's a goat in the third paddock named Tomás. Ask her. She gets a certain look about it."

Astrid laughed — one short, wet, furious laugh — and wiped her face with the heel of her hand, and for a moment they were just two children again in the orchard, stealing guavas and blaming the birds.

Then she set her jaw, and he watched the clerk come back, the girl who had beaten him at numbers since she was seven, and he knew the argument wasn't over. She opened her mouth to rebuild her case from the foundation, and Tomás did the thing he was actually best at in the world, better than jokes, better than smuggling:

He interrupted gently, and changed the ground.

"Astrid," he said. "Who did Mother send word to at dawn?"

She blinked. "...Duarte. About the boat."

"And who else?"

Something moved behind her eyes. "Rosa went to the compound," she said slowly. "Before first light. She said she had to feed the overseers' wives their breakfast lies before they woke hungry."

"Yes."

"If Brandt hears the boat leaves tonight instead of dawn—"

"Then he searches it tonight, or stops it entirely, or puts his own man aboard, or sinks it and calls it weather." Tomás shrugged. "Which means we don't get to spend the afternoon arguing. We get to spend it deciding, and then moving, and I've decided. So have you. You just haven't admitted it, because you're the honest one in the family."

* * *

Elias spoke from his place against the trunk, and his voice had changed — steadier, the voice of a man stepping off a ledge he'd been standing on for months.

"I'll go," he said. "But I want it said plainly, in front of both of you, so it can't be unsaid later. Your brother is giving up his life for this. Not dying — worse, staying. Living hunted in the mud for however long it takes. I've been that man. I ran from being that man. So whatever happens on the mainland — whatever I have to say, whoever I have to say it to, however long it takes — I'm saying it in his name too. Write that down somewhere."

"Don't be dramatic," Tomás said, because his throat had closed and drama was the only tool left in the box.

"It's not dramatic," Elias said. "It's bookkeeping. Your sister taught me. Everything owed gets written down."

Tomás laughed, and it came out cracked down the middle, and he let it.

* * *

The loading took less time than the arguing. There wasn't much to load: a water skin, dried fish wrapped in banana leaf, Elias's boots — repaired, Tomás noticed, with stitching that could only be their mother's, neat as prayer. Rosa had known. Rosa had probably known since before any of them, the way she always did, standing in her orchard listening to the trees.

Astrid pressed something into Elias's hand at the waterline: a fold of rice paper, sewn into oilcloth, small as a communion wafer. Tomás recognized the handwriting even upside down. Columns. Dates. Names written twice.

"The collar pages?" he asked.

"The spare set," Astrid said. "I keep the master against my ribs. If the boat is searched and they take these, I still have everything. If they take me, Elias still has everything." She looked at Tomás. "Nobody carries the whole truth alone. That was the whole point."

"When did you become the general?"

"Somewhere between the fire and the ball. It happened while you weren't paying attention, which is most of the time."

Elias waded out to the coracle, and Tomás watched him go — this stranger, this soldier, this evidence walking on two legs — and felt the strange double weight of it, grief and relief sharing one harness.

At the gunwale Elias paused. "The man I'm going to find," he said. "Brandt's buyer. He knows my face. There's a chance he's already been told to look for it."

"Then don't use your face," Tomás said. "Use your name. Names travel faster than faces, and yours is about to become worth a great deal of money to the right people. Lead with the money. It works on everyone."

For the first time, Elias smiled — a real one, lopsided, human. "Your family," he said, "is the strangest thing I met in this entire war."

"We get that a lot," said Tomás. "Go. Row. Be boring and survive."

* * *

The coracle slipped through the mangrove tunnel and out toward where, three coves down, Duarte's boat waited with its nets and its innocence. The rain thickened, then thinned, then settled in for the long work of the season, and the water closed over the wake like a ledger line ruled clean.

Astrid stood beside him, soaked, silent, watching.

"Well," Tomás said at last. "I believe this is the part where you tell me your plan for hiding me from an entire garrison until spring."

His sister turned to look at him, and there was something in her face he had never seen there before — not fear, not grief, but the bright hard gleam of a woman holding a key she hadn't shown anyone yet.

"As it happens," Astrid said, "Brandt has offered me a promotion."
