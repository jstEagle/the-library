## 28. Departure

The rain came down the way it always did in the last days before the strait closed — not falling so much as arriving, all at once, from every direction, as though the sky had finally remembered a debt of its own and meant to pay it in full.

I stood at the edge of the staging cove with my hand pressed flat against my ribs, where the master copy rode sewn into the lining of my jacket, and I thought: *this is what it feels like to be a ledger*. Full of other people's names. Heavy past all sense. Carried whether you want to carry it or not.

The boat was called the *Meridian Rose*, which was a lie twice over — she was neither meridian nor rose but a squat coastal trader with a cracked funnel and a hold that smelled of dried fish and diesel — and she sat low in the water already, taking on her last crates while the rain hammered her deck into a drum.

Elias stood at the foot of the gangplank, dressed in Tomás's spare shirt, which hung on him like a sail on a mast too thin for it. His wound had closed enough to walk on; his face had not yet caught up with the rest of him. He kept touching the collar of the shirt — our collar, my collar once, the one Rosa had unpicked at midnight and resewn with rice-paper pages nested against the cloth like moth eggs — and each time he touched it, he looked at me as if asking permission all over again.

"You'll ruin it, fidgeting," I told him. "Sweat is worse than salt water for paper."

"Is that a clerk's opinion or a sister's?"

"Clerk's. The sister's opinion involves hitting you."

That earned half a smile, which was more than he'd managed since dawn. But then his face went serious in the way that made him look suddenly older than his uniform had ever made him look young, and he said, "Astrid. Say it again. What you're doing. So I can carry it properly."

"I'm staying," I said. "Tomás goes to ground in the interior until the company's claim dies or he does, whichever comes first, and I stay and keep the families holding the pages organized, and when the commission comes — if it comes — I testify myself. The copy against your skin is testimony. The master against mine is proof. One of us has to be the witness and one of us has to be the archive, and Elias—" I stopped, because my voice had begun doing something embarrassing. "You're the witness. You saw Brandt's buyer with your own eyes. You're the only man on this island who can point at a face on the mainland and name it."

"And you're the only person alive who can reproduce those ledgers from memory if the sea takes me."

"The sea won't take you. It owes me a favor. My father keeps accounts over there now, and I intend to call them in."

He looked at me for a long moment through the rain, and whatever he saw — the drowned man's daughter, the girl who measured the world in carryable things — made him nod slowly, the way soldiers nod, which is to say: accepting an order he didn't like from someone with the rank to give it.

* * *

Tomás was late, which frightened me more than anything else that morning, because Tomás was never late. Tomás was the boy who'd shown up early to his own birthing, according to Mother, impatient to see what everyone was making such a fuss about.

When he finally came down the mangrove path he was soaked through, mud to the knees, breathing hard, and grinning — grinning, in the rain, with a soldier's warrant out for him and the interior waiting behind him full of leeches and hunger and whatever passed for safety among men who lived outside the ledgers.

"Well," he said, "you'll be pleased to know the coracle works beautifully. Terribly. Works terribly. It took on water at the second bend and I spent a quarter hour bailing with my hat, which is now ruined, which is fine, because hats are for people with futures."

"You went back?" I said. "Across? Why would you—"

"To say goodbye properly." He shrugged, and water ran off his shoulders like he'd been designed for it. "The warrant means they're watching the paths, not the water. Nobody watches the water this time of year except fools and fishermen, and I'm both."

"You could have been taken."

"I could have been taken any day for two years, little sister. That's the whole trouble with being wanted." He came close and put his hands on either side of my face, and his palms were cold and river-smelling, and he looked at me the way he used to look at me across the tollhouse counter when a family slid him a letter and a coin and he wanted them to know the coin wasn't why he did it. "Listen. If the boat sinks—"

"It won't."

"If the boat sinks," he repeated, with the patience of a man talking to a ledger, "and Elias reaches the mainland without the collar-pages, do you have the master? All of it? Word-perfect?"

"Every column. Every name. I've read it four hundred times, Tomás. I could recite it in my sleep, and some nights I think I have."

"Good." He exhaled. "Then even drowned, we win. Do you understand? Even drowned, we win. That's the position we've fought ourselves into. I'd like it noted in the record that I said so."

"Noted," I said, and my voice broke on it, and he pretended very courteously not to notice, which is the highest form of love my brother knows.

* * *

Rosa had refused to come to the cove. She'd said the orchard couldn't be left, that the roots needed tending, that someone sensible had to be standing in plain sight when Brandt's morning rounds came through — and all of that was true, and none of it was the reason, and we all three knew it, and we let her keep her dignity the way you let a diver keep his breath: by not mentioning how thin it was getting.

But when we reached the head of the mangrove path, there was a basket sitting on the stump where the path forked, covered with banana leaf against the rain. Inside: rice cakes wrapped in oilcloth, a flask of the terrible ginger tea she brewed when someone was ill, and a folded note in her small, upright hand, written the way she wrote everything — as though the paper might be subpoenaed later.

*For the boat. Eat before the water gets rough. Elias — the collar will itch where the stitching crosses your neck. Don't scratch it. Astrid — come home by the orchard path, not the beach. Tomás — there is a tin under the third mango tree on the ridge line. Blanket, matches, dried fish. Take it BEFORE dark, not after.*

She hadn't signed it. She never signed things anymore. Signing was for people whose names were still their own.

Tomás read it twice and laughed, wet and cracked. "Blanket, matches, dried fish. She's packed me like I'm going on holiday."

"She's packed you like a mother," Elias said quietly. "It's the same thing, only longer."

We divided the rice cakes standing up in the rain, because there was no time for anything gentler, and I remember thinking — absurdly, in the middle of everything — that they were good. Too good. Sweetened, which cost money, which meant she'd traded something for the sugar, which meant somewhere in the orchard there was a bargain I'd never learn about, tucked under roots the way everything she loved was tucked under roots.

* * *

The *Meridian Rose*'s mate came down the gangplank with rain streaming off his cap brim and shouted over the drumming deck: "Last call! Tide won't wait and neither will the weather, and the weather's the worse-tempered of the two!"

There was no crowd to see him off. That had been the whole design of it — no crowd, no farewells on the dock, no faces for Brandt's informants to count. Just three people and a basket on a stump and a boat leaving through rain so thick the far shore was a rumor.

Elias shook Tomás's hand first, and they held it a beat too long, two men who'd shared a drying shed and a fever and the particular intimacy of hiding together from men with lists. Then Elias turned to me.

And I don't know what I expected. Something formal, maybe. He was a formal man underneath the desertion — you could see the parade-ground ghost in how he stood, even soaked and starving and wearing my brother's shirt.

What he did instead was take my hand and press it, briefly, flat against his own chest — right over the collar, right over the names.

"They ride with me," he said. "Every one of them. I'll feel them the whole way across."

"You'd better," I said. "If you lose so much as a page I will swim after you, and I'm a better swimmer than you'd credit, and my mother taught me to hold grudges underwater."

"Astrid Almeida," he said, solemn as an oath, "I believe every word."

Then he went up the gangplank and didn't look back, which I understood was the discipline of it — you don't look back, because looking back is how the sea learns what matters to you, and the sea collects debts.

* * *

Tomás and I watched the *Rose* work her way out of the cove, listing slightly, her patched funnel trailing smoke that the rain bent sideways. We watched until the shape of her dissolved — not disappeared, exactly; the strait doesn't allow disappearing, it only allows *unproving*, the slow dissolution of anything into weather.

"He'll make it," Tomás said.

"Yes."

"You don't know that."

"No. But saying the other thing costs the same number of words and buys nothing."

He laughed — really laughed, the tollhouse laugh, the laugh that had smuggled forty families' letters through a checkpoint disguised as jokes — and then he turned to me and the laughter went out of his face like a tide.

"This is the part where we split," he said. "I go up the ridge, you go the orchard path. And I need you to hear one thing before we do, because I've been composing it since the coracle and I'd like to deliver it before the rain washes it away."

"Tomás—"

"Let me be the eloquent one for once in my life. You get the ledgers, I get the warrants." He held up a finger before I could object. "No. Hear it. When Father drowned, I decided my job was to keep you safe, and I have done it badly and loudly for nine years, mostly by getting into trouble that you then had to fix with arithmetic. And this morning I finally understand that you were never the one being kept safe. You were the one keeping *us* safe. Me, and Mother, and Elias, and every family holding a piece of that ledger under their floorboards. You've been the strongroom this whole time, little sister. The rest of us are just the noise around the vault."

I pressed my hand harder against my ribs. Under my palm, under the lining, under the oilcloth, the master copy sat against my skin like a second heartbeat — forty-one forged conscription entries, eleven duplicated names, one dead man's discovery, one living daughter's inheritance.

"Go," I said, when I could. "Before I say something that ruins your exit."

"Too late. You already did. 'Yes.' Devastating stuff." He kissed the top of my head, quick and hard, the way he had when we were children and he was taller than me and thought I didn't notice him checking whether I'd grown. "Orchard path. Not the beach. And Astrid—" He was already backing away up the slope, rain running down his grin. "When the bridge opens, be on it. Promise me the boring thing. Promise me you'll just *walk across*, like a woman with nowhere urgent to be."

"I promise the boring thing."

He raised one hand — not goodbye, exactly; more like a salute rendered by a boy who'd learned salutes from ferry captains — and then the mangroves took him, green and gray and rain, and my brother became interior.

* * *

I walked back the long way, the orchard path, because Mother's notes were law and because I wanted the extra minutes the way a drowning man wants air — not usefully, just desperately.

The trees stood black and dripping, the fruit heavy on the boughs, and under the third mango on the ridge line I could see the fresh-turned earth where Tomás would dig for his tin tonight, blanket and matches and dried fish, packed like a holiday, packed like a mother.

Halfway down the row I stopped, because my hand had found something in my pocket that shouldn't have been there. A rice cake, wrapped in oilcloth, with writing on the inside of the wrapping — visible only because the rain had soaked the leaf-thin paper translucent against the cake.

Her handwriting again. Small. Upright. Written as though the paper might be read by anyone, which, knowing her, it might.

*Astrid. Your father found the lists and it killed him. You found them and it made you. That is the whole difference between us and the company, and it is the only one we need. Come home by the orchard path. — R.*

I stood in the rain among the trees and I ate the rice cake, all of it, sweetened, expensive, loved, and I pressed the master copy tighter against my ribs, and I started walking toward the compound where Brandt was counting heads and the season was closing and the armistice rumor was a candle burning at both ends.

Somewhere out in the rain-dark strait, a man in my brother's shirt was carrying forty-one names across the water.

And here, on the island, the archive stayed open for business.

I had work to do.
