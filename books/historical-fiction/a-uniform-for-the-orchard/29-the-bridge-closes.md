## 29. The Bridge Closes

The bridge closed the way a mouth closes around a secret — slowly at first, and then all at once.

I stood in the crowd that gathered on the causeway side to watch it happen, because everyone always did. It was a ritual older than the company, older than the Verrone name stitched into every ledger I had ever copied: on the last dry morning of the season, the whole island came down to see the water take back its road.

The tide came in from both sides of the strait, patient as a clerk with a red pen. By noon the planks at the middle were dark to their nails. By two o'clock the gulls owned it entirely, strutting along the rails like inspectors who had found nothing worth reporting. And by three, when the first real rain of the season arrived — fat, warm drops that hissed against the boards like something cooking — there was no bridge at all. There was a strait. There was an island. There was us.

Tomás would have made a joke about it. Tomás could make a joke about anything; it was practically a trade skill, like net-mending or lying to overseers. But Tomás was somewhere in the interior with wet boots and a warrant with his name on it, and so the joke went unmade, and I felt its absence the way you feel a missing tooth — your tongue keeps going back to the hole.

*The island is sealed,* I thought. *No one comes in. No one goes out.*

Except, of course, that wasn't quite true, and the not-quite-true part was folded against my ribs at this very moment, wrapped in oilcloth, sewn into the lining of my mother's old market jacket: the master copy. Forty pages of Brandt's own arithmetic, proving he had sold every diver's name twice — once to the navy, once to the labor brokers — and drowned my father for finding out.

The rain picked up. The crowd didn't move. That was the thing about this island: we watched water the way other people watched theater. We had nothing else playing.

Rosa found me near the rail, her shawl darkening with rain faster than she could pull it over her head. She pressed a small bundle into my hands under cover of hugging me — bread, still warm, and inside the cloth a twist of paper holding salt fish. My mother expressed love exclusively through concealment: rice under tree roots, fish inside bread, truth sewn into collars. If she ever once simply said *I love you* out loud, I suspect we'd both have needed to sit down.

"Eat," she said. "You've gone thin again."

"I'm always thin. It's my natural state."

"Your natural state is stubborn. Thin is what happens when you forget meals." She looked out at where the bridge had been. Her face did the thing it always did when she looked at open water — went smooth and unreadable, a door closing quietly in a house where doors were meant to slam. "Six months," she said.

"Five and a half."

"Don't count in front of me. Counting is how you get caught." She said it lightly, but we both knew she wasn't talking about meals or months. She was talking about the way I counted everything now — days since Elias left, days until the armistice rumor might become armistice fact, days since Tomás vanished into the mangroves with Rosa pressing a bundle of food into *his* hands and telling him the same thing she'd told me: *don't count where they can see you counting.*

We walked home through the rain together, and the whole compound seemed to be doing the same arithmetic without saying it aloud. The rains weren't weather here. They were walls. For six months the strait would be impassable to anything bigger than a coracle, and even coracles only by prayer and madness. Whatever was going to happen on this island — whatever Brandt suspected, whatever the commission on the mainland did or failed to do with Elias's testimony — would happen behind closed water, with no witnesses in or out except us, and God, and the gulls.

The gulls, at least, took no sides.

* * *

Brandt announced my promotion four days later, at the compound assembly, and he did it in the voice of a man giving away something precious.

He'd had the workers called in from the beds early — which never happened, which meant the whole island knew something was coming before he opened his mouth. Two hundred divers and dockers and shell-graders standing in rows under the drying shed's awning while rain came down off the eaves in ropes, and Brandt standing on the loading platform with his hands clasped behind him like a bishop about to bless a fleet.

"The season closes," he began, "and I know your thoughts turn to your families, your fires, your festivals." A pause, warm as lamp oil. He had a gift for warmth, Brandt. It was the most frightening thing about him. "But before we turn inward, the company wishes to recognize service. Exceptional service."

His eyes found me in the third row. They always found me. I had learned to receive that gaze the way a ledger receives an entry: record it, file it, give away nothing in your face.

"Astrid Almeida," he said, and two hundred heads turned, and I felt the attention land on my shoulders like wet wool. "For diligence beyond her years. For accuracy beyond question. For discretion—" and here he smiled, just slightly, just enough for the front row to see and no further, "—which the company values above rubies. Effective immediately, she is promoted from copy clerk to keeper of the winter ledgers. All accounts pass through her hands until the bridge reopens."

Applause. Of course applause — you applauded Brandt the way you saluted a wave, because the alternative was drowning. I stepped forward when summoned. He shook my hand. His grip was dry and precise, like being held briefly by a well-oiled hinge.

"Youngest keeper in the company's history on this island," he told the crowd. Then, quieter, only to me, leaning in as if conferring a knighthood: "My office will be closed for the season. You'll work from the annex. Alone, mostly. I trust you'll find the responsibility... clarifying."

*I trust you'll find the trap comfortable,* was what I heard.

"Thank you, Mr. Brandt," I said, and smiled the smile I'd practiced in the back of the copper polishing pan — grateful, modest, slightly overwhelmed. A girl who had been given an honor. A girl who had no idea that honors from men like Brandt were leashes cut to length, and that the collar end was already buckled.

He released my hand. "Your father would be proud," he added, and that was the moment I understood this was not merely a leash but a message. He knew whose name sat on the page I carried. He was telling me, in front of two hundred witnesses, that he remembered exactly who I belonged to — or believed I still belonged to him.

I bowed my head. "I hope so, sir."

Under my jacket, against my ribs, forty pages of his own handwriting kept very still.

* * *

The annex was a narrow room behind the grading sheds with one window, one desk, and a stove that smoked unless you spoke to it kindly. I moved in my things: ink, pens, blotting sand, the little brass scale I used for weighing pearls and, occasionally, patience. Rosa sent a jar of her pickled limes, which was her way of saying *you live in a room now, so I will provision the room.* The families who held pieces of the dismantled duplicate ledger sent smaller signals — a flower left on the step, a fish hung on the door hook, Senhora Reyes pausing to complain cheerfully about her knees directly beneath my window, which was our code for *nothing new, all safe.*

I worked. That was the strange mercy of it. Winter ledgers are not complicated; they are voluminous. Inventory of cordage. Wages owed against debts accrued. The great slow subtraction by which the company owned everyone a little more each season. I entered figures in my neat clerk's hand and let the numbers put me to sleep like a lullaby, and if anyone had looked through the window they would have seen exactly what Brandt wanted them to see: his promoted girl, diligent beyond her years, buried in honest columns.

What they would not have seen was what happened each night, after the stove died and the rain filled the silence. I unlocked the floorboard where I kept the master copy, and I read it. Not all of it — a page a night, no more, the discipline of a woman feeding a fire one stick at a time so the smoke won't show. I read the names. I read the sums. I read the second column, the one where each diver appeared a second time with a different buyer and a different price, and I read the date beside my father's name, and the initials of whoever had signed off on the transfer of a man to a labor broker three weeks after the sea had already taken him.

They had sold a dead man north. Somewhere on the mainland there was presumably a plantation holding a debt against a ghost, and the ghost's daughter was keeping the books that proved it.

I put the page back. I locked the board. I lay in the dark and listened to the rain hammer the roof like a creditor at a door, and I thought: *five and a half months.* Five and a half months of sealed water, of Brandt's smile, of pickled limes and coded complaints about knees. Five and a half months of being the most trusted thief on the island.

It should have felt like safety. The rains meant no soldiers arriving, no boats of investigators, no commission swooping in to catch us mid-crime. The rains meant time — time to redistribute, rehearse, prepare.

Instead it felt like the inside of a bottle. And I was beginning to understand that Brandt had known precisely what he was doing. A leash doesn't only hold you. It tells you how far your world extends. Mine now extended from this desk to that stove to that window, with a promotion pinned to it like a medal on a prisoner.

* * *

On the ninth day of the rains, the trouble arrived wearing an umbrella and a good coat, which on this island could mean only one thing: a visitor from the mainland who had come anyway, rains or no rains, aboard the company's armored launch, which was the only vessel fool enough to cross a monsoon strait.

I saw the launch from my window and my stomach dropped through the floorboards, past the master copy, and into the earth beneath the annex.

Nobody crossed in the rains. Nobody except someone with business too urgent to wait — and urgent mainland business, in November, meant one of two words: *armistice* or *investigation.*

I made myself finish the column I was working on. My hand did not shake, and I noted that with the detached pride of a clerk reviewing her own performance: steady, accurate, discreet. Discretion above rubies.

Then I walked, unhurried, through the rain to the compound house, because a keeper of the winter ledgers had legitimate reason to report for instructions, and because if I ran, I would learn nothing and confirm everything.

The visitor was in Brandt's study with the shutters half-closed against the weather. He was naval — I knew the cut of that coat now, knew it the way you know a dog that has bitten your family — and young, and tired, and dripping on the good rug without apology. Brandt stood pouring him a drink with the hospitality of a man hosting his own execution.

"—can't stay, obviously," the naval man was saying as I knocked and was waved in. "Launch turns back within the hour. But headquarters wants confirmation before the commission sits. There are... irregularities alleged. Conscription schedules. Duplicate sales." He said it flatly, the way you'd read a shopping list, but I watched Brandt's shoulders the whole time, and I saw them do something I had never seen them do.

They rose. Half an inch. The tiniest possible shrug of a man deciding, in real time, how much of the cliff to stand on.

"Irregularities," Brandt repeated pleasantly. "In a war year, Captain, everything is irregular. I run a pearl station, not a parish."

"Mm." The captain drank. "There's a witness expected. A deserter, actually — turned up on the mainland, telling a story about this island. Names, dates, sums." He set down the glass. "If he reaches the commission, there'll be an inquiry. If he doesn't—" the shrug was the captain's this time, eloquent and ugly, "—well. The rains close the strait, don't they? Hard to testify across open water."

Something in the room changed temperature. I kept my eyes on the floor, on the pattern of the rug, on the captain's wet boots printing the company's carpet with the navy's mark, and I did my arithmetic fast and cold:

Elias had reached the mainland. Elias was alive. Elias was telling the story — but he had not yet reached the commission, and the navy knew he existed, and the launch turning back within the hour meant that within the hour, every naval eye between here and the capital would be looking for one ragged deserter in a country full of ragged deserters.

And Brandt — Brandt now knew that his duplicate ledger was not merely lost. It was *alleged.* In writing. At headquarters. Which meant the clock over his head had started ticking too, and a man with a ticking clock and a private launch does desperate bookkeeping.

"More wine?" Brandt asked, and his voice was warm as ever, and his eyes were doing sums of their own.

"No thank you. I'll want the tide." The captain collected his hat, paused at the door, and glanced at me — a glance with a list attached, checking me off against some description. *Clerk. Girl. Almeida.* "You're the keeper?"

"For the winter, sir."

"Keep well, then." He smiled without meaning anything by it. "Everyone says the island's safe as a vault once the bridge shuts. Nothing in, nothing out." He settled the hat on his head. "Funny thing about vaults, though."

"Captain?" said Brandt, in a voice like poured honey.

"They only keep people *out,*" the captain said. "Everything inside is already there." And he stepped out into the rain, toward his launch, toward the mainland, carrying the news that would send hunters after Elias like dogs after a wounded stag.

I stood in the study with my hands folded and my heart going like a coracle in a squall, and Brandt looked at me for a long, long moment.

"Well, Keeper," he said finally. "You heard all that, of course."

"Yes, Mr. Brandt."

"Curious timing, visitors in the rains." He smiled. "Almost makes a man feel crowded on his own island." He turned back to the window, to the grey hammering water where the launch was pulling away. "You may go. Work hard. The winter is long, and I do so hate unfinished columns."

I walked back through the rain to the annex, and inside, alone, I allowed myself thirty seconds of pure, undiluted terror — shaking hands, breath gone to rags, the whole works — because terror unspent ferments into mistakes.

Then I wiped my face, unlocked the floorboard, and pulled out the master copy.

Because here is what the captain's visit had told me, underneath the fear: the vault was not sealed against danger. The vault was sealed against *help.* No commission could come to us. No inquiry, no investigator, no friendly ship. Whatever happened next, we would handle it ourselves, in the rain, in the dark, for five and a half months, against a man who had just learned that his crimes had a witness on the loose and a deadline of his own.

And Brandt had a launch.

That night I wrote a single line on a scrap of rice paper, small as a grain, and sent it out with the morning's fish delivery to Rosa, who would pass it down the chain of complaining knees and hanging flowers until every family holding a piece of the truth had read it:

*He knows the witness lives. He owns the only boat. We move everything — now.*
