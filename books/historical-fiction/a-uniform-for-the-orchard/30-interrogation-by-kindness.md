## 30. Interrogation by Kindness

Herr Brandt had a philosophy of interrogation, and he was rather proud of it. Anyone could extract truth with a hot iron or a hanging rope — that was work for men with no imagination, men who mistook cruelty for competence. No. The truth, properly handled, came out the way pearl came out of oyster: not by smashing the shell but by making the creature open its own mouth.

Kindness. That was his instrument. Kindness, applied slowly, at the correct temperature, until the thing being interrogated could no longer remember a time when it had not been warm.

He had prepared for Astrid Almeida's promotion dinner the way other men prepared for naval inspections. He had the table set in the small room behind his office — not the grand hall; grandeur was for people who needed to be impressed, and this girl did not impress easily, which was precisely the problem. He had the cook prepare the snapper she was known to favor from the compound kitchens — he made it his business to know what everyone ate, and what they looked at when they thought no one was watching — and he had laid out, on the sideboard where she could not fail to see it, a single document in a leather folder.

Her indenture paper.

He had even practiced his smile in the shaving mirror that morning, turning his head left and right like a man checking a knot. He decided on the one that reached his eyes but only halfway, the smile of a man who wanted very much to be liked and had simply never gotten around to deserving it. It had worked on overseers' wives. It had worked on two navy paymasters and a bishop. He saw no reason it should not work on a sixteen-year-old clerk with ink stains on her fingers and a face like a closed ledger.

* * *

She arrived at seven o'clock exactly, because of course she did. That was the thing about Astrid Almeida that unsettled him more than any other thing: her punctuality was not obedience. Obedience trembled somewhere underneath it. Hers did not. She knocked, entered when invited, sat when offered a chair, and arranged herself with the economy of a woman who had counted how much of the evening each posture would cost her.

"Fräulein Almeida." He poured the wine himself, which was a performance in itself — Herr Brandt, who had servants for everything, lifting the bottle with his own hands. "You'll forgive the informality. I find the office produces better arithmetic after hours."

"It's very kind of you, sir."

There it was. *Sir.* Warm as a fish. He watched her take the wine, watch her hold it without drinking, and noted both facts in the great interior ledger he kept, the one with no columns that added up either.

"You've heard, I hope, about your advancement." He let the pleasure spread across his face like oil on water. "Head copy clerk. Full salary. A room of your own above the counting house, with a lock." He paused to let the word *lock* do its quiet work. "And, when the season permits, first consideration for passage on any company vessel. There will be boats again, you know. There are always boats eventually."

"That's very generous, sir."

"Generous." He laughed, delighted, and tapped the table with one finger. "No, no. Not generous. *Earned.* That is the difference between me and every other man who has ever held power over another human being, Fräulein Almeida. I believe in earning. Your mother believes in silence. The navy believes in forms filled out in triplicate. I believe a person should be paid precisely what they are worth." He leaned back. "The trouble is determining worth. Which brings me to an unfortunate matter."

He let the pause stretch. He was a connoisseur of pauses. This one he aged like the wine.

"There has been a theft," he said.

* * *

He watched her hands, because hands told him everything mouths would not. Hers stayed folded around the wine glass, steady as mooring posts. Only the thumbs moved — once, a small circular motion against the stem, like something smoothing a page.

"A theft," she repeated.

"The duplicate conscription ledger. Removed from my quarters on the night of the dinner. You were present that night. So were forty other servants, six overseers, and a string quartet that played badly enough to constitute its own crime." He smiled his half-smile. "So you see, I am not accusing anyone. Accusation is so crude. I am merely... opening negotiations."

"Negotiations," said Astrid Almeida, in the tone of a girl confirming the price of rice.

He rose, crossed to the sideboard, and brought back the leather folder. He set it down in front of her but did not open it. Anticipation, he had found, did half the work of any question ever asked.

"Do you know what this is?"

"My indenture," she said. No hesitation. Good. Or bad. With this one it was impossible to tell whether composure meant innocence or rehearsal, and he hated her a little for the ambiguity, the way a chess player hates an opponent who never frowns at a lost piece.

"Your indenture," he agreed. "Signed by your father's mark before he drowned, transferred through probate, countersigned by myself. Six years remaining, by my arithmetic. Six years of ledgers and salt air and a room you share with three other girls." He opened the folder now, slowly, and turned the page toward her. "Or nothing at all. Because here is my offer, and I make it only once, so attend to me."

He waited until her eyes lifted from the paper to his face. Her eyes were extraordinary — he'd thought so for two years and resented thinking it — dark and level and utterly unrevealing, twin wells with no bottom visible. Most people's eyes flicked away when they lied. Hers never did anything so obliging.

"Return what was taken," he said softly, "and tomorrow morning I burn this. In front of you. In front of your mother, if you like — mothers appreciate ceremony. And you walk out of this compound owing the Verrone Trading Company exactly nothing. Free papers, Fräulein Almeida. Do you know how many people on this island have free papers?" He answered himself, savoring it. "Four. The priest, the doctor, the harbormaster, and me. I could make it five."

* * *

She was silent long enough that he permitted himself the first sip of wine. Inside, he was conducting calculations at speed, the way he did during auctions. She hadn't said yes. She also hadn't said no. She hadn't asked *what ledger?*, which interested him enormously — a truly innocent clerk would have asked, if only to buy time. She knew precisely which ledger. Confirmation, then. His suspicion of the Almeidas was no longer suspicion; it was bookkeeping.

But knowing and proving were different columns entirely, and the second column remained stubbornly empty. He had searched the drying sheds twice. He had searched the Almeida house while Rosa stood in the doorway radiating the serene menace of a woman holding a ladle. He had searched the chapel, the cistern, the coffin workshop — and found nothing but dust, devotion, and one coffin of extremely poor workmanship. The pages had gone somewhere, and pages did not walk. People carried them. Therefore people. Therefore her, or her brother, or the deserter he had been assured by three separate informants had died of his wound — an assurance he distrusted on principle, since all his informants feared Rosa Almeida more than they feared him, which was a management failure he intended to address after the rains.

"Sir," Astrid said at last, and her voice had changed — softened, warmed, acquired just the faintest tremor, and he thought, *ah, there it is, the shell opening* — "I don't know what to say. If I had seen such a thing, a whole ledger, walking out of your quarters—" she shook her head, rueful, "—you'd think a person couldn't help noticing. But there were so many people that night. The quartet kept knocking over music stands. I carried trays until my arms ached. I don't remember faces, only elbows."

"Elbows," he repeated.

"Everyone has them, sir. They all look alike."

He laughed — genuinely, briefly, before catching himself — because it was a funny answer, and because laughing put people at ease, and because he suspected she had made him laugh on purpose and he wanted to know if she'd register the hit. Nothing crossed her face. She only refilled his glass with a small deft motion, hostessing him in his own house, and he felt the peculiar vertigo of a man who realizes mid-handshake that the other fellow is steering.

"Then we must hope," he said, recovering, "that the elbows talk among themselves. And meanwhile — the offer stands. Open-ended. Indefinitely." He tapped the indenture paper once more, then slid it back into the folder, unhurried. "You understand why I keep it on the table rather than in the drawer. I want you to see it every time we speak. I want it to be the third person in every conversation you and I have from now until the rains end. Every kindness I show you, you'll wonder what it costs. Every punishment falls on someone else, you'll wonder whose fault bought it. That is not cruelty, Fräulein Almeida. That is simply accurate accounting. Debts should be visible."

"Yes, sir," she said. And then — and this was the move he turned over in bed that night, examining it from every angle like a suspect pearl — she said, quietly, with what sounded like real feeling:

"I'm grateful you told me honestly. Most men in your position would have simply started punishing people and let everyone guess why. My father always said a fair enemy is worth more than a false friend."

He blinked. Somewhere in his chest, an old and undernourished organ stirred, the one that remembered being a junior clerk in Bremen with flour on his cuffs, wanting exactly this: to be seen as fair by someone whose seeing mattered.

*Damn her,* he thought admiringly. *She's fishing in my chest.*

* * *

He walked her out personally, past the lanterns, into the wet dark, and watched her go down the compound path with her straight back and her measured steps, and he stood there longer than a man in his position should, telling himself he was reading the direction of the wind.

Back inside, he poured the rest of the bottle and sat with the folder open in front of him, and did what he always did when a position grew complicated: he wrote it down. Not in any official book. In the little green notebook he kept locked in his desk drawer, where he recorded the island the way it actually was, beneath the way it was reported.

*A. Almeida. Knows too much, shows nothing. Offer made: freedom for return of goods. Response: gratitude, plausible, possibly genuine, possibly the best counterfeit I have ever held. Note: she thanked me for honesty. Nobody thanks me for honesty. Either she is innocent or she understands me better than anyone on this island, and I find I prefer not to determine which.*

He underlined the last sentence twice, then crossed it out, then underlined it again, because accuracy mattered more than comfort.

The deeper problem, he admitted to the notebook and the notebook alone, was time. The rains had sealed the strait; no boat in, no boat out, no commission, no armistice — only rumor, arriving in fragments with the supply launches brave enough to cross early swells. And the rumors had a flavor he did not like. *Signing soon. Commission forming. Witnesses wanted.* Words like *testimony* had begun appearing in other men's letters to him, and letters containing that word had a way of being followed by men carrying it.

If the war ended well for the company, none of it mattered — the lists would be old news, the divers would dive, the debts would hold. But if the war ended badly, if commissioners actually came with actual subpoena power, then somewhere on this island sat the pages that could unmake him, and somewhere on the mainland sat a deserter who had seen the buyer's face with his own eyes.

Two loose ends. One island. One sea.

He finished the wine and considered, not for the first time, the elegant simplicity of accidents. Drowning was common in monsoon season. So was fever. So was a coracle lost in the mangroves, so was a boy with a warrant running into soldiers who had orders not to bother taking him alive. The rains provided cover for all manner of housekeeping.

But — and here he caught himself, pen hovering over the green notebook — an accident required certainty, and certainty he did not have. Kill the girl and the pages might surface anyway, mailed, hidden, memorized. Leave her and she might yet be bought; the offer was good, the offer was generous, the offer was the kindest thing anyone had dangled in front of her in six years. Kindness, given time, opened everything.

He wrote one final line, capped the pen, and blew out the lamp.

*Patience. The oyster opens itself. Watch what she does with her hands.*

* * *

Three nights later, at the weekly tally, Astrid Almeida presented the compound accounts balanced to the penny, thanked him for the promotion in a voice pitched perfectly between warmth and deference, and asked — artlessly, helpfully, with the bright practical concern of a born bookkeeper — whether he would like her to begin reorganizing the storage ledgers ahead of the commission everyone was whispering about.

"*Our* records should be in perfect order," she said, "before any inspectors arrive. Don't you think, sir? Whatever anyone says about us, the numbers themselves should be beyond reproach."

He agreed warmly. He praised her initiative. He shook her hand.

And only after she had gone, standing alone in the counting house with the rain hammering the roof like applause, did the full shape of it arrive in him, cold and complete as a dropped anchor.

She wasn't hiding from the commission. She was *preparing the ground for it*. Every page she reorganized, every column she corrected, every discrepancy she flagged with a modest little note in the margin — she was building the prosecution's exhibit list with his own clerks, his own pens, his own authority, and leaving his signature on every page of it. She had taken his offer of freedom and turned it into a lever. She had accepted his kindness the way a diver accepts a knife: to cut with.

Herr Brandt stood a long moment in the lamplight, and then, despite everything — despite the danger, despite the humiliation, despite the green notebook waiting in its drawer — he laughed out loud, once, into the empty room.

Because it was magnificent. And because he had just understood, with the clarity that comes only too late, which player at the table had been counting which man's moves.

He snuffed the lamp. Tomorrow he would write to the mainland. Tonight he allowed himself, just once, the luxury of fear — and the beginnings of a plan to make the commission arrive on an island with no witnesses left to greet it.
