## 32. Rosa's Garden Party

The morning after Brandt's soldiers turned over every rice jar in the compound, I sent my daughter to buy limes.

"Mother," Astrid said, standing in the doorway with rain still dripping off her braid, "there are men in our lanes going through our neighbors' cooking pots, and you want *limes*."

"I want limes, sugar, coconut cream, and four pounds of ice if the fish-packing shed will spare it," I said. "And I want you to smile when you buy them. A woman who smiles while buying limes is a woman nobody follows."

She looked at me the way she used to look at arithmetic problems she hadn't solved yet — narrowed eyes, mouth slightly open, mind running ahead of her face. My daughter counts everything. It is her gift and her armor both. But there are things that do not go into columns, and one of them is this: when a house is suspected, the fastest way to un-suspect it is to fill it with the right guests and let them see nothing but cake.

"You're going to invite them," she said slowly. "You're going to invite *Brandt's wife* into this house. Two days after a search."

"Two days after a search is exactly when you invite them," I said. "A guilty woman hides. An innocent woman is insulted nobody asked sooner." I took the basket off her arm and began packing it myself, because she was still standing there with her jaw working. "Go on. And tell Senhora Vidal I'll need her good tablecloth back by noon, and tell her I said she may come too, since her husband counts the drying sheds and his ears are attached to his work."

"Astrid," I said, before she reached the gate, and she stopped without turning around because she knows my voice the way she knows the sound of rain starting. "Whatever they ask you at the shops — and someone will ask — we are giving this party because after such an ugly week, the ladies deserve something kind. That's all. Say it like you believe it."

"And if I don't believe it?"

"Then say it anyway. Belief is a crop, minha filha. Sometimes you plant it and water it and it comes up later, on its own schedule."

She went. And I stood in my kitchen with flour on my hands and a war in my chest, and I did what I have done for thirty years, which is put one thing in order so I can pretend the other things are also in order.

* * *

Let me tell you what the overseers' wives know, because everyone on this island assumes they know nothing, and that assumption has kept me fed with information for longer than some of those men have been alive.

Senhora Brandt — Greta, though she'd faint if I called her that in company — knows every figure her husband mutters over at night, because he mutters, and she lies awake beside him doing sums of her own about what his moods mean for her household accounts. She knows when he's pleased and when he's frightened, and a man like Brandt is never more frightening than when he is frightened.

Senhora Vidal's husband counts the sheds, yes, but she counts him, and she has told me twice over cards which clerks he trusts and which ones drink. Senhora Okonjo's sister works in the mail room at the company office, and Senhora Okonjo complains about it constantly — *the hours, the damp, the ink stains* — which is how I learn the mail schedule without ever asking. And Senhora Ferreira, the newest wife, young and lonely and desperate for anyone to be kind to her, would tell me the contents of her own medicine cabinet if I admired her hat long enough.

People think a woman's party is frippery. Sugar and iced tea and talk about hats. But I learned a long time ago, in the years after Duarte's father drowned and the Verrone Company held the paper on our lives, that gossip moves through a compound the way water moves through limestone — slow, invisible, and carving channels that last centuries. The men make the rules out loud and write them down where anyone can seize them. The women pass the truth hand to hand, unwritten, unseizable, in kitchens and doorways and over cards, and no soldier has ever yet figured out how to search a sentence once it's been spoken and forgotten by everyone except the woman who needed it.

So. Limes. Ice. Four pounds of it, which on this island in this season is a statement of wealth or madness, and I intended both.

* * *

By two o'clock my veranda looked like a ship that had wrecked a dressmaker's shop. Astrid had strung the paper lanterns Tomás made her the year before the warrant — I watched her hands shake slightly as she tied the knots, and I pretended not to, because pretending not to notice your children's fear is also a mothering skill — and the table wore Senhora Vidal's good cloth and my second-best cups, and the smell of the lime cake was doing its work before any guest arrived. Two of the divers' wives drifted past the fence on their way from the beds and slowed, and I cut them slices through the gate slats without a word, because a party that feeds only the powerful isn't a party, it's a bribe.

Greta Brandt arrived first, naturally. She always arrives first; arriving first lets you survey the room as territory rather than be surveyed in it. She was wearing gray silk that must have cost more than three divers earn in a year, and she looked tired underneath it in the way that powder cannot fix.

"Rosa Almeida," she said, taking both my hands, "you are either the bravest woman on this island or the maddest. After this week?"

"After this week," I said, "a body needs sweetness or she'll turn bitter, and bitter women age terribly." I drew her inside. "Sit. You look like you haven't slept."

"I haven't." And there it was — not even an hour in, offered up freely, because lonely people in unhappy marriages will trade sleeplessness for sympathy the way sailors trade salt for fresh water. "Ernst hasn't slept either. He's been at the office until all hours. There was some trouble with—" She stopped herself, glanced at the room, rearranged her face. "Business matters."

"Of course," I said warmly, and handed her iced tea, and thought: *trouble with the ledger, Greta. Trouble with a ledger that walked out of your husband's quarters and dissolved into forty households like sugar into tea.*

I did not say it. You never say it. You hold the cup steady and you let her keep talking.

* * *

The trick with pearls — and I shucked enough of them in my youth, before my hands got too stiff for the knife, to know — is that you don't pry them open. You hold the shell in warm water until the animal relaxes on its own, and then the thing you wanted simply lies there in the opened halves, gleaming, yours.

A party is warm water.

By three o'clock we had covered hats, the scandalous price of imported flour, the new priest's singing voice ("like a hinge," said Senhora Vidal, and even Greta laughed), and the soldiers. The soldiers came up the way weather comes up — nobody introduced the topic, it simply arrived, and everyone spoke around it carefully until Senhora Ferreira, bless her guileless heart, said what they were all chewing:

"It frightens me, all these searches. What are they even *looking* for?"

"Papers," said Greta, into her tea, and then, catching herself, "so Ernst says. Some discrepancy in the records. He won't speak of it, but he's been beside himself. Twice this week he burned documents in the yard — actual flames, in this rain — and told the servants to say nothing."

"Burning papers in monsoon season," murmured Senhora Vidal. "Desperate man's housekeeping."

"Greta," I said gently, before the silence could grow teeth, "you must make him eat. Men like that forget food exists. Does he still take his supper at the office?" This was bait dressed as wifely concern, and she took it like a hungry fish.

"He eats at his desk now. He's moved the strongbox into his private room — he never used to keep it there, it lived in the counting-house vault. And there's been a boat engaged. A launch, chartered quiet, through a broker in the port town. He told me it's for cargo, but cargo launches don't need a crew that can handle open water in storm season, and they don't pay in advance in silver." She pressed her lips together, aware suddenly of how much she'd said, and I poured her more tea and turned the conversation loudly to whether the Ferreira baby should be christened in the dry season or risk the church boat, and the moment folded itself away into the general noise like a pearl into a closed shell.

But I had it now. Three pearls in one afternoon: the strongbox moved. The documents burned. A fast launch, chartered quietly, paid in silver, meant for weather no honest cargo boat sails in.

My husband found those lists thirty years ago, and the water took him for it. I had promised myself I would never again learn something dangerous while sitting down comfortably. Yet here I sat, comfortable as a cat, holding the shape of Brandt's whole escape plan in my head between sips of tea — and understanding, coldly, completely, that a man who burns his papers and moves his strongbox and charters a storm-worthy launch is not planning to answer to any commission. He is planning to be gone before one arrives. And whatever he cannot carry, he will sink. Including, I suspected, certain people who knew too much.

* * *

The mainland contact came to me by way of Senhora Okonjo, in the strangest way, which is to say the most ordinary way: she was complaining about her sister again.

"—and now Inês says the mail room has orders to hold anything addressed to the commission, whatever that means, some government word, and send it up to Mr. Brandt first—"

"Hold mail for the *commission*?" I said, fanning myself as though bored. "What commission?"

"Oh, you know, the peace people. Armistice business. Apparently there's some inquiry being talked about for after the war ends, and Inês says the office is in a state about it, everyone whispering. She says there's one name that comes up whenever they whisper — a lawyer or official on the mainland, some man named Cordeiro, in the port capital. Ernst's clerk sends letters to him by private courier, not the mail boat, and Inês only knows because she franked one by mistake and got screamed at." Senhora Okonjo sighed and helped herself to cake. "Honestly, the secrets in that office. You'd think the war were being run from there instead of the pearl beds."

Cordeiro. I turned the name over twice in my head and set it down carefully where I could find it again, the way you tuck a lamp into a niche before a storm. So Brandt had a friend on the mainland — or a buyer, or a partner — and letters ran between them outside the mail. If the launch sailed, it would likely sail toward that name. And if my daughter's Elias ever reached the mainland ragged and half-drowned, as drowning men tend to do, a name is sometimes the only rope thrown to a stranger.

I memorized it the way my daughter memorizes columns. We are not so different, Astrid and I. She writes hers down small on rice paper; mine are written somewhere no search will ever reach, in the place behind the breastbone where I also keep the taste of the sea the day the company boats brought back only one of the two men who went out.

* * *

They stayed until nearly five. Greta left last, pausing at the gate to squeeze my hand and say, with sudden naked sincerity, "Thank you for this, Rosa. I forget, sometimes, that there's kindness on this island that isn't for sale."

"Nothing here is for sale, Greta," I said, and smiled, and meant it in every direction at once.

Afterward Astrid and I cleared the cups in silence until the last lantern was dark, and then she stood at the rail looking down at the wet garden and said, "Well?"

So I told her. All of it. The strongbox, the burning, the launch paid in silver, the mail held, the name Cordeiro. She listened the way she does everything — completely, greedily, filing each item into its column — and when I finished she was quiet a long moment.

"The launch," she said finally. "If Brandt runs before any commission lands, he takes his books out and sinks them, and every family on this island stays property forever." Her hands had gone still on the rail, and I saw the fear move under her skin like a fish under ice. "We recovered the pages from the store, Mother, but he doesn't know that. He thinks he's only missing pieces. If he thinks the whole truth is loose, he might sail sooner. Or he might—"

"He might start making sure certain witnesses don't live to testify," I finished for her. "Yes. I've been thinking about that since the second cup of tea."

"So what do we do?"

"What we've always done," I said. "We watch, and we pass what we see along, and we stay useful-looking and harmless until the hour comes to be neither." I touched her cheek, that sharp dear face of hers, sixteen going on sixty. "And you will tell me everything Elias learns and everything the families hear, and between us we will know more about that launch than its captain does. Let the men have their guns and their warrants. Information is also a weapon, minha filha, and it's the one they never take away from us, because they never see us carrying it."

She almost smiled. "The underestimated weapon."

"That's us. The whole sex. Write it down if you like."

But she didn't laugh properly, and neither did I, because even as we stood there in the cooling dark, I was already turning the afternoon over, hunting the flaw in it — and I found it, the way you find a splinter, all at once and painfully.

Every woman at that table had spoken freely. Every woman had gossiped the way women always gossip. Except one. Senhora Ferreira, sweet, lonely, grateful Senhora Ferreira, had laughed at all the right moments and drunk her tea and admired my orchard — and had asked, just once, near the end, in that artless young-wife way of hers: *"Rosa, do forgive me, but why do you suppose your son wasn't at the tollhouse when it burned? Everyone says he was always there."*

I had answered her easily. Too easily. I had laughed and said Tomás was done with the tollhouse, done with the company, off working the interior gardens, and she had nodded and said *of course, how sensible,* and the party had gone on like water closing over a dropped coin.

It was only now, standing in the dark with my daughter, that I remembered where I had seen that girl's particular quality of listening before. Not among wives. Among the company clerks — the careful ones, the ones Brandt keeps.

"Astrid," I said quietly. "How well do we actually know the Ferreira woman?"

Astrid went very still beside me. Down in the lanes below the orchard, faint through the rain, a lantern was moving toward the company office — moving fast, the way lanterns move when someone is late delivering news.

"I don't know," my daughter said. "Mother — I don't know at all."
