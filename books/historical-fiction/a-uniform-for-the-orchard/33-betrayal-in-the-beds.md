## 33. Betrayal in the Beds

The rain had taught me to read faces the way I once read columns.

Every morning since the monsoon sealed the strait, I walked the compound's edge and counted what could not be counted aloud: which families held pages of the dismantled ledger, which houses still lit their lamps past curfew, which neighbors looked at me too long when I passed. Forty-one pieces of truth, distributed like seed rice among twenty-two homes, each fragment worthless alone and catastrophic together. It had seemed clever in the lamplit hours after we cut the ledger apart — Rosa's scissors snipping through the night, her hands steady, mine less so. Cleverness, I was learning, is only safety that hasn't been tested yet.

The test came on a Tuesday, in the pearl beds, from the last direction I would have chosen.

* * *

Marisol Teixeira had been diving longer than I had been alive. Her shoulders were maps of old scars, her laugh carried across three rows of drying racks, and every child on the island had at some point been fished out of deep water by Marisol's strong brown arms. She had held my father's head above water the day he didn't drown — the day before he did — and she had never once spoken of it to me, which I understood as its own kind of tenderness.

She was also the one who had taken a page. Page nineteen, sheaf of names, tucked into the false bottom of her coconut-oil tin because she said — laughing, always laughing — *if the company comes for it, they'll have to smell my way to it.*

On Tuesday morning she surfaced from the third bed with something wrong in her face. Not fear. Fear I knew; fear looked like Elias in his first week, like Tomás running for the mangroves. This was flatter. This was a face that had already done its arithmetic and hated the answer.

"Astrid." She caught my wrist as I passed with my tally slate. Her grip was diver's grip — gentle until it wasn't. "Walk with me to the far line. My ears ache and I want someone to complain to."

Nobody complained to me. I complained to nobody. We both knew the ritual was a door, and I walked through it anyway, because refusing would have told her I'd already guessed.

We stood at the far line where the beds went murky over old shell rubble. The rain came down soft and businesslike. Marisol looked at the water as if it owed her an apology.

"They took the Ferrazes this morning," she said.

The world went very quiet under all that rain noise. "Taken."

"Before dawn. Soldiers and two of Brandt's men. Old Ferraz, his wife, the boy Joaquim. They put them in the storehouse cell and posted a guard, and by breakfast the wives were being told it was a matter of *unregistered correspondence.*" She spat into the water. "Correspondence. As if a grandmother writes letters."

I kept my face doing nothing. Inside, everything was falling — page seven was with Dona Ferraz, sewn into a quilt hem, and page eleven sat under Joaquim's mattress, and if either had been found—

"Were there papers?" I asked. My voice came out level. Somewhere in the last year I had grown a second voice for emergencies, flat as ledger paper. "Did you see them take anything?"

"No papers." She turned and looked at me then, straight on, and the flatness cracked and underneath it was something worse than fear. It was shame. "Astrid. Listen to me. There's going to be another search tomorrow. The whole east quarter. And I need you to hear it from me first, because—" her jaw worked, "—because I'm the reason they know there's anything to find."

The rain filled the silence between us.

"I sold my page," Marisol said. "Three days ago. To the broker's man who comes with the rice boats. He's been asking around since the bridge closed — offering rice, medicine for my sister's cough, and finally money, real money, more than five years of diving. And I thought—" she laughed, and it was the worst sound I'd heard that season, worse than the storm that took our roof, "—I thought, it's one page. One page out of forty. What can one page do? I even counted them first, God forgive me, I counted them like you count pearls, figuring the loss against the whole string."

"You told them about the others." It wasn't a question. My emergency voice again, steady while everything beneath it screamed.

"I told them it was part of a book. That's all I meant to tell them. But men like that don't stop at the first answer, do they? They asked who else had tins with false bottoms, who'd been friendly with your mother, whose houses the Almeida girl visited after dark. And I was tired, Astrid, and my sister couldn't breathe, and every question I answered bought another hour of not answering the next one." She pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "They know it's families in the east quarter. They know it's more than one. Tomorrow they'll turn every house over down to the floorboards, and the Ferrazes will sit in that cell until somebody's found guilty, because Brandt needs somebody found guilty so the rest of us stay quiet."

I should have felt fury. I had been saving up fury for months the way Rosa saved rice, and I reached for it now and found it wouldn't come. All I could see was Marisol's sister, thirteen years old, gray-lipped with the cough that had gone through the divers' quarters in September, the one Rosa had treated with honey and boiled ginger because there was no medicine left to buy. All I could see was the arithmetic Marisol had done, and how it was the same arithmetic the company forced on all of us every day of our lives: one thing against another thing, a sister's breath against a stranger's safety, and no column anywhere that added up clean.

"How many did you name?" I asked.

"Four." Her voice broke on it. "Four houses. But Astrid — the other eighteen, I never—"

"It doesn't matter." And then, because it did matter, because lying to Marisol would have made me Brandt: "It matters. But right now it doesn't help. Go home. Dive today. Be seen diving, be seen complaining about your ears, be exactly what you've always been. If you change your pattern they'll know you've talked."

"And you?"

"I'm going to move four families' worth of truth before dark."

* * *

Rosa took the news the way she took everything — standing up, sleeves rolled, already working.

"The Ferraz page first," she said. We were in the orchard, ostensibly thinning fruit that was weeks too green, our voices pitched under the drum of rain on leaves. "If they find names in that house tonight, the search tomorrow becomes a massacre. The others we might save. That one we must."

Page seven. Fourteen names, including two men listed as sold north who were, at that moment, hiding in the drying sheds eating rice from Rosa's cache. Including — I remembered without wanting to — the row where my father's name appeared twice, once as wages owed and once as debt collected, the double-entry that had started all of this.

"We can't be seen carrying anything," I said. "They'll watch the paths tonight. Brandt doubles the patrols whenever he's nervous."

"He's nervous now?"

"He's been nervous since the ledger vanished. He just doesn't know where to point it yet."

Rosa was quiet a moment, turning a green mango over in her fingers, and I watched her arrive at the idea the way she arrived at everything — practically, and slightly ahead of me.

"Fruit baskets," she said. "Tomorrow morning the overseers' wives expect their weekly delivery — Dona Verrone herself complained last week that the rains had thinned her table. Nobody searches a fruit basket going *into* the big house, Astrid. And nobody imagines the company's own ledgers sleeping under the company's own pineapples."

"The big house is the last place—"

"—is exactly where they'll never look, which makes it the best place, *for one night*. We move the pages in with the fruit, and tomorrow, when the search tears through the east quarter and finds nothing, we move them out again to new houses, ones Marisol never named." She set the mango down and looked at me with an expression I was beginning to recognize as the inheritance she kept trying to give me: grief and strategy wearing each other's clothes. "Your father used to say the safest hiding place is inside the wolf's own coat. He learned it the hard way. Let's learn it the easy one, for once."

* * *

We worked through the afternoon in a rhythm we'd perfected in the weeks since the cutting: Rosa harvesting, me carrying, the pages traveling inside hollowed breadfruit and doubled basket bottoms and, in one desperate case, rolled into the wax paper of a candle stub that Dona Luzia's girl delivered to the sheds as though fetching light were her own idea.

The Ferraz house first, at dusk. Old Ferraz's wife met us at the back door with the quilt already unpicked — she'd heard about the arrests by the compound's oldest telegraph, which is to say the laundry line, and she hadn't needed anyone to tell her why. Her hands shook as she handed over the hem section, fourteen names in my own careful stitching, but her voice didn't.

"Tell the boy's mother we'll hold," she said. "Tell her the Ferrazes have survived worse than Brandt. We survived his father."

Then the Lourdes house — because Marisol's four had included the Lourdeses, and page eleven under young Tomasinho's mattress had to travel too, and by the time we'd finished, the rain had thickened from drumming to roaring and the patrol lanterns were already swinging along the east paths, early, eager, hungry.

And then, in the black space between one lightning flash and the next, I understood what Marisol's betrayal had actually cost. Not the pages. Pages could run. What it cost was this: tomorrow, when soldiers pulled apart the homes of people who had hidden nothing, those people would stand in the wreckage of their kitchens knowing that a neighbor had pointed the soldiers there. Twenty-two houses had held together on a single thread — the certainty that everyone inside the secret was inside it together. Marisol had snapped the thread. And a secret that has been sold once is a secret that everyone knows can be sold twice.

I stood in Rosa's doorway watching the lanterns and did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding. Two families in cells. Four houses marked. Eighteen remaining, scattered among neighbors who now had excellent reasons to wonder about each other. Against all of it: one broker's man with deep pockets, one overseer with a missing ledger and a closing window of time, and somewhere out past the rain, past the sealed strait, a boat that had sailed in October carrying Elias and a collar full of names toward a commission that might or might not still exist.

We needed the pages closer, not scattered. We needed them somewhere one pair of trusted hands could gather them in an hour. And there was only one place on this island that every search would skip, every soldier would ignore, and every traitor would overlook — because it was too obvious, too audacious, too much like something Astrid Almeida would do precisely because it was the last thing anyone would believe.

"The storehouse," I said to Rosa, and saw her go still. "Brandt's own storehouse. Where they're holding the Ferrazes."

My mother looked at me for a long moment across the lamp flame, and I watched her decide — not whether it was madness, because we both knew it was, but whether it was *our* kind of madness. Then she reached up and began taking down the dried chilies from the rafters, which was Rosa's way of saying yes.

"If we're going to rob the company," she said, "we'll want a diversion worth remembering. Start making a list of everything Brandt can't bear to lose."

Outside, the rain kept falling, patient as a creditor. Somewhere in the dark, two families sat in a cell for a crime they hadn't committed, and thirty-nine pieces of the truth lay hidden in breadfruit and quilts and candle wax, waiting for morning — and for me.
