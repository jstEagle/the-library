## 34. Recovery Under Rain

The rain that came on the fourth day of the betrayal was the kind my mother called *honest rain* — no wind in it, no theater, just water falling straight down as if it had made up its mind and meant to keep its word. It filled the orchard ditches. It turned the compound paths to porridge. And it meant, above all things, that Brandt's sentries would be huddled under the storehouse eaves with their collars up and their tempers sour, watching nothing but their own misery.

"Good weather for thieves," Rosa said at breakfast, and passed me the last of the rice porridge without being asked.

She said it the way she said everything now — flatly, efficiently, a woman who had stopped wasting syllables somewhere around the year her husband drowned. But there was something underneath it, something almost bright. My mother had spent two days since the betrayal doing arithmetic behind her eyes, and I had learned to read her silences the way I once read ledgers: not the numbers themselves, but where they pressed hardest against the page.

Two families taken. The D'Souzas and the Marikars, dragged from their houses before dawn while the informer — a diver named Pauly Fernandes who had sold us out for a remission of debt and, I suspected, a bottle of company arrack — pointed with his chin from behind Brandt's shoulder. Two families, and with them, eleven pages of the duplicate ledger, seized from the places we had hidden them: inside a mattress ticking, beneath a shrine's false floor, rolled into the hollow handle of a fishing net frame. Eleven pages of thirty-eight.

I had spent those two days doing what I do best, which is counting. Counting what was lost, what remained, and what could be gotten back. The answer to the third question had surprised me.

"They'll hold the pages in the company store," I told Rosa quietly, in the space between one spoonful and the next. "Not Brandt's quarters — he won't keep evidence of his own crime under his own bed with the commission possibly coming. He'll want it somewhere he can claim he never touched. The store, back room, with the customs seals. He'll have logged them as *contraband, pending disposal*."

Rosa looked at me over the rim of her cup. "You've thought about this."

"I've thought about nothing else."

"And?"

"And," I said, "he has to log everything he seizes. Because Brandt is a man who believes paper protects him. He thinks if it's written down correctly, it can't be held against him." I felt my mouth do something that was almost a smile. "He wrote down where he put us, Mama. He wrote down where he put *us*."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about fury: that after enough years underwater, it stops boiling and starts to set, like pearl to shell. I had been angry since I was nine years old and watched men carry my father's name off a page and into a lie. I had been angry through every column that didn't add, every diver listed twice, every quiet theft dressed up as bookkeeping. But anger is a hot thing, and hot things make mistakes. What I felt now was colder and better organized. It had a floor plan. It had a timetable. It had, thanks to my mother's garden parties and Mrs. Verrone's loose tongue, a schedule of which sentry drank which brand of cheap rum and at what hour he began to feel philosophical about his duties.

The store sat at the landward edge of the compound, a long shed of coral stone and corrugated iron, built by men who assumed no one would ever be foolish enough to steal from the company on the company's own island. The back room held confiscated goods: smuggled tobacco, untaxed cloth, and now eleven sheets of rice paper that were worth more than all of it together. The front held salted fish, rope, lamp oil, and a clerk named Mr. Pinto, whose entire soul lived in a rubber stamp and who went home to his wife at sundown sharp, because Mr. Pinto believed that overtime was a form of theft committed against oneself.

The plan, such as it was, belonged to my mother.

"You'll go in through the loading door," she'd said, drawing lines in spilled flour on the kitchen table like a general who had learned cartography from hiding rice. "Elias cannot come — he walks like a wounded man because he is a wounded man, and pride makes him argue about it. Tomás is still in the mangroves and stays there until his face stops being on a warrant. So it's you."

"It should be me," Elias had protested anyway, from his corner, his arm finally out of its sling and only slightly reluctant about it.

"You," Rosa said, with the gentleness she reserved for people she was about to overrule completely, "will be sitting in this kitchen looking harmless when the soldiers sweep past, because they will sweep past, and if they find an empty house they will wonder what we're all doing elsewhere. You are the alibi, Elias. Alibis are not permitted heroism."

And herself? She would make the diversion. She would not tell me what it was until it was time, which I understood was her way of keeping me from arguing, and also her way of carrying her own share of the danger in her own hands. When I pressed her, she only said: "You steal the truth, *filha*. Let me steal their attention. Everyone should carry what they're best at."

* * *

We went out at the second hour past midnight, when even the dogs of the compound give up their opinions and sleep.

I wore my oldest dress, dark, and my hair bound flat, and around my ribs, against my skin, the master copy — twenty-seven pages folded small, sewn into a second collar I now wore day and night, sleeping in it, bathing around it, so that the names of every indentured family had become, quite literally, part of me. The knife rode at my hip. Not for use. For the feeling of it. A girl should bring something of her father on a night like this.

The rain fell with that honest persistence, drumming the iron roof of the store like impatient fingers. Two sentries stood under the eave by the loading door, and I crouched in the shadow of a stack of empty oil drums forty feet away and listened to them be miserable.

"—third night running," one was saying. "My boots have opinions. They are all bad ones."

"You think you suffer. I stood the jetty watch Tuesday. There is a crab out there, a specific crab, that has declared war on me personally."

"A crab."

"He knows what he did."

I nearly laughed. Fear and laughter live closer together than anyone admits; I think they share a coat.

Then the diversion arrived, and it arrived the way my mother does everything: precisely, unglamorously, and with total commitment.

Down the compound road came a light — lantern light, swaying wildly — and a voice raised in the particular wail of a woman whose night has gone entirely wrong. Rosa appeared through the rain half-carrying, half-dragging old Mrs. D'Cruz from the widows' quarters, who moaned theatrically and clutched her stomach as though attempting to give birth to a durian.

"Fever! Fever in the widows' house — three women down — someone must fetch the medical box from the store, quickly, quickly, or there will be dead widows and everyone will ask why the company let them die!"

It was brilliant. It was terrible. It was perfect. Because the medical box was kept in the *store*, and any sentry with sense would send the woman to Mr. Pinto rather than open the doors himself — but Mr. Pinto was home — and so the sentries would have to choose between waking Pinto, disturbing the overseer, or opening the store themselves to get rid of a howling widow. And Brandt, I knew, had left standing orders that the widows' quarter was to be kept placated at all costs; Rosa's garden parties had taught him that unhappy wives of senior divers made more trouble than any smuggler.

The sentries argued for exactly as long as I'd predicted — I could see them doing it, shoulders hunched, hands gesturing, the rain making their faces miserable — and then one broke for the overseer's quarters and the other unlatched the loading door to fetch the medical box himself, muttering about fevers, crabs, and the general injustice of colonial service.

I moved.

Forty feet of rain-slick ground, counted in my head beforehand, run in silence. The sentry had his back to me, wrestling the heavy door. Inside, darkness smelling of fish brine and lamp oil. I slipped through the gap before it closed, flattened myself between a barrel of salt cod and the wall, and listened to my heartbeat argue with the rain.

The sentry lit a lamp, found the medical box on its shelf near the front, muttered a curse at its weight, and carried it out — leaving the door, in his hurry and his misery, *unlatched*. Bless him. Bless his boots with opinions. I waited until his footsteps faded into the widows' chorus of moaning — Mrs. D'Cruz had really outdone herself; I would need to thank her with something from the orchard — and then I stood up in the dark of the company store and got to work.

* * *

The back room door had a customs seal across it, lead and wire, stamped with the company crest. I had brought wire cutters stolen from Tomás's old tollhouse kit, and I cut the seal the way I'd practiced on scrap wire a hundred times: cleanly, close to the lead, so the seal itself stayed whole. A broken seal could be restrung. A missing one could not. Somewhere in my chest, the ledger-clerk in me noted the irony that I had learned forgery-adjacent skills from the very system I was robbing. Brandt had trained me well. That was perhaps the joke the water had been saving all these years.

Inside: shelves, crates, dust, and on the second shelf, a tin document box with the company stamp and a label in Brandt's precise hand: *CONTRABAND — SEIZED 14th & 16th — PENDING DISPOSAL.*

Eleven pages. I counted them twice, because counting is what I am for. Eleven, yes — but wrong. I fanned them in the lamplight I dared not light, using instead the grey glow through the high vent window, and my cold fury turned, for one moment, to ice.

One page was not ours.

Ours were copied on rice paper, thin, soft-edged, written small in my own cramped hand or in the hands of neighbors. This twelfth page was company stock — thick, ruled, official — and when I angled it toward the vent-light, I saw my father's name on it. *Joaquim Almeida, diver, deceased — account settled against estate.* In Brandt's hand. With figures. With dates. With the little notation in the margin that I had seen once before, years ago, in a page I wasn't supposed to see: *as per original arrangement, J.A.*

*As per original arrangement.* As though my father's drowning were a clause in a contract.

They hadn't seized it. He had *added* it — moved it here, into the seizure pile, either to bury it among contraband where no auditor would look closely, or — and this thought arrived with its own cold wind — because he had begun to suspect which families still hid pages, and wanted bait in the box. A page too easy to find, waiting for whoever came.

I stood in the dark and let myself feel it for three full breaths. Then I took our eleven pages, folded them into the oilskin packet sewn inside my sash, and took my father's page too — because whatever trap it might be, I would not leave Joaquim Almeida sitting in a tin box labeled *pending disposal*. If it was bait, I would spring it on my own terms. If it was evidence, it was *ours*, and it was coming home.

I resealed the wire with the cut ends twisted tight — it would pass a glance and nothing more, but a glance was all anyone would give a contraband box in the rain — closed the back room, and was through the unlatched loading door and gone before the sentry returned the medical box, grumbling, to its shelf.

* * *

My mother met me two fields from the house, soaked to the bone, Mrs. D'Cruz already dispatched home with a bottle of the good arrack and my sincere compliments on her performance. We didn't speak until we were inside, door barred, lamp hooded, Elias rising stiffly from his corner with a question in his face.

"Eleven?" he asked.

"Eleven recovered." I laid them on the table, then laid my father's page beside them. "And one extra. His."

Rosa dried her hands, picked up the page, and read it all the way through without hurrying, the way she reads everything, as though the words owe her rent. Her face did not change. That was the frightening thing and the admirable thing about my mother: grief in her had been refined so long it no longer showed on the surface at all. It just made everything she did more exact.

"*As per original arrangement,*" she read aloud at last, softly. "Your father found the lists in the first year. Did I ever tell you how he died?" 

"You told me the boat capsized."

"The boat didn't capsize." She set the page down and smoothed it flat with both hands, once, as if tucking it into bed. "He was going to take the first copies across the bridge. Brandt's men followed him onto the water. Your father was the strongest swimmer on this island, Astrid. Stronger than any of them. Men like that don't drown in a squall they've swum through a hundred times."

The rain kept its honest rhythm on the roof. I thought of my father's name measured out in carryable things, all those years, and how I had carried it without ever knowing its full weight.

"So it's bait," Elias said quietly. "Or it's a confession."

"Both," Rosa said. "With Brandt it's always both. He writes everything down because he believes paper is loyalty." She looked at me, and something passed between us that had no words in either language we spoke. "Now we know what happened. Now we hold the proof of it. That's not loss, *filha*. That's ammunition."

But I couldn't stop looking at the page. *Pending disposal.* My father, filed under garbage, waiting nineteen years in a tin box.

"Mama," I said, and my voice came out steadier than I deserved, "we lost a page in the raids. The one naming the Marikar boy's conscription sale. Without it, his family's claim rests on testimony alone." I tapped my father's death record with one finger. "So here is the trade I'm proposing. We give Brandt back his belief that his bait worked. We let him think the thief took the easy page and ran. And meanwhile—" I spread our eleven recovered pages beside his confession, "—we move everything again, tonight, before first light, to places even Pauly Fernandes couldn't sell if he memorized them drunk. New hides. No patterns. Nothing under floors, nothing in mattresses. We hide the truth the way the orchard hides the rice."

Rosa almost smiled. Almost. On my mother, that was a parade with brass instruments.

"And where," she asked, "does a girl hide eleven pages so well that even the rain can't find them?"

I looked down at the master copy against my ribs, warm from my own body, and then at the fresh rice paper and needle and thread waiting in the sewing basket, and I said the thing I had been assembling all night, in the dark, between barrels of salt cod:

"In plain sight. In uniforms. Every washerwoman, every orchard hand, every widow who owes this company a grievance gets a new collar seam this week. Brandt searches houses. He has never once searched a hem." I threaded the needle. "Let him count heads. We'll count stitches."

Elias laughed — a short, rusty sound, the first real laugh I'd heard from him since the betrayals. "You're terrifying," he said, with unmistakable admiration.

"She's a bookkeeper," Rosa said proudly, and handed me the scissors.

Outside, the rain went on falling, steady and honest, covering the sound of thread pulling through cloth, of names being folded small and given away to be kept safe. And somewhere across the compound, in a dry room smelling of ink and rum, a man slept believing his bait had been taken — not knowing that by morning, the whole island would wear his crimes stitched against its skin, and that he had less time than he thought.

Because while we sewed, word came knocking softly at the shutter: a launch had been provisioned at the jetty, engines tested in the dark. Brandt's launch. Whatever he was planning, he planned to sail soon — and men who flee always try to sink what they leave behind.
