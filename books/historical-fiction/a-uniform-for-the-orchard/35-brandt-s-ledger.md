## 35. Brandt's Ledger

There was a particular pleasure in balancing a book, and Hendrik Brandt had never been ashamed of it. Some men found God in a chapel. Some men found Him in the pearl beds at dawn, when the light came up green and the divers rose dripping with their little fortunes in their teeth. Brandt found order in a column of figures that summed exactly where it ought to sum, and if that made him a smaller kind of believer, well — his religion had never once asked him to forgive anybody.

He sat in his quarters above the counting house with the shutters half closed against the rain and the lamp turned low, because a man who was about to do what he was about to do preferred to do it by lamplight anyway. It felt correct. There were conventions even in treachery.

The books lay before him in three stacks, and he regarded them the way a father regards three sons of very different temperaments.

The first stack was the honest ledger. The one the company saw, the one the auditors would see if any auditor ever braved the strait again, the one Astrid Almeida kept with her infuriating, beautiful precision. He had watched her hands move across those pages for two years now and had thought more than once that she was wasted on this island. Wasted on the company, even. A girl who could make ink behave like that deserved a bank in Colombo or a ministry in London, somewhere her talent could be properly exploited instead of merely used.

The second stack was the true ledger. The duplicate. Or rather — the original, since the first stack was the forgery, though "original" and "forgery" had long ago swapped places in his mind the way a river swaps banks in flood season. Every diver sold twice. Every name entered into the naval conscription rolls *and* into the labor broker's manifest, each sale paid into an account under a name that belonged to a dead man in Rotterdam whom Brandt had invented eleven years ago over a bottle of arrack and had maintained ever since with the tenderness of a gardener maintaining an orchid. Old Cornelis Verrone had thought the scheme was vulgar. Old Cornelis Verrone was also dead, which Brandt had always considered a professional commentary on his judgment.

The third stack was the smallest and the most dangerous: the witness list. Names of every man who had seen too much, every diver whose signature had been borrowed, every clerk who had carried a page without understanding it, every family on the island that could stand up in front of a commission and say *I know, I saw, here is my mark.* It was not a long list. Islands never are. But it was thorough, and Brandt prided himself on thoroughness the way other men prided themselves on courage — as the virtue available to them when all the fashionable ones ran out.

He poured himself two fingers of the good whisky, the bottle he kept behind the false back of the drawer where a sentimental man might have kept letters from a wife, and allowed himself a moment of accounting.

*Assets,* he thought. One launch, provisioned, fuel enough for the crossing in fair weather and just barely enough in foul. One escape timed for the dark of the moon, nine days hence, ahead of any commission ship, ahead of the armistice, ahead of the whole tedious reckoning the world seemed determined to have. One fortune in a Rotterdam account that did not exist and therefore could not be frozen, traced, or taxed.

*Liabilities.* One duplicate ledger currently missing from his quarters.

His jaw tightened pleasantly, the way a man's jaw tightens when he bites down on something bitter and decides to swallow it rather than spit.

* * *

The theft had happened the night of his own dinner, which was the sort of irony Brandt normally appreciated and currently did not. He had stood in his own parlor making charming conversation to the wives of men he despised while some light-fingered ghost walked into his bedroom and walked out with four pounds of paper that represented eleven years of careful, patient, elegant work.

He had narrowed the suspects within a day. That part had been almost restful. There were perhaps six people on the island with the knowledge to understand what the ledger was and the access to reach it, and of those six, five were accounted for by habit, cowardice, or alibi. Which left the Almeidas, the way everything on this island eventually left the Almeidas.

The girl. Of course the girl. He should have sent her north with the last labor shipment two years ago and told himself so monthly, but there had always been a reason to keep her — her arithmetic, her silence, the way the other clerks worked better when she was near, the small vanity of owning the finest instrument on the island. Vanity, Brandt reflected, was the only sin that billed you quarterly.

But knowing and proving were different columns entirely, and he could not search the Almeida compound, not yet, not openly. Because — and here he refilled his glass and permitted himself the day's second bitter joke — because he needed the boat to sail.

That was the exquisite bind, and he turned it over now like a coin he suspected was clipped. The launch that would carry him to the mainland carried cargo: the season's pearls, sealed and manifested, plus certain crates whose contents appeared on no manifest at all. If he raised the alarm about stolen papers, if he ordered the boat searched and delayed, the delay rippled outward. The naval cutter that shadowed the strait grew curious. The harbor master asked questions. And somewhere in the chain of questions a man might ask why the overseer of Verrone Trading was leaving the island three days before the rains with private crates and no transfer orders.

No. The boat sailed on schedule, quiet as a knife going into water. He would simply be on it, and the ledger problem would stay behind him on an island about to be sealed off from the world by four months of monsoon, where whatever scraps of truth remained would rot in rice jars and mango roots until they were compost, and by the time the bridge reopened he would be a memory with a foreign accent and no forwarding address.

It was clean. It was, he told himself, almost elegant.

Almost. Because elegance required that nothing float to the surface after him, and that was where the third stack came in.

* * *

He took up the witness list and read it through one final time, the way a man reads a poem he is about to burn — slowly, with attention, out of respect for the craft if not the sentiment.

The names were organized, naturally, by reliability. He had learned early that witnesses sorted themselves into three columns. Column one: the frightened, who would recant anything for protection and needed only to be left alone to forget everything. Column two: the bought, who were reliable right up until someone offered a higher price, and who therefore functioned less as witnesses than as auctions waiting to happen. Column three — the short column, the dangerous one — held the ones who couldn't be frightened and wouldn't be bought, and there were only four names in it, and he knew all four by heart the way he knew the numbers that didn't add up.

Rosa Almeida. Who had smiled at his wife's memory and fed his overseers' wives cakes for fifteen years and known, he was now nearly certain, the entire shape of the fraud since before he'd perfected it. He had assumed for years that her silence was purchased with the girl's schooling. Lately he had begun to suspect her silence had been purchased with nothing at all — that she simply understood, better than he did, how long a secret keeps when everyone holding it has roots.

Elias Verhoeven, deserter, presumed drowned, actually festering in somebody's drying shed like a bad oyster. Brandt had put the word out through the divers' lines weeks ago: a stranger, half-dead, taken in somewhere along the shore. The word had come back nothing, which meant either the man was dead or the island had decided, collectively and without meeting, to lie to him. Both possibilities offended him. The second one alarmed him.

Tomás Almeida, tollhouse boy, smuggler of letters, holder of a warrant that Brandt had signed with genuine reluctance because warrants created paperwork and paperwork created trails. The boy was in the mangroves now, presumably, eating crabs raw and hating him with the clean renewable energy of youth.

And the girl herself. Astrid. Who had served his dinner with steady hands and lifted his key — yes, he knew about the key; he had discovered the wax residue in the lock two mornings later and had stood there feeling the strange mixed sensation of a man who has been robbed by someone talented — and who had then, instead of copying what she needed, committed the one error her otherwise flawless style could not excuse.

She had taken the whole book.

A copyist would have copied. A copyist leaves the original in place and walks away with something that fits in a sleeve. Taking the ledger itself was the act of a person who wanted the thing *gone*, wholly gone, unmakeable — which meant she wasn't building a case, she was building a barricade. It meant she understood that paper can be retracted, disowned, dismissed as forgery, but an absence cannot. You cannot produce a ledger you do not have, and neither can you claim its contents were misread when no one can read them at all.

Brandt turned his glass slowly on the desk and admitted, privately, in the room where he admitted things to no one, that it was the best crime anyone had committed against him in twenty years. Then he finished the whisky and got to work undoing it.

* * *

The solution, when he laid it out on the blotter in his neat accountant's hand, was beautifully simple, and he admired it the way he admired all his worst ideas.

He could not recover the pages. Fine. Recovering pages was a recovery problem, and recovery problems were for policemen, and there were no policemen within four hundred miles of water. What he could do was make the pages worthless — and paper became worthless the moment the story around it changed. Let the commission come, months from now, whenever the war finished doing whatever the war was going to do. Let them land on his tidy island full of tidy families. Let them ask their questions.

And let every witness they approached discover that the evidence in question was *contaminated*. Forged, in fact — forged by a runaway clerk and a deserter working together, the clerk with her access to the counting house, the soldier with his grudge against lawful authority. Two criminals manufacturing scandal to cover their own crimes. The girl's indenture irregular? Show the commission her uncle's debt, real enough, documented. The deserter's testimony? Deserter. The word did half the work by itself. Half the world's injustice, Brandt had observed, was accomplished simply by choosing which adjective preceded which noun.

For that story to hold, though, the true ledger had to die. Not disappear — disappearances invited searches, and searches invited finds. Die. Go into the sea, publicly, traceably, in circumstances that told the story he wanted told: the conspirators, caught, destroying their own forgery. Better still if a few pages surfaced afterward, water-stained, torn, *selectively* damaged — pages that showed alterations, corrections, the honest-looking scars of dishonest documents. He had already begun preparing those. Forgery of a forgery. It was the closest his profession came to art, and he was, he conceded, rather good at it.

Which left only the living evidence. The list in his hand. Four stubborn names, and the stubbornness of names, unlike the stubbornness of paper, did not dissolve in saltwater.

He weighed the list on his palm. It weighed nothing, of course. Guilt was the only heavy thing on the island and none of it was his.

The plan wrote itself in his head with the satisfying click of a column coming out even. On the ninth night, in the dark of the moon, the launch would take on its cargo. Somewhere in the channel, in deep water past the shoals where the soundings dropped away to nothing, the engine would be stopped — routine, he would say afterward, checking a fuel line — and over the side would go a weighted sack containing the true ledger's surviving fragments, the prepared false pages positioned to be found, and the witness list sewn inside a canvas that would itself be weighted, because paper floats and he had no intention of letting any of it wash ashore like a message in a bottle for some fisherman with a conscience.

Evidence and witnesses' proof, sinking together. And the witnesses themselves? The witnesses would remain on an island sealed by rain, reachable by no commission, governable by whoever held the storehouse keys — and by spring, if any of them still talked, they would be talking about a story he had already written for them, in their mouths, in their fear, in the ordinary human hunger to believe the simplest explanation.

He dipped his pen. At the bottom of the witness list, beneath Rosa Almeida's name, he added a line in his small exact hand:

*Disposition: resolved with cargo.*

It was, he reflected, the phrase he used for unsellable stock. Damaged pearls that went into the sea rather than degrade the brand. He had done it before, with oysters that had spoiled in the beds, with a crate of gin that had come off the boat already leaking, with — once, early, regrettable, necessary — a diver named Susa who had seen the wrong page at the wrong time and whose drowning had been ruled the accident it technically was, since Brandt had never touched him, only arranged the weather of his life until the sea did the rest.

He blotted the line. His hand did not shake. His hand had never shaken, and he experienced a brief, cold flicker of pride in that, and beneath the pride, deeper, where he kept the things he never counted, a small dry voice that sounded annoyingly like old Cornelis saying: *you mistake stillness for steadiness, Hendrik. They are not the same animal.*

He silenced the voice the way he silenced all audits. He moved on to the next entry.

* * *

The rain thickened toward evening, drumming on the shutters, and Brandt worked by lamplight through the inventory of departure. Provisions: checked twice. Fuel: checked. Charts: the mainland approach marked in pencil, the channel buoy positions verified against the last survey, which was seven years stale but the navy hadn't updated it either, which he counted as a small mercy of a distracted world. Money: gold in a belt, notes in oilskin, the Rotterdam account a fiction with excellent credit.

At the bottom of the strongbox, wrapped in oilcloth, lay the false pages — his masterwork of the season, six sheets aged with tea and handled with tallow to look worried-over, bearing alterations in two hands, one of them a fair imitation of the girl's neat upright script. He had practiced her letterforms for an hour a night for a week. Her *f* had a small vanity in it, a flourish she probably didn't know she made, and reproducing another person's unconscious vanity was, Brandt thought, the entire trade of power in a single stroke.

He was admiring the forgery when the knock came — soft, apologetic, the knock of Karunatilleke the harbormaster's clerk, a damp little man built entirely out of apologies.

"Mr. Brandt. Beg pardon, sir. The launch."

"What of it?"

"She's — that is, I did the walk-round myself, sir, like you said, and the bilge—" The clerk wrung his cap. "Someone's been in her, sir. Stores shifted. And there's canvas gone. A length of canvas, and some cord, and — sir, I don't like to say it, but it has the look of a stowaway's kit. Or someone making ready. Someone small."

Brandt set down the pen with great care, aligning it parallel to the blotter's edge, because a man's habits were the last thing he surrendered and he intended to surrender nothing at all.

"Small," he repeated.

"Yes, sir. Small hands, by the look of the knots."

Outside, the rain came down on the island like a verdict being read slowly, and Brandt looked at his three stacks of books — the lie, the truth, and the list of everyone who could tell them apart — and felt the peculiar sensation of a balanced column suddenly refusing to sum.

Someone was planning to be on his boat.

He smiled, thinly, the way a ledger smiles: without warmth, without mercy, and absolutely without error.

"Well," he said to the clerk, to the rain, to the small-handed ghost in his accounts. "Then we shall simply have to count heads at the gangway. All of them."
