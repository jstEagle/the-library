## 36. The Storm Crossing

There is a particular kind of arithmetic a person learns when she has kept ledgers since she was eleven years old. It is not the arithmetic of schoolmasters — tidy columns, obedient sums. It is the arithmetic of what can be hidden in what must be carried. A page of rice paper folded eight times fits inside a collar seam. A knife the length of a finger fits inside a boot. And a girl of sixteen, thin from a season of short rations and bent from years over a copying desk, fits — barely, painfully, with her knees against her chin — inside the aft locker of Brandt's escape launch.

I had measured myself against that locker three nights before, by candlelight, in the boathouse while the watchman drank his arrack. I had measured the locker too: four feet by two by eighteen inches, lined with canvas that smelled of tar and old fish, positioned directly beneath the stern bench where Brandt liked to sit because it was the driest seat on any boat and he was a man who believed dryness was a form of rank.

Four feet by two by eighteen inches. I wrote it in my head the way I wrote everything, in a neat column, and beneath it I entered the other figures: one master copy of the forged conscription lists, sewn flat against my ribs in oilcloth; one knife; one canteen; and no plan at all for what would happen after the island fell behind us.

The plan, such as it was, ended at *get aboard*. Everything after that belonged to the water.

* * *

Brandt fled at night, which told me everything about how frightened he was, because Brandt was a man who did everything in daylight and made sure people saw him doing it. He announced his departure as an inspection tour of the mainland offices. He wore his good coat. He had his own ledgers crated and loaded by four men who were not permitted to ask questions, and I know they were not permitted to ask questions because I was the one who typed the instruction on the company letterhead, in my neat clerk's hand, while Brandt stood behind my shoulder breathing through his nose like a man pricing fish.

"Seal it well," he said. "Salt air ruins paper."

"Yes, sir," I said, and thought: *so does fire, sir, and so do commissions of inquiry.*

He had offered me a place in the launch's crew, actually. That was the joke of it — the leash disguised as honor again, extended one last time. "You've been useful, Almeida. A girl who can keep books is worth ten girls who can only dive." He said it the way a man praises a dog that has learned to fetch. "There's work on the mainland. Better quarters."

"I'm needed here," I said. "Mother's orchard. The rains coming."

He looked at me a moment too long, the way he had been looking at me since the duplicate ledger vanished from his quarters — a look that weighed and priced and found the total not quite adding up. Then he shrugged and went to supervise his crates, and I went home and told Rosa, in our kitchen, with the rain hammering the palm thatch like applause for a decision already made.

"He sails Thursday," I said. "Dawn tide."

Rosa was grinding chilies, and she did not stop grinding them, but her shoulders went very still, the way the whole house went still when Tomás used to come home late. "You're going to be on that boat."

"I'm going to be *under* that boat," I said, "in a manner of speaking."

She put down the grinding stone. She took my face in both hands, chili-bright fingers and all, and looked at me the way she had looked at me once before, the day my father's name came back to us attached to nothing but water. "Astrid," she said. "If the sea takes you, I will be furious with you for the rest of my life, and my life will be very long, because I intend to outlive every man in this company out of spite."

"That's the most encouraging thing anyone has ever said to me."

"It wasn't meant to encourage you." But her thumb brushed my cheekbone, once, and that was the blessing. Rosa did not say goodbye. Rosa said *be clever*, and pressed into my pocket a twist of paper containing dried fish and a lump of palm sugar, because even stowaways must eat, and mothers are mothers in every language including the language of smuggling.

* * *

Thursday dawn came gray and windless, which should have been my first warning. The old divers say a calm morning before the monsoon turns is the sea holding its breath, and the sea holds its breath the way a debt collector does — patiently, and right before it knocks.

I went aboard in the dark, in the hour when the watchman changed shifts and the harbor lamps burned low and oily. Getting into the locker required folding myself in half twice and entering feet-first like a letter going into an envelope, which is undignified, and I mention it only because dignity turned out to be the least of what I lost that day.

The oilcloth packet rode against my ribs. The knife rode at my ankle. The canteen I wedged where my shoulder could reach it. And then there was nothing to do but listen.

I heard the crew come aboard — six men, their voices flattened by early hour. I heard the crates go into the hold, heavier than office furniture had any right to be. I heard Brandt arrive last, because important men always arrive last, and heard him say, "Cast off. And if anyone asks at the customs point, we carry samples and correspondence," in the voice of a man rehearsing a lie he intended to use all the way across the strait.

The engine woke up grumbling. The hull began its small conversation with the water — knock, slap, knock — and then the mooring lines splashed free and the island began, without ceremony, to leave me.

I want to tell you I felt triumphant. What I mostly felt was seasick, cramped, and seized by a sudden vivid memory of every story Rosa had ever told about boats that went down in the strait, including the one carrying my father, which was not, perhaps, the story my nerves needed just then. The locker filled with the smell of tar and my own fear-sweat and the faint sweetness of Rosa's palm sugar leaking from its paper twist. I counted. It is what I do. I counted the engine's rhythm until it became a ledger line, and the ledger line became a rope, and I held onto it.

An hour, maybe two. The hull's voice changed as we cleared the headland and met the open channel — longer swells now, a slower, deeper roll, the boat lifting and setting down like something breathing in its sleep. Once, directly above my head, boots crossed the deck and stopped, and a voice said, "This locker's lashed shut, sir. Hasn't been opened since the northeast run."

"Leave it," said Brandt's voice, muffled by wood and canvas and my own heartbeat. "Nothing on this boat matters except what's in the hold."

*Nothing on this boat,* I noted in my mental ledger, *except one item valued beyond all others, currently filed under A for Almeida.*

Then the sea knocked.

* * *

It came the way the old divers said it would — the held breath let out all at once. The swell changed first, growing a shoulder, then a temper. Rain arrived not as drops but as a decision, a whole grey wall of it flung sideways, and the wind came up underneath it and got its hands under the hull and shook.

In the locker, I learned things about storms that no ledger teaches. I learned that a boat in heavy weather sounds like furniture being argued over by giants. I learned that canvas lining, loose at one corner, will whip your ear with the regularity of a schoolmaster. I learned that a canteen not perfectly wedged becomes a small brass animal determined to escape, and that catching it in the dark, in a rolling box, with your knees against your chin, is a full day's work.

Above me, chaos in a more organized register: shouted orders, the crash of something unsecured going over, Brandt's voice rising above it all with the particular shrillness of a man discovering that the sea has not read his contracts.

And then the hatch above the aft bench banged open, and cold green water came down the deck seams and into the locker through the gap where the lid didn't quite meet, and I heard Brandt shout, "Get forward, all of you! Get the tarp over the crates!" — followed by footsteps retreating, followed by silence, followed by the worst sound I have ever heard, which was the latch of the locker lifting.

Light fell in. Grey light, rain-light, and cut across it, braced against the rail with his good coat plastered to him and his hat gone somewhere into the strait, the face of Emil Brandt.

We looked at each other. There is a moment — I have since decided everyone gets exactly one — when a man sees something impossible and his mind offers him the kind version first. His face went through it: confusion, then the clerk's reflex of cataloguing (*girl, wet, aboard, impossible*), and then the arithmetic finished, and I watched him arrive at the sum.

"You," he said.

"Me," I agreed. It seemed pointless to deny it from inside his own locker.

"The ledger." He said it quietly, which was worse than shouting. The storm roared around us and he spoke quietly, leaning down into the rain, and his eyes had gone to the flat shape the oilcloth made against my soaked shirt as though he could read through cloth. "You've had it since the ball. Since the wax. All this time you've been sitting in my office taking dictation with it sewn into your clothes."

"You taught me to copy documents accurately, sir," I said. "I was a quick study."

Something moved in his face that was almost, horribly, respect — the respect a fox shows a trap that has learned to bait itself. Then his hand went under his coat, and I remembered my knife, and the sea chose that exact moment to pick up the launch and throw it.

* * *

I have been thrown by waves before. Every diver on this island has. But a wave throws you into water, which forgives; a wave threw Brandt's launch sideways and threw me against the locker wall hard enough to fill my mouth with the taste of copper, and threw Brandt off his feet entirely, and sent a crate in the hold loose from its lashings with a crack like a rifle shot.

By the time I had hauled myself out of the locker — out, into the open storm, because staying in a box with a cornered man was no longer the safer arithmetic — he was up on one knee with a pistol in his hand, and the rain was falling so hard the pistol might have been decorative.

"Give it to me," he shouted, and the wind took half the words and delivered the rest anyway.

"The lists?" I shouted back. "They're copies! Do you understand? Copies exist off this island whether I drown or not!" This was true, and also strategy, and also — I admit it — enormously satisfying to say to his face.

His finger tightened. The boat rolled. The shot went somewhere into the storm, and the recoil and the roll together put him against the rail, both hands grabbing it, the pistol still clutched, and the launch lay over so far that the channel itself seemed to lean in and look at us.

What happened next happened fast, and I will set it down plainly in Chapter whatever comes next, because some pages deserve their own heading. Here is what matters now: the sea had stopped being weather and become a party to the transaction. The crates in the hold — Brandt's own books, his private accounts, the second set of numbers by which he had sold every diver on this island twice — broke loose and slid toward the stern, toward us, iron-strapped and heavy as sin.

Brandt saw them coming and I saw him make the calculation, and it was the same calculation he had made his whole life, the one that had built him and would now unbuild him: *the books first.* The books were worth more than the boat. The books were worth more than anything. He reached for them even as the deck tilted past the angle where reaching is possible.

I grabbed the rail.

I am writing this account, so you know how that choice went. But standing there with the rain trying to peel my hands off the world and the master copy flat and warm against my ribs like a second heartbeat, I understood something with perfect clarity, the way you understand things underwater: the commission on the mainland could hang a man with testimony. The sea required no paperwork at all.

The launch stood on its side. The crates went for the rail. Brandt went for the crates.

And I held on — to the rail, to the oilcloth, to the name Astrid Almeida, which for the first time in five years belonged to no company's ledger but my own — as the storm leaned over us both and began, with great patience, to count heads.
