## 37. Overboard

The storm found us in the middle of the channel, which is where storms always find you if you have made the mistake of being somewhere you should not be.

I had been below for six hours, folded into the space behind the spare water casks where Brandt's crew stored rope that had gone rotten and no one had bothered to replace. The master copy rode against my ribs, sewn into the lining of my jacket the way I had once sewn names into a collar, and every time the launch pitched I pressed my arm against them as though they were a child that might be thrown from me. Forty-one pages. My father's name among them, written twice — once by Brandt's clerk in his careful fraudulent hand, and once by mine, small enough to hide under a thumbnail, true enough to hang him.

Above me, men shouted about the weather. Below me, I did arithmetic. It was what I was for. The channel was nine miles at its narrowest. A launch doing eight knots would cross in a little over an hour, and we had been underway perhaps forty minutes when the first wave came over the rail and someone said the word *turning back*, and someone else said the word *no*, and I understood that Brandt would rather drown than arrive late to his own escape.

I had counted on him being greedy. I had not counted on him being proud of it.

* * *

Let me say plainly how I came to be in a rope locker on a murderer's boat, because there is a version of this story where I sound brave, and I would rather the record show that I sounded wet.

Rosa's diversion at the store had bought us the pages back, all but one, and the one we lost still sits in my chest like a pearl with grit around it. After that night, everything moved quickly, the way water moves when the dam has already cracked and everyone is simply choosing where to stand. Brandt began packing his own books — I saw the crates through his office window, saw the way he handled them, tenderly, like infants. He meant to sink them mid-channel. Evidence, witness list, the whole architecture of his double sales, dropped into deep water where no commission could ever read them. And then he would present himself on the mainland as a loyal company man fleeing island chaos, and the war would swallow the rest.

He announced his departure the way he announced everything: as a favor. The launch would run ahead of the worst weather, he told the compound, carrying urgent correspondence to the mainland office. He even smiled at me across the dinner table — because by then I dined at his table, promoted, honored, leashed — and said, "You'll mind the ledgers while I'm away, Miss Almeida. You have such a steady hand."

"I'll mind everything," I told him, and meant more than he knew.

Getting aboard had been Rosa's idea, though she framed it as God's, which is her habit when she wants to avoid responsibility for excellent plans. The crew loaded stores until nearly dark; a girl carrying a crate of limes into the hold attracted exactly as much attention as a girl carrying limes deserves, which is none. I stepped into the rope locker, pulled a tarpaulin over myself, and listened to the island I had never left get smaller behind the sound of the engine.

Tomás knew. Tomás had held my face in both hands on the mangrove path and said, "If you drown, I will be so angry with you," which is the Okonkwo-Almeida way of saying *I love you, come back*. Elias was already north, or east, or wherever ragged men with testimony go when the whole coast is hunting them; I had to believe the sea was kinder to him than it had been to me so far.

So: not brave. Determined, damp, and extremely motivated. In my family these are considered the same thing.

* * *

The storm decided things before I could.

It came down in sheets, the kind of rain that makes the world feel like the inside of a drum, and the launch rolled so hard that the crates in the hold broke their lashings and one of them — I heard it happen, that particular splintering crunch — spilled its contents across the deck above my head. Books. Ledgers. Brandt's whole life in bound volumes, skidding on wet planks.

Then the hatch opened and light fell in, and Brandt himself stood silhouetted against the grey, screaming at his crew about his books, and I understood that my hiding place had approximately four minutes left in it.

There is a moment in every diver's life — my father taught me this, or rather the water did, and my father confirmed it — when you stop deciding and start doing. The breath before the descent. You do not think about the cold. You go.

I went up through the hatch into the rain.

* * *

The deck was chaos in the specific flavor that only money can produce. Two crewmen wrestled a tarp over the scattered ledgers while a third shouted that they should let the damned things sink, and Brandt stood amid it all in his good coat, snatching volumes out of the spray like a man saving children from a fire. He had tied a canvas satchel across his chest — the important ones, the forged lists' twin, the account book with two columns of buyers. His whole empire, worn like a bandolier.

And then he saw me.

I wish I could report that his face showed guilt. It showed arithmetic instead — the same look he gave a column that didn't add — and I watched him arrive at the sum: the missing ledger, the searches that had found nothing, the quiet girl who minded his books. His mouth opened to shout for the crew, and the launch chose that instant to roll, hard, starboard, and the deck became a slope and the slope became a slide, and three hundred pounds of company records went over the side in a single groaning avalanche of paper and greed.

Brandt lunged after them.

That was the thing I could not have predicted and yet, afterward, could not believe I hadn't: he lunged after *them*. Not the rail. Not the rope. The books. His hands closed on a ledger — I saw it clearly, I see it still, the gilt lettering blurred by rain — and the weight of it, and the weight of the satchel, and the angle of the deck took him the last foot together, and he went over the rail with his treasure hugged to his chest like a drowning man hugging a stone.

The sea took him the way the sea takes everything: without comment.

For one heartbeat the deck was silent except for the storm. Then one of the crew screamed his name and another threw a coil of line that unspooled short, and I went to the rail because some part of me — the part my mother raised, the part that hides rice under roots for people who are drowning — needed to look.

He was there in the foam, just under the surface, visible the way pearls are visible, a pale shape in the green dark. And here is what I want the commission to understand, what I would testify to under any oath they care to administer: I did not push him. I did not need to. The sea had done its own auditing, and the sea does not accept bribes.

But I will also confess this, because a woman who keeps two sets of books is no better than Brandt, and I have spent my life keeping only one: when his hand came up out of the water, reaching — not for the line, not for the rail, but back down toward the ledger sinking below him, fingers grasping at green water for his accounts — I did not throw him anything.

I stood at the rail with a rope in my hands and I watched him choose, one last time, between his books and his life, and he chose his books, and I let the choice stand.

The rope stayed coiled. The sea closed. That is the whole of it, and it is more than enough.

* * *

The crew turned the launch back within the hour. There was no argument about it; a captainless boat full of frightened men in a rising storm does not debate. They assumed I had come aboard at the last port stop — I let them assume it, wearing my best bewildered-orphan face, which has gotten me out of more trouble than any knife — and by the time we wallowed back into the strait the story had already settled into the shape stories take on that island: the overseer lost to the storm, clutching his papers, poor man, the company's loss so great, etcetera.

They put me ashore at the company dock in the rain. I walked up the hill through water running in rivers along the ruts, and at the gate Rosa was waiting with an umbrella she was holding entirely too high to do any good, because its real purpose was to hide her face while she counted mine for damage.

"You're soaked," she said.

"It's raining," I said.

"You're soaked *differently*," she said, which is the closest my mother has ever come to admitting she had spent the day imagining my corpse, and she pulled me inside and put rice water in me and sat me by the fire, and only then did I open my jacket and lift out the oilcloth packet and lay it on the table between us.

Forty-one pages, dry. Every name. Every double sale. My father's name, written twice, safe.

Rosa looked at them a long time. Then she reached across the table and touched the packet with one finger, the way you touch a sleeping child to confirm the breathing, and she said, "Your father kept saying the water gives back what it takes, if you're patient." She wiped her eyes with her wrist, briskly, as though annoyed at them. "He was wrong about most things, but he was right about that."

"The water didn't give him back," I said.

"No," she said. "It gave back something better. It gave back the truth about who took him." She stood, and tucked the packet into the bread tin where she had hidden rice for twenty years, because in our family the safest place in the world has always been inside something ordinary. "Now eat. Tomorrow we find out whether Elias made the mainland, and whether the commission is real, and whether any of this was worth the price."

"And if it wasn't?"

Rosa paused at the hearth, firelight on her face, and gave me the smile she usually saved for overseers' wives — the one with all the steel showing.

"Then tomorrow we make it worth the price," she said. "We still have the pages, Astrid. And now, thanks to the sea, we also have the only man who knew every buyer's name — and he can't contradict a single word."

Outside, the storm was turning. Inside, the bread tin sat on the table like a small tin ark, and somewhere north of us, if the water was kind, a ragged man was walking toward a room where history gets written down — and for once, for the first time in the whole drowned history of our island, the person holding the pen might be on our side.
