## 38. Testimony

The commission met in a customs house on the mainland, in a room with windows so tall they seemed embarrassed by their own light. I had never seen glass like it — not porthole glass, not the thick green panes of the company office — but tall, honest window-glass that let the whole gray harbor in and made no apology for anything.

A clerk with ink-stained cuffs took my name at the door.

"Astrid Almeida," I said.

He wrote it down without asking who held my debt, and something in my chest turned over. Sixteen years of being *the Almeida girl, Verrone property, ledger hand, indentured since her father's drowning* — and here was a stranger writing four words on a form as if that were the whole of me.

"State your business," he said.

I put the bread tin on his desk.

He looked at it the way men look at things that might be lunch. "That's your business?"

"That's the evidence," I said. "The tin is just how it traveled."

* * *

They gave me a chair in an anteroom and left me alone with it for three hours, which I suspect was deliberate. Governments, I have learned, test you with furniture. If you can sit still in an unfamiliar chair while important people decide whether you are a witness or a nuisance, you pass.

I passed. I had spent years sitting still while Brandt decided worse things.

I used the time the way I used every hour of my life: counting. The anteroom had forty-one floorboards, two portraits of men whose names I didn't know and whose collars were starched into permanent disapproval, one clock running four minutes fast, and a smell of paper, tobacco, and rain-wet wool that I decided was the smell of the rest of my life.

At some point a woman brought me tea in a cup with no handle. I drank it anyway. On the island we drank from whatever the sea hadn't taken, and a handleless cup seemed like a very small complaint to bring to a continent.

"You're the one from the pearl island," she said, not really asking. News travels faster than boats when the news is scandalous enough.

"I'm one of them," I said. "There are four hundred of us. Well." I corrected myself, because accuracy was the entire point of why I was there. "There were four hundred and six. Eleven sold north under false lists. One drowned finding out about it."

She looked at me for a moment longer than politeness required. Then she said, "They'll try to make you feel small in there. Don't let them."

"They can't," I told her. "I've been memorized by better men than these."

It came out sharper than I intended, and she smiled — quick, conspiratorial, gone again — and left me alone with the tea and the fast clock and the strange new feeling that I might, possibly, be funny under pressure. Tomás would have been proud. He'd always said grief was just humor that hadn't found its timing yet.

* * *

The hearing room was smaller than the windows promised. Seven commissioners sat behind a long table like a jury of uncles who'd heard everything already. A stenographer sat at a machine with keys, her fingers hovering. And in the corner, wrapped in a borrowed coat two sizes too big, sat Elias.

Ragged was too kind a word for him. Ragged implies edges. Elias looked like something the sea had chewed and reconsidered. His wound had healed crooked; his beard had opinions; and when he saw me walk in, he stood up so fast the coat slid off one shoulder, and for a moment neither of us could speak.

"You look terrible," I said, because if I said anything true I would have cried, and I had not crossed a strait in a storm to cry in front of seven uncles.

"You should see the other channel," he said.

The senior commissioner rapped the table. "Order. Madam — Almeida? Is that correct?"

"It's correct," I said. "Though I'm told that depends on which ledger you read."

Someone laughed. It might even have been a commissioner.

* * *

Here is what I learned about testimony: it is nothing like telling the truth. Telling the truth is what you do to your mother over rice. Testimony is construction work. You lay the truth down plank by plank, in order, load-bearing, and every time someone asks *are you certain*, you do not flinch, because the whole structure is standing on your certainty and everyone in the room knows it, including you.

I laid the planks.

The duplicate ledger — Brandt's private book, kept in his quarters, photographed into my memory page by page before Rosa and I cut the original into forty-one pieces and sewed them into the lives of forty-one families. The bread tin held my master copy, written on rice paper in letters so small the commissioner had to hold it to the lamp like an old man reading scripture.

"The conscription lists," the third commissioner said, reading. "Each diver's name entered twice. Once to the naval levy, once to a labor broker named Corvel. Payment received for both entries. The man serves the war and serves the broker simultaneously, and the company collects twice."

"Yes," I said.

"And the families remained indentured regardless."

"The debt stayed on the books either way. If the man went north, the family owed his passage. If he stayed — hidden, or dead — the family owed his passage. There was no column in which anyone stopped owing."

The stenographer's keys clattered. I watched her fingers and thought of my own hands on the company ledgers, years of them, copying columns that didn't add up, saying nothing. Every number I'd ever copied silently was in this room now, standing up straight and saying its piece.

One commissioner, a thin man with a voice like dry paper, leaned forward. "You were sixteen when you discovered the forgery."

"Fifteen," I said, "when I noticed. Sixteen when I understood what noticing costs."

"And you said nothing at first."

"For eleven months." I let the silence sit, because honesty was load-bearing too. "I was afraid. Fear is reasonable. But I want the record to show that the fear was not mine alone — it was installed. That's what the company sold, along with the pearls and the men. It sold the idea that speaking is more expensive than silence. For most people on that island, the arithmetic still works out that way. I'm here to change the exchange rate."

The dry-voiced man studied me for a long moment. "How old are you now?"

"Seventeen."

"Seventeen," he repeated, as if checking the figure against a column of his own.

* * *

They called Elias after the midday recess, and the difference between us was instructive. I had built testimony. Elias simply set his down on the table like a stone and let everyone look at it.

He told them about deserting — plainly, without decoration. "I ran from a ship that was being run like the company runs your islands," he said. "I don't dress it up. But I can tell you where Brandt banked the broker's money, because I carried the correspondence. I can tell you Corvel's face, his ship, the name he sails under this season. I can tell you that the eleventh name on that list — the drowned man — was entered twice by Brandt himself, in his own hand, and I have seen the hand."

"How do we know your hand from his?" asked the dry commissioner.

Elias took the pen from the table, wrote one line, and passed it down. "Compare," he said. "And then compare both against the ledger pages the young lady has been carrying against her ribs since before the monsoon closed the strait. Ink remembers. That's the whole tragedy and the whole hope of ledgers. Ink remembers what people wish it wouldn't."

I watched the commissioners pass his sample down the line, each holding it to the light. And I thought about my father — Joaquim Almeida, drowned name, the first entry I ever memorized — and I thought: *Pai. The ink remembered.*

* * *

It took them until evening to say the thing out loud, and when they finally did, it was almost disappointingly procedural.

"The commission finds the conscription lists of the Verrone Trading Company, seasons 1915 through 1917, to be forged instruments," the senior commissioner read, in the tone of a man reading a shipping manifest. "All levies arising from said lists are void. All debts contracted to secure compliance with said levies are void. The company's claims upon persons named therein are void, including—" and here he paused, and I felt every eye in the room arrive at me at once, "—including any claim upon the person of Astrid Almeida."

Void.

I had rehearsed this moment on the coracle, in the storm, in the anteroom with the fast clock. I had imagined shouting, weeping, falling to my knees. What actually happened was smaller and much larger: I breathed.

Not a gasp. Just a breath — deep, unhurried, all the way down to the bottom of me — the kind of breath I had forgotten bodies were allowed. Sixteen years of shallow water breathing. Sixteen years of taking in only what wouldn't be taxed. And now air, free air, whole lungfuls of it, nobody's air but mine.

Exhaling after years underwater. That was exactly it. My mother would have understood; divers know the feeling best. You come up and the first breath hurts and then it doesn't and then you wonder why you ever agreed to live any other way.

"Madam Almeida?" The senior commissioner was watching me with something almost like kindness. "Do you understand what has been read?"

"Yes," I said. "I'm free." I tested the words in my mouth like new coins. "I mean — I understand the claim is void. Whether I'm free is apparently a matter the bridge gets to finish deciding."

The commissioners didn't laugh. But the stenographer did — one bright syllable off her keys — and I counted it as official record.

* * *

Afterward, in the corridor, Elias caught up with me walking like a man whose legs had recently been reintroduced to him.

"So," he said. "What does a freed indentured clerk do with her first evening on the mainland?"

"I intend to eat something that isn't dried fish, sleep in a bed that doesn't rock, and write a letter to my mother that begins with the word *done*."

"Good plan." He hesitated, and I saw the guilt move across his face the way weather moves across the harbor — familiar, passing. "Your brother—"

"Is in the interior with forty-one families between him and a warrant that just died in that room," I said. "By now someone will have reached the island with word, or they won't, and either way Tomás is the luckiest wanted man alive, because the crime they wanted him for has been ruled a fiction. He'll be insufferable about it. He'll want a parade."

"He sounds worth a parade."

"He is," I said. "Don't tell him I said so."

We stood at the tall windows while the lamplighters worked the quay below, flames blooming one by one along the water like slow pearls.

"Elias. When you testified — the part about the eleventh name. That was my father."

He was quiet a moment. "I know," he said. "I read the list on Brandt's boat, years ago, before I understood what I was reading. Joaquim Almeida. Entered twice." He looked at me. "For what it's worth — I think he knew what the lists were. I think that's why the boat went down."

"I know that too," I said. "My mother told me in an orchard, over rice roots. We're a family that learns things in inconvenient places."

* * *

The letter to my mother took me four drafts, which was three more than any ledger page had ever taken me.

The first draft was all facts — void, void, commission, signed, sealed. The second draft cried through the middle of it, which ruined the ink. The third draft was jokes, which wasn't fair to her. The fourth began:

*Mãe — Done. All of it. The lists are void, the debts are void, the company's claim on us is void — yours, mine, Tomás's, the Cordeiros', old Marta's, everyone's. Eleven names come home from the north if they're alive to come. Pai's name comes home too, clean, off every book Brandt ever cooked.*

*I keep reaching for the ledger habit — counting what we're owed — and there's nothing left to count. Do you understand what that feels like? It feels like setting down a load so heavy I'd forgotten I was carrying it. My shoulders keep waiting for it to come back.*

*Tell the orchard. Tell the drying sheds. Tell the families who sewed pages into their hems and slept on them like stones. Tell them it worked — that the smallest handwriting on the island beat the biggest thief in the trade.*

*And tell Tomás, when he crawls out of the mangroves looking heroic, that he is NOT to take credit.*

*I'm coming home when the bridge opens. Not as property. As a customer, if the ferry wants my fare. Imagine.*

*Your daughter,
Astrid — just Astrid, no company after it, ever again*

* * *

I sent the letter and walked back to the harbor, because I couldn't help myself — because the harbor was water and water was arithmetic, and I wanted to stand in front of the sea as a free woman and see if it looked different.

It did, a little. Less like a wall, more like a road.

The tide boards on the customs house wall listed the crossings, and I read them the way I once read ledgers — hungrily, line by line. Storm damage to the northern causeway. Repairs beginning. And at the bottom, in fresh paint:

*Pearl Island crossing expected to resume with the dry season. Bridge opens on schedule, weather permitting.*

Weather permitting. Two little words that had governed my whole life, closing on me at fifteen, sealing the strait, trapping the truth in my collar. Now they were just two little words again, ordinary as a fare price.

The dry season was coming. The bridge would open — once, twice a year, faithful as it had always been — and this time I would be the one crossing it, carrying nothing but my own name.

Behind me, up the hill, the customs house lamps were going out one by one. Ahead of me, the water was turning silver with early moonlight, and somewhere past the horizon, on the far side of the strait, a tollhouse stood empty, an orchard waited under its rice roots, and a brother I had not seen since the monsoon swallowed him was — if luck and the commission's paperwork traveled well — already grinning at the far end of a bridge that was about to open.

I checked the tide board one last time, memorized the date, and started making plans.

First, though, I meant to find that dinner that wasn't dried fish. A freed woman has priorities, and I intended to honor every single one of them.
