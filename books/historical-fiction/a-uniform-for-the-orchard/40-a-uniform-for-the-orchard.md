## 40. A Uniform for the Orchard

The papers burned the way I had always imagined they would: quickly, and with less ceremony than they deserved.

I had expected them to fight me. Sixteen years of my family's lives pressed into a single sheet of company stock, stamped twice in Brandt's violet ink — I thought a thing that heavy should resist the flame, should hiss or spit or at least take some convincing. Instead it caught at one corner, curled like a prawn on a hot pan, and was gone before I had finished holding my breath.

"That's it?" Tomás said beside me. "All that paper, all that ink, and it burns like a fisherman's letter?"

"Everything important burns easily," Rosa said from the doorway of the orchard hut. She had a basin of seedlings balanced on her hip like a third child. "That's how you know it's true. Lies need to be carved in stone. The truth just needs a match."

We were standing behind the drying sheds, in the strip of orchard where Mother had kept her secret rice cache under the roots of the old mango for longer than either of us had known about it. The commission men had come through in the second week of dry season, taken their statements, stamped their stamps, and gone back across the water to wherever commissions go when they are finished pretending they never needed a sixteen-year-old girl to do their arithmetic. The company's claim on this island had been dissolved the way salt dissolves — slowly, then all at once, then invisible.

And here we were, still standing on the same dirt.

"You could plant something else," Tomás offered, kicking at a stone. "A coconut palm. Something useful."

"It's going to be a cashew," Rosa said, in the tone she used for ending discussions. "Cashews hold the bank of a slope better than anything else on this island, and the slope needs holding. Besides—" and here her voice did something complicated, the way it always did when she talked about Papa without saying his name— "your father liked cashews. He used to steal them off the trees by the mission road and blame the birds. The birds let him. They knew a fellow thief when they saw one."

Tomás laughed, and I laughed, and for a moment none of us mentioned that the hole we were standing over had once fed three families through two bad monsoons, or that the last time I had dug in this spot I had been hiding evidence of forgery rather than planting a tree. Some ground holds too many things to name them all out loud. You plant over it instead. That is what ground is *for*.

* * *

I want to be honest about what happened afterward, because honesty is the whole point now, and because if I have learned anything in these years it is that the tidy version of a story is nearly always the coward's version.

The tidy version is: the lists surfaced, the forgeries were voided, every indentured family walked free, and we lived happily in an orchard.

What actually happened took eleven months and cost more than any of us wanted to count. The families who had carried ledger pages — the Ferreiras, old Mateo and his daughters, the widow Sancha with her bad hip and her excellent memory — those people held pieces of the truth against their ribs for the better part of a year, sewn into hems and wrapped in oilcloth and buried in places only they knew, waiting for word that never seemed to come. Two of them died before the commission ever ruled. Not dramatically. Just the ordinary deaths that come for everyone eventually, except theirs came while still technically owned, which is a particular kind of theft I will not forgive as long as I have hands to write with.

And the page we lost in the raid — the one bearing Papa's name — I grieved for that page like a limb. It felt like the sea taking him twice: once off the pearl beds, once out of the record. It was Elias who found the answer, months later, in the least likely place. The navy had kept its own copy of the conscription rolls — of course it had; the navy keeps copies of everything, including, I suspect, its own mistakes — and there was Papa's name in their hand, purchased from Brandt's, dated the month before he drowned. The forged list existed in duplicate after all. The water hadn't kept him. The water had *filed* him.

When I read that line — *Almeida, J., diver, conscripted per schedule B* — I sat down on the commission office floor, right there among the clerks, and cried until a very embarrassed lieutenant brought me tea. It was not grief exactly. It was closer to what you feel when a tooth that has ached for years finally comes out and your tongue keeps returning to the empty place, astonished by the room.

So no, not tidy. But true, which I am learning is the better bargain.

* * *

The knife stays.

People keep asking me — well, two people have asked me, and one of them is Tomás — why I still carry Father's diving knife when the pearl beds are closed, when the company boats are sold, when there is nothing left on this island that requires cutting loose.

Here is why. On the night I ferried Elias through the mangroves with soldiers sweeping the shore, that knife cut the coracle free of a root snag in water up to my chin, and if it hadn't, there would have been no testimony, no voided lists, and I would currently be writing this chapter inside someone else's property. A knife is a small promise you make to yourself: *whatever happens next, I will not be helpless.* I intend to keep making it.

Besides, Papa would want it working. He was not sentimental about tools. He sharpened things because dull blades made bad cuts, and bad cuts made bad days. I keep the edge the way he taught me — whetstone, oil, patience — and every time I do it, my hands remember being small enough to sit between his knees and watch. That is worth more than the steel.

The ledgers, though. The ledgers I gave up.

This surprised everyone, most of all me. When the commission asked whether I wished to retain the master copy — as evidence, as memorial, as whatever a person retains a thing like that for — I said no. They looked at me the way Brandt used to look at columns that wouldn't add. Didn't I want proof? Didn't I want to hold it?

I had held it long enough. I had slept with it against my ribs through a sealed season and a storm crossing and the worst night of my life on open water. A person can carry the truth so long and so close that it starts to feel less like truth and more like luggage. And the moment the commission stamped its ruling — the moment the words *void, fraudulent, and of no legal effect* went onto the same kind of paper my indenture had been written on — the ledgers stopped being mine. They belonged to history now, and history has clerks of its own, better paid than I was.

What I kept instead was the knowing. You cannot confiscate that. Believe me; they tried for years.

* * *

Rosa planted the cashew on a Tuesday, which is the day she does everything, because Tuesdays are for beginnings and she decided this sometime before I was born and has never once explained herself.

She dug the cache open herself, refusing help, though she let Tomás stand nearby and narrate complaints, which I think she secretly counts as a form of assistance. The rice came out in its oiled cloth, gray with age and damp, good for nothing now but the compost — and Rosa held it up to the light with something like tenderness, this fossil of her long vigilance, this decade of feeding other people's children in secret.

"You could have told me," I said. I have said it before. I will probably say it again, the way you touch a healed bone to check it's really mended.

"I could have," she agreed, tucking the rice around the sapling's roots like a blanket. "And you'd have carried the worry in your face, and Brandt read faces the way you read numbers. No. Love keeps quiet sometimes. That's not a flaw in love. It's a feature." She patted the soil down. "It also plants trees, apparently."

The tree is knee-high now and stubborn, which is the correct temperament for anything growing out of our family's history. Tomás waters it when he remembers, which is rarely, and I water it when he forgets, which is often, and Rosa waters it when neither of us does, which is to say the tree survives entirely on maternal supervision, like everything else on this island.

* * *

Which brings me, I suppose, to the uniform.

You'll wonder about the title — I wondered about it myself for a long time, back when titles were things that happened to other books. Here is what I finally understood, standing in the orchard last month with dirt under my nails and my own name in my own mouth:

Every indentured family on this island wore one. Not cloth — nobody could afford cloth — but a uniform all the same, stitched out of debt and silence and the particular way you learn to lower your eyes when a company man walks past. I wore mine so long I forgot it wasn't skin. Tomás wore his as jokes, which is a kind of tailoring. Rosa wore hers as a garden, growing beauty on top of the chains so no one would look underneath.

And when the claim dissolved, we didn't get new clothes. There was no ceremony, no document handed over with ribbons. We just woke up one morning and the uniform was gone and we were standing in our own skins, blinking in the light like divers coming up too fast.

It takes practice, walking around in yourself. I still catch my shoulders doing the indentured stoop sometimes, the little apology the body learns. Then I straighten, and nobody strikes me for it, and I marvel all over again at the extravagant safety of standing upright.

* * *

In the evenings now, I write.

Not ledgers. I am done with columns; the sight of a ruled margin makes my eye twitch in a manner Tomás finds hilarious. What I write is smaller and stranger: what the water keeps.

It started as notes. The names of the drowned, beginning with Papa, because a list of the lost is the only ledger I'll ever keep again and it balances to zero owed. Then the stories attached themselves the way barnacles do — Papa's habit of singing off-key to calm the pearl oysters (they were not calmed; the oysters simply tolerated him, as we all did), the year of the red tide, the way the bridge sounds when the first dry-season carts cross it, a sound like the island clearing its throat before speaking.

Elias visited in the last month of rains, thin as a heron and grinning like Tomás, full of mainland news I mostly didn't need since I'd already written half of it down. Before he left, he read a few of my pages by lamplight, very quietly, for a long time.

"You're going to make a book of this," he said. Not a question.

"No," I said. "Books are for people whose stories are finished. This one's still happening. Every morning Rosa argues with a tree, and Tomás is courting the ferryman's daughter with all the grace of a capsizing dinghy, and the cashew will fruit in three years, and I intend to be here eating it. The book can wait for someone who isn't busy living."

He laughed and called me stubborn, which from a man who swam ashore to desert an army rather than sell his name twice is rich beyond counting.

But tonight, after the lamp was trimmed and the house went quiet, I sat with the pages again and found I had written something at the bottom of the last sheet without quite meaning to. My handwriting, my ink, and underneath the list of names, four words:

*Kept. All of it. Kept.*

The water keeps what it keeps. But so do I — differently, and on purpose, and in daylight.

Tomorrow is Tuesday. Rosa says the young tree needs a stake against the storms. I've already cut one, and sharpened the point of it with Papa's knife, and set it by the door.

Some things you burn. Some things you bury. And some things — the true ones, the ones that hold — you drive deep into the ground and tie the living to, so that whatever wind comes next finds them already braced, and standing, and home.
