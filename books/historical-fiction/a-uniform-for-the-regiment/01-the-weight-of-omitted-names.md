## 1. The Weight of Omitted Names

The winter of 1847 came down the river valley like a debt collector, and Marrow's Ford paid in the only coin it had left—men.

Xanthe Ashgrove knew the sound of that payment by heart. It was the cough first, wet and rattling, echoing off the stone walls of the east ward before dawn, when the frost had crept so deep into the mortar that breath hung visible even indoors. Then it was the smell: camphor and carbolic fighting a losing war against the sweeter stench of gangrene, the tallow candles, the boiled linen. Twenty years in this hospital had taught Xanthe that a ward had its own weather, and the weather that February was death moving slow, like ice forming on still water.

"Water," whispered the boy in the third cot from the window.

Xanthe went to him with the tin cup, tilting it carefully against lips gone grey as old pewter. Private Thomas Renwick, Seventh Foot, seventeen years old if he was a day, though his enlistment papers said nineteen because papers said whatever officers wished them to say. Fever had burned through him for six days now, following the amputation of his left leg below the knee—a wound taken not in battle but in a fall from the quarry road, drunk on army rum, sent up that road at midnight by a sergeant who wanted no questions asked about missing stores.

"Am I dying?" Renwick asked. They always asked, eventually, and Xanthe had long ago stopped lying to them. Lies were the officers' currency; Xanthe dealt only in truth.

"You are not dying tonight," Xanthe said, which was true, and let the boy hold onto it like a rope.

Behind him, the door to the surgeons' office stood half open, and through the gap Xanthe could see Lieutenant Harrow bent over the morning report, his pen scratching with the small, self-satisfied rhythm of a man committing paperwork rather than medicine. Harrow had arrived in autumn, pink-cheeked and ambitious, and he wrote his reports the way he did everything else—for the comfort of his superiors.

Xanthe finished with Renwick, checked the other eleven men in the ward—the ones who would live, the ones who would not—and then, when the corridor was empty and the wind was screaming against the shutters, slipped into the linen room.

The ledger lived behind the third shelf, in a gap between the wall and a loose board that twenty years of careful hands had widened just enough. Xanthe drew it out: a plain book, leather boards cracked and darkened, its spine stitched twice where age had threatened to part it. Not an official document. Nothing about it official except what it held.

By candlelight, Xanthe opened to the current page and read back over the winter's entries, names in a hand that had grown steadier over two decades, each one dated, each one annotated in a shorthand only Xanthe could read:

*J. Calloway, private, shot at the mill gate, 14 November. Reported: deserted.*
*M. Oduya, drummer, died of fever in the guardhouse, 2 December. Reported: transferred.*
*S. Whitlock, corporal, hanged himself after the floggings, 19 January. Reported: discharged, whereabouts unknown.*

Three names already this winter. And now there would be more, because Harrow's pen was scratching out a fourth lie even as Xanthe sat in the cold linen room listening to it scratch.

The report would come around by afternoon, as reports always did, and Xanthe would copy it into memory before it was filed away. But the ledger came first. The ledger always came first. It had come first through the retreat from Kessel's Crossing, when the wounded were left on the field and the casualty lists read *missing* instead of *abandoned*. It had come first through the cholera summer, when the dead were stacked like cordwood and counted as *fever, recovered*. It had come first through every quiet scandal and loud defeat, every name an officer had scraped away to keep his own clean.

*They were somebody's sons,* Xanthe's mother had said once, years ago, before she died, before Xanthe had understood that the sentence was a vocation. *Somebody is waiting for them to come home.*

Nobody waited anymore. That was the cruelty of it. The mothers died, the sweethearts married elsewhere, the villages forgot. But a name written down was a kind of waiting. A name written down refused to be nothing.

Xanthe dipped the pen and waited for the ink to warm.

* * *

The report arrived at four o'clock, carried by a corporal with chilblains on his ears. Xanthe took it with the neutral face of twenty years' practice and read it standing in the corridor, where the light from the high windows fell thin and grey across the page.

*Deceased:* none. *Deserted:* three. Privates Aldous Pike, Henry Marsh, and William Ferris, having absconded from their duties on or about the ninth instant, all effects forfeit.

Xanthe had washed all three of those men's bodies with his own hands. Pike had died of the putrid fever on a Tuesday; Marsh had drowned in the millrace, drunk, trying to cross the ice; Ferris had simply stopped, quietly, in the night, after the letter telling him his wife was dead had taken three months to reach him. None of them had deserted anything except, perhaps, hope. Desertion meant forfeited pay, forfeited pensions, shame mailed home to families who would never learn their sons had died in a bed, held by someone's hands, called by name at the end.

Xanthe returned to the linen room. The candle was nearly spent. In its guttering light, three new entries went into the book, the pen moving slowly, each letter given its full weight, as though the ink itself were a burial.

*Aldous Pike. Henry Marsh. William Ferris.* Died in hospital, February 1847. *Reported: deserted.*

Twenty-two years of entries. Four hundred and eleven names. Xanthe closed the book, slid it behind the third shelf, and pressed the board home, and stood for a moment in the dark with one palm flat against the wall, the way one might rest a hand on the shoulder of an old friend.

That was when Xanthe heard the drums.

* * *

They came up the river road at dusk, sixty mounted men with the snow swirling orange in the light of their torches, and the whole town turned out to watch them pass—not cheering, not jeering, just watching, the way cattle watch a storm come over the hills. At their head rode a colonel whose name Xanthe did not catch and did not need to; the uniform said everything. Requisition. Authority. The Crown's long arm reaching into the last corner of the frontier where it had never bothered to look before.

The proclamation was nailed to the chapel door and read aloud in the market square by lamplight, the herald's voice cracking in the cold. By order of the Commander-in-Chief, Eastern District: the garrison town of Marrow's Ford and all lands, stores, and properties within it were hereby requisitioned for military use until further notice. All public buildings were to be surrendered. All civilian movement restricted. And—all records, of every kind, civil and military, were to be inventoried, catalogued, and remanded to district headquarters before the spring thaw.

Every record. Of every kind.

Xanthe stood in the crowd with snow settling on shoulders and hood, and felt something turn over inside, slow and enormous, like the ice on the river beginning to shift. Around, neighbors muttered—farmers angry about grain levies, innkeepers furious about quartering soldiers—but their grievances were the ordinary grievances of ordinary requisitions. Only Xanthe stood in that square carrying a secret that made the proclamation feel like a hand closing around the throat.

Twenty-two years. Four hundred and eleven names. A book hidden behind a loose board in a room the army now owned.

They would inventory the hospital within days. Xanthe knew how inventories worked—clerks with lists, shelves emptied, walls tapped, floorboards pried. A loose board behind a linen shelf was a hiding place for a season, not for an occupation. And if the ledger were found, it would not merely be confiscated. It would be burned, and whoever kept such a book would be charged—what? Sedition? Forgery? It hardly mattered. The charges would be as invented as the reports the ledger contradicted.

Walking back through the blue evening, past the frozen well and the shuttered chandlery, Xanthe felt the fear arrive—not the hot fear of battlefields, which passed, but a cold, patient fear, the kind that settles into the bones and stays. And beneath it, rising like water under ice, something harder.

Resolve.

There was a magistrate across the river, over the border, a man named Corrigan who had once, years ago, asked too many questions about Kessel's Crossing and been politely ruined for it. He was still there. He still had a court, a clerk, and a grudge. If the ledger reached his office—if it became testimony instead of contraband—then four hundred and eleven names might yet be spoken aloud somewhere the army could not burn them.

But the river between here and there was still frozen solid, and the army was already setting torches along the banks, and there would be checkpoints—there were always checkpoints—between Marrow's Ford and anywhere at all.

Xanthe climbed the hospital steps, paused in the doorway, and looked back once at the town under its shroud of snow, at the torchlight moving along the river road, at the ice lying black and patient in its channel, holding its strength against the spring.

Then Xanthe went inside, to the wards, to the dying, to the last quiet hours of the world as it had been—and began, in the mind's careful ledger, to plan.

In the morning, a new officer would arrive to take inventory of the records. Xanthe did not yet know his name.

His name was Vane.
