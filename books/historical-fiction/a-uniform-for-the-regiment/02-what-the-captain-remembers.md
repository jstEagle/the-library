## 2. What the Captain Remembers

The requisition order came on a Tuesday, carried by a lieutenant too young to shave properly and too proud to knock. Xanthe read it twice while the boy shifted his weight in the doorway, breath steaming, boots leaving gray slush on the scrubbed boards.

*All records, registers, correspondence, and accounts of the garrison hospital at Marrow's Ford to be surrendered to the Office of the Provost Marshal for inventory and destruction of redundant matter.*

*Destruction of redundant matter.* The phrase sat in Xanthe's chest like a swallowed coal. Twenty years of careful ink—every name the officers' reports had swallowed, every man filed under *deserted* or *missing* or simply left blank—and now a clerk in a warm office would decide what counted as redundant.

"Inventory begins tomorrow," the lieutenant said. "Captain Vane will conduct it personally."

Xanthe folded the order along its crease. "The hospital's records are in order."

"I'm sure they are." The boy glanced past Xanthe's shoulder, into the ward where forty men coughed in the dark like a congregation of the drowned. "The Captain is thorough. They say he reads everything."

They say. In Marrow's Ford they said many things: that the river ice would hold till April, that the fever had come up from the port, that old Mrs. Calloway had buried her husband's debts with him. Xanthe had spent twenty years learning which things were true.

That night, after the lamps were trimmed and the last dose of laudanum measured out, Xanthe knelt before the loose stone beneath the dispensary shelf and lifted out the ledger. It was heavier now than it had been in autumn. Three names added in January, written small because small writing was harder to accuse of anything:

*Corporal James Ferrow — shot at the east palisade, 14 December. Reported: deserted.*
*Private Thomas Ockham — died of wounds, 2 January. Reported: missing.*
*Private Samuel Ashgrove —* 

No. That was the other ledger, the one kept only in memory. Xanthe closed the book, wrapped it in oilcloth, and pressed it back into the dark. Tomorrow a thorough man would walk these rooms with an inventory list, reading everything.

Let him read. Let him find what the army wanted found, and never know what was hidden under his feet.

But sleep did not come easily, and when it came, it came with the sound of a rifle bolt being drawn back, though no one in the dream had a face.

* * *

Elias Vane arrived at half past seven precisely, in a gray coat brushed clean of snow, carrying a leather folio and a reputation he hadn't asked for. He was not a tall man, but he stood as though height were a matter of discipline rather than bone, and he spoke to the orderlies by name—a habit that unsettled them more than shouting would have.

He began with the admission registers, because that was where inventories began, and worked through the winter's pages with the flat efficiency the War Office prized. Fever admissions. Amputations. Discharges. Deaths, cross-referenced against the burial returns. He noted discrepancies without comment: three men listed as *deserted* whose effects had never been collected, whose kit had been sold at auction within the week, whose names appeared nowhere in any desertion warrant. Officers did not sell the kits of men who might still come walking back through the gate. Elias wrote nothing down about that. Some observations were safer in the head than on paper.

It was near noon, in the third month of the register, that he saw the name.

*Ashgrove, T. Private.*

His hand stopped. The pen hovered, dropping a small black bead of ink onto the blotter, and for a moment the ward sounds—the sawing cough, the wind at the shutters—receded to nothing, and there was only the name in faded brown ink and the date beside it and the entry in the margin in another hand, a surgeon's hand: *executed by regimental order.*

Thomas. His brother Thomas, who had marched north six years ago with a new uniform and a laugh like breaking bread, who had been reported *lost with honor in action against the enemy*, whose empty coffin their mother had wept over, whose name was carved into the memorial stone in the chapel yard among the honorable dead. Lost with honor. Elias had worn his commission partly because of that phrase. Had believed it the way you believe the ground under your feet.

He turned pages backward with hands that did not quite obey. Found the fuller entry. *Found asleep at post. Struck a sergeant who struck him first. Court-martialed within the day. Shot at dawn, 3 October.* No trial record attached. No witness named. A single line, initialed by Major Halloway—Halloway, who still dined with the colonel every Thursday, who had pinned the ribbon on Elias himself two years later.

The ward noise returned all at once, too loud. Elias became aware that someone was watching him.

The nurse—Ashgrove, Xanthe Ashgrove, twenty years in this place, older than the stones of it—stood at the end of the row of beds with a tray of dressings, watching him with eyes the color of wet slate. Not curious. Waiting. As if the page had been left open on purpose.

"The entry," Elias said, and heard his own voice come out level, which cost him something. "The private. Thomas Ashgrove."

"Yes," Xanthe said.

"That isn't—" He stopped. Started again. "The official record states he fell in action."

"The official record states a great many things." Xanthe set down the tray. "You've been reading everything since morning, Captain. Have you found one entry yet that matches the truth?"

* * *

They met again at dusk, in the dispensary, because Xanthe chose it—small, windowless, smelling of camphor and iodine, a room where voices didn't carry. Elias came because he could not *not* come; he understood that about himself by then, the way a man understands a wound has gone septic.

"You knew I would find it." He stood with his back against the shelves. "The register. You let me see it."

"I hid nothing. Hiding is what your army does." Xanthe lit the lamp, and the flame steadied between them. "I keep a record of my own. Of everyone your reports have eaten. Deserters who were shot without trial. Men who died of the lash or the fever or a major's temper and were entered as *missing*. Twenty years of names."

"Show me."

"If I show you, Captain Vane, you become responsible for knowing. There is no putting it back behind your eyes. Do you understand me? Your career, your commission, perhaps your neck—they sit on the far side of that shelf, and once you cross over, they stay there."

Elias looked at the loose stone. Thought of Thomas laughing on the platform at Wexley Junction, uniform too new, promising to write every week. Thought of the phrase carved in chapel-yard stone—*lost with honor*—and of Major Halloway's hand shaking his father's at the funeral, saying *a fine soldier, lost too soon*, and meaning none of it.

"My brother was shot at dawn," Elias said quietly, "and my family knelt at an empty coffin and thanked God for his honor. Show me the rest of them."

Xanthe held his gaze a long moment—measuring him, weighing him, the way one weighs rope before trusting it with a man's life. Then knelt and drew out the ledger.

It passed between them like something consecrated. Elias turned the pages slowly, reading each name, each date, each method of erasure, and felt something in his chest rearranging itself around a new center of gravity. Here were men he had marched beside. Here were courts-martial he had suspected and dismissed. Here, in a cramped hand, was everything the army had spent twenty years teaching him not to see.

"Why show me?" he asked finally. "You don't know me."

"No," Xanthe agreed. "But you'll be inventorying until the ice breaks, and the requisition says all records are to be destroyed as redundant. When they come with the cart, I intend for this book to be across the river." A pause, deliberate as a scalpel. "And you are the only officer in Marrow's Ford who has a reason to want that as badly as I do."

Outside, faint but unmistakable, the river groaned—a long, grinding complaint, the sound of a giant turning in its sleep. Both of them listened.

"There are three checkpoints between here and the ferry," Elias said. "Provost at the mill, provost at the bridge, and the gate guard at the crossing. Every cart searched. Every traveler questioned." He closed the ledger and held it a moment longer than necessary before returning it. "If they catch you with it, you hang. And I hang beside you."

"Then we should both be very careful," Xanthe said, wrapping the oilcloth around twenty years of the dead, "and very quick. The ice won't wait for us, Captain. Neither will Halloway."

Because that was the other thing Elias had learned today, reading the initialed entries: Major Halloway's name appeared more often than any other hand in the book. And Halloway had requested the requisition himself.
