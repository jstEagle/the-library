## 3. Three Checkpoints

The ice spoke first.

All night it had been groaning beneath the floorboards of the hospital, a low animal sound rolling up from the river like something turning over in its sleep, and by dawn it had begun to crack—sharp reports, like muskets fired far off, like the war remembering itself. Xanthe stood at the window with a needle between his teeth, watching the grey water flex beneath its white skin, and thought: *three days, perhaps two.* After the ice broke, no one could cross. After the ice broke, the ledger would be nothing but paper in a requisitioned town, and every name in it would die twice.

He had chosen the apron carefully. The old one—the one with the wine-dark stains at the hem that no scrubbing would lift—because stained things were invisible things. No sergeant searched what he was afraid to touch. He sat by the cold hearth with the ledger open on his knees, its covers worn soft as saddle leather, twenty years of his own small handwriting marching down each page: *Private Aldous Merrin, shot at dawn, 12 March 1831, listed as deserted. Corporal Fenwick, fever, buried behind the north wall without a stone, listed as transferred. Sergeant Ilyan Vane—*

His hand stilled over that name. It still did, every time. Elias's brother, executed for mutiny that had been nothing but hunger and a refusal to march men into a frozen ford. Xanthe closed the book and began to cut the binding.

The thread was waxed linen, strong enough for sutures, and he sewed as he had sewn a thousand wounds: quickly, cleanly, hating the necessity of pain. Page by page the ledger came apart, folded into flat packets the size of playing cards, and slid into channels he stitched into the apron's lining—along the waistband, down the side seams, across the bib where a heart would sit if an apron had one. When it was done he held the garment up to the window light. It looked exactly like what it had always been. That was the whole art of hiding, he thought. Not concealment. Continuity.

A knock at the ward door. Elias Vane, out of uniform, wearing a civilian coat that fit him like an apology.

"They've posted the third checkpoint at the north gate," he said. "Mill, bridge, gate. They're searching everyone leaving town."

"Then I'll leave town."

"Xanthe." He said the name the way he'd begun saying it lately—as though testing whether it would hold his weight. "They know someone kept records. Colonel Hesse has been asking after you by name since Tuesday."

"Then I should hurry." He tied the apron on, felt the weight of twenty years settle against his ribs like a second heartbeat. "The magistrate's office across the river opens at eight. If these pages are in his hands before the ice breaks, no requisition order can reach them. They become testimony. Testimony is a different kind of paper entirely."

"And if they catch you?"

Xanthe looked at him—at the brother's face repeated in his, older, wearier, carrying the same name in different ink—and found he could not answer honestly, so he answered practically. "Then the names stay dead, and I stay quiet, and in thirty years some other nurse starts another ledger because nothing ever changes. I've done my years of quiet, Captain. I'm not doing any more."

Elias nodded slowly. He reached out as if to straighten the apron strap at Xanthe's shoulder—a small domestic gesture, absurd between them, tender as a wound—and then did not quite touch it. His hand dropped.

"The mill first," he said. "Sergeant Dray. He drinks. Go at mid-morning, when the bottle has done its work but before it's finished."

* * *

The mill checkpoint smelled of wet grain and gin.

Sergeant Dray sat inside the tollhouse with his boots up on a barrel, a red face above a red collar, and beside him a private so young he still had the look of being recently unwrapped. A queue of farmers waited in the slanting snow-light, carts creaking, breath steaming, everyone performing patience badly.

"Nurse Ashgrove," Dray said when Xanthe approached, and there was a nasty little curl in how he said it, the way men said the names of things they'd been warned about. "Hospital's on the list too, I hear. Records and all."

"All surrendered," Xanthe agreed pleasantly. "I've just come from delivering them. My feet will attest to the distance." He let exhaustion show in his shoulders, which required no performance at all. Then, softly, leaning on the tollhouse sill where the young private couldn't hear: "You look like a man who hasn't slept, Sergeant. There's a laudanum syrup at the hospital we issue for the cough. Strong stuff. Better than anything you'll buy in this town."

Dray's eyes went sly and slow. "Cost me?"

"A bottle for a look inside a basket." Xanthe lifted the covered hamper on his arm—bandages, lint, a flask of the promised syrup, all real, all innocent. Under the apron, against his stomach, the pages lay warm as a fever.

Dray glanced at the queue, at the private, at the hamper. Greed and laziness performed their old duet. "Go on, then," he muttered, waving the basket through without opening it. "Mind the ice on the path. Wouldn't want the army losing a good nurse."

*Not yet,* Xanthe thought, walking on. *But you're working on it.*

* * *

The bridge checkpoint was worse, because the provost at it was Marta Quill, and Marta Quill had known him since they were children stealing apples off the widow Crane's tree by the millrace.

She stood at the bridgehead in her provost's sash, snow gathering on her shoulders, and when she saw him her face did something complicated—recognition, then duty, then something older than both.

"Xanthe Ashgrove," she said. "Twenty years and they set me to search you."

"Life has a sense of humor."

"Don't joke." Her voice dropped. She glanced at the soldiers flanking the bridge, at the river grinding under its lid of ice. "There's a list at this checkpoint, Xanthe. Your name's near the top. If I wave you through and they find what they think you're carrying—" She stopped. She didn't finish. Twenty years ago she had cried at his mother's funeral; she had the same face now, trying not to.

"Marta." He stepped close, close enough that the words could travel only between them, under the wind. "You remember Tom Weller? Apprentice cooper? Went into the regiment in '29?"

"Everyone remembers Tom."

"He's in the ground behind the north wall with no marker, because he objected to flogging a boy half to death, and the report says he deserted. There are forty more like him. Forty-one." He watched her jaw tighten. "I'm not smuggling stolen goods, Marta. I'm smuggling the truth, and the truth is the only thing in this town the army hasn't already taken."

Marta stared past him at the river for a long moment. Snow landed on her eyelashes and did not melt.

"Sick call," she said loudly, to the soldiers. "This man's wanted at the garrison infirmary—fever case, contagious. Nobody touches him." Then, quietly, only for Xanthe: "Walk fast. And Xanthe—if it goes wrong, don't say my name."

"I never do," he said, and crossed onto the bridge, and did not let himself shake until he was halfway over the groaning ice.

* * *

The north gate was where the world ended.

He saw the trouble before he reached it: a mounted officer at the barrier, and beside the barrier, holding a paper, a clerk with a sharp memory and a sharper pen—the man who had inventoried the hospital records two days past, who had looked at Xanthe twice and written something down. Recognition moved across the clerk's face like weather.

"That's him," the clerk said. "That's the hospital man. Stop him."

Two soldiers stepped into the road. The officer dismounted, unhurried, a man who enjoyed his work. "Nurse Ashgrove. Colonel Hesse would like a word about certain documents believed missing from the garrison inventory. You'll surrender everything you're carrying, and then you'll come along quietly, or—" he smiled thinly "—not quietly. Whichever amuses you."

Xanthe stood very still. Under the apron, the pages pressed against his body like a second skeleton. He calculated distances, weights, the odds, and found them all insolvent.

Then the guard hut caught fire.

It happened fast—a shout, a bloom of orange through the hut's small window, flames climbing the oiled canvas roof with a hungry roar, and Captain Elias Vane stumbling back from the doorway holding a burning tin box, holding papers that even at a distance, even in firelight, Xanthe recognized: cream-colored, ribboned, stamped with the crest of the commission. Elias hurled the box into the snow, staggered, shouted, *"Fire! The orders are burning—all of it's burning!"*, and the checkpoint dissolved into chaos—soldiers running with buckets, the officer bellowing about the dispatches, the clerk shrieking about inventory, smoke boiling black against the morning.

Elias's eyes found Xanthe's through the smoke. One glance toward the open gate. Everything he could not say, said.

*Go.*

Xanthe went—through the gate, past the burning hut, onto the river road where the ice boomed and split beneath the pale sky like a door being broken open from the other side. Behind him he heard Elias shouting orders in a voice made of iron and ruin, heard the officer turn back toward the fire, heard nothing else but his own heart and the river, waking.

At the far bank, the magistrate's windows showed lamplight. Xanthe put his hand flat against the apron, against the names sleeping in their stitches, and ran.
