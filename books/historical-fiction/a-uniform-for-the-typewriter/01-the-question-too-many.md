## 1. The Question Too Many

The census taker arrived on a Tuesday, when the light through the workshop's salt-fogged windows was the color of weak tea. Theo Blackwood heard him before he saw him — the deliberate, unhurried tread of a man who had never once been chased out of a room — and felt his stomach tighten around nothing, the way it did before storms.

He sat at the third typewriter from the window, the one with the sticky *R* that required a second strike, typing death.

That was the honest way to put it, though no one in the Meridian Trading Company would ever have permitted such language. What he typed were "settlement abstracts," Crane called them. Summaries of accounts closed. Divers' names reduced to figures: wages withheld against equipment costs, against air-line rental, against the price of the coffin itself if the company had been generous enough to provide one. Elias Mundy, drowned off the Kestrel Bank, balance owing to the estate: four shillings, sixpence. Joseph Ferren, crushed by a falling shell crate, account settled in full. Settled. As though a man's life were a debt that could be squared by arithmetic, as though the sea were merely a creditor with poor manners.

Theo's fingers moved over the keys with the fluency of long practice. He had learned the machine the way other boys learned to swim — early, out of necessity, half-drowned by it. His father had signed him over to Meridian at fourteen, an indenture written on paper so thin Theo could read the company seal through it like a watermark on his own future. Six years now. He knew the ledgers better than he knew his own hands.

The door opened and the census taker came in with the smell of the street on him — pipe smoke, dust, the particular staleness of government offices everywhere. He was a small man, neat, with spectacles that caught the lamplight and turned his eyes into two blank coins. He carried a leather satchel and a clipboard of printed forms, and he looked around the workshop the way a customs officer looks at cargo: cataloguing, weighing, already deciding what would be taxed or seized.

"Mr. Crane?" he asked.

"In the back office." Theo did not stop typing. Stopping would mean looking up, and looking up would mean being seen, and being seen was the one thing he had spent six years learning not to do. "I'll fetch him."

"No need." The man smiled with professional mildness. "You'll do. You work here?"

"I'm a clerk."

"A clerk." He wrote something down. "Name?"

"Blackwood. Theo Blackwood."

"And your family? Parents living?"

The question landed like a fishhook set too deep. Theo struck the *R* twice and watched the letter take its place in a dead man's account. "My mother died when I was young," he said. "My father was a diver."

"Ah." The census taker's pen paused, and Theo understood, with the cold clarity of a man watching a wave rise beyond his ability to swim, that this was why the man had come. Not for the clerks. For the widows. The orphans. The families of the men whose names passed under Theo's fingers every day and came out the other side as balances owing. "A diver. With Meridian?"

"With Meridian."

"And he is—"

"Gone." Theo let the word sit there, plain and unarguable. Gone was true. Gone covered everything. Gone did not invite follow-up questions about dates, circumstances, settlements, sums paid to whom.

But the census taker was a patient man; patience was his trade, as invisibility was Theo's. "Gone how, if you don't mind my asking? We're compiling mortality tables for the coastal districts. Occupational hazards. The diving trade particularly interests the Department."

*The diving trade particularly interests the Department.* Theo felt the words go through him like cold water through wet cloth. Somewhere in some office, someone had begun counting. Counting was dangerous. Counting was the first thing that happened before anything else happened — inquiries, commissions, newspapers, ruin.

"He drowned," Theo said. "Six years ago. Before I was indentured."

"And the company compensated the family?"

"There was no family to compensate."

It was a lie so smooth it frightened him, because it was almost true. There had been no family but himself, and himself the company had taken as payment — fourteen years old, sign here, thumb here, the indenture papers thin enough to read the seal through. His father's death had been settled the way all their deaths were settled: quietly, arithmetically, with the boy thrown in as a rounding figure.

The census taker wrote for a while. The only other sounds were the typewriters — three of them going now, the other clerks having drifted in with the morning — and the sea, which never stopped, which had never once stopped, hushing against the pilings below the floorboards like something enormous breathing in its sleep.

"You type the settlement records yourself?" the census taker asked suddenly.

Theo's hands went still above the keys. One beat. Two. Then they resumed, because stillness was confession and motion was innocence. "Some of them."

"Fascinating work, I'd imagine. All those names." The man consulted his forms. "Would you say the divers' families are generally satisfied with the settlements? We've had complaints. Letters. A widow in Port Callow says her husband's wages were swallowed whole by 'equipment costs.' Air-line rental, she calls it. Charging a man to breathe."

"That would be a question for Mr. Crane."

"It always is." The census taker closed his satchel with a small decisive click. "Thank you, Mr. Blackwood. You've been very helpful."

*I have lied to a representative of the Crown,* Theo thought, watching the man's back recede down the corridor toward Crane's office. *And I have done it well.* He should have felt relief. Instead he felt the particular dread of a man who has just realized the tide has turned without his noticing — that the safe sandbar he stood on was dissolving under him even as he praised its firmness.

Crane appeared ten minutes later, smoothing his waistcoat, wearing the face he wore for officials: bland, cooperative, faintly bored. He paused behind Theo's chair on his way past.

"The Mundy abstract," he said quietly. "Finish it today. And the Ferren. And the rest of the March roll." A pause, the length of a held breath. "All of them clean, Blackwood. We've eyes on us now."

"Yes, Mr. Crane."

He typed until the lamps were lit. He typed the falsified death rolls with his own two hands, striking each key cleanly, making the lies legible, making them official, making them permanent in the only way ink knows how to be. And somewhere between the Mundy abstract and the Ferren, between one dead man and another, the thought arrived that had been waiting for him all day, patient as the census taker:

*Someone is asking questions now. Someone is counting. And every lie I type makes me complicit in the answer.*

He did not stop. That was the worst of it. He did not stop.

* * *

The workshop after dark was a different country. The other clerks gone, Crane gone home to his wife and his good whiskey, the building settling around Theo like an old ship at anchor — creaks and drips and the sea underneath everything, patient, keeping its own ledger.

He should have gone home. Home was a rented room over the chandlery, a cot, a candle, silence. But his hands had that itch in them tonight, the restless itch of a man who has lied all day and needs to touch something true.

He told himself he was staying to finish the March roll. It was even partly true. He finished it — forty minutes of steady, mechanical lying, the *R* needing its double strike, the ribbon fading toward gray — and then, instead of locking up, instead of leaving, he found himself pulling the older volumes from the archive shelves. The bound annuals. The consolidated rolls going back a decade.

He was looking for names, he realized. He hadn't known it until his hands were doing it. Names of men his father had dived beside. Men from the jetty, from the boarding houses, men who had taught him to hold his breath in the shallows and laugh at him when he came up gasping. Old Tam Voss. The Ansel brothers. How many of them were in these books, and how were they entered?

He found the 1905 volume first. Ran his finger down the settlement column. Voss, T. — settled. Ansel, P. — settled. Ansel, R. — settled. Settled, settled, the word marching down the page like ants over sugar, and beside each name a sum so small it was an insult dressed as a number.

Then 1904. Then 1903. He was three volumes deep, kneeling on the cold floorboards with the lamp pulled low, when he decided — he would tell himself later it was accident, but it was not, it had never been accident — to look for one more name.

*Blackwood.*

His father. Samuel Blackwood, diver, twenty-two years on the banks, a man whose lungs the sea had finally collected in the autumn of 1905, when Theo was twelve. Theo remembered the company boat coming in without him. Remembered the foreman handing his mother — no, his mother was already gone then; handing *him*, a boy of twelve — a folded paper and saying words like *compensation* and *unfortunate* and *the bank was rough that week*. Remembered crying, and being ashamed of crying, and vowing never to do it again where anyone could see.

He found the page. Found the year. Found the name.

**Blackwood, S. — settled.**

Settled. Paid off. Closed. Entered in the same hand, in the same ink, in the same column as every other drowned man, with a figure beside it that would not have bought his father's diving suit, let alone his father's life.

Theo knelt there a long time. The lamp hissed. The sea breathed beneath the floor.

*Invisibility,* he thought, and the word came with a taste like copper. *I thought I was invisible because I was careful. Because I kept my head down and my hands moving and my mouth shut. Six years I believed that.*

But the book lay open under the lamp, and the book said otherwise. His father's name, settled. His own existence, absorbed. The indenture, the thin paper, the seal showing through like a brand. None of it had been his achievement. He had never made himself invisible. Someone else had arranged it — neatly, efficiently, years ago, the same way they arranged everything — and he had simply lived inside the arrangement, grateful for the dark, calling the dark his own.

They had erased his father with a word. They had erased *him* with a signature. And tonight, kneeling among the archives he tended like a faithful dog tends a house that is not his, Theo Blackwood understood that the erasure was still in progress. That the census taker's questions, Crane's warning, the March roll drying under his fingers — all of it was the sound of the eraser moving.

He closed the volume gently, the way you close a coffin.

And then he sat very still in the lamplight, listening to the sea, and became aware of a fact he had somehow never noticed in six years of working among these shelves: that he knew these records better than any man alive. Better than Crane. Better than the auditors who came once a year and signed whatever Crane put in front of them. He knew which entries were false, because he had typed half of them himself. He knew the shape of the truth underneath, the way a diver knows the reef beneath the water's blur.

Somewhere in this building — somewhere in these columns, these consolidations, these summaries of accounts closed — was the true count. The real number of the drowned. The wages eaten, the bribes paid, the widows given four shillings and a receipt.

And somewhere outside this building, his father's voice came back to him across six years of silence, saying the thing he used to say on the jetty when the banks were rough and the young divers were afraid: *The sea keeps accounts, boy. Sooner or later, everything comes up.*

Theo reached for a fresh sheet of paper and fed it into the typewriter with hands that had begun, very slightly, to tremble.

Not with fear. Or not only with fear.

He began to type — not the March roll, not the settlement abstracts, but a list of his own, in his own order, according to his own memory: names, dates, banks, depths. The things the books left out. The things the books were built to leave out.

Outside, far down the coast, the Meridian offices stood dark, and in them slept the complete, original record of everything the company had ever done to the men of the water.

Theo intended to find it.
