## 2. What the Diver Left

The shell had been in Theo's tin box for six years, wrapped in a scrap of sailcloth, and until the night of the census he had never once looked at it properly.

He knew it by touch. The ridged lip, the nacre cool as a coin kept in a cellar, the small chip near the hinge where his father's fingers — scarred, salt-split, always smelling faintly of tallow and brine — had pried it from the seabed on some morning before Theo was born. It was a *Pinctada maxima*, a gold-lip, the kind that made men rich and killed them for trying. His father had carried it home in his shirt pocket like a medal, and when he died — drowned off the Serpent Bank with four other men whose names now lived only in Meridian ledgers marked *settled* — the shell had come to Theo along with a sea chest that held nothing else worth keeping.

He turned it under the lamp now, the way the census man had turned his questions. And there it was, where grief had never let him look: fine lines scratched into the nacre inside the lip of the shell, so shallow they vanished unless the light raked across them low and sideways.

Numbers. Two rows of them. A bearing and a distance, in the old diver's shorthand — fathoms from the stump of the beacon post, paces along the shoreward line at dead low water.

His father had written him coordinates. His father had written him coordinates on the one object he'd known no clerk would ever throw away, because to Theo it was not paper but a relic.

Theo sat very still for a long time. The lamp hissed. Somewhere below his window a cart passed, its axle singing against the iron rails of the tramway, and the sound went through him like the memory of a voice.

*If anything happens to me,* his father used to say, laughing, wringing seawater out of his hair on the jetty while nine-year-old Theo counted his catch into the company tally-book, *you write it all down, boy. Paper doesn't hold its breath.*

He had thought it a joke. Divers joked about dying the way other men joked about weather — constantly, and without meaning anything by it.

* * *

The old diving jetty stood a mile north of the town, past the boiling-down works, past the Chinese gardens where the ground steamed in the early heat. The Meridian Company had abandoned it eleven years ago when they built the new deep-water wharf, but nobody had bothered pulling up the piles. They leaned out over the mudflats now like rotten teeth, crusted white with salt-bloom, and the tide went out beneath them so far you could walk on the wet sand and hear the whole structure creak and tick as it cooled.

Theo came on a Sunday, when the workshops were shut and Crane was at church performing his public devotion to a God who presumably did not audit accounts. He wore his oldest clothes and carried a canvas bag as if he were going fishing, which half the town did on Sundays, and he waited behind the mangroves until he was certain the flats were empty.

Dead low water. The bearing from the shell put the spot three fathoms — eighteen feet, in his father's land-measure — from the fourth pile, along the shoreward line. He paced it off twice. The sand there was ribbed with tide-work, and he dug with his hands, then with a broken oar-blade he'd brought, hauling out grey stinking slurry in armfuls, his shoulders burning, his heart keeping a stupid frantic time against his ribs.

Nothing. Nothing. A rusted fishhook, a bottle, more nothing.

Then the blade struck something that did not give — something with an edge, a corner. He dropped to his knees and clawed with his bare hands, and the mud came away from a flat sheet of copper-sheathed wood the size of a hymnal, strapped in tarred canvas, sealed at every seam with pitch.

A satchel. Waterproofed. Buried.

His father had gone down to this jetty — or come here at night, at some hidden hour, in the last weeks before the Serpent Bank took him — and buried his own testimony in the mud like a seed, trusting the tide to keep it, and trusting his son to find it, someday, if the son ever learned to read a pearl shell instead of merely treasuring it.

Theo knelt in the cold slush and could not move for a moment. Grief is a strange cargo; it does not lighten over six years, it only changes trim, rides lower, waits. Now it rose all at once, and he pressed both hands flat against the satchel and said, aloud, to no one, "I found it, Da."

The gulls screamed somewhere over the channel. He got the satchel into his bag and walked back along the flats with his legs shaking, looking — he could not help it — at every figure on the far shore, every sail, every smudge of smoke.

* * *

He did not open it in his room. Too many walls in a boarding house have ears, and his window faced the street. Instead he waited for Wednesday, when the workshop emptied at noon for the half-holiday, and let himself into the typing room alone with the satchel under his coat, because there was one place in the world guaranteed dry, private, and lit — and because, though he would not admit it even to himself, he wanted his father's words to be read first by lamplight and the smell of ink, among typewriters, among the tools of the trade that had been forced on Theo and never on the man who had earned them.

The pitch seals cracked away under his knife like old candle wax. Inside, wrapped in oilskin, were three account-books bound in sailcloth, a bundle of letters tied with tarred twine, and a pencil-written note on the flyleaf of the first book:

*True rolls. Keep them dry. Give them to a man who can print.*

Beneath it, smaller, almost shy: *For my boy. You were always better with words than I was.*

Theo read the first ledger standing up, and then he had to sit down.

It was everything the March roll was not. Every dive season since 1899 set out in his father's blunt hand: names, boats, depths, bottom-times — and beside each dead man, the true cause. *Paralysis. Burst lung. Kept down too long, mate short on windlass.* Beside each widow, the sum actually paid, and beside that, in a column Theo's father had headed *what Meridian keeps*, the withheld wages, the "insurance" premiums deducted and never insured, the sums entered in the company books as *compassion payments* that had never left Crane's office drawer. And folded into the letters, receipts — actual signed receipts — from two harbour officials and a police sergeant, dated across five years, acknowledging sums received "for discretion re: casualty returns."

Nineteen men. Nineteen names in the true roll that appeared nowhere in the company's records except as entries in a profit column.

Theo sat in the empty workshop with his father's handwriting shaking slightly in his grip, and understood that the dread he had felt at the census table — the dread of being invisible — had been aimed at the wrong thing all along. Invisibility was not his condition. It was Meridian's product. They manufactured it, name by name, ledger by ledger, and sold it back to the town as order.

Well. He had a typewriter. He had hands that could do ninety words a minute without looking. And he had, at last, something worth saying.

* * *

He began that same night, and every night after, when the boarding house slept.

He worked by the shaded lamp with a blanket pinned over the curtain, feeding the machine one sheet at a time, retyping his father's ledgers onto fresh paper in a fair, neutral, unimpeachable clerical hand — dates cross-checked, columns ruled, each page initialled *T.B.* in the corner like any honest abstract. He typed the death roll entire. He typed the wage deductions. He copied the officials' receipts word for word, including signatures described in detail, and beneath each page he typed the source: *per true rolls of Josiah Blackwood, diver, deceased March 1905, recovered [date], held in trust by his son.*

It was slow, exacting, sacred work. Ninety words a minute meant nothing here; here he made himself go slowly, checking every figure twice against the sailcloth books, because an error in these pages would not cost a clerk a rebuke. It would cost the truth its credibility, and the truth had only one copy in the world.

By the third week he understood what the feeling was that had been growing in him like warmth in frozen hands. Not safety — he checked the street below too often for that, and once, hearing boots on the stair at midnight, he sat motionless for twenty minutes with the pages under his shirt until the boots passed on. Not peace, either.

Hope. Dangerous, specific hope. The hope of a man who knows where the bodies are written down, and has decided they will be read.

He was copying the final season — 1904–05, the Serpent Bank season, his father's own last entries, figures in a hand grown shakier as the lungs grew weaker — when he noticed, tucked into the back binding of the third sailcloth book, a loose slip he had somehow missed. His father's writing, but hurried, pressed hard:

*Crane suspects the true rolls exist. If I drown, it won't be the water that did it. Tell no one until it's printed. Not the priest, not your mother's people, NO ONE.*

And beneath that, a final line that stopped Theo's breath:

*He watches the mail. Post nothing. Carry it yourself.*

Theo looked at the clock above the door. Half past one. Outside, the town lay quiet, and down at the new wharf the Meridian offices slept their fat, well-ordered sleep, and somewhere in them — he was suddenly, coldly certain of it — Crane was already wondering why his best clerk had taken to walking alone on the tidal flats on Sundays.

Three days later, Theo smelled smoke coming from the direction of the archive room, and heard Crane calling his name.
