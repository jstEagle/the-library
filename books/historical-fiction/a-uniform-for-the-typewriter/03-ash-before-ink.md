## 3. Ash Before Ink

Theo heard it first in the rhythm of Crane's boots.

For eleven years he had learned the workshop by its sounds—the wet slap of shell on the sorting table, the cough of the stove, the particular drag of Mr. Crane's left heel where a pearl splinter had lodged in the leather decades ago and never worked itself free. Drag-step, drag-step. That morning the boots came down the corridor of filing cabinets faster than usual, and they stopped at Theo's desk without the courtesy of warning.

"You've been at the ledgers after hours."

It was not a question, but Theo had learned that some questions wore the clothes of statements so you would answer them like confessions. He kept his fingers on the keys of the Remington, the carriage still holding half a line: *diver no. 44, cause of death —*

"Cramp," he said aloud, finishing the lie he'd typed a hundred times before, and looked up with the flat, tired face of a clerk who wanted only his supper. "The heat makes me slow, sir. I stay late to keep pace."

Crane's eyes were the color of shallow water over sand—pale, and just as good at hiding what lay beneath them. He picked up the death roll from Theo's desk, riffled it once with a thumb, and set it down again exactly where it had been, which was its own kind of message.

"A census man came through Broome way," Crane said. "Asking after families. Asking who signed for the divers' effects." A pause, long enough for the stove to pop. "Someone's been talking to someone, Blackwood. Paper doesn't walk out of this building on its own legs."

"No, sir."

"Mm." Crane straightened his cuffs. "There'll be a tidy-up of the archives this week. Old paper attracts damp, and damp attracts rot." His heel clicked away—drag-step, drag-step—and Theo sat very still until the outer door closed, then looked down at the line of type and saw that his hands had gone cold enough to make the next words come out crooked.

*Cause of death: cramp.* His father had drowned with his lungs full of water and his chest full of wages Meridian never paid, and somewhere in a cabinet behind Theo's back was the page that said *settled*, as if a dead man could be squared away like an invoice.

That night Theo did not go home. He waited until the lamplighter passed, let himself in through the coal chute he'd oiled quiet a week ago, and carried his father's satchel to the typing room. The true ledger lay open under the green lamp: names, dates, depths, the sums withheld, the officials bought at two pounds ten a signature. Forty-one pages retyped. Nine remained.

He fed the first of them into the machine and began.

* * *

Crane stood in the doorway of the manager's office above the sorting floor and watched the harbor lights come up one by one, and thought about paper.

He had spent thirty years learning that a company did not live or die by pearls. Pearls were luck. A company lived by what its papers said, and died by what they said to the wrong man. The census taker was nothing—a little government man with a little government form. But forms bred inquiries, inquiries bred inspectors, and inspectors, given enough time among the cabinets, would find the seam where the false rolls met the true ones. There was always a seam. He had sewn most of them himself.

He poured a measure of rum and did not drink it.

The old archives had to go. Every original roll, every wage book prior to '04, every letter that proved what letters prove. Fire was honest work; fire kept no copies. He had done it before, in '98, when the flu took nine divers in a fortnight and the truth of it threatened to take more than that. A lamp knocked over, a cry in the night, and by morning the town had a tragedy to mourn instead of a crime to prosecute. Grief, properly managed, was cheaper than justice.

But there was the boy to consider.

Blackwood. The diver's son, quiet as furniture, quick with figures, the sort of clerk managers dream of because he wants nothing—or seems to. Yet the census man's questions had landed somewhere, Crane was sure of it. He had watched the boy's shoulders that day, and shoulders told truths faces were trained out of. And the boy stayed late. Too late. Men who stay late are either devoted or afraid, and devotion has a different posture.

Tomorrow night, then. The stove stoked high, the archives cleared, the building warm and empty except for whatever the boy intended to be empty about. If Blackwood had taken anything, the burning would flush him; a thief must run to his loot or watch it burn, and either way he shows himself.

Crane drank the rum at last, tasting the harbor in it, salt and iron. Below him the sorting floor lay dark, four hundred drawers of paper sleeping in their cabinets like things buried at sea.

By Friday, he thought, there would be nothing left to find.

* * *

Thursday night, page forty-six.

Theo's fingers had learned the true ledger the way his father's hands had known the rope ladder—without looking, without thinking, each figure struck clean. Outside, the wind had turned and come off the water smelling of rain that wouldn't fall, and the stove in the corner of the typing room ticked hotter than it should have. He noticed that. He noticed the smell underneath the coal smoke too, sweetish, like varnish, like the shellac they used on the sorting tables.

Then he noticed the light under the door of the archive room, orange where it should have been gray.

He was across the room before he decided to move. The archive door was hot to the touch. Through the iron grate of the transom he could see them—flames walking along the tops of the shelves, unhurried, thorough, eating the wage books first as though they knew which pages mattered. Someone had laid it well. Oil, probably, poured along the aisles while the building emptied at six.

Nine pages. Eight, now—he had finished eight. One remained, and it was the worst one: the page with the officials' names, the bribes, the signatures. Without it the rest was grief. With it, the rest was evidence.

Smoke came under the door in a gray tongue. Theo went back to the desk. His hands shook so badly he misstruck the first line twice, and he made himself breathe the way his father had taught him to breathe on the ladder—slow, count four, hold, count four—because panic spends air faster than work does. *You don't rush at the bottom,* his father used to say. *The bottom doesn't care how fast you are.*

The typewriter hammered. Page forty-nine grew line by line while the smoke thickened and the flames found the pitch of the roof beams overhead and began, softly, horribly, to sing.

*Commissioner Harrow — £14, quarterly. Inspector Denny — £9 and a share of—*

The door of the typing room opened.

Crane filled it, coatless, sleeves rolled, a fire-iron in one hand and soot already striping his cheek. Behind him the corridor glowed. For a moment neither man moved, and Theo understood with terrible clarity that Crane had not come to save the building. He had come to see what the fire flushed out.

"Well," Crane said.

Theo pulled the last sheet from the platen. His eyes went, once, to the mail sack.

It stood by the outer door where the evening postman had left it for the morning collection—canvas, bulging, tied at the neck. Somewhere inside it, beneath three days of ordinary correspondence, lay forty-eight pages in a brown envelope addressed to the *Northern Advertiser*. He had posted nothing yet. That was the bet. The sack would be collected at seven; the fire brigade would arrive before seven; and between those two facts Theo had built his whole future, thin as a shell wall.

"The ledgers," Crane said, advancing. "Where are they?"

"What ledgers?" Theo's voice came out steadier than his hands. "Sir, the building's burning."

Crane hit him with the back of the fire-iron—not the head, Crane was too careful for heads—and Theo went into the desk, into the typewriter, feeling the machine bite his ribs, tasting copper. Hands seized his collar and hauled him upright.

"The boy who stays late," Crane said, close, breath hot with rum. "I watched you for weeks. Where is it? Your father's papers—the satchel—where?"

So Crane knew. Had known, maybe, all along, and tonight was not cleanup but harvest. Theo spat blood onto the floorboards and said nothing, and the saying-nothing cost him another blow, and another, and between them he heard the roof groan and thought: *the bottom doesn't care how fast you are.* He folded the last page small, small, smaller, and when Crane wrenched him around to shake him, Theo's hand slipped into the open case of the Remington and pressed the paper flat against the machine's inner frame, into the space where the typebars slept, and shut the lid.

Crane threw him down. Through swelling eyes Theo watched the manager stand over him, silhouetted against the orange corridor, weighing something—whether a body in a burning building was a problem or a solution.

Seven o'clock, Theo thought. Seven o'clock, and the postman comes.

"The sack," Crane said suddenly, turning toward the door, and Theo's heart stopped—until Crane merely kicked it aside, spilling letters across the threshold, hunting for brown envelopes among the white, tearing two or three open with his thumb while the fire climbed the walls. Bills. Circulars. A dentist's reminder. Nothing.

When Crane looked back, Theo had crawled halfway to the window and was dragging himself up through it, glass taking a stripe of blood from his forearm, falling into the wet sand outside as the windows behind him blew out in a breath of heat.

He lay in the dark and watched his inheritance burn, and felt under his jacket, where a bruise was already blooming, the shape of nothing at all—for the last page was in the typewriter case, and the typewriter case was still inside, on the desk, in the fire.

No. Not quite. He remembered, with a surge that hurt worse than the beating: he had moved the case to the veranda step at dusk, meaning to carry it home. It sat there now, thirty feet away in the smoke, ordinary as luggage.

Theo crawled toward it as the roof came down behind him in a fountain of sparks, and somewhere down the hill, faint but coming, he heard the fire bell—and, closer than the bell, footsteps on the shingle, and Crane's voice calling his name through the smoke, no longer careful at all.
