## 4. A Uniform for the Typewriter

The summons came on a Tuesday, delivered by a boy in a red cap who did not know whose door he was knocking on and would not have cared if he had. Theo read it twice at the threshold of his rented room—the same room he'd kept since the fire, though the rent had doubled once the newspapers printed his name—and then set it on the table beside the typewriter, where it could be seen.

*Theodore Blackwood. You are required to appear before the Commission of Inquiry into the Meridian Trading Company, Broome Courthouse, Thursday next, ten o'clock, to give evidence under your own name.*

Under your own name. The clerk who typed the summons had no way of knowing what those four words cost, or what they were worth. Theo touched them anyway, lightly, the way he had once touched pearl shell to judge its weight.

Three weeks had passed since the workshop burned. Three weeks since Crane's fists found him in the smoke and the constables' lanterns found them both, and the typewriter case went into a mail sack that was already rolling down the hill toward the pier on the last lugger south. He remembered little of that night in its proper order: Crane's voice gone ragged, the question *where are the pages* repeated like a man praying backwards; the taste of copper; the strange calm that settled over him when he understood that lying was no longer necessary—only silence, and silence was the one thing his father had left him in abundance. By the time they pulled him upright on the shingle, the case was a mile out on dark water, and Crane was screaming at men who had already stopped listening to him.

The newspaper had run the ledger across four columns. THE MERIDIAN PAPERS, the headline said, and beneath it the numbers Theo had retyped night after night by lamplight: forty-one divers dead in nine years, their wages withheld, their families paid a fraction and made to sign papers marked *settled*. His father's name was in the third column. *Blackwood, Elias — settled.* Someone at the paper had drawn a box around it, as if even grief needed highlighting to be believed by readers in the south.

They had spelled his name wrong in the first edition. Right in the second. He had bought both.

* * *

He went down to the old diving jetty on the Wednesday, the day before the inquiry, because he wanted to stand there once more without anyone watching, and because there was nowhere else that felt like it belonged to the truth. The tide was low. Barnacles had begun to reclaim the pilings where the satchel had waited eleven years for a son patient enough—or blind enough—to follow an etched shell home. He crouched and put his palm flat on the boards. They were warm from the sun, and rough, and utterly indifferent, and he loved them for it.

His father had been a quiet man. That was the thing the newspapers could not fit into their columns, the thing the commission would not ask about tomorrow: not the deaths, which were arithmetic, but the silence, which had been a kind of love. Elias Blackwood had known what Meridian was doing—he had counted the drowned himself, carried the widows' bundles to their doors, watched the company clerk enter false figures into false books—and he had said nothing aloud, because a diver with a loud mouth did not keep his air hose attached, and a diver with a family did not keep his family fed. So he had written it all down instead, in a hand like rope-knots, and sealed it in oilcloth, and sunk it beneath the floorboards of the trade, and died before he could decide whether telling the truth was courage or just another way of drowning.

For eleven years Theo had thought his own invisibility was a talent. Now he knew better. Invisibility was never something you achieved; it was something arranged for you, by people who benefited from the arrangement. Crane had arranged it. His father, in his gentler fashion, had arranged it too. The census taker with his questions too many had merely been the first person to ask Theo whether the arrangement suited him.

It hadn't. It never had.

A fishing boat chugged past the jetty's end, and the wake slapped the pilings, and Theo stood, and brushed off his knees, and went back up the hill to iron the only shirt he owned that still had both its cuffs.

* * *

The courthouse smelled of varnish and nervous sweat. Theo had been inside it exactly once before, at fourteen, to sign the indenture papers that bound him to Meridian until he turned twenty-one; he remembered the pen scratching, and the clerk not looking at him, and the peculiar sensation of becoming, in the eyes of the room, a line item. Today every eye in the gallery found him and held on. His name was spoken aloud—not Blackwood, boy, or you there, clerk, but *Theodore Blackwood*, full weight, three syllables of it—and he walked to the witness box feeling the name settle over him like a garment cut too large, still, but cut for him at last.

They asked him about the ledgers. About the nights of retyping, the satchel, the coordinates on the shell. They asked him to identify entries, and he did, his voice steady in a way that surprised him, because the words had been typed so many times in his head that speaking them felt almost like transcription. When counsel for the commission asked whether he had acted alone, Theo looked past the barristers to Crane, sitting flanked by two constables in the dock, his face grey as wet ash, his hands folded as if still waiting for someone to bring him a book to balance.

"He wrote it first," Theo said. "My father. I only gave it a uniform."

There was a rustle through the gallery at that—a few murmurs, one dry cough—and the commissioner leaned forward and asked him to explain. Theo considered. Somewhere behind him a journalist's pencil stopped moving, waiting.

"A ledger is just numbers," he said. "Numbers can be burned. Numbers can be rewritten—that was my job, for years; I know how easily it's done. But a true account, typed fair, signed, sent out into the world in a mail sack—" He paused. "That has a uniform on it. It can be recognised. It can be called by its name." He heard the echo of the summons in his own sentence and let it stand. "My father couldn't give it that. I could. So I did."

Crane's arrest had come quietly, ten days after the fire, at the Pier Hotel over a plate of chops he did not finish. The charges ran long: conspiracy, falsification of records, the death of a diver named Okada whose *settled* entry Theo had retyped forty times and could recite in his sleep. There would be more names before the commission finished. There would be a trial. Theo had been told—warned, really—that his testimony would be needed again, and again after that, that anonymity was a country he had emigrated from permanently, with no return passage booked.

When he stepped down, his legs were shaking, and he found he was smiling anyway.

* * *

The typewriter sat on his table where it had sat for three weeks: scorched along the left flank, one key—*R*—warped and silent, the black enamel bubbled into small grey craters like a shoreline seen from very far away. The salvage men at the ruined workshop had offered to clear it away with the rest of the ash. Theo had carried it home instead, four miles, resting twice, cradling it like something newborn or newly dead; he still wasn't certain which.

It had been his father's, once, before the indenture, before Meridian. Elias Blackwood had used it to write the letters that taught his son his letters, and then to write the secret accounts, and then it had passed to the company as part of the boy's binding, and spent years typing lies under Crane's roof. Two owners, two masters, one machine. On the last night it had sheltered the truth inside its case while the building came down around it, the way a body shelters breath.

Theo sat before it now, the summons and the newspaper clipping pushed aside, and rolled in a clean sheet. The lamp threw his shadow tall against the wall. Outside, the town was talking—about the commission, about Crane, about compensation for the widows, about whether the pearl fleets would sail in spring or scatter—and somewhere among the voices, he knew, were men deciding whether to be afraid of him.

Let them decide. He set his fingers on the keys, avoiding the dead one, and began to type.

*To the Editor,* he wrote. *My name is Theodore Blackwood. My father was a diver.*

The *R* stuck when he reached *father*, and he pressed harder, and it gave, half-formed but legible—the whole machine groaning toward the light. He did not stop to fix it. There were pages yet, and ink enough, and for the first time in his life, all the time in the world to say it plainly, in his own hand, under his own name.
