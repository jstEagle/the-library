## 1. The Weight of Small Gears

The frost had come to Vellmark three nights early, and Sable Dravenmoor felt it in the hinges of the city before he felt it in his own bones.

He left the shop before dawn, as he always did, though there was no appointment waiting and no reason to be abroad so soon. Habit was a kind of mechanism too, he told himself — a train of wheels set in motion years ago and never stopped, each morning engaging the next with a click he no longer heard. He locked the door of Dravenmoor & Son behind him, paused — as he always paused — at the faded lettering on the glass, and did not scrape away the frost that blurred the word *Son*. The frost was kinder than a chisel. It let him pretend the sign said only *Dravenmoor*, only himself, only one man with no history attached.

The river was smoking. That was what the old ferrymen called it, the way the water gave up its warmth to the air in slow grey ribbons, and this morning the mist lay along the Maren like something sleeping. Sable crossed the lower quay with his collar up and his eyes down, cataloguing the city's silences the way other men counted their coins. There was the silence of the fish market, shuttered now, its awnings lashed down against a wind that hadn't yet arrived. There was the silence around the customs house steps, where the stone was scrubbed cleaner than any other stone in Vellmark, though no one could say why they still bothered after twelve years. And there was the silence that followed him personally, the small pocket of it that moved when he moved: people saw the lean figure in the grey coat with the oil-stained cuffs and thought, *clockmaker*, and then thought nothing further, which was precisely the design.

Being unnoticed was a craft like any other. He had learned it young, and he had learned it well, and for twelve years it had kept him whole the way a case keeps a movement — sealed against dust, against fingers, against inquiry.

But cases open. He knew that better than anyone.

* * *

The commission had come two days before, delivered not by messenger but by hand, which was itself unusual enough to make him read the letter twice. The paper was heavy, cream-colored, stamped at the head with the observatory's seal — a star over a gear, the ink slightly uneven, as if pressed by an aging thumb.

*M. Dravenmoor,* it began, and he had noted the error at once — *Mister* rather than *Master*, the honorific of a man who dealt in wool or nails — *the master clock of the Observatory of Vellmark has ceased to keep time. It has kept time for ninety-one years without failure, through two floods and one bombardment, and I am given to understand you are the last craftsman in this city who might coax it back to life. I will not insult you by pretending I wrote to others first.*

It was signed simply, *Herr Brandt, Keeper.*

Sable had sat with the letter for a long while after reading it, turning it over, holding it near the lamp as if some second message were written between the lines in milk or lemon juice. There was nothing there but what it said. And yet the observatory district — he had not been up there since he was eleven years old, since the autumn when everything changed, and the observatory stood above the tannery quarter like a witness on a hill, all glass eye and brass nerve, watching everything and saying nothing.

He had written back accepting before he fully understood why. *Only work,* he had told himself, setting pen to paper. *A clock is a clock. Gears are gears. The dead do not live in escapements.* He had repeated it like a counterspell while his hand formed the words *I accept your commission* in his careful draftsman's script, and the counterspell had worked about as well as counterspells usually do, which is to say it soothed the surface while leaving everything underneath exactly as it was.

* * *

Now he climbed.

The road from the quay to the observatory district wound up through streets whose names Sable made himself recite silently, like a boy at catechism, because naming them proved he was not afraid of them: Cooper's Lane, where the vats used to steam; Ropewalk; the Weavers' Stair with its hundred worn steps; and then the long straight rise called Clerks' Row, because the men who had kept the city's records had lived there once, their windows facing east toward the light they needed for reading fine hands.

Elias Voss had lived on Clerks' Row.

Sable did not look at number fourteen as he passed it. He looked at it very carefully, in fact, with the fixed and deliberate attention of a man studying anything except the thing before him — the lintel, the iron boot-scraper, the crack running through the third window's pane like a frozen lightning stroke. A woman's washing hung in the courtyard behind, anonymous sheets gone grey in the cold. Someone else lived there now. Someone else had lived there for twelve years, and the city had arranged itself around that absence the way water arranges itself around a dropped stone, closing over, forgetting its own shape.

That was the great silence, the mother of all the others: the silence of a city that had decided, collectively and without ever speaking of it, that certain things would not be spoken of. Sable had grown up inside that decision. He had helped build it, brick by brick, silence by silence, until the wall was high enough that even he could no longer see over it — except at night, except in dreams, except when a letter came on cream paper with a star and a gear pressed into it and his hands began, treacherously, to remember.

At the top of the Weavers' Stair he stopped to catch his breath and found himself looking back down across the roofs of Vellmark, all the red tiles going rust-and-brown under the frost, the river a dull pewter band stitched through the middle of it all. And there, at the river's narrow waist, the bridge — the Bridge of Saint Aldric, seven arches, four hundred years old, the single thread by which Vellmark hung between the world and itself. He watched the early carts crossing it, tiny with distance, and felt the familiar tightening in his chest that the bridge always gave him: the knowledge that there was exactly one way out of this city, one road, one span of stone, and that every plan he had ever made for leaving had begun and ended with that thread.

He had made such plans perhaps a hundred times. He had never once followed one past the first arch.

* * *

The observatory rose above him at last, white dome catching the low sun so that it seemed to hold a coal inside it. The building below the dome was older, brownstone and iron, and the master clock occupied its own chamber in the tower's heart — he remembered that much from the school visit his mother had scraped together the fee for, back when she was still trying to give him a future larger than a workbench. He remembered standing beneath the great dial, nine feet across, feeling the tick of it come up through the floor into his boots, into his teeth, and thinking that here at last was a machine too large to hide inside.

The door was answered by a tall old man with white hair cut short as a soldier's and hands that were clean in the particular way of hands that have given up manual work but still expect to do some. His eyes were pale grey and missed nothing, which unsettled Sable more than any scrutiny he'd endured in years.

"Herr Dravenmoor." Not a question. "You received my letter."

"And answered it," Sable said. "Herr Brandt."

"You came yourself. I half expected you to send an apprentice." Brandt stepped back to let him in. "Forgive me. Twelve years ago you would have been too young for me to write to at all, and yet I find I know your work by reputation. The cathedral turret clocks. The automaton at the Merchants' Hall that everyone swears has a soul. You are careful with mechanisms that matter."

"They're all mechanisms that matter to someone."

"Yes," Brandt said, and looked at him a moment longer than courtesy required. "Come up. I'll show you the patient."

* * *

The clock chamber smelled of dust and machine oil and cold stone, and beneath those smells something else, faint and metallic, that Sable could not immediately place and did not try to. The master clock stood against the eastern wall like a monument: a case of black walnut taller than two men, its face a circle of enamel white as a held breath, its single hand — the observatory kept sidereal time, hours marked off for stars rather than ships — lying still at 4:41.

"Stopped on the night of the twelfth of October," Brandt said, behind him. "Twelve years ago. The pendulum simply — quit. I have checked the suspension, the crutch, the pallets. Nothing is broken. It is as if the clock decided, on that night of all nights, that it had seen enough."

Sable knelt at the base of the case and did not answer. Through the glass panel in the pendulum housing he could see the bob hanging motionless, and the sight of a pendulum at rest affected him strangely, the way an unfired kiln affects a potter — all that potential, held in abeyance, waiting.

"When did it stop?" he asked. "Precisely. What hour?"

"The hour shown. A little before dawn."

*The twelfth of October.* Sable kept his face still, kept his breathing level, kept the whole practiced apparatus of his invisibility running smoothly while something behind it turned over slowly, heavily, like a gear meshing where no gear should be. The twelfth of October. The night the clerk Elias Voss was found drowned in three feet of canal water with his ledger satchel missing. The night the whole city had agreed, in its great collective silence, to call an accident.

He had been eleven. He had been on the far side of town, asleep, knowing nothing. But everyone in Vellmark knew the date the way they knew the flood line carved into the customs house steps — not because anyone spoke of it, but because no one ever had, and the not-speaking had engraved it deeper than any chisel could.

"A coincidence," Brandt said, and Sable realized he had spoken aloud, or nearly. "Or not. I have had twelve years to consider the question, Herr Dravenmoor, and I find I no longer care which it is. I care that the heart of this observatory stands still, and that I am too old and too clumsy to mend it. Can you?"

Sable laid his palm flat against the walnut case. The wood was colder than the room warranted. Somewhere inside, beneath the dial, beneath the trains and the escapement, something waited that had waited twelve years, and he felt — absurdly, unprofessionally — that it was waiting for *him*, that the letter had been less a summons than a trap sprung by patience, and that the old man's grey eyes had known it before he did.

"I can," he said. "It will take some days. The movement must come apart entirely."

"Take whatever days you need." Brandt moved toward the door, then paused, his hand on the frame. "One condition. Whatever you find in there — and a clock this age, this history, holds all manner of things; repairmen's notes, coins slipped into cases by sentimental journeymen, the occasional dead mouse — whatever you find, bring it to me before you discard it. Some things in this tower belong to the record."

*To the record.* The phrase went through Sable like a splinter finding its depth.

"Of course," he said.

When Brandt's footsteps had faded down the stair, Sable stood alone in the round stone chamber with the stopped clock, and outside the narrow windows Vellmark lay spread out below him in the frost — the roofs, the river, the bridge with its thin traffic, the tannery quarter hidden among the smoke of the morning fires. From up here he could see everything. That was the purpose of the place. He wondered, with a disquiet that would not resolve into either fear or sense, whether the clock, when it ran again, would watch as well.

He opened his tool roll. Brass and steel clicked softly against stone. *Only work,* he told himself, selecting the key for the case lock, telling it to his hands, to his wrists, to the pulse ticking in them — the one clock in the room that had never stopped.

The lock turned. The case swung open on hinges that groaned like something waking.

And inside, threaded into the great wheel-train where no honest mechanism had any business carrying weight, he saw at once that something was wrong — or rather, that something was *extra*: a shape where the schematic of his memory insisted there should be none, a darkness lodged between the going train and the strike work, wired in deliberately, skillfully, by hands that had understood the machine almost as well as he did.

His own reflection looked back at him from the polished pendulum bob, small and bent and pale.

He reached in anyway.
