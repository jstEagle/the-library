## 2. Summons

The observatory district had not changed so much as it had settled, the way dust settles in a house where no one has swept for years but no one has quite left either. Sable Dravenmoor climbed the switchback road from the river quarter with his tool case knocking against his knee and counted the silences the way other men counted their money — out of habit, out of need, because he had learned at eleven years old that a silence could be spent like coin if you knew its value.

There was the silence of the tannery lane, which he did not walk past, never walked past, though it would have saved him a quarter hour. There was the silence of the shuttered customs house, whose windows had been papered over since before the war and whose doors now wore the chalk marks of requisition officers who had come, looked, and moved on to softer targets. There was the silence of St. Aldric's bell, which no longer rang the hours — its rope cut, someone said, for a funeral that never ended — so that the whole eastern quarter told time by the observatory clock instead, or had, until the night it stopped.

He catalogued these the way he catalogued gears: by size, by wear, by what they would bear. A silence under load behaves differently from one at rest. He had built a life on knowing the difference.

The road bent upward through bare lindens and past houses whose owners had learned, in the way of people living through an occupation's first frost, to keep their shutters half closed — enough light to work by, not enough to seem prosperous. Smoke stood straight up from the chimneys. The air smelled of coal and coming ice.

At the top of the hill the observatory rose against a sky the color of unpolished pewter: a squat stone tower with a copper dome gone green at the seams, and beside it the older campanile housing the master clock, its face white as a blind eye, its hands frozen at nine minutes past eleven. Sable had seen that face from across the city all winter long, every year, twelve winters now. He had made himself stop seeing it. That was a skill too.

The door was oak, iron-banded, older than the guild rolls. Before he could knock, it opened.

"You're the clockmaker." The man was seventy if he was a day, tall but stooped like a beam bearing too long under snow, with a face of deep creases and eyes that were startlingly, uncomfortably clear. "Dravenmoor. You came."

"Your message said the movement had stopped entirely," Sable said. It was not confirmation and it was not courtesy; it was the professional register he put on like an apron, and most people accepted it as both. "That is unusual. These trains don't simply stop. They slow, they drift, they lose time. They do not stop."

"And yet." The man stepped back to admit him. "Herr Brandt. Keeper of this tower for thirty-one years, which makes me either very devoted or very foolish, depending on whom you ask." His voice was dry, unhurried, the voice of a man who had spent decades talking mostly to machinery and stars. "Come in. Mind the third stair. It complains."

The interior was cold in the particular way of buildings heated grudgingly — a stove somewhere below radiating just enough warmth to keep the pipes from bursting and no more. Sable stood in a round stone vestibule hung with instruments he recognized and some he didn't: barometers with hairline cracks, a sextant on a velvet stand, star charts curling at their edges like leaves in autumn. Everything was clean. That surprised him. Abandoned places are dusty, and this place was not abandoned, whatever the city believed.

"The workshop is above. The clock is above that." Brandt took up a lantern though the morning offered grey daylight enough, and led him up a spiral stair worn into a smooth trough by a century of feet. "I'll tell you plainly, Herr Dravenmoor, since you strike me as a man who prefers plain speech even when he doesn't return it. I wrote to six clockmakers before you. Four refused without hearing terms. One quoted me a sum meant to insult. And one — the youngest — asked why I hadn't let it stay stopped."

Sable climbed, watching the back of the old man's head, the precise economy of his steps. "And what did you answer?"

"I said a stopped clock is a lie told twice a day, and a frozen one is a lie told always." Brandt paused at a landing to catch breath, waving off nothing, admitting nothing. "The city looks to that face. When it stopped, people began telling time by rumor. Rumor keeps worse hours than any escapement I've ever known."

* * *

The clock room occupied the top of the campanile: a high drum of a chamber with the great dial's back filling one wall — a lattice of iron spokes behind the painted face — and the movement itself rising through the center like the skeleton of something enormous and patient. Three weights hung silent on their cables. The pendulum, longer than Sable was tall, hung dead still, and its stillness filled the room worse than any noise would have.

He set down his case and opened it and let his hands do what his hands did best, moving among the familiar shapes — pliers, pinions, the box of pegwood, the oil horn — while his mind stayed carefully, professionally blank.

"When did it stop?" he asked. It was the first question any clockmaker asks. It establishes everything: corrosion, oil degradation, thermal damage to the arbor.

Brandt did not answer immediately. Sable heard him set the lantern down, heard the creak of him settling onto the keeper's stool near the wall, a stool polished by thirty-one years of the same sitting.

"The night of the fourteenth of Frostmonth," Brandt said. "Twelve years ago."

Sable's hands went still over the case. Only for a moment. He let them resume their sorting, one tool, then another, each laid in its place with a click.

"Frostmonth," he repeated. "Cold snap?"

"The coldest in a decade. But the clock had run through colder. Its father ran through colder still." Brandt's voice remained level, almost conversational, and that levelness itself was information. Men who have rehearsed an answer speak it smoothly. "It stopped between one stroke of eleven and the next. Not wound down — I checked the barrels within the hour. Not fouled — the oil was sound. It stopped as though something had been placed in the train. As though it had been *asked* to stop."

"That's not how machines behave," Sable said.

"No," Brandt agreed. "It isn't."

Sable turned at last and looked at the movement properly. It was a magnificent thing, a gravity train of the old school, built by hands two generations dead, every wheel brass and steel, the escapement a deadbeat anchor of the finest proportion. Even stopped, even cold, it had dignity. He approached it the way he approached all broken things: with respect first, diagnosis second.

He examined the winding squares. Untouched. The great wheel teeth — sound. The pallets — he tilted his lamp toward them — sound, lightly oiled, oiled *recently*, within months, which meant someone had maintained this machine after its death, embalming it. He frowned at that and said nothing about it.

"The fourteenth of Frostmonth," he said again, and kept his voice in the register of a man confirming dates for a service ledger. "Something else happened that night. There was a clerk. A court clerk. It was in the gazette for weeks afterward."

He felt rather than saw Brandt go still on the stool.

"Elias Voss," Brandt said.

"Voss." Sable tested the name in his mouth the way he'd test a gear for true running — lightly, listening for wobble. "They never found who did it. Or they said they never found. The city being what it was that winter."

"The city being what it has always been." Brandt rose, slowly, joints announcing themselves, and came to stand beside him at the movement. Up close he smelled of lamp oil and wool and something herbal, camphor maybe, the smell of a man who lived alone with valuable things. "You have a good memory, Herr Dravenmoor."

"For dates and trains," Sable said. "It's the trade. Everything in a clock is connected; you learn to hold connections." He lifted his loupe to his eye and bent to the escapement, and through the little glass ring the world narrowed to steel and shadow, which was where he liked it. "If you want my preliminary judgment: there is nothing here that stops a train like this. No seizure, no breakage, no gummed oil. Either the fault is deeper in the going train than I can see from here, or—"

"Or," Brandt said.

"—or someone intervened." He straightened. He removed the loupe with deliberate care, folded its arm, set it down. Professionalism was a garment and he wore it well, but he could feel the seam of it straining at the shoulder. "Which is not a repair question. That is a question for whoever locked this tower for twelve years."

"I didn't lock it," Brandt said. "I *kept* it. There's a difference, and the difference is the whole of my employment." He moved to the narrow window that faced east, over the rooftops toward the river, and stood looking out at the flat grey light. "Do you know what a keeper does, Herr Dravenmoor? Everyone thinks he winds and oils and sweeps. Mostly he watches. He watches the weather for the astronomers. He watches the city for the almanac men. And some nights he watches for things that have nothing to do with stars at all."

"I was engaged to repair a clock," Sable said quietly.

"You were engaged because your name came up three times in three different quarters as the man who asks no questions. Do you know what a recommendation that is? In this town? At this moment?" Brandt turned from the window. The clear eyes were not unkind, but they did not blink away either. "I am an old man on a hill, and the army that won this war is coming down the river to sit out its negotiations in our parlors, and when they arrive they will bring their own clerks, and their own historians, and their own ideas about what happened in this city twelve years ago and who should be permitted to remember it. I have kept something safe for a long time on the understanding that keeping is not the same as using. I find, lately, that the understanding may need revising."

The pendulum hung between them, dead, patient.

"I can restore the train," Sable said. "I can't restore anything else. I'm a clockmaker, Herr Brandt. What stopped this clock twelve years ago is outside my trade."

"Is it?" Brandt asked, softly.

And there it was — the question sliding in under the apron of his professionalism, finding the gap he had spent twelve years keeping sewn shut. Sable looked at the old man and understood suddenly that he had been expected, that the letter summoning him had perhaps been less a commission than a summons in the older sense, and that the fourteenth of Frostmonth was a date Brandt had chosen to say aloud precisely to watch what crossed his face.

Nothing had crossed his face. Twelve years of practice guaranteed that much. But inside, beneath the practiced blankness, something shifted its weight — the way a house shifts when ice takes hold of its foundations — and Sable Dravenmoor heard it clearly, though he gave no sign.

"I'll need the room warm before I open the barrels," he said. "Cold steel and warm hands make poor decisions. Give me until tomorrow to assess. My fee stands as quoted. And Herr Brandt—" he met the old man's eyes at last, and let one degree of warning show through, no more, "—if there is something in this tower besides a clock, I advise you to keep it out of my way. I fix mechanisms. I have no interest in what people hide inside them."

"Of course," said Brandt, in the tone of a man agreeing with the weather. "Only the mechanism, then."

Sable worked through the afternoon in the cold tower with the dead pendulum swaying nothing above him, cleaning, measuring, mapping the movement in his notebook with the loving exactness that was the only honest language he had left. He did not think about Elias Voss. He did not think about a night twelve years ago and a boy who had run home through the tannery smoke with his lungs full of the worst smell in the world. He was very skilled at not thinking. It was, after all, his second trade.

But when full dark came and he descended the spiral stair with his case in hand, Brandt was waiting at the door with the lantern raised, and beyond him, down the black shoulder of the hill, the whole city lay spread out under the first hard frost of the season — and across the river, faint but unmistakable, a garrison horn sounded three long notes, paused, and sounded them again.

"They've reached the far bank," Brandt said, not sounding surprised. "The delegation. They'll close the bridge by morning, mark me. Ice in the piers, they'll say, and it will even be true. Nothing crosses in or out of Vellmark until the treaty men are done talking." He lowered the lantern, and its light drew his creased face out of the dark like a figure emerging on a developed plate. "You'll be staying in the city this winter after all, Herr Dravenmoor. I imagine you find that thought as comforting as I do."

Sable said nothing. Downriver, the horn sounded again — three notes, pause, three notes — the signal for a city sealing itself shut, and he stood on the observatory step feeling the trap close around him with the smooth, well-oiled precision of a mechanism he himself might have built.

* * *

He walked home through streets gone brittle with frost, and it was only when he reached his own door, key already in the lock, that he allowed himself the thought he had been refusing all day, setting it down finally like a heavy tool returned to its case:

The clock had stopped the night Elias Voss died.

Not drifted. Not slowed. Stopped — between one stroke and the next, as if asked.

And the keeper had watched him all day with those clear, unblinking eyes, saying the date aloud twice, waiting for something. Waiting, Sable thought as the lock turned under his thumb, for a reaction Sable had sworn twelve years ago, on his mother's knee, in a whisper, never to give anyone.

Tomorrow he would open the barrels. Tomorrow he would find what had been placed in the train.

He told himself, climbing the stairs to his cold rooms, that it would only be a fault in the metal. He was, he reminded himself, a man who fixed mechanisms.

The frost on the windowpane, when he lit his candle, had formed in long parallel lines, like the strokes of a hand writing something down.
