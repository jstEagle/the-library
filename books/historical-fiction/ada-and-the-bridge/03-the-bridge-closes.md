## 3. The Bridge Closes

The ice came down the Vell in the night, and by morning it had taken the bridge.

Sable heard it before he understood it — a groaning from the river, deep and structural, like a great door being forced somewhere beneath the city. He lay in his narrow bed above the workshop listening to it, counting as he always counted when he woke before light: the tick of the regulator clock on his wall, which he had built himself at nineteen and which had never once stopped, not through the coldest winters, not through the worst years. Sixty ticks to the minute. Three thousand six hundred to the hour. Numbers were a floor you could stand on when everything else tilted.

Then came the horns.

They began at first light — three long notes, then two short, then three long again. Sable sat up. He knew the signal code; every child in Vellmark knew it, though it had not been sounded in his lifetime until now. Three-and-two meant *the crossing is closed.* It was the horn the garrison on the far bank blew when ice made the bridge impassable, when no wagon, no rider, no man on foot could be trusted onto planks sheathed in black glass. In the old almanacs it happened perhaps once in ten years, and never, in living memory, this early in the season.

He dressed in the dark out of habit, though there was nothing to dress for. No customers would come up the crooked lane to Dravenmoor & Sons — the sign still said *and Sons* though there were no sons, only Sable, and had been for twelve years, since his mother died and left him the shop, the debt, and the silence. He lit the stove anyway. Hands wanted work even when the mind wanted nothing, and hands that wanted work would find something to ruin if you didn't give them better employment.

From the workshop window he could see a slice of the river between the rooflines — grey water running fast with plates of ice riding on it like shattered crockery — and beyond it, the far pier of the Winter Bridge, its stone shoulder furred white. The bridge itself he could not see from here. But he did not need to see it. He had grown up in a city that lived and died by that span of iron and timber, and he knew its moods the way other men knew the weather. When the horns blew three-and-two, the bridge was dead. And when the bridge was dead, Vellmark was an island.

An island, he thought, standing at the window with his tea going cold in his hand, with an army on it.

Because that was the other thing. That was the thing everyone had been pretending not to count all autumn, the way you pretend not to count the days since a letter should have arrived. The armistice negotiations had stalled at Corven Ford in October, and the delegations had scattered to winter quarters within their own lines — except that Vellmark sat almost exactly on the line, close enough that the neutral commissioners had looked at the maps and said, why not here, the town has the Custom House, the town has the observatory, the town has walls and cellars and a bridge we can hold. The town has, thought Sable, the misfortune of being convenient.

The advance party had come through three weeks ago: forty riders in the slate-grey coats of the Provisional Authority, polite as tax assessors, billeted by rotation in houses whose owners were paid a modest indemnity and asked, very courteously, to make room. They had requisitioned the Custom House and half the guildhall. They had posted notices in three languages announcing the forthcoming presence of the Armistice Commission and requesting the citizenry's patience and cooperation. The notices used words like *provisional* and *temporary*, and Sable had noticed — because noticing was what he did, what he had trained himself to do instead of speaking — that none of them gave a date.

Now the horns told him why.

He opened the shop at nine because routine was a kind of ballast. Mrs. Halloway came for her mantel clock, which he had promised to regulate, and he adjusted the pendulum while she talked about the price of tallow and the insolence of the requisition officers, and he made the small sounds a man makes to show he is listening — *mm, yes, is that so* — without ever once saying anything that could later be repeated. This was the discipline of his life, honed over twelve years to a finish finer than any polish he put on brass: to be present without appearing, agreeable without committing, remembered by no one. His mother had taught him it without ever naming it. *Say little,* she'd say, brushing his hair before school in the last year they had together. *A boy who says little is a boy who lasts.* He had lasted. He was lasting still. Some mornings the lasting felt like a craft he had mastered so completely that it had swallowed him whole.

By eleven, the street outside had changed character. Not busier — quieter, but with the wrong kind of quiet, the quiet of held breath. People stood in knots at the corner by the chandler's, talking low and fast. A boy ran past with a handbill. Sable stepped out and took one from a nail where the crier had left a sheaf.

It was printed in Vellmark's own press, under the Authority's seal: **BY ORDER OF THE PROVISIONAL AUTHORITY — NOTICE TO THE INHABITANTS OF VELLMARK.** The ice having closed the Winter Bridge, and the season rendering the ford roads impassable, the Armistice Delegation of the Allied Powers would take up winter residence in the city pending resumption of negotiations. Reinforcements of the garrison would arrive by river barge as conditions permitted. All inhabitants were reminded of curfew provisions, billeting schedules, and the requirement that civil administration continue in orderly fashion. The word *orderly* appeared four times in one page.

At the bottom, in smaller type: **THE BRIDGE WILL REOPEN WHEN CONDITIONS PERMIT.**

*When conditions permit.* Sable read the phrase three times. It was a sentence with no teeth in it, no bones, nothing to hold onto. Conditions permitted when someone in authority decided they permitted. The river might freeze solid by solstice and stay frozen till March — the old men at the market were already saying it would be a hard one, the geese had gone south early, the beech mast was heavy. A hard winter meant a closed bridge for months. It meant Vellmark sealed like a jar, with him inside it.

And inside it also, arriving within days or weeks: the historians.

Everyone knew about the historians. Word had come down the river with the last open-water barge — that the Commission travelled with a corps of official chroniclers, men whose task was to assemble the record of the war as it would stand in the histories, district by district, town by town. There had been a list, someone said. A schedule of towns and the chapters allotted to each. Vellmark's chapter, the bargemen said, was already drafted.

Already drafted. Before the historians had seen the town. Before they had spoken to a single soul in it. Sable folded the notice into quarters and put it in his pocket, and did not examine too closely why his hands were not quite steady, why some animal part of him — some part he kept locked below the floorboards of himself, next to a night twelve years old and the smell of the tannery — had begun, very quietly, to pace.

* * *

He spent the afternoon doing what he always did when the world grew too loud: he worked small.

There was a repeater watch on the bench belonging to a merchant stranded — ironically, thought Sable, not bitterly, he didn't allow bitterness, it was an indulgence and indulgences made noise — on the far side of the river, a man who had crossed the bridge a fortnight ago to settle accounts in Corven and could not now come home. The watch's strike train was worn. Sable stripped it down to its wheels and laid them out in order on the baize, and lost himself in the geometry of it: the snail, the hammer, the gathering pallet. A repeater was a machine for making time audible in darkness. You pressed a button and the mechanism counted the hours aloud against your palm, so a man could know where he stood in the night without lighting a candle.

*A machine for knowing where you are in the dark.*

His mother had owned such a watch. She had sold it, near the end — along with everything else sellable — and Sable had never asked where the money went, because asking was a form of saying, and saying was forbidden. He had been fourteen. He was twenty-six now, and he could strip a movement blindfolded and had not spoken a full true sentence about the tannery to a living soul, and some nights he wound his own regulator clock and felt like a man keeping time in a locked room.

The shop bell rang at four. He looked up expecting Mrs. Halloway again, or a billeting officer, and instead found Herr Brandt standing in the doorway, stamping snow off his boots.

The keeper of the observatory clock came down from the hill perhaps twice a year, usually to buy oil or have a lens frame repaired, and he had never once, in all Sable's memory, made conversation that was not strictly necessary. He was a tall, stooped man with a face like a draughty corridor — nothing wasted in it, nothing warm either, or so it had always seemed. Today his cheeks were red from the climb down and his eyes moved around the shop quickly, cataloguing, before settling on Sable with an expression that was almost apologetic.

"Dravenmoor."

"Herr Brandt." Sable set down his tweezers. "You've come about the oil?"

"The oil can wait." Brandt glanced at the window, at the street, at the knots of people still standing at the corners. Then, lower: "You'll have heard the horns."

"Three-and-two."

"You know what follows."

"I imagine the whole city knows what follows." Sable kept his voice level, professional, a surface you could slide off. "The delegation winters here. The garrison doubles. We learn to say good morning in another language."

Brandt did not smile. He came further into the shop, uninvited, which was unlike him, and stood looking at the bench of laid-out wheels as if they were a text he was trying to read. "The master clock stopped," he said.

Sable waited. It was a trick of his trade, waiting — most people rushed to fill a silence, and in filling it, told you everything.

"It stopped the night before last," Brandt went on. "Dead. Not run-down — stopped, mid-swing, as if something had lodged in the train. I've kept that clock forty years and I have never known it to stop clean. And now—" he gestured vaguely toward the window, toward the river he couldn't see either, "—now I am to have forty clerks and three colonels and God knows what commission of historians sleeping a hundred paces from my door, and the one thing in that building that must not stop is stopped."

"What do you want of me?"

"I want you to come up the hill and look at it." Brandt turned and faced him fully, and for a moment something moved behind the draughty-corridor face — something old and tired and careful. "I'm told you're the best hands in Vellmark. I'm told you don't talk much. Both recommendations weigh equally with me, at present."

Sable looked at the old man and felt the first cold thread of something that was not quite fear and not quite refusal. The observatory. He had not been up the observatory hill in twelve years — not since the week after, when the whole town walked up for the memorial service and Sable had stood at the back with his mother's grip bruising his wrist, and the master clock had tolled the hour over their bowed heads, indifferent and magnificent, and he had thought: *it saw everything from up there. It sees the whole city, and it says nothing. Like me.*

"I'll need to see the movement before I promise anything," he said. Which was not a yes. Which was also, he knew already, not a no.

"Tomorrow, then. Come at first light, before the garrison's surveyors arrive." Brandt moved to the door, then paused with his hand on the latch. Outside, across the rooftops, the sky was going the color of pewter, and the wind had swung north, and the first flakes were coming down — fat, deliberate flakes, snow with intent behind it. "Dravenmoor. Do you understand what has happened today? Truly?"

"The bridge is closed."

"The bridge is closed," Brandt agreed. "Which means that whatever comes next comes to us, and whatever we do next, we do it here, with no road out and no one coming to save us or judge us or write it down but the ones already inside these walls." He said it flatly, a man reporting a measurement, but his eyes stayed on Sable a moment too long. "Mind how you spend the winter."

The bell clattered; the door shut; the old man's footsteps crunched away down the lane, growing fainter, and then the snow filled in the sound entirely.

Sable stood a long while in his shop among the ordered wheels and ticking cases. Through the window the snow thickened, erasing the street, the corners where people whispered, the world beyond the world. Somewhere out on the river, ice ground against ice with a sound like slow grinding teeth. The bridge was closed. The army was coming. The historians were coming with their pre-written chapters. And he — Sable Dravenmoor, who had spent twelve years perfecting the art of being unnoticed in a town of six thousand souls — was sealed inside with all of it, with no road out, until the thaw.

He should feel trapped. He told himself he felt trapped. But underneath the claustrophobia, underneath the dread that sat in his chest like a weight of wet sand, something else stirred — something he refused to name, refused even to look at directly, the way he had refused for twelve years to look directly at a certain night, a certain smell, a certain sound of wagons in the dark.

He closed the regulator case he'd been avoiding all afternoon. Its pendulum swung steady, sixty to the minute, faithful as ever.

*It sees everything,* he thought, remembering the clock on the hill, *and it says nothing.*

Tomorrow he would go up and open it, and find out why it had finally, after twelve years, stopped.

Outside, the snow fell without pause, sealing the city like wax seals a letter.
