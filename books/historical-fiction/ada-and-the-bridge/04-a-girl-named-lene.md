## 4. A Girl Named Lene

The ink in the records office was always cold.

Lene had learned this on her first morning, three months past, when she'd dipped her pen into the communal well by the window and found the fluid sluggish with winter's approach, dragging against the nib like something reluctant to be born onto the page. She had learned to warm the inkwell between her palms before the first entry of the day — a small ritual, invisible, hers alone. The occupiers' clerks thought her careful. Careful was acceptable in a copyist. Careful was even prized. No one looked twice at a girl who warmed her ink.

No one looked at Lene at all, and this was the great achievement of her life so far.

She sat at the third desk from the north wall of the Custom House annex, the one with the warped leg shimmed with folded paper, beneath a window that framed a sliver of the river and, when the light came right, the far abutment of the bridge. The bridge had been closed for six days now. Ice had taken it the way ice always took it, silently, at night, and in the morning the garrison horns had sounded their flat brass notes across the water and Vellmark had understood: no one in, no one out, and an army settling into its bones for the winter like a guest who intended to stay until spring made the furniture his own.

Lene copied documents. That was all she did, officially. The victors' administration ran on paper the way the town had once run on the river, and every order, requisition, casualty list, confiscation notice, and directive that passed through the office of the Provisional Commission existed first as a handwritten original and then as fair copies — two, sometimes three — produced by clerks like herself. Her hand was small and exact. She had been told so by Herr Fassbinder, the senior clerk, in the tone of a man complimenting a well-made chair. *Your letters do not wander, Fräulein Lene.* She had thanked him. She had not told him that her letters could not afford to wander; that precision was not a virtue but a discipline, the way prayer is a discipline, the way holding still over a wound is a discipline.

Because Lene memorized everything.

It had begun as instinct and hardened into method. Every document that crossed her desk passed through her eyes twice — once to copy it, once to keep it. She kept them the way a miser keeps coins, in the dark treasury behind her forehead: the requisition of four hundred blankets from the guildhall stores, dated the ninth; the order transferring custody of the harbor customs ledgers to the commission's archive, which meant out of the town's hands forever; the list of persons whose petitions for review would not be heard before midwinter. She kept names, dates, file numbers, the small bureaucratic music of conquest. At night, lying on the narrow bed in the room she rented from the widow Halm above the chandler's shop, she recited them back to herself in the dark like a litany, like a rosary, until sleep came. It was the only way she knew to hold what could not otherwise be held. Paper burned. Ink faded or was blotted. But a memory rehearsed nightly, entered into the ledger of a mind that had nothing left to lose —

She kept accounts. Her father had taught her that much, if he had taught her anything else too briefly.

Elias Voss. Court clerk of Vellmark. Dead twelve years this autumn.

She did not think of him in the daytime. Daytime was for work, and work was survival, and survival required a face like everyone else's face — mildly tired, mildly deferential, interested in wages and bread and the price of lamp oil. But some mornings the ink went cold in her hands anyway, and she had to warm it twice.

* * *

On the morning the casualty list came through, the frost had drawn ferns on the inside of the window glass, and the stove in the corner of the office smoked in the way it did when the wind came off the river from the east. There were nine copyists at work, seven of them local hires like Lene, two brought by the commission from the capital. The commission men sat nearer the stove. This was the natural order of things now and nobody remarked upon it.

Herr Fassbinder moved along the desks distributing the morning's work, a stack of originals squared neatly under one arm, his breath fogging faintly in the unheated middle distance of the room. He set a document before Lene without ceremony.

"Casualty reconciliation," he said. "Quarterly. Two copies, clean hand. The commission wants it bound before the delegation arrives from the capital."

"Bound for what?" asked Wendel, the boy at the next desk, who was seventeen and asked questions the way boys that age ask questions — to hear his own voice fill a room.

Fassbinder gave him the look reserved for such questions. "For history, boy. Everything goes into the record eventually." He moved on.

Lene drew the document toward her and broke the seal.

Casualty lists were among the worst of her daily diet, and she had developed a technique for them: she read the columns as pure typography, name-shapes and numeral-patterns, refusing for as long as possible to let the shapes become sounds and the sounds become people. It worked about half the time. The other half, some name snagged — a name that belonged to a face she'd seen at market, a schoolroom, a wedding — and then she copied that line slowly, giving it the dignity of deliberation, because speed felt like a second killing.

This list was long. The reprisals of the occupation's first months had filled it, and the skirmishes after, and the sickness that had gone through the eastern parishes when the wells were fouled. Column after column: surname, given name, age or estimated age, parish, cause of death where known, disposition of remains. Her pen moved. *Aldermann, Petra. Brenner, Josua. Colm, the brothers Ivo and Anselm.* The frost melted slowly on the glass. Somewhere below, in the yard of the Custom House, soldiers were stacking firewood with the rhythmic thud of axes splitting logs, and each split sounded, distantly, like something breaking.

She reached the section headed *Deaths Under Inquiry — Unresolved*, a category that meant, in the commission's careful language, deaths the administration acknowledged but declined yet to explain. And there, midway down the page, in the original clerk's hurried hand:

*Vos, Elias. Court functionary (former). Cause: fall. Disposition: municipal ground.*

Lene's pen stopped.

The nib rested on the page and a bead of ink swelled at its point, round and black and patient, threatening to bloom. She watched it the way one watches a held breath.

*Vos.* Not *Voss.* One *s* missing. Her father's name, spelled wrong, in the official account of his death, which said *fall* — a word doing more work than any word should have to do — and *municipal ground*, which meant an unmarked plot in the paupers' section behind the Church of Saint Aldric, where the gravedigger worked fast in cold weather and the stones were shared three to a family.

She had known, of course. She had stood, at fourteen, at the edge of that ground in a coat too thin for November, while a priest who could not look at her said the words quickly. She had known the list would come eventually; every death went into the record; that was the whole nature of records, the whole terrible faithfulness of bureaucracy, which forgot nothing and understood less. Still, seeing it — seeing his name reduced to a misprint in a column of the dead, filed under a cause that was a lie so lazy it didn't even bother to be elaborate —

The ink bead trembled at her nib.

Around her the office went on: pens scratching, Wendel coughing, the stove ticking as the iron expanded, Fassbinder somewhere behind her muttering over an inventory. The world did not pause for grief; it never had; it had not paused twelve years ago either, when the news had come to their apartment and her mother had gone very still and then very busy, because stillness invited questions and questions were dangerous, and within a month they had left Vellmark ahead of whatever came next, and within a year her mother had died of the leaving, quietly, in a rented room in a city whose name Lene had spent years training her mouth not to say.

Her mother's last instruction had been simple, delivered in the flattened voice of someone conserving strength for dying rather than talking: *Be no one, Ada. Be so plain the world walks past you.* And so she had become Lene — Lene Marrow, orphaned copyist, letters that do not wander — and she had been no one so successfully that here, forty paces from the commission's own archive, no one had ever once looked at her twice.

But her father had believed the opposite of disappearing. Her father had believed in ink. *A thing written truly cannot be unwritten,* he used to say, inking his fingers to the knuckle over court depositions while she sat on the floor beside his desk drawing letters of her own. *They can burn it, they can ban it, but somewhere it exists, and existence is a kind of victory.* He had been a keeper of accounts. He had died, according to this document, of a *fall*.

Lene lifted her pen from the page. The ink bead sank back down the nib, unspent. Small mercies.

Then she did what she had trained herself to do, what three months of this office had honed into reflex: she corrected nothing. She copied the line exactly as it stood — *Vos, Elias. Court functionary (former). Cause: fall. Disposition: municipal ground* — reproducing the error faithfully, letter for letter, because a copyist who emended originals was a copyist who got noticed, and being noticed was death, literal death, the kind her father had suffered. Her hand did not shake. She had made sure of that years ago, practicing at night, copying her father's old letters until her wrist learned steadiness as a form of love.

But as she wrote the false spelling, she did something else, something so small it would have been invisible to anyone watching, which no one was: she formed the letters of *Vos* with exactly the same care she gave every other name on the page. Not faster. Not slower. Not smaller. Because the error was theirs, and she would carry it faithfully — but she would carry it *exactly*, and she would remember it, and one day there would be a record that spelled the name rightly, and she intended to be alive to see it entered.

Grief, she had found, could be managed the way ink was managed: kept warm, kept fluid, kept from blooming where it would ruin the page. You held it in the instrument. You did not let it spill.

She finished the list. Two copies, clean hand. She sanded them lightly, stacked them square, and set them in the out-tray. Then she pulled the next document toward her and began again.

* * *

At the midday break the copyists were permitted forty minutes, and most of them took it in the yard or the corridor, where a woman sold hot barley broth from a cart. Lene usually took her forty minutes at her desk, eating bread she'd brought, because the yard was where garrison soldiers congregated and soldiers talked to girls, and being talked to was a risk she did not take.

That day, however, Herr Fassbinder detained her.

"A moment, Fräulein Lene." He stood beside her desk with a ledger under his arm and an expression she had learned to read carefully, because Fassbinder's expressions were the closest thing the office had to weather reports. "You have a good memory."

"Thank you, sir."

"It wasn't a compliment. It was an assignment." He set the ledger down — a tall volume bound in gray board, its spine stamped with the double-headed cipher of the Provisional Commission. "The historians arrive within the week. From the capital. Kessler's people. They'll want access to everything, and everything is not in order, because the war was not orderly." He tapped the ledger's cover. "Disposal register. Every document slated for destruction passes through this book before it burns — date, description, authorizing signature. It has fallen behind. Six weeks behind. You will bring it current."

Lene felt something turn over inside her chest, slow and heavy, like ice shifting on the river.

"Destruction, sir?"

Fassbinder shrugged, a small economical gesture that conveyed the entire moral architecture of a career civil servant serving whichever government occupied his building. "Paper accumulates. The commission keeps what it needs and disposes of what it doesn't. Redundant drafts, superseded orders, material of no administrative value." He was already turning away. "Current by Friday. The register stays at your desk. Do not remove it from the office."

"No, sir," said Lene, to his back.

He paused, half-turned. For an instant something flickered in his face — not kindness, she decided afterward, but perhaps its distant cousin, the recognition one tired man extends to another tired person caught in the same machine. "Copyists who are accurate," he said, "are never blamed for what they copy. Remember that. It is useful advice in any regime." Then he was gone toward the stove, and the commission men made room for him, and the ordinary machinery of the afternoon resumed.

Lene opened the disposal register.

Six weeks of backlog lay in its empty ruled pages, waiting for her hand. And she understood, sitting very still in the cold center of the room, that Fassbinder had just handed her the one document in the entire occupation that mattered more than all the others — because a destruction register was a map of erasure. Every entry she would make described something about to stop existing. Date, description, authorizing signature: the full anatomy of each disappearance, recorded by the destroyer's own hand, in the destroyer's own book, and delivered into the keeping of the last person anyone would ever suspect of caring.

They thought they were giving her drudgery. They were giving her the index of everything they intended the future never to know.

She took up her pen. She warmed the inkwell between her palms first, though the ink was not cold, because the ritual steadied her, and she needed steadiness for what came next, which was this: she would enter each doomed document into the register in her small exact hand — and she would *keep* each entry, twice over, the copy and the memory, filing them in the treasury behind her forehead alongside the requisitions and the casualty lists and the misspelled name of her father.

*A thing written truly cannot be unwritten.* Even if only one person alive had read it truly.

By Friday the register was current. By Friday night, reciting entries into the dark above the chandler's shop, Lene knew the shape of the commission's amnesia better than the commission itself did: which orders had been duplicated and destroyed, which interrogations had been transcribed and burned, which months of the tannery district's correspondence had simply — vanished, the register noting their disposal with the bland efficiency of a hand that did not understand what it was signing away.

Or did understand. That was the question that kept her awake past the litany, staring at the ceiling beams. Did they know what they burned? Was it carelessness, or design? Because if it was design — if somewhere in the capital a narrative was already being drafted, smooth and exculpatory, with blank spaces where certain weeks of Vellmark's history should have been —

She turned over. Through the floorboards came the smell of the chandler's wax, honey-sweet, and beyond the window the frozen bridge held its long white silence across the river, closed, impassable, a door shut for the winter.

Three days later, a courier in commission gray came up the stairs of the Custom House carrying dispatches, and among the papers that crossed Lene's desk that morning was a travel authorization, routine, one line among dozens, which she copied in her flawless hand and kept in her memory forever:

*Dr. Edmund Kessler and party, Commission Historical Section, authorized passage to Vellmark. Purpose: preparation of the definitive account of the occupation period. All local records placed at the Section's disposal.*

All local records. Placed at their disposal.

Lene finished the line, dotted the final letter, and sat for a moment with her hands folded, feeling the cold ink-well warmth fade from her palms. They were coming to write the book. And she was the girl who memorized what was destroyed, sitting forty paces from the archive, wearing a name that was not hers, holding — entry by entry, night by night — the counter-account, unwritten, unprovable, kept nowhere but in the dark behind her eyes.

It would not be enough. She had known it would not be enough for twelve years. Memory needed paper; testimony needed witnesses; truth needed a body, a vessel, something that could survive fire and scrutiny and time. Her father had understood that. Her father had died believing it.

Somewhere in this city, she thought — watching the frost ferns on the glass, listening to the distant axe-thuds in the yard — somewhere in Vellmark, if he had been the man she remembered, her father would have hidden something. He would not have trusted the courts, or the church, or the neighbors. He would have trusted what he had trusted all his life: the patient, faithful, mechanical keeping of a record.

She looked out the window, past the yard, past the smoking chimneys of the lower town, up to where the observatory hill rose black against the pale sky, crowned with its dome, and beneath the dome the tower, and in the tower the great master clock that had not run, the children still said, since the year her father died.

The clock had stopped the night Elias Voss fell.

Lene had heard that story all her life, in whispers, in the way of things townsfolk say without knowing whom they're saying them to. She had never let herself believe it meant anything. Coincidence, grief makes patterns, the dead leave coincidences everywhere like dropped buttons.

But now she was no one, and invisible, and the historians were coming, and the clock had been silent for twelve years, and her father had believed that a thing written truly cannot be unwritten.

She bent back over her work, letters that do not wander, face like every other face. But that evening, when the office emptied and the lamps were trimmed, Lene Marrow lingered at the window a moment longer than a copyist should, looking at the dark tower on the hill, and began — carefully, precisely, in the ledger behind her forehead — to plan how a girl named no one might find out what the clock kept.
