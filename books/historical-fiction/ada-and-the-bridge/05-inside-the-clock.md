## 5. Inside the Clock

The master clock of Vellmark Observatory had not been built so much as grown — that was Sable's first thought as he stood beneath it in the cold dome of the clock room, craning his neck at the great wooden case that rose from floor to ceiling like the trunk of some iron-hearted tree. It had been assembled over forty years by men who had each added their own amendments: a new escapement here, a rewound barrel there, brass patches soldered over old mistakes, a pendulum rod replaced in 1811 with one half an inch too long and then corrected by a compensating jar of mercury that no one had ever fully explained to his satisfaction. The clock was a palimpsest. Every generation of keepers had written on it, and none had erased what came before.

He had been in this room once before, twelve years ago, and he had promised himself he would never stand in it again.

Sable set down his tool chest on the flagstones with a click that echoed strangely in the round stone chamber, and told himself, for perhaps the hundredth time since signing Brandt's commission paper, that this was only work. A stopped clock. Worn pallets, probably, or a broken suspension spring, or ice in the weight channels — the observatory was poorly heated, and cold thickened oil until it turned to glue. There was no mystery in it. Machines failed for ordinary reasons. He would find the ordinary reason, fix it, take his fee, and go home to his shop on Tanner's Lane — *not* Tanner's Lane, he corrected himself; they had renamed it after the war, though everyone still called it Tanner's Lane, the way a tongue returns to a lost tooth — and he would say nothing to anyone, because saying nothing was what he did, and he was very good at it.

Twelve years of being very good at it.

He unrolled his leather apron, tied it at the back, and began.

* * *

The case opened in three sections. The lower doors swung wide on hinges still true, revealing the weights — two long lead cylinders, each the size of a man's leg, hanging slack on their cables like hanged things. Sable checked the cables first, running them through gloved fingers inch by inch, feeling for the fraying that would explain everything simply. They were sound. Of course they were sound. Herr Brandt maintained this clock the way a priest maintains a relic; the man had all but slept in this room since the war, if the dust patterns were anything to judge by. The floor around the case was swept in a careful circle. Everywhere else, the grime of years lay undisturbed, gray and even as felt. But within reach of a standing man's arms, the wood gleamed faintly where hands had polished it, over and over, across a decade of nights.

Sable noted this and put it away with the rest of what he noticed and did not say.

He climbed the ladder bolted inside the case and opened the middle section, where the going train lived. Here the smell changed — machine oil and cold metal and, beneath it, something older, something like the inside of a church. The wheels rose above him in their dark hierarchy, the great barrel with its chain wound tight, the intermediate wheels with their cut teeth, each tooth shaped by hand in an age when hand-shaping was all there was. He held his lantern up. Light moved across the brass like water.

The train was intact. He could see that before he touched anything — the wheels seated properly on their arbors, the pinions engaged, no tooth missing, nothing jammed. Which meant the failure, whatever it was, lay higher up, in the escapement, or lower, in the strike work, and neither of those places should have stopped this clock. This clock was built with the redundancy of a fortress. It had been designed, sixty years ago, by a horologist named Aldous Kreyn who had reportedly said he wished his mechanism to outlast the city that paid for it.

Kreyn had gotten his wish so far. Something else had stopped it.

Sable worked upward rung by rung, and the room below him fell away into shadow, and the silence of the tower gathered around him like water rising. It was a particular silence, this one. Vellmark was full of silences — he had catalogued them for twelve years the way other men collected stamps or grievances. There was the silence of Tanner's Lane, which every household kept, a silence with a shape to it, a hole in the middle where six families used to be. There was the silence of his mother's kitchen in her last year, when she spoke only of bread and weather and never, ever of March. There was the silence of the guild hall, where the masters' portraits hung and no plaque mentioned what any of them had signed. And there was this silence — the observatory's — which was different from all the others, because it was not empty. It was a silence with something *inside* it. He had felt it even as a boy of eleven, standing where he stood now, and he had felt it again two days ago when Brandt met him at the door and said, *It stopped the night Elias Voss died,* and watched him with eyes that were not asking a question but waiting for an answer Sable had refused to give.

He reached the escapement housing.

* * *

The deadbeat escapement sat at the top of the train like a small crowned head, its anchor-shaped pallets poised over the escape wheel, governing the whole descent of the weights, tick by tick, the way a tollkeeper governs a road. In a healthy clock it was the most alive part of the machine — always moving, always releasing, always catching. In a stopped clock it was just metal holding its breath.

Sable got his shoulder against the housing panel and began to loosen the retaining screws.

They came free too easily.

That was the second wrong thing, and he paused with the screwdriver still seated in the last screw, listening to his own heartbeat in the closed dark of the case. Screws in a clock that had been running for decades seized. Corrosion crept into the threads, oil hardened to varnish, and a horologist expected to fight for every turn, to feel the resistance of years. These screws turned as if they had been removed recently — weeks ago, months at most — cleaned, greased, and refitted. Someone had been inside this housing. Recently. Repeatedly.

Brandt, he thought. The keeper oils what he can reach.

But Brandt was seventy-three years old and had a tremor in both hands, and these screws had been refitted by someone whose grip was steady, whose turns were even, who had finished each one with exactly the same quarter-turn past snug. Sable knew that signature. Every craftsman left one. His own screws went a half-turn past snug, always had, a habit his master had beaten into him at fourteen. Whoever had closed this housing had a lighter touch than Brandt and more discipline than most guild masters.

He lifted the panel free and set it across the ladder rails, and looked into the heart of the escapement.

* * *

For a long moment he saw nothing wrong at all. The anchor pivots were clean. The pallet faces showed fresh polish, correctly angled. The suspension spring — ah, there, the spring was cracked, but only recently, a fatigue fracture, honest wear, the kind of failure that stopped clocks all over the world every winter. He almost laughed. An ordinary reason after all. He could have a new spring cut and fitted in two days; the commission would be simple; he would be gone from this room by week's end.

Then he saw the wire.

It ran from the back of the escapement frame — from a small bracket screwed to the inside face of the housing where no bracket needed to be — down along the frame's edge, secured at intervals with waxed thread, disappearing behind the escape wheel into the darkness between the movement and the case wall. It was fine wire, copper, the kind used for lacing bellows and binding coil springs. It had no mechanical purpose whatsoever. Nothing in a clock's design called for copper wire laced along an escapement frame with waxed thread.

Sable crouched on the ladder platform and brought the lantern close.

The bracket held a cylinder.

It was brass, sealed at both ends with threaded caps, about the length of his forearm and thick as his wrist — the kind of container a surveyor used for map rolls, or a chemist for reagents, or a man for documents he wanted to survive fire and flood. It had been wired into the escapement frame with a deliberateness that made his stomach turn slowly over: not hidden *near* the clock, not tucked into the case, but *integrated* — laced into the movement itself, part of the machine, riding every vibration, every tick, held in the one place in Vellmark that a man like Herr Brandt could be trusted to guard with his life and that no soldier, no clerk, no historian of the victors would ever think to open, because who dismantles a working escapement?

Except the clock had stopped. Twelve years ago. The night Elias Voss died.

And the escapement spring was newly cracked, and the screws were recently turned, and the wire was waxed and snug, and Sable understood, with the slow cold certainty of a man watching ice close over a bridge, that the cylinder had been placed here *after* the stopping. That the stopping itself might have been arranged — a spring weakened, a pallet adjusted, a clock killed deliberately, made into a tomb, made into a reason to summon repairmen year after year until the right repairman came.

His hands were shaking. He looked at them with detached interest, the way he'd once looked at a shattered fusee spring, and waited for them to steady, and they did not, and so he gripped the ladder rail with one and extended the other, finger by finger, toward the cylinder.

* * *

The caps unscrewed stiffly, then freely. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone brittle with age, was paper. A sheaf of papers, folded once, packed tight, and beneath them a smaller bundle — loose sheets, older, foxed at the edges, covered in a cramped legal hand he recognized without knowing why until he read the first line.

*Testimony concerning events at the Vellmark tannery, the night of 9–10 March.*

The tannery.

The word went through him like a gear tooth through a thumb. He sat down on the platform without deciding to, the lantern trembling in his left hand so that shadows wheeled around the case walls, and he made himself unfold the first sheet under the light.

Elias Voss's hand. He knew it now — the court clerk's writing, the famous precise italic of the man who had recorded half the city's official life for thirty years, the hand on every deed and writ and inquest Sable had ever seen pinned to the guild board. But this was not public record. This was ledger-work: columns of names, dates, sums. Payments received. Payments made. Signatures described in detail. And entries — dozens of them, dated across the winter of the occupation — beginning with the words *Witnessed* or *Reported to me* or *I saw.*

*I saw wagons enter the tannery yard at the third hour after midnight. I saw them leave empty before dawn. I record the names of the drivers as given me by the night watchman, who has since fled the city.*

Page by page. Entry by entry. The massacre rendered in a clerk's immaculate hand, every atrocity given its column and its figure, the dead listed where they could be counted and marked *number uncertain, not fewer than* where they could not. And then — Sable's breath stopped — near the end of the sheaf, a different kind of entry. Not testimony gathered. Testimony *witnessed directly*, and the witness named:

*A boy observed the courtyard from the roof of the cooperage adjoining. Approximately eleven years of age, son of the widow Dravenmoor of Tanner's Lane. I interviewed the child on 14 March. His account corroborates the watchman's in every particular. He recalls the number of wagons (four), the direction of departure (river gate), and a detail no adult witness has offered: that one wagon returned for a second load before dawn. The child is frightened and will not speak publicly. I have recorded his statement separately and sealed it. His mother has asked — reasonably — that his name not appear. I accede, but note for the future record that the boy writes a fair hand for his age and signed my interview book himself, in my presence, at my request.*

Signed himself. Signed his own name, twelve years ago, in Elias Voss's interview book, in his careful apprentice's letters, because a kind man with a clerk's faith in records had knelt down to his level and said, *If you write it, it counts. If it counts, it cannot be undone.* And he had believed it. Eleven years old, and he had believed that ink was stronger than fear.

His mother had spent the next year teaching him otherwise.

Sable became aware that he was reading his own name — his full name, *Sable Dravenmoor*, set down in Elias Voss's hand inside a sealed cylinder inside a clock — and that his childhood handwriting was described in the margin in Voss's neat annotation: *writes a fair hand; letters well-formed; the boy should be apprenticed to a scrivener or a maker of instruments.* They had made him an instrument-maker. Or nearly. A clockmaker. A keeper of small gears and honest measures, who had spent twelve years measuring time for a town that measured nothing, that wanted its March erased, and now the victors' historians had come up the river to do the erasing properly, with prefaces and citations and clean blank margins.

And here it all was. Lodged in the machinery. The past, physically wound into the escapement of the master clock, ticking behind it — no, not ticking, the clock was dead, the clock had been murdered the same season as its author, and the truth had sat in the dark for twelve years waiting for hands steady enough and cowardly enough and skilled enough to open the case.

*Waiting for you,* said a voice in his head that sounded unpleasantly like Herr Brandt's. *That's why you're here. That's why now.*

He should reseal it. That was the sane course. Screw the caps down, lace the wire back, replace the panel, fit a new suspension spring, let the clock run another twelve years, take his fee, forget — he was good at forgetting, he had a journeyman's certificate in it —

But his hands, which had finally steadied, were already folding the pages back along their original creases, already wrapping them in the oilcloth with a care that had nothing to do with sanity and everything to do with the fact that a document like this deserved gentleness, that Elias Voss had died — *had been killed*, the ledger said as much in its final entries, threats noted, a warning about a man named Kessler's predecessor, a last entry dated four days before the death: *I believe I am followed. If anything happens to me, the record is in the place we agreed* — and dead men's papers deserved better than to be handled like contraband.

Though they were contraband. Though the moment he carried them out of this tower he became a smuggler of the worst possible cargo, in a town full of garrison patrols, with the river frozen and the bridge shut and nowhere to run.

Sable climbed down the ladder with the cylinder strapped flat against his chest beneath his coat, feeling its weight the way a man feels a broken rib. At the bottom he stood a while in the shadow of the dead clock, listening to the wind move around the dome overhead.

He would read it all tonight, every page. And then he would decide — tomorrow, tomorrow — whether he was still a man who said nothing.

But even as he latched his tool chest, he caught himself doing what he always did with mechanisms: counting the parts, weighing the tolerances, asking how it could be made to work. A record needs authentication. A record needs a witness. A record, to count, must enter the account — and somewhere in this occupied, frozen, silenced city, there had to be a way.

Above him, in the dark of the case, the empty bracket waited where the truth had lived, and Sable Dravenmoor carried it out into the gray morning like a man carrying a live coal in cupped hands, knowing that sooner or later, he would have to set it down somewhere — or be burned by it.
