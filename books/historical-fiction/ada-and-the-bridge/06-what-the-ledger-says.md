## 6. What the Ledger Says

The candle had burned down to the last inch of its first hour when Sable finally worked the brass cylinder free of the clock's housing.

He had told himself he would wait. That he would set it on Herr Brandt's workbench, cover it with a cloth, and sleep — or lie awake, which amounted to nearly the same thing in this tower — and open it in daylight like a reasonable man, a craftsman examining an unfamiliar mechanism. But daylight was twelve hours away and winter beyond the windows, and the cylinder sat in his palms warm from the clock's interior, heavier than brass had any right to be, and his hands were already moving before his mind finished arguing.

It was not locked. That surprised him most. Twelve years sealed inside the escapement housing of the master clock, wired into the very heart of Vellmark's timekeeping, and Elias Voss had trusted it to no lock at all — only to the assumption that no one would ever look. A clerk's faith: that records are safe so long as no one reads them, because no one ever does.

Sable turned it under the candlelight. The cylinder was the kind used for shipping surveyor's notes upriver, watertight, threaded at both ends, but this one had been modified — the seam soldered over and then carefully filed back down, invisible unless you knew to feel for it, which Sable did now, running his thumbnail along the metal the way he would feel for a hairline crack in a balance wheel. Someone had wanted it found eventually. Not easily. But eventually.

* * *

The solder gave way to his smallest iron and a patience he did not know he still possessed. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff with age, was a ledger.

Not a book, exactly. A sheaf of folios sewn together with waxed thread in the manner clerks bound their working copies — Elias Voss's manner; Sable recognized the stitching from the municipal documents his mother had once carried home to copy by firelight, back when copying other people's words had seemed an honest trade. The paper was good stock, rag-laid, chosen to outlast its author. On the first leaf, in a hand that started controlled and grew steadily more compressed as though the writer were running out of room or courage or both:

*A true account of certain transactions in the city of Vellmark, entered against the day when entry becomes possible.*

Below that, a date. Sable knew the date the way he knew his own hands. It was nine days after the tannery.

He read the first entry standing up. He read the second sitting down, because his legs informed him they could no longer be trusted. By the third he had drawn Brandt's stool to the candle and hunched over the folios like a man warming himself at a fire that gave no heat.

*Entry the first.* A name Sable did not know — Aldous Kremp, tanner — followed by a figure, forty-one souls sheltering in the curing sheds on the night of the twelfth. Then a second name, and here the ledger changed character entirely, because this name came with a title: *Magistrate Corven Hale, who signed the order of requisition, ref. Municipal Seal 114-C.*

Sable had spent twelve years not thinking about the number 114-C. He had seen it stamped on grain shipments, on timber warrants, on the broadsheet notices pasted to the customs house announcing curfew hours. It was everywhere in Vellmark, the way watermarks are everywhere in paper — visible only if you look, invisible if you don't, and Sable had made a profession, indeed a life, of not looking.

The ledger looked. Entry after entry, in Voss's tightening hand: dates, sums, signatures. The requisition order for the tannery district, signed by Hale, countersigned by Deputy Provost Emmerich Vahl — *who received payment of 300 crowns through the office of* — and there the ink was smudged, as though Voss had written fast and blotting could not keep pace with what he knew. Payments routed through a shipping concern whose owner's name appeared four entries later in connection with the movement of wagons on the night itself. Wagons. Sable's stomach clenched around the word. He had been eleven years old, crouched behind a stack of tanbark barrels across the lane, and what he remembered first was not the fire and not the screaming but the wagons — six of them, drawn up in the yard with their beds empty, waiting, as though the night were a delivery scheduled in advance. As though someone had done the arithmetic beforehand and known how many wagons forty-one people would require.

The ledger had done the same arithmetic. There it was, itemized: *Six wagons, hired of Brenner & Sons, half paid in advance, balance settled the following morning by draft drawn on the municipal contingency fund, authorized under seal 114-C.*

Sable put the candle down because his hand was shaking and he did not want to spill wax on a dead man's testimony. He had carried the memory of that night the way one carries a splinter worked too deep to draw — not pain exactly, after the first years, but a pressure, a knowledge of something foreign lodged in the flesh. He had never once tried to look at it directly. His mother had taught him otherwise: *You saw nothing, Sable. You were nowhere. If anyone asks, you were home sick.* And he had obeyed her so thoroughly that obedience had become amnesia, amnesia become temperament, temperament become the whole architecture of a man who crossed streets to avoid being noticed and took work in towers because towers stood apart from the world.

Now the ledger held his memory up in front of him, transcribed, cross-referenced, and initialed, and he found he could not look away, because it was not his memory alone anymore. It was evidence. It had witnesses' names attached to it. It had numbers.

*Forty-one souls.* Voss had written it twice more in subsequent entries, as if testing whether repetition might make the figure less unbearable, and each time it came back larger in implication: forty-one, and then the breakdown, families separated into columns with a bookkeeper's merciless clarity. Eleven men. Fourteen women. Sixteen children.

Sixteen children. Sable had been a child himself that night, hiding among barrels that smelled of oak gall and lime, watching lamplight move across the yard, listening to a voice he had later spent years persuading himself he'd imagined — a voice reading names aloud, unhurried, almost gentle, checking them against a list. He had thought, in his childish way, that it must be some kind of roll call, some official counting, because officials counted things, and counting was orderly, and orderly things could not be what the sounds coming from the curing sheds said they were.

The ledger confirmed the voice. *The list was read by Deputy Provost Vahl personally,* Voss had written. *I have seen the list. It is in his own hand. He kept it, I believe, as assurance against future dispute — a record of his own thoroughness.*

A man who kept receipts. Sable understood this instinct intimately; it was the instinct of every craftsman, every clerk, every keeper of accounts. You wrote things down because memory failed and disputes arose. Vahl had written down forty-one names so that no one could later claim he had missed one.

And somewhere — this was the thought that made Sable stand again and walk to the window and press his forehead against the frozen glass until it ached — somewhere that list might still exist. In an office. In a file. Possibly in a desk drawer belonging to a man who, according to the final pages of this first gathering of folios, had not fallen with the old regime at all.

Voss had noted it with a clerk's flat precision that somehow conveyed more horror than any condemnation could have: *Emmerich Vahl has transferred his services to the victors' administration. I am given to understand he now supervises the reconciliation of local records with the provisional government's archives. The man is thus charged with organizing the very documents that would convict him. I do not doubt he will prove efficient.*

Efficient. Twelve years later, the word had proven prophetic. The victors were in the city now, wintering behind closed ice, and their historians were arriving with their clean narratives, and the man responsible for reconciling the records was the man who had read the names aloud by lamplight while a boy hid behind barrels and learned that silence was the only country that would have him.

* * *

The candle gutted. Sable trimmed the wick with his fingernail and kept reading, because stopping had become impossible — the way a clock, once weighted, cannot be unweighted, only run down or stopped altogether, and he refused to stop.

The middle entries were the worst, worse even than the tannery, because the tannery he had known about — had *been*, in the awful sense that his body was part of its evidence — whereas these were revelations, each one a small door opening onto a corridor of further doors. A payment to the harbor master for the quiet disposal of a barge manifest. An instruction to the parish register office concerning the reclassification of deaths. *Enter as fever,* Voss had recorded, quoting the instruction verbatim, and beneath it, in his own voice breaking through the clerk's neutrality like bone through skin: *There was no fever. I attended two of the burial pits myself, in my capacity recording vital statistics. I count it the great failure of my life that I recorded what I was instructed to record, and kept the truth only here.*

Only here. Wired into a clock. In a tower where no one came except an old keeper and, once in twelve years, an apprentice summoned to fix what had stopped.

Sable looked up at the master clock's case, looming in the dark above the workbench, its pendulum hanging still, and felt the full shape of what Voss had done. The man had taken the town's public time — the time by which the docks loaded, the markets opened, the curfews rang — and hidden inside its beating heart the account of what that time had actually contained. Every hour Vellmark had measured since had been measured over a buried testimony. The clock had stopped the night Voss died, Brandt had said, as though grief could stop a train of gears. Now Sable wondered if it was simpler and stranger than that: perhaps the clock had simply refused, in whatever way mechanisms refuse, to go on keeping time for a town that kept its secrets.

No. He shook his head at himself. Clocks did not refuse. Clocks stopped when something stopped them, and he was here to find what, and meanwhile a murdered clerk lay in the ground with his truth soldered into brass, and the truth named living men.

Because that was the thing, the thing that turned the reading from grief into something colder and more purposeful. The ledger was not a lament. It was an indictment, constructed with a lawyer's care by a man who had known he might never see a courtroom. Every entry named its principals. Every payment traced its route. Voss had built the document the way Sable built a movement — every wheel meshing with the next, tolerances exact, nothing decorative, nothing wasted — so that if any single gear of it were examined, the whole train would necessarily turn.

And three of the names, Sable realized with a slow cold settling in his chest, were names he had seen recently, freshly, in the broadsheets posted since the garrison arrived. Names attached to the victors' provisional administration. Men who had signed off on the tannery now serving the historians and archivists who had come to write Vellmark's chapter — writing it, if Ada— if the rumors in the lower town held any truth, exculpatory and blank where the curing sheds should burn in the record.

They would write around it. They would reconcile the records. And the one document that could stop them had spent twelve years inside a stopped clock, waiting for a coward to find it.

Sable caught at the word even as he thought it, flinching from it, and then, in the honesty of the small hours, declined to flinch. Coward was accurate. He had been coached into silence by a terrified loving woman and he had honored her bargain past her death, past reason, past the point where silence protected anything but his own comfort. He had crossed to this district telling himself it was only work. He had opened the cylinder tonight only because his hands betrayed him into curiosity. Everything he had done for twelve years had been designed to ensure that Sable Dravenmoor would never have to be anyone, anywhere, at any cost.

And the ledger in front of him listed sixteen children, and one of the surviving children of that district — not of the sheds, but of the lanes around them, another child who had heard things through walls — might well be grown now, and walking somewhere in this occupied city under a name that was not her father's, keeping herself alive by precision, the way he kept himself alive by invisibility.

He didn't know why that thought came to him, or where from. Perhaps from Voss's preface, which mentioned a daughter only once, in a line Sable had read past without absorbing and which now returned to him with the force of a struck bell: *If this account survives me, let it be said that my daughter knew nothing of it, and must be left knowing nothing, for her safety lies in her ignorance.*

Left knowing nothing. Living under some invented name. Somewhere in the ranks of the occupation's clerks, copying documents, correcting nothing.

Sable sat back. The candle had drowned in its own wax; he did not relight it. In the dark the clock's case stood over him like a second keeper, and inside it, where the brass cylinder had lived for twelve years, there was now a gap — a small absence in the machinery that he would have to fill with something, because a clock with a gap tells false time, and he had come here, whatever else he told himself, to make the clock tell true.

He thought about the gap all night. By the grey beginning of morning he had admitted what it meant: he could not put the ledger back. Whatever he decided to do — carry it to Brandt, carry it to the authorities, bury it in his mother's grave, burn it and let Voss's careful gears rust unworn — he could not return it to the escapement and pretend the finding had not happened. Knowledge, once entered into a man, ran forward like a train of wheels. You could stop it. You could not unwind it.

Down in the town, faint through the frost-furred glass, he heard the garrison horns sound the change of watch. Winter had closed the bridge. The victors were settling in. Somewhere in their offices a historian was sharpening a narrative, and a deputy provost was reconciling records, and a girl with a false name was bending over a copying desk, memorizing which papers went to the fire.

And in the observatory tower, an apprentice clockmaker sat in the dark holding a dead man's testimony against his chest, understanding at last that the commission he had accepted was never the clock.

The clock, he now suspected, had been the summons.

Outside, far below, on the white road along the river, a small figure was climbing toward the observatory through the snow — though Sable, watching the dawn come up grey over Vellmark, could not yet know who it was, or what citation had sent her, or that her father's handwriting was pressed flat against his heartbeat inside his coat.
