## 8. The Keeper's Bargain

Herr Brandt had not slept in the observatory for eleven years, but he had never truly stopped keeping watch there.

He woke before dawn in the narrow room behind the instrument hall, as he did every morning, and lay still beneath the wool blanket, listening. The tower spoke to him in the language of settling timber and cooling brass. Above his head, through two floors and the great dome, the master clock hung silent in its case, and its silence had a particular quality now — a silence with a weight inside it, like a sealed room, like a held breath. He had lived with that weight so long he sometimes forgot it was not the natural condition of the world. Then a morning like this one would arrive, and he would remember, and the remembering sat on his chest until he rose.

Outside, the river had gone quiet in the way it did when the ice was thickening. He could hear the garrison horns from the far bank, thin and clear in the frozen air, announcing the change of watch. The occupiers had settled into the custom house and the barracks like guests who had decided the house was theirs, and the town had folded itself small around them, the way a body folds around a wound.

Brandt dressed by candlelight. His hands, which had once been steady enough to adjust a meridian circle to the width of a hair, shook a little now in the cold. Age, he told himself. Only age. He was seventy-one years old and he had been telling himself *only age* for twelve years, and the two lies had grown comfortable together, like old men on a bench.

He climbed the stairs.

* * *

The clock room smelled of oil and dust and, faintly, of the cedar lining of the instrument cases — a smell that had become, over the decades, the smell of his whole life. He had come to this observatory at nineteen as an assistant computer, calculating ephemerides by hand, and he had never left. The city below had gone through its plagues and its prosperities and its terrible year, and the tower had gone on measuring the sky above all of it, indifferent, exact. He had loved it for that indifference once. He had come to hate it.

He unlocked the clock case with the small brass key he kept on a cord around his neck, beneath his shirt, where it had lived for twelve years. The key had worn a groove in his sternum. He was aware of it the way a man is aware of a scar — not as pain, exactly, but as a fact of his geography.

The master clock stood taller than a man, its case of black walnut, its face of white enamel gone faintly yellow at the edges. It had stopped at eleven minutes past four in the morning on the fourteenth of October, twelve years ago, and Brandt had let it stand stopped ever since. The town believed the clock had simply died of old age, as clocks do, as men do. The town believed a great many convenient things.

He opened the case and looked, as he looked every morning, at the escapement. At the small brass cylinder wired into the works with fuse silk, tucked into the space where a maintaining power spring should have been. It had not moved. It had not been disturbed. No one had come to take it, because no one knew it was there, because the only other person who knew was dead, and the only person besides Brandt who had ever seen it was a boy of eleven, and boys of eleven forget, or are made to forget, and this boy had been made to forget so thoroughly that he had made a profession of forgetting.

*Sable Dravenmoor.* Brandt said the name to himself, testing it. The boy was a man of twenty-three now, a clockmaker's apprentice with a reputation for fine work and no conversation. Brandt had watched him for years the way a man watches a tool he may one day need — noting the steadiness of the hands, the quality of the repairs that came out of the little shop on Binder's Lane, the way the young man moved through the market as though trying to take up no space at all. Brandt recognized that way of moving. He had practiced it himself for twelve years. It was the walk of a man carrying something breakable.

He closed the case and locked it and went down to put the kettle on, because today was the day, and a man should not approach the most difficult conversation of his life with cold hands.

* * *

The young clockmaker arrived at mid-morning, as arranged, and Brandt watched him come up the observatory drive through the frost-killed garden, past the sundial that had told only lies for three months of every winter since before either of them was born. Sable Dravenmoor walked with his tool bag over his shoulder and his collar up and his eyes down, and Brandt thought: *he counts his steps. He knows exactly how many steps it is from the gate to the door, and he counts them, because counting is a way of being somewhere else.*

"Master Dravenmoor." He opened the door before the boy — the man — could knock. "You found us, then. Some of my colleagues doubted you would come, this close to the closing of the bridge."

"The commission was accepted before the bridge closed." Sable's voice was quiet and level. His eyes went past Brandt into the hall, taking in the instrument cases, the dim stair, the smell of the place — reading it, Brandt thought, the way he himself read the sky. "I keep my commissions."

"So I have heard." Brandt stood aside to let him in. "Come up. The clock is on the third floor. Mind the fourth stair from the landing; it has opinions."

He led the way and felt the young man's attention on his back the whole climb — measuring, cataloguing. Good. A craftsman's attention was exactly what the situation required, and Brandt had waited twelve years for a craftsman, because a locksmith could be bought and a soldier could be frightened and a priest could be reasoned with, but a man who understood mechanisms would understand, without being told, why a thing hidden in a mechanism must stay hidden *in* the mechanism, and what it costs to take it out.

In the clock room, Sable stopped two paces inside the door and simply looked at the stopped clock for a long moment. Brandt watched him look. He saw the apprentice's eyes go over the case, the face, the stopped hands — eleven minutes past four — and he saw something flicker there, some small contraction, gone as fast as it came.

"You knew it was stopped," Brandt said. Not a question.

"The whole city knows. It's been stopped since before I could—" Sable stopped. Recalibrated. "It's been stopped a long time. I understood it was a question of the escapement."

"The escapement." Brandt smiled without warmth. "Yes. It is always, in the end, a question of the escapement. May I ask what you were told about the commission?"

"That the clock is to be repaired. Restored to running order. That the observatory is to remain open through the winter, as a — a sign of continuity." Sable's mouth twisted very slightly. "The garrison commander's phrase, I'm told. Continuity. They want the tower working when the delegation's historians arrive, so the town can be photographed with its instruments running."

"And what did they tell you about why it stopped?"

Sable was silent a moment. "That the mechanism failed. That the parts are no longer made. That it would take a particular kind of patience."

"Particular kinds of patience," Brandt said, "are the only kind worth hiring." He crossed to the case, took the cord from around his neck, and unlocked it — and here he hesitated, one heartbeat, two, because he had rehearsed this moment for years and rehearsal had not made it lighter. Then he opened the case wide, and stepped back, and said:

"Before you touch it, Master Dravenmoor, I must tell you what I am about to tell you, and I must ask you to hear all of it before you speak. Can you do that? Can you listen to the end of a thing?"

The young man looked at him. For the first time, Brandt saw past the careful blankness — saw the intelligence underneath it, and the wariness, and beneath the wariness something older and more tired than a twenty-three-year-old should carry. "I can listen," Sable said.

"Then listen."

* * *

"Twelve years ago," Brandt began, "on the night of the fourteenth of October, the clerk Elias Voss came up this hill in the rain. I know it was raining because I remember the sound of it on the dome — a sound like the whole sky whispering. He was forty-one years old. He was the registrar of the town court, and he was a man I had known slightly and liked moderately for twenty years, the way one likes a man one plays cards with. He came to my door at half past three in the morning, and he was carrying a brass cylinder, and he was bleeding from a cut over his eye, and he said to me: *Brandt, you keep the sky's time. I need you to keep mine.*"

Sable had gone very still.

"He would not tell me everything that night. He was a careful man — the most careful man I ever met, and I include in that category every astronomer I have served under, which is saying a great deal. He told me that he had been keeping a record. That the record contained the names of men in this town who had done things in the war year that the town would one day need to answer for, and the names of men beyond this town — men who had signed the orders, men who had taken payment, men who were, even then, positioning themselves to be on the right side of whatever peace was coming. He said the record was the only true account in existence, and that every other account would be written by the survivors of the wrong side. He said — I have had twelve years to memorize his words, so I will give them to you exactly — he said: *The victors will write that we were victims, and the town will be grateful, and both will be lies, and the lies will hold for a hundred years unless someone keeps the true account somewhere they cannot burn it.*"

Brandt's hand found the edge of the clock case and rested there.

"He asked me for one night and one day of my silence. In that time he built — he *built*, Master Dravenmoor, in thirty hours, with my tools and my help and without once telling me what he was building toward — a hiding place inside this clock. He said no one would ever dismantle a stopped clock's escapement carelessly, and no one would ever repair a stopped clock at all, if the town could be persuaded that the clock was dead. He said a stopped clock was the safest strongbox in Vellmark, because grief and superstition would guard it better than any lock. And then, on the second night, he went down the hill to finish something — I never knew what; I think now he meant to retrieve the original depositions from wherever he had kept them, to move them, to make the record whole — and he did not come back."

The kettle-sound of the wind in the dome filled the silence. Sable had not moved. His face, in the grey light from the clock room's one window, had the quality of a face carved from the same walnut as the case.

"They found him at the tannery," Brandt said. "You will know the story the town tells. That he slipped on the ice. That the river took him. That he was melancholic, that he had debts, that he walked into the water. I have heard every version. I have heard them from men I knew to be liars and from men I believed to be honest, and the honest ones believed what they said, which is the most efficient kind of lie there is. The town wanted him to have drowned himself, because a man who drowns himself is a sad story, and a man who is murdered for what he wrote is a *dangerous* one."

"And the clock," Sable said. His voice was not quite steady. "It stopped that same night."

"It stopped at eleven minutes past four in the morning. I was here. I heard it stop — a sound like a held breath released. And I went to the case, and I found what he had made, and I understood in that moment that I had been made a keeper." Brandt looked at the young man steadily. "I did not know what was in the cylinder. I did not open it. He had made me swear — on the one thing I had left to swear on, which was this tower — that I would not read it, that I would only keep it, and keep it *stopped*, until such time as someone came who could finish the work. He said he would come back in a week. He said, *if I don't come back, wait for a clockmaker with clean hands.*"

"And you've waited twelve years."

"I have waited twelve years." Brandt heard the whole weight of it in his own voice, and did not try to disguise it. "I have waited while the men in his ledger were promoted, and decorated, and welcomed into the new order. I have waited while the victors' commissions came and went, writing their clean histories. I have waited while I grew too old and too slow to be of any use to the work, and while every year the record sat in the dark became a year the living knew less and the dead knew everything. And then, three weeks ago, the garrison came, and the armistice was announced, and I understood that the waiting was over — that the moment Voss had built this thing against had *arrived*, and it had arrived with a deadline, and I did not have the hands for it anymore."

He turned and looked at Sable Dravenmoor fully.

"So I sent for you. Not because you are the best clockmaker in Vellmark — though I am told you may be. I sent for you because you have skill, and because you have no allegiances. No party, no faction, no debts to the families in the ledger, no gratitude to the garrison. You have kept yourself out of everything for — how long? Since you were a boy. I have watched you do it. A man with no allegiances is the only man who can be trusted with a document about everyone's allegiances." He paused. "And there was one more reason. A smaller one. Twelve years ago, on the night Voss came up the hill, he said something strange to me. He said, *I have already shown it to one child, and the child will keep it better than any man, because a child who is made to be silent keeps the silence like a stone in his shoe.* I did not understand him then."

He watched Sable's face. He watched the blood leave it and come back.

"I think," Brandt said quietly, "that the child was you."

* * *

For a long moment the young clockmaker did not speak, and Brandt did not press him. He had learned, in twelve years of keeping watch, that the truth comes out of people the way water comes out of deep ground — you cannot pull it; you can only make the conditions right and wait.

When Sable finally spoke, his voice was very low. "You don't know what's in it."

"No."

"You've guarded it for twelve years and never read it."

"I have guarded it for twelve years and never read it," Brandt said, "and I will tell you what that has cost, since you are young enough to still believe things can be done without cost. It has cost me every friendship I had, because a man who will not explain why he lets a town's clock stand dead is a man who becomes strange. It has cost me my standing, because I refused the commission three times — three commissions from three governments, each of them certain the clock could be fixed and each of them wrong, because I saw to it they were wrong. It has cost me—" he stopped, and found that his throat had closed, and waited, and went on. "It cost me my brother. He came up this hill in the second year, after too much wine, and he said, *Aldous, whatever you are keeping in that tower, it isn't worth what it's making of you, and if you don't give it up I will go down and tell them myself.* And I let him believe I would have him stopped. And he went down the hill, and he did not tell them, and he never came up again, and he died two winters ago without my having spoken to him." He looked at his own hands. "So. That is the price of the thing I am asking you to take. I do not ask you lightly."

Sable's eyes had gone to the open clock case. To the escapement. Brandt saw the young man's gaze move through the mechanism the way water moves through a channel — reading it, finding the cylinder, finding the fuse silk, finding the geometry of the hiding place, and Brandt understood that in ten seconds the apprentice had understood the whole of Voss's construction, and that the understanding had landed on him not as a discovery but as a sentence.

"If I take it out," Sable said slowly, "the clock can be repaired. If the clock is repaired, the observatory stays open. If the observatory stays open—" He stopped.

"Then we have a tower," Brandt said, "with a legitimate reason to exist, in a town about to be full of men who write histories. A tower where papers can be copied. Where meetings can be held under the cover of maintenance. Where a clock that runs is the best hiding place in Vellmark, because no one dismantles a running clock." He let that sit. "Voss built the lock. I kept the lock. But neither of us, Master Dravenmoor — neither of us has the hands to do what comes next. The record must be read. It must be verified — seals, hands, witnesses, whatever the law of this armistice will require, and I am too old to learn a new law. It must be copied, and moved, and entered somewhere it cannot be erased, and every one of those acts is a crime in the eyes of the garrison, and every one of them requires a craftsman."

"You're asking me to finish what he started."

"I am asking you," Brandt said, "to inherit it. There is a difference, and the difference is that an inheritance cannot be refused without cost. I carried this twelve years because no one else was there to carry. You were there tonight. That is all inheritance ever is — being the one who is there when the thing needs a keeper."

The wind moved through the dome above them, a long slow breath. Down in the town, faintly, the garrison horns sounded the half hour, and the sound came up the hill thin and cold, and both men listened to it, and both understood what it meant: that time in Vellmark had been taken over by other people's clocks.

Sable Dravenmoor stood before the open case for a long time. Then he set down his tool bag, and took off his coat, and folded it over the bag with the particular care of a man who has just made a decision and is giving his hands something to do while his mind catches up.

"Show me the escapement," he said.

* * *

They worked through the afternoon, and it was, Brandt would think later, the first afternoon in twelve years in which he had not been alone with the silence. Sable's hands were everything the reports had promised — steady, quick, economical, without a single wasted motion — and he worked in a silence of his own, but it was a different silence from the town's, a working silence, a silence with a purpose in it, and Brandt found it almost unbearably restful.

The young man examined the cylinder without removing it. He traced the fuse-silk wiring, tested the tension, mapped the geometry of the concealment, and twice he went still in a way that Brandt recognized — went still the way a man goes still when a memory has walked into the room.

It was near dusk, the light going blue in the clock room, when Sable spoke without looking up from the works.

"The man who built this hiding place. Voss. He wired the maintaining power to take the strain off the cylinder when the train was released. It's — it's elegant. It's more than a hiding place. It's a *cradle*." A pause. "He must have been here for hours."

"Thirty," Brandt said.

"He trusted you with all that."

"He trusted me with none of it," Brandt said. "That was how he trusted people. He gave them a task and no reasons, so that they could not betray what they did not know." He looked at the young man's bent head, at the careful hands among the brass, and something in him — something that had been clenched for twelve years — shifted its grip, not releasing, but shifting, the way a man carrying a heavy load shifts it from one shoulder to the other. "He would have liked you."

Sable did not answer. His fingers rested on the case's inner frame, on the walnut, an inch from the brass cylinder, and Brandt watched him and understood that the young man was not touching the mechanism at all — he was touching the past, the way one touches a wall that is known to be thin.

It was full dark when Sable straightened, and wiped his hands, and said, in the flat careful voice of a man reading from a document he has not agreed to sign:

"I'll need to take it apart properly. The whole escapement. It can't be done in an afternoon, and it can't be done while anyone is watching, and—" he hesitated, and Brandt saw the hesitation cost him something, "—and there may be others who need to see what's inside. In time."

"Others," Brandt said.

Sable looked at him, and in the lamplight his eyes were very clear and very guarded, and he said, "The garrison has clerks. The clerks have copyists. And one of the copyists has been asking questions about a citation that leads, by a very short road, to this tower."

Brandt felt the old cold move through him, the cold of the night Voss had not come back. "How do you know that?"

"Because," said Sable Dravenmoor, "she's coming up the hill tomorrow."
