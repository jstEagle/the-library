## 9. Lene Reads Over Shoulders

The records office of the occupation kept its warmth like a miser keeps coin: grudgingly, and only near the stove. Lene had learned in her first week which desk caught the heat and which caught the draft from the door, and she had arranged to be moved twice — once for a bad knee she did not have, once for fear of the light — until she sat where she wanted, three desks from the fire and one from the shelf of current correspondence. From there she could read over shoulders without ever lifting her eyes from her own page.

It was a skill her father would have admired, though it was not one he had taught her. He had taught her letterforms and margins and the difference between a record and an account — *a record is what happened, Ada; an account is what someone is prepared to answer for* — but the reading-over-shoulders she had taught herself, in the months since she had become Lene Hartl of Aldenmoor, orphaned by the war, recommended to the commission's clerical pool on the strength of a fine hand and no family to ask after her.

She copied all morning, as she did every morning. Requisitions. Ration schedules. A memorandum on fuel allotments for the winter garrison that used the word *provisional* eleven times. Her nib scratched; her posture was perfect; her mind ran underneath her hands like water under ice.

Because that morning, when Captain Veyre's aide had come in stamping snow off his boots, he had carried under his arm a parcel wrapped in oilcloth, and he had said — not to her, not to anyone, just to the room at large, in the tone men use when they want their own importance overheard —

"The historians are here."

Lene wrote out *eleven (11) cords, seasoned birch*, and did not look up.

* * *

She had been waiting for them since the armistice was signed at Merrow Field. Not the soldiers — soldiers were weather, you endured them or you died of exposure — but the historians. She had understood, with a clarity that sometimes frightened her, that the war would end twice: once when the guns stopped, and once when the books were written. The first ending had come in autumn. The second was coming now, up the frozen road from the bridge, in oilcloth parcels, carried by aides who stamped their boots.

Her father had believed the second ending could be fought. He had died believing it, or because of it — the distinction had stopped mattering to her sometime around the second month — and everything Lene Hartl did in this office, every perfectly formed capital, every memorized destruction order, existed inside that belief like a wire inside a wall.

So when the parcel arrived, she watched it.

Veyre himself came down to receive it, which told her its weight before she saw it weighed. The captain was a careful man, gray at the temples, courteous in the way of officers who have never once needed to raise their voices because the machinery raised them instead. He unwrapped the oilcloth at his own desk with the deliberation of a surgeon, and what came out were books. Six of them. Bound in blue-gray boards, gilt-lettered spines, the kind of binding that cost more per volume than a Vellmark tanner earned in a season.

Lene copied her requisition line, blotted it, squared her paper, and rose to carry the completed folio to the intake basket beside Veyre's desk, because that was her duty, and because the route passed within four feet of the books.

She did not slow. Slowing was how you got noticed. She simply walked, eyes down, and let her memory do what her father had trained it to do since she was six years old: hold a page.

The topmost volume lay open where Veyre had set it down. She took in what she took in — a title page, a running head, a page number — in the half-second the walking gave her, and was back at her desk before the cold of the window draft reached her again.

*The War of the Coalition Succession: An Administrative History.* Volume the third. And beneath the running head, in type already set, already printed, already bound:

*Chapter XIV: The Vellmark Corridor.*

Printed. Bound. Gilt on the spine.

The chapter on Vellmark was already written, and the war's own occupying commission had only just arrived to see the town.

* * *

She worked through the rest of the morning with her blood gone quiet and strange, the way it went when she was most afraid — not hot, not racing, but slowed, deliberate, a clockwork heart. Around her the office breathed its ordinary breaths: the scratch of nibs, the cough of the clerk with the pipe, the stove ticking as the coal settled. Somewhere beyond the shuttered windows the garrison horns sounded the midday watch, and the sound came through the glass thinned and far away, like something remembered rather than heard.

At the noon interval, when the senior clerks went down to the mess and the junior men went out to smoke in the lee of the wall, Lene remained at her desk with bread and a pickle and the appearance of a girl too poor to buy the mess ticket and too shy to stand in the cold. It was true enough. It was also useful. Two winters of being Lene Hartl had taught her that poverty and shyness were the best disguises yet invented; no one interrogates a person they pity.

Veyre did not go to the mess either. He stayed at his desk with the blue-gray volumes, and he read, and he made notes in pencil, and once he drew a volume toward him so sharply that it knocked his inkwell and he swore — softly, in his own language, a word Lene pretended not to know.

When the others began filtering back, he summoned her.

"Hartl."

She crossed the floor with her hands folded. "Captain."

"You have a good hand." He said it the way a man appraises a horse — not unkindly, not kindly. "And you're from outside the town. Aldenmoor?"

"Yes, Captain." The lie had worn smooth as a river stone. She could say it asleep.

"Then you'll have no... attachments here. No feelings about local matters." He tapped the open volume. "I need forty fair copies of Chapter Fourteen by week's end. Clean work, no errors, no commentary. The annex requires them ahead of the delegation's session."

"Yes, Captain."

He looked at her a moment longer than the order required — long enough that she felt the old animal attention rise along her spine, the sense of being weighed — and then dismissed her, already turning back to his notes.

Forty copies. By week's end. Of the chapter that would tell the victors' world what had happened in Vellmark.

She returned to her desk and sharpened a fresh nib and waited for the afternoon to arrange itself into privacy.

* * *

The copying began at two o'clock, and it was the strangest work she had ever done, stranger even than the destruction orders, because this time she was not erasing the past but manufacturing it, line by line, in her father's trade, with her father's precision.

*Chapter XIV: The Vellmark Corridor.*

She read each sentence twice — once to copy it, once to keep it — and the fury came slowly, the way cold comes into a house, seeping under doors, finding every gap.

The chapter ran thirty-one pages. It covered the corridor campaign in the administrative register the victors favored: supply lines, rail capacity, grain levies, garrison rotations. It was, she admitted through her anger, well made. Whoever had written it had never seen the places described, and had made them smooth and plausible and dead. Vellmark appeared as a node. The bridge appeared as infrastructure. The river appeared as a logistical consideration.

And the people — the people appeared exactly twice.

Once, in a paragraph on civil administration: *"Local officials proved generally cooperative in matters of requisition, and the transition of authority was effected without significant incident."*

Without significant incident.

Lene's nib paused above the paper, and she let it pause, because a copyist may hesitate over a difficult word, and *cooperative* was a difficult word, and she let the hesitation last exactly as long as a difficult word deserved and no longer, and then she wrote it out, beautiful and exact, *cooperative*, with the little loop on the p that her father had drilled into her at six years old at this very size of desk in a house that no longer existed.

Cooperative. The word for Elias Voss, court clerk of Vellmark, who had refused to certify the requisition ledgers, who had spent the last year of his life copying testimony in a hand small enough to hide inside a clock, whose body had been pulled from the winter river with the coroner's verdict of *accident* entered by an official whose name Lene knew, whose name lived now in her memory alongside dates and payments and the tannery, always the tannery—

She turned the page.

There it was. Or rather, there it wasn't.

The narrative moved from the autumn of the occupation directly to the spring levies. Between them, between October and March, lay a gap she could have posted a wagon through. Thirty-one pages, and the space where the tannery should have been was blank — not visibly blank, that was the horror of it, not an absence anyone would notice unless they knew to notice, but a smoothness, a seamlessness, sentences flowing over the place like water over a stone, *garrison provisions were consolidated at the eastern works during this period* — the eastern works, the tannery, named for what it became rather than what it was, mentioned only as a location where things were stored, as if the thing stored there in the last week of October had been grain.

No names. No date. No incident.

They had not denied it. That was what made her hands cold on the warm pen. Denial could be answered; denial was an argument, and arguments could be lost. This was not denial. This was architecture. The chapter had been built with the tannery left out the way a building is built with a room left out — load-bearing walls placed elsewhere, the whole structure standing perfectly sound upon the omission, so that to point at the missing room afterward would be to seem mad, to be accused of wanting to weaken the whole house over a thing no honest blueprint ever showed.

And the histories were printed. Bound. In triplicate, in oilcloth, arrived before the historians themselves, who would spend their weeks in Vellmark conducting interviews, gathering corroboration — collecting, she understood suddenly, not evidence but *confirmation*. You do not research a chapter that is already typeset. You garnish it. You find the cooperative officials and quote them, and any voice that says otherwise becomes an anomaly against the published record, a crackpot, a griever who cannot accept the war's hard arithmetic.

Her father's ledger was not merely hidden, then. It was preemptively orphaned. Whatever it said, the official account had already decided otherwise, and the ink on the official account had been dry for months.

Lene copied on.

By the fourth hour she had learned the chapter's grammar of silence intimately — where the passive voice gathered like fog (*measures were taken*, *authority was vested*, *the situation was resolved*), and where, in contrast, agency bloomed whenever credit could be claimed (*the Commission established*, *the delegation secured*, *the historian's office has determined*). She catalogued it the way Sable Dravenmoor — no; the way a clockmaker might catalogue a mechanism — part by part, spring by escapement, until she could have drawn the whole machine from memory.

She was on her ninth copy when she found the footnote.

Footnote twelve, page twenty-six, attached to the paragraph on the eastern works. It read: *"One uncertified document purporting to concern this period circulated briefly in the town prior to the clerk E. Voss's death; it was examined and found to lack authentication, and no further copies are known to exist."*

Lene read it three times.

*Lack of authentication.* So they had found traces of it — or feared traces, or had heard rumors through the same channels that had sent men to her father's office in the last week of his life — and they had done the cleverest possible thing: they had mentioned it. Buried it. Pre-poisoned the well, so that if the document ever surfaced, the record stood ready to receive it with a shrug: *ah yes, the uncertified paper, we looked at that.* They had written her father's life's work a death certificate in advance and filed it in a footnote.

And — *no further copies are known to exist.*

They didn't know about the cylinder. They didn't know about the clock. Which meant they had never found it, in twelve years, which meant Herr Brandt's tower had held, which meant—

Which meant it still existed, and it lacked authentication, and those two facts together were the entire shape of her remaining life, and she had better begin treating them so.

She finished footnote twelve in her flawless hand. She blotted it. Around her the office settled into its end-of-day rhythms; the stove was fed; Veyre locked the blue-gray volumes in the iron cabinet with the good lock, the one she could not pick, though she had studied it for a month out of thoroughness rather than hope.

"Nine today," Veyre said, counting her folios. "Continue tomorrow. Accuracy over speed, Hartl. These texts must be perfect."

"Yes, Captain," said Lene, meaning: I will know your book better than you do.

* * *

She walked home through the occupied town as the lamps were lit, past the shuttered chandlery and the garrison sentry at the corner who nodded to her because she was nobody, past the mouth of Tanner's Row where the snow lay unmarked and blue in the dusk, and she let herself stop there for ten seconds, ten seconds only, the way she allowed herself every few days, looking down the row at the dark bulk of the eastern works with its boarded windows.

*I am going to put you back,* she told the blank space in Chapter Fourteen, in the silence of her own skull, where no historian could file it. *Word by word. Date by date. Name by name. You will be the hardest sentence in the book.*

Then she walked on, because grief on an open street was a luxury with a price, and she climbed the hill toward the observatory district — telling herself, as she had told herself for a week, that it was merely a walk, merely curiosity, merely the habit of a copyist who liked to know the ground of her citations — while in her pocket her fingers closed around the scrap of paper bearing a single line she had copied from a shipping manifest that morning, almost as an afterthought:

*One master clock, observatory of Vellmark, stopped these twelve years; keeper requests examination.*

Twelve years. Stopped the year of the footnote. Stopped the year of the river.

The lamplighter was working his way up the hill road, and behind and above him the observatory dome stood black against the early stars, and Lene Hartl — who corrected nothing in the casualty lists, who flinched at nothing in Chapter Fourteen, who had not wept since Merrow Field — quickened her step toward the tower, because tomorrow the historians would begin their interviews, and whatever the clock knew, it was time someone asked it.
