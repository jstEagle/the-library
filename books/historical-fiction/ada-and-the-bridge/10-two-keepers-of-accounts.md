## 10. Two Keepers of Accounts

Sable heard her boots on the tower stairs long before she reached the top — a quick, even rhythm, nothing like Herr Brandt's slow deliberate climb or the garrison officers' heavy, unhurried tread. Whoever was coming up the two hundred steps of the observatory stair was young, and in a hurry, and had decided before arriving that hesitation would be a weakness.

He set down his burnishing cloth and considered, for a moment, simply closing the housing panel and pretending to be nothing but a repairman. It was a skill he had. Twelve years of practice at being furniture.

The panel stayed open. He could not have said why.

She came through the doorway like weather — a small woman in a clerk's grey coat, wool cap pulled low, a canvas satchel slung across her body with the strap worn pale at one spot where her hand habitually gripped it. She stopped when she saw him, and he watched her do the same arithmetic he had just done: assess, decide whether to conceal, decide not to bother.

"You're the clockmaker," she said. Not a question. Her accent was Vellmark's own, but flattened, deliberately sanded down, the way people spoke when they'd trained themselves out of a district's telltale vowels.

"Sable Dravenmoor." He gave the name the way he always did — quietly, as if apologizing for its weight. "Commissioned by Herr Brandt."

"Brandt is at the garrison office this morning. Signing papers about your permit to work here." Her eyes went past him, to the great brass movement of the master clock, its pendulum hanging still, its escapement exposed under his lamp. "You've been in the housing."

"I'm repairing it. That requires opening it."

"Of course." She crossed the room without asking leave, which he noted, and set her satchel on Brandt's workbench, which he noted harder. From it she drew a slim folder bound in grey cord — occupiers' issue, the kind of folder that traveled from the records office into archives and never came back out. "I'm looking for a citation. Document 4471-C, filed under municipal correspondence, autumn of twelve years ago. The index says it references 'the observatory timepiece, maintenance thereof.' I need the original, or a certified copy, or failing both, whoever last saw it."

"And you came to the clock itself," Sable said slowly, "because?"

"Because the citation trail ends in three places. A fire at the chancery annex burned some files — conveniently, only some. A flood in the cellar rotted others — also selectively. And the rest were transferred to the victors' historical commission last month." She turned to face him fully, and he saw then what he had missed at first: not a clerk's dullness behind her eyes but something banked and burning, a furnace seen through a cracked door. "Document 4471-C does not exist anymore. But the index card survives, and index cards are written by people who have read the documents they describe. I want to know who wrote that card, and what they read."

"Then you want an archivist, not a clockmaker."

"Then I want someone in this tower who has had his hands inside that clock's housing for eleven days," she said, "and can tell me honestly whether anything else lives in there besides gears."

* * *

Ada watched him weigh the question, and she watched the way he weighed it — not like a man deciding whether to lie, but like a man deciding how much truth he could afford. She knew that arithmetic intimately. She performed it every morning in the records office, choosing which queries to make aloud and which to bury in the margins of her copying, which supervisors to smile at and which to be invisible before.

His hands, she observed, were beautiful and ruined in the way of craftsmen's hands, scarred at the knuckles, stained faintly blue at the cuticles from whiting and bluing compound. He stood between her and the open clock housing without appearing to stand anywhere at all — a stillness so complete it was almost aggressive. People who moved like that had learned to move like that. Nobody was born a wall.

"The clock stopped twelve years ago," he said at last. "The night of the ninth of Frostmonth. That's in the maintenance ledger — the stoppage, not the date's significance. Herr Brandt called me because the garrison wants the observatory functioning during the occupation. Symbolic continuity, civic morale. So they say."

"Do you believe them?"

"I don't require beliefs to repair an escapement." He said it evenly, but his thumb moved once across his forefinger, a small unconscious rubbing, the gesture of a man touching an old scar. "Why does a copyist in the occupying administration care about a maintenance document from before the war?"

"Because the document cites correspondence about this clock, and the correspondence was written by my—" She stopped. Recalculated. He saw the stop; she watched him see it, and made the decision to spend rather than save. "By a man whose papers keep turning up burned, flooded, or transferred. Every road I follow ends in one of those three. I have begun to think that is not chance. I have begun to think someone has been tidying."

"Tidying."

"History gets dirty, Master Dravenmoor. Somebody's job is to clean it. They're doing it now, ahead of the treaty — you've seen the historians arrive, surely. Kessler's commission. They brought their own paper. Did you know that? Reams of it, pre-ruled, pre-headed. A history of this war already written before they interviewed a single soul in this town."

Something moved behind his eyes at the name Kessler — not surprise, exactly. Confirmation. As if she had named a symptom he had already diagnosed.

"And you think a maintenance record for an observatory clock matters to that history," he said.

"I think everything matters to it, or nothing does, and I decline to live in the version where nothing does." She pulled the index card from her folder and laid it on the workbench between them, face up, like a card played in a game neither had agreed to. "Read it."

He didn't touch it. He leaned over it instead, hands clasped behind his back — a man careful about leaving prints, she thought, a man trained by something. The card bore the archival hand of old Clerk Hessler-Vane, whom Ada had copied after for two years and knew like a voice: the fine downstrokes, the distinctive hooked *f*.

*Doc. 4471-C. Corr., obs. timepiece. Ref. sealed instr. re: escapement. Author: E.V.*

Three letters. *E.V.* Ada had stared at those letters for six weeks across the width of an index drawer.

When she looked up, Dravenmoor had gone very still — more still than stillness, if such a thing existed. His gaze was not on the card but slightly beyond it, at the clock, at the dark aperture of the housing she had spent eleven days wondering about.

"'Sealed instruction regarding the escapement,'" she quoted. "An escapement doesn't take instructions, Master Dravenmoor. It takes impulses. Regular, measured impulses. Whatever was sealed into this clock was meant to release something on a schedule — or to hold something until a scheduled release. Either way, it was engineering. Deliberate. Someone built a hiding place into the machinery of the town's official time."

"You've given this a great deal of thought for a copyist."

"I give everything a great deal of thought. It's cheaper than grief." She heard the words come out sharper than intended, truer than intended, and hated herself a little for the slip. His eyes came up to hers then, and whatever he found there made him look away first — the first retreat he'd offered her, and she understood it as a courtesy, or a confession, or both.

* * *

They circled each other for another hour, and Sable felt the strange sensation of a lock testing keys.

She asked questions the way he imagined a surgeon asked about pain — precise, unsentimental, interested only in what the answer revealed. When did Brandt leave. How often did garrison officers climb the tower. Had anyone else requested access to the housing. Had he catalogued the movement's components against the original build specifications, and if so, might she see the catalogue.

That last one stopped him cold, because of course he had catalogued it, and of course the catalogue was the thing he could not show anyone — the inventory with its single extra line, its brass cylinder wired into the train like a heart lodged in the wrong chest.

"No," he said.

"Why not?"

"Because you haven't told me who you are."

The words came out before he chose them, and once out, they changed the shape of the room. She didn't flinch. She studied him with that furnace-door gaze, and he realized he had made an error and a discovery in the same breath: the error was showing his hand; the discovery was that she respected the play.

"All right," she said. "A trade, then. Not identities — I'm not fool enough to trade mine to a stranger, and I assume you're not either. Trades of *work*. I'll show you what I know about the paper trail. You show me what you know about the machine. We keep our names in our pockets until the pockets are safer."

It was, he thought, the most honest offer anyone had made him in twelve years.

* * *

What she showed him, spread across Brandt's workbench in careful stacks she controlled like card tricks: the pattern of destruction. Chancery fires clustered around certain months. Cellar floods likewise. Transfers to the commission that swallowed whole years of municipal record. Laid side by side, the accidents drew a shape — a silhouette of erasure with clean edges around specific names, specific nights.

"They're not burning history," she said, tapping the arrangement. "Burning draws attention. They're *relocating* it. Everything inconvenient goes into the commission's custody, where it becomes legally inaccessible, then physically misfiled, then eventually lost. Paper doesn't die dramatically anymore. It dies of bureaucracy."

"You talk like someone who's watched it happen."

"I copy the destruction orders," she said flatly. "Part of my duties. Beautiful penmanship required. I write out, in fair hand, the lists of what is to be pulped. My supervisor compliments my letterforms." Her jaw tightened once and released. "So yes. I've watched it happen. Closely. At close range. In my own handwriting."

Sable looked at the stacks of evidence, at the fierce tired precision of her, and felt something in himself lean toward her the way a plant leans toward a light source — involuntarily, against its own training. Twelve years of leaning away from everything, and now this.

Then it was his turn, and he stood at the edge of his own secret the way a man stands at the edge of ice, testing.

He did not show her the cylinder. He showed her the *space*. He took the build specification — the honest one, the public one he kept for Brandt and the garrison — and laid it beside his working notes, and let her see the discrepancy in the count of components. One item in the housing that belonged to no drawing.

"There's something in the escapement train," he said. "Brass, cylindrical, sealed. Wired in by someone who understood the mechanism better than its makers did. It doesn't affect the timekeeping. It affects nothing. Except that it's there, and it's hidden, and it was installed — by the solder marks — roughly twelve years ago."

Ada Voss — though he didn't yet know her name, wouldn't learn it for days — went absolutely motionless, in a manner he would come to recognize as her equivalent of other people's shouting.

"The night the clock stopped," she said.

"The night the clock stopped."

"Whoever hid it stopped the clock to do it. Stopped official time in Vellmark to install a secret inside it." She laughed — a short, hard sound with no humor in it, only wonder weaponized. "Do you understand what that means? Everyone remembers the night the town's clocks went wrong. It's a story mothers still tell. The observatory died and no one could fix it for twelve years. It's the most famous silence in this city. And someone used it as a vault door."

They stood looking at each other across the workbench, across the index card and the specifications and the shape of an erasure drawn in dead documents, and Sable felt the last of his caution fail — not collapse, but fail, the way a dam fails, quietly and totally.

"I haven't opened it," he said. "The cylinder. I've had it in my hands and I haven't opened it."

"Why not?" she asked softly.

"Because once it's open, it exists. Right now it's a component. Once it's open, it's testimony, and testimony makes demands." He met her eyes. "I've spent twelve years not making demands on anyone. Including myself."

She reached across the bench and turned his working notes toward herself, and began, without asking, to rule a fresh column beside his component list — her clerk's hand, fine and exact, building a table for evidence that did not yet officially exist.

"Then we'll open it together," she said, "and we'll do it properly. Sealed openings witnessed, condition documented, chain of custody written out before we touch anything. If this is what I think it is, it will need to survive scrutiny by people paid to destroy it. We are not going to hand them a damaged exhibit." She glanced up. "Unless you'd rather keep being furniture."

The word landed precisely where she'd aimed it, and he almost smiled — the first candidate for a smile in longer than he could measure.

"It's a bargain, then," he said. "Paper and machine."

"Keepers of accounts," she corrected. "Both kinds."

Outside, the garrison horns sounded the midday hour, and above them the master clock hung silent in its tower, keeping — for a little while longer — the only honest time left in Vellmark.
