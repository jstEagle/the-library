## 15. Escapement

The trouble with the master clock, Sable decided on the third morning, was that it had never been broken at all. It had been *stopped* — deliberately, lovingly, by someone who understood the mechanism well enough to halt it without violence. A wedge of felt, old and grey as mouse fur, sat wedged between the pallet stones and the escape wheel, and around it the oil had been allowed to thicken into amber glue. Whoever had done it — Brandt, surely, twelve years ago, in the first raw week after Elias Voss fell from the observatory stair — had wanted a death that looked like mechanical failure. A clock that stopped when its keeper's heart stopped. The town had accepted the poetry of it. Towns always preferred poetry to paperwork.

Sable sat on his stool inside the clock case, knees drawn up, a jeweler's loupe screwed into one eye, and let his fingers learn the machine the way a blind man learns a face.

The escapement was a deadbeat, Graham-type, older and finer than anything else in Vellmark — the kind of work that came out of a single workshop in another country, another century, brought up the river on a barge and hoisted through the observatory roof in sections. The escape wheel had thirty teeth, each polished to a mirror along its locking face, and every tooth carried a small crescent of wear where the pallet had caught it, year after year, tick after tick, for longer than anyone in the town had been alive. He found himself tracing those crescents with a fingertip, thinking: *this is what faithfulness looks like.* Not grandeur. Repetition. The same small surrender of motion into rest, motion into rest, ten thousand times an hour, until the wheel's patience became the town's time itself.

His own shop kept him honest about gears, but this — this was a cathedral of gearing. The going train rose above him like scaffolding in a mine shaft: great wheel, center wheel, third wheel, fourth, each arbor turning in its own brass bushing, each meshing with the next in a chain of counted teeth. The pendulum hung below, nine feet of iron rod and a lens-shaped bob, and when he finally freed it, he would have to set it swinging with his own hand, because no clock starts itself.

He had not yet freed it. Three days in, and he had cleaned everything but the heart.

* * *

It was not cowardice, he told himself, that stayed his hand on the escapement. It was strategy. A running clock was a living clock; a living clock needed a living repairman; and a living repairman could come and go through the observatory door with his tool roll under his arm and no one — not the garrison quartermaster who now signed the requisitions for the district, not the bored sentry at the foot of the hill, not Kessler's polite clerks who had begun appearing at odd hours with their questions shaped like courtesies — no one would think twice.

A stopped clock invited questions. A mending clock answered them before they were asked.

So he worked in plain sight, slowly, visibly, letting the whole hill watch him do exactly what he had been paid to do. He oiled the train with whale oil warmed in his palm. He trued a bent tooth on the fourth wheel with a burnisher finer than a needle. He replaced the gut line on the striking weight, knotting it the way his father had taught him — twice around, through the eye, back over itself — and stood a long while afterward with his thumb pressed against the fresh rope, remembering his father's hands, which had been enormous and gentle and had smelled forever of metal polish, and which had gone into the ground two winters after the tannery night, still silent, still safe, still bought.

Below him, through the clock room's south window, Vellmark lay under its first true snow. The river had gone the color of unpolished pewter. The bridge crossed it in a long dark seam, and along the bridge, at intervals precise enough to have been measured, stood the garrison's new lanterns — burning all night now, since the delegation had settled in for the winter. From up here the lights looked almost festive. Sable knew better. He had walked past them twice, and each time had felt the same thing: not watched exactly, but *counted*, the way the escape wheel counted teeth. One. Two. Three. Every crossing entered somewhere in some ledger he would never be permitted to read.

The occupiers had made the observatory a kind of courtesy. They liked it, the officers did; it gave them a view and a story — *the old astronomical tower, how picturesque* — and so the sentries let tradesmen pass. That was the bargain Sable had constructed, gear by gear, without ever saying it aloud to anyone: the clock's illness was his passport. As long as the master clock remained sick, he remained necessary. And while he was here, necessary and bent over brass, other things could happen in this room that had nothing to do with timekeeping.

Things like copying.

* * *

Ada — Lene, he corrected himself, even inside his own head, because names were load-bearing structures now and a careless syllable could bring the whole roof down — Ada arrived at dusk on the fourth day, climbing the tower stair with a portfolio of "records requiring verification" and an expression of perfect bureaucratic tedium that Sable was beginning to understand was her finest disguise.

"The commission wants the municipal water accounts cross-checked," she announced to the empty stairwell, loudly enough for any ear, and then, arriving in the clock room, much more quietly: "They want nothing of the sort. They want me where I can be seen doing something harmless."

"Then we should both be seen," Sable said, and handed her an oil can.

That was how the evenings went, thereafter. She sat at the little desk beneath the window with the water accounts spread open — genuine documents, genuinely dull — and copied figures in a clerk's round hand while he worked above her in the case of the clock, and between them, passed hand to hand under the pretext of handing tools, moved the pages that mattered. A leaf of the ledger, transcribed in her small fierce script, folded to the width of a pencil and slipped inside the hollow handle of a file. A list of signatures, compared against the garrison duty rosters she memorized by day. Once, a photograph — Voss's study, preserved behind police seals, taken by someone whose name Ada would not give — showing the very shelf from which the brass cylinder had been taken, twelve years ago, by whoever had wired it into the escapement before the felt wedge stopped the world.

"You understand what you've built," she said one evening, not looking up from her copying. "This room. It's the best hiding place in Vellmark."

"It's a clock."

"It's a clock *that works*. No one searches a working clock, Sable. Soldiers search boxes, desks, mattresses, altars. But a running movement — they'd have to stop it to search it, and stopping it means calling a repairman, and calling a repairman means explaining to their superiors why they broke the town's time." She dipped her pen. "You've hidden the truth inside the one object in this city that everyone needs and no one dares touch."

He turned that over while he fitted a new click-spring to the great wheel. She was right, and he both admired and feared how right she was, because it meant the plan had grown beyond him, had become a thing with its own weight and momentum, like a train of wheels once the weight was wound. He had wanted only to fix a clock. Now the clock was becoming an argument.

"And when it runs again," he asked, "what am I then?"

"Then you're the man who fixed it," she said. "And you keep coming back anyway, because master clocks want *looking after*. Regulation. Oil. Nobody questions a repairman's habits." At last she looked up, and in the lamplight her eyes had the flat bright quality he'd learned to read as anger held at a temperature just below boiling. "We don't hide the record, Sable. We keep it in motion. There's a difference. Hiding is what guilty people do. Keeping is what witnesses do."

* * *

On the sixth evening, Herr Brandt climbed the stair slower than usual, one hand on the rail, and stood in the doorway a long moment watching them — the girl with her pen, the young man with his loupe — before speaking.

"Do you know," he said, "I used to wind this clock myself, after. Twelve years. Every Sunday, I came up and I checked the weights and I touched nothing, and every day of those twelve years I listened for it to start again." He lowered himself onto the bench by the wall with the groan of a man whose joints kept their own calendar. "Elias told me once — the night before — what he'd put inside it. He said, *a clock is the only honest witness in a town, Brandt. It sees everything and repeats nothing until it's asked.*"

Sable's hands went still on the movement. Above them, in the frozen dark of the dome, snow ticked faintly against copper.

"He chose the escapement," Brandt went on, "because the escapement is the part that decides. Every other wheel only carries what it's given. But the escapement — it releases. Tooth by tooth. It decides how much truth gets through, and when." The old man's laugh was dry as the felt wedge in Sable's pocket. "He thought that was funny. Clerk's humor."

Ada had stopped writing. "Herr Brandt. If the record is ever entered — truly entered, at the annex, before the signing — the clock will have to be opened. In front of people. Taken apart."

"I know."

"They'll find the space where it lived."

"I know that too." Brandt folded his hands over his stick. "Let them find it. Let them find a clean empty socket where the testimony used to sit, and let them ask why the socket exists at all. An empty hiding place is more eloquent than a full one, child. It says: *something was kept here, and then it was given.*" He nodded toward the silent pendulum. "Besides. It's winter yet. We have time. How is the train?"

"Cleaned to the fourth wheel," Sable said. "The escapement last. I want the pallets faced before I let it run. And the pendulum will need regulating for weeks — a clock this size doesn't find its rate quickly."

"Weeks," Brandt repeated, and looked out the window at the bridge lights, and said nothing more, but Sable saw the arithmetic move behind his eyes, the same arithmetic Ada carried: midwinter coming down the river like ice floes, the treaty date fixed and fixed and fixed, and three weeks suddenly feeling like the width of a pallet stone.

* * *

He worked alone that night past midnight, because the escapement could not wait any longer and neither, he understood, could he.

He removed the felt wedge first, and held it in his palm — twelve years of dust pressed into a shape, the negative image of a pause — and set it aside without ceremony. Then he took up the pallet frame, disassembled it on the bench cloth, and examined the stones under the lamp. Two needed refacing. The entry faces had worn a slight hollow, enough to alter the drop, enough to make the beat uneven, and a clock that beats unevenly loses time the way a witness who hedges loses credibility: imperceptibly at first, then ruinously.

So he stoned them. Small circles, light pressure, checking against the gauge every dozen strokes. It was the kind of work that emptied the mind of everything except the surface under the stone, and he let it, gratefully, the way a man lets cold water close over him. His mother's voice was quiet tonight. *Say nothing, Sable. Be small. Be the boy nobody looks at.* For years the words had been a shelter. Tonight, for the first time, they sounded like what they were: instructions written by a frightened woman in a burning year, mistaken by her son for a personality.

The hollows came level. The stones gleamed. He reassembled the frame, adjusted the drop until both sides fell equal, and set the crutch true against the rod.

Then there was nothing left between the clock and running except his own hand.

He stood a moment in the lamplight, listening to the building breathe. Below, the town slept under snow, all its clocks obedient to this one, all its mornings waiting on this mechanism's permission. Somewhere across the river a patrol crunched past, and he counted the footsteps the way he counted teeth — one, two, three, four — and made himself stop counting, because counting was fear wearing a craftsman's coat.

*Purpose*, he thought, testing the word like a finished part, checking it for play.

He reached into the case and took hold of the pendulum bob — cold iron, heavier than grief — and drew it aside.

He let go.

The first tick fell into the silence like a coin into a well. Then the second answered it, and the third, and the escapement began to eat its way through the minutes — release, lock, draw, release — each tooth giving up its little portion of withheld time, and the sound filled the tower and poured down through the floors and the stairwells and out into the frozen night of Vellmark, and somewhere below, he knew, people were waking and smiling without knowing why, because the master clock had spoken after twelve years and the town's scattered hearts had found their common beat.

Sable stood inside the case with his palms open at his sides and watched the wheels turn and felt the tremor of it come up through the floorboards into his boots, and thought: *this is what we are now. Not hidden. Kept in motion.*

He was still standing there, listening, when the bell below struck two — the first hour the clock had struck in twelve years — and in the ringing aftermath he heard, faint on the stair, footsteps ascending. Unhurried. Two sets, one heavy, one light, and a voice he did not know saying, pleasantly, to whoever it was with him:

"No need to wake the repairman. We'll only look at the clock."
