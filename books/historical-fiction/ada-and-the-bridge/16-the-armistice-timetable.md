## 16. The Armistice Timetable

The notice came to the records office the way all important things came to it: folded twice, stamped twice, and carried by a boy too young to understand what he held. Ada watched him set it on Frau Ostermann's desk with both hands, solemn as an acolyte presenting a relic, and she felt the change in the room before anyone had read a word of it. The lamps seemed to burn lower. The pens, which had been scratching in their usual desultory rhythm, went quiet one by one, like birds stopping to listen for weather.

She was copying an inventory of requisitioned grain — the third such inventory that month, each figure slightly smaller than the last — when Frau Ostermann cleared her throat and read aloud.

"By order of the Provisional Commission: the Armistice Instrument will be executed at midwinter, in the Custom House at Vellmark, in the presence of the signatory delegations and the neutral observer." A pause. "Upon execution, all municipal, judicial, ecclesiastical, and private records within the occupied territory shall pass into the custody of the Historical Commission, there to be catalogued, preserved, or—" the old woman's voice caught, just slightly, on the word "—disseminated according to commission protocols."

*Disseminated.* Ada's pen did not stop moving. She had learned that much discipline in her months as Lene: the hand goes on, the face goes nowhere, the heart is a locked drawer inside a locked cabinet inside a room no one visits. But beneath the stillness her mind was already working the arithmetic of it.

Midwinter was twenty-two days away.

Twenty-two days, and then every scrap of paper in Vellmark — every ledger, every deposition, every casualty list and tax roll and court minute — would belong to Kessler's commission. To men who arrived with their histories already bound in calfskin, their chapters already titled, their blanks already drawn where inconvenient truths should sit. Whatever they found after midwinter, they would find *as owners*. And owners decide what a house remembers.

She finished the line she was writing — four hundred sacks, less twelve spoiled — blotted it, and looked up with what she hoped was the mild blankness of a copyist who has heard a notice and thought nothing of it.

"Midwinter," said Petra Voss — no relation, though Ada had checked, exhaustively, in her first weeks; the woman's people were millwrights from upriver — "that's soon. I thought the talks would drag past spring."

"The victors are tired," said Herr Lindqvist from the far desk, not looking up from his stamps. "Tired armies sign quickly. Tired armies want their names on things before anyone asks them questions."

"Questions about what?" Petra asked, and laughed a little. "It's Vellmark. We lost. What is there to ask?"

Ada bent again over her grain figures. *What is there to ask.* The tannery, she wanted to say. The night of the ninth of Frostmonth. The wagons. The smoke that my father wrote about until the writing stopped. The eleven pages of his hand that I have carried in memory across three towns and two false names, because paper can be burned but a daughter who was taught shorthand by her father cannot be searched.

Instead she said, "More grain inventories, I expect," and the room chuckled its tired chuckle, and the notice was pinned to the board by the door where everyone could read it and no one could do anything about it.

* * *

That evening the cold came down off the hills like something with intent. Ada walked home through streets where the snow had been trampled grey and refrozen into a crust that rang underfoot. The garrison horns sounded the evening watch, and the sound rolled along the river ice and came back doubled, as if the frozen water itself were answering.

She let herself into the narrow room she rented above the chandler's shop, lit her candle, checked the door bar, checked the floorboard — all habits, all performed in the same order every night, the liturgy of a person living under a name not her own — and then sat at the little table by the window and allowed herself, at last, to think.

Twenty-two days.

She took out the cheap notebook she kept for work memoranda and turned past the grain figures and requisition forms to the pages at the back, written in her own careful hand but encoded in the shorthand her father had invented for his court minutes — a system of hooks and circles and raised dots that looked, to any casual eye, like the doodling of a bored clerk. Elias Voss had been proud of it. *No one steals what no one can read,* he used to say, tapping her nose with his pen. *A record hidden in plain sight is safer than any vault.*

He had believed that right up until they came for him.

She read through what she had memorized and transcribed: the payments. The names of the men who had signed the requisitions that emptied the tannery district the week before the massacre. The signature of the deputy magistrate who had ruled the deaths accidental, a man whose name now appeared — she had seen it herself, in the occupiers' own personnel files, which passed across her copying desk with terrible regularity — on the victors' list of cooperative local officials, recommended for retention.

Her father had died for these pages. Someone else — she did not yet know who — had hidden them well enough that they had never surfaced. Twelve years, and the truth sat somewhere in this city like a seed under snow, waiting.

And now the snow had a deadline.

Because here was the thing she understood better than anyone in Vellmark except perhaps the historians themselves: a document does not exist legally until it enters a record. Her father's ledger, however complete, however damning, was — in the eyes of every tribunal and treaty office in the world — *nothing*. Ink on paper. A curiosity. Contraband, even, if found in the wrong hands. The victors' commission would not burn it; burning was crude and left ash. They would simply fail to catalogue it, misplace it, absorb it into some sealed annex marked *pending review*, and within a generation it would be a rumor, and within two generations not even that. Kessler's histories were being drafted *now*, she had seen the proofs — exculpatory, elegant, with a chapter on Vellmark's war that mentioned hardship and regret and never once the ninth of Frostmonth.

After midwinter, the official record would close like a book. And whatever was not inside the book would never have happened.

Unless it was entered before the ink dried.

She blew out the candle and sat in the dark, listening to the river groan against the bridge pilings, and made herself say it plainly, the way her father had taught her to state a problem before attempting a solution: *I have twenty-one days to find my father's testimony, authenticate it, and enter it into the armistice record through channels controlled entirely by the men it condemns.*

It sounded impossible stated so plainly. Good, she thought. Her father had also taught her that impossible things merely required more precise planning, and that despair was simply imprecision wearing a dark coat.

* * *

The next morning she went early to the observatory hill.

She told herself it was reconnaissance. She had been assembling, over recent weeks, the thread of a citation — a reference in a seized court register to documents "lodged for safekeeping" by the late clerk E. Voss, the lodging location given only by a notation she had spent three nights decoding: *the place that keeps the hours.* It might mean nothing. It might mean the clock tower. She needed to see the ground before she committed anything to action.

But honesty, in the privacy of her own skull, required more. She went because the observatory was the only building in Vellmark that had ever meant safety. Her father had taken her there as a child, to see the great clock, and she remembered standing on a stool while he pointed out the escapement — *the heart of it, Ada, the part that decides when time may move* — and she had felt, for the first time, that the universe might be a machine that could be understood, and therefore survived.

She climbed the hill through snow that squeaked and gave. The observatory rose against the pale sky, its dome furred with frost, its weathervane frozen mid-point between two winds. And below it, spanning the river, the bridge stood closed — she could see the garrison barriers, the sentry huts, the chain stretched across the roadway with its padlock fat with ice. The town's single road out, shut since the freeze. Occupied, patrolled, impassable.

Everyone in Vellmark spoke about the closed bridge as an inconvenience. Ada had come to think of it differently. The bridge was the armistice in miniature: the way out existed, visibly, tantalizingly, and was not for them. When the treaty signed, the chain would come off, the victors would march away over it, and Vellmark would be left holding whatever version of itself the victors permitted.

She was standing there, breath steaming, studying the observatory door, when the door opened and a young man came out onto the steps with a leather roll of tools under his arm.

She knew him slightly, by sight: Sable Dravenmoor, the clockmaker's apprentice — not so young an apprentice anymore, she supposed, though the title clung to him — the quiet one, the one people forgot was in a room. He had been working at the observatory since autumn, summoned to mend the master clock, and the whole town knew the story because it was the only interesting story winter had provided: the great clock that had stopped dead twelve years ago, on a certain night, and the craftsman hired at last to wake it.

Twelve years ago. The night her father died. She had noticed the coincidence long ago and filed it away among her other unresolvable griefs.

Dravenmoor saw her and stopped, one hand on the rail. Up close his face was thinner than she'd imagined, with the wary patience of a man who has spent years being overlooked and has built a life inside that oversight. He nodded — a small, precise nod, a clockmaker's nod — and made as if to pass her down the steps.

"You're repairing the master clock," she said. Not a question. She kept her voice in the register of idle curiosity, a copyist making conversation about weather and machinery. "The one that stopped?"

"It stopped," he agreed. "It's starting again. Slowly."

"Do you know why it stopped? The night it stopped?"

Something crossed his face — fast, gone, like a fish turning under ice. "Machinery fails," he said. "Springs fatigue. Escapements wear."

"On that particular night, though. Of all nights." She smiled to soften it. "My father used to say clocks keep honest time and dishonest everything else."

"Your father sounds like a wise man."

"He was a clerk," Ada said, and watched, and saw — she was almost sure she saw — the words land somewhere deep in him, the way a key slides into a lock whose existence the lock's owner had hoped was forgotten. "He worked at the courthouse. Before."

The wind moved along the hill. Somewhere below, a garrison horn sounded, flat and administrative.

"Before," Dravenmoor repeated. His eyes had gone, briefly, to the observatory door behind him, and back. A man guarding something, or aware that something near him needed guarding. She had spent months learning to read officials' faces across a copying desk; she knew the grammar of concealment as well as she knew her shorthand.

"I'm Lene," she offered. "I copy documents at the records office. If you ever need the history of the clock consulted — repair logs, the keeper's filings, anything official — I have access to most of the municipal archive." She paused, precisely. "What remains of it."

He studied her for a long moment. Snow began to fall between them, fine and dry, the kind of snow that doesn't melt on wool.

"That's generous," he said at last.

"It's self-interested," she said. "I'm interested in what happened here. All of it. The parts that get written down and the parts that don't." She held his gaze steadily, offering nothing but the fact of her attention. "Some of us intend to remember accurately, whatever gets published at midwinter."

At the phrase *midwinter* his jaw tightened, barely — a movement she measured in fractions, the way her father had taught her to measure the drift of a bad clock. So he had seen the notice. So he had done the same arithmetic she had. Twenty-one days, and the book closes.

"Accurately," he repeated.

"Accurately," she said. "Whatever it costs."

He looked past her, down at the frozen river and the chained bridge, and when he spoke again his voice was quieter. "You should know the keeper doesn't admit visitors. Herr Brandt. He's... particular about the clock."

"Then I'll come back when he isn't looking," Ada said, and was rewarded — startled, actually — by the ghost of a smile at the corner of the man's mouth, there and gone like the fish under the ice.

"Careful, Fräulein Lene," he said. "In this town, the things worth finding are usually the things someone died to hide."

"Yes," said Ada. "I know that better than you could guess."

She went down the hill with the snow thickening around her, and behind her she heard the observatory door close, softly, deliberately, like a page being turned.

* * *

That afternoon the answer to her largest question walked into the records office wearing a grey coat and an expression of courteous exhaustion.

Herr Kessler had visited before — twice, briefly, to inspect the handling of seized archives — but today he came with two assistants and stayed an hour, and Ada, assigned as usual to the copying desk nearest the consultation table, copied nothing at all for most of it. Instead she listened, and memorized, and watched.

Kessler was not what the town had feared. The occupiers' lead historian was a lean, greying man with ink-stained fingers and the stooped shoulders of someone who had spent decades bent over tables. He spoke to Frau Ostermann gently, thanked her for the office's diligence, asked after the health of her staff. When he examined a register he did so with genuine tenderness, turning leaves as if they were the wings of moths. And it was precisely this — the courtesy, the care, the evident love of documents — that made Ada's skin crawl worse than any brutality would have. A thug burns papers in anger. A man like Kessler files them away in perfect order, forever, where no one will ever think to look.

"Gentlemen," Kessler was saying to his assistants, spreading a schedule on the consultation table, "the annex timetable. The instrument signs at midwinter, which gives us nineteen working days. Cataloguing of the municipal holdings concludes in fourteen. Judicial archives by the seventeenth. Private papers — diaries, correspondence, business ledgers — we begin collecting next week. Voluntary surrender first, of course." A thin smile. "Then assisted."

One of the assistants, a round young man with a red nose, asked about the courthouse holdings. "The pre-war criminal registers, sir. There are gaps. The seizure inventory notes volumes missing."

"Missing volumes are noted as missing," Kessler said mildly. "We do not chase ghosts, Herr Fenn. We build the record from what exists. That is the entire discipline." He tapped the schedule. "What exists, gentlemen, arranged in order, is history. What does not exist did not happen. This is not cynicism. It is method."

Ada's pen hung motionless above her page. *What does not exist did not happen.* Her father's opposite creed, spoken with equal conviction, from the other side of the same desk.

Kessler gathered his schedule, thanked Frau Ostermann again, and paused at the door — paused, Ada felt, with intention — to survey the room. His gaze traveled across the clerks, the desks, the shelves, and settled for a moment, weightlessly, on her. On her hands. On the shorthand-like density of her current transcription.

"A diligent hand," he observed, to no one in particular. "Vellmark should be proud of its record-keepers. After midwinter, the Historical Commission intends to honor them properly. Every document in this town, finally, in its right place."

He bowed, very slightly, and was gone, taking the warmth out of the room with him like a draft.

Frau Ostermann sank onto her stool and pressed her hand to her chest. "Nineteen days," she murmured. "God help us, it's real. It's really happening."

Around her the clerks exchanged glances — relief, mostly. The waiting would end. The victors would leave. Life would resume. Only Ada sat very still among them, doing the arithmetic that mattered:

Fourteen days until the municipal holdings were catalogued and sealed. Nineteen until the signing. And somewhere in this city — possibly in a clock tower on the observatory hill, in the keeping of a silent clockmaker who flinched at her father's profession — lay a murdered man's testimony that had to pass from hiding into record in less time than it takes to catalogue a single shelf.

She dipped her pen. She wrote the date at the top of a fresh page, and beneath it, in her father's secret characters, invisible to every eye in the room: *Twenty days. Find it. Prove it. Enter it.*

Outside, the light was already failing. Midwinter was coming down on Vellmark like dusk, and somewhere under the snow, the clock on the hill was beginning, tick by patient tick, to move again.
