## 18. The Second Cylinder

The candle had burned down to a thumb's width when Sable found the reference he would spend the rest of the night failing to forget.

He had been reading the ledger the way he read anything — slowly, with the patience of a man accustomed to mechanisms that rewarded attention. Elias Voss's hand was a clerk's hand: upright, economical, each numeral built from strokes of identical weight, so that a *3* and an *8* could be told apart only by the small discipline of their closures. Sable had come to know that hand intimately over the past weeks. He knew where Voss pressed harder at the end of a line, where his ink pooled in the descenders, where fatigue made the dates drift a half-degree off the ruling line. He knew it the way one knows any machine — by its tolerances.

Which was why the entry of 9 November stopped him like a stripped gear.

It was not the content. The content was terrible but familiar now; Voss had recorded payments and signatures for eleven pages without once letting his hand shake, and the tannery night occupied four lines of the same dry accounting: *Wagons, six. Men, thirty or more. No order signed; payment authorized retroactively by* — and there the names, three of them, two of whom now sat on the victors' own commission as honored assessors. Sable had read those lines perhaps forty times. He could recite them. He sometimes did, silently, while filing gear teeth, as though repetition might wear their edges down into something survivable.

No. It was the format that was wrong.

Every other entry in the ledger terminated in a small bracketed note — *(copy held)*, *(original filed)*, *(witness declined)* — Voss's private system of provenance, meticulous as a clockmaker's parts list. But this entry ended differently. The bracket said: *(originals secured. See the second mouth.)*

Sable set down the magnifying loupe and sat very still in the observatory's undercroft, listening to the building breathe around him. Above his head, through two floors of timber and stone, the master clock ticked with the steady, indifferent heartbeat he had restored to it — his own work, his own cover story, the great escapement releasing its seconds into the dark like grain through a millstone. He had grown fond of that sound these past weeks. Tonight it sounded less like comfort than like a countdown he had not known he was keeping.

*The second mouth.*

Voss had been a clerk, and clerks thought in metaphors of plumbing and architecture. A mouth was where a river entered something larger. Or where water left it. Sable closed his eyes and saw Vellmark as Voss must have seen it: the river bending through the town like a gray ribbon, the bridge straddling it at its narrow waist, and at the bridge's near foot the old tollhouse — squat, granite-hipped, half sunk into its own foundations after a century of floods — with its arched culvert where the millrace water ran out beneath the roadway and spilled back into the current.

A second mouth.

His hands were not shaking. He noted this about himself with a strange detachment, because twelve years ago they would have shaken, and because over the past month he had learned to distrust every steadiness in himself as possibly another kind of fear. He turned back through the ledger instead, hunting, and found what he should have noticed weeks before: a pattern in Voss's brackets, so faint it might have been nothing. Three entries — all concerning the sworn depositions, the witness statements Voss had taken in the weeks after the tannery, the statements no tribunal had ever seen — carried the notation *(originals secured)* rather than *(copy held)*. And each of those entries contained, buried in the date or the sum, a digit sequence that matched nothing else in the ledger's arithmetic.

Sable was an apprentice no longer, whatever his title said. Numbers that refused to reconcile were numbers trying to speak.

He worked until the candle drowned in its own wax. The sequences resolved, under the kind of cross-referencing he did daily with wheel counts and tooth pitches, into coordinates of a sort: distances and bearings, expressed in the surveyor's convention used on the bridge plans he had studied as a boy — everyone in Vellmark had studied them; the bridge was the town's pride and its whole fortune. He copied them onto a scrap of paper, then burned the scrap in the candle flame and watched the numbers curl and vanish, because some part of him already understood that what he had just decoded was a map to the thing that would either save them all or hang them, and maps were dangerous things to keep.

Then he climbed the tower stairs to stand beneath the clock and think about the bridge.

* * *

He went down to look at it the next morning, because a man who repairs clocks may walk anywhere in Vellmark without explanation, and walking was still the safest thing he did.

The river had begun to think about ice. Thin plates of it rode the current past the bridge piers, clicking together like porcelain, and the air above the water had the flat mineral smell of deep cold coming. The bridge itself rose ahead of him — seven granite arches, two hundred years old, its parapets carved with the names of benefactors long dead — and across its full width, from tollhouse to tollhouse, the garrison's barriers stood: sawhorses, chain, sandbags, and at the center a checkpoint hut of raw new timber that looked like a splinter driven into an old man's wrist.

Occupied territory. That was the phrase Ada used, and she was precise with words the way he was precise with tools. Not the town — the town was merely governed. The bridge was *occupied*, garrisoned day and night, because whoever held the bridge held Vellmark's throat, and both armies' staffs understood arithmetic better than mercy. Soldiers crossed it on rotation. Supply wagons crossed it under escort. And the victors' historians — Kessler's people, with their pre-drafted histories and their courteous, patient questions — came and went across it like blood through a heart, carrying away the town's records one sealed crate at a time.

Sable bought chestnuts from the brazier near the south approach, because standing still invited notice and eating chestnuts did not, and he studied the tollhouse while pretending to study the river.

It was smaller than memory. Everything was. In his childhood the tollhouse had been a giant, a troll's house, its foundation stones each taller than his father, its culvert arch a cave-mouth that boys dared each other to enter at low water. Now it was just an old building, brown-stoned and mossed, leaning a degree eastward with the patience of geology. The customs service had abandoned it years before the war; the windows were boarded; a garrison sentry stood beside the door with his collar up and his breath smoking, bored enough that his eyes slid past Sable twice without catching.

But the foundations. That was the thing. He walked along the embankment path, chestnut smoke curling from his fist, and let his eyes do the measuring his hands could not. The tollhouse did not sit on the bank. It sat *over* it — its north wall founded on the bedrock shelf, but its southern rooms cantilevered out on massive stone piers above the millrace outfall, and between the piers, behind the tumble of flood-silt and frost-shattered masonry, the old culvert ran back into darkness under the roadway itself. A void. Dry, most likely, above the flood line. Unmapped since the last survey, sixty years ago. A place a careful man with a lantern and a crowbar could reach only from the water side — or not at all.

And the water side, in winter, belonged to the ice and the sentries both.

Sable finished his chestnuts, dropped the shells into the brazier, and walked home the long way with his heart going hard and quiet, the way a clock goes when you have just discovered the flaw that explains everything.

Because here was the truth he had turned over all night and now confirmed against the daylight: the ledger was testimony, but it was a copyist's testimony, Voss's own hand summarizing what others had sworn. What the tribunal would demand — what Ilse Marren had already warned them the neutral signatory would require — was the originals. The sworn depositions, signed by witnesses, most of them dead now, their signatures beyond forgery or doubt. Without them, Voss's ledger was an accusation. With them, it was a record.

And Voss, who had trusted no vault, no office, no colleague — Voss, who had wired his secret into the escapement of a clock because clocks were the one thing in Vellmark no occupying army would ever bother to open — had put the depositions where no army would ever bother to dig. Under the bridge. Inside the town's own bones. In the one place whose capture mattered more to the victors than any document hidden within it.

They would have to go and take it back from behind the barriers themselves.

* * *

Ada arrived at the observatory at dusk, as she had taken to doing, entering through Brandt's side door with her hood down and her false name left outside like wet boots. She carried bread and a stub of cheese wrapped in cloth, the ritual offering that transformed conspiracy into something resembling hospitality, and she stood warming her hands at the little stove in the workshop while Sable laid it all out for her — the bracketed notes, the digit sequences, the arithmetic, the culvert.

She listened the way she always listened: completely, without interruption, her eyes fixed slightly to the left of his face as though reading a page held just behind his shoulder. When he finished she was silent for a long moment, feeding the stove a sliver of wood.

"The millrace," she said at last. "Under the third pier."

"Under the roadway, behind the piers. The culvert runs back maybe twenty feet before the old flood gate. If Voss's numbers mean what I think they mean, the cache is in the gate chamber — dry, sealed, forgotten." He heard his own voice steadying itself on technical ground and let it. "I checked the survey conventions against the bridge plans in Brandt's collection. The bearing matches the culvert axis exactly. It isn't coincidence. Coincidence doesn't repeat across three separate entries."

"No," Ada agreed softly. "Coincidence is for historians."

Something passed across her face when she said the word — the cold fury he had seen in her once before, when she told him about Kessler's pre-drafted chapters, Vellmark's history already written with the tannery rendered as regrettable fog. She crouched at the stove, and the firelight moved on her cheekbones, and Sable thought again what he had thought a dozen times now: that she had built herself into a person made entirely of precision, grief compressed into accuracy the way carbon compresses into diamond, and that he had never met anyone braver, though he suspected she would deny the word.

"They'll move the records across soon," she said. "Kessler's commission. Every crate gets a manifest, every manifest gets countersigned, and once the treaty annex closes, anything still in Vellmark belongs to them. Legally. Permanently." She looked up. "If the depositions are under the bridge, we have to get them before the ice does. Before Kessler's inventory does. Before—" She stopped herself, which she rarely did.

"Before my mother's bargain finishes costing everything it cost," Sable said quietly.

That startled her. He had not meant to say it aloud; it had simply come up the way water comes up through stone. He crossed to the workbench and busied his hands with a disassembled strike train, because his hands always needed somewhere to be when the rest of him was exposed.

"You've been thinking about her," Ada said. Not a question.

"I've been thinking about what silence buys." He fitted an escape wheel to its arbor without looking at it. "My mother paid twelve years of safety with hers. I've been paying the interest ever since. And now the principal has come due, and it turns out the debt is buried under a bridge guarded by men with rifles, and winter is closing the river, and we have—" he glanced at the almanac nailed by the door, at Ada's penciled mark on it, the number that shrank every morning— "seventeen days."

"Fifteen," Ada said. "I recounted the courier schedule yesterday. The annex closes early. Kessler asked for it."

Of course he had. Sable felt the familiar cold settle behind his sternum, the cold that had lived there since the night in the tannery yard, since his mother's hand clamped over his mouth in the dark and her whisper — *you saw nothing, you will always have seen nothing* — became the load-bearing wall of his entire life. He had spent twelve years being unnoticed, and it had worked, and now the work of noticing had fallen to him instead: noticing brackets in a dead man's ledger, noticing sentry rotations, noticing how many steps it was from the embankment path to the waterline, noticing that the ice on the millrace was forming faster this year than any year he could remember.

"We can't walk across," he said. "The checkpoint searches everyone leaving the town side. And even if they didn't — if we're stopped with documents on us, everything ends there. Marren won't certify contraband recovered from a body."

"So we don't carry them across." Ada rose, and there was something in her voice he recognized from the records office stories — the flat calm of a woman who had spent months memorizing which papers were condemned to destruction, cataloguing atrocities by candlelight while smiling at the men burning them. "We go in from the water side, take the originals out of the gate chamber, and bring them back the way we came. Nothing crosses the bridge at all. The bridge never knows we existed."

"And the water side?"

"Is frozen." She said it plainly, and then, watching his face, allowed the first ghost of the thing that was almost humor in her. "Or it will be, within the week. Ice is a road, Sable. It's the only road in Vellmark the garrison doesn't hold — yet."

Yet. He caught the word and turned it over. She had chosen it deliberately; everything she chose was deliberate. Which meant she had already thought past tonight, past the plan, to the objection forming in his chest.

"The garrison patrols the bank," he said. "I counted three sentry positions on the walk down. And the ice below the weir won't take weight for weeks — the millrace keeps it thin right up to the culvert mouth. Anyone crossing it goes through water at minus ten degrees, at night, carrying paper that cannot get wet, beneath a bridge full of soldiers."

"Yes," said Ada.

"And Brandt—"

"Brandt is seventy-one years old and walks with a stick. He is not crossing the ice." Her voice gentled on the old keeper's name, and Sable understood that she had already fought this particular battle in her head, and lost it, and accepted the loss the way one accepts a broken tooth. "He'll be our eyes. There's a window in the observatory dome that looks straight down the river bend. On a clear night he can see the whole approach. Lantern signals — he taught me the code himself, says it's how they did it during the last occupation, before either of us was born."

Sable looked at her for a long moment. The stove ticked. Overhead, muffled through floors of timber, the master clock released another minute into the dark, and another, patient as sediment.

"You've already decided all of this," he said. "You came here tonight to tell me, not to ask me."

"I came here tonight to show you this." From inside her coat she drew a folded sheet of paper and laid it on the workbench among the gears. "The garrison duty roster. Copied from the board at the guardhouse, three nights running. Look at the bridge watch on the nights after the river freezes hard. They halve it. Cold makes them lazy, Sable — even Kessler's soldiers. Especially Kessler's soldiers. They believe the ice protects them. Nobody crosses a frozen river to rob a tollhouse. Nobody has ever been that foolish."

"Nobody sane," Sable murmured.

"That too."

He unfolded the roster and read it twice, committing it to the place in his mind where he kept tooth counts and pendulum lengths, the place where things went to become permanent. Sentry rotations. Relief times. The gap — narrow, real, human — between the midnight patrol and the two o'clock one, when the checkpoint lamp burned unattended and the wind came down the river valley hard enough to make sentries turn their backs.

Seventeen days. Fifteen. The ice would need perhaps eight more nights of this cold to bear a man's weight below the weir. Which left them a window measured in single digits, somewhere in the coldest week of winter, under the guns of an occupying army, to extract the sworn testimony of the dead from inside the town's own foundations.

It was impossible. He examined the feeling carefully, the way he examined any mechanism that refused to run, looking for the actual fault rather than the apparent one. And found, to his lack of surprise, that the fault was only fear — fear wearing the costume of impossibility, as it always did.

"All right," he said.

Ada looked at him. In the stove light her eyes were very dark and very steady, and for a moment neither of them was a clockmaker or a copyist or Lene or anyone's daughter; they were just two people holding the same fragile thing between them.

"All right," she echoed.

* * *

He dreamed of the tannery that night — the first time in months — and woke before dawn with his mother's hand on his mouth, except it was not her hand, it was memory itself, pressing down, saying *nothing, you saw nothing.* He lay in the dark listening to the frost crack against the window glass like distant rifle fire, and below it, faint through the floorboards, the master clock counting out the seconds he had promised to restore and had, in restoring, spent.

After a while he got up, lit the lamp, and took out the scrap of paper where he had drafted, in pencil, the list of what they would need: rope, lantern with a shutter, oilskin wrap, the crowbar from Brandt's cellar, chalk. Ordinary words. An ordinary shopping list for an extraordinary crime.

At the bottom of the list, without quite deciding to write it, he added one more item in his small careful hand:

*A reason to live if the ice fails.*

He looked at the words for a long time. Then, because he was his mother's son after all, and because some silences were debts rather than comforts, he left them there — and blew out the lamp, and began to count the nights until the river froze.
