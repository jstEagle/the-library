## 20. Midnight Under the Tollhouse

*Sable*

The cold came up through the soles of his boots first, then through the stone of the bridge itself, as if the river were reaching for them through twelve feet of ice and granite. Sable crouched in the shadow of the third arch pier and counted his own heartbeats the way he counted a pendulum's swings — steady, deliberate, refusing to hurry.

Above him the bridge stretched away into the dark, its lamps extinguished by garrison order since the ice closed it. Only the far tower of the custom house showed a light, and beyond that, faintly, the observatory hill where Herr Brandt was supposed to be asleep in his chair with a book open on his chest. That was the story they had built: Brandt at home, Sable at home, no one under the bridge at all.

Beside him, Ada Voss — Lene, he had to remember to think of her as Lene if anyone heard — knelt with her gloved hands flat against the frozen snowmelt pooling between the cobblestones. Her breath came out in small white ghosts that she had learned to swallow, turning her head into her shoulder each time she exhaled. She had been practicing that, he realized, for years. A girl who had taught herself to breathe quietly.

"The grate," she whispered. "My father drew it in the margin. The third pier, downstream face, below the old waterline."

Sable turned and looked down the sheer curve of the pier to where the ice shelf met the masonry. Below the old waterline meant below the ice, which meant the tunnel mouth — if it existed — opened onto air now, exposed like a secret the winter had pried loose.

"Rope won't hold on this stone," he said. "The frost has the surface flaking. I've seen it crumble under less."

"Then we don't use rope."

She said it simply, the way she said most things, and began unbuttoning her coat.

* * *

*Ada*

She had rehearsed this descent a hundred times in her head during the long afternoons in the records office, while Kessler's historians dictated their clean sentences and her pen copied them without trembling. In the rehearsal there was always a ladder, or a ledge, or some courtesy the stone extended to her. Now, hanging by her fingers from an iron mooring ring rusted nearly through, boots braced against ice-slicked granite, she understood that her father had not designed his hiding place to be reached. He had designed it never to be found.

Her arms burned. Below her, the ice shelf jutted out from the pier like a broken shelf of glass, and beneath the shelf she could see it — darkness, a rectangle of deeper black where the masonry did not close. Air moved out of it, faintly warmer than the night, smelling of dry paper and dust and stone. Paper. Her father's paper. She almost wept at the smell of it and made herself stop, because tears froze on lashes and tears made noise.

"Sable." His name barely carried. "I'm at the opening. I can't see the floor."

"Feel with your feet. Don't drop."

"I wasn't going to drop."

"You were going to drop," he said, and there was something in his voice she had learned to hear over these weeks — the clockmaker's flatness that was actually care, wound tight as a mainspring. "Everyone drops. Plan for dropping."

She felt with her feet. Found a lip of stone, a step cut into the pier's inner throat — her father had cut steps, of course he had, Elias Voss who measured everything twice — and lowered herself down into the dark until her boots found solid ground and her shoulders were still above the ice line, inside a cavity no wider than a coffin stood on end.

She struck the shuttered lantern, let one thin blade of light escape.

Steps went down. Narrow, dry, cut clean. And on the wall beside the first step, scratched into the stone in a hand she knew better than her own — because it was the hand that had taught hers — a single word:

*VERIFIED.*

* * *

*Sable*

He gave her ten minutes. Eleven. At eleven minutes and thirty seconds he stopped counting, which was its own kind of alarm, and began working himself down the mooring ring after her, telling himself the whole way that a man who repaired escapements understood tolerances, understood how much load a rusted iron ring could bear before it became a falling man.

The cold inside the pier was different from the cold outside — still, sealed, the cold of a place that had been shut for twelve years. Ada stood at the bottom of the steps with the lantern held high, and her face when she turned to him was the face of someone standing in a room they have only ever imagined.

"It's here," she said. "It's all here."

The chamber was small — perhaps six feet by eight, cut into the pier's core above what must once have been a maintenance crawl. Along two walls ran wooden shelving, and on the shelving, wrapped in oilcloth and waxed linen against the damp, lay rows of documents. Dozens. Sable saw the seals before he saw anything else — red wax, stamped with the circuit court's mark, the same mark he had spent weeks learning to reproduce in wax and ink for the authentication work. Originals. Sworn depositions, each with a witness signature, each countersigned.

Elias Voss.

Ada knelt and lifted the first packet with both hands, the way one lifts a sleeping child. She unwrapped the oilcloth and read the heading and made a sound low in her throat that was not quite a word.

"Marta Quill," she read. "Sworn the fourteenth of — Papa took her deposition. He took everyone's. He came back here night after night and—" Her voice caught and steadied, caught and steadied, a pendulum finding its beat. "There are forty-one packets, Sable. Forty-one witnesses."

Sable lifted another. The handwriting was precise, clerkly, unhesitating. Dates, names, payments recorded, signatures of officials — the same names that filled the brass cylinder hidden in the clock's escapement, but here expanded, testified, sworn. Here was the skeleton; the ledger in the clock had only ever been its shadow.

"We take all of it," Ada said. "We wrap it exactly as it was wrapped. If any of this is found missing—"

"No." He heard his own voice come out harder than he intended. "We take half. We leave half, rewrapped, undisturbed. If Kessler ever finds this room, it has to look untouched. And if we're caught carrying nothing, we're just fools walking on the ice. If we're caught carrying forty-one sworn depositions, we're hanged and the record dies with us."

She looked at him for a long moment across the lantern light. Then she nodded, once, and began dividing the packets with a copyist's exactness — twenty here, twenty-one there — choosing which half would make the journey and which would wait in the dark for a second night that might never come.

That was when they heard the boots.

* * *

*Ada*

Not on the bridge — worse. On the ice. The sound came through the frozen river itself, a crunching rhythm, four men at least, moving along the bank toward the pier. Toward them.

She killed the lantern and the dark closed over them like water. In the blackness she felt Sable's hand find her shoulder and press — down, stay down — and they folded themselves into the corner behind the shelving, among the remaining packets, and did not breathe.

The boots stopped directly overhead. Muffled voices filtered down through the stone, flattened by the granite into shapeless murmurs, and then one voice resolved — a laugh, young and careless, and the scrape of something dragged across ice.

"They're checking the tollhouse," a voice said, clearer now, echoing down through some fissure in the masonry. "Kessler's orders. Nobody crosses, nobody lingers. Sweep it again."

Again. They had swept it before. Which meant someone had already thought to look here — or someone wanted Kessler to believe they had looked.

The footsteps moved off along the bank, fading. Ada counted to three hundred before she let herself exhale, and even then Sable's hand stayed on her shoulder, holding her in the silence, until the river gave back nothing but wind.

"They'll come back," he whispered. "A sweep means a schedule. We go now, tonight, with what we have."

They climbed. The mooring ring groaned under Sable's weight and shed a scale of rust into the dark, and for one endless moment Ada hung at the top listening to him dangle over the ice shelf, hearing the iron sing its thin note of failure — and then his hand closed over hers and he was up, breathing hard, and they were flattening themselves against the pier's downstream shadow, moving along the bank away from the patrol's route, toward the gap in the embankment wall where the old coal chute offered a path up to the streets.

They were twenty feet from it when the lantern swung around the corner of the tollhouse.

* * *

*Sable*

Later, Sable would try to reconstruct those seconds and fail. There was the light — sudden, total, a soldier's bullseye lantern thrown full across the embankment — and there was the shout, and there was Ada's arm locking through his as they dropped behind the coal chute's brick parapet, the satchel of oilcloth-wrapped depositions pressed flat between his body and the frozen wall.

"Halt! Who's there!"

And then, impossibly, from the other direction — from the bridge road above, from the direction of the observatory hill — a second light. A lantern bobbing fast down the bridge approach, and a voice, cracked and old and carrying beautifully in the frozen air:

"Here! Over here! Thieves — thieves at the tollhouse — help, please, somebody—"

Herr Brandt.

Sable's mind refused it. Brandt was home. Brandt was asleep with a book on his chest. But the voice kept coming, closer, and it was unmistakably the keeper's voice, pitched into a panic so artless it could only be performance, and Sable understood with a horror that arrived whole, like a gear stripping its teeth, that the old man had watched them go, had followed, had seen the patrol's lantern before they had — and had chosen.

Boots thundered past their hiding place, four soldiers running toward the new light, shouting for the thief to stand. Sable pressed his hand over Ada's mouth though she hadn't made a sound. Through the gap in the parapet bricks he saw the lanterns converge near the bridge's foot, saw a small stooped figure in a heavy coat raise his arms, saw the figure turn — deliberately, unhurriedly — and look back up the embankment, straight toward the coal chute, toward the dark where they crouched.

Brandt couldn't have seen them. It was too dark. But Sable felt the look anyway, felt it land like a hand on the shoulder, and in the lantern light the old man's face did something no frightened man's face should do.

It smiled. Briefly. Then the soldiers took his arms, and the smile was gone, and the voice was pleading again, confused, an old man protesting a misunderstanding — and the patrol marched him away up the bridge road toward the garrison, his abandoned lantern left burning on the ice like a small yellow wound.

* * *

*Ada*

They did not speak until they were three streets from the river, in the doorway of the disused chandlery where the watch never bothered to look. Ada's hands shook so badly she could not manage the satchel straps, and Sable took it from her without comment, and for a moment they stood in the freezing dark holding the bag between them like something alive.

"He knew," she said finally. "He knew the risk and he walked into it."

"He bought us the street," Sable said. His voice was very quiet. "Maybe more than the street. They'll question him. They'll hold him."

"Then we get him out."

"No." He said it immediately, and she heard what it cost him. "Ada. If we move for him, everything ends. The record, the certification, all of it. He knows that better than any of us. He's kept this secret for twelve years — do you think he'd trade it now for his own comfort?"

"That isn't your choice to make for him."

"No," he agreed. "But it might be the choice he's already made for us."

She wanted to argue. She assembled the argument — its premises, its citations, the whole architecture of it — and found that every beam of it rested on the same foundation: that Brandt had been taken unaware, that he had not chosen, that they still had time to undo it. And she knew, with the terrible precision her father had trained into her, that the record did not support it.

The satchel sat between them, heavy with forty-one sworn voices. Twenty-one of them. Half the truth, saved. The other half lying in the dark under the bridge, waiting for a second night that the garrison's sweeps might permit and might not.

"We deliver these tomorrow," she said at last. "To Ilse Marren. Before Kessler hears one word of what happened at the tollhouse tonight. And then—" Her voice failed and she rebuilt it. "Then we find out what they're charging him with."

Sable nodded. As they slipped out of the doorway and into the frozen streets, he said, so softly she almost missed it:

"When they ask him who helped him — and they will ask — he'll give them something. He always has an answer ready. God help us, I hope it's a good one."

Across the town, in a lit window of the garrison headquarters, a lamp burned late. Behind it, a man with careful hands opened a file marked *TOLLHOUSE — INCIDENT*, and began, methodically, to write.
