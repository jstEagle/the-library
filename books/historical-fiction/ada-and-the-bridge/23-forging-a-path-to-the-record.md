## 23. Forging a Path to the Record

Ada had learned the annex's rules the way she had learned everything since her father died: by copying them.

The armistice annex was governed by Article Eleven of the provisional protocol — she had transcribed it twice, once for the garrison commandant and once, in fair hand, for the historians' commission — and it said, in the dry language that treaties used to disguise their mercy, that *any party to the negotiation may submit documentary evidence to be entered into the record of the proceedings, provided such evidence bears certification by a neutral signatory and attestation by a witness of standing*.

Provided. The word did all the work. Anyone might submit; almost no one could.

She sat in the records office after the lamps had been trimmed for the night shift, a single candle burning low beside her copying slate, and drew on scrap paper the shape of the problem as she understood it. Not words — words were dangerous here, even innocent ones, and she had trained herself out of the habit of writing what could hang her. She drew boxes. A box for the ledger. A box for the depositions they believed waited beneath the tollhouse, cold and patient under occupied stone. Between those boxes and the annex itself, three more boxes, connected by lines like the gears of some mechanism she was only beginning to see whole.

A neutral signatory. A certified witness. A filing clerk.

Three names she did not yet have. Three people who would have to agree to risk themselves for a town most of them owed nothing to.

She thought of her father's hands moving across his ledgers at night, the small scratch of the pen, the way he would pause over a name as though weighing it before committing ink to paper. *Every entry is a promise*, he used to tell her, when she was small enough to sit on his knee and watch. *You are promising that this happened. You are promising you will not take it back tomorrow.* She had been eight. She had not understood. She understood now, sitting in an office built by the men who had killed him, surrounded by documents whose entire purpose was the careful, orderly unmaking of what had happened.

The candle guttered. Outside, snow came down over Vellmark with the soft persistence of something that meant to stay.

* * *

She began, the next morning, with the neutral signatory, because without one nothing else mattered.

There were exactly four neutral parties attached to the armistice process in any capacity. Two were commercial delegations from trading leagues with interests in the river tolls — compromised before they unpacked. One was a military observer from a kingdom so distant its name appeared in Vellmark's records only as a source of dyestuffs; Ada had checked, and the observer had dined twice with Kessler already. That left the medical delegation: the relief commission, four souls housed in the converted granary by the north gate, who had arrived in autumn with crates of bandages and stayed through the freeze because wounded men do not observe armistice schedules.

And among them, Ada remembered, a woman.

She had seen her only once, in passing — a tall figure in the grey-and-white of the relief service crossing the custom house yard with a satchel of documents held against the wind, unhurried where everyone else hurried, as though she carried her own weather with her. The clerks had spoken of her with a particular kind of grudging respect. *The delegate*, they called her, or *old Marren*. Ilse Marren. Twenty-six years in the field service, someone said. Had certified prisoner exchanges on three fronts. Had, according to a story told half-admiringly in the mess, once refused to countersign a prisoner list until two names that had been struck off were restored to it, and had stood in the snow for a day and a night until they were.

If half that story was true, she was the person Ada needed. If all of it was true, she was the most dangerous person in Vellmark — dangerous to the plan, because a woman like that could not be managed, could not be steered, could only be trusted. And trust, in the winter city, was the one currency Ada had almost none of.

She copied the relief delegation's supply manifests that morning — it was her assigned work, harmless work, work that let her eyes travel where they needed to go — and she noted that Delegate Marren's signature appeared on eleven documents, each signature slightly different in pressure but identical in formation, the letters upright and economical, a hand that wasted nothing. A hand that had signed true things for twenty-six years and knew the weight of signing.

One name. A gamble, then. But a calculated one, and Ada wrote the calculation nowhere at all, keeping it behind her eyes where Kessler's clerks could not copy it.

* * *

The second box troubled her more.

*A witness of standing.* The phrase had a legal pedigree she could trace through the older protocols in the archive — standing meaning property, or office, or profession recognized by both parties. A washerwoman would not do. A clockmaker would not do, though Sable Dravenmoor's testimony lived inside him like a swallowed coal; she felt it in him every time the tannery came near the conversation, a heat he banked and covered and refused to look at directly. What stood between him and standing was twelve years of deliberate silence, and silence, she knew better than anyone alive, does not burnish a reputation. It erodes one. It leaves a man with no history at all, and a man with no history cannot attest to anything.

No. The witness had to come from the other side of the ledger — someone whose standing the victors themselves had established, whom they could not dismiss without dismissing their own machinery.

She spent two evenings going through the garrison's own appointment rolls, copying them faithfully while her mind ran underneath like water under ice. And there, in the middle of the second evening, she found the shape of it: chaplains and physicians attached to the occupation were accorded standing in all proceedings touching the welfare of the civilian population. It was written into the protocol itself, Article Nine, sub-section four. The victors had built the door into their own wall. They simply assumed no one from the town would ever have cause — or courage — to walk through it.

Father Anselm, then, at St. Brigit's-under-the-Hill, who had buried eleven of the tannery dead in consecrated ground when the order had come down that they were to go into the lime pit unnamed. He had kept the parish register open that night, Ada's father had written in the ledger, *and recorded each name in his own hand against the civil prohibition, noting time of death and condition of body, and I have seen the page.* Her father had counted Father Anselm among his three sources. The other two were dead. The priest remained, old now, half-deaf, tending a congregation that the war had thinned to widows and boys too young to have done whatever it was the reprisals punished.

An old deaf priest. A gamble. But her father had trusted him with names, and her father had trusted almost no one, and trust passed down like that — from the dead to the living, hand to hand across the years — seemed to Ada the only kind worth having.

* * *

It was the third name that frightened her.

A neutral signatory certifies the document. A witness of standing attests to its provenance. But between certification and entry into the record lay the whole apparatus of the secretariat: the trays, the queues, the intake stamps, the junior clerks with their rubber-tipped fingers and their authority to lose a paper forever simply by setting it face-down. A document could be perfect, certified, attested, sealed in wax and prayer — and if it reached the wrong tray it would surface again, if ever, in Kessler's own files, catalogued as evidence against whoever had submitted it.

They needed someone inside. Someone with access to the intake chain and the will to use it crookedly in the service of straight things.

Ada reviewed the junior clerks in her head the way she had taught herself to review everyone in this building: by what she had seen them keep, and what she had seen them destroy. Most of them were grey men doing grey work, careful as cats about their own safety. Herr Fenn, who wept quietly at his desk on the anniversary of something he never named. The twins Brandl, who competed at speed and cared for nothing else. Young Tomas Rey —

She stopped there, pen lifted, and made herself think slowly.

Tomas Rey. Nineteen, perhaps twenty. Quiet in a way she recognized, because it was her own quiet — not emptiness but containment. He had come south with the occupation staff the previous summer and requested, unusually, assignment to local records rather than field administration. He was competent without being ambitious. He never volunteered. And once — once, three weeks ago, sorting the destruction queue — Ada had watched him lift a requisition order for the pulping of the municipal court archives, read it, and set it back down in the stack upside down.

It should have gone to the incinerator detail on the morning cart. When the cart came, the order was missing. It turned up two days later misfiled in the land-survey series, where it would sit, inert and forgotten, until the war ended or the paper rotted. An accident? Clerks made mistakes; she had made them herself, deliberately, a hundred times. But she had watched his face as he set it down. He had not looked like a man making an error. He had looked like a man planting something.

She began, very carefully, to watch him.

For six days she watched, and the pattern held like a watermark. A destruction order for the harbor master's logs, misfiled into shipping insurance. A directive regarding the school registers, sent sideways into a series that had not been touched since spring. Never the important things — he left those alone entirely, perhaps wisely, perhaps because he could not reach them — but always something. Small salvations, performed invisibly, at intervals regular enough to be intention.

He was saving what he could, the way she memorized what she could, the way her father had hidden what he could in a clock tower. All over the occupied city, Ada realized, in offices and kitchens and parish sacristies, people were conducting the same quiet war she was: the war of the small preservation, the single document slipped past the fire. They had never spoken of it because speaking was death. But they were legion, and they were hers, if she could find a way to ask without asking.

* * *

On the seventh evening, she engineered the meeting.

The destruction queue required two clerks to verify and sign — a rule designed to prevent exactly what Tomas Rey had been doing, and defeated by it, as all such rules are, because rules assume the conspirators are strangers. Ada arranged, through the mild administrative friction she was expert at generating, to be paired with him on the evening verification. They worked through the stack in the yellow light of the office lamps, their signatures falling alternately down the margin like two kinds of rain, and neither spoke beyond the necessities until the stack was done.

Then, closing the ledger, Ada said, in the tone of a tired colleague making tired talk:

"You misfiled the harbor logs."

The lamp flame leaned. Somewhere down the corridor a door closed, and they both went still in the way of people who have practiced stillness, and when the sound faded she saw that he had not denied it and was not going to.

"Did I," he said.

"The shipping insurance series. Sub-series nine." She kept her voice level, bored almost, the voice of a clerk correcting an error. "I put it right."

For a long moment he looked at her, and she watched the calculation move behind his eyes — the same calculation she performed daily, threat or ally, grave or door — and then something in his shoulders changed, a settling, like a man deciding which weight to carry.

"They burned my brother's regimental record," he said quietly. "After the reprisals. There was a list of men taken, and then there wasn't a list. My mother writes to me every month asking me to find it. There is nothing to find. Someone decided there shouldn't be." He squared the verified stack against the desk, precisely, unnecessarily. "So I make sure some things aren't found either. It isn't much."

"It's a great deal," Ada said. "It may need to be a great deal more."

He looked up sharply. Outside the frosted window the observatory hill stood black against the last light, and the master clock, running again these many weeks, was striking the hour — she could hear it faintly even here, even through glass and snow, the patient counting of a machine that kept what was given to it.

"What more?" Rey asked.

And because the hour was late, and the corridor empty, and because she had carried the plan alone long enough that its weight had begun to deform her, Ada told him — not everything, not yet, but the shape of it: that there existed a record of the tannery, sworn and detailed; that it could still enter the annex if three conditions were met before midwinter; and that the third condition was a man at the intake table willing to receive a certified document and send it where treaties say such documents must go, rather than where commissions prefer they go.

Rey listened without moving. When she finished he was silent for the length of ten heartbeats — she counted, because counting steadied her — and then he said, "My brother's name is Anton Rey. If the record is entered, it enters complete. Every list restored. Every name."

"Complete," Ada agreed. "That is the whole point of it."

He nodded once, and picked up his cap, and at the door he paused with his back to her and said, "The night verification falls to us again on Thursday. Bring your shape of it. We'll want to know which tray."

Then he was gone, and Ada sat alone with the lamp and the snow ticking against the glass, and took out her scrap of paper, and in the smallest hand she possessed drew a fourth box, and connected it to the rest.

Four fragile people, she thought. A priest who was half-deaf and wholly brave. A delegate who had stood in the snow for the sake of two names. A boy who saved papers one misfiling at a time. And herself — the daughter of the murdered clerk, living under a name that was not hers, assembling a truth out of borrowed courage because she had none of her own to spare.

Each name a gamble. Each gamble a life that could be taken apart by one careless afternoon of Kessler's attention.

She folded the paper small and held it over the candle, watching the plan curl and bloom into flame, because the plan could live nowhere but in her, now. In her, and in the clock, and in whatever lay waiting under the frozen river with the patience of the drowned.

Thursday, she thought, pinching out the wick. Thursday we learn which tray.

And far above the sleeping town, through snow and dark, the master clock struck the quarter hour — steady, indifferent, and keeping.
