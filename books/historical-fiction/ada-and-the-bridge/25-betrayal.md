## 25. Betrayal

The washhouse on Tanner's Lane still smelled of lye and wet wool in winter, but Marta Quill had long since stopped smelling it. Forty years of steam had scoured the sense out of her nose the way the river scoured the bridge pilings every spring — patiently, thoroughly, until nothing sharp remained. She was grateful for that, most days. A woman who could not smell the tannery two streets over could almost forget what had been rendered there one November night twelve years ago.

Almost.

She fed another bundle through the mangle, cranking with the flat of her palm because her knuckles had gone bad in the cold, and watched the water press out of a garrison shirt in thin dark lines. Occupier linen now. Half her trade had gone to the delegation since they'd come to winter in Vellmark — officers' collars, clerks' cuffs, the endless white shirts of men who sat at tables and wrote things down. They paid well and tipped better and never haggled, and every basket she carried back up to the Custom House felt like carrying coals into a house where you hoped not to be noticed by the fire.

Marta had survived twelve years by being exactly this kind of woman: broad-backed, red-handed, quick with a joke about the weather and slow with anything that mattered. When the wagons came to the tannery that night — when she'd stood in her doorway with a lamp she hadn't dared light, counting wheels in the dark, counting the shapes slumped in them, counting until she lost count and made herself stop — she had done precisely nothing. She'd gone inside. She'd banked her fire. In the morning she'd washed the garrison's linens as though soap could reach everything.

It was a good bargain. It had held for twelve years.

It broke on a Tuesday, in the third week of December, when the young officer with the careful boots came to her door while she was hanging sheets in the freezing yard.

* * *

He wasn't one of hers. That was the first thing she noticed, and it frightened her more than the uniform did. The officers whose shirts she washed were known quantities; she knew their stains, their sizes, which of them chewed their collar-points and which pretended not to sweat. This one wore his wool like it had been fitted yesterday, and he stood at the edge of her clothesline reading the tags pinned to the sheets as if they were dispatches.

"Frau Quill." He said it correctly, which meant someone had written it down for him. "Captain Veyr. May I trouble you?"

"You can trouble me all you like if you're bringing work." She kept her hands moving. Pegs. Sheet. Peg. A woman's hands busy are a woman's hands safe; her mother had taught her that before she taught her letters.

"Work, yes. Eventually." He smiled the way frost forms on glass — gradually, from an edge inward. "I'm told you knew Herr Elias Voss."

The peg in her fingers went suddenly very small and very heavy. "Knew him? Everyone knew him. Court clerk. Quiet man. Dead man, these twelve years." She snapped the last peg home harder than she needed to. "If you've got shirts for me, Captain, bring them round the side."

"I have questions, actually." He didn't move from the line. Snow was starting again, fine and dry, the kind that doesn't melt on wool. "Small ones. The sort that take a moment and save everyone time later. You lived on Tanner's Lane in the year of the reprisals."

It was not a question, so she did not answer it. She took down an empty basket instead, giving herself something to hold, something between them wider than the width of a sheet.

"You saw the wagons," Captain Veyr said gently. "On the night of the fourteenth."

"There were always wagons on Tanner's Lane. Tannery carts, night soil carts—"

"These were not tannery carts." Still gentle. He might have been asking after her health. "You counted eleven. You stood in your doorway with your lamp unlit because lighting it would have meant being seen seeing. Isn't that right, Frau Quill?"

The yard went very quiet around her — the particular quiet of snow beginning, absorbing sound before it lands. Marta heard her own breathing and hated how loud it was.

"Who told you that?" Her voice came out lower than she intended. "Who's been saying I saw anything?"

"No one has said anything. That's rather the point." He stepped closer, and now she caught his smell — clean wool, ink, and under it something like the inside of a drawer where paper has lain a long time. "Twelve years ago, no one said anything, and a clerk died with his ledger unfinished, and a town agreed together to have seen nothing. And now there is a commission here writing the history of this war, Frau Quill, and my superior believes — he is a careful man, my superior — that histories built on silence are houses built on ice. Someone will fall through eventually. Better we find the thin places ourselves."

"And if I've nothing to say?"

"Then you'll say it pleasantly, and I'll write down that Frau Quill, washerwoman, saw nothing, knows nothing, and has served the delegation faithfully with her laundry since autumn." He produced a small notebook — black, gilt-edged, the kind clerks carry — and opened it against his palm without writing anything yet. "That entry costs you nothing today. But entries accumulate, Frau Quill. And there are other kinds."

She looked past him, down the lane, at the ordinary world going on: smoke from chimneys, a boy dragging a sledge, the tannery roof white with snow as though it had been forgiven. Twelve years of ordinary world, purchased at a price she had paid once and thought was final.

"What other kinds?" she asked, and knew, even as she asked it, that asking was already the wrong answer.

* * *

They left her the afternoon to think about it, which was its own cruelty. She washed. She wrung. She mangled. She carried baskets through streets that seemed, for the first time in twelve years, full of eyes — though when she looked directly at any of them, the eyes were only windows, only strangers, only the same gray faces she'd passed ten thousand times.

But the mind does its own arithmetic whether or not the mouth consents. Sitting by the copper that evening, stirring shirts with the wooden paddle, Marta found herself remembering things she had spent a decade not-remembering, and the terrible thing was how easily they came. Not buried at all, then. Just stacked, the way she stacked finished linen: flat, orderly, waiting for someone to ask for them by name.

She remembered the wagons. Eleven — he'd said eleven and she'd flinched, because eleven was right, she'd counted eleven, and she had never told anyone the number, not her husband, God rest him, not the priest, not anyone. Which meant either the captain had guessed, or someone else who'd seen that night had talked before she had.

She remembered the tannery foreman's face the next morning, gray as tallow, opening the gates early and hosing the yard down in freezing weather when every sensible man let ice do the work.

And she remembered — and here her hand went still in the warm water — she remembered the observatory.

Because that was the thing, wasn't it. The thing she had folded smallest and pushed deepest. Two nights after the wagons, unable to sleep, she had climbed the hill path toward the observatory district — not going anywhere, just walking, the way grief walks people — and she had seen a light burning in the tower at an hour when the keeper should have been abed. And below, at the base of the hill, half-hidden by the wall, a figure standing in the snow watching the lit window. Not walking. Standing. A small figure. A boy, she'd thought then. Some child unable to sleep too.

She had thought nothing of it. Children couldn't sleep in those weeks; the whole town was an unslept child. She had turned back down the hill and put the image away with the rest.

Now it rose up out of the stack like a sheet shaken loose in wind, and she could not smooth it flat again.

* * *

The captain came back the next morning, and the morning after that, and each time he brought no threats at all, which was worse than any threat. He brought courtesy. He brought small gifts — sugar, once, real sugar, worth more than her week's takings. He brought questions shaped like kindnesses.

"Your son, Frau Quill. Pieter. He's on the river barges now?"

"He's a grown man with his own life, Captain."

"Of course. Barges pass through occupied territory, though. Permits, inspections. So much paperwork on the rivers these winters. One would hate for a permit to go astray over a misunderstanding."

There it was. Not even hidden. Marta stood in her yard with the sugar in her fist, squeezing it through the paper until it creaked, and understood that she was not being asked to betray anyone. She was being asked to confirm a suspicion the captain already owned, and the only question left was whether she would be paid for it in safety or in ruin. Twelve years ago she had chosen silence and it had cost her nothing but the slow rot of knowing. Now silence itself had become the crime.

On the fourth morning he set the black notebook open on her scrubbed table and laid a pencil across it, crosswise, like a small barrier.

"Just this," he said. "A list. Everything you remember about the nights following the fourteenth. Lights, movements, persons. Anything unusual. You needn't interpret, Frau Quill. Interpretation is my trade. Memory is yours."

"My memory's old and full of holes."

"All memory is. We mend the holes together." He turned the notebook toward her. "Or I can mend them myself, from whatever materials come to hand. I'd rather use yours. Yours are honest materials. I can tell."

"How can you possibly tell?"

"Because you haven't lied yet. Liars lie quickly and completely. You've only been quiet, and quiet is a different material entirely. Quiet means there's something in it." He smiled his frost-forming smile. "Take your time. I'll call tomorrow."

He called three times. On the third visit, Pieter's barge-permit lay on the table beside the notebook, and Marta understood that the hour of choosing had closed behind her somewhere without her hearing the door.

She picked up the pencil.

* * *

What she wrote filled two pages, and it was true, and that was the unforgivable part — she could feel the truthfulness of it in her own hand, the way the words came fast once started, the way details surfaced she hadn't summoned. The light in the tower. The figure in the snow. The foreman hosing the yard. Old Hessel the night watchman, dead now himself, who'd once gotten drunk on the anniversary and wept about "the count" without ever saying what count. She wrote it all in her careful washerwoman's script, the script she used for marking linens, each letter upright and blameless.

At the bottom the captain added a line of his own and read it aloud to her before she signed, because he was scrupulous that way, or wanted her to believe he was:

*"Witness states that unusual activity centered upon the observatory tower in the weeks following the reprisals, and that persons unknown visited the tower at irregular hours during the present season."*

"The present season," Marta said. "I never wrote that."

"No. But you told me, when we spoke on Tuesday, that you'd delivered laundry up the hill twice this month. To the observatory. For the clockmaker working there." He tapped the page with one finger. "And that on your second delivery, the door was barred, though the appointment was made. Barred doors at noon are unusual, Frau Quill. Unusual things cluster. Light in a tower twelve years ago, a barred door this month — a historian sees a pattern. My superior sees one too. All you've done is help us see it sooner."

"I told you about the laundry because you asked where I'd walked."

"I know." And for a moment — one strange, unwelcome moment — the captain looked tired, almost sorry, a man doing arithmetic he disliked. "None of this is malice, Frau Quill. It's weather. Cold fronts move, and towns stand in the way. Sign, please."

She signed. Marta Quill, in her upright blameless letters, and the pencil scratch of it sounded to her like the sound a sheet makes tearing along a fold — one small rip, and then the whole cloth gives, and no mangle in the world presses it back whole.

He folded the pages away inside his coat, thanked her for the tea she hadn't offered, and paused at the door.

"For what it's worth," he said, not turning around, "the clockmaker interests us anyway. Clocks stopped the night Voss died, clocks repaired the month historians arrive — my superior collects coincidences the way some men collect medals. You've only shown him the shape of a thing he was already reaching for." Now he did turn, and his face was perfectly pleasant. "Sleep well, Frau Quill. Your son's papers will be renewed by Friday."

The door shut. The snow went on falling in the yard, covering her footprints and the captain's alike, making the ground look untouched, the way it had looked for twelve years, the way nothing would ever look again.

Marta stood in the middle of her washhouse with her hands hanging empty at her sides, and slowly, joint by joint, she began to shake — not weeping, not yet, just the body's honest revolt against what the mind had signed. She thought of the clockmaker's face the day he'd taken delivery of his collars, young and careful and looking at her as though he too were keeping count of things. She thought of the girl at the Custom House, the copyist with the steady eyes who'd asked her once, gently, whether the water on Tanner's Lane ran clear in spring.

Oh, she thought. Oh, what have I done. What thread have I just handed them, and whose throat is it wound around?

Up the hill, past the barred door, behind the running clock, she did not know what moved or who hid there. But she knew the sound a town makes when the soldiers start knocking on doors in earnest. She'd heard it once before, twelve years ago, and she had sworn — with lamp unlit, with door shut, with the whole weight of her silence — never to hear it again.

By Friday, she promised herself, staring at the snow, by Friday she would find a way to warn them.

Behind her, on the table, the faint dent of the pencil still marked the wood where the captain's notebook had lain — a shallow groove, like the first hairline crack in a casing, running straight toward the observatory hill.
