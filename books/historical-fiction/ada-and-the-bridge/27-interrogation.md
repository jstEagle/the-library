## 27. Interrogation

They came for her at the copying tables.

Ada had learned to read a room from the doorway — the angle of the light, the position of the senior clerks' chairs, the small weather of men's moods expressed in how they set down their inkwells. So she knew before she looked up that morning that something was wrong, because the room's ordinary noise had thinned the way air thins before snow. The scratch of pens continued, but underneath it there was a listening quality, as though every copyist in the hall were writing with one ear turned toward the door.

Two garrison soldiers stood at the end of the long table where she worked. Not marching in — standing. Waiting. That was worse than marching. Soldiers who marched were following orders; soldiers who waited had been told to wait for someone specific.

"Copyist Lene Ferrin," said the nearer one, not quite a question. He held a folded paper. Her false name in someone else's handwriting — she could see the loops of it upside down, and noted with a distant, clinical part of her mind that the clerk who'd written it pressed too hard on his downstrokes. A nervous hand, or an emphatic one. It mattered which.

"That's me," Ada said, and heard her own voice come out level, slightly bored, the voice of a woman interrupted over nothing. Twelve years of practice lived in that voice. She laid her pen across the blotter, squared the document she'd been copying — a requisition schedule, harmless, dull — and rose. "Am I wanted for something?"

"You're wanted," the soldier said, "for questioning."

The word *questioning* did a strange thing to the room. She felt it move through the other copyists like a current through water — not panic, nothing so legible as panic, but a collective leaning-away, a sudden fascination with their own pages. Only old Frau Behrend, three seats down, let her eyes flick up for half a second: *hold steady, girl.* Or perhaps Ada imagined it. Perhaps it was only an old woman checking whether the ink on her own page had dried.

Ada gathered her shawl. She did not hurry. Hurrying was a confession in its own grammar. She folded the shawl over her arm the way she always did, checked — visibly, deliberately — that her work was left in order, weighted under the paper knife, and walked between the soldiers to the door as though they were escorts to some tedious administrative errand. Inside, beneath the ribs, her heart was going like a bird against glass. But inside was hers. Inside had always been hers.

*They know about Marta,* she thought, as the door closed behind her and the cold of the corridor took her breath. *Marta retracted once already; if she's spoken again, then Kessler has a thread, and the thread runs through the observatory, and everyone who touches the observatory gets pulled.* She catalogued it the way she catalogued everything now, everything dangerous: names, dates, connections, entered into the ledger of her memory where no raid could reach it. Sable. Brandt. The clock. The cylinder that no longer lived in the clock because it lived somewhere better now, hidden inside the very machinery of the town's official time.

*And me,* she added. *Lene Ferrin, copyist, third year of service, no family, no history worth the ink.*

That last part was the problem. That last part had been the whole architecture of her survival, and this morning, for reasons she could not yet see, someone wanted to test whether it would bear weight.

* * *

The records office kept its interrogations in the annex — a low stone room behind the registry vaults that had once stored salted fish, according to Frau Behrend, back when the building belonged to the customs house, back before the war had made paperwork into a weapon. They had scrubbed the smell out, but the cold stayed; cold lived in old stone the way grief lived in old families, beyond the reach of any cleaning.

There was a table. Two chairs. A lamp on a chain, unlit — daylight came gray through a high slit window, enough to see by, chosen precisely so that nothing in the room would feel like comfort or like theater. Ada noted all of it, noting being what she did instead of screaming, and sat in the chair facing the door without being asked, because being asked would have put her one request behind before anything began.

Herr Kessler came in alone.

She had seen him four times before, always at a distance, always in motion through the corridors of the occupying administration — a broad, unhurried man with silvering hair and spectacles that he wore pushed up on his forehead when reading and lowered onto his nose when looking at people, as though people required a different instrument than documents. She had built her picture of him out of those glimpses and out of the paper trail he left behind him, because a man's papers were his true portrait and Kessler's papers were immaculate: marginalia in a fine controlled hand, corrections that improved rather than merely corrected, a mind that filed the world the way she filed it.

Now he closed the door himself, softly, and took the chair opposite her, and lowered the spectacles onto his nose, and looked at her.

"Copyist Ferrin." His voice was mild. Mildness, Ada knew, was the most expensive thing a man in his position could wear; it cost more than shouting and bought more. "Thank you for coming so promptly."

"I wasn't given the option," she said.

A flicker — not quite a smile, but the place where a smile would go if he kept such things. "No. You weren't. I'll try not to waste your morning." He produced a notebook, small, leather-bound, and a pencil, and did not open either. They lay on the table between them like two sleeping animals. "You've been in our employ three years. Your file is thin — that's not a criticism; thin files are honest files. You came to Vellmark during the second winter of the war. No family listed. References from the parish relief office in Halden, which is burned now, along with rather a lot of its records. Unfortunate."

"Bombs are unfortunate," Ada agreed. "I was in Halden when it happened. I lost—" She stopped herself at exactly the right length. A rehearsed pause. Grief, portioned. "I lost everyone I'd have listed."

"I'm sorry," Kessler said, and the terrible thing was that he sounded as though he meant it. He might have. Men like Kessler could mean things and still do what they did; that was what made them what they were. "You copy well. My staff speak well of you. Meticulous, they say. A memory for detail." He tilted his head slightly. "An unusual memory, they say."

"Copying is detail."

"Copying is detail," he conceded. "But you do more than copy. You notice. Last month you flagged an arithmetic error in a supply manifest that three clerks above you had passed. In autumn you observed that two destruction orders bore sequential serial numbers but dated six weeks apart, and brought it to your supervisor's attention." He opened the notebook now, unhurried, and made a small mark in it — she could not see what. "Most copyists copy. You audit. May I ask why?"

"Because numbers that disagree are errors, and errors get innocent people shorted on rations." She let a breath of impatience color it. "I watched a warehouse clerk's mistake cut a village's flour allotment by a third. Nobody caught it for a month. If my noticing inconveniences anyone, I'm sorry, but I'd rather inconvenience a clerk than starve a village."

It was a good answer. She knew it was good even as it left her, because it was true — that was the discipline of her life now, to build every layer of the lie out of load-bearing truth. Kessler wrote another small mark.

"And yet," he said gently, "the discrepancies you notice have a pattern."

The cold of the room moved closer. Ada kept her hands still on her knees.

"A pattern?"

"Destruction orders. Misfiled requisitions. Documents that should have reached my commission and didn't." He looked up from the notebook, and behind the spectacles his eyes were not unkind, which was somehow far worse. "I don't think you're careless, Copyist Ferrin. I think you're careful. And I find myself wondering what a woman with no family, no history, no attachments worth listing — a woman whose entire past burned in Halden — is being so very careful *for*."

*He knows,* said the voice in her chest, the animal voice, the one below the discipline. *He doesn't know what, but he knows something, and he has brought you here to watch which way you break.*

She let the silence sit for a count of three, then sighed — a small, tired, worker's sigh.

"Herr Kessler. I copy eight hundred pages a week in a language half of which isn't mine. Yes, I notice things. Noticing is how I keep the work from flattening me. If noticing is now a crime, then arrest me, but I'd like it written down that I asked what the charge was."

"No charge." He closed the notebook, and she understood that the interview, so far, had been the overture. What followed was the piece itself. "I wanted to meet you. That's all. I make a point of meeting people whose names cross my desk more than twice." He rose, and she rose with him, and she thought — for one full, foolish breath — *that's all, it's over, hold, you held* —

At the door, pausing with his hand on the latch, he said, without turning:

"Elias Voss had a daughter, I believe. The court clerk who died twelve years ago. There was a girl. Eleven years old, or near it." He turned then, spectacles catching the gray window-light so she couldn't read his eyes. "The parish register lists her as dead. Fever, the same winter. But registers burn too, don't they. Along with rather a lot of their records." A beat, gentle as snowfall. "Did you ever hear of her, Copyist Ferrin? In Halden, or anywhere?"

* * *

Afterward — after she had answered, after she had been escorted back through the corridors with the same unhurried courtesy, after the annex door had shut behind her — Ada would take the scene apart a hundred times, entry by entry, the way she took apart everything that frightened her, until fear became structure and structure could be managed.

What she remembered saying was: "Voss. There was a clerk named Voss in the old court — my father dealt with him once over a land title, I think. I was a child. I remember the name more than the man." And then, letting puzzlement crease her brow just so: "Dead, was he? How?"

"Fever," said Kessler, watching her. "According to the record."

"The record again," Ada said. "You seem to have doubts about records today, Herr Kessler."

And Kessler had laughed — a real laugh, short and quiet, almost admiring — and said, "So I do, Copyist. So I do. Mind how you walk home. The ice on the bridge steps takes people every year."

Then he had gone, and the soldiers had walked her back to the copying hall as though escorting her home from church, and she had sat down at her table, and taken up her pen, and copied forty-one pages without a single error while her blood rang like struck metal.

* * *

She did not go straight to the observatory.

This was discipline, not cowardice — though by evening she could no longer fully separate the two, and sat in the rented room she kept in the tanner's widow's house with the lamp turned low, forcing herself to wait out the hours until a visit to the tower would look like nothing more than what it had always looked like: the copyist who brought Herr Brandt his newspapers and his cough lozenges, because old keepers needed someone, and she was a girl who noticed things.

*Fever,* Kessler had said. *According to the record.* But Elias Voss had not died of fever. Elias Voss had died with his skull broken on the ice below the bridge, and the record said fever because the record had been bought, and Kessler knew it — Kessler, who doubted records, who collected doubts the way other men collected medals. Which meant the question about the daughter was not a question. It was a key fitted to a lock, offered casually, to see whether she would flinch when he tried it in the door of her face.

*He suspects a survivor. He does not suspect me. Not yet.* She laid the facts in a row like instruments on felt. *He has a dead girl, eleven years old, and a living copyist with a burned past, and between those two facts there is nothing — nothing but instinct, and instinct cannot be entered into evidence.* Yet. Instinct became suspicion became warrant, the way water became ice, imperceptibly and then all at once, and midwinter was coming, and the signature was coming faster.

Her hands, finally, when she let them, were shaking. She watched them shake with a kind of detached interest, as if they belonged to another Lene — no. As if they belonged to Ada, the buried girl, the fever-dead girl, the one who had watched her father leave for the courthouse on the last morning and had memorized, God help her, the exact way his coat collar stood up on the left side where the lining had torn, because even at eleven she was already what she was, a keeper of useless precise details, and that detail had outlived him when nothing else had.

*He asked about her,* she thought. *He said her name in a room with me in it — my name, the name I haven't heard aloud in twelve years except in my own head at night — and I sat there and gave him a stranger's childhood and he wrote it down.* Her father's name, misspelled in a casualty list; her own name, resurrected as bait. The occupiers spent names like coin. Well. She had spent one back — had spent *Lene* freely, lavishly, until the false name was worn smooth with use and the true one stayed hidden in the dark of her like the cylinder in the clock, wired into the mechanism of her days, keeping time, keeping everything.

Tomorrow night they filed the record. Tomorrow night, if Tomas Rey held, if the neutral signatory came, if the seal — whatever miracle Sable was working with the seal — if all of it held, then Elias Voss's testimony would enter the annex under law, and after that it would not matter what Kessler suspected about a copyist, because the truth would be in the machine, official, certified, beyond the reach of burning.

*One day,* she told her shaking hands. *Hold one more day.*

A soft knock at the street door — the widow, bringing the supper she pretended not to charge for. Ada banked the lamp, went down, carried the bowl up, ate without tasting, and was washing the bowl when she saw, through the frost-ferned window, a figure standing across the lane under the broken lamp bracket. Not moving. Not hiding, either. Just a soldier, muffled to the eyes, planted in the snow like a post.

Watching her window.

Ada dried the bowl, set it away, and blew out the lamp, and stood in the dark doing sums: one watcher, one night, one day left. Whatever Kessler had decided about her, he had decided it tonight.

She began, quietly, to pack the things she could not bear to lose — her father's ring on its cord, the first page of his handwriting she had ever stolen back from destruction — and listened to the wind come off the river, and counted the hours until dawn, when the observatory stairs would creak under her feet one more time.
