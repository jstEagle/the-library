## 29. Countdown to Signature

Tomas Rey learned the news the way he learned everything now — by standing still in a corridor and letting other people's urgency wash past him like water around a stone.

He was carrying a tray of inkwells from the supply room to the annex floor when he heard it: two of Kessler's senior clerks, their voices pitched low but not low enough, moving fast in the opposite direction.

"—moved up. The signature. He's asked for the twentieth."

"The twentieth? The timetable said midwinter—"

"Kessler says midwinter is a sentiment, not a date. The delegates are already here, the annexes are drafted, and every day the city breathes is a day something can go wrong. His words. Something can go *wrong*."

The inkwells rattled faintly on the tray. Tomas kept walking, unhurried, a man with nothing on his mind but stationery, and turned the corner before he allowed his face to do anything at all.

Two days.

They had planned for three weeks. Three weeks had felt like both an eternity and not nearly enough — three weeks to authenticate, to certify, to find a filing clerk willing to risk his neck, which was where Tomas came in, and he had told himself there was time. Time to prepare. Time to rehearse. Time to become, gradually, the kind of man who could do what he had already secretly been doing for months, only openly, only all at once, with no way back.

Now there were two days, and the becoming would have to happen tonight.

* * *

The records office occupied the former customs house, and in winter its windows wept. Condensation ran down the glass in slow channels, and the light that got through was gray and thin as broth. Tomas sat at his desk in the corner — the desk they'd given him when he arrived eight months ago, junior enough to be invisible, senior enough to touch paper — and pretended to collate requisition forms while his mind ran the arithmetic of catastrophe.

Here was what he knew, assembled piece by piece over months of careful, deniable attention:

The armistice minutes were to be signed on the twentieth now, two days hence, in the great hall downstairs. With them would be signed a schedule of records transfer: all municipal archives, all court registers, all documents of any kind originating in Vellmark and its district would pass into the custody of Commissioner Kessler's historical commission, there to be catalogued, excerpted, and — this was the part Ada had explained to him with a flatness that had frightened him more than shouting would have — selectively destroyed. Not burned in the square. Nothing so honest. Simply lost. Misfiled beyond recovery. Reduced to a line item in an inventory: *papers, miscellaneous, deteriorated, disposed.*

He knew because he had been misfiling those destruction orders himself since autumn. A destruction order crossed his desk — *incinerate, lot forty-one, tannery district litigation, years nine through eleven* — and instead of routing it to the incinerator clerk he routed it to dead storage, sub-basement two, a shelf behind a broken stove where no one had looked in a decade. Then he forged the return receipt. It was the first dishonest thing he had ever done in his life, and he had done it the way a man steps off a ledge: without deciding to, discovering midair that he had already decided.

His brother Joren was the reason. Joren, taken in the reprisals after the tannery, listed among the *administratively transferred*, a phrase that meant nothing and covered everything. Two years now without a letter, without a grave, without so much as a number attached to his name. When Ada Voss — Lene, then, though he knew her true name now, had known since the night she stood in the archive stacks with candle-shadow under her eyes and told him who she was — when she had shown him the ledger, the names, the payments, the signatures, he had read his brother's name in the column of the taken and understood that the document was not history. It was a mirror. It showed him exactly what he was prepared to burn down.

And now he had two days.

* * *

She came to him at the end of the afternoon, in the one place they had agreed was safe: the map room, vast and cold, where the occupiers' surveyors hung their charts of a country whose borders were about to be redrawn. Anyone could be in the map room. No one ever was.

Ada wore her copyist's shawl and carried a folio of nothing important. Up close, in the gray light, Tomas could see how the last weeks had carved her — the sharpness at her cheekbones, the way her eyes moved to the door before settling on him. She had her father's face, he realized again; he had seen Elias Voss's portrait once, in the old town gazette, and the resemblance was a thing she could never fully hide, only soften.

"Tell me," she said. No greeting. There was no time left for greetings.

"It's true. The twentieth. I saw the amended schedule myself an hour ago — I pulled it, copied the reference number, put it back. Kessler petitioned the delegate directly. The neutral signatory has agreed." He hesitated. "There's more. The annex closes tomorrow at noon. Final inventory. After that, nothing enters or leaves without the commissioner's personal countersignature."

"Then we file tomorrow morning."

"We file *tonight*." He heard his own voice come out harder than he intended. "Lene—Ada. Listen to me. Tomorrow morning is what everyone will expect if they expect anything. Tonight the building empties. Half the clerks are seconded to hall preparations — bunting, chairs, the delegate's escort. The night rotation is four men, and I know three of them by name and habit. If the record enters the annex tonight, it's logged, sealed, and inside the treaty's own architecture before anyone wakes up. By noon tomorrow it's just another annexed document, one of two hundred, and pulling it out means explaining why. Kessler can't do that without opening the very question he's spent twelve years closing."

Ada was quiet. Outside, wind worried at the tall windows, and somewhere below, a work crew dragged something heavy across the flagstones of the courtyard — the scaffolding for the ceremony, perhaps, being built around a table where men would sign away the right to remember.

"You've thought about this," she said finally.

"I've thought about nothing else since you told me about the certification. Ilse Marren signed yesterday?" 

"Before dawn. In the observatory. She examined the seal, the hand, the witnesses. She certified it." Something moved through Ada's face when she said it — not triumph, something older and wearier, the look of a woman setting down a weight she had carried so long her shoulders didn't know how to straighten. "The record is legitimate. Now it only has to be *filed*."

"Then here is what tonight looks like." Tomas kept his voice level and low, the voice of a man discussing requisitions. "The annex intake closes at six. But the intake register doesn't close — it stays open for late military dispatches, always has. Any commissioned officer or authorized civilian clerk may enter a document for annexation up until the final inventory seals the room. I am an authorized civilian clerk. My authorization covers routine correspondence, which is what this will be dressed as — a certified record, submitted for inclusion per Article Eleven, bearing the neutral signatory's certification mark. Ilse Marren's name will stop the first clerk who touches it. Her name is the one word in this building that makes people afraid to be careless."

"And after you file it?"

"After I file it, it goes into the pending cabinet, and at seven o'clock the pending cabinet is locked, and the key—" He paused. "The key goes to the duty officer. Who is Henrick Saul. Who owes me money, dislikes Kessler, and drinks himself horizontal by nine. I have already asked him, hypothetically, whether a man might retrieve a misfiled dispatch after hours, and he told me hypothetically yes, for the price of the debt forgiven."

Ada stared at him. "You arranged this already."

"I arranged it the day Marren certified. I didn't tell you because if it fell apart there'd be nothing to confess." He almost smiled. "I'm learning how this works. You taught me."

For a moment neither of them spoke, and in the silence Tomas felt the strangeness of it again — that he, Tomas Rey, who had spent eight months being furniture, was now standing in a frozen map room planning the theft of history with the daughter of a murdered clerk, and that the plan was good, and that the fear sitting in his chest was not the fear of being caught but the fear of the hour between six and seven, the hour when the paper would pass out of his hands and into the machine, and anything could still go wrong.

"There's one problem," Ada said.

"One?"

"The certification is genuine, the seal is genuine, but the *depositions* — Sable recovered them from the tollhouse, and Brandt is in custody, and Marta Quill testified only yesterday. Kessler knows documents are moving. He pulled Marta in, she gave him the observatory, and even if the raid found nothing, he is not a man who stops looking. If he is in the building tonight—"

"He won't be. He'll be at the delegate's dinner. Pre-signature courtesies. I checked the engagement book." Tomas hesitated. "But his deputy, Vossler, will be here. And Vossler reads everything. He's the one who flagged the audit discrepancy I manufactured to get the medallion out of evidence — he signed the query. He doesn't know yet what he found. But he found the thread."

"Then we pull the thread first," Ada said, "or we cut it."

* * *

They parted before dusk, separately, by different stairs, and Tomas walked back across the courtyard through the falling dark feeling the whole city press in on him — the garrison horns sounding the change of watch, the ice groaning on the river, the bridge a black spine against the last gray light, closed now for five months and holding its secret in its foundations like a swallowed stone.

He thought about Joren.

He thought about it the way he thought about everything now, which was to say constantly and in fragments: Joren at fourteen, teaching him to whistle through cupped hands; Joren at nineteen, laughing on the ferry deck the summer before the reprisals; Joren at twenty-two, gone, reduced to a phrase in a government form. *Administratively transferred.* Somewhere in Kessler's commission archives — he was almost sure, he had traced the routing numbers twice — there was a transfer ledger with a page that named where the taken men had gone. He had not dared look for it yet. But if the record entered the annex tonight, if Vellmark's complicity became the official account, then the reprisals would become part of the record too, and the ledgers would have to be opened, and somewhere in them, perhaps, a number would attach itself to his brother's name. A place. A grave, even. Even grief needed coordinates.

That was what he was really filing tonight, he understood. Not a document. A set of coordinates for the dead.

* * *

At half past five he stood in the annex anteroom with the folder in his hands and waited for the room to empty.

The folder weighed almost nothing. That was the terrible thing about it — all that weight, twelve years of testimony, a massacre, a cover-up, a murdered clerk, a boy made silent, a girl made ghost, a washerwoman's terror, a keeper's sacrifice, a clockwork hiding place, a seal melted and remade — and it weighed less than the requisition forms he carried every day. Paper was like that. It held enormity lightly. It was the hands that shook.

The last of the day clerks filed out at ten to six, complaining about the cold and the bunting. The anteroom stove ticked. Through the inner door he could see the annex itself: long tables, the pending cabinet against the far wall, the intake register open on its lectern, a single lamp burning. Beyond it, the sealed doors of the archive proper, behind which lay the pre-drafted histories — he had seen Kessler's chapter on Vellmark once, by accident, in a stack awaiting the commissioner's revision, and the blank space where the tannery should have been had haunted him worse than any atrocity description could have. An absence, carefully ruled and margined, waiting to be permanent.

Henrick Saul appeared at six, keys jangling, red-eyed, smelling already of the flask in his coat. "Rey. Late dispatch?"

"Civilian submission. Certified record, Article Eleven." Tomas laid the folder on the lectern and opened the register to the correct page. "You'll want to log the certification mark. Red Cross delegate, Ilse Marren."

Saul squinted at the folder, at the seal impression, at the name. Something flickered behind his eyes — the instinct of a small man near a large thing — and then died, smothered under indifference and the prospect of the flask. "Marren. Huh. She's signing day after tomorrow." He licked his pencil. "Article Eleven, certified record, submitted Rey, authorized clerk. Time of receipt—" he consulted the wall clock "—six-oh-four."

"Pending cabinet," Tomas said.

"Pending cabinet." Saul heaved himself up, unlocked it, dropped the folder into the drawer among the evening's military dispatches, locked it, and pocketed the key. Then he paused, and for a moment the flickering instinct returned, and he looked at Tomas with the mild, dangerous curiosity of a man who has survived every regime by noticing things and choosing not to.

"You're the one been misfiling things," Saul said. Not accusing. Almost admiring. "Sub-basement two. Don't look surprised, boy, I did the dead-shelf inventory in November. Somebody's been very tidy back there." He shrugged his coat collar up. "Debt's forgiven either way. Whatever happens day after tomorrow — I was never good at hypotheticals, and I'm worse at them drunk. Go home, Rey."

He shuffled out. The lamp hissed. The stove ticked.

Tomas stood alone in the annex with the register entry drying under his hand — *6:04 p.m., certified record, Article Eleven* — and understood two things at once. The first was that it was done: the record was inside, logged, sealed in the pending cabinet, one step from the treaty's own body, and no one could remove it without explaining why, publicly, before the delegates, before the neutral signatory, before history.

The second was that Saul had known. Had known for weeks, perhaps, and had said nothing, and had chosen tonight, of all nights, to say it aloud — which meant either that Saul was safe, or that Saul had just told someone, or that Saul simply wanted Tomas to know he had counted the cost and found it acceptable. With Saul there was no way to tell. There never had been.

Tomas blew out the lamp and let himself into the dark corridor, and was halfway to the stairs when he saw the light.

It came from beneath the door of the commissioners' offices — a strip of gold on the black flagstones, where there should have been none, because Kessler was at the delegate's dinner and Vossler, as far as the engagement book showed, had left an hour ago.

Then he heard the voice. Low, unhurried, courteous, unmistakable — the voice of a man reading aloud to himself, savoring the shape of a sentence.

Commissioner Kessler had come back early.

And through the door, faintly, Tomas caught the words he was reading: *"—pending cabinet, entry six-oh-four, certified record, Article Eleven, submitter T. Rey—"*

The hook of the next chapter caught in Tomas's chest like a fishhook: Kessler had the register in his hands, and the countdown was no longer theirs alone.
