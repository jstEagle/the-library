## 30. Everything Depends on a Seal

The candle had burned down to its last thumb's width when Sable found the gap.

He had been through the depositions eleven times now — eleven slow, lamplit passes over Elias Voss's careful clerk's hand, checking each page against his own transcription, hunting for the flaw that would undo them. The paper was right. The hand was right. The dates aligned with the garrison duty rosters Tomas Rey had copied at such risk. Fourteen sworn statements, taken in the weeks after the tannery night, each one signed by a witness whose name Sable knew — some of them people he passed in the market without meeting their eyes, people who had spoken once, twelve years ago, and never again.

The documents were true. That was the cruelty of it. They were true and complete and utterly worthless, because they were missing the one thing that would make them more than paper: the seal of the Vellmark magistrate's office, pressed in violet wax at the foot of every certification, the embossed stamp that said *this record was entered under oath, witnessed, and lawfully kept*. Without it, Kessler's commission could dismiss the whole ledger as forgery with a shrug. With it — with it, the depositions became evidence, admissible to the annex, unignorable.

And the seal was gone.

Sable set down the magnifying loupe and pressed the heels of his hands into his eyes until he saw orange ghosts. He knew where the seal had gone, or rather he knew what Brandt had told him three nights ago, in the low urgent voice of a man confessing something he'd carried longer than his bad knee: that when the soldiers came to strip Voss's office after the murder, Brandt had reached the room first. That he had taken the seal matrix itself — the brass stamp, heavy as a door-knocker — because leaving it would have meant leaving the power to certify *anything*, including whatever false record the victors chose to write. That he had carried it home wrapped in his scarf like a feverish child.

And then, two years later, in the worst winter of the occupation's first stretch, when there was no coal and no work and his wife was coughing blood into a rag — Brandt had done what desperate men do with brass.

He had melted it.

Sable had sat with that confession in silence, watching the old keeper's hands tremble around a cup of tea he didn't drink. "I told myself it was just metal," Brandt had said. "Metal is metal. It warms the same either way." A pause. "It wasn't the same."

Now, alone in the observatory workroom with the master clock ticking above him like a patient heart, Sable turned the story over again and felt something shift — not despair, but the clockmaker's instinct that fires when a mechanism almost works. Because Brandt had not thrown the metal away. He had sold it to the foundry on Cooper's Lane, the one that did commemorative work, and Sable had spent the last day doing arithmetic he should have done sooner.

*What does a seal matrix become?*

Brass is brass. It goes into the crucible and comes out as whatever the mold demands. And in the year the seal died — Sable checked the date in Voss's ledger, then checked the foundry's surviving order book, which Tomas had photographed page by page — Cooper's Lane had cast exactly one large commissioned run in that quarter. One. For the garrison's tenth-anniversary observance, before the armistice froze everything.

A commemorative medallion. Three hundred struck. Distributed to officers and civic worthies at a ceremony Sable dimly remembered from childhood — the band, the banners, his mother gripping his wrist too tightly as they stood in the crowd.

*Vellmark Remembers Her Loyalty.*

Even then, even at nine years old standing in that square, Sable had understood the medal was a lie. Now he knew it was worse than a lie. It was the seal of lawful witness, melted down and recast into an emblem of forgetting — worn on the chests of the very men who needed Vellmark to remember nothing.

He laughed once, a short ugly sound with no humor in it, and the candle guttered.

* * *

Morning brought Ada, hood up, cheeks pink from the river wind, carrying bread and a look that said she hadn't slept either. She set the loaf on the workbench among the gear wheels and read his face the way she read documents — completely, and without mercy.

"You've found the problem."

"I found the problem two days ago. I've been trying to find a way around it since." He pulled the ledger toward him and turned it so she could see the bare space at the foot of the first deposition, where wax should have been. "Every sworn statement in this cache was certified under the magistrate's seal. No seal, no authentication. Marren can't certify it — she told us as much herself; a neutral delegate attests to provenance, but provenance has to be demonstrable. The chain breaks here." He tapped the empty circle of absence. "Kessler won't even need to argue. He'll just say: anyone can write fourteen statements. Where is the office seal? And we will have no answer."

Ada sat down slowly. She broke the loaf in half and handed him a piece without looking at him, an old habit of shared tables that had grown between them somewhere along the way, unremarked. She chewed. She thought. He watched her do it — grief managed through precision, always — and waited.

"Brandt took the matrix," she said at last. "You told me. Before the soldiers stripped the office."

"Yes."

"Then it exists somewhere. Brass doesn't stop existing."

"He sold it to the foundry during the famine winter. They melted it." He let her arrive at it herself, because he had learned that Ada trusted conclusions she reached more than ones she was given.

Her jaw tightened. "Melted into what?"

"The medallion. The anniversary strike. Three hundred of them." He reached into his pocket and set the thing on the bench between them — he had traded a week's wages to a pawnbroker on Thursday for this one, a duplicate that some officer's widow had sold off. It was heavier than it looked, the size of a plum, thick as two coins. On its face, the bridge in relief, the observatory tower behind it, and around the rim the motto in raised letters. On the reverse, the garrison crest.

Ada picked it up. Turned it. Weighed it in her palm the way he weighed escapements. Then her eyes came up and he saw her understand, all the way through, and he saw something else too — the cold fury from the records office, the fury that had a mind attached.

"The certifying seal," she said quietly, "is inside this."

"Inside three hundred of these. Or parts of one seal across several — the matrix would have been cut, folded into the melt with scrap. There's no telling how much of it went into any single casting. But the metal is *there*. Somewhere in those three hundred discs is enough of the original matrix to—" He stopped, because saying it aloud made it sound insane.

"To recast the seal," Ada finished for him. "To make a new matrix from the old metal and press a true impression with it."

"It wouldn't be a forgery," Sable said, and heard how defensive his voice sounded, and made himself say it plainly instead. "Or — it would be, in the sense that the object would be new. But the seal isn't the brass, Ada. The seal is the authority it carries. The authority was Voss's office, lawfully held, and the matrix was only its body. If we remake the body from the same metal, the same engraving, cut by the same hand—" He shook his head. "Ilse Marren will decide whether it holds. She's the neutral signatory. If she examines it and rules the impression valid, then it's valid. That's what neutrality means. It means someone impartial says so."

"And if she rules against us—"

"Then we have nothing, and the treaty signs, and Kessler's clean little history becomes the only history." He turned the medallion over under the lamp. The bridge caught the light along its raised edge. "But there's a smaller problem first."

Ada almost smiled. "Smaller."

"The medal I hold is a duplicate bought from a pawnshop. Even if it contains enough of the matrix metal — even if Marren accepts remade brass — we need the *originals*. The ones actually issued, traceably, to named recipients, so their provenance is documented. And those are—" He exhaled. "Those are mostly in garrison hands. Officers' effects. Commemorative issue, kept in regimental records as distributed property. The rest are in private houses across Vellmark, and half the owners would turn us in for a warm recommendation to the commission."

"Not all of them," Ada said slowly. "Some were given to civilians. Civic worthies, you said. Town councilmen. Guild masters." Her voice had gone distant, running through lists the way she ran through destruction schedules. "My father certified half this town's paperwork. People owed him. Some of them still—" She stopped. Started again, flatter. "Some of them stayed silent for twelve years. They might not want to help."

"They might want to help very much and still be too afraid."

"Then we need medals that aren't in afraid hands." She set the pawnshop duplicate down and looked at him with the expression he had learned to dread and rely upon equally: the look of a woman about to state impossible odds out loud because stating them was the first step to beating them. "Say it plainly, Sable. All of it. What stands between us and a certified record?"

He made himself count. It was a clockmaker's discipline — enumerate every obstruction before touching a single wheel, or you'll disassemble a movement you can't reassemble.

"One," he said. "We must establish which issued medallions contain sufficient matrix metal. That means the foundry's working records — pour weights, scrap ratios, the mold log. Tomas may reach some of it. Maybe."

"Two."

"We must recover at least two or three issued medals, with documented provenance, from recipients who won't betray us — and we have perhaps days, not weeks, because the annex closes at midwinter and Kessler has already asked to move the signature up."

"Three."

"The metal must be recast and engraved into a working matrix, in secret, by someone skilled enough to reproduce Voss's office seal exactly — and the engraver who cut the original died four years ago. Which leaves me. And I make clocks, Ada. I make things that tell the truth by moving. I have never cut a seal."

"Four."

"The finished impression must satisfy a Red Cross delegate that it is the lawful continuation of a dead office's authority, not a fabrication — with Brandt in custody, unable to testify to anything except that the clock talks to him, and with Marta Quill having already shown the whole town what happens to witnesses." He looked down at his hands. They were steady now; they had been steady for months, ever since the tower raid, as if terror had burned off some final tremor and left only craft. "That's the list. Four doors, each locked, and the corridor outside them filling with water."

Silence. Above them the master clock ticked through the interval, patient, indifferent, keeping time whether anyone was saved or not.

Ada turned the medallion in her fingers one last time and set it beside the ledger's blank circle, the missing seal's ghost. "Twelve years ago my father hid the truth inside a clock because he thought no one would ever open it," she said. "Now the truth needs a seal that was melted into a lie that three hundred men wear on their coats." She looked up. "It's almost elegant. My father would have appreciated the irony. He had a terrible appreciation for irony."

"Ada—"

"Tomas can get the foundry logs — he misfiled half the commission's requisitions already, one more document won't hang him any faster." She was standing now, pulling her hood up, planning aloud in the rapid flat voice that meant she had already left the room in her head. "The civilian medals — I'll write letters. Unsigned, untraceable, asking the right questions of the right households. Fear runs both ways; some of those worthies are more afraid of being remembered as silent than of being caught helping. As for the engraving—" She paused at the door and looked back at him, and for a moment the fury and the grief and the terrible precision all softened into something that was simply faith, offered freely, which was the most frightening thing she had ever given him. "You repaired a clock no one had touched in twelve years. You'll learn to cut brass."

She was gone before he could answer, which was perhaps the point.

* * *

Sable stood a long while at the workbench after her footsteps faded on the tower stair. Then he took the pawnshop medallion to the small forge he used for annealing mainsprings, lit it, and did the thing a careful man should not do with his only sample.

He cut into it.

The saw blade bit through the raised relief of the bridge — through the stone arches where he had walked as a boy, through the tower where he now stood — and the brass fell open like a wound, showing the duller heart-metal within. He filed a cross-section smooth, polished it, fixed it under the loupe, and compared it against the fragment of original seal matrix that Brandt had confessed to keeping — one corner, snapped off during the theft, hidden for twelve years in the lining of a hatbox, surrendered to Sable like a relic.

The color matched. The grain matched. Under magnification, the faint swirl of old impurities — the same smelt, the same batch, the same office — ran through both pieces like a watermark through paper.

It was in there. The seal was in there, distributed through three hundred rounds of commemorative forgetting, waiting for someone to gather it back.

Sable turned the cut medallion in his fingers and felt the full weight of what remained settle onto him: the foundry logs, the frightened households, the engraving, Marren's judgment, and above it all the closing window of midwinter, two days nearer tonight than yesterday. Impossible odds, stated plainly. He had promised himself he would always state them plainly now — that was the whole lesson of his life, the whole debt.

He banked the forge, took up his gravers, and laid out beside them the impression Brandt's fragment preserved: half of the magistrate's crest, the eagle's wing, the curve of the wreath. Twelve years of quiet, and now this — a boy's steadied hands learning, in secret, in stolen hours, to carve back into existence the instrument that could make lying illegal again.

Outside, the horns sounded the evening watch, and somewhere below in the occupied town, three hundred medals gleamed on three hundred coats, each one carrying a piece of the truth that its wearers had been decorated for burying.

Sable picked up the graver, set the tip against the scrap brass, and made the first cut.
