## 31. The Evidence Room

Ada had counted the hours the way her father used to count pages: not with a clock but with an ache in the wrist, a dryness in the eye, a sense of paper accumulating somewhere in the dark like snow against a door.

Two days remained. Tomas had said it that morning in the copying room, his voice pitched beneath the scratch of forty pens — *two days* — and then bent back over his ledger as though he had said nothing at all, because saying nothing was the whole grammar of survival in Vellmark now. The annex would close at noon on the final day. Kessler's commission had already begun crating its files, and the evidence room — Room 9, off the Custom House's eastern corridor, where everything seized since the occupation began lay under numbered seals — would be locked and inventoried before the ink on the armistice dried. Whatever entered Room 9 after tomorrow morning would leave Vellmark in a crate marked *ANNEX C: LOCAL MATERIALS, PENDING REVIEW*, and *pending review* meant never. Ada had read enough destruction orders to know what pending review became when the crates reached the victors' capital: pulp, ash, a line in someone's tidy summary — *materials of no evidentiary value*.

The medallion was in Room 9. She was certain of it. Sable had traced the seal's history twice over from Brandt's halting recollections: the certifying stamp of Elias Voss's office, brass, broken into fragments when the garrison first ransacked the clerk's chambers; the fragments gathered up — why, no one could say, except that soldiers collect, and clerks inventory, and inventories must be stored — and melted down in the Custom House foundry along with other confiscated metal, cast into commemorative medallions struck for the officers of the first occupation winter. Twelve years ago. One medallion had gone to each officer present at the founding of the garrison command. Most were lost or traded away long ago. But one officer had kept his, worn it at his collar every winter since, died last spring of a bad lung, and had his effects sealed into Room 9 pending disposition by the commission.

Captain Aldric Fenner. Dead men keep their promises; they simply stop knowing they're keeping them.

Ada sat at her copying desk with a casualty list moving under her pen and thought about seals. Her father had explained them once, when she was small enough to sit on the floor of his office and sort his quills: a seal is not the truth, he said. A seal is only the door the truth is permitted through. Anyone can own a door. What matters is whether what comes through it can stand in the light afterward without burning. She had not understood him then. She understood him now with a precision that felt like a wound dressed too tightly — she needed the door, and she needed it tonight, and the thing behind the door was a dead man's face stamped into stolen brass.

She wrote the next name on the list correctly. It was not her father's name. She corrected nothing else.

* * *

Tomas Rey had learned the Custom House the way water learns a channel: by flowing where resistance was least and remembering every stone.

Three years in the records annex had taught him which corridors emptied at which hours, which clerks took their supper early, which sentries gambled at dice in the guardroom between the second and third rounds, and — most valuable of all — how audits worked. An audit, properly manufactured, was not a lie. It was a question asked in the correct form, at the correct time, by a person with the correct authority, such that refusing to answer it caused more trouble than answering it falsely. He had misfiled destruction orders for eleven months using exactly this principle, burying each one in a folder whose number suggested a jurisdiction it did not have, so that any auditor searching for it would conclude it had been transferred and any clerk searching for the transfer would find a folder that legitimately existed and just as legitimately did not contain it. The orders were not destroyed. They were simply unreachable, which in the empire of paper amounted to the same mercy.

Tonight he intended to audit Room 9.

He stood in the eastern corridor at half past nine, holding a clipboard he had filled out himself in the hand of Under-Secretary Vahl, whose signature he could reproduce better than Vahl could, and whose authority extended — this was the beautiful part, the part Tomas had verified three times — to pre-disposition inventories of sealed materials. The treaty was two days out. Every room in the Custom House would be sealed for the signing ceremony. A conscientious junior clerk conducting a final pre-seal inventory check was not suspicious. A conscientious junior clerk was the most boring object in the world, and boredom was the thickest wall ever built.

Room 9's keeper was a corporal named Dussek, elderly, half-deaf in his left ear, devoted to his ledger and to the small stove that kept his corner of the corridor warm. Tomas had spent four evenings cultivating him — not friendship, nothing so dangerous, merely familiarity: a shared complaint about the cold, once a heel of bread, twice a question about the inventory system phrased with just enough incompetence that Corporal Dussek had been unable to resist explaining it at length. People who explain their systems to you have already decided you are harmless. That decision was worth more than any forged signature.

"Evening, Corporal." Tomas let the door swing shut behind him, muffling the corridor wind. "Under-Secretary Vahl wants a reconciliation run before the sealing order takes effect. Discrepancy flagged in the seizure ledgers — item counts versus physical holdings, Room 9 specifically."

Dussek looked up from his stove with the expression of a man being handed a burden he had predicted all his life. "Discrepancy. In my room."

"Not in your room, Corporal. In the *paper about* your room. You know how it is upstairs." He tapped the clipboard. "They want it cleared before the commission arrives or it becomes a report, and if it becomes a report it becomes somebody's winter."

That last sentence did the work. Dussek sighed, heaved himself up, and fetched his ring of keys. "Half an hour," he grumbled. "And you write what I tell you to write. My ledger balances. It has balanced for three years."

"I'm sure it has, Corporal. We're just proving it to people who doubt arithmetic."

* * *

Ada waited two streets away, in the doorway of a shuttered chandlery, wearing the grey coat and plain cap of a garrison laundress — clothes she had borrowed from Marta Quill's sister, who owed the resistance of the town more than she knew and had stopped asking questions months ago. The disguise was thin. Laundresses moved through the Custom House at certain hours with certain baskets, and Ada's basket contained folded linen over a flat wooden box lined with wool, and inside the wool, wrapped in oilcloth, lay the tools Sable had made over three sleepless nights at his bench: a jeweler's saw thin enough to pass through a seam, a set of fine chisels, a small bottle of acid for cutting a bezel, and — the piece that had cost him the most labor — a replacement medallion.

Sable had cast it himself, from pewter and a scrap of soft brass, aged with vinegar and smoke, struck from a mold taken off a rubbing of the original design. On a table, in good light, a jeweler would spot it in ten seconds. But Room 9 was not a jeweler's table. Room 9 was a corporal's inventory count conducted by lamplight against a ledger, and the ledger recorded a medallion, not the metallurgy of a medallion. The real one would walk out under Ada's coat. Its twin would lie in the evidence tray wearing its number and its dust, and when some future auditor finally held it to the light — years from now, if ever — the discrepancy would dissolve into the general fog of wartime record-keeping, unattributable, unprosecutable.

Provided everything went correctly. Ada turned the sentence over in her mind the way she turned documents, looking for the forgery in it. Nothing went correctly. Things went *sufficiently*. Her father's testimony — hidden in a clock for twelve years — had gone sufficiently. Her own life under a borrowed name had gone sufficiently. She had stopped believing in correctness around the time she learned to copy a casualty list without her hand shaking, and she had learned that at fourteen.

A church bell rang the half hour. Somewhere ahead, the Custom House windows threw yellow rectangles onto the frozen street, and beyond them, in Room 9, a deaf old corporal was unlocking drawers for a boy with a forged clipboard, and the face of Captain Aldric Fenner — dead, decorated, commemorated — hung at a dead man's collar in drawer fourteen, waiting to become the seal that certified a massacre.

Ada pushed off the doorframe and walked toward the light.

* * *

The trouble with inventories, Tomas reflected, was that they were honest work done dishonestly, and the body knew the difference even when the mind refused to.

His hands were steady. His handwriting on the tally sheet was steady. But sweat had gathered along his spine in a way it never gathered during the misfiling, because misfiling was private — a crime committed alone, witnessed by no one, deniable forever. This was different. This was a crime with an audience of one old soldier and a co-conspirator due through the door in eleven minutes, and every minute that passed, Dussek's finger moved down the ledger and Dussek's voice read aloud — "*Item 61, field desk, oak, one; Item 62, sabre, pattern '09, one, blade notched*" — and every item matched, and Tomas began to understand with a rising, sour panic that perhaps there would be no discrepancy at all, that Vahl's fictional audit would find the room in perfect order, and that he had built an entire architecture of permission on a foundation of nothing.

Drawer twelve: correspondence, seized, four bundles. Matched.

Drawer thirteen: personal effects, deceased officers, pending disposition. Matched.

"*Item 88,*" Dussek read, and licked his thumb, and turned the page, and the page was the wrong page — Tomas saw it instantly, the corporal had skipped a line in the lamplight, his eyes were old — "*Item 89, medals and decorations, assorted, seven.*"

Seven. The ledger said eight. Tomas had checked it six times. Eight items under the heading *medals and decorations, assorted*, and the eighth entry, in the cramped notation of some long-departed clerk, read: *medallion, commemorative, founding winter, Capt. A. Fenner (dec.), one.*

"There," Tomas said quietly, and pointed at the gap between the seventh medal in the evidence tray and the empty space beside it where dust had protected a clean circle on the velvet. "There's your discrepancy, Corporal. Count them."

Dussek counted them. His lips moved. His face went through a journey — puzzlement, dawning offense, and then something worse, a kind of wounded fear, because a missing item in a sealed room on the eve of a treaty was not an inconvenience, it was a career, possibly a charge. "It was here," he said. "I signed for eight. I've signed for eight for a year."

"And now there are seven, and in forty hours this room gets a wax stamp on the door, and whatever's missing becomes *missing on your watch*." Tomas kept his voice level, almost kind. He hated himself for the kindness and admired himself for it in the same breath. "Unless we find it entered differently. Unless the paperwork shows it was released. Transferred. Anything but *gone*."

"Released to whom? Who'd release a—"

And that was when the knock came, three soft raps, and Ada entered carrying a laundry basket, and the whole delicate machine of the evening shifted into its final gearing.

* * *

She had rehearsed the entrance in the chandlery doorway until her reflection in the dark glass performed it without her. Laundress, evening collection, captain's quarters, third floor. She gave the sentry at the stair the bored nod of a woman who had made this walk two hundred times, and the sentry — young, cold, thinking about dice and supper — returned the nod of a man who had seen two hundred laundresses and distinguished none of them.

But the rehearsal ended at the door of Room 9, because through the door she heard Tomas's voice saying *missing on your watch*, and she understood in one cold flash that the plan had arrived at its hinge without her, that the discrepancy was live, and that she was no longer the thief entering the vault — she was the answer arriving on cue.

She knocked. She entered. And she played the only card the moment offered.

"Beg pardon," she said, in the flattened accent Marta's sister had drilled into her over two evenings of tea and terror. "Corporal — the captain's effects. The late Captain Fenner. His sister came to the quartermaster Tuesday, wanting his things released to the family, and there's a chit." She produced it from her apron — a genuine requisition form, blank, which she had lifted from the quartermaster's tray that afternoon and filled in herself in a fair imitation of a quartermaster's clerk, requesting release of *personal mementoes, Capt. A. Fenner (dec.), to next of kin*, signed with a squiggle that could be read as any of four names. "Only nobody downstairs knows what's in the room, so they sent me up to ask what's held, so the family can say what they're claiming."

The genius of it — she would allow herself, later, in the dark, one moment of admiration for her own nerve — was that it required nothing. It asked Dussek to do only what he loved: consult his ledger, recite his inventory, assert the perfection of his custody. It gave the missing medallion a story before anyone had to tell one. And it put Ada, standing in Room 9 with her basket, within four feet of drawer thirteen.

Dussek squinted at the chit, held it near the lamp, read it aloud in the slow voice of a man for whom reading was labor. "Released to next of kin. Hm. Well. There's effects pending disposition, that's true enough. Sabre. Watch. Letters." His finger moved down. "Medals, assorted, seven—" and here his voice snagged, caught on the raw place Tomas had opened, and Ada watched the two lies meet in the old man's face and fuse into one serviceable truth: the medallion was already gone, therefore it might plausibly have been released, therefore the chit might be the record of a release that had happened above his head, as everything always happened above his head.

"You know," Dussek said slowly, "there *was* a runner, back in the autumn. Took a parcel to the family, I mind the entry—" He flipped pages with sudden desperate hope. "Here. October. *One item, personal, released to Capt. Fenner estate, escort: Pvt. Lom.* Signed and everything. See?"

There was no such entry. There never had been. But the page was crowded, the ink faded, and grief makes generous readers, and Tomas — God keep him, Ada thought, God keep that brave lying boy — leaned in and said, "Well, there, Corporal, that reconciles it, doesn't it? Item released, ledger's sound, you just initial the correction and I'll carry it upstairs."

And while the two of them bent over the ledger, arguing gently about the proper form of initials, Ada knelt at drawer thirteen as though steadying her basket, slid it open on oiled runners that made no sound at all, and lifted Captain Fenner's collar insignia aside with fingers that did not shake — twelve years of copying other people's deaths had taught her hands perfect bureaucratic calm — and took the medallion from its velvet bed.

It was heavier than she expected. Brass holds weight the way guilt does. She turned it once in the lamplight and saw the struck face of the founding winter — crossed keys, the garrison motto, the date — and along the rim, worn nearly smooth by a dead man's thumb, the faint raised ghost of the seal that had once certified her father's sworn depositions. Elias Voss's authority, melted and re-struck and worn at the throat of the man whose garrison had killed him. Her father's door, carried for twelve years by the men who had nailed it shut.

She laid the twin in its place. Pewter and scrap brass, vinegar-smoked, dusted with lint from the bottom of her basket. On a jeweler's table, ten seconds. In the lamplight of Room 9, forever.

She closed the drawer. She stood. She said, in her laundress voice, "Tell the family the letters are here, anyway. They'll want those," and Dussek, still fighting with his initials, waved her toward the door without looking up, and she went, and the corridor swallowed her, and the medallion burned its circle of cold through the linen and the wood and the wool against her ribs like a second heart.

* * *

Tomas finished the reconciliation sheet at twenty minutes past ten, initialed by Dussek in a hand that shook slightly, countersigned by Under-Secretary Vahl's perfect forgery, and filed — he made sure of this personally — in the folder that no future auditor would think to open, because its number belonged to a jurisdiction that did not exist. The room's ledger balanced again. Room 9 would receive its wax stamp at noon in two days' time, sealing behind it seven assorted medals, one pewter impostor, and a paperwork trail that would take a forensic commission a decade to unpick, if anyone ever cared to try.

He walked out through the eastern corridor, past the stove, past the sentry dreaming of dice, into air so cold it felt like a slap from a stranger. Ada was waiting at the corner of the chandlery, and when he reached her she did not speak, only drew the medallion partway from her coat so the lamplight caught the rim — the worn ghost-lettering, the seal's broken circumference — and he felt his knees go loose with a relief so total it was indistinguishable from exhaustion.

"It's smaller than I imagined," he said.

"Most true things are." She wrapped it away again, deep in the wool. "Sable will need till dawn to cut the bezel and mount the matrix. Ilse Marren examines the record at first light."

"Then we've done it."

Ada looked back once at the Custom House, its yellow windows, its patient crates, the great machinery of forgetting running smoothly through its final rotations — and she thought of her father's voice across a floor of quills: *a seal is only the door.* Tomorrow, if the cutting went well and Marren's hands were steady and Kessler slept, the door would open, and what came through it would have to stand in the light.

"No," she said. "We've done the easy part. Now we have to certify the truth in a town full of men paid to deny it — and one of them, I think, already knows we're trying."

She had seen the light burning, as they passed, in the commission's third-floor window. Kessler's window. Burning at ten o'clock at night, two days before a signing he had fought to accelerate, in a building whose evidence room had just been audited by a clerk who should never have needed to be there.

The lamp in the window went out as they watched.
