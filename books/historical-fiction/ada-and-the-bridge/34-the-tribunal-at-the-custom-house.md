## 34. The Tribunal at the Custom House

The Custom House had not held a court since before the war, and it wore its disuse the way an old man wears his dead wife's coat — with a kind of stubborn, shabby dignity. Sable Dravenmoor stood in the doorway of the long hall and felt the building take his measure. Dust hung in the shafts of light that fell through the high clerestory windows, turning slowly, like the dust inside a clock case. Rows of benches that had once served customs clerks now served townspeople, and the townspeople filled them in silence, breath steaming, because the garrison had requisitioned the coal and the stoves stood cold.

At the far end, where the weighing-master's desk had been, someone had draped a grey cloth over trestles and set three chairs behind it. Two were occupied already: a garrison major whose face suggested he had been told to attend and resented the instruction, and Ilse Marren, the Red Cross delegate, her grey shawl pinned at the throat, her hands folded on the table before her like a woman waiting out a long train delay. The third chair stood empty. It had a card propped against its seat — RESERVED — and Sable understood, with a lurch, that the card had been placed there that morning by someone who did not yet know whether Kessler would come.

He did come. Sable saw him enter from the side door, alone, carrying nothing but a leather folio, and the hall's silence changed quality — not louder, but denser, the way a room changes when a clock that had been ticking stops. Kessler crossed to the reserved chair, looked at it for a moment as though assessing a document of dubious provenance, and sat.

Sable found a place near the back, beside Ada. She wore her Lene clothes, the plain grey of a records clerk, but she had done something to her hair, pulled it back severely, and her hands in her lap were absolutely still. Only her eyes moved, cataloguing the room the way she catalogued a file: exits, faces, the two garrison stenographers at their little table with their blank books, the soldier at the door who was not looking at the door.

"He came," she breathed.

"He came to bury it," Sable said. "Or to watch us try."

"Then let him watch."

The major rose, cleared his throat, and read from a paper in a voice that made the paper sound like a complaint about freight tariffs. There would be a hearing, he said, informal, non-binding, convened under the humanitarian annex provisions cited by Delegate Marren, to receive testimony relevant to events in Vellmark during the occupation and the preceding administration. The record of the hearing would be transmitted to the armistice commission. He did not say *entered into the record*. He said *transmitted*, and Ada's still hands tightened once, and loosened.

Ilse Marren spoke next, briefly, in accented but precise Vellmarkish. "This is not a trial," she said. "There is no accused before this table, and no verdict will issue from it. There is only a record, and the people who can fill it. I have spent thirty years learning that a record is the only thing that outlives all of us — the victors, the vanquished, the witnesses. What is spoken here today will be spoken forever. I ask everyone in this hall to speak accordingly."

Then she looked down at her papers and said, "The commission will hear the first witness. Marta Quill."

* * *

Marta had dressed in her best, which was to say her most respectable black, and she had washed her hair and pinned it under a bonnet that made her look, Sable thought, like a woman attending a funeral. Which, in a sense, she was.

She walked the length of the hall with her chin up and her hands gripping a folded handkerchief so tightly that the knuckles went white, and Sable remembered her as she had been three weeks ago in her kitchen, telling them what she had seen from the wash-line on the night of the tannery — the wagons, the lanterns, the men who came back at dawn with their boots dark to the knee — and then remembering, two days later, the visit from the garrison officers, and coming to the observatory white-faced to take it all back. *I saw nothing,* she had said then, weeping with shame. *I was confused. It was dark. I'm an old woman and my eyes are bad.*

Now she sat in the witness chair that had been carried in from the magistrate's office, and Ilse Marren leaned forward and said, gently, "Frau Quill. You gave a statement to this delegation's officers on the ninth of the month, and withdrew it on the eleventh. You are not required to explain why. But I would like you to tell the hall what you told me on the ninth."

Marta looked out at the hall. Sable saw her find the faces she needed — Ada's, his own, and, near the front, Tomas Rey, sitting among the clerks with his cap on his knees. She found something else too, or someone: her daughter, who had come at dawn from the mill village, and who was watching her mother now with an expression that dared the whole hall to make her ashamed of what she was about to do.

"On the night of the fourteenth of Frostmonth," Marta said, and her voice was thin but it carried, the way a wire carries, "twelve years ago, I was hanging wash late because the frost was coming and I wanted the linens in before it. From my yard I can see the lane behind the tannery. I saw four wagons come up the lane with their lamps hooded. I saw men unload them. I saw — " she stopped, and the handkerchief came up to her mouth, and she mastered herself with an effort that Sable could feel from the back of the hall, a physical thing, like a gear tooth catching and then turning. "I saw them carry things out of the wagons that did not move. And in the morning the tannery was shut, and the Voss family was gone, and the town was told there had been a fever."

She lowered the handkerchief. "Two weeks ago, two officers came to my house and reminded me that my son-in-law holds a garrison supply contract. They did not threaten me. They were very polite. They brought sugar." She said the word *sugar* the way another woman might say *blood money*, and a sound went through the hall, low and involuntary, like wind in a rigging. "I withdrew my statement because I was afraid. I am still afraid. I am more afraid of what this town will be if I keep my mouth shut than I am of them. That's all. That's what I saw, and I saw it clear, and my eyes were better then."

The stenographers' pens scratched. Ilse Marren asked her four questions — dates, distances, names — and Marta answered each one plainly, and when she was done she stood, and the major said she was excused, and she did not leave. She walked back to the bench where her daughter sat and sat down beside her, and her daughter took her hand, and Sable looked away because some things are not meant to be witnessed even by a witness.

Kessler had not moved. He had watched Marta Quill the whole time with his head slightly tilted, the expression of a man reading a document he has read before and finding, this time, a marginal note in an unfamiliar hand.

* * *

From the clerks' bench, Tomas Rey watched the washerwoman sit down and felt his own heart going like a hammer in a loose case.

He had rehearsed this. He had rehearsed it on the walk over, in the washroom of the records office, standing at the mirror telling the wall: *I am entering a document into evidence. I am entering a document into evidence.* As though the sentence were a key and repetition would cut it true. But now the moment was here and the folio under his arm weighed what a folio weighs, which is nothing, which is everything.

Ilse Marren said, "The commission will now receive documents. The clerk will identify himself."

Tomas stood. His legs worked, which surprised him. He walked to the table, and when he turned to face the hall he made himself look at the major, at Marren, and last at Kessler, because he had decided that if he could look at Kessler he could look at anyone, and he found — this unsettled him more than anything else that morning — that Kessler was looking back at him with something that was almost recognition. Almost sympathy. *I know what you are about to do,* that look said. *I have done it myself, in my way.*

"Name and office," said Marren.

"Tomas Rey. Junior clerk, fourth section, records division, garrison administration." His voice came out steady and he was grateful to it, the way you are grateful to a horse that carries you through fire without being asked. "I have served in the Vellmark office for fourteen months."

"And what do you wish to enter?"

"A file." He set the folio on the table and opened it with hands that only shook a little. "It is a destruction order, countersigned and executed, dated eight months ago, authorizing the incineration of the municipal registry's annex holdings — coroner's inquests, gendarme reports, and correspondence of the former town administration for the years of the occupation." He laid the pages out flat, one by one, and the sound of paper on wood was very loud in the cold hall. "This is the order itself. These are the transit manifests. And this — " his voice caught, and he steadied it, because his brother was in this next sentence and his brother deserved a steady voice — "this is the incineration log, signed by the furnace attendant, listing the lots received. Except that it is not complete. Because I did not burn all of it. I have been misfiling destruction lots for seven months, since I learned what was in them. What I am entering today is the order, the manifests, and a certified inventory of what was actually destroyed and what was not — the latter prepared by me, and attested by Delegate Marren's office on Tuesday last."

The major stirred. "Clerk Rey. You understand that you are describing the falsification of official records, and admitting to it, under your own name."

"I understand that, sir."

"You understand the penalty."

"I understand that my brother, Ansel Rey, was taken in the reprisals of two years ago and that every document concerning his arrest was in that annex, and that someone signed an order to burn it, and that if I had not taken it out, no one would ever have known what was done to him or on whose authority." Tomas heard his own voice rise and made himself bring it back down. "I am not asking this commission to forgive me. I am asking it to read the file. The truth does not stop being true because it was rescued dishonestly."

The hall made no sound at all. Sable, at the back, found Ada's hand and held it, and her grip was fierce and cold.

It was Kessler who spoke. He did not stand. He turned in his chair, and his voice was mild, almost regretful. "The fourth section's filing protocols require a second countersignature on all destruction lots. Whose countersignature appears on the order you have entered?"

"Deputy Commissioner Harsch's, sir."

"And Deputy Commissioner Harsch is presently attached to the armistice secretariat."

"He is, sir."

Kessler looked at the order for a long moment. Then he closed the folio, slid it back across the table to Marren, and said, in the tone of a man noting a discrepancy in a footnote: "For the record, I have no objection to the document's admission. Its provenance is irregular. Its contents, I suspect, are not." And he turned back to the front, and Tomas Rey walked back to the clerks' bench with his ears roaring, not knowing whether he had just been saved or measured for a noose, and suspecting, correctly, that with Kessler the two were often the same procedure.

* * *

Ada watched the morning move through the hall like weather, and she held the ledger against her ribs under her coat and thought: *this is what it looks like when it works.*

Not the way she had imagined it in the long nights at the observatory — not triumph, not the scales righting with a great groan of justice. It looked like a cold room and a tired major and a stenographer's pen running out of ink and being refilled. It looked like Marta Quill's white knuckles. It looked like a junior clerk admitting a crime in order to prove a larger one. The law was not descending on Vellmark; the law was being built here, this morning, out of whatever people were willing to stand up and say, and it was being built badly and bravely and it was the only law there was.

Marren called the next witness. A former gendarme, gray-faced, who confirmed the transit of prisoners to the tannery on the night in question and the presence of an officer whose name he spelled out letter by letter, and Ada wrote the letters in her mind as he spoke them, filing them in the place where she kept her father's real name and the correct spelling of the dead. Then a chandler's widow. Then, after the midday recess, a man who had driven one of the wagons and had carried the guilt of it for twelve years like a stone in his boot, and who wept openly and was permitted to.

And through all of it, Kessler sat and listened and took notes in a small even hand, and asked perhaps six questions in the whole day, each one precise, each one — Ada realized with growing unease — aimed not at the witnesses but at the shape of the record itself. Where documents corroborated testimony. Where they did not. Which names appeared on paper and which appeared only in memory. He was not defending the old regime. He was auditing the truth, and Ada understood, watching him, that a man like this could not be beaten by testimony alone. He would yield only to a document he could not impeach. Everything, then, still came down to the ledger in her coat, and the seal, and the certification that Ilse Marren had signed at dawn in the observatory with her own hand — and whether it would be entered before the annex closed, and whether Kessler would let it.

Near dusk, Marren looked at her papers and then, deliberately, at Kessler, and said: "The commission has heard testimony and received documents. One item remains. A certified record, compiled by the late court clerk Elias Voss, authenticated under seal by this delegation's humanitarian office, offered for annexation to the armistice minutes."

The major frowned. "I was given no notice of — "

"You have notice now," Marren said, and did not raise her voice at all.

And Kessler stood.

* * *

Sable's whole body went tight. At the front of the hall, Kessler straightened his folio on the table before him, and the light from the clerestory windows had gone the color of old brass, and the cold had deepened until the stenographers were writing in gloves.

"I have no objection in principle," Kessler said, "to the admission of the Voss record." A murmur went through the benches; Ada's hand closed on Sable's wrist. "I have read it. It is a remarkable document — meticulous, sourced, and damning. I have spent my life among documents, and I know a true one when I hold it." He paused, and looked out across the hall, and Sable had the sudden vertiginous sense that the man was not addressing the tribunal at all but the town, the record, perhaps himself. "But a true document entered into a false procedure is worse than no document at all. It can be challenged, annulled, discredited — and once discredited, it is dead forever, and it takes every witness who touched it down with it. This commission is improvised. It sits without statute, on borrowed authority, in a building that has not held a court in a generation. If the record is entered here, tonight, by this table, the victors' secretariat will strike it within the month as procedurally void, and no one will ever be permitted to enter it again."

He turned to Marren. "Delegate, you have the authority under the humanitarian annex to do one thing that this commission cannot. You may convene, on your own certification, a formal hearing of the annex itself — with stenographic record, with the signatories present, with the protections of the treaty text. It requires forty-eight hours' notice. The annex does not close until the signature." He laid his folio down flat. "I move — not as the victors' historian, but as a member of this commission — that the signature be delayed two days, and that the Voss record be heard where it cannot be struck."

The silence that followed was the longest of Sable's life. He heard, absurdly, the memory of the master clock's escapement, the tick he had restored with his own hands, and he thought: *he is buying us time, and he is spending his own career to do it, and neither of us knows yet which of us is the fool.*

Ilse Marren looked at Kessler for a long moment. Then she took up her pen.

"So entered," she said. "The hearing of the annex will convene at the Custom House, the day after tomorrow, at nine o'clock. The commission will hear the Voss record in full." She set down the pen and looked out at the hall, at the town that had filled it all day and had not left. "And the commission will hear its witnesses. All of them."

Sable felt Ada turn to look at him, and he did not need to see her face to know what was in it, because it was in him too, rising like water in a sealed gauge: two days. The annex. His name, spoken aloud, into a record that could never be struck.

The hall was emptying around them, and somewhere outside, the horns of the garrison sounded the winter dusk, and the clock in the observatory tower, a mile away and invisible, struck six — true, at last, to the minute.
