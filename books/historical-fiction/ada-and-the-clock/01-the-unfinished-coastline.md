## 1. The Unfinished Coastline

There is a particular hour in a mapmaker's workshop—somewhere between the last light leaving the harbor and the first cart rattling over the cobbles—when the ink dries exactly as it should. Not too fast, not so slow that it blooms. I have spent three years learning to love that hour, mostly because it asks nothing of me except that I stay awake for it.

I was awake for it now. I was always awake for it.

The workshop of Aldous Iversen, Mapmaker and Engraver to Whoever Was Still Buying, sat on Tern Street like an old man who had stopped caring what the neighbors thought. The sign outside had faded until it read ALDO S IVE SE , which Tomas said we ought to fix and which I privately thought was more honest than the original. The windows were thick with salt haze from the harbor. The floorboards had learned the shape of every footstep that ever crossed them, including mine—including the ones that weren't supposed to exist after dark.

On the table before me lay the final coastline of the great atlas: the northern shore of the Vellmark peninsula, all ninety miles of it, drawn from surveys Master Aldous had made in his last good year, when he could still climb the headlands without stopping twice to catch his breath.

I dipped my pen. I held it against the lip of the well the way he'd taught me—one second, no more, let the excess fall back—and I began at Cape Merren, where the coast turns its shoulder to the sea like a woman declining to be photographed.

*Steady,* I told myself, in his voice, because my own voice had never once managed to sound calm about anything.

The line came off the nib clean. A coastline, if you're doing it honestly, is never one line. It's three: the line the water draws, the line the rock insists on, and the line a sailor needs at midnight in fog with money on the tide. Aldous used to say most mapmakers gave you one and called it truth. He wanted all three, layered, in weights of ink fine enough to make a jeweler weep. Which is why the atlas had taken eleven years, why it would take four more even now, and why exactly six people alive knew it existed.

Five, actually. But we'll get to that.

I worked the cape. The lamp hissed. Somewhere below, the harbor slapped at its pilings with the patience of something that has outlasted every map ever made of it.

Three years since Master Aldous died, and the workshop still pretended otherwise. His coat still hung behind the door. His spectacles still sat folded on the blotter where he'd left them, one lens starred with a crack he'd refused to have mended because, he claimed, the flaw improved his view of Vellmark's less reputable districts. His chair creaked when anyone but me sat in it, as though the wood itself had opinions about succession.

Officially, Aldous Iversen was ill. Unofficially—that is, according to the fiction I maintained with the diligence other people reserved for religion—he was ill, reclusive, and occasionally well enough to bark instructions through his bedroom door, which is why the apprentices were expected to work long hours without supervision. The barking, naturally, was me. I'd gotten quite good at it. There are mornings when I catch myself mid-bark and think: *Petra, you are a twenty-two-year-old woman impersonating a dead man's temper, and you are better at it than he ever was.* Then I put the kettle on and get on with the day, because grief keeps its schedule whether or not you approve of it.

He hadn't died dramatically, which would at least have been useful. No duel, no shipwreck, no scandal. His heart simply stopped one Tuesday evening between correcting the soundings of Graywater Bay and complaining about the price of lamp oil. I found him slumped over plate forty-seven, his cheek on the copper, and for one absurd moment my first thought was that he'd smudge the wax ground. My second thought was that I was now alone with an unfinished atlas, a forbidden reputation, and a corpse that needed explaining before morning.

I did what any sensible apprentice would do. I moved him to his bed, closed his eyes, opened the window to let the sea air in—it does wonders for the recently deceased—and began composing the lie I would live inside for the rest of my life, or at least until the atlas was done, whichever came first.

The doctor was paid to write *declining health, confined to his rooms*. The neighbors were given a story about a stroke that spared his mind. The guild was sent letters in a hand I'd spent two years practicing—an imitation of his cramped, impatient script, the *d*s leaning forward like men in a hurry. And the workshop continued to produce charts, corrections, and the slow, magnificent atlas, all under the direction of a master who was, by then, a box of ash in the cellar that I kept meaning to deal with and kept not dealing with.

I am aware how this sounds. I've had three years to rehearse justifications and none of them survive daylight. So let me offer only this: he asked me to. Not in words—the heart attack rather cut short the dictation—but in everything else. In the letter I found later. In the way he'd spent his final months teaching me not drafting or engraving, though he taught those too, but *disappearing*: how to be overlooked, underestimated, unremarked. How to be furniture. A girl who served the Halvard household before the fall learns certain skills whether she wants them or not, and Aldous, bless his suspicious heart, had recognized the training when he saw it.

But I'm getting ahead of myself, which is a habit of mine and a poor one for a narrator. The atlas first. The atlas always first.

*

By the time the church bells counted four, I had finished Cape Merren and the long shingle spit below it, and I allowed myself the ritual: setting down the pen, flexing my hand, and reading back over the night's work with the severity Aldous would have applied and twice the kindness. It was good work. I don't say that from vanity—I have very little left by four in the morning—but because it was true, and because he would have found the one place where the third weight of ink wavered at the spit's end, and he would have made me do it again, and I would have sworn under my breath, and then it would have been perfect.

*Iversen's Atlas of the Northern Waters.* Eleven years of his life, three of mine. Two hundred and six plates projected; one hundred and sixty-one finished. When it was done it would go to the engraver abroad, be printed in numbers too many to suppress, and every harbor master, fisherman, smuggler, and naval officer from here to the ice would hold in their hands a map of waters the Admiralty preferred to keep vague. That was the point. That had always been the point. Truth, published, is a difficult thing to unpublish.

Which brings us, inevitably, to the plate I was not supposed to touch.

Plate 208. The one with no title, no number scratched into the copper, and a lock on the flat drawer beneath the survey cabinet that Aldous called *the filing arrangement for matters above your station*, which was his way of saying *don't*, and whose key he wore around his neck until the day he didn't need keys anymore.

I want it understood that I had obeyed him for three years. Three years of walking past that drawer every single day, twice on Sundays, while the atlas grew around whatever secret lived inside it like a pearl around grit. I had invented theories, discarded them, reinvented worse ones. I had, during a fevered week last winter, become convinced the drawer contained nothing but his private correspondence and possibly cheese. That theory collapsed the morning I caught myself standing before it with the poker in my hand and no memory of crossing the room.

And tonight—tonight of all nights, with the northern coastline finished and the atlas one hundred and sixty-one plates closer to the world—I broke.

Not dramatically. I'm telling you this story, so you know I'm not going to pretend to heroics I don't possess. I simply finished the spit, cleaned my pen, looked at the drawer, and looked at it a moment too long. And the looking became standing, and the standing became kneeling, and suddenly I was on the cold floor with the lamp pulled close, staring at a brass lock that had defeated me for three years because I had, until this moment, been trying to honor a dead man instead of finish his work.

Here is the thing nobody tells you about obedience to the dead: they stop being able to explain themselves. Aldous had his reasons. Aldous always had reasons, some of them even legal. But reasons locked in a drawer rot faster than paper, and I was the one left holding the smell.

Besides. I told myself. Besides, the atlas cannot go abroad incomplete. Whatever is in there, it belongs to the whole—or the whole belongs to it. Either way, a promise is a debt, and I had inherited all of his.

I fetched the tools.

Locks, I will say in defense of my character, are not my vice. This was the first one I'd ever picked, and it showed. It took me the better part of an hour, two broken picks, one genuinely profane word in the old Halvard kitchen dialect that I hadn't spoken aloud in seven years, and a strip of whalebone from a corset I'd outgrown in every sense. When the bolt finally gave—a soft, apologetic *clack*, like a disapproving aunt clearing her throat—I sat back on my heels and felt, distinctly, the floor tilt under me. Not fear exactly. Fear comes later, wearing a uniform and carrying a ledger. This was older. This was the feeling of stepping off a known map.

Inside the drawer lay three things.

A bundle of letters tied in green ribbon. A small tin of salts that I recognized from the Halvard house—Lady Halvard's lavender-and-vinegar, the smell of every sickroom and every secret of my childhood, and my chest went tight as a knot at the sight of it. And beneath both, wrapped in oilcloth, a copper plate.

I lifted it into the lamplight.

It was a coastline. Of course it was a coastline; the man had thought in coastlines the way priests think in psalms. But not ours. Not the northern waters, not the peninsula, not anything I'd surveyed or sounded or walked. This was a coast farther south—farther south than any chart in the atlas was meant to reach—and it was not empty. The engraving was half-finished, exquisite, and dense with marks that were not soundings.

They were positions. Anchorages. Channels. The kind of detail that is not geography but *access*—who could land where, at what tide, under whose guns. And in the lower margin, in Aldous's own hand, cut into the copper itself so it could never be separated from the plate, a line of text I read three times before my eyes would accept it:

*For E.H., that he may find his way home.*

Elias Halvard. Nineteen years old now, if he lived, exiled these seven years along with everyone who'd ever carried his name, declared enemy of the crown by a proclamation read aloud in every square of Vellmark with appropriate ceremony. A boy I had once taught to gut a fish badly and who had once, aged eleven, paid me in buttons for smuggling him extra bread.

Aldous had forbidden anyone to finish this plate. Had locked it away, worn the key against his heart, and died with it unfinished.

And I knelt on the cold floor of a dead man's shop, holding the reason why, understanding finally that the atlas was never only an atlas—and hearing, somewhere out in the waking city, the front gate of Tern Street swing on its hinge.

At half past four in the morning, nobody honest opens that gate.

I blew out the lamp.

* * *

Darkness in a workshop is a different country. Every object you've known by daylight becomes an ambush. I stood absolutely still behind the survey cabinet, plate pressed to my chest through my apron, listening the way Lady Halvard taught the household staff to listen during the bad year—mouth open, eyes shut, counting the sounds and sorting them.

Footsteps. One person. Light, unhurried, familiar in a way that loosened my shoulders half an inch before caution snapped them back. Familiar was not the same as safe. Familiar was, in my recent experience, considerably more dangerous.

The steps crossed the outer room. Paused at the threshold of the workroom, where the darkness still smelled of my lamp oil.

"Master Iversen?" Tomas's voice, pitched low and careful. "I heard the lamp."

Of course he had. Tomas slept in the loft above the bindery like a cat that had negotiated favorable terms, and woke at the strike of a match three floors down. Three years I had worked the night hours around his sleeping, and three years he had slept through them, and tonight—the one night I had a stolen state secret against my ribs—tonight he heard the lamp.

"The master's asleep," I said, in Aldous's rasp, from the darkness. "Go back up."

A pause. Long enough to be polite; short enough to be deliberate.

"You're at the coast section," he said. Not a question. "Cape Merren. You started it Tuesday."

"I did."

Another pause, and then, mild as milk: "Only—the flat drawer's open. I can see it from the stairs."

I looked down. In the grey seep of pre-dawn through the salt-hazed windows, the drawer hung out of the cabinet like a tongue.

"Mind your plates, Tomas," I said, and heard, even as I said it, how much of Aldous had died into me.

He went. I listened to him climb the stairs, unhurried, and settle onto his cot with a creak that might have meant nothing at all. I stood in the dark with the plate against my heart for a long time afterward, doing the arithmetic I would be doing for weeks to come: what he saw, what he'd say, what a clever, ambitious, observant young man does with knowledge he hasn't yet decided the price of.

Outside, the sky went the color of dishwater, and Vellmark began to wake, and the census men—though I didn't know it yet, wouldn't know it for another day—had already begun writing down names.

Mine, as far as the world knew, was Petra Iversen. Niece to the master. Apprentice. Furniture.

It had been true for three years. I intended it to stay true.

I wrapped the plate back in its oilcloth, returned the drawer to its lockless sleep, and went upstairs to wake a dead man for breakfast.
