## 2. One Question Too Many

The census taker came on a Tuesday, which I have since decided is the sort of day governments choose for their cruelties precisely because no one expects anything of a Tuesday. Mondays you brace for. Fridays the city has already gone soft with anticipation. But Tuesday—Tuesday a man can walk into your workshop while your hands are stained to the wrist with ink, and you cannot even claim to be busy, because busy is what mapmakers always are and therefore proves nothing.

He arrived without knocking properly, which in Vellmark marks a man as either very important or very new to the city, and Brann was neither, so I understood at once that the rudeness was policy.

"Petra Iversen?" He said my name the way one reads an entry in a ledger—checking it against something. Against what, I would learn later. That morning it simply sounded like a name, my name, three years worn smooth enough that it fit me like a coat I'd stopped noticing I was wearing.

"Apprentice," I said, not looking up from the coast of some invented archipelago I was inking for a merchant's dining room—work Master Aldous had called *money maps*, work that fed us so we could do the true work free. "If this is about the window tax, the glazier's bill is paid."

"Not the window tax." He set a leather satchel on Tomas's workbench without asking, which told me he'd been briefed on whose bench was whose, or hadn't bothered to find out, and both possibilities were unpleasant. "Census verification. The Crown requires confirmation of household records. You are listed here as apprentice to Aldous Iversen, deceased—" he consulted a paper with the delicacy of a man turning over a dead beetle "—three years past. Is that accurate?"

"Master Iversen died in the winter of '44. Heart failure. The physician's certificate is with the guild."

"I have the certificate." A thin smile. He was perhaps forty, with the kind of face designed by God specifically to be forgotten—the color of porridge, features arranged with an economy that suggested He'd run short of ideas by the time He reached Vellmark. Only his eyes worked hard. They went everywhere: the racks of plates, the drying charts pinned like laundry, the locked drawer in the master's old desk that I had never once opened and thought about constantly. "It's the other records I'm interested in."

"Other?"

"Prior households." He said it lightly, the way a man says *weather* before mentioning the storm. "Where apprentices served before their current positions. The census tracks movement, Miss Iversen. People are like water—they leave channels, and channels tell you where they've been."

I kept inking. This required concentration, because what I wanted to do was stop inking, and stopping is how liars die. My hand drew the little islands' southern shore in a line as steady as if I'd been born doing nothing else. In a sense I had.

"I came to the workshop six years ago," I said. "Before that I was in service. A housekeeper's position outside the city. The family has since—moved away." True, every word, in the way that matters least. "I don't imagine the census cares much about scullery work."

"The census cares about everything. That's rather its purpose." He produced a printed form and began filling it in, reading aloud as he wrote, a habit I would come to loathe with my whole body. "Name. Position. Duration. Prior employer's name?"

"Aldous Iversen."

"Prior to that."

"A household in the country," I said. "I don't recall the surname. They were not the sort of people one remembers." Which was the most elegant lie I have ever told, because it made forgetting itself the alibi, and only someone who remembered them perfectly could appreciate the craftsmanship.

Brann looked up. For a moment the ledger-reading eyes became something else—patient, almost kind, the way a trap is kind. It holds you gently right up until the arithmetic changes.

"You don't recall," he repeated. "A servant forgets her employers. That's unusual. Servants usually remember everything—it's most of what they own."

"Tomas!" I called toward the back room, loud enough to crack my own composure slightly. "The census man has questions!"

This was cowardice dressed as efficiency, and I regretted it immediately, because Tomas came through the doorway wiping plaster dust off his hands looking exactly like what he was—an ambitious young man who had never once been asked a question he didn't want to answer.

* * *

Tomas handled the next quarter hour the way he handled everything: beautifully, and with an eye toward who was watching. He gave his history—born two streets over, apprenticed at fourteen, parents living, all documented—and Brann wrote it down with the satisfaction of a man confirming that most of the world still behaved.

But when Tomas finished, Brann didn't close his book. He turned instead to the wall where Master Aldous had hung, years ago, in some mood of vanity or defiance I never fully understood, a framed engraving of his own finest commission: a survey chart of the northern firths, signed with both his mark and a second mark beside it—a crest, a stag's head crowned in laurel.

"That's an interesting piece," Brann said.

"It's the master's best work," Tomas said. "Everyone says so."

"It's the collaboration that interests me." Brann tapped the second signature without touching the glass. "Lord Halvard commissioned this survey. Before the troubles."

"Before," Tomas agreed carefully.

"And yet it hangs." Brann let that sit in the room like smoke. "A work bearing the arms of a family now attainted, displayed openly in a licensed workshop. Most businesses removed such things after the edicts. Yours didn't."

"The master said a map doesn't take sides," I said. "He said ink is ink, whoever pays for it."

"A common sentiment among mapmakers." Brann smiled again, and this time the smile had teeth in it somewhere, though I couldn't see them. "And an uncommonly convenient one." He closed his book. "Thank you both. The bureau may have follow-up questions. We're verifying prior households across the trade—apprentices move quietly, and quiet movement concerns the Crown."

"We've nothing to hide," Tomas said warmly.

"No," Brann agreed. "Most people don't. It's remarkable how often the ones who say it loudest are telling the truth." He gathered his satchel, paused at the door as if remembering something trivial, and produced a small card—cream stock, guild-printed, the census seal stamped in blue. "In case you recall anything further, Miss Iversen. Names are like maps. The longer you look away from them, the harder they are to draw accurately."

Then he was gone, his footsteps fading down the stair, and the workshop settled around us with the particular silence of a room that has been measured.

* * *

Tomas laughed first, which surprised me. "Scullery girl from the country," he said, shaking his head. "'Not the sort of people one remembers.' Petra, you delivered that like scripture. Where did you learn to lie like that?"

"From being asked questions," I said. "Where did you learn to enjoy them?"

"I don't enjoy them. I respect them. There's a difference." He picked up Brann's abandoned form-copy from the bench, read it idly, and frowned. "Did you notice? He didn't ask about residents. Census men ask about residents—that's the entire occupation. Births, deaths, lodgers, who sleeps under which roof. He asked where we'd *been*."

"I noticed."

"He knew the Halvard name before he saw the frame. He walked in knowing there was something to find." Tomas set the paper down and looked at me with an expression I couldn't read and have spent a long time since learning to read: interest, sharpened into something with edges. "You went pale. When he touched the crest. Just for a moment."

"I went pale because a government man put his finger on my master's best work like he owned the glass. Any apprentice would."

"Any apprentice," Tomas agreed pleasantly, and dropped it.

That should have comforted me. Instead it lodged in me like a splinter, working deeper whenever I forgot it. Because Tomas was many things—vain, clever, hungry in the specific way of talented people born one rank below their appetites—but he was not careless, and he did not drop things. He filed them. I watched him do it for three years without understanding that I was watching a ledger being kept, and that someday the accounts would come due.

* * *

That evening, after Tomas left, I took down the card Brann had left and examined it under the good lamp, the one Master Aldous had used for checking proofs. Cream stock, blue seal, his name—*C. Brann, Assistant Enumerator, Bureau of Records and Verification*—and beneath the printing, in the corner where a watermark might sit, a faint embossing that I had missed during the visit itself because he'd held the card face-out, angled, deliberate.

A crest. A stag's head, crowned in laurel.

Struck through with a single diagonal line, pressed deep into the paper—not printed, not part of the card's manufacture, but added afterward, by hand, with a stylus or a heavy pen nib, the way a clerk cancels an account. Crossed out. Deliberately, neatly, unmistakably crossed out, and then handed to *me*, held at just the angle where I would find it only after he'd gone, when finding it could do its work.

He hadn't been asking questions. He'd been delivering an answer. *I know where you served. I know whose crest used to hang on your wall. And I know you know that I know.* The card wasn't contact information. It was a hook, baited and left in plain water, waiting to see what rose to it.

I sat down on the master's stool—he'd have hated anyone sitting there; I sat there anyway, every night, out of a grief that expressed itself as trespass—and I felt it arrive. Not fear exactly. Fear is hot, and this was cold, a single drop of it sliding down behind the sternum, precise as a plumb line finding bottom.

Three years I had been Petra Iversen, orphaned apprentice, unremarkable, invisible, safe. Three years of routine so perfect it had become a kind of armor: open at seven, ink until ten, market on Thursdays, guild dues quarterly, sleep in the loft above the plates. And underneath the armor, in the locked drawer I had never opened and thought about constantly, the thing that made the armor necessary—the plate Master Aldous forbade anyone to finish, and whatever else he'd left sealed with it.

Brann knew about the Halvards. Perhaps not everything. Perhaps not yet. But a man who crosses out a crest by hand is a man who intends to cross out more than crests.

I turned the card over. Blank. Of course blank. Men like Brann don't write threats; they issue stationery.

Outside, the harbor bells rang the nine o'clock, and somewhere beyond the rooftops the sea did what it always does, indifferent and enormous. I looked at the wall where the framed survey hung, at the two signatures side by side—my master's steady hand, and beside it the mark of a lord now attainted, whose son Elias had been a boy of eleven the last time I'd seen him, carrying books he was too proud to admit were too heavy.

*I know where you served.*

Yes. And worse—I knew what I'd promised, or what the master had promised in my hearing, in another life, in peacetime, when promises between great houses and humble servants still seemed like the kind of thing the future would honor.

I put the card in the drawer of my workbench, face down, and did not sleep, and in the morning the coastline I'd inked had a flaw in it—one island drawn half a degree off true—which no one would ever see, but which I would know about forever.

That is the trouble with lying well. The maps remember.

* * *

It was three days before I could make myself do it, and I tell you honestly that I chose the drawer because of the card, and I have never decided whether that makes Brann the author of everything that followed or merely the man who knocked on a door that was already unlocked. I unlocked Master Aldous's desk at dusk, with the shop shut and the lamp low, and I reached past the plate—for the moment, past the plate—to the letter beneath it, in its envelope gone soft with age, sealed with wax I recognized because I'd carried messages sealed with it once, long ago.

The stag's head, crowned in laurel.

Unbroken. Waiting.

I broke it, because a man had crossed the same crest out on a cream-colored card, and because I understood suddenly that the crossing-out was coming for everything else in that room unless someone moved first.

The letter was dated seven years back. It was addressed to no one, which is to say it was addressed to me, which is to say Master Aldous had known for seven years exactly who would end up holding it, and had said nothing, and had let me believe for three years that the silence meant safety.

It began: *To the hand that finishes my atlas—*

And I stood there in the lamplight, an apprentice in a locked shop with a government man's card in my pocket and a dead man's promise opening in my hands, and I understood that the cold drop behind my sternum had been the last easy feeling I would have for a very long time.
