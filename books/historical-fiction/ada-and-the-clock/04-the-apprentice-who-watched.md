## 4. The Apprentice Who Watched

There are three kinds of people who come into a mapmaker's workshop at midnight: drunkards looking for the harbor, thieves looking for copper, and apprentices looking for glory. I have been all three, though not in that order, and never on the same night — until the night I caught Petra Iversen bent over the sealed plate like a woman praying to a god she didn't believe in.

Let me be honest about how I found out, because honesty is a currency I intend to spend carefully later.

I wasn't looking for anything. That is the lie I'd tell a magistrate. The truth is I was always looking for something. An apprentice in Vellmark who isn't looking for something is an apprentice who intends to die an apprentice, and I had watched enough masters die comfortable to know the workshop rewards neither loyalty nor talent. It rewards leverage. Master Aldous Iversen used to say that a good mapmaker learns to see what isn't drawn — the coastlines under the coastlines, the trade winds behind the trade winds. He meant it as a lesson in cartography. I took it as instruction.

So when Petra started staying late — later than her usual late, which was already indecent for a girl who rose before the gulls — I noticed. And when she began locking the flat drawer under the drafting table, the one with the brass plate on the front that said *PROPERTY OF A. IVERSEN* as if that distinguished it from any other object in the building, I noticed harder.

* * *

The workshop sleeps in layers. Old buildings do. First the street goes quiet, then the harbor bell slows its complaining, then the house next door stops coughing, and finally the workshop itself settles into its own particular silence — a creak of drying paper, a tick of cooling inkstone, the small animal sounds of vellum contracting in the damp. I know these sounds the way a sailor knows rigging. Which meant that on the third Tuesday of the month, when I heard a sound that did not belong — the soft scrape of the drafting stool being dragged rather than rolled — I lay in my cot above the shop and listened like a customs officer at a hull.

Petra worked by candle-shield. Clever girl. A shielded candle throws its light down and inward, so from the stairwell you see nothing but a faint warm bloom on the ceiling, easy to mistake for moonlight off the plaster. But moonlight doesn't scratch. Moonlight doesn't pause, hold its breath, and scratch again. And moonlight certainly doesn't whisper to itself in the cadence of someone reciting longitude corrections under her breath like a rosary.

I went down the stairs the way I go everywhere — quietly, and with an exit already chosen.

Through the gap where the door had shrunk away from its frame — old houses again, generous with their secrets — I watched her unroll the sealed plate.

I want you to understand what that plate is, because I didn't fully understand it myself yet, and my ignorance is the only reason I'm telling this story instead of selling it. Every atlas has a heart. Ours had forty-one plates of coasts and currents and soundings gathered over thirty years of Master Aldous's obsessive, magnificent, ruinous career. Forty plates were finished or nearly so. The forty-first — the northern approaches, the deepwater channels past the headlands where no merchant ship has honest business — sat in its drawer with a wax seal across the wrapping that Master Aldous himself had pressed the winter before he died. *Not yet,* he'd said, when I asked. Just that. Not yet. As if the plate were wine. As if the plate were a wound.

And now Petra had broken the seal — no. Wait. Let me be precise, because precision is the only virtue I own outright. She had not broken the seal. She had lifted it whole, with a warmed knife and a patience I found genuinely unsettling, the way you're unsettled watching someone pick a lock with perfect tenderness. The seal sat intact beside her elbow on a scrap of felt while she inked the coastline beneath it.

She was finishing his forbidden map in his forbidden hand. She'd been practicing his lettering — I recognized it because I'd spent two years trying to steal it myself and knew every trick of it, the way he hooked his *f*s like little anchors, the way his numerals leaned east. She had it nearly perfect. Nearly. There was a hesitation in her strokes, a half-breath of doubt before each curve, that the master never had. He drew like a man who believed the world would hold still for him. She drew like a woman asking its permission.

I stood in the dark with my heart going like a bilge pump and thought: *Well, Tomas. Here is your life, lying on a table under a candle shield.*

* * *

Here is the thing about Petra Iversen that everyone gets wrong, including — especially — herself: she thinks she is invisible. She has built herself into such a plain, competent, gray-wrapped shape that the merchants who come in for charts look straight through her, and even the ones who notice her notice only the hands, quick and clean, and the eyes that stay lowered just long enough to seem shy without ever actually surrendering information. Three years she has worn this costume. It's good work. I'd compliment it if she weren't wearing it at my expense.

But I had three years too. And I'd noticed things. Small things, filed away the way I file everything, in the drawer under my ribs that opens only when I'm alone:

She counted money like a lady counting other people's money — quickly, accurately, and slightly above it.

When the census rolls came through last spring she read hers twice and burned it in the stove, which is careful, but then she fed the fire until nothing remained, which is frightened.

She knew the Halvard quarter — the old Halvard quarter, before the name got scraped off the street signs — better than any apprentice should. Once, delivering a chart to a solicitor, she navigated the back lanes there without hesitating at a single corner, the way you only walk streets you grew up serving in.

And once — this is my favorite, I keep it like a coin in a pocket — a client mentioned, in passing, the exiled family's estate out east, some anecdote about the orchards gone wild, and Petra's pen did not stop moving, did not hesitate, did not blot. Which is exactly what a person does when they've trained themselves, hour upon hour, against precisely that moment. Innocence doesn't practice.

I don't claim to know what any of it means. I claim to know what it's worth.

* * *

The next morning I began my gentle campaign, because a siege conducted loudly fails, and because — I'll admit it here, in the privacy of my own skull where no one can tax it — because I liked her. Like her. Present tense, God help me. There is a version of this story where Tomas Rell is a villain, cold-eyed and patient, and I want it noted that the villain version of me also brings her coffee.

"You were up late," I said, setting the cup by her elbow. "I heard the stool."

Her pen paused for exactly one heartbeat. "Couldn't sleep. Did some reading."

"Reading." I looked at the open book beside her — a tide almanac, correctly chosen, boring as a sermon. "Which chapter?"

"The tedious one."

"Those are usually the truest." I smiled. She smiled back the way a fortress smiles at an ambassador, and I went off to grind pigments feeling like a man who'd knocked on a door and heard, from inside, furniture being moved against it.

Good, I remember thinking. Good. Let her bar the doors. Barred doors tell you where the treasure is.

Over the following week I made a study of casualness. I mentioned, while trimming vellum, that Master Aldous had kept certain commissions private even from us, hadn't he, strange man, wonderful man. I mentioned that the guild examiners were sniffing around unfinished works lately, wanting inventories, wanting provenance. I mentioned — lightly, oh so lightly, a feather dipped in tar — that whoever completed the master's atlas would be remembered for it. Whoever. Genderless. Innocent as rain.

Petra received each of these offerings with the same pleasant blankness, thanked me for the coffee twice, and changed the subject to pigment prices, which she understood I understood was a rebuke.

But here's what she couldn't help: she started working earlier instead of later. She moved her midnight hours toward dawn, when the household above was stirring and my listening post lost its value. A guilty person changes nothing. An innocent person changes nothing. Only a person who knows she is watched adjusts her schedule, and the adjustment told me everything her face refused to.

I could have hated her for it. Some part of me did, briefly, hotly — the part that had spent years being the clever one, the reliable one, the one masters praised in the tone you use for a well-made hinge. She arrived three years ago with nothing but a surname she wore like a borrowed coat and a grief she wouldn't discuss, and within a season she was the master's favorite, the keeper of his final work, the heir to his masterpiece — and she hadn't even wanted it. That was the unforgivable part. She carried the atlas like a debt. I would have carried it like a crown.

But hate is expensive, and I was saving.

* * *

On Thursday it rained the way Vellmark rains in autumn — sideways, vindictive, personal — and business stopped, and the workshop filled with the smell of wet wool and lamp oil, and Petra and I sat across from each other at the long table doing the dull sacred work of correcting place-names on coastal sheets, and for an hour it was simply pleasant. This is the trap of her, I've decided. She is genuinely good company. Wry in a dry, buried way, like humor stored in a cellar. When she forgets to be invisible, she's funny, and kind in the specific practical manner of someone who has known need — she'll re-ink your ruined line without comment, she'll save you the last of the good paper, and she'll never, ever mention it afterward, because mentioning kindness makes it a transaction and she cannot abide debts.

I, on the other hand, keep meticulous accounts.

"Tomas," she said, apropos of a fishing village we'd both misspelled differently, "why did you come to the workshop? Originally."

No one had ever asked me that. Apprentices are assumed to have been assigned, like ballast. I turned my pen in my fingers and considered how much truth the market would bear.

"My father sold charts," I said. "Bad ones. Beautifully drawn, wildly wrong — he'd shift a sandbar if a captain insulted him." I heard my own voice do something unexpected, soften somewhere. "He drowned off a coast he'd misdrawn himself. I came here to learn to draw them right." All true. All arranged, like flowers, to hide the stem. What I didn't add: that I'd learned within a year that *right* is a matter of payment, that every chart is a weapon pointed somewhere, and that my father's sin wasn't dishonesty but poverty.

Petra looked at me across the lamplight with those lowered-then-lifting eyes, and for a moment — one treacherous moment — I thought she might tell me something real. Her mouth opened. Closed. She said, "That's a good reason," and bent back to her village, and whatever had risen in her sank again, deliberate as a diver.

That night I took out paper.

* * *

I should describe the letter, since I wrote it and then did not send it, which may be the most honest act of my life to date, and also the most strategic, though even I can't fully separate the motives anymore, and I've stopped trying, mostly.

It was addressed to no one. That was the point. It was addressed to *the office concerned*, which in this city could mean the census bureau, the admiralty, the guild, or the harbor police, depending on which window you carried it to and what mood the clerk was in. Information is the one cargo in Vellmark that never spoils and never glutted the market.

In it I wrote what I had seen: an apprentice working after hours on sealed materials belonging to the late Master Iversen; evidence of concealed identity or at minimum concealed history; the location and nature of the locked drawer; my professional assessment that the forty-first plate contained material of governmental interest, given its subject — the northern approaches, the channels the navy had been hungry for since before the exile. I signed it with my full name, because anonymous letters are discounted ten percent at every counter in this town, and I am nothing if not attentive to price.

Then I folded it, sealed it, addressed it, and put it in the bottom of my trunk, under the spare shirts, and lay on my cot and stared at the ceiling beams and felt like a man holding a lit lantern in a powder store, admiring the light.

Why didn't I send it?

The coward's answer: because once sent, it's spent, and I'd have nothing left to trade. The sentimentalist's answer: because of the coffee, the corrected lines, the hour at the long table when she almost told me something true. Both answers are true, which is the problem with me generally.

The real answer, the one I set down here so that someday, if this all goes wrong, the record shows I knew exactly what I was: the letter wasn't worth more unsent. It was worth more *pending*. A threat delivered is a threat concluded. A threat held is a relationship. And I wanted — this is the ugly, tender root of it — I wanted to matter to her. Not as a colleague. As a contingency. If she trusted me, fine, pleasant, useful. If she ever needed to fear me, the letter would be waiting, patient as a reef under fair water. Either way, Tomas Rell would no longer be the hinge. Hinges get replaced.

I blew out the candle. Downstairs, faint through the floorboards, I heard the soft scrape of the drafting stool being dragged to the table.

* * *

Friday morning brought a visitor, which is how these things always arrive — not as consequence but as weather.

I saw him from the window first: a broad, unhurried man in a good coat gone slightly travel-stained, standing across the square studying our shopfront the way buyers study horseflesh. He consulted a card in his hand. He crossed the street without hurrying, which is the walk of a man with authority and no appointment, and the worst combination there is.

He introduced himself at the door as Brann, census bureau, and asked — politely, terribly politely — whether the workshop employed any apprentices whose prior households might bear review.

Prior households.

I watched Petra go still over her inkpot, stillness so complete it looked like calm, and I watched the visitor watch her not-calm with the mild professional interest of a man who has done this many times and enjoys the parts that aren't in the manual. And I stood there with my hands clean and my trunk heavy with an unsent letter, doing rapid arithmetic — what he knew, what she knew I knew, what I could afford to know out loud —

— and heard myself say, before I had quite decided to, "We're just apprentices, sir. Ink and errands. Nothing worth reviewing."

Brann smiled at me like a man marking a card, left one of his own on the counter, and departed into the rain.

Petra didn't thank me. That's the thing I keep turning over. She didn't thank me — she looked at me instead, one long measuring look, the first time in three years she'd ever really looked, as if the fortress had finally opened a shutter to check whether the siege engines were mine.

They are, I suppose. They just haven't fired yet.

That night I took the letter from the trunk and read it once by candlelight, all that neat damning prose in my best copyist's hand, and I did not burn it. I put it back under the shirts, square in the middle, where finding it would be easy — for me, or for whoever came looking.

Downstairs, very softly, the stool scraped.
