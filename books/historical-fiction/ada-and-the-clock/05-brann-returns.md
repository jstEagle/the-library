## 5. Brann Returns

The rain came to Vellmark that October the way debt collectors come: politely at first, then all at once and through every gap you'd forgotten you had. It drummed on the workshop's north skylight for three days straight, and by the fourth morning the whole city smelled of wet wool and river mud, and I had stopped pretending I wasn't waiting for something.

I knew he'd come back. Men like Brann always come back. The first visit is a courtesy; the second is a habit forming. And habits, once formed in a census bureau, tend to harden into policy.

He arrived at half past nine, while the light was still too thin to work by and Tomas was still out fetching sizing from Halvorsen's chandlery — a errand I had suggested, gently, because some conversations a person needs to have alone.

"Frøken Voss." He stood in the doorway dripping onto our good floorboards, hat in hand, rain beading on the grey wool of his shoulders. He did not look like a man who owned an umbrella. He looked like a man who considered umbrellas a form of vanity. "You'll forgive the hour."

"I'll forgive the weather," I said. "It can't help itself. Neither, I suppose, can you."

Something flickered behind his eyes that might have been amusement. With Brann it was difficult to say. His face was built for patience — long, unremarkable, the sort of face you forget before the door closes behind him. Which is, of course, precisely what makes such faces dangerous.

"The bureau has an interest in workshop records," he said. "Commercial registries, apprentice indentures, correspondence, accounts. Standard procedure under the Enumeration Act. You wouldn't have read it — few do — but Section Twelve provides for inspection of premises engaged in the production of documents intended for public distribution."

"We make maps," I said. "Maps aren't documents. They're pictures of places. Places belong to everyone."

"Places," said Brann, "belong to the crown. Maps merely describe them. Descriptions, frøken, are where trouble lives." He produced a folded paper from his coat — not a warrant, I noted, though he held it as if the difference were a technicality he expected me not to notice. A request. An *invitation*. The bureau was very fond of invitations; declining one was possible, in the way that swimming away from a net is possible. "Three days. Inspectors will call Thursday morning. Everything should be available. Nothing should be missing."

"And if something is missing?"

His gaze moved past my shoulder, slow and unhurried, across the workshop — the plan chests, the press, the great table scarred by thirty years of compass points, the shelf where Master Aldous's atlas-in-progress lay wrapped in oilcloth like a body awaiting burial or resurrection, depending on one's politics. "Then someone will have made a very interesting choice," he said. "And I do so enjoy interesting choices. They tell me everything I need to know about a person."

He put his hat back on. At the door he paused, without turning around.

"You served a household before your apprenticeship, I believe. Your references mentioned it vaguely. Households are like weather in this country — everyone passes through several." A beat. "Do give the matter some thought before Thursday. Memory is such a cooperative organ when it wants to be."

Then he was gone, and I stood listening to his footsteps fade into the rain, feeling the particular cold that comes not from fear exactly but from its first cousin: the understanding that a clock you cannot see has started running.

* * *

Tomas came back smelling of turpentine and gossip — Halvorsen's chandlery being the exchange floor for every rumor on the eastern quays — and found me pulling charts from the flat files with more energy than the task required.

"Inspection?" he said, when I told him. He whistled low. "Section Twelve. That's the one they used on the printing houses last spring, after the pamphlets. They took apart old Sørensen's shop rivet by rivet. Found a press plate hidden in a plaster bust of the king, would you believe. Very disrespectful to the bust."

"Tomas."

"Yes?"

"Do we have anything that shouldn't be found?"

And there it was — the question hanging in the damp air between us, innocent as a loaded pistol. He looked at me for a moment longer than the question strictly required, and his face did something complicated that I chose, in the interest of my own survival, not to read.

"We have three years of unpaid paper invoices," he said lightly, "and a jar of brushes Aldous let go moldy rather than admit he'd bought hog bristle. Beyond that, frøken Voss, we are the most boring establishment in Vellmark." He hung up his coat. "Though I'd tidy the correspondence drawer. Inspectors love correspondence. It makes them feel literary."

He was right, and worse, he was helpful, and I hated how much relief I took from both.

* * *

Here is the arithmetic of my situation, laid out plain, the way I lay out a survey before committing ink:

In the locked bottom drawer of Master Aldous's desk — a lock I had picked eleven days ago with a fishhook and no small amount of shame — sat a letter bearing the seal of the house of Halvard, written in Aldous's own careful hand seven years ago and never sent. It was a promise. A peacetime promise, made when promises still seemed like things a man could keep: that when the atlas was finished, it would go to Elias Halvard, the old lord's grandson, along with the final plate — the coastal defense chart, the one map in the collection the crown would burn men for owning. Aldous had died protecting it. I had inherited the protecting, whether I'd signed for it or not.

And now the crown's clerks were coming Thursday to look through everything.

I could burn the letter. That was the sensible course. Paper burns; grief doesn't; and a dead man's promise carries no legal weight against a living woman's neck. Every rational instinct I owned voted for the fire.

But I had spent three years finishing his coastlines, inking his unfinished bays, matching my hand to his until sometimes at midnight I couldn't tell which lines were whose — and I will tell you something about that kind of work. It teaches you that a map is a promise too. Every contour says: *this is true, I measured it, I stake my name on it.* You don't spend three years keeping another person's cartographic faith and then break the human kind over a drawer lock. Or maybe you do. But I couldn't, and I've stopped apologizing for it.

So the letter needed a hiding place, and it needed one tonight.

* * *

Every trade has its graveyard. In mapmaking it's the wetlands.

Nobody buys wetland charts. Farmers know their own marshes, hunters know them better, and merchants cross water only where they can build on it. Our flat file drawer marked *V — Marsh & Fen* held forty-one charts accumulated over twenty years, and I could count on one hand the times any had been sold, framed, or even sneezed upon. They were rolled, tied with string, and abandoned — the cartographic equivalent of the family members nobody mentions at dinner.

More importantly: they were *mine*. I maintained that drawer. I re-rolled every chart each spring against the damp. No inspector, however thorough, lingers over a wetland chart. Even Brann's breed of patience has limits, and those limits smell of peat.

I took down the third roll from the left — a survey of the Sorvagen fenlands, 1839, rendered in browns so cautious the whole thing looked embarrassed — and slit the hollow of the roller with a bone folder. The letter went in folded to the width of a knife blade, wrapped in waxed cloth against the damp. Then a smear of hide glue, a strip of matching paper over the seam, two hours under weight while I worked the presses with hands that refused to stop shaking.

That's the part no one tells you about lying. Not the planning — planning is almost pleasant, a puzzle with stakes. It's the *doing*. Standing there with glue on my fingers, pressing a dead man's promise into a chart of a marsh neither of us ever saw, I felt sick in a way I hadn't felt even the night I buried his last plate beneath the floorboards. Because burying a thing is a single act. This was becoming a person who buries things. There is a difference, and the difference compounds daily, like interest.

When it was done, the chart went back in its place among its forty brothers, indistinguishable, dull, safe. I tied the string in the same sloppy knot I always used. Details matter. Inspectors notice fresh knots the way hawks notice movement.

Then I sat down at the great table, took up the coastline I was inking — the Jutland shore, Aldous's last unfinished stretch — and made myself work until my hand steadied. Which took longer than I'd like to admit.

* * *

Wednesday night I barely slept. I lay in my room above the workshop cataloguing everything the inspectors might touch: the apprenticeship papers — clean, forged well enough three years ago that even I half-believed them; the accounts — honest, because poverty is the best camouflage; the correspondence — mostly orders, plus two letters from a cousin in Dragefjord who didn't exist until I invented her, complete with a convincing hand and tedious news of a wedding.

At some hour past midnight I heard Tomas's door open and his footsteps cross the landing. Pause. Move on. Downstairs, a minute later, the scratch of a pen.

I told myself it was nothing. Men write letters at midnight. Ambitious young men write letters constantly — to guild masters, to printers in the capital, to anyone who might lift them out of a dying workshop in a port town full of rain. I told myself that, and I believed roughly sixty percent of it, and I lay staring at the ceiling doing sums with the other forty.

* * *

Thursday came grey and punctual.

Two clerks, one inspector — a broad, incurious man named Foss who smelled of tobacco and authority — and, standing slightly apart in the doorway where the rain couldn't quite reach him, Brann.

They took the workshop apart with bureaucratic gentleness. Foss read the accounts twice. The clerks went through the correspondence drawer page by page; one of them lingered on Cousin Ingrid's wedding letter and I experienced a full-body conviction that I would die in that room, and then he yawned and moved on. They opened the flat files. They unrolled charts — fjords, grain districts, the shipping lanes — with the brisk disrespect of men handling other people's lifework.

Brann himself drifted. That was the worst of it. He didn't search; he *wandered*, touching nothing, reading spines, letting the others do the digging while he did something far more dangerous, which was watching me watch them. Whenever a clerk pulled a chart I hadn't personally vetted, something in my chest performed a small controlled collapse. When they reached the V drawer — the wetlands — I was copying a scale bar and my pen did not waver by a hair's width, and I want credit for that, wherever credit is recorded.

Foss flipped through three wetland charts, pronounced them "agricultural," and closed the drawer.

By noon it was over. The atlas, wrapped in its oilcloth on its shelf, drew a glance and no more — just one completed folio among many, its forbidden plate long since gone from its binding, its remaining pages innocently geographic. Foss signed a slip of paper saying the premises were in order. The clerks reassembled themselves. Brann settled his hat.

"Everything in order," he said, to no one in particular. Then, to me, with the mildness of a man remarking on the tide: "Remarkable, how much a small workshop accumulates in three years. Papers especially. They get everywhere. Into drawers. Under floors." He smiled without warmth. "Paper remembers, frøken Voss. That's its whole virtue and its whole crime. Do take care what yours remembers."

The door shut behind them. Rain resumed its argument with the skylight. Across the room, Tomas emerged from the mending alcove where he'd spent the entire inspection conspicuously repairing a roller, and gave me a look I couldn't file anywhere — part solidarity, part question mark.

"Well," he said. "We're boring after all."

"We're boring," I agreed.

That evening, after dark, I checked the string on the wetlands drawer. Same sloppy knot. The chart weighed true. The letter slept on in its paper tomb, patient as its author.

And yet walking home from the water closet an hour later, I caught myself reciting — unbidden, word for word, in a voice seven years younger — the words old Lord Halvard had spoken over the atlas the night the pact was sealed. Words I hadn't thought of since. *A promise made in peacetime binds harder than any made in war,* he'd said, laying his ringed hand flat on the first folio, *because war makes liars of necessity, and peace has no such excuse.*

I had been fourteen, carrying candles, invisible, memorizing everything the way servants do.

Now the peace was long dead, the lord was dead, the master was dead, and the promise sat rolled up in a chart nobody wanted, waiting for me to decide what kind of liar I intended to become.
