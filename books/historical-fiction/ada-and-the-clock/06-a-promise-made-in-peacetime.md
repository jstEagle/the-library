## 6. A Promise Made in Peacetime

Seven years ago, I learned that lords drink their tea like ordinary men—too hot, too fast, and with the same grim surprise when it scalds them.

I was sixteen, and a servant in the Halvard household on the cliff road above Vellmark harbor. Not a grand position. I carried coal, polished silver that only saw daylight twice a year, and fetched things for Lady Halvard's sister, who had opinions about everything and volume to spare. But it was work, and it was warm, and in the winter of 1840 warmth was not a small thing.

I had been with them two years by then. Long enough to know that Lord Halvard took his walk at dawn regardless of weather, that young Elias read at dinner until his father pretended not to notice, and that the household ran on a quiet current of kindness that I mistook, at sixteen, for simply how houses were. It was not how houses were. I would learn that later, expensively.

* * *

The day the mapmaker came, it had been raining since Tuesday, the kind of grey sideways rain that makes the whole coast feel like it's being slowly erased. I remember because I spent the morning arguing with the fire in the west parlor, which had decided that wet birch was a personal insult and refused to catch. I won, eventually. I usually did with fires. It is one of the few contests in my life where I have maintained an unbroken record.

Aldous Iversen arrived looking like a man who had been folded into a carriage too small for him and never quite shaken back out. He was fifty-odd then, broad through the shoulders the way men who lean over drafting tables all their lives somehow still are, with ink stains up both forearms like he'd lost a fight with an octopus and made peace about it. He stood dripping in the entrance hall, hat in hand, and looked around at our modest grandeur with the expression of a man assessing a wall for load-bearing qualities.

"You're the mapmaker," said Mr. Osten, the steward, who possessed the conversational range of a locked door.

"Master Aldous Iversen, cartographer, at your lordship's service." Then, catching sight of me hovering with my coal scuttle: "And you're the one who keeps this place from freezing. God bless you. My workshop has a stove that works out of spite."

I liked him immediately. This should have been a warning. Liking people has historically gone very badly for me.

* * *

They met in the library—the long room with the windows that caught the sea light—and because the household was short-staffed that week, I was the one sent in with the tea tray. Which is how I came to be present, technically invisible, in the way servants are trained to be: eyes down, ears open, a piece of furniture that happens to breathe.

Lord Halvard sat behind his desk. He was not yet the broken thing the newspapers would later make of him; he was seventy then and straight-backed, with white hair cut short and hands spotted with age but steady. Elias, fifteen, was supposed to be at his lessons but had stationed himself by the window with a book, radiating the studied indifference of a boy listening to every word.

"The atlas is finished," Master Iversen said, without preamble. "All but one plate."

"And the crown wants it," Lord Halvard said. Not a question.

"The crown wants to buy it. There's a difference, though admittedly a fine one." Iversen turned his cup, not drinking. "They'll pay well. They always pay well. And then they'll lock it away, and the coasts will belong to whoever holds the key, and no fisherman or pilot or fool in a leaking boat will ever see what his own shoreline looks like."

"That sounds like treason, Master Iversen."

"It sounds like geography, my lord. Treason is just geography with feelings attached."

I nearly dropped the sugar tongs. Servants do not laugh. I converted it into a cough, badly, and both men glanced at me and dismissed me in the same instant, which is its own kind of skill.

Lord Halvard leaned back. "You could have sold it quietly. Why bring it here?"

"Because you asked me to build it, thirty years ago, before either of us had anything to lose. Because half those soundings are yours—we took them together in that rotten little sloop you insisted on calling seaworthy." Iversen smiled, briefly, and it aged him twenty years in the wrong direction. "And because war is coming. Don't look at me like that. Everyone knows. The ministries know, the merchants know, the fish know. And when it comes, someone will decide that maps are weapons, and they will not be entirely wrong."

Elias had lowered his book. I watched him over the rim of the teapot—the whole scene, really, framed in the window beside him: rain, grey sea, two old men dividing up the world.

"So what do you propose?" Lord Halvard said.

"A pact. In peacetime, while it still means something." Iversen set down the cup and drew from his coat a document folded in thirds, and I understood, with a servant's unerring instinct, that this was why he'd come through the rain instead of waiting it out. "When the atlas is done—all of it, including the plate I've sealed—I deliver it into Halvard hands. Not to sell. To keep. To publish, if it ever becomes possible, under both our names. Your soundings, my penmanship. A record that belongs to the country, not the crown."

"And if the crown objects?"

"Then we shall have a disagreement of long standing." Iversen's smile again. "I've been disagreeing with the crown since before you were knighted."

There was a silence in which the rain did most of the talking. Then Lord Halvard laughed—a real laugh, rusty, like a door opening after winter—and I remember thinking that I had served in that house two years and had never once heard it.

"You always were a romantic with ink on his hands," the old lord said. "Very well. Witnessed and sealed. Though I warn you, Iversen—if it comes to war, promises made in peacetime have a way of becoming expensive."

"All the best ones are."

* * *

I wish I could tell you I understood, that afternoon, what I was witnessing. I did not. Sixteen-year-olds understand tea trays. I saw two old friends make a solemn fuss over paper and thought, kindly, that rich men needed hobbies too.

What I remember instead is the smaller things, which is how memory always cheats you. The way Elias came off the window seat the moment the sealing wax appeared, pretending he'd been drawn by curiosity rather than hunger to be included. How his father let him pour the wax himself—let him, a fifteen-year-old boy, press the Halvard crest into the cooling red—and how Elias held his breath doing it, as if the seal might judge him. How Iversen looked at the boy afterward and said, "Well. Now it's partly yours to guard, young master. Congratulations on your inheritance of trouble," and Elias grinned like he'd been handed a sword instead of a joke.

How Lady Halvard found us all still in the library at dusk, the pact signed, the atlas promised, and said only, "Dinner is at eight, and if any of you have made history, wash your hands first."

It was a warm room. That is the truest thing I can say about it. Warm with rain outside and fire inside and four people—I count myself, because for one afternoon the furniture that breathes was allowed at the table—four people believing that the future was a thing you could fold in thirds and put in a drawer.

* * *

War came, as wars do, on a schedule nobody admitted to knowing in advance.

I watched the household change season by season. First the visitors stopped coming—no one wants to be seen dining with a man the ministries have started frowning at. Then the money tightened; we went from three footmen to one, and the silver saw daylight more often because it kept getting sold. Then the letters began arriving with government crests, and Lord Halvard stopped reading them aloud to his wife over breakfast and started reading them alone with the door shut.

I stayed. People ask me sometimes why I didn't leave, and I've never had an answer better than: where would I go? Loyalty and inertia wear the same clothes, and from the inside you cannot always tell them apart.

The night the soldiers came for the archive, I was nineteen. I will not describe it fully—not now, not here. Some rooms you carry with you whether or not you furnish the description. What I will say is that they took everything with official courtesy: the papers, the ledgers, the portraits, the letters. They were polite the way a tide is polite. And Lord Halvard, who had walked at dawn every morning of his life regardless of weather, sat down afterward in the emptied library and did not get up for a very long time.

Elias was twenty-one. I saw him stand in that gutted room and make a face I had never seen on the boy who'd poured the sealing wax—a face with no boy left in it.

Three days later, the household was dissolved. The staff scattered. And I did the single practical thing of my life to that point: I walked down the cliff road to the mapmaker's workshop, knocked on the door, and told Aldous Iversen that I was excellent with fires, adequate with ink, and completely without a surname anyone would find interesting.

He looked at me for a long moment. "You were in the library that day," he said. "With the tea. You coughed at the wrong moment."

"I apologize for that."

"Don't." He stepped aside to let me in. "It was the correct response to everything said in that room. I merely lacked the courage to join you."

That was seven years ago. That was another country, the one called Peacetime, whose borders closed behind us all.

* * *

Now it is 1847, and I am alone in a workshop above a sleeping street, holding a letter I found in my dead master's locked drawer, addressed in his careful hand to a family declared enemy of the state.

*I, Aldous Iversen, cartographer, do promise...*

I have read it eleven times. It does not change on the twelfth reading, though I keep checking, the way you check a wound hoping it's healed. The pact stands. The atlas must go to Elias Halvard—to the last of them, the exile, the traitor-in-print, the name that gets men visited by census takers with crossed-out crests on their cards.

Master, you sentimental old octopus-fighter. You knew, didn't you? Standing in that rainy library, laughing with a doomed lord—you knew exactly what you were handing down, and to whom, and how much it would cost. You let a serving girl hear the whole of it because some part of you was already writing her into the will.

Outside, the city sleeps, unaware it is about to become a battlefield of paper. Inside, the candle burns low, and somewhere across town—or across the sea, no one will say—a young man who once held his breath over sealing wax waits for a promise he probably believes died with the peacetime that made it.

I blow out the candle.

Tomorrow I begin figuring out how to reach a ghost.
