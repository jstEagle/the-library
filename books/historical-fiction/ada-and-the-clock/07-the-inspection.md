## 7. The Inspection

*Petra*

They came at nine in the morning, which I took as a courtesy and an insult in equal measure. Nine was the hour when the workshop smelled most of itself—sizing glue and lamp oil and the particular dust of vellum that gets into your hair and never entirely leaves—and they arrived knowing we would be mid-task, hands dirty, tempers thin. Bureaucrats have an instinct for the moment a person is least able to argue.

There were four of them. Two clerks with ledgers under their arms, a younger man whose job appeared to be standing near the door and looking significant, and Brann.

Brann had shaved since his last visit. Someone had told him that a clean jaw reads as honest, and someone else had told him that a gray coat reads as impartial, and he had believed all of it. He stood in the doorway of the workshop with his hat held to his chest like a man entering a church he didn't believe in, and he said, "Mistress Petra. Master Tomas. Thank you for receiving us."

"We weren't asked," said Tomas.

"Which is why I'm thanking you." He smiled at his own cleverness. It was the smile of a man who has been praised for thoroughness his whole life and has mistaken it for virtue.

I set down my pen. The trick, I had learned over three years of hiding in plain sight, is to never look like you're setting anything down carefully. You set it down the way you'd set down a sandwich. "You'll want the records room, then."

"Eventually." Brann walked the length of the workshop slowly, trailing one finger along the edge of the plan chest without actually touching it—a man demonstrating restraint as though restraint were evidence of innocence. "We'll begin wherever you keep your current commissions."

Tomas glanced at me. I gave him nothing back, because there was nothing left to give; everything incriminating had spent the last two nights rolled inside a chart of the Vellmark wetlands that no one had purchased in eleven years, wedged behind the sample rack where even I forgot it existed most days. That was the whole architecture of my defense: boredom. I had hidden a promise made by a dead man behind the least interesting object in the building, and I was betting four government men on the proposition that nobody reads about drainage.

It seemed, that morning, like a good bet.

* * *

*Tomas*

Here is what nobody tells you about ambition: it looks exactly like loyalty from the inside. You tell yourself you're covering for her because she's your friend, because the workshop is yours too, because some clerk from the census bureau has no business pawing through Aldous Iversen's life's work. And all of that is true. All of it. It's also true that I noticed, three weeks ago, that the wetland chart had been moved six inches, and that Petra's lamp burned past midnight twice the week before, and that the sealed plate—the one the master forbade anyone to finish, the one with a wax stamp on its wrapper that Tomas Voss would have cut off his own thumb before breaking—was no longer in the locked drawer.

I hadn't opened the drawer. I want that recorded somewhere, in whatever ledger heaven keeps for apprentices. I had only looked at it, and looked at her, and done the arithmetic that ambitious people do without meaning to: *what does she know that I don't, and when does that stop being her advantage?*

So when Brann asked to see current commissions, I said, "I'll show you," before Petra could open her mouth, and I watched something flicker across her face—relief or suspicion, and honestly the two look nearly identical on her—and I led the clerks to the commission shelf like a man giving a tour of his own house.

"This is the harbor survey for the Customs House. This is a estate map for the Brandt family, orchards, very dull. This is—" I let my hand rest on the wetlands chart, casually, the way you'd rest a hand on a friend's shoulder, "—a drainage study nobody wants. Been sitting here since before I apprenticed."

One of the clerks reached for it.

The room went very quiet in the specific way rooms do when you realize you've just made a decision you can't take back. Because if he unrolled it and the letter was still there—if Petra hadn't moved it after all—then I had just handed a dead man's promise to the crown myself, and every noble thing I'd told myself about loyalty curdled into testimony.

The clerk touched the paper. Petra, behind him, had gone the color of unsized linen.

And then Brann said, "Leave it. Records first. We're not here for wallpaper."

*Petra*

He said it lightly. He said it like a man who genuinely did not care about a rolled chart of marsh grass, and I believed him, and then I stopped believing him, because a man like Brann does not survive in the census bureau by overlooking things. He overlooks things *on purpose*, and files the fact of the overlooking away for later, the way a moneylender files a name.

But I am getting ahead of the morning. Let me stay in it, in the smell of glue and the gray coats moving through the workshop like weather, while Tomas performed the best act of his life—better than he knew—by being exactly himself: helpful, eager, faintly boastful about his own penmanship. The clerks liked him immediately. People always liked Tomas. There is a kind of man who makes authority feel flattered simply by obeying it gracefully, and the bureau drank him in.

They went through the records room for two hours. I know because I sat at my desk pretending to ink a coastline and did not produce one single clean line the entire time. My hand knew what my face refused to admit: that somewhere in those ledgers were three years of entries in Master Aldous's handwriting, and among them, if Brann read closely, were supply orders for a quantity of fine vellum far exceeding any legitimate commission. The atlas. He'd bought materials for the atlas in plain sight, month after month, trusting to bureaucratic blindness.

Brann emerged holding exactly that ledger.

"Iversen ordered a great deal of paper," he said.

"He was a great deal of mapmaker," I said.

Tomas laughed. Out loud. A short, bright, appalled laugh, and then he covered it with a cough and said, "Forgive her, sir, she's been like this since the funeral. Grief comes out sideways in some people." And Brann looked at me for a long moment—not angry, not fooled, but *interested*, which is worse—and wrote something down.

I could have kissed him or killed him. I honestly don't know which impulse was stronger. Both felt roughly the same in the chest.

* * *

*Tomas*

Afterward I couldn't tell you whether I saved her or signed her death warrant with better handwriting. Both, probably. Ambition keeps two sets of books; it learned that from the merchants.

Brann's men found nothing, because there was nothing to find—that much I was sure of by noon, watching Petra's shoulders drop half an inch when the wetlands chart stayed rolled. But Brann himself lingered. He stood in the middle of the workshop with his hat back on, looking up at the framed maps on the walls the way other men look at portraits of generals, and he said, "Your master worked on something large at the end. An atlas, the licenses suggest."

"A trade atlas," Petra said. "Coastlines, harbors. Commerce, mostly."

"Commerce," Brann repeated, tasting the word. "Of course. And when it's finished?"

"It will be sold," she said. "To merchants. For money. Which we will pay you taxes on, if that's what this is really about."

I winced. She has this gift, Petra—she can insult a man so precisely that he can't prove it happened. Brann's jaw tightened by a fraction, and then he smiled again, that ledger-keeper's smile, and said, "When it's finished, we'll want first sight of it. New regulations. Cartographic materials touching coastal defense require review." He let the words sit there. *Coastal defense.* As if a map of fishing depths were a cannon.

"The atlas contains no defenses," Petra said. "Unless you count sandbars."

"Sandbars have defended Vellmark longer than its army has." And he tipped his hat and left, taking his clerks and the younger significant man with him, and the door closed, and the workshop exhaled.

Petra sat down at her desk. Her hand, when she picked up the pen, was steady. I noticed that. I notice everything; it's my curse and my intended career. Steady hands, steady voice, a woman who had lied to a government official before breakfast and gone back to inking a coastline as if the morning had been nothing but weather.

"You coughed well," she said, without looking up.

"Grief comes out sideways," I said. "In some people."

That earned me half a smile. Half. I've been collecting halves from Petra Iversen for three years and I could paper a wall with them, and it occurred to me, standing there with my heart doing something complicated in my chest, that I had spent the entire inspection making choices, each one small, each one defensible, and that strung together they formed a shape I recognized with a cold little lurch: a debt. She owed me now. Not gratitude—Petra doesn't deal in gratitude, she deals in *obligation*, which is gratitude with interest—and someday, not today, I would come to collect.

I hated myself a little. Then I got out my pens, because the harbor survey wasn't going to draw itself, and self-hatred, unlike ink, dries nothing.

* * *

*Petra*

That night, after Tomas left—after I listened to his boots fade down the stairs and waited another hour out of habit—I unlocked the drawer, took out the letter, and read it again. I had it nearly by heart now, but reading it is different from remembering it. Remembering is mine. Reading is his voice, and I wanted his voice, because the day had taught me something I didn't want to learn: that hiding the atlas was no longer enough. Brann hadn't come for the plate. He'd come to establish that the atlas existed, that it was nearly finished, and that he would see it the moment it was done. He had gotten everything he wanted except the thing he didn't yet know to ask for.

*A promise made in peacetime holds in wartime, or it was never a promise.* Old Lord Halvard had said that once, in a drawing room full of candles, seven years and another life ago. I had been a servant girl standing by a door, memorizing it without knowing why.

The letter named Elias. Of course it named Elias—it was written to be delivered to him, or rather to be delivered *about* him, Aldous being the sort of man who wrote instructions instead of letters. *To my successor, whoever holds this work when I cannot:* the letter began, which meant it was addressed to me and Aldous had known it, the sly old ghost. *Deliver the finished atlas to Elias Halvard. Not to his father, not to his agents, to Elias. He will understand why the last plate must remain unfinished until he sees it.*

Except I had finished the last plate. Three years alone with a dead man's instructions and a forbidden coastline, and I had inked it, and now the single most dangerous piece of paper in Vellmark lay sewn—no. Not yet sewn. Lying flat, wrapped in oilcloth, beneath the floorboards under my cot, where I had moved it two nights ago at the cost of a splinter I still felt when I gripped a pen.

The workshop had passed inspection. The atlas had passed inspection. And I stood in the lamplight holding a dead man's handwriting, understanding with perfect clarity that passing inspection was worth nothing—that Brann would be back, and next time he would bring more than clerks—and that the only person between a promise and the burning of it was a liar who had gotten very good at lying, and a friend who might be one too.

Across the room, Tomas's stool sat empty. On it, folded, was a note in his tidy hand: *You owe me an explanation. No hurry. —T.*

No hurry. From Tomas, who hurried at everything, who measured his life in deadlines met ahead of schedule. No hurry meant *forever*. It meant he intended to hold it over me indefinitely, gently, the way you hold a door open for someone you're also following.

I put the letter back in the wetlands chart. I blew out the lamp. And I began, in the dark, to do arithmetic I'd been avoiding since Brann first walked in with his crossed-out crest: how much Maren charges, how much I have, and how many days Vellmark will let me keep both.
