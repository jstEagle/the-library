## 8. Letters to a Ghost

There is a particular kind of arithmetic that no mapmaker teaches, though we practice it daily: the conversion of everything you own into what you need. I had been doing sums like that for three years without calling them anything so dramatic. The cost of lamp oil against the cost of bread. The price of a second coat against the shame of wearing the first one shiny at the elbows. I could calculate the distance from Vellmark to the southern border to within a hair's width of the plate, but I could not tell you, that morning, how much money I had left in the tin behind the third drawer, because I was afraid to count it.

The letter sat on my worktable under a paperweight shaped like a compass rose. It had taken me two days and eleven drafts to write four sentences.

*To E.H. — The atlas is finished. Your father's promise holds. Tell me where.*

Eleven drafts. You would think a woman who inks coastlines for a living could manage a coastline of her own without drowning in it. But every draft betrayed me differently. One sounded like a servant begging. One sounded like a blackmailer. One — my favorite failure — sounded so cautious it could have been read aloud in front of Brann himself and he'd have yawned. In the end I kept the shortest one, because the truth, when you finally stop decorating it, tends to be brief.

Four sentences addressed to a man who might be dead. That's what nobody tells you about writing to exiles. Elias Halvard had not been seen in Vellmark since he was nineteen years old, since the night soldiers carried his father out through the harbor gates and the family name became something you whispered rather than said. Seven years is long enough for a boy to become a stranger. It is also, I reminded myself as I folded the paper into thirds, long enough for a boy to become a corpse. The census bureau would prefer the latter version. It made their paperwork simpler.

I needed Maren.

* * *

Maren Skov ran fish carts out of the Saltmarket before dawn and ran other things out of the city whenever the other things paid better. Everyone knew this the way everyone knows which neighbors water their beer and which constables take their coffee with cream — as an ambient fact, unspoken, load-bearing. She was sixty if she was fifty, built like the prow of a working boat, with hands so cracked and salted they looked like they'd been mending nets since before the harbor had a name. She had sold Master Aldous charts from the outer islands for years. She used to bring us smoked herring at midwinter and call him "old ink-hands" until he threatened — every year, sincerely — to ban her from the shop.

He never did. He trusted her more than he trusted half the men who signed his ledgers. Which meant she was either the safest person in Vellmark or the most dangerous, and there was only one way to find out, and that way involved telling her things.

I went down to the Saltmarket at the gray hour when the carts were loading, when the gulls were arguing over herring heads and the whole quarter smelled of brine and ice and honest labor. Honest labor has a smell; dishonest labor smells mostly the same, which is why it thrives.

Maren saw me before I saw her. Of course she did. She'd spent forty years spotting customs officers in a crowd; one apprentice in a good coat pretending to browse was hardly sport.

"Map-shop girl." She didn't look up from the crate she was nailing shut. "You're far from your pens."

"I came to buy fish."

"No, you didn't. You've got ink on your right middle finger and none anywhere else. A fishwife has ink nowhere or everywhere." She drove the last nail home and straightened, cracking her back like a ship changing tack. "What do you want, Petra Iversen?"

My name. Not the workshop name, not *the master's girl*, but the name I'd buried three years ago in a pauper's grave alongside a man everyone believed had died alone. I felt it land somewhere between my shoulder blades.

"I don't know what you mean," I said. "My name is—"

"Girl." She wiped her hands on her apron and finally looked at me, and her eyes were the flat gray of open water, the kind that doesn't care what you throw into it. "I carried your master's parcels to places the crown doesn't have names for. I know whose kitchen you learned to curtsy in. I've known since the week you turned up in that shop with a servant's walk and a scholar's hands and thought nobody would notice." She shrugged. "Nobody noticed. Nobody looks at anything anymore. That's why maps matter. So. What do you want?"

I had rehearsed this too. I want to send a letter, I'd practiced, casual as ordering tea. What came out instead was quieter and worse.

"I need something taken out of the city. A letter. To a person who may not be reachable."

"Reachable," Maren repeated, tasting the word. "That's a careful way to say it."

"I'm a careful person."

"You're a terrified person who's learned to fold herself small. There's a difference, and the difference gets people drowned." She jerked her chin toward the alley behind the carts. "Walk with me. People watch mouths in the market. They can't watch feet."

* * *

We walked the long way along the sea wall, past the drying racks, while she told me nothing and I told her less, and somehow by the end of it she knew everything anyway. That is either a gift or a trade secret, and I suspect Maren would insist it's the latter.

"So," she said, when we reached the stretch of wall where the stones were green with old weed and the gulls gave up on dignity entirely. "You want a letter run to Elias Halvard. The son of the man whose name got your master killed."

"Aldous wasn't killed for the name. He was killed for the atlas."

"He was killed for being stubborn about paper," she said. "Which in my experience is the most fatal kind of stubbornness. Ink dries faster than blood and lasts longer, and the crown knows it." She leaned on the wall, looking out at the gray line where the harbor ended and the rest of the world began. "You know what happens to letters, girl? Letters get opened. Letters get read by men with soft hands and hard questions. And then the person who wrote the letter, and the person who carried it, and the person who received it all get to share a cell and compare handwriting."

"I'm not asking you to carry it yourself. Just put me on a path to him. Or him on a path to me."

"And if 'him' is a bureau informant now? Seven years is a long time. Hunger does things to loyalty."

"Then I'll have written to a ghost, and no harm done."

"No harm done," Maren said, with a laugh that had no humor in it whatsoever, like a bell rung in an empty church. "You sound like your master. He used to say that exact thing, standing exactly where you're standing, about exactly this family. Two weeks later the soldiers came." She turned and looked at me fully then, and for a moment the flat gray went somewhere deeper. "I liked Aldous. He paid fair and he never asked me to lie, only to be discreet, and there's a difference a woman can live with. So I'll hear your offer. Not agree. Hear."

Here is the humiliating part. I had prepared it in advance, standing in front of the cracked mirror above the workshop washbasin, and even in rehearsal it had made me flinch.

"I have thirty-one marks saved. I'll give you twenty."

Maren waited. She was very good at waiting; I imagine the sea taught her.

"That's the offer," I said.

"For a letter to a traitor's son, through waters the revenue cutters sweep twice a day." She shook her head slowly, almost sadly. "Twenty marks buys you a letter thrown off the pier with a rock tied to it. Same result, faster."

"It's everything I have except eleven marks."

"I know. I did the same sums when I was your age. Everyone does them eventually — what am I worth, what will they pay, what can I live without. It's the ugliest mathematics there is, and the crown invented it." She pushed off from the wall. "So here's my counter-offer, and mind you take it or leave it, because I won't haggle with a frightened girl, it's indecent. Twenty marks now. The rest when the job's done — whatever the job turns out to be. Because it won't be a letter, in the end. Letters are just the first knot in the rope. If Halvard answers you at all, he'll answer with demands, and demands need carrying, and carrying needs me. So you're not paying me for a letter. You're hiring a pilot for a voyage you can't see the end of yet."

"And the rest of the fee?"

"When the voyage ends. Whatever it costs." She held out a hand rough as barnacle crust. "Or leave it, and go back to your coastlines, and pray the census men stay stupid. That's an option too. It's the one most people take, and most of them die comfortable."

I put the coins in her palm. Twenty marks. Three years of skipped meals and patched gloves and telling myself next month, next month. They made very little sound for the weight of them.

"You should know," I said, as she weighed them without counting — counting is for amateurs, apparently — "if this goes wrong, I'll never say your name. Whatever they do to me."

"Oh, I know you won't." She tucked the coins away somewhere in the architecture of her clothing. "Not because you're loyal. Because you'll be too busy protecting your own neck, and rightly so. Loyalty's a luxury, girl. Discretion is a trade skill." She picked up her crate as though we'd been discussing herring all along. "Leave the letter at the third barrel on Pier Six, tonight after the lamps are lit. Wrapped in oilcloth, weighted, marked with a chalk cross on the underside. And Petra—" She paused, and for once the flat gray eyes warmed by perhaps one degree. "Your master used to write to the old lord, you know. Before the war. I carried those too. Half the time the letters said nothing at all. Just talk of tides and inks and the price of vellum. But you could feel it underneath — two men keeping a promise alive by feeding it paper. Paper keeps better than promises do. Remember that."

She walked back toward her carts, and I stood at the sea wall doing arithmetic again, the uglier kind: what it costs to keep a promise alive when the men who made it are both dead, and whether I had anything left in me that wasn't already spoken for.

* * *

I left the letter at the third barrel on Pier Six that night, after the lamps were lit, exactly as instructed. Oilcloth, weighted, chalk cross on the underside. My hands were steady. I remember noticing that with some surprise — after everything, the fear hadn't gotten into my hands yet. It lived elsewhere. In the tin behind the third drawer, now holding eleven marks and a receipt for everything else I owed. In the space where twenty marks' worth of future had been.

Walking home through the dark streets, I caught myself doing what I always did when the sums turned ugly: reading the city. The lamplighter's route, the constable's patrol interval, the sightlines from the bureau windows. Master Aldous taught me that a mapmaker sees a city the way a physician sees a body — not as streets and buildings but as circulation, pressure, points where the whole system can fail. I had never needed the skill for survival before. Now it was becoming the only thing I owned outright.

The workshop was cold when I let myself in. I lit the lamp at the worktable and looked, as I always looked, at the atlas — the finished coastline, the forbidden plate hidden in the wetland chart, the letter's twin sitting in my memory in Maren's keeping now, headed for wherever ghosts receive their mail.

Three days, Maren had said. Fish carts go out, fish carts come back, and sometimes things come back that aren't fish. Give it three days, and if nothing comes, give it three more, and if nothing comes after that, girl, then either your ghost is dead or he's smarter than both of us.

On the morning of the fourth day, I found a scrap of paper wedged under the workshop door, written in a cramped hand I almost didn't recognize, on paper cheap enough to burn safely.

It was not from Elias.

It was unsigned, and it said only: *They asked Tomas about you today. He answered well. Too well. Be careful who covers for you.*

And beneath that, in different ink, added later, smaller:

*The wetland chart is missing from the inspection inventory. Someone counted.*
