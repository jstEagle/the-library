## 9. Tomas's Price

There is an art to watching someone without their knowing, and I flatter myself that I learned it from the best. Master Aldous used to say a mapmaker's first tool was not the pen but the eye—that any fool could draw a coastline, but only a patient man could see where it *wanted* to go. I have been patient with Petra Iversen for three years. Tonight, patience pays its dividend.

She thinks I sleep like a stone in the loft above the drying racks. She thinks my coughing fit at supper was about the smoked herring. She thinks, in short, that Tomas Vey is exactly what he appears: a second apprentice of modest gifts and modest ambitions, good with the guillotine, better with gossip, harmless as damp paper.

Let her think it a while longer.

* * *

I should begin where the trouble began, which is not tonight but eleven nights ago, when I came back for the letter knife I'd left on the cutting bench and found the workshop dark in a way that workshops never are.

A workshop at night has sounds. The cooling stove ticking. The stacks settling as the damp leaves them. Old Aldous's cat—may he rest, the cat, not the master, though both went within a season—used to patrol the aisles like a small furry inspector. But there was also light, that night, a thin gold line under the door of the plate room, and a sound I knew intimately because I'd heard it every working day since I was twelve: the whisper of a burnisher on copper.

Someone was finishing a plate after hours.

Now, apprentices sneak into the workshop for all manner of reasons—to practice, to pilfer scraps of vellum, once, memorably, to court the chandler's daughter among the rolled charts, which taught me more about the acoustics of this building than any lesson did. So I did what any sensible colleague would do. I put my eye to the keyhole.

Petra sat at the master's own desk, the one nobody uses because sitting in it feels like trespassing on a grave. Before her lay the sealed plate. Not just any plate—the plate. The ninth, the unfinished one, the one Master Aldous locked away years ago and forbade by name, in front of both of us, with the particular gravity he reserved for matters of state rather than craft. He'd said it was flawed. He'd said the survey data was unreliable. He had said, in his dry way, that a mapmaker who drew the northern coast wrong would answer for it to God and to the Admiralty, and he preferred to disappoint neither.

And here was Petra, three years his junior in everything but grief, burnishing that coastline into being like she'd been born knowing it.

I watched until my knees ached. I watched her dip the pen, hold her breath over a curve of shore, and—this is the detail I keep returning to, the grain of sand in my boot—I watched her *not copy*. A copier checks the source every third stroke. Petra worked from memory, or from something worse than memory. Her hand moved the way the old man's hand moved. I'd spent six years watching that hand; you don't forget its grammar.

When she finished and wrapped the plate in felt and returned it to its drawer—or what I believed was its drawer—I went back up to the loft and lay awake doing arithmetic.

Not the sentimental kind. The useful kind.

* * *

Here is what people misunderstand about ambition. They think it's loud. They think the ambitious man is the one who elbows to the front, who claims credit at the guild dinner, who signs his name large. Nonsense. The loudest men in this city are the ones whose ambition died young and doesn't know it yet. Real ambition is quiet. It bides. It learns the whole shape of a thing before it cuts.

I learned it from watching Master Aldous lose. There—someone ought to say it plainly, so I will: the great Aldous Iversen, whose coastlines hang in the Admiralty and whose atlas was going to be *the* atlas of our age, lost. He spent his last decade drawing beautiful truths for a family that fell, and when the Halvards were broken and banished, all that beauty became contraband. He died with his masterpiece three plates short and his name quietly struck from two royal commissions. I stood at his funeral between the guild men who spoke of him in past-tense honorifics, and I thought: *he had the talent and wasted it on loyalty.* Loyalty is a luxury good. It looks splendid in a shop window and starves you slowly in your own house.

Petra, apparently, disagrees. Petra has spent three years keeping a dead man's promise alive in secret, inking treason one coastline at a time, and she does it for free. For *love*, if you can credit it. That's the part I find genuinely difficult—not the danger of what she's doing, but the economics of it. She's spending the finest hand in this workshop on a debt no creditor will ever acknowledge.

Unless, of course, someone acknowledges it. Unless someone makes sure that when the atlas finally surfaces—and things that fine always surface, they bob up like drowned men—the name on it isn't just the dead master's and a ghost's.

Mine.

* * *

Tonight I catch her properly. No keyholes, no pretense. She works late on a commission chart—harmless, legitimate, the estuary soundings for a grain merchant—and I come down the stairs at the hour when the stove has gone grey and the lamplight makes us both look older than we are. I've rehearsed this. Not the words, precisely, but the temperature of them. Warm. Wounded, even. A friend arriving with bad news gently.

"You missed supper," she says without looking up. "The herring fought back."

"Everything fights back in this city lately." I lean against the bench. Casual as a Tuesday. "You know what I saw today? Brann again. The census man."

That gets her. Just a flicker—her pen pauses mid-hatch, a half-second hitch in the rhythm, then resumes. Three years ago that pause would have lasted longer. She's gotten good. Good frightens me more than clumsy would.

"He's thorough," she says. "It's his whole personality."

"He asked about *you* this time." I let that sit. "Prior households. Where apprentices served before their articles. Very strange questions for a census. Ours counts chimneys and loaves, not biographies." I scratch my ear, a man mildly puzzled. "I told him you came up from the provinces. Farm family. Father a cooper. You learn to lie fast around here or you learn to leave."

Now she does look up. Her face does something complicated and settles on gratitude, and I want to shake her, because that's the tragedy of Petra Iversen in one expression: she receives treachery as kindness and files it under debts owed.

"Thank you," she says. "Cooper's daughter. I'll remember my father's trade."

"Oh, I'm sure you will," I say warmly, and watch the words go past her like a ship she didn't notice.

We talk a while—about the merchant's chart, about the price of lamp oil, about nothing—and every sentence I spend is a coin laid on a table. When she finally packs up her pens and heads for the stairs, I say her name, and she stops, and I feel the whole evening pivot on its axle.

"The plate drawer," I say. "The one with the false bottom. Under the ninth plate's slot. You keep it well oiled. Barely a squeak."

* * *

I want to record, for honesty's sake, that some part of me hated saying it.

Not conscience—let's be precise with ourselves; I checked, thoroughly, and found none operating—but something adjacent. Something that remembers Petra teaching me the wetland hatching pattern my first year because Master Aldous was too impatient to demonstrate twice. Something that remembers her splitting the last apple in the winter of the bread riots when we were both hungry enough to consider the cat. Friendship, or its cheap provincial cousin. It exists in me somewhere, the way a splinter exists: small, embedded, occasionally throbbing, and not worth an operation to remove.

But here is the ledger, and I keep honest books even when their contents shame me.

If the census bureau finds that plate first—and they are circling closer than Petra knows, Brann is not a man who asks questions he doesn't already suspect answers to—then the atlas is seized, the workshop is closed, and Tomas Vey goes back to being a jobbing chart-copier until his eyes give out. If Petra runs, and she's close to running, anyone can smell it on her—then everything she built vanishes with her, and I inherit dust. In every scenario where I do nothing, I lose.

In the scenario where I speak now, while there's still time to negotiate, I don't have to lose.

So when she turns, and the color leaves her face in that slow tide way it does—she never flushes, Petra, she *drains*, which took me years to learn to read—I make my voice soft. Softer than it's been in years.

"I'm not going to report you," I tell her. "Sit down. Please. This goes easier sitting."

She doesn't sit. Fine. Standing it is.

"How long?" she says. Her voice is level. Impressive, really. I'd have stuttered.

"Eleven days. Since you started the northern coast. And before you ask—no one else knows. Not yet. That's rather the point of this conversation."

"You watched through—" 

"The keyhole, yes, the first night. After that I merely noticed things. You're careful, but you use the master's stroke order, and nobody alive uses his stroke order except you, and you only do it when you think you're alone. Also you've been buying extra lamp oil out of your own wages and telling the oil merchant it's for cleaning. Petra. I grew up poor in a house full of brothers. I know what hidden expenditures look like."

Something moves across her face—not fear now. Something colder. Appraisal. She's reclassifying me in real time, the way you reclassify a dog when it shows teeth you hadn't counted. I let her look. Better she sees me clearly now than discovers me later.

"What do you want, Tomas?"

Straight to it. Good. Sentiment would insult us both.

"Three things," I say, holding up fingers like a schoolmaster, because a little theatre steadies the room. "First: silence. My silence, ongoing, permanent, free of charge—which, believe me, given what the census bureau pays informants these days, is the expensive item on this list. Second: the atlas carries my name. Co-author. 'Aldous Iversen, Petra Iversen, and Tomas Vey.' Small type if you insist, but on the title page, not the acknowledgments."

Her jaw actually drops a fraction. "The atlas isn't mine to—"

"It's nobody's to anything," I cut in, gentle as arithmetic. "Your master is dead. His patron is attainted and exiled. The work exists in a legal void, which means it belongs to whoever finishes it. You finish it. I am therefore offering to attach myself to the only version of events in which it survives at all. Think of my name as ballast, Petra. A single author with your history is a target. Three authors is an institution. Institutions are harder to burn."

"And third?"

Ah. Third.

"The plate," I say. "For safekeeping."

"For safekeeping." She repeats it flatly, tasting the lie in it, because she's not stupid, whatever else she is.

"There's a copyist on Copper Lane I trust—trust in the limited sense that he owes me money—who can pull a duplicate in four days. You keep your original. I keep a proof. If anything happens—if the bureau comes, if you disappear, if the building burns—your work survives somewhere you can't burn down or bury. Call it insurance. Call it a hostage. Call it what it is: I'm not letting you be the single point of failure for six years of the best cartography this city has produced."

All true, incidentally. That's my favorite kind of lie, the one assembled entirely from load-bearing truths. The plate copy *would* preserve the work. It would also mean that from the moment the first stylus touched copper, the atlas existed in two places, and one of those places was inside my control. Petra hears the friendship in my proposal. What she should hear is the architecture.

"Tomas." She says my name the way you'd set down a heavy object. "Do you understand what that map is? Do you understand why he sealed it?"

"I understand it shows the northern approaches with fortifications marked that officially don't exist," I say, because I do, I've done my reading, the master talked in his sleep near the end. "I understand it's worth hanging to whoever holds it and worth more to whoever published it first. Which is precisely why it needs to exist in more than one pair of hands. You're treating it like a relic. I'm treating it like an edition. One of us is a mapmaker."

That lands. I watch it land. Her hands have curled at her sides and her breathing has gone shallow and quick, and beneath the anger I can see the terrible machinery of her mind grinding through my logic and finding, hatingly, that some of it holds.

"You'd sell it," she says quietly. "If the price came."

"Yes," I say, because lying to her now would break the only thing holding this conversation together, which is that I am the one honest villain she knows. "Probably. If the price came high enough. Here's what you get in exchange for my probably: I get you to the finish line alive. I get the atlas finished, printed, and named. I get three names instead of two. You get your dead master honored and your exile served and your map delivered to whoever you're so determined to deliver it to—yes, I know there's someone, don't insult us both. Everyone wins the amount they care about most."

"And what do you care about most?"

The lamp gutters between us. Somewhere outside, a drunk is singing something patriotic and badly.

"To matter," I say. "You inherited yours, Petra. Grief handed you a purpose the way a rich uncle hands over an estate. I had to build mine out of offcuts. Don't stand there judging the masonry."

* * *

She doesn't say yes.

She doesn't say no either. She stands there for a long moment with the lamplight carving her face into halves—one lit, one dark, which feels less like symbolism than like simple bad positioning of the lamp—and then she gathers her coat and her satchel and tells me she needs to think, and climbs the stairs, and her footsteps cross the loft overhead, deliberate, unhurried, a woman performing calm for an audience of one.

I stay below a long while. I bank the stove. I put the commission chart back in its rack. I touch the plate drawer's false bottom once, lightly, the way you touch a scar to confirm it still hurts.

Here is my confession, made to no one, in the empty workshop, where confessions cost least: I meant some of it kindly. The copy genuinely protects the work. The name genuinely shields her. If she's wise—and she is wise, she's the wisest person I know who keeps mistaking wisdom for loyalty—she'll take the bargain, and in five years we'll laugh about this over the printing proofs in some safer city, three names on a title page, immortal in small type.

And here is my other confession, made to the same empty room: if she refuses—if she decides her ghost of a promise is worth more than her living skin—then I will not be able to protect her from what happens next, because I will have already set it in motion. Brann left a card with me too, weeks ago. I told him nothing. But I kept the card. Men like me keep cards.

Upstairs, the loft floor creaks. Once. Twice. Then silence—the particular silence of someone moving quickly and quietly toward the back stairs, toward the plate room, toward the drawer with the oiled false bottom.

Going to check on it, I'd wager. Going to count her treasures the way misers count coins after a stranger admires them.

I should let her. I should go up to bed and let the night settle and trust that eleven days of patience bought me eleven more.

But my boots are already carrying me to the foot of the stairs, and my hand is already on the rail, and I am thinking—with the cold clarity that arrives, always, at exactly the wrong moment—that I never actually told her which night I first saw the light under the plate room door.

And she never asked.
