## 10. The Wetland Chart

There is a particular hour in a mapmaker's workshop when the light does nothing useful at all. Too late for work, too early for lamps, the grey seeping through the north windows like water through bad caulking. I had spent three years learning to love that hour, because it was the one time the workshop belonged entirely to me — no customers, no Tomas humming his insufferable little scales over his engraving tools, no ghost of Aldous Iversen standing at my shoulder in everything except actuality.

On the morning I went to retrieve the letter, the grey was thicker than usual, and I remember being grateful for it. Grateful. That is the detail that has since curdled every memory of that day: that I stood there thanking the weather for its discretion, like a conspirator, like a woman who had finally accepted what she was.

I had not slept. This will surprise no one who has ever kept a secret the size of a nation's disgrace under their floorboards, metaphorically or otherwise. Brann's card sat in my apron pocket where I had put it three days ago and could not stop touching, the way you touch a loose tooth. The Halvard crest, struck through with a single ink line. Not defaced, exactly. *Cancelled.* As though a family seven hundred years old were a subscription that had lapsed.

The letter was hidden in the wetland chart.

I want to defend the choice, even now. Everyone in Vellmark knows the wetland charts. They hang in the workshop's back racks by the dozen, rolled and tied with plain cord, depicting the Sedgeflats east of the river in exhaustive, soul-crushing detail: reed beds, eel weirs, drainage cuts, the seasonal flooding of the meres. No one had bought one in eleven years. Aldous used to say they were his pension against vanity — proof he could make something so accurate and so dull that God himself would not bother to steal it. The chart I'd chosen depicted the Mirefen parcels, all forty-one of them, and inside its roll, pasted flat against the paper with wheat starch, lay the letter I had found in Aldous's locked drawer. His promise. My inheritance. The peacetime oath that made me, as of three days ago, a criminal in waiting.

I took the chart down with hands that knew the motion and did it anyway too fast, the way you do anything your body understands is dangerous before your mind consents. Cord off. Roll open on the table. And then —

Paper crackles differently after it has been handled. I cannot explain this to anyone who hasn't spent their life with maps; it is like explaining color to the blind. A chart that has hung untouched for three years opens with a certain stiff reluctance, a complaint in the fibers. This one opened easily. Obediently. It had been opened recently, and more than once, and rolled again by someone whose hands were not mine.

Someone had read the letter.

* * *

I did not panic. I want that recorded, whatever else history decides about me. For perhaps ten seconds I stood absolutely still with the Mirefen parcels spread out before me like an accusation, and in those ten seconds I performed a complete audit of my life.

Tomas. The name arrived without ceremony, the way the worst truths do. Tomas, who had been watching me across the workshop for weeks with those pleasant brown eyes that missed nothing. Tomas, who had asked — gently, oh so gently — why I worked late, whether I slept at all, whether I ever dreamed about the atlas. Tomas, who two nights ago had lingered by the back racks "looking for a coastal survey" that I now understood he had never wanted.

And yet. Doubt is a luxury I could not afford, but doubt came anyway, wearing useful clothes. Because there was another possibility, wasn't there? Brann. A search conducted while I slept. A man with a warrant-adjacent manner and a government's patience. The letter described the plate — Aldous had named it, numbered it, given its dimensions and its hiding place in his own hand, because a promise meant to survive war must be written plainly enough for a servant girl to execute. If Brann had read it, I was already finished, and the only question left was whether I died quickly or administratively.

But if it was Tomas—

If it was Tomas, then the man who shared my meals and my master's memory and my three years of grief had opened my inheritance, read my doom, and rolled it back up and said nothing. And worse — worse — some traitorous corner of me noted that he hadn't taken it. He could have. He could have carried it straight to the census bureau and collected whatever they pay informants these days, probably in the same currency they pay everyone: permission to keep existing.

He hadn't taken it. He had *left* it. Which meant either mercy, or patience, or a trap still closing.

I refolded the letter along Aldous's original creases — his creases, worn soft as cloth — and I made my decision the way Aldous taught me to make maps: not from the pretty parts, from the margins. What did the margins say? That the hiding place was blown. That whoever read it knew about the letter, and therefore possibly about the plate, and therefore about everything. That the workshop, which had been my fortress and my tomb for three years, was now the most dangerous room in Vellmark.

The plate itself was safe. That much I confirmed with trembling efficiency — the loose panel beneath the floorboards behind Aldous's old desk, the copper plate wrapped in oilcloth, cool and heavy and real as sin. Plate Nine. The coastline the crown had forbidden to exist. The true map of the Halvard harbors, drawn before the exile, annotated in Lord Halvard's own hand, evidence that the family had committed no treason except the crime of owning things worth stealing. Aldous had died rather than surrender it. Three years I had guarded a secret I didn't fully understand until the letter explained it.

And now the letter had been read, and the clock that had been ticking quietly in my chest for three years began to hammer.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about running: it requires no decision. Deciding takes time, and time was a currency I had stopped possessing somewhere around the moment my fingers registered that wrongness in the paper. I sewed the plate into my coat.

Not figuratively. Actually sewed it. There is a needle and waxed thread in every mapmaker's kit — we bind our own field books — and I have steady hands, praise be to Aldous's brutal apprenticeship, though that morning they shook enough that the stitches along my left hem look, even now, like the work of a drunk spider. Nine stitches to the coat seam, plate flat against my ribs, cold copper warming slowly to my skin until I could no longer tell where the metal ended and I began. Good. Let it be part of me. Everything else was already being taken.

I packed nothing else. This sounds heroic and was actually arithmetic: anything I carried would have to be explained, and explanation is just confession with better posture. I took the letter. I took Aldous's small field compass, which was worthless for escape and priceless to me. And at the last moment, in an act of sentiment I still haven't forgiven myself for either way, I took the atlas — the great unfinished thing, three years of my life bound in boards — because I could not leave it lying open on the bench like a corpse for whoever came next.

Then I stood in the middle of the workshop and looked at it. The smell of ink and size and the particular dust that lives in the grain of old drawing tables. Tomas's stool pushed in neatly at his station. Aldous's chair, which I had never once sat in, because some thrones you don't usurp even when they're empty. I said goodbye to none of it, because saying goodbye felt like an invitation for the room to remember me.

I went out through the delivery door into Copperlane, pulling my hood up, walking at the pace of a woman with an errand. Not fast. Fast is a flag. Slow is a shroud. Errand-pace, that forgotten gait between them, the walk of someone utterly unremarkable, and I had practiced it for three years without knowing I was rehearsing for this.

* * *

The square was busy the way mornings are busy — carts, fishwives, a knot of children tormenting a dog with more patience than sense. Ordinary noise. Ordinary light. I was halfway across, passing the fountain with its green-stained Neptune, when the feeling arrived.

You know the feeling. The one that walks up the back of your neck on feet made of ice. Someone is watching you, and not the way crowds watch — not the diffuse, accidental attention of strangers — but specifically, deliberately, the way a cat watches a bird it has decided not to catch *yet*.

I did not turn around.

This may be the hardest thing I have ever done, harder than the sewing, harder than the leaving. Every nerve in my body screamed to look, to confirm, to know — because knowing is a comfort even when the knowledge is terrible. But turning would confirm it for him too. A face checked over a shoulder is a signature on a confession. So I walked, errand-paced, hood up, plate against my ribs like a second heart, and I let the watching happen, and I catalogued what I could see without turning: in the long window of the chandler's shop across the square, the reflected street, and in the reflected street, near the fountain, a figure in a bureau-grey coat who was not moving and not buying anything and not pretending very well to be anything other than exactly what he was.

Brann.

Of course. Of course it was Brann, and not because he had caught me — he hadn't, not yet — but because Brann understood something I was only now learning: that you don't need to seize a person to trap them. You need only let them see the edges of the net. He stood there in my peripheral world like a period at the end of a sentence, and the sentence was this: *We know. We are letting you run. Run.*

Or — and this thought arrived with its own special horror — or it was not Brann at all. Grey coats are common. Paranoia is more common. Perhaps it was a man waiting for his wife. Perhaps I had finally cracked, three years of secrecy doing what siege engines could not.

It didn't matter. That was the lesson of the morning, delivered free of charge: it no longer mattered whether I was hunted or merely haunted, because both required the same response.

I turned down Tanner's Row, away from the fish market, away from Maren's route — no, wait, toward it, I corrected myself, then corrected myself again, then stopped correcting myself because course-correcting visibly in the street is its own confession. I walked. I let my feet carry me south while my mind ran ahead, mapping options the way I mapped coastlines: here be certainty, here be void.

Maren. The smuggler who ran charts out with the fish carts, who had named a price I couldn't pay and looked at me with eyes that priced everything, including me. She was a risk. She was also the only door in Vellmark that opened outward.

Tomas. Who might have read the letter, might have sold me, might be at this moment sitting in Brann's office trading my name for his apprenticeship papers. Or might be innocent, worried, wondering where I'd gone with the day's work undone.

Aldous. Dead three years, and still the steadiest counsel I had. What would he say? He would say what he always said when I panicked over a misprinted plate: *The map is not the territory, girl. Stop staring at your errors and look at the ground.* The ground. The actual ground. Where was I actually standing? On Tanner's Row with a forbidden copper plate sewn into my coat and a dead man's promise in my pocket, moving through a city that had begun, quietly, to close.

I cut east at the cooper's yard, doubling back the way Aldous had taught me to think — never in straight lines, straight lines are how authorities draw their thinking — and looped toward the river district with the wetlands smell of the Sedgeflats faint even here, salt and rot and rope. Behind me, I could not see him anymore. Ahead of me, I could see nothing at all, which was somehow worse.

At the mouth of Rope Walk I permitted myself one glance back, one, the way you permit yourself one taste of a poison to gauge its strength. The street behind was empty. The square beyond was out of sight. Whether Brann followed, whether he had never moved from his post by the fountain, whether he had already gone to the workshop to find it stripped and the plate gone — none of it visible, none of it knowable, and I understood with sudden clarity that this was the new shape of my life: a woman navigating by instruments she could no longer trust, through waters that had been resurveyed overnight, every landmark shifted, every depth suspect.

So I did the only thing a mapmaker can do when the map fails. I started making a new one, in ink that wouldn't dry, on paper that hadn't been made yet, beginning with the only fixed point I had left.

Maren. Fish carts. The price I couldn't pay.

I turned south toward the docks, plate cold against my ribs again as the wind came off the water, and I walked faster now — not errand-pace, not slow-as-a-shroud, but the pace of a woman who has finally chosen her direction and prays to whatever god oversees runaway apprentices that she has chosen it before the city chose for her.
