## 11. Maren's Net

There are three kinds of people who come to Maren Skov after dark. The ones who want to buy something, the ones who want to sell something, and the ones who have nowhere else in the world to stand.

The girl on my doorstep that night was the third kind, which meant she was also the second kind, whether she knew it or not. Everyone who has nowhere else to stand eventually has something to trade. I've made a comfortable living off that arithmetic for twenty years, and I've buried four husbands, two boats, and one official reputation doing it.

She was soaked through, salt-stained at the hem, and carrying herself like a woman transporting a sleeping infant she couldn't set down. I know that posture. It's the posture of someone with contraband sewn into her coat.

"Petra," I said. "Iversen."

Her face did something interesting — a flicker, quickly smothered, of *how does this fishwife know my name* — and then smoothed itself flat the way apprentices learn to do when masters are watching. Good training. Bad instinct, around me.

"Maren." She glanced past my shoulder into the dark of the house. "I was told—"

"You were told I run charts out with the fish carts. You were told correctly. Come in before the street remembers you standing here."

* * *

My house smells the way it has always smelled: herring, tar, lamp oil, and the particular sourness of money hidden in damp places. I put her by the stove because she was shivering, and I put soup in front of her because a fed witness is a calm witness, and I sat down across from her and let the silence do its work.

Silence is the best tool I own. Cheaper than bribery, more reliable than threats. Most people can't bear three minutes of it. They'll fill it with anything — confessions, prices, names.

Petra Iversen lasted eleven minutes. That told me something about her too.

"I need a chart sent out," she said finally. "To Elias Halvard."

I didn't blink. Blinking is for amateurs. "The exile."

"The exile," she agreed.

"The traitor."

"That's what they're calling him."

"That's what the crown is calling him. The distinction matters if you plan to keep your head." I ladled myself some soup, mostly for something to do with my hands while I looked at her. "You understand what you're asking. Halvard correspondence isn't charts anymore, girl. The moment his name goes on anything leaving this harbor, it stops being cargo and becomes evidence. Evidence gets weighed differently. Evidence gets people hanged."

"I'm prepared for that."

"People always say that." I blew on my spoon. "They say it in exactly that tone, too. Steady, rehearsed, a little insulted that anyone would doubt them. Then the soldiers come and they weep and they offer up everyone they've ever shared bread with. I've seen it eleven times. I count."

Something moved behind her eyes — not fear. Calculation. She was deciding how much of herself to show me, same as I was deciding about her, and neither of us pretending otherwise. I liked her better for it. I like honest liars. They're the only company worth keeping in Vellmark.

"What's your price?" she asked.

"There it is." I smiled at her. "See? Eleven minutes and already talking like a smuggler. Your master would be proud or horrified, depending on which stories are true."

"You knew Aldous Iversen."

It wasn't a question, but I answered it anyway. "I knew him. He bought passage once for a crate he swore contained surveying instruments. It contained surveying instruments. That's how much I trusted him — I checked anyway, and I've never checked anyone since without thinking of him." I set the spoon down. "He paid well and lied beautifully and never once brought trouble to my door. You, girl, are trouble walking upright. Brann's men were asking at the docks about a mapmaker's apprentice before you ever came to me. Which means either you led them here or you outran them here, and I haven't decided which I'd prefer."

"I didn't lead them."

"Everyone says that too."

* * *

I gave her a pallet in the back room among the runners — the fish-cart folk, six women and a boy named Anders who could drive a cart through a closed market faster than any man alive and had the fines to prove it. They took her in the way runners take in anyone: without questions, with an extra blanket, with a nod that said *you eat last until you earn better.*

That's the code of the carts. Nobody asks where you slept before. Everybody watches where you sleep now.

For three days she worked like she'd been born among us. Gutting fish at dawn, mending nets with hands that were clearly built for finer things but learned fast, hauling brine barrels up from the quay without one word of complaint. Anders took to her immediately, which surprised me — Anders takes to no one — and by the second evening he was teaching her the harbor signals, the little arrangements of lanterns and crates that tell a runner which pier is watched and which is clean.

She was earning her place. That was the problem.

Because here's what I know about Petra Iversen, or whatever her true name is — and oh yes, I knew there was a truer name; Aldous Iversen did not hire household servants out of charity, and nobody hides inside an apprenticeship for three years unless they're hiding from something with teeth. Here's what I know: she came to me needing Elias Halvard found. And everything she did afterward — every barrel hauled, every net mended, every hour of honest sweat — was payment toward that need. Not kindness. Arithmetic.

Which is fine. I respect arithmetic. I've lived my whole life on it.

But a person running on arithmetic will hand you over the moment the sums change. And I needed to know, before I committed my carts and my runners and my neck to her sums, exactly what she was carrying and exactly who else wanted it.

So on the fourth night I sat down across from her again, with the lamp turned low, and I stopped being kind.

"Time to talk plainly," I said. "What's in the coat?"

Her hands went still over the net she was mending. Just for a heartbeat. Then they resumed, steady as tide. "Why does it matter?"

"Because everything matters, girl, that's the whole disease of this city right now. Because Brann doesn't chase apprentices. Census men don't burn archives over schoolgirl maps. Whatever you're carrying is worth enough that the government is willing to look ridiculous pursuing it, and I don't ferry things worth looking ridiculous over unless I know precisely what I'm ferrying." I leaned forward. "And because if they take you on my pier, they take my route, my runners, and my house. Your secret isn't yours anymore. It's mine too. I should know what I own."

She looked at me for a long moment. I watched her weigh it — not whether to trust me, she was too smart for that, but how much truth a woman like me could carry without dropping it.

"A map," she said at last. "One plate. The final plate of Master Iversen's atlas. The one showing—" she hesitated, and I saw her decide against the lie mid-breath, "—the coastal fortifications as they actually stand, with the survey errors the Admiralty has been publishing deliberately for six years."

Well. There it was.

I sat back. My heart was doing something unprofessional, so I ignored it, the way I ignore most of my body's opinions.

"They burned the Halvard archive last week," I said. "Publicly. Ash in the fountain square. And the atlas is next on their list, and you're sitting in my kitchen with the one piece of it that could cost them a war or win them one." I laughed — I couldn't help it. "Aldous Iversen, you magnificent dead fool. You hid the truth of the coast in your apprentice's coat."

"It was his promise," Petra said quietly. "Not his hiding place. He made a promise in peacetime, before all of this, and I'm the only one left alive who can keep it."

"And promises are heavier than plates," I said. "Yes. I remember being young and certain. It passes."

"Will you send it?"

"I'll send it." I held up one finger before hope could ruin her face. "On terms. First — I don't carry the plate itself yet. Too hot. We send word first, find where Halvard can receive, establish the meeting on our schedule, not his. Second — my price just went up. Passage for the plate is one thing. Passage for a national embarrassment is another entirely."

"How much?"

"Not coin." I studied her across the lamplight, and I gave her the only honesty I give anyone. "Information. You'll tell me, in time, everything — who helped you, who betrayed you, what Brann offered you and what you refused. Not tonight. But before the plate leaves this house, I'll know the whole shape of it. That's my price. Truth, delivered in installments, at a rate I set."

"That's not a price," she said slowly. "That's ownership."

"Now you understand commerce." I stood and gathered the bowls. "Welcome to the fish carts, girl. Everyone here sells something. The runners sell speed. Anders sells nerve. I sell certainty — other people's certainty, mostly, the knowledge of who's watching and who isn't. And you?" I carried the bowls to the basin and spoke with my back to her, because some truths go down easier that way. "You sell your secrets. Slowly, if you're lucky. Quickly, if you're not."

Behind me, the long pause of a woman doing sums she doesn't like.

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you walk out that door with your plate and your promise and Brann waiting somewhere in the dark, and I wish you luck with genuine feeling, because I liked Aldous and I see him all over you." I turned around. "But you won't refuse. Because you're not really choosing between me and freedom. You're choosing between me and alone. And alone, girl, is the one thing in Vellmark that costs more than anything I charge."

* * *

She didn't answer that night. Smart girl. Never accept terms while your pride is still bleeding.

But the next morning I found her at the gutting table before dawn, working beside the runners, and when Anders passed her a lantern to practice the pier signals, she took it without hesitation. And at noon, when I came out to check the carts, she fell into step beside me and said, in a voice stripped of everything but practicality:

"The colleague who betrayed me. His name is Tomas. He wants co-authorship credit on the atlas and the plate itself, and he sold nothing to Brann yet that I know of — but he knows where I hid the letter, which means he knows the letter exists, which means the bureau may already have read it. If they've read it, they know about the promise. If they know about the promise, they're not hunting the plate anymore." Her jaw tightened. "They're hunting Elias. Using me as the line to reel him in."

I stopped walking.

Because that, of course, was the thought I'd been circling for four days and refusing to name: that the girl in my kitchen might be bait without knowing it, or worse, knowing it and coming anyway. That Brann — and I'd crossed pens with census men before; they're not fools, whatever their paperwork suggests — might not want the map at all. Maps can be redrawn. A living Halvard heir in custody, caught accepting treason from inside Vellmark — that's a trial. Trials make examples. Examples make quiet winters for governments.

Every instinct I own said *walk away.* Twenty years of survival, four dead husbands' worth of hard lessons, all of it screaming the same thing: when someone hands you a package the whole government wants, the package is not the cargo. The package is the hook.

But I looked at her — salt-cracked hands, ink stains still ghosting under her fingernails despite a week of fish work, that stubborn set of her shoulders that Aldous Iversen used to get right before he did something magnificent and unforgivable — and I thought: *she knows.* She's done the same sums I have. She's carrying the hook knowingly, because the alternative is letting the promise die, and she has decided her word weighs more than her life.

God preserve me from people like that. They're the only ones who change anything, and they're always somebody else's funeral.

"All right," I said. "Here's how it happens. Word goes north with Thursday's carts — Anders drives, he's clean, he's never been searched in nine years. We tell Halvard a pier, a night, a signal. And Petra—" I waited until she looked at me. "If this is a trap, even one you walked into blind, I will feed you to it myself to save my runners. Understand? Sentiment is for people who can afford it. I can't."

"I understand," she said. And the worst part, the part that kept me up that night listening to the gulls argue over the harbor, was that she clearly, calmly did.

* * *

Thursday came grey and smelling of rain that wouldn't commit. I watched Anders load the morning catch, watched him tuck the sealed oilskin packet beneath the false bottom of the brine crate with the bored expertise of a man hiding his ninth thousand thing, and I felt the old familiar sickness in my stomach that I always feel when cargo leaves my hands — the moment when certainty stops being mine and becomes the road's.

Petra stood beside me on the quay. She hadn't slept. Neither had I, but I've had practice looking like I have.

"He'll come," she said. Not hopeful. Certain. The way people are certain about ghosts.

"Exiles are careful," I said. "Careful men don't come to piers named in letters that passed through a city crawling with Brann's men."

"Then he'll send someone."

"Or he'll send nothing, and wait, and test us. Exiles test everything. Trust got burned out of them like rot out of timber." I squinted at the horizon. "Don't mistake caution for refusal, girl. Silence is often the sound of a man checking a rope before he hangs his weight on it."

She was quiet a while. The carts pulled away one by one, wheels grinding on wet cobbles, gulls screaming their eternal complaint overhead.

"Maren," she said finally. "When this is done — if it's done — what happens to us? To me? To the runners?"

"Nothing good," I said honestly. "Best case, you disappear south with new names and I retire richer than I deserve. Worst case—" I shrugged. "Worst case, we're all footnotes in a trial nobody's allowed to read."

"That's not comforting."

"Comfort's for the rich and the dead. I deal in the living." I started back toward the house, then paused. "Oh — one more thing. This morning, before dawn, a man asked at the fish market which carts ran north on Thursdays. Described himself as a customs clerk. Had soft hands and a census man's shoes."

I watched the blood leave her face, and I hated myself a little for enjoying it, and I hated the world a lot for making enjoyment necessary.

"So," I said. "Now we know they're reading our mail before we write it. Which means Thursday's packet is already in Brann's pocket, which means whatever pier we named, he'll be there first." I turned and looked at her fully, and I made sure she saw that I understood what I was about to say, and said it anyway.

"We need a new pier. A new night. And a message that reaches Halvard without touching any road Brann owns. Which means, girl, that you and I are about to do something stupid, expensive, and possibly fatal." I smiled, because smiling keeps the fear in its cage. "I haven't been fatal in years. Let's see if I remember how."

Behind us, at the mouth of the quay, a figure in a census man's shoes turned away unhurried, like a man who had heard exactly enough.
