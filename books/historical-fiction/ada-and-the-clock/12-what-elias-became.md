## 12. What Elias Became

The reply took eleven days.

I know because I counted them the way you count stitches in a wound—without meaning to, without any hope it helps, just to have something to do with your hands that isn't wringing them. Eleven days of hauling fish carts and rolling charts for Maren, of sleeping in the loft above her smokehouse where the smell got into everything, including, I'm fairly certain, my dreams. Eleven days of watching the harbor mouth and telling myself I wasn't watching it.

Maren finally tossed the packet at my chest on a Tuesday morning, the way she handed over everything: as if it were a herring of dubious freshness and she had better things to do than care whether I caught it.

"You didn't get this from me," she said, which was her way of saying good morning.

"I never get anything from you," I agreed. "You are a figment."

"A figment who charges rent." She jerked her chin toward the loft. "Read it up there. Not on my dock. If a man in a bureau coat comes asking, I've never seen your face, and I'll say so convincingly, because I practice."

She was joking. Mostly. With Maren there was always a sliver of ledger underneath the joke, and I'd learned not to ask which column it belonged to.

* * *

The packet was small. Oilcloth, tied with waxed cord, addressed in a hand I almost didn't recognize—and then recognized completely, the way you recognize a voice through a wall. Elias had written like this as a boy: letters pressed too hard into the paper, every downstroke dug in, as if he were trying to leave a mark on something softer than the world.

I sat on the edge of my cot with the oilcloth on my knees for longer than I want to admit. There is a particular cowardice in hesitating over good news. You begin to suspect that anything worth being afraid to open will turn out to be exactly what you feared.

Inside were two things: a strip of paper, and a pressed sprig of something dried and brown that crumbled a little when I touched it. Sea lavender. From the salt meadows below Halvard House, where the old lord used to send us servants on pointless errands in summer just so we could see it blooming. He called it "the poor man's garden." Elias used to pick it by the fistful and give it to anyone who looked tired.

He was eight the first time he gave some to me. I was eleven and carrying a basket heavier than myself, and he solemnly informed me that flowers made burdens lighter, which was nonsense, which was also somehow true.

The note read:

*To the person claiming to be Petra Iversen, apprentice to Aldous Iversen.*

Not *Dear Petra*. Not even *To Petra*. I sat with that for a moment and felt it land exactly where he'd aimed it.

*I am told there exists an atlas my father commissioned, finished in secret these three years past, and that a promise binds its delivery to me. I am told many things. Most of what I am told is bait, and the rest is bait that has learned to write letters.*

*So. Prove you are who you say. The Petra I knew was afraid of gulls, could not swim, once set fire to the stillroom trying to make ink from elderberries, and cried at Lord Aldous's funeral so hard that Cook thought she was ill. If you can answer these four truths, I will believe a letter. Believing a person is another matter, and costs more.*

*Do not send the atlas. Do not send word of where it is kept. Send nothing a bureau man would thank you for. Send only the answers, and one thing more: tell me why you waited three years.*

*E. H.*

*P.S. — The sea lavender came off the roof of a granary in Torvstad. It grows wild in the gutter-spouts there. I found that funny once. Perhaps you still will.*

I read it four times. Then I did the undignified thing and laughed until I had to press my sleeve against my mouth, alone in a smokehouse loft, shaking—the kind of laughter that lives next door to sobbing and borrows its furniture. Because the answers were easy. Every one of them was easy. I *had* been afraid of gulls—I still was, they were flying rats with opinions—and I still couldn't swim, and the stillroom fire was a stain on the flagstones that Cook had cursed about for years, and yes, I had cried at Master Aldous's funeral as though grief were a tap I couldn't shut, though I'd had very good reasons for crying that no one guessed at.

But the last question. *Tell me why you waited three years.*

That one had no easy answer, because the true answer was shameful, and shame is the one cargo no smuggler will carry for you.

* * *

Here is the truth, since I'm apparently keeping a record of truths now: I hadn't known about the promise for most of those three years. But I had suspected. A man doesn't seal a drawer with two locks and look at it the way Aldous looked at it unless something in it matters more than his life, and Aldous died looking at it. I had spent three years with the suspicion sitting in my chest like a swallowed stone, and I had done nothing. I had inked coastlines. I had swept floors. I had been *safe*, or the workshop version of safe, which is a candle burning behind shutters while the whole street goes dark.

Why hadn't I opened the drawer?

Because opening it meant the promise became mine. And promises to dead men are heavy in a way living ones aren't—you can't ask them to release you, can't renegotiate terms, can't even be sure they'd recognize the world the promise now lived in. Lord Halvard was dead. His house was confiscated. His son was a name spoken only in the lowered voice people use for plague ships. Delivering an atlas to Elias Halvard in 1847 wasn't a favor anymore; it was treason with excellent penmanship.

And I was nobody. That was the other truth under the stone. A servant girl turned mapmaker's ghost, whose entire survival strategy for three years had been *be unremarkable*. Every plan I owned ended in staying small. The promise required me to become enormous—to become visible, traceable, catchable—in a city that had been teaching me daily the price of being seen.

So I waited. I told myself I was finishing the work first, honoring the craft before the vow, and some of that was even true. Aldous had spent his last years on that coastline; you don't abandon a dying man's masterpiece half-inked. But craft was the polite excuse. Fear was the honest one. I had let a dead man's promise sit locked in a drawer while I perfected the art of looking busy.

And now Elias—who had eaten whatever exiles eat, who wrote letters with his pen stabbing the page, who couldn't afford to trust even the memory of a girl who used to bring him sea lavender—that Elias was asking me to account for the waiting. Of course he was. I'd have asked too. Exile strips you down to the audit.

What stung wasn't the suspicion. What stung was knowing it was fair.

* * *

Maren found me that evening still sitting cross-legged among my drafts, the sea lavender reduced to a brown smudge on the floorboards and my answer letter three sheets deep in crossed-out beginnings.

"You look like a woman composing a confession," she said.

"Close enough."

"Is it to him?"

"It's to a boy who stopped existing seven years ago. I'm writing to his replacement and hoping the address works."

Maren considered this, chewing on the stem of her pipe unlit—she saved the tobacco for days when the harbor froze, which in Vellmark was a philosophical position rather than a weather event. "In my experience," she said, "the replacement usually remembers more than the original. The boy forgets things. The exile keeps receipts."

"That's supposed to comfort me?"

"It's supposed to be useful. Comfort costs extra." She leaned in the doorway, and for a moment the shrewd dockside mask slipped and something older looked out—a woman who had also once been somebody else somewhere else, though I've never learned who, and never will. "You're going to tell him about the three years?"

"I'm going to tell him the truth about the three years."

"Mm." She tapped the pipe against her palm. "Truth's expensive cargo. Breaks easy. Half the time it arrives damaged and both parties pretend otherwise." She straightened. "Send it anyway. Men in exile die of silence faster than hunger. I've watched it happen."

Then she left, because Maren never lingered near sincerity longer than it takes to step over it.

* * *

I burned six drafts. The seventh went out on Thursday's fish cart, wrapped in oilcloth inside a hollowed salt barrel, addressed to a chandler's shop in Torvstad that may or may not exist.

This is what I told him:

*To E.H., who has every right to his suspicions and my compliments on his paranoia, which is well-earned and neatly organized.*

*The gulls, yes. To this day. They know something about me that I've never learned myself. I cannot swim, and I consider this wisdom rather than deficiency—the sea and I have a professional relationship and I see no reason to complicate it with intimacy. The stillroom fire was Master Aldous's fault entirely; he told me elderberry ink was possible and neglected to mention that "possible" and "advisable" are different words. Cook blamed me for nine years and I let her, because correcting her would have required admitting I tried to impress him, and a girl must keep some shames private.*

*I cried at his funeral because I loved him. That is the simple answer. There is a longer one, and here it is, since you asked for truth and I find I've run out of alternatives:*

*I waited three years because I was afraid. Not of the bureau—though they are worth fearing—but of the promise itself. Your father's pact with Master Aldous was made in peacetime, by men who believed maps could outlive politics. Then the politics ate the peacetime, and the promise became contraband, and I became the only courier left alive who carried it. I was a servant, Elias. My whole strategy for staying breathing was to be nothing. Your name was a sentence with no appeal. Opening that drawer meant agreeing to matter, and I had gotten very comfortable mattering to no one.*

*I am not proud of this. I am not asking you to admire it. I am telling you because you asked, and because if you accept the atlas from me, you deserve to know the condition of the hands that bring it: they held back for three years, and then they didn't, and the difference between those two states is the entire story of what kind of person I am.*

*As for proof that I'm not bait: I can't give you any that a clever bureau man couldn't forge, and Brann—the census man hunting this—is cleverer than either of us can afford. So instead I offer you arithmetic. If I were bait, I would already have given them the plate. They would have rewarded me, quietly, the way they reward everyone who feeds them. Instead I sleep above a smokehouse and roll herring carts for a woman who charges me rent in secrets. Bait does not pay for the privilege of hanging on a hook.*

*Come yourself, or send someone you trust, or send no one and let the atlas rot in a wetland chart forever—it is yours, all of it, and I will honor whatever you decide, because the promise was never conditional on your gratitude. Only on my delivery.*

*One correction, though, and I'll insist on it: you were wrong about one item. I didn't cry at the funeral because I was ill. Cook thought so, yes. But I had just helped Master Aldous load the forbidden plate into the false bottom of his chart cabinet two nights prior, and I understood at the graveside that I was the only person left in Vellmark who knew it existed. Grief and inheritance arrived together, and I have not yet sorted which weight is which.*

*P.I.*

*P.S. — The sea lavender in the gutters of Torvstad is fitting. Flowers were always better at exile than we were.*

* * *

I gave the letter to Maren on Thursday and immediately began to regret every sentence in it.

That is the trouble with honesty sent through the post: you cannot follow it down the road apologizing. Somewhere between Vellmark and Torvstad, my confession would pass through hands I'd never see, and each of those hands would learn a little more about the location of my heart, which is precisely what I'd sworn three years ago never to show anyone. I had hidden the plate inside a wetland chart no one buys. I had hidden the letter inside my fear. And now I'd posted the fear itself, wrapped in oilcloth, trusting it to strangers, because a boy I'd last seen at fourteen had asked me a direct question and apparently I was constitutionally incapable of lying to children, even grown, sharpened, suspicious versions of them.

"You'll hear back inside two weeks if it worked," Maren said, counting coins that weren't hers into a purse that was. "If it didn't work, you'll hear nothing, and you should assume the silence means the letter's being read aloud in a warm room by men with good boots."

"Thank you. Truly, the reassurance is balm."

"Balm costs extra too." But she paused at the door, and her voice dropped out of the market register into something quieter. "The part you wrote about sorting grief from inheritance. Don't wait for the sorting to finish. Some cargoes travel mixed their whole voyage. Doesn't stop them arriving."

I wanted to ask how she knew. I wanted to ask a great many things about Maren Coldharbour, née whoever-she-had-been. But survivors respect the sealed drawers of other survivors. It's the one courtesy our kind affords.

* * *

The two weeks passed the way siege weeks pass—slowly at the edges, terribly in the middle.

I worked the fish carts. I mended nets I had no feeling for. I learned the runners' names and their tells: young Sif who lied badly about her age, old Kestrel who lied beautifully about everything, the brothers Voss who communicated entirely in grunts and could still conduct a full argument. I kept my head down and my ears open, which in Maren's household was the local religion, and I said my prayers to the harbor every dawn in the form of scanning the fish carts coming in from the south road.

On the twelfth day, the news arrived before any letter did.

It came the way news comes in a port—sideways, shouted, already mangled. I was at the smokehouse when Kestrel's cart rattled in off the south road at a pace that meant trouble, and the runner behind him was shouting before the wheels stopped: the Halvard archive, the seized family papers, the whole boxed remainder of a great house's memory—burned. Publicly. In the Customs Square, that morning, with an announcement read aloud and a crowd assembled to watch, because the bureau understood theater the way it understood everything else: as a tool for making examples.

They had fed seven decades of letters, ledgers, portraits, and private papers into a bonfire in front of the customs house, and a man named Brann had stood at the podium and declared it the first cleansing, and had promised—promised, in front of witnesses, with the satisfaction of a man reading his own promotion—that the crown's purification of seditious materials would continue until complete.

I stood in the fish-smelling cold and did the arithmetic before I could stop myself.

The archive was ash. Which meant the atlas was no longer one document among many. It was the last Halvard paper in existence—the final testimony of a house the crown was erasing root and branch. Which meant its value had changed. Which meant the hunt for it would change with it.

Which meant Brann's next bonfire already had a name on the ticket.

Maren appeared beside me without seeming to walk there, the way she did. She was watching the crowd around Kestrel's cart, but her voice was pitched for me alone.

"They'll come for the workshops next," she said. "Every mapmaker, every printer, every archive in Vellmark. Inventory first. Then confiscation. Then flame." She finally looked at me, and her eyes had gone flat and bright, like ice over moving water. "Your master's shop is on the registry, girl. I've seen the lists. Whatever you're hiding, wherever you're hiding it—your window just closed."

Behind her, the smokehouse chimney breathed its slow gray ribbon into a sky that had gone the color of old vellum.

Two more days, I calculated. Maybe less. Enough time for one more letter—or for deciding that letters were finished, and it was time to move what couldn't be replaced before the men with good boots came to do their inventory.

The fish carts had carried my hope south. Now they'd have to carry me somewhere worse: forward.
