## 13. The Burned Archive

The smoke reached the workshop before the news did.

I smelled it at dawn — a wrongness in the air, like a candle snuffed in the next room but multiplied by a thousand, by ten thousand. Vellmark has its own smells: tar, brine, fish guts, the tanneries downriver when the wind forgets its manners. This was none of those. This was paper burning. And not just any paper. I knew the smell of old paper, of ledgers and letters and vellum that had been sitting in drawers since before I was born, because I had spent three years with my nose pressed to exactly that.

By the time Maren's boy came pounding up the stairs — he wasn't supposed to know where I was sleeping now, which told me how badly the day would go — the whole city knew. They'd burned it in the Customs Square, the Halvard family archive. Every deed, every letter patent, every marriage record and land grant going back two hundred years, carted out in official barrels and fed to a public bonfire while a clerk read the proclamation from steps that still bore the scorch marks of an older, less tidy government.

"They say it was diseased paper," the boy said, breathless. "Contaminated. Sedition in the bindings."

"Sedition in the bindings," I repeated. "That's a new one."

He shrugged the shrug of a fourteen-year-old who had already learned that governments lie the way rivers run downhill — constantly, and with great force — and left me alone with my hands shaking over the ink pot.

Here is what nobody tells you about watching a family be erased: you don't feel it as politics. You feel it as housework. I thought of Lady Halvard's letters to her sister, tied in blue ribbon, which I had once carried up three flights of stairs on a tray. I thought of little Elias's copybooks, if those were archived too, with his terrible left-slanting hand and his insistence on drawing sea monsters in the margins of his geography lessons. Ash. All of it ash, and a clerk reading out the word *diseased* as though paper could catch morality the way it catches mold.

They weren't destroying records. They were destroying proof. Proof that the Halvards had ever existed, had ever owned, had ever been promised anything — including, I thought with a cold turn of the stomach, anything by Master Aldous Iversen.

Which meant the atlas was no longer contraband. It was now the last remaining evidence of a promise the crown very much wanted forgotten.

And I was sleeping forty feet above it.

* * *

Maren found me at midday in her back room, where I'd been pretending to mend nets with the competence of a woman who has never touched a net in her life. She took one look at my hands, took them away from the netting before I did further damage, and sat down across from me with the particular patience of someone about to charge money for bad news.

"You've heard," she said.

"I've smelled."

"Then hear the rest." She kept her voice low, though we were alone, because Maren's voice went low whenever information changed value. "There are men asking after you. Not Brann's usual sort — well-dressed ones. Bureau men with clean boots. They went to the workshop yesterday evening."

My stomach did something complicated. "Tomas?"

"Your Tomas answered the door himself. Told them you'd gone north to your family." She raised an eyebrow. "You have a family now. In the north. Apparently they're ill, and that's why you left so suddenly, and why you took nothing with you."

"That's more detail than I'd have given."

"That's why he gave it. Liars who give no detail get asked again. Liars who give too much get believed." She picked a bit of rope off her sleeve, considered it, put it down. "Course, a liar who gives that much detail is also a man keeping receipts. Remember that."

"I'm trying very hard not to remember anything today."

"Mm." She stood, brushed off her skirts. "One more thing. The clean-boots men left a card. For you. By name."

She put it on the table between us like she was laying down a losing hand. Plain cream stock, the census bureau's seal, and inside, in a small, precise, infuriatingly courteous hand:

*Madam — The bureau requests the pleasure of your attendance tomorrow, eleven o'clock, at your former place of employment. Matters of mutual interest have arisen. No action will be taken against you pending this conversation. — Inspector Brann*

"*Mutual interest*," I said.

"He does write pretty," Maren agreed. "For a man whose job is arithmetic on human beings."

"No action pending." I turned the card over, as though Brann might have written something useful on the back, like *this is a trap*. "That's the part they always mean least."

"Well," said Maren. "Now you know what I sell, and what it costs. So here's some free, and mind it, because free from me is rarer than honest weather. When a hunter tells you no harm will come to you, he has already decided what harm will come to you. He's just letting you pick the shape."

* * *

I didn't sleep. I sat in the workshop loft instead — yes, I know, I'd gone north to nurse my fictitious sick family, but there are places a body goes when it can't think anywhere else, and for three years that place had been Aldous's workroom, smelling of hide glue and linseed, and I was apparently going to die for the habit of comfort.

The atlas lay where I'd hidden it, under the floorboards beneath the drying rack, wrapped in oilcloth beside the plate. I lifted them out and held the plate up to the lamp.

The forbidden coastline. The one Aldous had sealed in lead foil and locked away, the one he'd told me — the only time I ever heard fear in his voice — *never finish, never show, never let it exist until the world is safe for it.* It showed the northern approaches in detail the navy itself didn't publish. Channels, soundings, the old Halvard landing rights marked in red. A map that proved the crown's claim on those waters was built on a theft, and proved exactly who had been robbed.

In peacetime it was scholarship. In wartime it was treason. In 1847, with the archive smoking in the square, it was the only witness left.

"You should have burned it yourself, old man," I told him. His chair sat empty by the window, the way it had for three years, the way it would sit empty until someone else burned this building too. "It would have saved everyone such trouble."

But he hadn't, because Aldous Iversen did not destroy truth. He just made other people carry it. I set the plate back under the floorboards and thought, with a kind of exhausted clarity, that tomorrow at eleven o'clock I would walk into a room with a man who wanted it, and I would need to decide — before I opened my mouth — whether I was the woman who surrendered it or the woman who didn't.

The trouble was, I genuinely didn't know yet. That frightened me more than Brann did.

* * *

Eleven o'clock. The workshop had been tidied within an inch of its life, mostly by me, partly by Tomas, who hovered with the anxious energy of a man standing in his own house while strangers appraise the furniture. He'd offered twice to be present for the interview. Twice I'd declined. He'd accepted the second refusal with a look I couldn't read, which lately was the only look he had.

Brann arrived at four minutes past eleven, precisely, because a man like Brann is never early and never late, only exact. He looked different out of his census-gray uniform — civilian clothes, good ones, the costume of a functionary on the rise. He looked around the workshop with genuine appreciation, which somehow made everything worse.

"You kept it exactly," he said. "The benches, the racks. Even the smell. Remarkable, how a room remembers its dead."

"The room doesn't remember anything. I clean it."

"Yes." He smiled. "So I understand."

He walked the length of the room, trailing one gloved finger along the map press without touching anything important, and stopped at the window where the light fell best, and delivered the morning's news as though reporting weather.

"You'll have seen the smoke. The Halvard archive was destroyed yesterday, pursuant to the Sanitation of Records Act. Two hundred and six barrels. A satisfying fire — green wood, unfortunately, so a lot of smoke and not much spectacle. The clerks were disappointed." He turned. "The atlas Master Iversen was commissioned to complete is now classified as residual contamination. Same act. It will be collected and burned, probably next week. There's a list. Your master's name is on it. Yours, under your apprentice identity, is on it too — as a custodian, not a principal. Custodians who cooperate are struck from the list. Custodians who don't..." He spread his hands. "Well. The list is short, and fires are cheap."

I kept my face doing what it had learned to do over three years of hiding in plain sight: nothing much. "The atlas is a commercial work. Charts and coastlines. Half of it's already sold."

"And half of it is a plate showing the northern approaches, with certain annotations, which I believe you have moved from wherever your master hid it. Don't look surprised — the drawer lock was scratched, and scratched locks tell stories." He let that sit. Then, gently, horribly kindly: "Petra. May I call you Petra? Or shall we use the other name? I have both, you know. The household registers survived in duplicate. The archive burned the copies; the census keeps the originals. That's rather the point of a census."

There it was. Three years of careful anonymity, folded up like wet paper in a man's coat pocket. I felt the floor tilt a degree and steadied myself against the map press.

"What do you want?" I asked. My voice came out level. Small victories.

"To offer you a future." He took a document from his case and laid it on the workbench, squaring its corners with two fingers. "Immunity. Full immunity, in writing, signed by the director of the bureau. Your name cleared, your apprenticeship recognized, a recommendation to the Royal Cartographic Society — they owe us favors; we count people for them. You could have your own workshop inside five years. You're wasted here anyway, mending nets and hiding. You're the finest hand I've seen come through the provincial schools, and yes, I checked that too. Talent like yours shouldn't spend its life being afraid."

"And the price."

"Surrender the plate. And names." He said it simply, without relish, which was the most frightening thing about him. "Who helped you move it. Who's sheltering you. What routes you considered. I'm not asking for a confession — confessions are for trials, and trials are for people the ministry wants to make examples of. You're not that. You're a girl who inherited a promise she shouldn't have. I understand inheritance. I want to help you put it down."

*A promise she shouldn't have.* As though peacetime were a country that could be annexed retroactively. As though seven years ago, in a warm study smelling of brandy and beeswax, an old lord and an old mapmaker hadn't shaken hands over a dying fire and made each other swear.

I thought of Elias — wherever he was, whatever Maren's routes had become, whether he was even alive, whether my letter had reached him or been eaten by the same machinery that ate archives. I thought of the blue-ribboned letters going up in green-wood smoke. I thought of immunity, written and signed, and a workshop of my own, and five years from now not flinching at footsteps on stairs.

It would have been so easy. That's how they design these things. Not like a knife pointed at you — like a knife offered handle-first, politely, with your name engraved on it.

"Inspector," I said, "you've made a mistake about me, and I'd like to correct it while we're being friendly."

His expression didn't change. "Go on."

"You think I'm holding the plate because I'm afraid to lose it. I'm holding it because I'm the only person left who knows what's true. Burn the archive, burn the atlas, burn me — the truth doesn't shrink. It just gets carried by fewer people, and the fewer people carry it, the heavier it gets." I looked him straight on. "I haven't decided anything. But I'll tell you what I won't do: I won't hand you names. Names are the only currency in this city worth more than maps, and I don't spend what isn't mine."

Something flickered behind his eyes — not anger. Recalculation. I had, apparently, failed to behave like a cornered animal, and Brann's whole method assumed cornered animals.

"A pity," he said, and meant it, which was worse than menace. "Because here is what happens next, so you can't claim surprise. The atlas is confiscated Thursday. If the plate is with it, undamaged, I will consider that cooperation and strike your name from the list — no names required. If it isn't..." He picked up his hat. "Then I stop treating you as a custodian and start treating you as an accomplice, and the people around you start being treated as accessories, beginning alphabetically with whoever answers your door."

Tomas. He meant Tomas. He'd watched Tomas answer the door; of course he had.

He paused at the threshold, and turned back with the air of a man adding a postscript he'd already composed. "Oh — one thing more. The young master. Elias Halvard. We have reason to believe correspondence has entered the city addressed to him. Smuggled correspondence." His gaze rested on me with terrible mildness. "If such a letter exists, and it passes through your hands, remember: the archive burned in one afternoon. People burn faster."

Then he was gone, his clean boots ringing down the stairs, leaving the immunity document on the bench between us like bait on a hook.

* * *

Tomas came out of the back room before the door had finished closing. Of course he'd heard. The workshop's walls are thin; I'd counted on that once, in another life.

"You refused him." His voice was strange. "To his face. You refused full immunity."

"I stalled."

"You refused him." He crossed to the bench and stared down at the document, and I watched something move across his face that I couldn't name — envy, maybe, or its ugly cousin, resentment wearing envy's clothes. "Do you know what that paper is? That's a life, Petra. That's everything either of us has worked for, handed to you, and you looked at it like it was a bill."

"It comes with a hook in it."

"Everything comes with a hook in it!" He turned on me, and for a moment the gentle, watchful Tomas was gone and someone hungrier stood there. "You've had three years of playing the martyr in secret and it's rotted your judgment. The atlas is going to burn Thursday either way. The archive is already ash. The Halvards are finished — finished, do you understand? Keeping their plate doesn't unfinish them. All it does is decide whether we burn with them."

"*We*," I said quietly.

He stopped. Heard it. I saw him hear it.

"Tomas," I said, "why did you lie to the clean-boot men about where I went?"

The hunger drained out of his face and left something worse behind: caution. "Because you're my colleague."

"Is that all?"

"What else would it be?" But he wouldn't quite meet my eyes, and Maren's voice was in my ear — *a liar who gives too much detail is a man keeping receipts* — and suddenly the whole shape of the last month rearranged itself in my chest like a chart redrawn with new soundings.

Thursday. Two days. Confiscation, or the plate gone somewhere Brann's clean boots would never find it. And somewhere out past the harbor walls, if Maren's routes still breathed, a letter might be moving toward a half-starved exile who had every reason to believe the world had forgotten him.

"Tomas," I said, "I need you to leave the workshop tonight. Just for the evening. I have work to finish."

He looked at me for a long moment. "What work?"

"The kind," I said, "that gets done alone."

And he nodded slowly, and went to fetch his coat, and I stood in the middle of the room Aldous left me, listening to the door click shut, knowing I had perhaps one night, one route, and one chance — and no idea at all which of the people I loved would sell me first.
