## 14. Tomas Chooses

There is a particular kind of silence that falls over a mapmaker's workshop at night, and I had always thought of it as mine. The others went home to their suppers and their sweethearts, and I stayed behind with the lamps and the linseed smell of fresh ink, learning the coastlines by heart the way other men learned psalms. Master Aldous used to say a map was a promise you made to strangers. He said it to Petra, mostly. I don't think he ever said it to me. That should have been my first warning about myself—that I kept count.

Now the workshop had been inspected twice, sealed once, and reopened with a government sticker on the door that peeled at one corner no matter how often I pressed it down. I pressed it down every morning. It was the only part of the place I still controlled.

The census man came for me on a Thursday, which felt insulting. Thursdays were ink-mixing days. I was grinding lampblack into linseed oil when Brann let himself in without knocking, the way officials do, as though doors were a courtesy extended to them rather than required.

"Apprentice Tomas Rehn," he said. Not a question. He'd have known my shoe size by now.

"You're standing in my workspace," I said. "There's a rule about that."

"There's a rule about everything," Brann said. "That's rather the point of me."

He was not what I'd expected, which is its own kind of trap. I had imagined a brute—a man built like a bailiff, all shoulders and warrants. Instead he was slight, gray at the temples, with the careful hands of someone who did close work. His eyes went around the workshop the way mine did: reading it. Shelves of rolled charts. The great table scarred with compass points. The locked drawer in Aldous's old desk, empty now, its key missing—though I had watched Petra's face the day the inspectors asked after it, and faces are maps too, if you know how to read them.

"I've read your file," Brann said, pulling a stool toward him and sitting with the ease of a man who intended to stay. "Three years' apprenticeship. Good penmanship. No family worth mentioning."

"Thank you."

"It wasn't a compliment." He picked up a burnisher from the bench and turned it over. "It was an observation. A man with nothing to lose is either very safe or very dangerous, and the bureau hasn't decided which you are. I'm here to help them decide."

* * *

I want to be honest, since no one else in this story is going to be. I want to record, here at the beginning, what I told myself while Brann sat there turning my tools over in his soft gray hands.

I told myself: *I am protecting her.*

Hold on to that. Watch how long it lasts.

* * *

"The Halvard girl," Brann said.

"She has a name."

"She has several, apparently. Petra Iversen. Petra Lund. There was a household register from the Halvard estate that lists a kitchen girl of roughly her age and coloring, unaccounted for since the exiles. And now there's a census inquiry that says the late master's apprentice keeps odd hours, works alone on plates she shouldn't touch, and received a visitor last month from a woman who runs fish carts out of the Saltmarket." He set down the burnisher with exaggerated care. "You see my difficulty, apprentice. Everyone in this city lies to me, but almost no one lies well. You, though. You've been practicing."

I said nothing. In the three years I'd worked beside Petra, I had learned that silence is a tool like any other, and that she wielded it better than anyone—but I'd also learned where she kept hers sharpened, and I had spent enough midnights watching her work to borrow the technique.

Brann waited. He was good at waiting. Then he opened his coat—not dramatically, nothing so vulgar—and drew out a folded paper, which he smoothed flat on the workbench between us.

It was a letter. Unsent. My handwriting.

*I have seen her working on the sealed plate. I do not yet know what it shows. I will write again when I do.*

My stomach performed a slow, professional collapse. I had written it weeks ago, in the first hot week of suspicion, meaning to send it to my uncle in the Interior Ministry—a man who owed my father money and repaid debts in favors. I had never posted it. I had carried it around instead the way you carry a stone in your pocket to remind yourself of your own weight, and I told myself every morning that I would throw it away, and every evening that tomorrow was a better day for throwing.

Where had he gotten it? My coat. He'd gone through my coat at some point—the inspection days, surely, when we'd all stood in the yard with our hands visible while their people combed the benches. Of course. Of course they had. I had been so busy wondering whether Petra noticed me covering for her that I never wondered whether anyone noticed me at all.

"Interesting document," Brann said. "Ambitious. A young man informing on his colleague to advance himself." He tapped it. "Except it was never sent. Why?"

"It's none of your business why."

"Everything in this city is my business; that's what makes the city run." He leaned back. "But I'll guess. You didn't send it because sending it would make you an informer, and you prefer to think of yourself as something else. An observer. A historian. A man merely taking notes." His mouth did something that was almost a smile. "I know the type. I was the type. We tell ourselves that watching is not choosing."

"And what are you?" I said. "A patriot?"

"I'm a man who chose, years ago, and has been spared the further expense of deciding anything." He said it flatly, without pride, and somehow that was worse than smugness would have been. "Now. Here is what happens next. The bureau has scheduled confiscation of this workshop's holdings. Everything burns or goes into the closed archive—burning, most likely; paper about the Halvard coast has become politically inconvenient. Before that happens, certain parties would find it useful to know whether any materials left this building recently. By unusual routes. Fish-cart routes, for instance."

The room got very quiet, or I did. One of us.

"I don't know anything about fish carts."

"No," Brann agreed pleasantly. "You don't. But you know who does. And you know that the person who hid a certain letter inside a chart of wetlands—which, incidentally, we found the hollow in the shelf where it sat, apprentice, we are not fools—has been seen twice now near the Saltmarket piers." He rose, buttoned his coat, and paused at the door. "Tomorrow, then. Think about which kind of man you are. The bureau pays both kinds. It only forgives one."

Then he was gone, and I stood among my beautiful maps, each one a promise made to strangers, and understood that I had until tomorrow to become either a traitor or a coward, and that these were, as far as I could determine, the same thing wearing different coats.

* * *

That night I did not sleep. I sat in the workshop with one lamp lit and did what I always did when the world became illegible: I drew.

I drew the harbor. I drew the Saltmarket piers, the fish carts, the narrow lane Maren's runners used behind the salt houses—I knew it because Petra had once traced it absently on a scrap while explaining tide tables, back when we still talked about things that weren't secrets, and my memory hoards whatever she gives it. I drew the route out of the city that a person would take if a person were smuggling a copper plate sewn into her coat. I drew it beautifully. I could have sold it to Brann in the morning and it would have been worth more than my letter, more than my testimony—because it wasn't hearsay, it was craft, and craft convinces.

And then I looked at what I'd drawn and understood what I was looking at.

Not a map of the harbor. A map of Petra's escape. Drawn in my hand. Ready to hand over.

Here is the arithmetic I did, sitting there in the lamplight, and I record it honestly because dishonesty is the whole disease and maybe naming it is the beginning of the cure, though I doubt it:

If I gave Brann the route, they would take Maren, take the carts, seal the harbor lane. Petra would lose her way out of the city. She might be caught. If she was caught, she'd name no one—she was constitutionally incapable of surrendering anyone, it was her single flaw and her single virtue—and she would hang or rot or vanish into some archive herself, and the atlas—

The atlas would come to me. Who else? The inspectors already treated me as the senior apprentice. With her gone and Aldous dead, the atlas would pass to my hand, my name. *Tomas Rehn, cartographer.* The name on the title page of the great work, the one thing I had wanted since I was fourteen and first saw Aldous Iversen's coastline of the northern ice, so fine it looked like breathing.

That was the honest sum. Mercy on one side of the ledger, ambition on the other, and the terrible discovery that they balanced almost exactly, and that whichever way I tipped I could dress the tipping in the other's clothing afterward and believe it.

I tore the drawing up. All of it. Small pieces, into the stove.

Then I took out a fresh sheet and began drawing it again, because a man who destroys evidence looks guilty, and a man who redraws it can tell himself he was only studying geography.

God help me. I actually believed that for nearly a full minute.

* * *

Brann came at dawn, which was theatrical of him and effective, because dawn is when a man's defenses are still folded up small from the night.

He didn't sit this time. He stood over my bench, where the redrawn chart lay half-finished, and looked at it for a long moment, and I watched him understand exactly what it was and what it meant that I had drawn it.

"You made a choice," he said, "and then you tried to unmake it, and then you remade it. I've read confessions with less honesty in them than this piece of paper." He rolled the chart up and tucked it under his arm—my work, taken without a word of payment—and said: "The route. Now. Names optional."

So I gave it to him. The lane behind the salt houses. The cart schedule, Tuesdays and Fridays before market. Maren's name—I gave him that much, she was already marked, hiding her would have been hiding a lighthouse—and the signal system, the chalk marks on the pier posts. Everything except the one thing. Everything except her.

"And the person carrying your plate?" Brann asked. "The apprentice. Give me the connection and the bureau handles the rest gently."

"Gently," I repeated.

"We have a range of gentlenesses."

"I don't know what you mean."

"Tomas." He said my name the way you'd tap a barrel to hear if it was full. "You drew her escape route from memory. You know her better than her confession would tell me. Don't insult us both with ignorance."

"I know an apprentice who works too many hours," I said, "and hides things in wetland charts because nobody buys them. That's all I know. If the bureau can't find the rest, perhaps the bureau should hire better mapmakers."

For a moment I thought he'd strike me, or laugh. He did neither. He looked at me with something I hadn't expected—something almost like respect, or its counterfeit—and said, "You'll live with this longer than she will, whoever she is. That's not a threat. It's an observation. Men like you always survive; it's your whole tragedy." He paused at the door. "Your uncle will hear that you cooperated. Your name stays off the lists. For now."

And then I was alone with my lamp and my inks and the peeling government sticker on the door, and I did the thing I will regret until they put me in the ground, which is: I felt relieved. Warmly, thoroughly relieved. My name off the lists. My claim intact. My hands clean, or near enough to clean that in poor light they'd pass.

* * *

Two days later the fish carts stopped.

I heard it in the market, in the changed texture of the crowd—the way word moves through a city faster than any courier, mouth to mouth, a shiver through the stalls. They'd taken Maren at the Saltmarket pier on Friday before dawn. Seized the route. Two of her runners scattered; one didn't get far.

I told myself: *I didn't name her.*

But I had drawn her pier. I had given the schedule and the chalk marks. A name is only the smallest part of a person anyway; give a man enough of the shape of someone and the name becomes a formality. Brann hadn't needed me to say *Maren*. He'd needed Tuesday and Friday, and the chalk, and the lane behind the salt houses, and I had handed them over wrapped in my own fine linework, and somewhere in the gray hour before dawn they had used my map to catch a woman whose crime, as far as I could ever learn, was moving paper for people with no other way to move it.

And Petra—I walked past the workshop that afternoon and saw her through the window, bent over the great table, inking something, calm as tide. She didn't know yet. Or she knew and was already moving, because that was Petra: grief converted to motion at speed. Either way, she did not look up, and I stood outside in the cold like a beggar at a shrine I'd robbed, unable to go in, unable to walk away, holding the one thing I had left her—her name, unsaid—with both hands like it weighed something.

It doesn't weigh anything. That's the filthiest part. Silence costs the silencer nothing at the time. It only comes due later, with interest, and the interest is yourself.

That night I unlocked the drawer where I kept the atlas's index pages—the ones Aldous had entrusted to me, the ones that proved my hand had touched the great work—and I sat looking at them, waiting to feel like a cartographer. Waiting to feel like the man whose name belonged beside the master's on the title page.

What I felt instead was a new entry writing itself in a ledger I couldn't close: *fish carts stopped, Friday, before dawn, by his line.* And beneath it, fainter, in handwriting I recognized as my own future: *she will find out what you drew. They always find out. And then you will learn what she keeps in that silence of hers, and whether there is room in it for mercy.*

Across the square, past the dark windows of the workshop, a light was burning that had no business burning at that hour.

She was leaving. I knew it the way I knew coastlines—by instinct, by the shape of absence beginning.

And I stood in my doorway with my lamp lit and my guilt unpaid, and I did not call out to warn her, and I did not run to stop her, and I told myself—as the light went out and her shadow crossed the square toward the wetlands shelf, toward the letter, toward whatever came next—I told myself I was giving her a head start.

Even now, I can almost believe it.
