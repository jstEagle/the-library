## 15. The Fish Carts Stop

I have been arrested twice before, and both times I had the decency to see it coming. The first was in my nineteenth year, for running untaxed brandy through the Saltgate in a coffin—my own idea, and a good one, until the widow whose husband we borrowed decided to attend the burial. The second time was over letters, which is what everything comes back to eventually, if you live long enough in this trade. Paper is the only cargo that never spoils, never stinks, and never forgives you.

So when the fish carts stopped rolling on a grey Thursday morning, I knew before anyone told me.

You learn to read a route the way a sailor reads weather. My runners—six of them, seven counting Petra, who was never officially one of mine but had the look of a woman becoming one whether she liked it or not—moved on rhythm. Herring cart out at dawn, back by eight with the ice melting off the barrels. Cod cart an hour later, because old Sef would rather lose money than be early to anything. When that rhythm broke, it broke for one of two reasons: sickness or soldiers. And nobody in Vellmark had been sick since spring.

I was standing in the yard behind Maren's Charts & Sundries—which is what the sign said, though no living soul had bought a sundry from me since the coronation—when Sef came around the corner at a dead run, which for Sef meant something between a trot and a determined stumble. His face told me the rest before his mouth caught up.

"They're at the gate," he said. "Bureau men. Grey coats. They've got the harbor master with them, and he's brought *paper*."

The harbor master bringing paper was the part that chilled me. A man like Halloran could be bought, bullied, or bored into looking elsewhere; I'd done all three over the years, sometimes before breakfast. But paper meant orders from above Halloran, and there wasn't much above Halloran except the crown and the census bureau, and only one of those two cared about fish carts.

"Which gate?"

"The north one. And Maren—" He wiped his mouth with the back of his hand, a habit that made him look twelve years old instead of fifty. "They've got lists. Names written down. They asked for you by name."

"Well," I said. "Isn't that flattering."

It's a strange thing, being flattered and terrified in the same breath. It happens more often than people admit. I sent Sef home—I told him through the marsh road, don't argue—and I went inside and did what any sensible woman does when her world starts closing: I took inventory.

* * *

Here is what you should understand about me, since you'll be hearing the story secondhand anyway: I trade in information the way other women trade in fish. Not because I love it—though I do, a little, the way a gambler loves cards—but because information is honest in a way nothing else in Vellmark is. A barrel can lie about its contents. A face can lie about anything. But a fact, properly weighed and properly sold, stays a fact no matter who buys it. I have sold to bureau men and smugglers, to customs clerks and to the Halvard remnant, back when there was still a Halvard remnant worth the name. I never lied to a customer. I just never told them everything they were paying for.

Petra came to me three weeks ago with a name she wanted carried out of the city and a look on her face I recognized from the mirror, years ago: the look of a person who has finally stopped calculating whether to act and started calculating how. She couldn't pay what the job cost. I told her so plainly, because lying to her would have been both unkind and bad business—she'd have found out, and then she'd have known I saw her as someone to be managed, and managed people make sloppy partners.

So I gave her terms instead. Debt, deferred. Interest accruing. And in exchange—not charity, mind, never call it charity—I kept her letter moving through channels I trust more than most, toward a man in exile who had every reason to believe the world had forgotten him.

And now grey coats were at the north gate asking for me by name, which meant somebody had talked, or somebody had watched, or both. In my experience it's usually both, because the bureau doesn't gamble. They collect.

I filled a satchel. Not with the obvious things—a sensible fugitive takes bread and a knife—but with the things that mattered: three ledgers too dangerous to burn carelessly and too damning to leave behind, a packet of letters tied with green string, and the little brass weight I use to hold charts flat against wind, which my mother gave me and which has no value whatsoever except to me. Then I sat down at my own table, poured myself a cup of the cheap gin I keep for guests I dislike, and waited to see how the morning would come for me.

It came through the front door, which showed a certain lack of imagination.

* * *

There were four of them. Three grey coats and one clerk, thin as a bookmark, carrying a document folder under his arm the way a priest carries a sacrament. The leader was a captain type—young enough to be ambitious, old enough to have buried whatever conscience ambition requires. He looked around my shop with the expression of a man cataloguing sins.

"Maren Coldharbour?" he said, though I could see my own name painted on the window behind him.

"That's the establishment. The woman's negotiable."

He didn't smile. Bureau men rarely do; humor is overhead expense. "Maren Coldharbour, you are detained under provisions of the Census Security Act, section eleven, pending inquiry into the movement of restricted correspondence."

"Restricted correspondence." I let the words sit in my mouth like a stone. "Captain, I sell charts. Fishermen buy them. Sometimes a wife comes in wanting a map pretty enough to hang over the mantel. If correspondence has been moving through here, it's been hiding in the herring."

"Where are your records?"

"In my head, mostly. The rest are in that drawer, and I'd thank you not to bend the wood—it's older than the city charter."

The clerk opened the folder. I watched him do it the way I watch all men open folders: for what they look at first. He looked at a page near the middle, not the top, which meant the important thing was neither accusation nor warrant but a list. Lists mean names. Names mean someone upstream of me had already drowned.

They went through the shop with the thoroughness of men being paid by the hour and the dullness of men who hate their work, which is the most dangerous combination there is—the careless careful. The captain found the false bottom under the chart cabinet in under six minutes, which annoyed me professionally; I'd have given myself eight. Inside it they found what I'd left for them to find: two years of genuinely boring transactions, a few bribes recorded in code so mild it might as well have been a recipe book, and nothing whatsoever about letters going east with the fish carts.

Because that record lived somewhere else entirely, and it lived in the head of a woman they were currently not searching hard enough.

"You move more than charts," the captain said. It wasn't a question, so I didn't answer it.

"I move what pays. That's the whole philosophy. You're welcome to write it down."

He wrote it down. God help us all, he actually did.

Then, as they were binding my hands—not roughly; even now, procedure has its courtesies—he leaned close, and for the first time the man underneath the uniform looked out at me. Young. Tired. Possibly human.

"The girl," he said quietly. "The mapmaker's apprentice. Was she one of yours?"

And there it was. The whole architecture of my arrest laid out in one question. They didn't want Maren Coldharbour, keeper of sundries. They wanted the thread, and I was the thread's end, and threads end either at a spool or at a pair of scissors.

"I don't know any girls," I said. "I know customers. Customers pay their debts."

Something flickered across his face—disappointment, maybe, or respect, the two wearing similar clothes—and then he straightened up and became a uniform again, and they walked me out through my own front door into a street gone quiet in that particular way streets go quiet when everyone is watching through shutters.

* * *

Now. Here is where I must confess something, because this account will likely outlive me and I refuse to be remembered as braver than I was.

In the wagon, with my wrists bound and my satchel confiscated and my life's inventory reduced to whatever a clerk could carry in a folder, I was afraid. Properly afraid, the kind that sits in the stomach like swallowed ice. Not of pain—I'm past the age where pain impresses me—but of usefulness. Because I am useful, that's the truth of it, and useful people in custody get used. Somewhere in a bureau office there was a man with soft hands who would ask me questions in a reasonable voice, and I knew—with the certainty of a woman who has spent thirty years weighing what people will pay—that eventually, given enough time and the right pressure, I would tell him something true. Not everything. Never everything. But something. A thread end frays. That's what thread ends do.

And the thread, pulled taut, led to a mapmaker's apprentice named Petra Iversen, who owed me money she couldn't pay, and who had trusted me with a promise that wasn't mine to break.

So I did the only arithmetic left to me. While the wagon jolted through the north quarter, I began composing the lie I would give them—the long, boring, self-incriminating lie that leads nowhere. Smuggled brandy, yes. Untaxed tobacco, certainly. Letters for homesick sailors, absolutely, charge me for it. A girl apprentice? Let me think. There was a girl who bought coastal charts last autumn, paid in mixed coin...

Every hour they spend believing that lie is an hour she doesn't spend in a cell. Every day is a day. I am not a hero; heroes die young and get statues. I am a merchant, and this was simply the largest transaction of my career: selling myself, slowly, at the highest price the market would bear, to buy time for a debt I'd chosen to carry.

I only hope she runs far enough to make the purchase worthwhile.

* * *

But this chapter isn't only mine, and I know it, and you know it, so let me tell you what the street saw afterward, because the street sees everything and tells it for free.

The fish carts stopped that morning and did not start again. Bureau men stood at the north gate turning back carts, opening barrels, reading manifests with the slow delight of men who enjoy ruining mornings. By noon the word had traveled the way word travels—down alley mouths, through wash-line gossip, over fences—to a workshop near the mapmakers' quarter where a young woman with ink-stained fingers heard it from a neighbor's boy and understood, in the space of a single sentence, that her way out of Vellmark had just been closed like a door.

She came to the yard that evening—I learned this later, from Sef, who tells it badly but tells it true. She came expecting a fence, a cart, a plan. What she found was an empty yard, a shuttered shop, and Sef sitting on a barrel looking like a man at his own funeral.

"Gone?" she said. "Taken?"

"This morning. Grey coats. They knew her name." Sef scratched his jaw. "Knew yours too, I'd wager, or they wouldn't have bothered."

I'm told she stood very still for a moment, the way water goes still just before it freezes. Then she asked the only question that mattered: "When does the atlas go? When do they come for the workshop?"

Sef didn't know. Nobody outside the bureau knows a confiscation schedule; the bureau barely knows its own. But the boy had seen the clerks' lists, and lists get acted on in order, and the archive had burned on a Tuesday, and the bureau does not believe in letting grass grow.

"Soon," Sef said. "Days. Maybe tomorrow."

And here, according to Sef—and I believe him, because he has no imagination whatsoever and therefore cannot invent anything this good—Petra Iversen laughed. One short, hard syllable, with nothing of humor in it, the sound of a woman watching the last exit close and discovering she doesn't want it anymore.

"Then I stop hiding it," she said, "and start moving it."

She turned and walked back toward the mapmakers' quarter through the blue dusk, alone, carrying nothing visible, and Sef says—and this from a man who once described a shipwreck as "a bit damp"—that watching her go was like watching a lit fuse decide which direction to burn.

I tell you this from memory, or from hope, or from wherever such things live when their owner sits in a bureau cell composing beautiful lies. Take it as you find it. The fish carts stopped. The city closed its fist. And somewhere in the dark, a mapmaker's daughter stopped running from the fire and started walking toward it.

God forgive me for what I may yet tell them. And God help the bureau if she reaches him first.
