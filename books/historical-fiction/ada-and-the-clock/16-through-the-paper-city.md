## 16. Through the Paper City

There is a particular kind of silence that falls over a city at the hour when the last drunk has gone home and the first baker has not yet lit his oven. I had spent three years listening to it through the workshop shutters and thinking it peaceful. Walking through it with a stolen map sewn into my coat and a census man somewhere behind me, I discovered it has another name entirely.

It is called being alone.

I had left by the coal chute. Not because I am dramatic—though Master Aldous would have argued that anyone who sews a copper plate into her coat lining has a flair for theater—but because Brann was watching the door. I had seen him from the window as I pulled the letter from Maren's wetland chart, seen him standing in the square like a man admiring architecture, his hat tipped back, his hands folded behind him with the patience of someone who has already won and is merely waiting for the paperwork.

He had watched me flee. And he had not followed.

That was the worst of it. A man who chases you might lose you. A man who watches you go already knows where you're bound—or believes he does—and simply walks.

* * *

Let me tell you what Aldous Iversen taught me about maps, because it matters now more than it ever did when he was alive and grumbling about my ink weights.

"A map is not the territory," he said, on some gray afternoon when the light through the workshop windows made everything look like it had been drawn in pencil. "The territory is what it is. The map is an argument about the territory. Every mapmaker chooses what to show and what to leave out, and those choices are where the power lives."

He tapped the great atlas then—the one whose final coastline I had inked alone three nights before my world cracked open. "Governments make maps for soldiers and tax collectors. I make them for people who need to disappear."

I had thought he was being poetic. Aldous was not a poetic man; he was a precise one, precise the way a blade is precise. He had spent forty years making beautiful atlases that sold to merchants and gentlemen, and underneath all of that, in the plates he never showed clients, he had been making something else: an atlas of the city's omissions. The storm drains the surveyors drew but the published charts omitted. The rights-of-way that predated every charter and were never formally extinguished, so that legally—barely, dustily—a footpath still ran across the courtyard of the Customs House. The rooftops connected by party walls built before fire codes separated them. The forgotten stairs inside the old salt warehouse that went down to a canal gate no one had locked since the war, because no one remembered it existed.

He called these plates his *honest* work. He kept them in a drawer that locked, and he told me once, in the only conversation we ever had about the Halvards, that a promise made in peacetime is the only kind worth keeping, because wartime makes liars of everyone and peacetime lets you choose.

I had chosen, seven years ago, without knowing I was choosing. Now the choosing had teeth.

* * *

The first rule of disappearing in Vellmark, according to Plate Nine, is that you do not use streets.

Streets are where lanterns are, where watchmen walk their rounds on schedules any apprentice could copy from the municipal charts—which is precisely how Aldous had copied them, years ago, and annotated them with the watch's actual patrol times, which apparently differed from the official ones by as much as twenty minutes in the poorer quarters. The city guards its paper truths jealously and its real ones not at all.

So I went down.

The storm drain under Cooper's Lane is marked on the 1811 survey and on nothing since. It runs forty paces beneath the street and empties into the canal through a grate that Aldous's notes described, in his small exact hand, as "rusted open since at least '29; rats disagreeable but not dangerous." The rats were indeed disagreeable. They scattered ahead of my boots in a rustling tide, and I confess I spoke to them—a woman crawling through a sewer with a national treasure against her ribs may be forgiven for wanting conversation—but they offered no intelligence beyond the obvious.

Forty paces of darkness, measured not by fear but by memory. I had memorized the plate. Of course I had memorized the plate; I had inked half of it myself during the years I was supposed to be grinding pigment and keeping accounts. Aldous used to test me. "Petra. The canal gate at the salt warehouse—which side does it hinge?" Left. "The party wall between the tannery and the cooperage—load-bearing or not?" Not, which is why the roof route works. "How many steps from the Customs House right-of-way to the fish market arch?" Eleven, and the twelfth is broken.

"You'll thank me someday," he said, and I said, "Yes, master," the way apprentices say yes, master, meaning *never*, meaning *you old tyrant*, meaning *I am seventeen and immortal and this is tedious*.

I thanked him in the dark under Cooper's Lane, ankle-deep in cold water, and the thanking felt like grief, which is what thanking usually feels like when it arrives too late.

* * *

I surfaced at the canal gate, pushed it open—it moved with a groan that sounded, in the silence, like a public announcement—and stood a moment in the reeking dark letting my eyes adjust.

Behind me, faint but distinct: boots on stone. Not running. Walking. Brann walked the way he questioned—unhurried, certain that the truth would come to him if he simply stood still enough.

But here is the thing about following a mapmaker through her own territory: you cannot follow the person; you can only follow the map. And unless Brann had seized the workshop's plates—and perhaps he had, perhaps the inspection had turned up everything after all—he was navigating by the published city, the argument the government had agreed to believe in. The published city said Cooper's Lane ended at a wall. The published city had no opinion about salt warehouse stairs.

I gave him the wall.

* * *

The rooftop route began above the tannery, and I will spare you the smells except to note that whatever else the night took from me, my sense of smell surrendered early and without dignity. The party wall between the tannery and the cooperage was exactly where Plate Twelve said it was, wide as a table, and I crossed it with the atlas's future pressed against my chest and Vellmark spread out below me like the thing it actually was rather than the thing its charts claimed.

From up there the city looked honest for once. You could see the lies. The harbor district glowed with the lamps of commerce, fat and confident. The quarter where the Halvard house had stood was darker—not ruined, just unlit, a hole in the city's attention shaped like a family. And out past the breakwater, the black water where ships went that carried exiles away and, if the smugglers were to be believed, sometimes carried letters back.

Elias was out there. Somewhere past that blackness, in whatever room exile had given him, waiting to learn whether a promise made when we were both different people still weighed anything.

And between him and me: one census taker with a walking pace like a closing fist, one burned archive, one betrayed route, and one apprentice whose letter I had found unsent and unreadable in his desk—I had looked, God forgive me, after the inspection, because Tomas covering for me so smoothly had felt less like loyalty than like a merchant appraising goods—and one copper plate sewn into a coat that was already cutting into my shoulders, because copper weighs what truth weighs.

Maren had been taken two nights ago. I had watched from a doorway as the fish carts were stopped at the gate, as she stood in the lantern light with her chin up and her hands visible and her eyes doing one quick sweep of the crowd—not searching for rescue, I understood later, but confirming that I wasn't in it. Confirming her investment hadn't been wasted. She'd sold routes and charts for thirty years and everyone knew she'd sell information eventually; she had simply held out long enough to sell it late.

They had her route. Which meant they had her map of me.

So the question, as I crouched on a cooperage roof with the cold coming off the water, was not whether Brann would find me. It was whether I could reach the harbor before his information reached his feet.

* * *

The salt warehouse stands at the end of Saltpeter Row, a vast blind building the city stopped using when the salt monopoly collapsed and never quite decided to demolish. Its upper floors are storage for nothing; its lower floor is storage for less. Inside, according to Plate Nine, a staircase descends in the northwest corner—"built for the tide-gate keepers, decommissioned 1822, door painted shut but paint is not mortar."

The paint was indeed not mortar. It was paint, and it peeled away under my knife in satisfying ribbons, and behind it was a door that opened onto eleven steps and a landing where the twelfth step was broken, exactly as promised, and below that a low brick tunnel running toward the harbor along a line no surveyor had bothered with since the crown last cared about tide-gate keepers.

I want to be clear about what this tunnel was. It was a mistake. It was a piece of infrastructure built for a purpose the city forgot, left intact because demolition costs money and forgetting is free. Vellmark is full of such things—rights-of-way nobody exercises, easements nobody remembers, doors that exist in deeds but not in minds. Aldous had spent forty years collecting them the way other men collect stamps, and he had done it, I finally understood, not as a hobby and not even as craft, but as insurance. As inheritance. He had known that someday the atlas would need rescuing, and he had built the rescue into the city itself decades in advance, patiently, in copper and ink, and then died before he could use a single mile of it.

The atlas was guiding itself out of the burning house. I was just the pair of legs it had borrowed.

I walked the tunnel counting steps to keep panic arithmetic at bay. Panic is very good at sums. It counts your enemies and multiplies. It calculates the distance to the harbor and divides by your own exhaustion. I countered it with Aldous's numbers instead—forty paces of drain, eleven steps, the tunnel's length given on the plate as six hundred and twelve feet, a figure so specific it had to be love.

* * *

The tunnel let out through a grate onto the shingle below the fish market arch, and there, for the first time all night, I saw the harbor open before me: black water, riding lights, the masts of the herring fleet leaning at their moorings like tired dancers.

And there, for the second time all night, I heard the boots.

He had not taken the wall. I don't know how he'd found the warehouse—perhaps he hadn't needed to; perhaps he had simply reasoned that a mapmaker would go to ground in a mapmaker's places, which is the flaw in every clever plan, that it is legible to anyone who can read. But there they were, unhurried, on the cobbles above the shingle, and when I flattened myself against the arch's shadow I saw his silhouette against the gaslight of Saltpeter Row, hat brim, folded hands, the whole patient architecture of him.

"Miss Petra," he said, conversationally, to the darkness, because of course he didn't know precisely where I was, but he knew the shape of the night as well as I did now, and the shape had only one exit. "You've cost me two constables, a fishwife, and a considerable amount of my superiors' patience. None of those will be missed. But I'll tell you what will be." He came down the shingle steps slowly, each footfall placed like a word in a sentence. "The boy. Elias Halvard. There is a ship out there tonight that means to take him papers, or take him money, or take him away, and my commission ends when he is in custody. Yours ends whenever you decide to stop running."

I pressed deeper into the arch. The plate dug into my ribs. Below the shingle, half-submerged at low water, the pilings of the old pier ran out into the black—the derelict pier, the one Maren's runners had used twice, the place where a reply had once been tucked into a crevice for me, smelling of tar and salt, in handwriting that had gone hard and thin since I'd last seen it.

*Proof,* Elias had written. *Not the atlas. Anyone can send the atlas. Send proof you aren't bait.*

I had come to give him his proof and my answer both: the plate itself, the one thing the government wanted badly enough to burn an archive over, handed over with nothing asked in return. That was the only signature I trusted.

Brann's boots crunched closer. Twenty feet. Fifteen.

"The remarkable thing," he continued, in the tone of a man discussing weather, "is that you could have fled the city weeks ago. Any port, any ship, gone. Instead you stayed and finished a coastline. Sent letters. Kept appointments with dead men's promises." Ten feet. "People who run want to escape, Miss Petra. You don't want to escape. You want to *deliver*. It's the most predictable thing about you."

He was right. That was the humiliation of it—he had read me like one of Aldous's charts, and every line was accurate, and knowing it changed nothing, because a promise doesn't stop weighing what it weighs just because your enemy has measured it.

Five feet. His shadow slid across the mouth of the arch.

And then the tide did what Aldous's maps said it would do, because of course it did; he'd charted that too, the low-water window at the derelict pier, ninety minutes when the mudflats lay exposed and a person could move along the pilings below the shingle where no boots could follow—ninety minutes that began, by my count, roughly four minutes ago.

I dropped over the arch's edge onto the shingle, rolled, and was moving along the seaward face of the sea wall before his shout finished echoing, boots sinking in wet sand, the black water sliding past on my left and the city's lamps receding on my right, and out ahead of me, far out at the end of the rotting pier, a single shuttered lantern blinked twice—

—the signal Maren had taught me, passed along before they took her, from a runner named Sif who owed me nothing and paid anyway—

—and behind me, on the shingle, Brann stopped shouting and started walking again, unhurried, patient, certain, because he no longer needed to catch me at all.

He only needed to see where I was going.
