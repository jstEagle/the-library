## 17. The Harbor Meeting

### Petra

The pier at Saltrow had been condemned twice, once by the harbor authority and once by God, and both parties had lost interest before finishing the job. Its pilings leaned like old men sharing a secret, and its planks were soft enough that every step was a negotiation. I had chosen it for exactly these qualities. No one inspects a place they've already written off.

I had been waiting an hour when I realized my hands wouldn't stop moving — smoothing the coat, touching the lump where the plate rode against my ribs, checking a pocket I'd checked eleven times already. Seven years of hiding in plain sight had taught me to be still. It hadn't taught me what to do with all this motion.

Maren's boy had brought the message back two nights ago, folded inside a herring invoice: *Saltrow pier, low tide Thursday, come alone, come dark.* Eleven words from a man I'd last seen as a fourteen-year-old stealing honey off the kitchen sill while I held the ladder and pretended not to notice.

He would be different now. Everyone said so. The Halvards who survived exile came back as something else, if they came back at all.

The tide was going out. It made a sound beneath the boards like slow breathing, like the sea deciding whether to keep me or not.

Then footsteps on gravel behind me, unhurried, and I understood that he had probably been watching me for most of that hour too. Of course he had. I'd have done the same.

"Petra Iversen." My name in his voice — deeper than I remembered, scraped at the edges — and then, drier: "Or whatever we're calling you this decade."

I turned. Elias Halvard stood at the landward end of the pier with his hands visible and empty, which told me more about his life since I'd seen him than any letter could. He was twenty-one and looked forty on a bad day, lean the way men get lean when hunger does the tailoring. But the eyes were the same ones over the honey pot. Watching everything. Deciding everything.

"Elias," I said, and found my voice steadier than my hands. "You've grown."

"You haven't," he said. "You're still standing in doorways waiting to see if you're welcome." He came forward a few steps, boots careful on the rotten planks. "That was always your gift, Petra. Making yourself small enough to survive in someone else's house."

"And yours was taking things off shelves," I said. "Look how well we've both applied ourselves."

Something flickered across his face — not quite a smile, but the ghost of one, haunting a ruin. "You got my letter."

"I got your question." I didn't move closer. Neither did he. The space between us was seven years wide and neither of us seemed willing to be the first to cross it. "You asked for proof I'm not bait."

"Yes."

"So ask."

* * *

### Elias

She'd changed less than I expected and more than I could afford to think about. That was the trouble with Petra Iversen — she had a face you could trust, calm as a chart legend, and I had learned in exile that trustworthy faces were the currency of every trap ever built. Brann's people had faces like hers. I knew because I'd been shown them. Photographs, sketches, descriptions read aloud by candlelight in a room where the walls were thinner than the lies.

*An apprentice at the mapmaker's workshop. Former servant of your father's household. If she contacts you—*

If she contacted me. She had contacted me. And here I was anyway, standing on a dying pier because some promise made in peacetime had crawled out of my father's grave and into my chest, and because — though I'd never write this to anyone — I wanted it to be her. I wanted the girl who held the ladder to be real. I wanted one thing from before the fall to have survived intact.

Which is precisely what a trap designer would count on.

"Proof," I said. "All right. When you left Maren's message, you gave the route through the fish carts. Tuesday's run went out through the north gate."

"Yes."

"It was stopped. Not searched — stopped. Held for two hours and let go. Someone wanted us to know they could." I watched her face. Nothing moved in it, but nothing moved in charts either, and charts could kill you if you misread them. "Two days later, you arrange a meeting at a pier with one road in and no road out. You're either the bravest fool in Vellmark or the best bait they've ever dressed."

"Those are my only options?"

"They're the only ones I can afford to consider." I took another step. The boards groaned under both of us now, complicit. "So. Convince me. And know that I've spent three years learning to hear lies, and everyone who tells them sounds sincere."

She considered me for a long moment. Then, instead of arguing — which is what I'd braced for, what I'd prepared answers against — she reached into her coat.

Every instinct I owned screamed at once. I almost drew the knife I wasn't supposed to be carrying.

But what she pulled out wasn't a weapon or a signal flare. It was a letter, creased soft with handling, sealed with wax I recognized before my mind finished recognizing it. My father's seal. The stag and the compass rose.

And beneath it, wrapped in oilcloth, flat and heavy and unmistakable even in the dark —

A copperplate printing plate. Engraved coastlines catching the moon.

My knees nearly went. I want that recorded somewhere, by someone honest: Elias Halvard, enemy of the crown, nearly wept on a condemned pier over a piece of engraved copper.

"This is—" I couldn't finish it. I knew exactly what it was. Every child raised in that house had heard about the forbidden plate, the map Aldous Iversen refused to finish for anyone, the one that showed what the Admiralty surveys actually found versus what the crown ordered published. The difference between those two maps was a war's worth of lies, drawn in ink.

"The atlas is elsewhere," Petra said quietly. "Finished. All of it. This is the last piece — the one Master Iversen forbade anyone to touch until the world deserved it." Her jaw tightened. "He died before he decided the world ever would. I decided for him. You can hate me for the presumption later; there's a queue."

"Why give it to me tonight? Why not send it out with Maren and stay safe?"

"Because a thing like this shouldn't travel blind," she said. "And because you asked for proof." She held out the letter. "Read it. Then decide what I am."

* * *

### Petra

He read it by matchlight, cupping the flame like a man used to wind. I watched him read my master's words — Lord Halvard's voice coming off the page after seven years, asking Aldous Iversen to keep faith, promising that the truth of coasts would outlive the politics of courts. Watching Elias read it was like watching a man drink water after years of thirst and trying not to show how badly he'd needed it.

His hands shook. Only slightly. Only at the edges. I knew that shake. I'd had it for three years, every time I opened the drawer.

"Well?" I said, when the match burned down toward his fingers.

He struck another without answering, read it again, and then folded it along its existing creases with a precision that hurt to watch — as if the letter were the only orderly thing left in his life and he intended to keep it that way.

"You hid this in a wetland chart," he said. "In the workshop. Rolled. Third shelf, behind the Dutch imports nobody buys."

Cold walked down my spine wearing my own coat. "How—"

"Tomas Vey wrote to a man who wrote to a man who wrote to me." His mouth twisted. "Your colleague has been selling descriptions of you for months, Petra. Your habits. Your hiding places. He kept your name out of his letters — I'll grant him that much sentiment — but he sold everything else. Including, two weeks ago, the fact that you'd moved something heavy into the wetland charts."

The pier swayed. Or I did. Hard to say.

"He told me you were bait," Elias went on, softer now. "He also told me you worked alone, slept four hours a night, and spent three years finishing a dead man's atlas for nothing. Bait doesn't do that. Bait gets paid." He looked up, and for a moment the hard exiled stranger receded and the boy with the honey pot looked out of him, starving. "I came tonight half expecting to arrest you myself. Instead you hand me the one object in Vellmark worth more than my head. Demanding nothing."

"Should I demand something?" I asked. "Would that be more convincing?"

"Infuriatingly, yes."

"Then consider it demanded retroactively. You owe me a debt of gratitude payable whenever convenient, interest accruing." I held out the plate with both hands, because it was heavier than copper and we both knew it. "Take it. Please. It's been trying to get out of my coat for weeks and I'm tired of it knowing me better than I know myself."

He took it. Carefully, the way you take a sleeping animal or a lit fuse.

And then — the strangest part, the part I would turn over in my mind for years afterward — he simply stood there holding his inheritance, the whole reason for his survival and his family's ruin and my three years of ink-stained invisibility, and said:

"What happens to you now?"

"Nothing," I said. "Nothing happens to me now. That's rather the point. I'm done."

"No, you're not."

"Elias—"

"You think they let it end?" He gestured back toward the city, a black mass studded with lantern-light, breathing smoke. "Tomas sold your habits to my people. Who do you imagine he sold them to first? A census bureau doesn't spend two months circling a mapmaker's shop over tax records. They know the atlas exists. They know it's finished. They know *you* finished it. The moment you handed me this plate, you became the most interesting woman in Vellmark, and I include the harbor master's wife, who is genuinely fascinating."

Despite everything — the hour, the rotting boards, the shaking I was doing my best to disguise as cold — I laughed. It came out cracked but real.

"That's the proof," I said. "If I were bait, I'd have better material prepared than jokes."

"If you were bait," he said, "you'd be funnier. They recruit the charming ones." He tucked the plate inside his coat where mine had lived, and I felt the strangest pang watching it go — three years of my life, leaving my body for the last time. Underneath it, I discovered, was just Petra. Smaller than advertised.

"We should move," he said. "Low tide ends in an hour, and there's a boat."

"A boat to where?"

"Out." He said it the way other men said *home*, and I hated how much it cost him to make it sound simple.

I turned to follow him down the pier — and stopped.

Because at the landward end, where the gravel met the weeds, a lantern had come alive. Just one. Just briefly. The kind of brief, deliberate light a man shows when he wants you to know the light exists.

Elias saw it too. I felt him go still beside me, felt the arithmetic running behind his eyes, fast and cold and seven years practiced.

"Petra," he said quietly. "How many roads lead to this pier?"

"One," I said. My mouth had gone dry as old paper. "One road in. You said so yourself. No road out."

Behind the lantern, in the dark, I heard the sound I'd been dreading for three chapters of my life without knowing it:

Boots. Many of them, on gravel, walking unhurried toward the water.

They weren't hurrying. They didn't need to.
