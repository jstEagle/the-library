## 22. A New Hand at the Table

The press in Gellhaven smells of ink and river water, which is to say it smells like home, if home were a thing you could carry in a crate and reassemble in a country that had never heard of Vellmark.

I had been in the city four days before I admitted this to myself. Four days of sleeping in the loft above the print shop with my boots on, because a woman who has spent two years learning to leave rooms quickly does not shed the habit just because the rooms have gotten kinder. Four days of watching Elias Halvard argue with typesetters in three languages and lose gracefully in all of them. And then, on the fifth morning, I came downstairs and found that someone had cleared a space for me at the worktable — a full third of it, dusted, lamp trimmed, a fresh pot of ink set out in a dish I did not recognize — and I understood that I had already been absorbed into something whether I consented or not.

Elias was standing at the press with his sleeves rolled, feeding sheets through one-handed while he read a letter with the other. He looked better than he had on the pier at Vellmark. Everyone looks better when they are not actively being arrested, but Elias had crossed some line between surviving and living that I could see now in the way he stood, weight on both feet, as though the floor were his.

"That's your space," he said, without looking up. "If you want it."

"I want the whole table."

"You can have the whole table after we finish the first atlas." He finally glanced over, and there it was — that wariness he still carried about me, worn smoother now but not gone. A man who has learned that promises can be bait does not entirely stop checking the hook. "I've been thinking about the first plate."

"Have you." I hung my coat on the peg by the door. The coat was new. The coat was also, if you knew where to look, the same cut as the old one, because I had discovered that a woman sews her habits into her clothes along with everything else, and I saw no reason to fight it.

"The southern coast of Vessing," Elias said. "Where your master's survey ends and the guesswork begins. Forty miles of shoreline nobody has properly charted since the war started, because the only people who go there now are smugglers, refugees, and revenue men, and none of them write anything down."

"And you want to chart it."

"I want *us* to chart it." He set the letter down. "Routes for the hunted, Petra. That was your phrase, not mine. Safe crossings, sympathetic harbors, which villages will hide you and which will sell you. An atlas for people the official maps pretend don't exist. But it starts where every honest map starts — with a coastline somebody actually walked."

I looked at the cleared space on the table. At the ink. At the lamp someone had trimmed without being asked, which meant either Elias or the widow Voss who ran the shop floor, and either answer meant the same thing: they had decided I belonged here before I had.

"You understand," I said, "that the last coastline I inked got a workshop burned down."

"I'm counting on it. You come with a reputation now."

"It's not the kind of reputation that helps."

"It's exactly the kind that helps. Nobody burns an anonymous woman's maps twice. They burn them once, publicly, badly, and then spend the next decade being embarrassed about it." He smiled — the first easy smile I'd seen from him, and it changed his whole face, made him briefly into the boy I'd carried water for in another life. "Besides. Brann told me himself, before they put me on the boat. He said the crown wants quiet endings now. Quiet endings need somewhere quiet to end. That's what we're building. Somewhere quiet."

I did not say what I thought, which was that Brann had said no such thing to Elias, because Brann had said it to me through the bars of a cell window while the workshop burned, and even a bitter man tells the truth once or twice a year by accident. Instead I sat down at the table, uncapped the ink, and pulled a blank sheet toward me.

"What scale?" I asked.

Elias blinked. "What?"

"For the coast. What scale do you want it drawn?"

He came around the table and looked down at the empty sheet, and I watched him understand what I was really asking — not about cartography but about authorship, about whose hand would appear in the lines. Seven years ago I had been a servant girl carrying ink water for great men's pacts. Three years ago I had finished a dead man's atlas in secret, wearing anonymity like a second coat. Two years ago I had traded myself to a bureau to buy a stranger's freedom, and the strangest part of that bargain, the part I still hadn't said aloud, was how little I had minded giving up the hiding, once the choice was mine.

"Half inch to the mile," Elias said quietly. "Your hand, start to finish. And sign it."

"Sign it?"

"The atlas publishes under three names. Aldous Iversen. Elias Halvard. Petra Iversen." He said my name the way you set down something heavy — carefully, and with relief. "If the crown comes for this one too, let them know precisely whose work they're burning. Let them read the names in exile papers for the rest of their natural lives."

* * *

It took me three days to draw the first line.

Not because the line was difficult. Because I kept stopping to look at my own hand doing it.

You would think, after everything, that the act of inking a coastline would be nothing to me. I have drawn thousands of miles of coast that I never saw, coasts invented by merchants' optimism and admirals' vanity, coasts that existed only because Master Aldous said they should and I made them so. But this line was different, and it took me until the second evening to understand why: every other line I ever drew was drawn to be believed by someone else. This one was drawn to be *used*. Somewhere south of Vessing there was a cove — I knew it from Maren's runners' talk, from the fish-cart gossip that had kept me alive — where boats put in after dark and families stepped off onto stones with everything they owned in bundles. If I drew that cove wrong, people drowned. If I drew it right, people lived, and never knew whose hand had held the pen.

There is a peculiar loneliness in that. There is also, I found, a peculiar strength.

On the third day Elias brought me coffee — actual coffee, Gellhaven being a port that still traded with everyone, which is the chief virtue of ports — and stood behind my shoulder looking at the half-finished coast.

"The headland's wrong," he said.

I didn't turn around. "Which headland."

"The one at the river mouth. It bulges. In '43 the survey showed it cut back almost straight."

"In '43 the survey was done from a revenue sloop that couldn't approach the shore without getting shot at, so they drew what they could see from two miles out and filled in the rest with hope." I tapped the paper. "Maren's runner Torvald wintered in that river mouth two seasons running. He says the headland erodes visibly every storm season. The bulge is current. The straight line is nostalgia."

Elias was quiet a moment. Then he laughed — short, surprised, real. "Whose charts did you learn skepticism on?"

"Aldous taught me the drawing. Maren taught me the doubting." I dipped the pen. "Between the two of them I learned that every map is an argument, and the winner is whoever was standing closest to the ground."

"That should go in the preface."

"The preface is going to say a great many things I intend to make you write yourself."

* * *

The letter came in November, which in Gellhaven means rain that falls sideways and a general agreement among the population to stay indoors and drink. I was alone in the shop — Elias had gone upriver to negotiate paper stock, the widow Voss had gone home to her rheumatism — when the boy from the harbor came in soaked to the collar and handed me an envelope with no return address.

I knew the hand before I broke the seal. I had spent enough nights forging Aldous's correspondence to know handwriting the way priests know scripture.

*Petra* — it began, in a slanted, careful script —

*I am writing to tell you something I should perhaps have said on the pier, but the pier had armed men on it, and I find sincerity difficult under fire.*

*They gave me back my father's letters. All of them, in a crate, with an apology so bureaucratic it may have been sincere. Among them is the pact he made with Aldous Iversen — the original, signed, sealed in wax I remember watching melt. I sat up all night reading it. Do you know what struck me? Not the terms. The date. They signed it eleven days before the first mobilization order. Eleven days. They thought they had time. Everyone always thinks they have time.*

*You kept the promise anyway. You kept it with no proof it was wanted, no payment, and every reason to believe it would destroy you, and it very nearly did. I have thought about what to call that. Debt doesn't cover it. Gratitude is too small a coin. So I will simply say: the atlas bears your name now, in print, permanently, in a form no bureau can amend. Whatever else happens, that sentence exists.*

*And now the request, because you will have noticed by now that I am my father's son and cannot open my mouth without one. The southern route needs surveyors. People who can walk a coast and remember it. I know three such people in the borderlands, and they trust no one, least of all strangers with clean nails and good pens. But they might trust a woman whose name is on a banned atlas. Infamy opens doors that respectability cannot.*

*Come south when the ice breaks. Bring your hand and your skepticism. Bring the argument about headlands; I want to hear you make it to someone whose life depends on losing it.*

*In debt and in print,*
*Elias Halvard*

I read it twice. Then I folded it along its original creases, went to the drawer where I kept my things — few enough that the drawer echoed — and put it beside the other letter, the peacetime promise in Aldous's hand that had started all of this. The two letters lay together like bookends holding up nothing yet, which is, I suppose, how most books start.

Outside, the rain threw itself against the windows with the enthusiasm of something that had nowhere better to be. Inside, the first coastline dried on its board, forty miles of Vessing shore rendered true by a smuggler's memory and a dead man's training, waiting for its plate, its press, its thousand copies scattered into pockets and floorboards and false bottoms across a continent that preferred not to know what happened to the people it exiled.

I lit the lamp, sat back down at my third of the table, and began the rivers.

Because here is what I understood, sitting there in the smell of ink and river water, in the lamplight, in the plain sight I had chosen instead of the hiding I had survived: Aldous Iversen had left me an inheritance, and for three years I had thought it was a burden — an atlas to finish, a promise to keep, a war of paper I never asked to fight. It took a burned workshop and a cell window and a stranger's freedom bought with my own surrender to show me what he had actually left me. Not a debt. A craft. A way of seeing the world as it is and drawing it anyway, knowing that the truth is contraband and drawing it anyway, knowing they will burn it and drawing it anyway, because paper is patient and names are permanent and somewhere, someday, someone fleeing in the dark will unfold a chart by candlelight and live because a woman she will never meet refused to draw the pretty version.

That is power. It is not the loud kind. It is the kind that fits in a coat lining.

The ice would break in March. The borderlands were a hundred miles south and a lifetime away from any census taker's questions. And when I got there, I would walk the coasts myself, boots in the mud, salt in the ink, and every line I drew would carry three names — a dead man's, an exile's, and mine.

I dipped the pen. Outside, the rain kept on. Inside, a river found its way to the sea.
