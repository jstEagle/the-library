## 1. The Weight of Water

There is a mathematics to drowning that the Meridian Trading Company never put in its ledgers, though God knows they put everything else in them.

One minute underwater buys you perhaps two dozen strokes with a hand rake. Two minutes and your vision starts to silver at the edges, pretty as frost on a window. Three minutes and you begin to have opinions about your father's debts that are not entirely rational. I have never gone past three. Not because I couldn't. Because I'd worked out early that the company would bill my mother for the funeral, and she had coughed her way through enough winters without adding my coffin to her accounts.

So: one hundred and eighty seconds, give or take a prayer. That was the whole of my fortune each time I went down off the jetty at Port Cavanagh, and I spent it the way the company wanted—filling the basket with mother-of-pearl shell, hauling up on the rope so old Tam could crank the windlass, then going down again until my fingers went white as the shell itself and stopped belonging to me.

The tide was low that morning in March of 1898, which meant the water over the shell beds was shallow and warm as bathwater gone lukewarm, and it meant I could see my own hands clearly, which I generally tried not to do. A diver who looks at her own hands too long starts thinking about what those hands could be doing if they belonged to somebody else. Writing letters, maybe. Or holding something other than a rake. Useless thoughts. The water doesn't pay for useless thoughts.

"Fourteen minutes down," Tam called when I came up the last time before the midday bell, and he said it like a man reporting the weather, which was exactly right. Fourteen minutes. In fourteen minutes I had earned—by Calloway's own figures, chalked on the office slate where all of us could admire our industry—fourpence against a debt of sixty-two pounds eleven shillings, owed by Edmond Galloway, deceased at sea these three years past, debt inherited entire by his widow and daughter per the terms of contract clause nine, sub-clause four, which I could recite the way other girls recited hymns.

Sixty-two pounds eleven shillings. At fourpence a morning, allowing for rain and funerals and the days my lungs gave out, I would clear that debt sometime around the coronation of whichever poor soul followed Victoria to the throne. I did this sum often. It was a kind of hobby. Some girls embroidered; I did arithmetic that always ended in despair, and I did it neatly.

I sat on the jetty planks wringing out my hair while Tam coiled rope beside me, and beyond him the harbor lay flat as hammered tin, all the way out to the reef line where the water turned from green to a blue so deep and indifferent it looked like a different country. Which, in a sense, it was. Everything past the reef belonged to ships and weather and men with names on brass plates. Everything inside it belonged to Meridian.

"You're getting faster," Tam said.

"I'm getting older."

"Same thing, out here." He squinted at me with his one good eye—the other he'd given to an oyster bed in '89, and the company had docked his wages for the season it took him to heal, on the principle that a half-blind diver raked less shell. There was no clause for lost eyes. I checked once. "You want to watch that, girl. Getting fast just means they raise the quota."

Tam was the only person in camp who gave advice worth the salt it was cured in, so I nodded and filed it away next to all his others, in the drawer marked Things I Already Knew But Pretended Not To.

* * *

The walk home from the beds took twenty minutes along the shingle road, past the drying sheds where the shell steamed and stank like a thousand drowned moons, past the company store with its windows full of goods none of us could buy on credit because credit was how Meridian ate people—politely, in installments. Past the church, which was Anglican, and the smaller building behind it, which was nothing at all, and the quarters where the islander divers slept six to a room and were spoken about in the plural, always, as if they were weather. *The islanders are quiet this season. The islanders don't complain.* Men with names like Manu and Teiki and old Joseph, who had taught me to hold my breath when I was nine years old by making a game of it, and whom I had known for a decade and still could not tell you, honestly, whether they were free to leave. They signed papers. Everyone signed papers. That was what paper was *for*.

Our house stood at the end of the track where the scrub took over from the shingle—a word I use generously for a box of iron and timber with a lean like a drunk leaving church. Smoke stood out of the chimney, thin and wrong-colored, the gray that means damp wood or a coal fire dying. My mother's fire was always dying. She fed it the way she fed herself, grudgingly, a piece at a time, saving the rest for later, for someone else, for a day she seemed to believe was coming when it would be needed more than now.

"Mum," I said, ducking through the door. "It's me."

She was in the chair by the window with the mending in her lap, and she looked up with that smile she kept for me, the good one, and then she bent her head back to the shirt she was sewing and coughed into her shoulder, twice, deep, the sound of it like a branch cracking somewhere in the middle of a tree.

The cough had come north with us the year Father's boat went down—or rather it had arrived the winter after, settling into her chest the way fog settles into a harbor, and it had never lifted. Dr. Fennimore in town charged five shillings a visit, which was more than I made in a week of good tides, so Mum treated herself with boiled eucalyptus and stubbornness, and I told myself it was only a cough, the way you can tell yourself anything if the alternative has teeth.

"There's bread," she said, when she had her breath back. "And there was a letter."

My hands stopped moving on their own, wet hair dripping onto the floorboards. Letters were not a thing that happened to us. Letters cost money and implied correspondents, and we had neither to spare.

"From the company?"

"No, love." Something moved across her face, quick as a fish under a boat. "From nobody. An advertisement. For corsets." She held up the paper, and indeed it was an advertisement for corsets, printed in Sydney, promising ladies a silhouette such as nature intended. "I thought you might like to look at the drawings. Silly, but pretty."

I looked at the drawings. They showed women with waists like wasps and spines like question marks, standing in attitudes of perfect leisure, and I thought: whoever drew this has never met the sea. Then I folded it away carefully, because Mum had saved it for me and pretending to want things was a kindness we traded back and forth like currency, and in our house it was the only currency that never ran short.

"How were the beds?" she asked.

"Thin near the point. Good past the marker buoy." I hung my diving dress on its nail. "Calloway's raised the quota again. Half a ton by month's end."

"He can't do that."

"He can do whatever's written down, Mum. That's what writing-down is *for*." I said it lightly. I'd learned to say most things lightly; weight sank you, and sinking was a professional hazard. But she heard underneath it anyway, because mothers keep sonar where other people keep hearts.

She set down the mending. Her hands, I noticed—noticed properly, the way you notice a thing you've been refusing to notice for months—were thinner than they'd been at Christmas, the knuckles standing up like knots in driftwood.

"Freya." She paused, and I watched her decide, visibly, between two sentences, and choose the safer one. "Don't go past the buoy again. The current's turning with the season."

"I haven't been past the buoy."

"I know." She picked up the mending. "I know you haven't."

Which meant she knew I had, and knew why—because the deep bed past the marker held the thick shell, and thick shell meant extra weight against the quota, and the quota was all that stood between us and Calloway's gentle pen moving through the ledger to add interest, penalties, adjustments, the little annotations by which a family's future was quietly eaten. She knew, and she didn't say, and I didn't confirm, and this was the architecture of our household: load-bearing silences everywhere, and both of us walking carefully across the floors of them.

I ate the bread. It was stale. It was the best thing I'd tasted all week, because hunger is honest that way.

* * *

Here is what I believed, at nineteen years old, in March of 1898, sitting on our doorstep watching the sun go down over a harbor that owned me:

History was not for the likes of the Galloways. History was a ship that came into port with admirals aboard, and governors, and company men with clean collars, and it took on cargo and left again, and whatever it carried away—shell, or men, or years—it carried away for purposes of its own. We were not passengers on history. We were ballast. And ballast does well to keep quiet and sit low in the hold, because anything that rises gets thrown overboard.

This wasn't bitterness, or not only. Bitterness implies surprise, and I had never once been surprised. It was more a kind of seamanship. You read the water you're given. You trim your sails to it. You do not shake your fist at the wind, because the wind has never once cared, and shaking fists wastes breath, and breath, in my trade, was money.

I was good at being unremarkable. I want that understood, because later—well. Later there will be people who say I was brave, and I'd like the record to show that bravery was never my plan. My plan was to be a gray thing on a gray shore, to dive and haul and count and keep my eyes down and grow old at the exact speed required to outlast a debt, and then die small and dry and unmolested by anyone important.

That was the plan. I had held to it for years with the discipline of a woman counting seconds underwater, which is to say: absolutely, and while slowly blacking out.

Because here is the other thing, the thing under the thing, the thing I kept in the deepest part of the hold where even I didn't go often: some mornings, coming up off the shell beds with my lungs burning and the light breaking green above me, I would think—and the thought would arrive whole, unasked, like a body surfacing—*someone should write this down. Someone should write down what fourteen minutes costs. Someone should write down old Joseph's name, and Tam's eye, and what the islanders' quarters smell like in the wet season, and what my mother's cough sounds like at three in the morning, and what it is to spend your whole life as a line item.*

Someone should write it down. Because if no one wrote it down, then it hadn't happened, and if it hadn't happened, then it would happen forever.

Then I'd break the surface, and Tam would call the time, and the thought would go back under where I kept it, wrapped in canvas like the shell, weighted down like the strongbox of some drowned man.

Everyone has a strongbox, I suppose, though most of us sink ours where no one will ever dive.

* * *

I dreamed that night of my father, which was unusual, because I had trained myself out of dreaming of him years ago, the way you train yourself out of any expensive habit.

In the dream he was on the water in the old boat, the *Ada*, and he turned around and said something, and in dreams you always know what people say even though their lips make no sound, and what he said was a number. Sixty-two pounds, eleven shillings. He said it kindly, the way he'd said everything, my father, who could make a debt sound like a lullaby.

I woke before dawn with the taste of salt in my mouth and lay listening to my mother breathe in the next room. Long pause, then the crack of the branch, then the long pause again. I counted her breaths the way I counted everything. Eleven to a minute. Down from thirteen in the summer.

Outside, the harbor had begun to turn pewter, and the first gulls were working the tide line, and somewhere down the hill the company bell rang the hour and men began to move toward the sheds like water finding its level.

I got up. I lit the fire. I put the kettle on and thought about fourpence and sixty-two pounds and the distance between them, which is a distance you can measure in years or in fathoms, depending on which direction you're drowning from.

It was a Thursday. I remember that because Thursdays were ledger days at the Meridian office—the day Mr. Calloway balanced the books and posted the accounts, and the day I sometimes carried the shell tallies up to the counter, since I had the neatest figures of anyone in camp and neatness, in Calloway's world, was next to godliness and considerably cheaper.

I did not know, walking up that hill with the tally book under my arm and the sun coming up hot on the back of my neck, that I would ever open the ledger wider than my own page. Why would I? Curiosity, as Calloway liked to remark, was a luxury, and luxuries were how families like ours drowned.

But the sea gives nothing warning. Neither, as it turns out, does arithmetic. All I knew was that the tide was low, the quota was high, and my mother's breaths had dropped to eleven a minute—and that I had sixty-two pounds, eleven shillings to earn, and a lifetime of unremarkable days ahead of me in which to do it.

I was wrong about the lifetime. I was wrong by about a fortnight.

But I didn't know it yet, and so I walked up the hill to the company office with my head down and my figures straight, invisible as ever, carrying the morning's numbers like a good little ballast girl—never guessing that the books waiting on Mr. Calloway's desk already held my name in places I had never signed, and that the water I'd been diving all my life had been keeping secrets the whole time, patient as the tide, waiting for someone foolish enough to finally look down and count.
