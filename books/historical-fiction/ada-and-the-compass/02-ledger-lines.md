## 2. Ledger Lines

There is a particular smell to a company office that no amount of sea air will ever wash away. It is ink and lamp oil and the ghost of men's sweat pressed into chair leather, and it says, before anyone speaks a word: *the numbers were here first, and the numbers will be here after you are gone.*

I had been copying figures in the Meridian Trading Company's port office for eleven months by then. It was considered a mercy of sorts — my mother's lungs had gone soft as wet paper, and diving four hours a day was killing me faster than it was paying off my father's debt, so Mr. Calloway had moved me indoors at half wages and called it kindness. The other divers' children looked at me with something close to pity. I understood why. A girl at a desk was a girl who had stopped fighting, and everyone knew what happened to girls who stopped fighting.

What they didn't know was that I had discovered arithmetic.

Arithmetic, you understand, is not taught to contract labor beyond what is needed to count shells into a weighmaster's pan. But nobody can stop a girl from counting on her own time, and I had spent years counting my father's debt down in breathless minutes underwater, so when I sat at Calloway's second desk with a fresh nib and a ledger open under my hands, the numbers did what numbers do for people like me: they kept me honest, and they expected the same of everything else.

Mostly they obliged. Rations drawn, rations owed, shell landed, weight deducted, interest compounded with the gentle relentlessness of tide. It was all perfectly legible, perfectly cruel, and I copied it out in my neat schoolroom hand without feeling much of anything, because feeling anything about Meridian's ledgers was like feeling something about the weather.

Then, on a Tuesday in the wet season, the numbers lied to me.

* * *

It started small, the way most dangerous things do. I was transcribing the quarterly ration requisition — flour, salt pork, tea, the sacred tobacco — against the diver rolls, because Calloway liked the two columns reconciled before he arrived, and I was the sort of employee who gave him nothing to be displeased about. That was the whole architecture of my survival. Be accurate. Be quiet. Be furniture.

The roll listed forty-one active divers. The ration column totaled provisions for forty-six.

Five extra men. Five men eating Meridian flour who appeared nowhere on the active rolls.

I told myself it was an error first, because that is what sensible people tell themselves. Clerks make mistakes; a man gets transferred between stations and his name lags behind his appetite. So I went back through the previous quarter, and the one before that, and found the same five-man surplus, quarter after quarter, going back two years. Errors, I have learned, do not keep such faithful appointments.

So I checked the deaths.

This was the part I would later understand I should never have done, though I confess the doing of it felt, at the time, less like trespass than like finishing a sum. The death register sat in the same cabinet as everything else, because Meridian did not imagine anyone would look. Why would they? Dead men cost the company nothing and grieve no one whose grief required accounting. Their entries were brief. Boat lost in the roads. Fever. Taken by shark, one entry said, with the flat economy of a man noting a broken crate.

Fourteen names in three years. And there, in the margins beside six of them, in Calloway's own small precise hand, the notation: *rations continue.*

Rations continued. For dead men. Drawn monthly, signed for by initials I did not recognize, shipped — I checked the manifests, because by now I could not have stopped if the building had caught fire — shipped north, to Thursday Island, to places with names that meant nothing to me except that none of them were graveyards, because dead men do not eat flour and dead men do not sign chits and dead men, whatever else may be said of them, do not get shipped anywhere.

I sat very still with my pen lifted off the paper, because a drop of ink had gathered at the nib and I understood, in some animal way beneath thinking, that where it fell mattered.

Six men, drowned on paper, alive somewhere in the arithmetic. Or — and this thought arrived unbidden and cold — six men dead in every way that counted, their deaths merely *recorded* wrong, so that someone could go on drawing their rations. Either way, the ledger was lying. And a ledger lies only for its keeper.

"Miss Galloway."

I want it recorded that I did not jump. I turned my head with the unhurried indifference of the truly innocent, which took considerable practice, and found Mr. Calloway standing at my shoulder with a cup of tea he had not offered to share.

Mr. Calloway was perhaps fifty, grey as driftwood, with the kind of face that made you work to remember it afterward and failed every time. He wore his courtesy like a diver wears lead boots: correctly, and for ballast. He had never once raised his voice in my hearing. He had never once needed to.

"Mr. Calloway," I said. "The reconciliation's nearly done."

"Mm." He set the tea on his desk and came around behind my chair, and I felt the particular pressure of a man reading over a woman's shoulder, which is a pressure you feel along the back of the neck. His eyes went down the columns. I watched him the way you watch a shark cruise past — motionless, polite, cataloguing.

"You've been thorough," he said.

"The columns wouldn't balance otherwise."

"No. They wouldn't." He reached past me and turned back two pages, to the death register, and laid one finger — dry, clean, nails trimmed with terrible precision — beside the marginal note *rations continue.* "Tell me what you see there."

There was no safe answer. I understood that immediately, the way you understand the water has closed over your head. Any answer was a confession of having looked. Having looked was itself the crime; the content of what I'd seen was almost incidental.

"I see a clerical inconsistency," I said. "Probably carried forward from an old error."

"Clerical." He tried the word out, tasting it. "You know, Miss Galloway, when I moved you from the water to this office, several people advised against it. They said a diver's daughter has sand in her blood. That she'll grow restless at a desk. That restlessness in a contracting family leads to trouble." He straightened, and folded his hands, and smiled at me with real warmth, which was the most frightening thing he could have done. "I defended you. I said, Freya Galloway is the most reasonable young woman in this camp. She understands her position. She knows that her family's account stands at—" and here he recited the figure from memory, to the shilling, my father's debt, the number I had spent seven years drowning toward, "—and that she is a sensible contributor to its reduction."

"I try to be."

"Yes. You do. You copy beautifully. Your figures are clean." He paused. "Clean figures are worth more than gold in this office, Miss Galloway. Because figures are all we have. The shell rots, the boats sink, the men — well. Men are unreliable in every direction. But the figures remain. The figures are the company's memory." He leaned in slightly, and dropped his voice to the register of a confessor or a physician delivering a diagnosis. "And when a clerk begins to *remember on her own account*, we have a difficulty. Not an accusation. A difficulty. Do you see the distinction?"

"I believe so."

"Curiosity," Mr. Calloway said, "is a debt too. It accrues. Quietly, at compound rates, just like everything else in this world. Most people never notice the interest until it's called in." He picked up his tea and sipped, and the smile returned, mild as morning. "I'd hate to call in yours. You're far too useful. Now — the quarterly abstract wants finishing by Friday. I'll take it as read that the death register holds nothing further of interest to you."

It was not a question. Questions, from Mr. Calloway, permitted answers.

"Nothing further," I said.

"Splendid." And he went into his inner room and closed the door, and I heard him pour himself a second cup, and the ordinary sounds of an ordinary afternoon resumed around me like water resuming its level after a stone has sunk out of sight.

* * *

I finished the abstract. Of course I finished the abstract; I am telling you how I survived, and survival meant giving him nothing, ever, not even the shape of a thought left showing on my face. My hand did not shake. I checked it twice, held it up to the lamplight, and it was as steady as any hand in the territory, and I felt a strange cold pride in that steadiness, as though my body belonged to some wiser sister who had taken over while I stood inside myself and screamed.

Because here is the thing. Here is the thing I had believed all my life, the doctrine my mother fed me with my supper and sewed into my collars: *be unremarkable and you will be untouched.* History, my mother said, belongs to admirals and company men; our family's business is to stay out of its way, the way small fish stay out of a net's path, invisible in the murk. Don't look at them. Don't be seen looking. Be furniture, be weather, be nothing, and the great machinery of the world will pass over you like a shadow.

And I had done it. Seven years of it. I had made myself so small that even my own grief had learned to whisper.

But Mr. Calloway had stood behind my chair and named my father's debt to the shilling without opening a book, and spoken of curiosity accruing interest, and smiled at me with warmth, and I understood — sitting there in the lamplight with my flawless copy drying before me — that invisibility had never once protected us. It had only made us easier to overlook while the accounts were arranged. He had known exactly who I was the entire time. He had measured me, priced me, filed me, and today he had simply shown me the drawer where he kept me. *Curiosity is a debt too.* Meaning: I already knew what you know. Meaning: I have always known. Meaning: the net does not chase the fish, Miss Galloway. The net is simply the shape of the water.

That night I walked home along the tideline with the wet wind pushing at my back, and my mother's coughing came through the wall of our hut ahead of her lamplight, thin and doubled over, and I stood outside listening to it and thought about the six names in the margin. *Rations continue.* Somewhere those men ate, or were eaten, and the paper called them dead, and my father's boat had gone down in these same waters three years ago, and I had counted every minute of every dive since as payment on a debt that had outlived the man who incurred it.

I had believed the ledger because believing the ledger was safer than believing anything else. Numbers don't lie, my father used to say, back when he was alive the first time. Numbers can't lie.

He was wrong. I had seen where they lie. They lie in the margins, in a dead man's handwriting, in small grey initials that signed for flour that fed men the company had killed on paper — or worse, hadn't.

I went in to my mother. I lit the lamp. I said nothing, because saying things is how people drown on land.

But I did not sleep, and near dawn, watching the grey come up over the harbor, I caught myself doing the thing I had trained out of myself for seven years: wondering. Wondering who signs for a dead man's rations. Wondering whether the harbor master's brother — lost off the Warrior reef the winter before last, fever, the register said — was eating somebody's flour right now, somewhere north, in a place with no graveyard.

Wondering costs interest. Mr. Calloway had said so himself.

The next morning, a boat came in on the mail tender, and among the few passengers who stumbled down the jetty into our light was a stranger — big, sun-scalded, moving like a man whose body remembered violence the way mine remembered water — carrying nothing but a canvas satchel he held the way a priest holds a book.

And he stood at the foot of the gangway, scanning the beach as though he were looking for someone specific, and when his eyes passed over me they stopped. Just for a moment. The way a man stops when he sees a familiar face in an unfamiliar country.

I looked away first. I was, after all, furniture.

But I felt his gaze stay on me all the way up the beach, patient as tide, and I thought: *curiosity is a debt too.*

His had just begun to accrue. Mine, I feared, was already called in.
