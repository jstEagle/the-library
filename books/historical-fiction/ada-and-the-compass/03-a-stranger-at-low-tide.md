## 3. A Stranger at Low Tide

The steamer put me ashore on a jetty that seemed embarrassed to exist—pilings gone soft as bread, planks the colour of old teeth, and a smell rising off the mudflats that I can only describe as honest. Rot, salt, fish guts, and under it all something sweeter and more rotten: money. That was my first lesson about Meridian. The company didn't hide what it did. It simply made sure you were too tired or too hungry to notice.

I stood there with a satchel against my ribs and let Australia introduce itself.

Two oceans I'd crossed to reach this particular nowhere. Out of Singapore on a tramp freighter whose captain charged me double for the berth and half again for pretending not to see me weep in it. Before that, Colombo, where a doctor told me the scar tissue on my hands would ache every cold morning until I died, and that I should be grateful it wasn't worse. Gratitude is a currency I've learned to counterfeit well.

The scars are from fire, mostly. A warehouse in Batavia that burned one night with three men inside and a company's paperwork inside them. I got out with the letters and without the skin of my palms. Some trades take their fee up front.

But I'm getting ahead of myself, which is a habit of mine when I'm frightened, and I was very frightened standing on that jetty, though I doubt anyone watching would have known it. Fear looks different on men like me. It looks like stillness. It looks like a man studying a mudflat with tremendous interest while his heart tries to climb out through his throat.

A boy was mending nets near the jetty's foot, and boys who mend nets know everything, so I sat down beside him on an overturned dinghy and asked my first question of this country.

"Fine harbour," I said, which it wasn't.

"It'll do," said the boy, not looking up.

"I'm looking for people named Galloway."

His hands stopped. Only for a moment—a stutter in the rhythm of the mesh—but I've spent years learning to read small hesitations, because large ones are easy to fake. He looked at me then, quick and sideways, the way you look at a snake you hope is dead.

"Divers' camp," he said. "Past the shell heaps. But nobody here knows the Gallaways."

He said *nobody* knowing full well he meant *somebody*. He meant: whoever asks after them had best have a reason. I thanked him and left a coin on the dinghy, and he watched me go with the expression of a boy composing exactly what he'd say tonight, and to whom.

So. The town already knew someone might come asking. Which meant either my friend had been careless in his last months—and he hadn't been; dying had made him careful as a spider—or someone else had sent word ahead. I walked past the shell heaps doing arithmetic I didn't like.

* * *

Let me tell you about the man whose errand brought me here, because a letter delivered without its history is just paper.

His name was Daniel Galloway, and I met him seven years ago in a hospital ward in Thursday Island, both of us wrecked by the same industry in different currencies—he by debt, I by a fire. He had a diver's shoulders and a clerk's hands and eyes that went through you to the wall behind, cataloguing exits. We played cards for buttons because neither of us had money worth gambling. He lost cheerfully and often, which is how I knew he was honest. Dishonest men win.

It took him two years to tell me his story, and he told it in pieces, the way you hand over something breakable. How he'd been a diver for the Meridian Trading Company, contracted into a debt that grew no matter how much shell he brought up—the books always heavier than the baskets. How the boats came back light some seasons and the pay sheets came back heavy anyway. How he saw a thing one night off a place called Viper Reach that he couldn't unsee: a lugger running dark, men going aboard who never appeared on any manifest, and a diver's body going over the side that had been breathing ten minutes before. And how he understood, in the way a man understands a shark's shadow rather than the shark, that the only way out of Meridian was to be dead in their books.

So he drowned. On paper. A boat lost with all hands in a squall, insurance paid, debt closed, wife widowed, daughter orphaned on paper too. And then he lived, badly, guiltily, in the margins of ports where nobody asked names, sending what money he could home through channels that smelled of brine and bribery, and carrying in his head the names and dates and figures that could hang a company.

For years he carried it alone. Then his lungs began to fail—not the diver's lung, he said, the other kind, the kind that starts inside—and he looked at me across a card table scattered with buttons and said, "Ash, if I die holding this, they win twice. Once taking the islanders, once burying the truth with me."

He wrote for eleven days. Letters, a statement, a copy of pages he'd memorised and copied out longhand in three different hiding places so that no single seizure could kill them all. He sealed each envelope and addressed them in a slanted, urgent hand, and the last thing he gave me was not the satchel but a look.

"You'll want to read them," he said. "Don't. Not the girl's. A man can defend his own secrets. Hers aren't mine to carry."

So I carried them unread, which is harder than it sounds. A satchel of sealed envelopes will keep a man honest or make him curious, and I've been both by turns across two oceans and four ports. In Colombo a customs officer held the bag for a night and I didn't sleep, imagining the wax broken, the words loose in the world. In Singapore a wharf rat tried to cut the strap and got my boot instead, and got the scar on his shin to remember it by.

And now here I stood in a town with one street, three taverns, and a company office built of stone while everything else leaned on timber—the way power always builds, thick and low, so you understand it isn't going anywhere.

* * *

I found the divers' camp toward evening, past the shell heaps that towered like pale dunes, reeking sweetly of death and lime. The camp was rows of huts on stilts above the mud, laundry strung between them like signal flags, and children everywhere—barefoot, quick, silent in the way of children who have learned that being noticed is expensive.

I asked my question carefully this time. A woman mending a sail told me the Galloway house was the third past the water tank, and then told me, unprompted, that she didn't know anything else about anybody, which is camp language for *I know everything and I've decided you're less dangerous than interesting.*

The third hut past the water tank. Smoke from the chimney. A woman's coughing audible from thirty yards—deep, wet, the sound of lungs filing complaints they intend to win. And on the step, hauling water, a girl.

I knew her at once, though I'd never seen her. Not from any likeness to a photograph—I'd never been shown one—but by elimination and by the set of her shoulders. She carried two buckets like they weighed nothing and her like they weighed everything: upright, economical, a body that had been taught early that wasted motion is wasted life. Seventeen, perhaps eighteen. Hair dark with sweat at the temples, tied back with string. Hands that were wrong for a girl her age—scarred, nicked, the nails cut short for work underwater.

She set down the buckets, straightened, and looked straight at me.

And there it was. There *he* was.

Daniel Galloway's eyes. I don't mean the colour—I couldn't have sworn to the colour of the man's eyes, we played cards by lamplight. I mean the way they worked. That flat, measuring look that goes through your coat to your pockets, through your face to your intentions, and files you away somewhere behind the breastbone. I'd seen it across seven years of card games. Now it was aimed at me from a girl's face on a doorstep, and I felt like a document being read.

"You're staring," she said. No fear in it. Just an observation, filed.

"I'm sorry." My voice came out rougher than intended; salt and smoke had done things to it. "I'm looking for the Galloway family."

"We're not buying anything."

"I'm not selling."

"Everyone's selling something." She picked up the buckets. "Good day."

And she was gone through the door, and I was left standing in the mud with two oceans in my legs and nothing in my mouth.

I should have followed. Every practical instinct said *follow, deliver, be done.* But a voice that sounded suspiciously like Daniel's said, *Not like that. Not on the step with the coughing woman inside and the neighbours counting.* Dying had taught him patience the hard way, and apparently it was contagious.

So I found a room above a tavern called the Saltbush, paid for a week, and sat by the window with the satchel between my feet like a dog that wouldn't settle.

* * *

Three days I watched the port learn its own shape around me.

I learned that the Meridian office opened at seven and closed when Mr. Calloway—it was always *Mr. Calloway*, spoken with the reverence people reserve for weather—chose to close it. I learned that divers drew rations against their debts and that the debts moved in only one direction, like tide through a one-way channel. I learned to drink slowly in the Saltbush's parlour, saying little, because the man who talks is the man who's remembered, and I had come here to be forgettable right up until the moment I couldn't be.

And I learned about the girl.

Freya, the camp called her—when it spoke of her at all, which was rarely and carefully. She dived. That surprised me until I thought about it; of course she dived, the debt had to be fed, and grief is a crop that keeps producing. She went out with the morning boats and came back with the afternoon ones, and between those two facts she had built herself a life so quiet it was nearly a disguise.

I watched her once at the company office window, copying figures, and saw her stop. Saw her go still over the page in a way I recognised, because I'd seen Daniel go still the same way over a newspaper item about a missing lugger. The stillness of a person whose eye has caught on a thread that doesn't belong in the fabric. Then a man came along the verandah—grey suit, grey manner, the kind of neatness that is itself a threat—and she bent back over her work, and the thread was dropped, or seemed to be.

That would be Calloway, then. Even across a street I could feel the temperature drop around him. Some men collect debts; he collected *people*, whole and labelled.

On the fourth morning I made my decision, and it felt less like courage than like a boat leaving a dock—irrevocable, and mostly a matter of casting off.

* * *

Low tide empties this harbour like a basin, baring miles of grey-brown flats threaded with creeks, and the townspeople use the exposed hours the way other towns use market day. I found her at the far end of the jetty where the mud lay open and gleaming, gulls picking at the banquet below, and she was doing nothing at all—just standing at the rail, looking out, a bucket of shell at her feet and her hands resting on the rail as if the sea owed her money. Which, in a manner of speaking, it did.

I stopped a polite distance off. She didn't turn, but her weight shifted onto the balls of her feet, and I understood that she'd heard me coming and had decided to let me arrive.

"You again," she said.

"Me again."

"If you're from the company, I've nothing to say that helps you."

"I'm not from the company." I set the satchel on the rail between us, and her eyes flicked to it and back to the horizon, quick as a card turned and turned back. "I'm from Thursday Island. From a hospital ward, if you want the exact address."

Now she turned. The measuring look again, and this time it stayed.

"There was a man there," I said. "A diver. Bad chest. Played cards for buttons and cheated at nothing, which in my experience makes him nearly unique among men. He died nine weeks ago. Before he died, he spent eleven days writing."

The wind came off the flats, carrying that sweet rot smell, and pushed a strand of hair across her face and she didn't move it, which told me she'd stopped breathing normally.

"He left instructions," I went on, quieter. "One letter was for a magistrate in Sydney. One was for a lawyer in Melbourne. And the rest—" I tapped the satchel. "The rest are addressed to Galloway. Freya Galloway. This port."

The silence stretched so long that a gull landed on a piling six feet from us, considered the situation, and departed, evidently finding it duller than expected.

"My father is dead," she said finally.

"Yes," I said. "That's what everyone believes. It's also what he wanted believed. But the handwriting on these envelopes—" and here I made my mistake, or my honesty, depending on how history judges it, because I reached into the satchel and drew out the topmost letter and held it where she could see the address, the name, the slanting urgent hand.

She took it from me the way you'd take something that might be alive. Or venomous.

I watched her read the outside of it—three words of an address and her own name—and I watched three years land on her all at once. Her jaw tightened. Her knuckles whitened around the paper until I feared for the envelope. And her eyes came up to mine, and they were not grieving eyes. They were the other thing, the hotter thing, the thing that in my experience follows grief the way lightning follows thunder.

"When," she said. It wasn't a question. It was a demand wearing a question's clothes.

"The date on the postmark is four years old. The date he finished writing is nine weeks ago. He spent the time between staying alive long enough to finish what he started."

"And what did he start?" Her voice had gone low and even, which frightened me more than shouting would have. "What did he write that was worth letting us think he was at the bottom of the sea?"

"That's yours to read, not mine to summarise. I gave my word." I hesitated, and then broke my own rule about talking, because she deserved one true thing from me. "But I'll say this. He didn't stay away because he stopped loving you. He stayed away because he loved you and the company was watching, and he chose your living over your knowing. Whether that was cowardice or sacrifice is a ledger I couldn't balance, and I tried."

Something moved behind her face—too fast and too deep to name—and then it was shut, banked, filed away in whatever strongroom she kept behind those borrowed eyes.

"The tide's turning," she said.

It was. The water was creeping back up the flats, filling the creeks, erasing the morning's evidence. She put the letter inside her shirt, against her skin, and looked at me one last time with her father's measuring gaze.

"Tonight," she said. "After dark. The third hut past the water tank. Come alone, and come having decided whether you're a friend or a problem, because my mother hasn't the strength for problems and I haven't the patience."

She picked up her bucket of shell and walked back along the jetty without hurrying, and I stood there in the returning tide-smell with an empty satchel and a heart hammering like a fist on a door.

I had delivered the letter. What I hadn't counted on was the reader.

Because I'd imagined this moment across two oceans—imagined tears, maybe, or disbelief, a girl collapsing into the arms of relief. I hadn't imagined her taking the letter the way a soldier takes ammunition. I hadn't imagined that look, which was Daniel's look sharpened by youth and grievance into something new, something that made me think of fuses and dry summers.

Somewhere behind me, gulls screamed over the recovering water, and in the company office a grey man in a grey suit was no doubt writing down everything the town had told him about a stranger with a satchel and salt-scars and questions.

Nine weeks ago a dying man made me promise to deliver proof. I'd done it in four days.

Now all I had to do was survive the consequences, and teach a seventeen-year-old girl with her father's eyes and the sea's patience that the hardest cargo I'd ever carried wasn't paper at all.

It was what happens to a family when the truth comes home from the dead.
