## 4. Handwriting of the Drowned

He came to the house at the hour when the light goes thin and everything looks like something it isn't.

I'd seen him twice before I let him speak to me—once on the jetty where he stood too long among the pearl luggers like a man trying to remember which one had wronged him, and once outside the company store, where he watched me weigh shell with the expression of someone doing arithmetic he didn't like the answer to. Strangers in Port Cessnock were not rare exactly, but they were rare in the way that mattered: they arrived with money, they bought what they came for, and they left before the heat or the mosquitoes taught them humility. This one had the look of a man who intended to stay past the lesson.

So when he turned up at our door on the Tuesday, with his satchel and his salt-scars and his hat held politely against his chest, I did what I always did with trouble.

"I don't buy anything," I said. "And we owe enough already."

"I'm not selling." He had a voice like gravel that had been rained on—worn smooth in places, sharp in others. "My name is Ash. Tobias Ash. I've come a long way to find the Galloways."

"You found them. There's the house, there's the debt, there's my mother coughing herself inside out if you'd like to hear the whole inventory."

His eyes went past my shoulder, toward the sound of Maren's coughing through the wall, then back to me. And I saw it then, the thing that had been nagging at me since the jetty—the familiarity I couldn't place because I'd spent three years refusing to look for it. He wasn't looking at me like a stranger. He was looking at me like a man checking a face against a memory.

"You have his eyes," he said quietly.

The words landed in my chest like a dropped anchor.

"Whose?"

"Your father's."

* * *

I should have shut the door. Any sensible woman in my position would have shut the door, and I want it recorded that I understood this perfectly well even as I stepped aside and let him into our single room, because grief makes idiots of us all and I had been rationing mine for so long that when someone offered me more of it, I took the whole supply without asking the price.

Maren was propped on her cot, grey as unbleached calico, her hands folded on the blanket in the posture she used when she wanted the world to believe she was resting rather than dying. She looked at Tobias Ash the way you look at weather coming over the water—not surprised, exactly. Bracing.

"You," she said.

"You know him?" I asked.

"No," said my mother, too fast and too evenly, which from Maren Galloway was as good as a signed confession. "Sit down, Mr. Ash, before you fall down. You look like something the tide rejected."

He sat on our one stool. He set the satchel on his knees and kept both hands on it, the way men hold things they're afraid will be taken. Up close I could see the scars properly now—long white ridges across the backs of his hands and up one forearm, the kind coral leaves on you when it decides not to keep you.

"I knew your father," he said to me. "In Thursday Island. In Singapore. We worked the same boats for a while. His name was—" 

"Aldous Galloway," I said. "Diver. Drowned. Three years ago this October, off the Verran shoals, when his lugger went down in a squall. The company sent a letter and a bill. The letter expressed condolences. The bill expressed the balance outstanding, which was forty-one pounds, fourteen shillings, and sixpence, and I have been paying it ever since, sixpence at a time, out of shell money, while the interest grows like mould." I heard myself and stopped, because I could hear how rehearsed it was, how many times I had recited those figures in the dark. "If you've come about the debt, Mr. Ash, you're twenty-two months late and short by forty-one pounds."

"It's not about the debt."

"Everything is about the debt. That's the first thing they teach you here."

He looked at Maren. She gave him a small nod, and I remember thinking, with the strange clarity of the moment before a wave breaks, that she nodded like a woman granting permission for something she had already decided long ago—and that I hated them both for it before I even knew what it was.

Tobias opened the satchel.

* * *

There were seven letters. He laid them on our table one at a time, squared their edges with his scarred fingers, and pushed them toward me across the wood, and I looked at the topmost envelope for a long moment without touching it, because I already knew.

You spend your childhood learning a person's handwriting before you learn to read it. My father wrote his letters with a forward slant, like a man leaning into wind. He crossed his t's with a single impatient stroke that sometimes caught the next word. His capital G's had a flourish underneath, a little tail, because he thought—no, he *knew*, he'd told me once, laughing, salt-crusted and sun-stupid on a lugger's deck—that a man's name ought to travel well. The address on the envelope in front of me read *Mrs. A. Galloway, divers' quarters, Port Cessnock*, and the G had its tail, and the slant leaned into a wind that had been blowing for three years without reaching us.

The postmark was smudged but legible. Darwin. Dated the fourteenth of March, 1901.

Three years after the boat went down.

I didn't cry. I want that understood. Whatever else happened in that room, whatever I became in the following minutes, I did not cry, though my hands shook hard enough that I had to press them flat against the table to still them, and the table was rough old hardwood and I felt every grain of it through my palms like the world insisting on its own reality while mine came apart.

"He's alive," I said. Not a question. Questions leave room for mercy.

"Yes," said Tobias Ash.

"For three years."

"Yes."

"And he wrote." I touched the envelope, just the corner of it, the way you'd touch something hot to prove it was real. "Seven times. Seven times, and none of them—" I stopped. Started again. "Where are these from? When did you get them?"

"The first came to me two years ago, care of a shipping office in Batavia. Your father and I had an arrangement—if either of us fell out of the world, the other would know where to send word. He fell out of it sideways. The rest came on slowly, through ports, through people I trusted. Some I can't account for the delays. Mail in the islands is a rumor with a stamp on it." He paused. "The last one reached me in February. It asked me to come here myself. It said—" and here he hesitated, and I learned later that Tobias Ash hesitated only when the truth was worse than the lie available to him, "—it said he couldn't explain in writing. That the letters weren't safe. That if anything happened to him, someone would need to know why."

"Someone," I repeated. "Not his wife. Not his daughter. Someone."

"Freya," my mother said softly.

"No." I picked up the top letter now, turned it over. Unopened. Sealed with a blob of wax that had been cheap when it was poured and had gone brittle since. "He sealed them. He sat down in some port town—Darwin, God help me, he was in Darwin, that's four days' sail, five in bad weather—he sat down within sight of the sea that supposedly ate him, and he addressed these to my mother, and he sealed them, and he gave them to a stranger to carry, because he couldn't risk—" My voice did something I didn't authorize. I clamped down on it. "Risk what, Mr. Ash? What is worth three years? What is worth watching us eat less every season? Do you know what my mother sells to make the interest payments? Do you know she went without medicine last winter because I told her we'd be clear of it by summer, because I believed him drowned, because being the orphaned daughter of a dead diver is at least a fixed quantity, it's a number on a ledger, it doesn't change on you—"

"Freya." Maren again, and this time there was iron under it. "Open it."

* * *

I broke the seal with my thumbnail because I refused to fetch a knife, as if using a knife would be conceding ceremony to him.

The paper inside was good stock, better than anything in our house, better than anything the Meridian Trading Company issued to its indentured staff, and that detail alone made something go cold and quiet in me. He was eating well, wherever he was. He was writing on good paper.

*Maren,* it began, and then nothing for a line, just the pen pressed hard enough to nearly tear, the ink blot spreading like a small drowning. Then:

*I know what you will think of me and you will be right to think it. I am alive. I am sorry. Both of those sentences are true and neither one is any comfort, and I have written this page eleven times.*

I read it standing up. I don't remember deciding to stand; my legs simply informed me we were no longer sitting. The room got very far away. I was aware of Tobias Ash motionless on the stool, watching me the way you watch a person wade into deep water, and aware of my mother's breathing, slow and deliberate, a woman conserving air for what was coming.

*They did not sink the boat by accident,* the letter said. *I have to write it plainly because I have been trying for ten lines to find a gentle way and there isn't one. Calloway's men scuttled her. They meant it to look like the storm. They meant me to be in her. What they didn't know was that I'd been in the hold that night and seen what was loaded aboard, and I understood that a diver who has seen certain cargo does not stay above water long. So I made sure I wasn't in the boat, and I let them believe I was, and I have been letting the whole world believe it ever since, including you, including my own girl, God forgive me, God forgive me, I chose the lie over the truth and the lie was cheaper.*

I put the letter down on the table. I looked at it. I looked at the other six envelopes beneath it, all sealed, all patient, all full of pages written by a living hand in a dead man's name.

Then I laughed. I'm not proud of it. It came out of me like something escaping, short and ugly, one bark of it, and it frightened Tobias Ash visibly, and I suppose a laughing widow's daughter is an alarming thing.

"Cheaper," I said. "He says the lie was cheaper." I picked the letter up again and read aloud, because apparently some part of me had decided that if I was going to be destroyed in my own kitchen, I would at least conduct the proceedings myself. "*The lie was cheaper.* Let's do the sums, shall we? Forty-one pounds, fourteen and six, plus interest, compounding quarterly per the contract he signed and I inherited. Two hundred and eighty hours a year underwater, minimum, at a rate the company sets and I don't. Mother's medicine forgone. Meat forgone. Shoes mended until there was more string than shoe. Every sixpence I've earned since I was fifteen has gone into a grave that turns out to be empty." I looked up at Ash. "Was he laughing when he wrote that? Was 'cheaper' his word?"

"He wouldn't use a word like that," Ash said. "That's you talking."

"Don't flatter yourself with insight, Mr. Ash. You delivered the mail. You don't get credit for the weather inside it."

"Freya Galloway." He stood up off the stool, and up close his eyes were the exact color of deep water over sand, and yes, they were my father's eyes, that was the obscenity of it, that I could see my father looking back at me out of a stranger's face. "I crossed two oceans carrying those letters. I buried a friend in Batavia who helped me get them. I have been robbed twice, fevered once, and detained by officials in three ports whose names I'll not say aloud in a company port. I am not your enemy and I did not write what's in those envelopes. But you should read all of them before you decide what you feel, because the last one isn't about you at all. The last one is about the men in that cargo. And I've seen what happens to girls who learn about cargoes like that in company towns." He paused. "It's the same thing that happens to divers who see them."

The room went still. Outside, the afternoon was doing its usual work—the slap of water on piles, the winch groaning somewhere along the jetty, a lugger's engine knocking over toward the anchorage, the ordinary sounds of a port that had never once suspected itself of anything.

"What cargo," I said.

Ash glanced at my mother. And here is the thing I will think about for the rest of my life, however long that turns out to be: my mother did not look away. She met my eyes straight on, grey-faced and hollow-chested and calm as ballast, and she said, "Read the last letter, love. And then I'll tell you what I've known for two years."

Two years.

The ground didn't shift under me so much as reveal that it had never been ground at all—just the deck of a boat I hadn't known I was standing on, and everyone around me had known, and the sea was where it had always been: waiting, patient, full of things that sink.

I picked up the last envelope. The seal cracked like a small bone breaking.

*Freya,* my father had written. *If you are reading this, then Ash found you, and I owe him another life, and I owe you the truth, which weighs more than any pearl I ever brought up. Listen carefully, because there may not be time for me to say it gently...*

I read. And what I read made the debt, the drowned boat, even the three stolen years seem like the small betrayals—the trimmings—because my father had witnessed what the Meridian Trading Company really shipped in the dark of night from this port, and he had run from it instead of standing against it, and every entry in the company ledger that had never added up suddenly, horribly, did.
