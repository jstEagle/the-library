## 5. What the Mother Knew

There is a particular skill to lying to your own child, and I want it noted, before anything else, that I was very good at it.

Not the flamboyant kind of lie—the kind that wins prizes at dinner parties or gets men hanged. My lies were small and domestic and load-bearing. *Your father's boots wore out years ago; there's no sense keeping them.* *He never wrote much anyway, you know how he was with a pen.* *The money in the flour tin is from mending nets for the Olsens.* I told them the way you lay bricks: one at a time, evenly, without looking up, until one day you step back and find you have built a wall between yourself and everything you love.

My daughter thought she inherited her silence from her father. She got it from me. I simply had two more years of practice.

* * *

It began, as most ruinous things do, on an ordinary evening. Two years ago this past March. The lamp was low because oil cost money, and Freya was at the table copying figures for the company—she did that even then, my careful girl, earning pennies by writing other people's debts in a fair hand—and I was mending a net that belonged to nobody, because mending something is cheaper than thinking.

The knock came soft. Three taps, then a pause, then two. I have never learned who taught him that rhythm, or why he believed it would survive three oceans and two years of his absence, but there it was against my door like a heartbeat I'd convinced myself had stopped.

I opened it to a Kanak man I didn't know, barefoot, sea-bitten, holding a parcel wrapped in oilcloth as though it were a sleeping infant he'd been told not to wake.

"Maren Galloway," he said. Not a question. Men who carry messages across that much water don't ask; they deliver.

"I'm not—" I began, which is what I said when anyone came to the door in those days, and still say now, out of habit, to census takers and charity women and God.

"He said you'd deny it," the man said. "He said, *she'll say she isn't herself. Tell her the boat was called the Kestrel and she hated the name.*"

I looked at the Kestrel's name painted on nothing, hanging in the air between us, and I stepped aside and let the past into my kitchen.

* * *

Here is what I will not do, sitting here with this paper and this pen and however many nights remain to me: I will not pretend I opened that parcel with trembling hands. My hands were steady. They are always steady. It is the rest of me that shakes, and I keep it where people can't see, the way you keep a cracked cup at the back of the shelf and only drink from it alone.

Inside the oilcloth: thirty pounds in mixed coin. A letter. And a small tin of candied ginger, which is such a stupid, tender, unforgivable thing to send that I sat down on the stool and laughed until the laughing turned into the other thing, and the messenger stood in my doorway staring at the floor like a man who has delivered a coffin before and knows the family needs a minute.

The letter was in Tom's hand. You must understand about Tom's handwriting—it leaned. Everything he ever wrote leaned forward, like a man perpetually starting to stand up, perpetually changing his mind. I had spent nineteen years reading that lean. Love letters, debt notes, the christening record for our daughter whose name he chose from a book of Norse sagas because he said a girl should be named after something that survives cold.

*Maren,* it began, and then four pages of the leanest writing he ever produced.

I could quote it to you whole. I could recite it backward, the way you can recite a thing you've read every night for two years while pretending you haven't. But the parts that mattered were these:

*I'm alive. Don't hate me yet; wait until you've read to the end, then hate me efficiently.*

That was Tom all over. Even his contrition kept accounts.

*The boat went down, but I wasn't in it. Calloway put me in it, near enough. I saw what they do with the islanders, Mare. The ones marked dead in the book aren't dead. There's a buyer down south and a ship that comes quiet, and I signed papers I shouldn't have signed and I knew too much to keep knowing it. A drowned man is a safe man. So I drowned. It took me two days to swim to the mainland and I thought the whole way: this is the last dishonest thing I will ever do, and I knew even as I thought it that it was the first of hundreds.*

And then, further down, the part I built a church around:

*Nobody must know I live. Not a soul, not a hint, not a look held too long. If Meridian learns there's a Galloway who can testify, they won't come for me—they can't reach me—but they'll come for you. For you and the girl. A witness with family is a witness with a handle. Be dull, Mare. Be poor and dull and sick if you have to. Nobody investigates a woman nobody remembers.*

*Be dull.* He might have written *be safe*, and meant the same thing, and it would have sat differently in me. But Tom always did prefer instructions to comfort. He sent love the way other men send postcards: briefly, from a great distance, unsigned.

At the bottom, beneath his name, he had added a postscript in smaller letters, as though leaning even harder:

*Tell Freya nothing. Not because she'd tell anyone—because she wouldn't. Because a girl who knows a secret starts carrying it, and I've seen what carrying does to carriers.*

I read that line forty times before I understood it was the kindest thing he'd ever written, and the cruelest, and that both judgments could be true without cancelling each other out, the way a coin keeps its value whether it lands heads or tails.

* * *

So I made a doctrine.

You'll want me to say I agonized. I didn't—not the way Freya agonizes, turning a thing over until both sides are worn smooth. I am my mother's daughter from a whaling station where feeling was something you did after the work. I read the letter twice, I hid it inside the binding of the Bible where Tom knew I'd never look because Tom knew I only read the grim Psalms, and I got up the next morning and began the long, patient work of making my daughter invisible.

Some of it was already done. That's the joke of it—that's the bitter little joke I keep under my tongue like a stone. Freya had taught herself to disappear long before I systematized it. She'd learned it watching me watch the company men, learning which doors to be near and which to be nowhere around. All I did was give her instinct a curriculum.

*Don't sing where the company men can hear; a voice is a thing remembered.* *When Calloway speaks to you, agree, and forget nothing, and show neither.* *Wear grey; grey dries fastest and photographs worst.* I said these things lightly, the way mothers do, folding instruction inside complaint so it goes down without tasting of medicine. And underneath, always, the same silent sentence, the one I repeated to myself at night like a rosary with one bead: *because if they ever think she matters, they will use her, and I have seen what they use.*

Was it love? Yes. I will die saying yes, and I will die knowing it doesn't fully answer the question, the way *it was for the best* doesn't un-break what broke.

But here is what the doctrine cost, itemized, because I am my father's daughter too and we kept accounts:

It cost her friends. I discouraged them—gently, always gently. *That Olsen girl talks too much; talkers repeat things.* What I meant was: *everyone she talks to is a thread someone could pull.*

It cost her grief. When Tom's death stopped being fresh and became fact, I watched my daughter carry a mourning she wasn't allowed to examine, because examination leads to questions and questions lead to the shore where the answers live. She grieved in the dark, privately, the way she did everything, and I let her, because her grief was a door and I had nailed mine shut and hers had to match.

And it cost me her trust—not suddenly, not all at once, but the way a hull takes water: invisibly, below the waterline, so that the day she finally noticed, we were already sinking and neither of us knew how to swim toward each other instead of away.

Two years I kept it. Thirty pounds hidden in the flour tin, spent a pound at a time on rent and medicine and once, shamelessly, on a ribbon for Freya's hair that I claimed came from the mission stall. Every pound a small treason. Every purchase a letter from a ghost.

And every night, the cough getting worse, and the ginger tin emptying, and me thinking: *he said wait, he said be patient, he said when it's safe*—and the safe never coming, because safe is not a place, it's a direction, and Tom had pointed us at dullness and walked the other way.

* * *

Which brings us to last night. To Tobias Ash standing in my kitchen with salt-scars on his hands and my husband's eyes looking out through someone else's face—no, that's wrong, that's the romantic version. Ash has his own eyes, tired and level and older than his years. What he had was Tom's letters, dated after the drowning, and my daughter's face going through stages like weather: confusion, recognition, disbelief, and then the one I dreaded, the one I'd been dodging for two years—rage, arriving exactly on schedule, aimed past Ash entirely, straight at me.

Because daughters know. However well you brick up the wall, they hear the hollow sound when they knock.

"You knew." Not a question. Freya doesn't waste questions on things she's certain of. "You knew, and you let me count his debt in breaths. You let me dive his grave."

"His grave is a fiction," I said. "That's rather the point."

"Don't." Her voice had the flat calm I recognized with horror as my own, handed back to me like a borrowed coat. "Don't be clever at me, Mother. Not tonight."

And Ash, bless him, moved toward the door and said he could come back, and we both said no in the same tone, and he stayed by the window pretending to study the harbour, which is a skill I suspect he genuinely possesses and was merely being polite about.

So I told her. All of it. The knock, the messenger, the Kestrel, the ginger, the letter in the Psalms. I told her about *be dull* and about the handle and the carrier. I watched her face while I spoke, and I will tell you what I saw there, because I have earned the right to be precise about my own destruction: I saw relief arrive before the anger finished. Underneath the fury—underneath, where children keep their truest ledgers—some part of my daughter was thinking, *he didn't leave us, he fled for us, the arithmetic holds.* And another part, louder, was thinking, *you chose his secret over my grief, and that I cannot forgive, and may not need to.*

Both parts correct. Both parts hers. I have spent her whole life deciding what she should carry; the least I owe her now is the weight itself, undiluted.

"Why two years?" she asked finally. "Why not tell me at year one? Why not tell me when the cough started, when the money ran short, when—" and here her voice did break, one clean crack, "—when I dove the wreck. When I dove his boat. You let me dive a lie."

"Because the wreck was the safest place for the truth," I said, "and I couldn't tell you that without telling you everything, and everything was a loaded gun, and I had watched you hold things carelessly since you were six."

"I don't hold things carelessly."

"No," I agreed. "You never did. I lied to myself too. It was a busy household for falsehoods."

Ash turned from the window then, and asked the question I'd been waiting for with my heart going like a diver's pulse: whether Tom had said where he was. Whether there was a way to reach him.

And I am ashamed—truly ashamed, in the old-fashioned sense, the kind you confess—to tell you how fast I answered, how many years of rehearsed denial fell away the moment someone asked in the right voice, the voice of a man who had crossed two oceans to finish another man's errand. I heard myself say Thursday Island before I had decided to say anything at all. Two years of doctrine, undone by one direct question, which is either proof that secrets decay or proof that I was tired, and I suspect it is proof of both, in equal measure.

Freya stared at me. "Thursday Island," she repeated. "He's been a fortnight's sail away this whole time. He could see the port from the mail steamer. He could have—"

"He could have nothing," I said, sharper than I meant. "Don't you understand yet, girl? He can't exist until the company can't punish his existing. He is a dead man doing sums, and the sum only works if we stay dead to him. That was the bargain. That was always the bargain."

"And you accepted it for both of us."

"Yes," I said. "That is what mothers do. We accept terms on behalf of people who aren't at the table. Ask any admiral's wife. Ask any company man's daughter. Half of history is somebody's mother signing something."

That silenced her, which I regretted immediately, because silencing Freya has never once worked and I know better. Sure enough, she came back around within the minute, quieter, worse:

"The ledger page. In his letters—the one naming the buyer. You've known Meridian sells living men for two years, and you taught me to wear grey."

I had no answer prepared. I had two years of answers prepared, but none of them survived contact with her actual eyes, so I gave her the truth, which is the one luxury left to women in my position:

"I taught you to survive it," I said. "I am not proud of the difference. But there is a difference, and on the day you understand it, you'll either forgive me or you won't, and both of those are allowed."

She stood there a long moment, my daughter, with her father's lean in her spine—all of us leaning, in that kitchen, toward something none of us could name yet. Then she picked up her coat, because it was nearly time for the office and figures don't copy themselves and debts, unlike daughters, are never forgiven for lateness. At the door she paused.

"The flour tin," she said. "The extra pounds. I counted them, Mum. Years ago. I thought you were saving to buy me out of contract."

And she left before I could arrange my face, which was, I suspect, precisely why she said it.

* * *

So now you know. Now she knows. The wall I spent two years building came down in one evening, and I discover that what I feel is not grief exactly, and not relief exactly, but the strange light-headedness of a woman setting down a load she had forgotten she was carrying—followed hard upon, as it always is, by the knowledge that the road ahead is worse than the one behind, and that I have just handed my daughter a map of it.

Because Ash is still in my kitchen, laying out those letters beside each other like a surgeon laying out instruments, and Freya's copied figures are coming home tonight in her fair, careful hand, and somewhere in the overlap between a drowned man's handwriting and a company ledger, something terrible is taking shape. I can feel it the way divers feel weather coming in their teeth.

I burned nothing yet. Remember that, whatever happens. Whatever I do in the coming days—whatever ends up in the stove, whatever ends up in the water—remember that tonight, for one whole evening, I still believed the truth could be carried gently.

Outside, the tide was turning, and far down the jetty a company lantern swung in the wind like a slow, patient pendulum, counting out something I did not yet want to hear.
