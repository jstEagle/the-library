## 6. The Island Manifest

*Freya*

There is a particular quality to lamplight in a house built of weatherboard and secrets. It makes shadows of everything honest and leaves the dishonest things—letters from drowned men, ledgers that don't add up—sitting in plain view like guests who've overstayed.

Tobias Ash had spread my father's letters across our kitchen table alongside three sheets I'd copied from the Meridian office, and for a long while neither of us said anything. The kettle ticked on the stove. My mother slept in the next room, her cough mercifully quiet for once, as though even her lungs understood that what was happening at this table required stillness.

"Read it again," Tobias said. "The fourth letter. The part about the *Kestrel*."

I picked up the page. My father's handwriting slanted forward like a man hurrying away from something, which I supposed he had been. Three years I'd known that slant only on the debt notices Meridian sent after his boat went down—the cold arithmetic of what we owed, rendered in his hand because he'd signed surety for every shilling. Now here was the same hand writing other things entirely.

"*The Kestrel took on eleven at Port Moresby,*" I read. "*Only six signed the manifest. Calloway told the office five were lost overboard in the crossing. But I stood on the deck at Thursday Island and counted heads coming off. Eleven walked off, and six went into the company books as ever having existed.*"

"And your copied sheets," Tobias said. "Read me the ration figures."

I'd copied them from memory that afternoon, hunched over the copying desk in the office while Calloway's pen scratched above me like a bird looking for worms. "Men listed as deceased. Rations drawn against their names for months after. Some for years." I set the pages beside the letters. "I thought it was sloppiness. Clerks are lazy everywhere; I'd have put money on it."

"You wouldn't now."

"No." I looked at the two sets of paper, mine neat and small-handed, his salt-stained and urgent, and felt something cold walk up my spine and settle between my shoulder blades. "Dead men don't draw rations, Mr. Ash. Dead men don't need rice and tobacco. So either the company is feeding ghosts—and I'll grant you they're capable of most things, but generosity to the dead isn't one of them—or the dead aren't dead."

Tobias sat back. The lamp caught the scars along his jaw and made them shine like channels on a chart. "Eleven men came off the Kestrel. Six went into the books. Five went—" He stopped, because there are some sentences you can see the end of and choose not to say.

"Went somewhere else," I finished for him. "Somewhere with its own books. Somewhere that pays by the head."

We let that sit. Outside, the tide was going out, and the mudflats gave off their low rotten smell, and somewhere a curlew was making its idiot grief-noise, and all of it carried on exactly as if the world were the world I'd always thought it was.

"The manifest," Tobias said finally. "Your father mentions a manifest. If Calloway filed the Kestrel's papers as he filed everything, there'll be a copy in the records room. Deaths logged, names struck through, maybe even a note about compensation paid to next of kin who never existed." He rubbed his eyes. "A drowning on paper is very tidy. No body, no inquiry, no questions from anyone who might notice five grown men evaporating."

"I notice things," I said. "It's practically my only accomplishment. That's why Calloway keeps me copying—I'm cheap, accurate, and invisible." I heard how it sounded the moment I said it, the little boast wrapped in self-pity, and Tobias almost smiled, which annoyed me more than anything else had all evening.

"Invisible is useful," he said. "For once in your life, Miss Galloway, being overlooked may be the most valuable thing you own."

* * *

*Tobias*

I had crossed two oceans carrying a dead man's letters, and in all that time I had imagined many versions of this night. In none of them did I imagine sitting in a pearl diver's kitchen watching a girl of nineteen connect dots faster than the magistrate's clerks in Sydney ever had.

Freya Galloway read documents the way I'd seen surveyors read coastline—one pass for shape, a second for danger. She had laid out the letters in date order without being asked, weighted the corners with the flat stones divers use to anchor line, and when she found a discrepancy she tapped it twice with one finger, a small precise gesture, like knocking on a door she intended to open.

"The dates," she said now. She tapped the letters in sequence. "Your friend—my father—writes about the Kestrel in March of '96. Then again in June. Then this one, October, where he says he's done counting and wants out." Her finger stopped. "And then nothing until the last letter, the one telling him to send someone, dated four months ago."

"He stopped writing because he was being watched. Or because writing was becoming dangerous."

"Or because of what happened in between." She pulled the last letter toward her and read aloud, and her voice did something I wasn't prepared for—it went flat and careful, the voice of a person holding a door shut against a flood. "*They took a boy off the Ada at Thursday Island. Fifteen, maybe. His uncle signed for him as collateral and the uncle died in the season, so there was no one to ask where the boy went. I asked anyway. I asked in front of the wrong man. Two days later my mooring lines were cut clean, not frayed, cut, and I understood that the sea was going to be blamed for whatever came next, so I let it be blamed. Forgive me. It was the only lie that kept me breathing, and I have hated myself for it every day since, which is not the same as being sorry enough.*"

She set the letter down. Her hands were steady. Her face was not.

"My father's boat," she said. "The *Ada*. They named the story after the boat that supposedly killed him. Cut lines, storm, no trace." She laughed, one short ugly note. "I was fifteen too. When he 'drowned.' We buried an empty box because there was nothing to bury, and I remember thinking even then that the sea gives back everything eventually—timber, boots, bodies. Everything except him. And I thought: he wasn't in the sea at all. I was twelve, and I thought it, and then I was taught to stop thinking it, because thinking it didn't pay the debt."

"Freya—"

"Don't." She held up a hand. Not angry. Worse than angry—organized. "Don't comfort me yet. Comfort is for afterward. Right now I want to be exact." She turned back to the table. "Five men off the Kestrel, sold onward. A boy taken off the *Ada*. Deaths faked on paper so the company never has to explain a missing islander to anyone—no colonial office inquiry, no missionary asking after souls, nothing. You sell a man in Port Moresby or Fiji or God knows where, and here in the books he quietly drowns." She tapped the ration sheets. "And the beautiful thing, the truly elegant thing, is that the rations keep getting drawn. Because the dead man's contract doesn't die with him. His debt transfers, his rations get sold on to the next crew, and Meridian profits twice—once selling the man, once selling his food. Calloway runs a slave trade with a stationery budget."

I had heard the trade described before, in Sydney drawing rooms where reformers used careful words like *labour migration* and *indenture abuses*. None of them had ever made it sound like a stationery budget. I thought the magistrate would want her at the trial just for the sentences.

"There's more," she said. "You know there's more. Your face has gone careful."

"Your father saw a drowning," I said. "Not a faked one on paper. A real one. A man who objected to being resold—your father doesn't name him, but he describes it. Off the Kestrel's tender, in the roads at Thursday Island. He writes that the man was put over the side alive and held under with a boat hook, and that Calloway watched from the launch and afterward remarked that it saved the cost of a return passage." I made myself keep going. "He witnessed it from the water. He was diving nearby. He surfaced close enough to hear."

Freya was very still. "That's why he ran."

"That's why he ran. He could have reported it—but to whom? The resident magistrate on Thursday Island banked with Meridian. He could have kept quiet and stayed safe—but he'd already asked about the boy, and asking is a signature. So he chose the third thing. He drowned himself instead, on paper, and started collecting proof. Letters, dates, names of men bought and sold. He meant to send it south eventually. He ran out of time, or nerve, or both, and so he gave it to me." I looked at my hands. "A dying man in a boarding house in Batavia pressed a satchel into mine and said, *Take it to the daughter. She counts things. She'll understand.* Those were nearly his last words. *She counts things.*"

"He got that much right about me at least." But her voice had changed. The flatness was cracking, and underneath it I could hear the flood winning. "Do you know what I did today, Mr. Ash? I sat at that copying desk and I wrote out the debt figures of men who were never dead, and above my head Calloway scratched away with his pen, keeping his lovely books, and I thought: this is arithmetic, this is just how the world balances. I thought history belonged to admirals and company men and it was my job to stay out of its way." She gathered the stones off the letters one by one, setting them aside with exaggerated care, the way you handle things when you want very badly to throw them. "Those weren't debts. They were people. Men with uncles and nephews and mothers waiting on islands for boats that were never coming back, and I entered them into my neat little columns like sacks of shell."

"You couldn't have known."

"I could have looked." She stood, and the lamplight threw her shadow tall up the wall, taller than any shadow of hers I'd seen on the jetty. "That's the difference, isn't it? Looking. Everyone in this port looks away. It's a whole economy of looking away, and I was good at it. I was *excellent* at it." She turned to the window, to the black water beyond. "My mother made me invisible to keep me safe. My father drowned himself on paper to keep himself alive. And I thought invisibility was wisdom. It was just surrender with better manners."

* * *

*Freya*

Tobias left near midnight, slipping down the path to the jetty camp where he'd taken a bunk, moving through the moonlight like a man who had practice at not being seen—which, I was beginning to understand, he did.

I didn't sleep. I sat at the table with the letters and my copied sheets and did what I do, which is count.

Five men off the Kestrel. One boy off the Ada. Ration entries stretching back nine years in the office books, long before the Kestrel, long before me—dead divers whose rice was drawn and drawn and drawn. I had assumed each was a separate sloppiness. Now they resolved into a pattern, the way individual waves resolve into a swell when you're far enough out. Nine years. How many men? Fifty? A hundred? Sold north and west into plantations and pearling luggers and places whose names I didn't know, each one folded neatly into a paper grave, each grave drawing rations until the clerk got bored.

And my father had known. Had stood in the water and watched a man be murdered and understood that the murder was a routine, a procedure, an entry in a ledger, and had chosen—God help him, and I was not ready to, not yet—to disappear rather than fight, and had spent three years gathering paper in the dark while his wife coughed and his daughter counted breaths underwater to pay for his sins.

I should have hated him cleanly. It would have been simpler. Instead I sat in the grey hour before dawn holding a letter in which he wrote, *I think of your hands, Freya-girl, how sure they are on the line*, and I cried the way you cry alone at nineteen—furiously, silently, with an eye on the door.

Then I stopped crying, because crying was another form of staying invisible, and I was finished with it.

Here is what I knew by sunrise. This was no longer about a debt, or a father, or even a family. Somewhere in the records room of the Meridian Trading Company lay a manifest with five names struck through, and behind those five names stood fifty more, and behind the fifty stood everyone in the divers' camp who owed what could never be repaid and knew better than to ask why. The company had written its version of events in ink, in duplicate, filed and cross-referenced. History, Mr. Calloway believed, belonged to whoever kept the books.

Well. I kept the books.

I was washing the lamp glass when my mother appeared in her doorway, wrapped in her shawl despite the heat, her eyes going straight to the papers on the table the way a bird goes to a snake.

"How much did he tell you?" she asked.

"Enough to know it wasn't only us he abandoned." I turned to face her. "Mama. There's a manifest in the records room. The Kestrel. I need to see it, copy it, every line. And I need you to tell me everything you know about the records room—when Calloway dines, when the clerks go home, which windows stick."

Something moved through her face—fear, and beneath the fear, unexpectedly, pride, surfacing like a drowned thing finally given leave to rise.

"Calloway dines upstairs on Fridays," she said. "Alone, with the good claret, and he locks the outer door at eight." She paused at the threshold, one hand on the frame. "The lock is older than he is, Freya. Your father installed half the fittings in that office, back when Meridian trusted him. Ask Tobias if he still carries picks."

Outside, the tide was turning. I could hear it—the soft colossal shift of the harbour deciding to move—and for the first time in my life it sounded less like a warning than like a door, opening.
