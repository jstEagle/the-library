## 7. Calloway's Kindness

The summons came on a Tuesday, which was wrong in itself. Mr. Calloway did not summon divers on Tuesdays. Tuesdays were for the ledgers, and Wednesdays for the shell weigh-ins, and Thursdays—Thursdays were when the company remembered you owed it your lungs. A Tuesday summons meant either someone had died or someone had noticed me, and I had spent nineteen years arranging my life so that no one would ever do the second.

I dressed in my good dress, which is to say the one with only two mends, and I walked to the office with my hands folded like a girl going to church, though the last time I'd been inside a church the minister had looked straight through me as though I were a draft coming under the door.

The Meridian Trading Company office was the finest building in the port, which is a sentence that should embarrass everyone who has ever lived here. It had glass windows and a verandah with actual balustrades, and inside it smelled of ink and camphor and money that had passed through so many hands it had forgotten where it started. The floorboards were polished by other people's feet. Mine included.

"Miss Galloway." Calloway's clerk, Pell, gestured me through to the inner room with the particular delicacy of a man who has learned that everything in this building is someone's funeral waiting to be scheduled.

Mr. Calloway stood at his window with his back to me, watching the harbor the way a spider watches a web—not hungrily, exactly. Bookkeepingly.

"You'll take tea," he said, without turning. It was not a question. In this town, nothing Mr. Calloway said was ever a question; questions were for people who could afford uncertainty.

"I don't drink tea," I said, because it was true, and because some small stubborn animal in me insisted on offering one true thing even in a den of accounts.

He turned then, and smiled, and poured me tea anyway into a cup so thin I could see light through its rim. "Then hold it. Warm hands make for steady nerves, and I find I prefer steady nerves in my interlocutors."

*Interlocutors.* That was Calloway all over. He'd come up from Melbourne fifteen years ago with a clerk's accent sanded down to something almost local, and he spoke like a man who kept a dictionary on his nightstand the way other men kept a revolver. Perhaps he did. I wouldn't have bet against it.

I held the cup. My hands were steady anyway, but there seemed little profit in saying so.

"Sit down, Freya. May I call you Freya? Your father always said—" He stopped himself, a small theatrical wound, hand to breast. "Forgive me. It still catches sometimes. A fine diver, your father. Finest I ever contracted."

The tea tasted of tannin and something sweeter underneath that I chose not to investigate.

* * *

Here is what I knew about Mr. Ambrose Calloway before that morning: he collected debts the way the tide collects shells, patiently, indifferently, and without any malice you could name or fight. Here is what I learned about him that morning: none of that was true. There was plenty of malice. It had simply been aged, like wine, until it poured smooth.

"Your account," he said, drawing a ledger toward him—I watched that ledger the way you watch a snake you've been assured is dead—and ran one long finger down a column. "You've made extraordinary progress. Extraordinary. Nineteen years old, and you've reduced a debt that broke grown men. Your father's debt, of course, but you took it up like a birthright."

"It was arithmetic," I said. "There was nothing else to do with the numbers."

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "There rarely is, for people like you."

He let that sit between us the way you'd set down a heavy box—here, hold this, see how it weighs—and then he did the thing I will be turning over in my mind on my deathbed, whenever that comes, whether it comes wet or dry.

He dipped his pen, crossed out a figure, and wrote a smaller one.

"That's a reduction," I said stupidly. "Of the principal."

"A reduction of interest, retroactive two years, and a reduction of principal by thirty percent." He blotted it neatly. "Consider it an adjustment for exemplary service."

Nobody adjusted anything for exemplary service. I had seen men lose fingers to the shell beds and been charged for the bandages. I thought of the entries that didn't add up, the dead men whose rations walked off the books month after month, and the cold feeling that started in my stomach then traveled out to my fingertips and made the teacup chime faintly against its saucer.

"There's a condition," I said.

"Freya." He said my name like a man tasting it. "You wound me. Is kindness so foreign a currency in this port that you can't take payment without checking it for counterfeit?"

"Yes," I said.

And he laughed—a real laugh, warm as a stove—and I understood in that moment that he liked me. That was the horror of it. Somewhere in the machinery of his regard, I had become a favorite. The clever little diver who read. Who kept her head down and her sums true. You cannot fight a man who likes you. You can only disappoint him, and disappointment, in Calloway's economy, accrued interest.

"The condition," he said, recovering his gentleness, "is simply conversation. I'm a busy man, but I confess I enjoy our talks. So: talk with me. Tell me about the camp. Tell me about your mother's health—I hear she's poorly."

My mother's health. The cough had gotten worse since the cold snap, rattling through the walls of our hut at night like surf. We had no medicine. Medicine cost what medicine always costs here, which was more diving, more years, more of me converted at market rate into somebody's dividend.

"She coughs," I said carefully.

"Coughs." He nodded sympathetically. "This climate is cruel to English lungs. Now—I have connections in Thursday Island. A chemist there compounds a tincture, laudanum base with squill syrup, that works wonders on the chest. I could have a bottle on the next coastal run. No charge. Consider it a company benefit."

A company benefit. The words went through me like a fishhook going through bait—clean entry, ugly purpose.

"And what would the company want in return?"

"Nothing." He spread his hands, palms up, a man showing he carried no weapons, though men who carry no weapons are usually the ones you should count the fingers on. "Nothing but good news, Freya. This is a small town and a lonely posting, and I like to know what happens in it. Who comes and goes. A stranger arrived lately, I understand. Big fellow, sea-scarred, asking after families. Asking after *Galloways*, someone said."

There it was. Not hidden, not even disguised—he laid it out on the desk between us like a merchant laying out cloth, and waited to see if I'd buy.

I had prepared for this question for days. Tobias and I had built a story the way you build a breakwater, stone by stone, expecting the tide to test it. Tobias Ash was a wrecker salvaging the *Adelaide Star*. He'd heard the Galloway name from my father years back on a pearling lugger out of Cooktown. He wanted to buy father's old dive gear, if any survived. Simple. Verifiable in its dullness. Dullness was the whole architecture of it.

"He's a salvager," I said. "Working the *Adelaide Star* wreck. He knew my father, years ago, on a lugger out of Cooktown. He wanted to buy Da's old gear."

"Did he now." Calloway's eyes were pale grey, the color of the water before weather. "And did you sell him any?"

"He didn't want it after he saw the state of it."

"Mm." He made a small note—I could not see what—and the sound of the pen was very loud in the quiet room. "Wreckers are a rough trade. You'd want to be careful of strangers, Freya. A girl alone, a sick mother, a house at the end of the track where nobody passes by. Bad men notice such things. Good men too, of course." He smiled again. "We all notice things. The question is only ever what we do about them."

* * *

I have thought since about how a person knows they're caught. It isn't a snap, not a trapdoor. It's slower than that, and colder. It's realizing that the warmth you've been standing in is a spotlight, and that it found you some time ago—you simply hadn't turned around to see it.

Because here is what I understood, sitting in that chair with the untouched tea cooling in my grip: the reduction wasn't new. Nothing he offered that morning was new. The interest adjustment, the medicine, the soft voice—it was all retroactive. He had been reducing things and noting things and adjusting things for weeks. Every weigh-in where he'd spoken to me courteously instead of curtly. Every time Pell had waved me through without searching my bag. Every small mercy I'd catalogued as luck or fatigue or the ordinary caprice of the company—I had filed them wrong. They weren't lapses in his attention. They *were* his attention. I had been measured the way you measure a reef before dynamiting it: thoroughly, and with love, in the sense that a surveyor loves his charts.

And worse—worse than the trap itself—was the knowledge that I'd walked into it carrying exactly what he wanted. Because I had answered his question about Tobias smoothly, yes, smoothly enough—but I had answered it *quickly*, and quick answers are their own confession to a man who interrogates numbers for a living. A truthful story has dead air in it. Mine had none. And Calloway, I would have bet the compass my mother keeps wrapped in flannel, had noticed.

"Your mother will have her medicine by Friday," he said, rising, signaling with one finger that the audience had concluded and the sentence had begun. "And Freya—the debt reduction stands regardless. I want that understood. Whatever else happens." He came around the desk and opened the door for me personally, another impossible courtesy, and lowered his voice as though confiding in a daughter. "Whatever else happens. That's the kind of man I am. Ask anyone."

Ask anyone. As though anyone in this port would answer honestly about Ambrose Calloway within earshot of his windows. As though honesty were a thing this town grew naturally, rather than a crop you had to smuggle in and grow in the dark like opium poppies.

"Thank you, Mr. Calloway," I said, and my voice was level, and I hated how level it was, how well-practiced, how exactly the voice of a girl who has spent her whole life being unremarkable—which was, I saw now, precisely the voice he'd been listening for all along. Invisibility wasn't safety. Invisibility was just a longer runway.

* * *

Outside, the light hit me like a slap after the lamplit interior, and I stood on the verandah a moment letting my eyes adjust, and that was when I saw them.

Two men I didn't know, leaning against the rail by the shell stores, doing the universal pantomime of idleness—spitting, studying the harbor, examining their own knuckles. But they'd been there yesterday too. And the day before. And one of them wore boots too clean for a diver, boots that had never seen the inside of a waterline.

They didn't look at me. That was the polished part. Nobody looks at the girl who doesn't exist.

I walked home along the shore road with my basket empty and my mind full, running the morning over and over like a line snagged on coral, looking for where it had cut me. The tea. The pen. The word *retroactive*, sitting in my memory now with its little brass fittings gleaming. Retroactive. He'd decided to be kind to me weeks ago. Before Tobias ever reached the jetty? Or after? Had the stranger been reported the day he stepped ashore, the way everything is reported eventually, the way the tide reports the moon?

At the house, my mother was propped on her cot doing her breathing exercises, the ones the district nurse had taught her before the nurse moved somewhere with cleaner air. She looked up at my face and knew before I said anything. Mothers are the original company men; they keep the best ledgers of all.

"What did he offer?" she asked.

"Medicine. For you. Real medicine, from Thursday Island." I set the empty basket down harder than I meant to. "And thirty percent off the debt. And he asked about strangers in town, Ma, and I gave him the wrecking story and it went down smooth, too smooth, and he wrote something in his book, and there are men on the jetty who've been there three days wearing boots that never touched water, and—" I stopped, because my voice had begun to climb toward something I couldn't afford. "Ma. He was kind to me. That's the thing. He was *kind*, and I sat there drinking his tea trying to work out the price, and the price was that there is no price, because I'd already paid it. I'd already been paying it for weeks without knowing. He bought me with courtesies. He marked me down as an asset, and assets get—" I swallowed. "Assets get used. Or written off."

My mother was quiet a long moment. Then she said, in the flat practical voice that had raised me, "Then we stop being assets."

"How? He owns the debt. He owns the jetty and the boats and the doctor and half the constabulary, and now apparently he owns my gratitude, which is worth more than all of it."

"We stop being assets," she repeated, "by becoming something he hasn't got an entry for."

I wanted to laugh. I nearly did. But she was looking at me with those drowned-grey eyes of hers—my father's eyes, God help us both, the same eyes Tobias had recognized across a crowded jetty—and behind all that weariness was the woman who had once, before the debt and the disgrace and the doctrine of invisibility, sailed a lugger through a cyclone with a broken arm because the alternative was losing the crew's pay.

"Friday," I said. "The medicine comes Friday. Which means he expects something by Friday. Men like Calloway don't invest ahead of schedule unless the return is dated."

My mother closed her eyes and breathed, four counts in, six counts out, the way the nurse taught, each breath a small negotiation with her own chest. "Then whatever we're going to do," she said, "we do it before Friday."

I stood in the doorway with the afternoon coming gold through the cracks in the boards, and I thought of the ledger in the records room with its neat columns of the living listed among the dead, and of the letters hidden in my father's strongbox at the bottom of the sea, and of Tobias Ash somewhere across the camp watching the same two men in clean boots and doing his own arithmetic.

Friday. Three days.

Somewhere above the office, I realized, Calloway dined every evening at seven, alone, with the records room below him and the lamp left burning—and I began, right there in the doorway, with my mother's breath counting time behind me, to think about keys, and ink, and whose handwriting I might learn to forge.
