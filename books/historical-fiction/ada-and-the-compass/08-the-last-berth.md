## 8. The Last Berth

There is a particular arithmetic that men like me learn too late, and it has nothing to do with money. It is the arithmetic of one and three. One berth. Three people who need it. I stood on the jetty at Port Meridian counting those numbers the way a drowning man counts waves, and every sum came out the same: somebody gets left behind, and it will be my fault, because I am the one holding the ticket.

The ticket itself was a modest thing—a strip of cardstock, the company's steam-printed letterhead, the words *SOUTHERN CROSS — SYDNEY — ONE (1) PASSENGER — SECOND CLASS* in ink so faded it looked apologetic. I had paid for it with nearly everything I carried: the last of the money Daniel Galloway had pressed into my hands on Thursday Island two years ago, plus what remained of my own wages from the pearling luggers up north. The purser at the shipping office had looked me over—my scarred hands, my salt-ruined face, my clothes that had crossed two oceans and been sorry about most of them—and quoted me a price designed to make me walk away.

I did not walk away. That is the one virtue I can claim without lying.

* * *

I had come to this coast because of a dying man's promise, and I want to be honest about how little choice there was in it. Daniel had taken the fever on Thursday Island in the wet season of '96, and I had nursed him through three weeks of it in a room above the pub, listening to him talk about a wife named Maren and a daughter named Freya as though they were saints he'd failed. He talked about the debt he'd run from, about the boat he'd let sink, about the night he watched a diver go under off the Meridian grounds and understood that the water hadn't killed him—the company had, on paper, weeks before he ever touched the sea.

"They list men dead," he told me, between coughs that bent him double, "so they can sell them north. And nobody asks questions, because dead men don't draw rations and dead men have no mothers." He made me swear to carry his letters south if the fever took him. Then the fever didn't take him—he rallied, meaner and thinner—and I stayed anyway, because by then I'd read the letters myself and understood that carrying them was not a favor. It was an obligation, the kind you can't put down once you've picked it up.

When word came that the letters might matter—that a reformist magistrate in Sydney named Wexford was building a case against indenture trafficking and wanted witnesses, documents, anything with a signature—I bought passage south and started walking toward the past.

I found Freya Galloway on the jetty at low tide, hauling shell bags twice her weight, and I knew her instantly, though I'd never seen her. She had her father's eyes. That sounds like sentiment, but I mean it mechanically: same grey, same flat watchfulness, the look of someone measuring water depth before committing. She saw me looking and gave me nothing. A girl trained to be unremarkable. I recognized the training because I'd had it too, in different waters.

* * *

The Southern Cross lay at anchor in the roads, a fat-bellied coastal steamer with rust streaking her flanks like old tears, taking on coal and stores. Nine days to departure. I learned this from the harbor master, a broad silent man named Quill who spoke perhaps forty words a day and spent all of them wisely. He also told me, unprompted, that the berth beside mine was empty, and that the company had booked out half the passenger list with clerks traveling "on business," which meant Calloway was moving paper south ahead of any trouble.

"You'll want to sail light," Quill said, which I took to mean: they will search you.

I thanked him. He looked at me a long moment—longer than he'd looked at anyone since I'd arrived—and said, "You're the one asking after the Galloways."

It wasn't a question. In a port this size, everyone knows everything within a week, and I had been clumsy. Asking after a family in a town where invisibility is survival is like striking a match in a powder store. I had already done damage before I'd delivered a single letter.

"I'm a friend," I said.

"Nobody's friend comes here." But something moved behind his eyes—not warmth exactly, more like an old wound acknowledging weather. Later I would learn why. Later I would learn his brother's name was on a manifest marked *deceased* while the man himself was shoveling guano on a island fourteen hundred miles north. But that afternoon I only knew that Quill walked away from me slowly, as though leaving something unfinished on the jetty planks.

* * *

That evening I sat across the table from Maren Galloway and watched her count breaths.

Not metaphorically. She actually counted them—I could see her doing it, the small pause between inhale and exhale, the way her hand rested flat on the table as though steadying herself against her own chest. The cough came in waves now, and between waves she sat very still, conserving, the way divers do. Her daughter dove for shell; her body had learned the same discipline from the inside out.

"Nine days," I said, and laid the ticket on the table between us.

Maren looked at it the way you'd look at a snake. Not with fear, precisely. With assessment.

"And who goes?" she asked.

"That's rather the problem."

"My husband sent you."

"Your husband asked me to deliver letters. The rest—" I spread my hands. "The rest is arithmetic."

Freya was at the stove with her back to us, pretending not to listen, which in that house was a full-time occupation. The lamp threw our shadows large against the wall, three shapes bent over a small rectangle of cardstock as though it were a map to buried treasure, which in a sense it was.

"Maren." I kept my voice level. "Magistrate Wexford needs testimony. Your husband's testimony, ideally—he witnessed the drowning off the northern grounds, he can name names, dates, buyers. Without him, the letters are allegations. With him, they're evidence. But your husband is on Thursday Island, four days' sail north, and the berth sails south."

"I know where Thursday Island is," she said dryly, and then coughed, and the cough went on long enough that Freya turned from the stove despite herself.

"The alternative," I continued, when the room was quiet again, "is that you sail. You've lived inside the company's books for twenty years. You know the debt system, the ration draws, the contracts. You could testify to the machinery even without the drowning."

"And leave my daughter here."

"Yes."

"No," she said simply.

"Maren—"

"She stays, I stay. That's not negotiable, Mr. Ash, so don't spend your breath on it. I spent enough of mine already." She said it without drama, the way people say the truest things, and I understood I had just lost an argument before it began.

Which left Freya. Which left the ledger—which we didn't yet fully possess but all believed existed somewhere in the company records or in Daniel's hidden papers. One berth. A mother who refused it. A father who couldn't reach it in time. A daughter who wouldn't take it while either parent needed it more. And a stack of documents that could topple a trafficking operation but weighed nothing, breathed nothing, loved no one.

Documents, I thought, are the ideal passengers. They don't get seasick. They don't argue. They don't look at you with their father's eyes when you tell them they're being left behind.

* * *

Later, Freya walked me out to the gate. The night was warm and smelled of salt and mangrove, and somewhere down the shore a lugger's crew was singing badly. She stopped with her hand on the latch and didn't open it.

"How much did it cost?" she asked. "The berth."

"It doesn't matter."

"It matters to me."

I told her. She absorbed the figure without blinking; she was a girl who counted things for a living, debts and minutes and shell weights. I watched her do sums behind those grey eyes and knew she was arriving at the same place I'd been standing all day.

"There are nine days," she said. "Things change in nine days."

"Things deteriorate in nine days. That's usually the direction."

A ghost of a smile crossed her mouth and vanished. "You're cheerful."

"I'm honest. It looks the same from a distance."

She opened the latch, then paused. "Mr. Ash. If it comes down to my mother, or the truth going south—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "My mother thinks she's already spent. She talks about herself like a ledger entry in red ink. But she's the only reason I'm alive, and I don't mean that the way people usually say it. I mean she made herself invisible for twenty years so I could be small enough to survive. If the choice comes, and it's her against the cause—" her jaw set, and she was suddenly so much her father's daughter it hurt to look at her, "—the cause loses. Tell me you understand that."

I understood it. I also understood that Wexford's case would likely collapse without strong evidence, that the trade would continue, that other daughters would dive other debts. Both things were true. That's the cruelty of arithmetic—it doesn't care which column you weep over.

"I understand," I said, which was true, and "we'll find another way," which I desperately hoped would become true, and she nodded once, satisfied or pretending to be, and went inside.

* * *

I walked back along the shoreline road, past the company office with its one lit window—Calloway working late, or performing lateness—and past the divers' camp where cooking fires guttered in the wind. At the end of the jetty I stopped and looked north, toward Thursday Island, invisible beyond the curve of the coast. Somewhere up there a man who shared my passengers' blood was waiting on a decision he didn't know was being made, comfortable in his cowardice, ready to be summoned or forgotten depending on which way a girl with her father's eyes decided to break.

One berth. Nine days.

And beneath my feet, in the dark water slapping against the pilings, something else waited too—Daniel Galloway's sunken boat, and whatever he'd hidden in her hull, and the truth about the night he died on paper. I'd seen Freya looking north along that same stretch of water that afternoon, with an expression I recognized because I'd worn it crossing two oceans: the look of someone who has decided to stop waiting for the past to arrive and go fetch it themselves.

I should have known then what she was planning. I was, after all, the expert on running toward trouble.

But that night I only stood on the jetty with a ticket in my pocket worth one life, and did the arithmetic again, and got the same answer, and hated it.

Three days later, Freya Galloway borrowed my boat without asking, took a diving line and a lantern, and rowed out alone toward the wreck of her father's ship—straight into black water, on a rising tide, in the dark.
