## 9. Night Dive

The tide turns at half past eleven, which means the water over the wreck will be slack around midnight, which means I have three hours to talk myself out of this and one hour to do it anyway.

I had intended to do neither. I want that on the record, wherever records are kept for girls like me—which is nowhere, which is rather the point of my life so far.

It was Tobias who put the idea in my head, though he'd be horrified to know it. He didn't say *dive the wreck*. He said, in his careful way, turning his cup on the table as if it might bolt: "Your father wrote that he left something behind. Something he couldn't carry and couldn't risk posting." A pause. "He said you'd know where."

And I did know where, the way you know where a splinter is lodged. The *Adelaide May* went down on the reef shelf a mile past the point—ran her keel onto coral in a squall, or so the story goes—and her ribs still stand up out of the sand at six fathoms like the carcass of some enormous animal the sea picked clean. I have swum over her a hundred times since I was old enough to swim at all. I have looked at that broken spine and thought: *there is where my father died*, and felt nothing so grand as grief, only the dull arithmetic of a girl whose bread depended on a dead man's debts.

Now the arithmetic has changed. Now there is a strongbox down there with my name on it, if not literally then in every way that matters, and the company wants Tobias by Friday, and Calloway smiles at me across the counter like a man who has already counted my fingers.

So: midnight, slack water, six fathoms, alone.

My mother sleeps with the door open so she can hear me move through the house. She has done it since I was small; I used to think it was tenderness. Lately I suspect she was keeping an inventory. Tonight I leave through the window over the woodbox, which groans on its runner no matter what, so I lifted the whole sash out weeks ago when I first began climbing out to sit on the roof and hate things in private. I wedge it back after. In the kitchen I write her a note and then burn it in the stove, because a note is evidence and evidence is a debt too, according to Mr. Calloway, and I am learning the company's catechism well enough to pray against it.

The moon is three days off full and lies on the water like spilled milk. I take the dinghy—the camp's third dinghy, the one with the cracked thwart nobody borrows—and row out past the point with my diving line coiled at my feet and the stone weight that hooks to my belt, and I do not let myself think about how many times I have made this exact trip in daylight, in company, under contract, with Meridian's tally book waiting on the jetty to convert my breath into their coin.

Tonight the breath is mine.

* * *

You would think a girl who dives for a living would find night water unremarkable. You would be wrong. Day water is work; night water is a country. The reef that by day is a market—clams gaping, crayfish swaggering out of their cracks like small landlords—at night goes quiet and strange, all edges softened, everything listening. My lamp hangs from the dinghy's bow and throws a cone of yellow down maybe two fathoms before the dark drinks it. Below that cone, the water is the color of a closed door.

I anchor over the wreck by feel, counting strokes from the point the way I've done since I was twelve. Then I sit in the rocking boat and do the thing divers do, which is argue with ourselves.

Here is the argument's shape. For: the box, the ledger page, whatever proof my father salted away in the ribs of his own ruin. Against: six fathoms at night, alone, with a line that can foul and a current that can turn and a company that would very much like me at the bottom of any convenient body of water provided it looked accidental, because accidents are Meridian's native language. I have read enough entries in that office now to know how fluently they speak it.

Also against: the simple animal fact that I am afraid.

I was raised to believe fear was sensible. My mother's whole doctrine—be small, be dull, be nothing they bother to write down—is fear with good posture. And it kept us alive, more or less, the way a snail's shell keeps it alive: safe, and carried everywhere by someone else's tide.

But here is what the last fortnight has taught me, in Tobias's letters and Maren's confessions and Calloway's courteous little knives: invisibility isn't safety. It's just danger with better manners. They marked me months ago. I felt the pencil note go into the margin beside my name and told myself it was imagination. It was inventory.

So I hook the stone to my belt, spit in my mask and rinse it the way Pa taught me before he taught me anything else, and go over the side.

* * *

The first fathom is cold, the second is colder, the third stops mattering. I equalize my ears, hand over hand down the anchor line to the weight, and hang there a moment while my eyes adjust and the reef resolves itself out of the murk—coral heads like sleeping cattle, a cloud of baitfish parting around me without interest. The lamp above makes a trembling coin of light. Everything below it is mine to guess at.

I have the wreck's geography in my hands before my eyes find her. Six fathoms down the sand shelves, and the *Adelaide May* lies with her bow uphill, wedged where the storm shoved her twelve years ago—or however long ago; the dates have become unreliable, seeing as how the man who sailed her has been reliably dead for five years and reliably alive for three. Her starboard rail is gone. Her mast lies along the reef like a fallen tree, crusted white with coral, home now to a city of small things that have never heard of Meridian Trading.

There is a particular feeling in diving your father's grave when you have recently learned he isn't in it. It is not anger, exactly, or not only. It is the sense of having mourned at the wrong address for years. I run my glove along a rib of the hull and the coral cuts cold through the leather, and I think: *you were never here*. All those days I swam above you and budgeted my breath against your debt—I was holding vigil over an empty room.

Which does not mean the room is empty tonight.

The letters said: *where the mast foot meets the keelson, beneath the ballast stone I always cursed.* I remember the ballast stone. There were four loose ones in the bilge and Pa swore at them in three languages every time we shipped water. I find the fallen mast by touch, follow it down its length to the step, and begin moving stones.

This is the tedious part, and I will confess the terror arrived during the tedium, which is how terror prefers to travel. Not as a shark, all fin and announcement, but as a slow seepage. My lamp is forty feet up and wavering. The stones are heavy and the sand sucks at them and my air is a purse I can feel emptying, and somewhere in the fourth minute of shifting rock in the dark a thought arrives fully formed, the way the worst thoughts do: *if my line fouls down here, no one knows where I am.*

No one ever knows where I am. That has been the strategy.

My hand finds something that is not stone. Iron—a ring bolt, set into the keelson, and threaded through it, a length of chain gone furry with growth, running down into a hollow between two frames where the sand has banked like snow against a fence. I dig. Sand fills the hole behind my fingers. I dig again, faster, and my heart picks up its pace and my chest begins its early argument about air, and I make myself slow down because panic underwater is a debt collector too—it takes everything you have and calls it interest.

Third attempt, the chain comes free. It is shackled to a box.

* * *

I should tell it plainly, because everything after happened fast and I want at least one honest account of it, even if the account is only me.

The box came up out of its hollow trailing sand, iron-strapped, smaller than a biscuit tin, and heavier than sin. I got both arms around it and turned for the surface, and that was when my line fouled.

Not on the wreck. On me. In the dark I had let the lifeline pay out slack around my hip, and when I turned the loop dropped and caught on the same ring bolt I'd found the chain through, cinched like a noose pulled tight. One hard tug told me the truth of it: I could not rise, and the box in my arms made my hands useless, and my air was already spent past the comfortable mark, and forty feet above me a coin of lamplight rocked gently on a world that did not know I was down here.

Here follows the part where I nearly drowned, and I will relate it without heroics, because there weren't any.

I dropped the box. That was the first decision and I made it badly late—ten seconds of stupid clinging to the thing I'd come for before my lungs overruled my greed and I let it fall into the sand. Then I got my hands to the fouled line and pulled, and it held, and pulled again, and it held, and the third pull was not strength but fury, a full-body convulsion of it, and the loop jumped the bolt and I shot upward with my ears shrieking.

Too fast. I knew it was too fast even as the water brightened around me, knew what rapid ascent does to a diver, had seen old Salty Kew come up crooked and coughing blood one season—but the surface was coming whether I agreed to it or not, and I burst through into air and lamplight and hauled myself gasping over the gunwale like something the sea had reconsidered.

I lay on the boards with my heart going like a pump handle and the sky wheeling overhead, and I did the arithmetic I always do, the one that keeps me sane: count the breaths, count them again, keep counting until the world narrows to numbers and numbers can't lie. Twelve breaths. Thirty. At fifty my hands stopped shaking enough to notice they were bleeding where the coral had gotten through the gloves.

And then—this is the part I did not expect—I laughed.

Not much. A short, ugly, delighted sound, there in the bottom of a borrowed dinghy a mile from shore at one in the morning. Because lying there with blood on my gloves and my ears ringing, I understood something that has been assembling itself in me for weeks without my permission.

They talk about the sea taking people. The company talks that way especially—*lost to the sea*, entered neat in the ration columns, men erased in tidy copperplate as though the ocean were a clerk on their payroll. My father was lost to the sea. The islanders were lost to the sea. Every inconvenient man in that ledger drowned, eventually, on paper, whenever paper required it.

But I was down there tonight, and I saw what actually happens at six fathoms in the dark. The sea doesn't take anyone. It just holds what falls into it, patiently, without opinion, until someone with a line and a lamp and a reason comes down and takes it back.

Meridian writes the drowning. The water merely signs it.

Well. I have decided I am done being signed for. I have been entered in other people's books since before I could walk—entered as debt, as daughter-of-a-debtor, as a ration figure and a dive tally and a marginal note in Mr. Calloway's courteous hand. Tonight the sea tried to enter me too, and I came up out of its ledger breathing.

* * *

I went back down.

Tobias would say that was madness, and he'd be right, and I did it anyway, because the box was on the bottom and so was everything else—my mother's medicine, our exit, the case, the whole brittle architecture of Friday—and because I had already paid the fear once and refused to pay twice for the same goods.

Second descent, calm, methodical, the line paid out clear over my shoulder where I could see it. The box came up easy. I lashed it under the thwart with the spare rope, wrapped it in my oilskin, and rowed home with the moon sinking westward and my shoulders screaming and something in my chest sitting differently than it has sat for years. Lighter. No—that's not honest. Not lighter. *Claimed.* Like land that has finally been surveyed.

At home I did not wake my mother. I built the fire up in the kitchen stove, set the box on the table, and worked the padlock with a poker tip and a hairpin until it surrendered with a click that sounded enormous in the quiet house.

Inside: oilcloth, and under the oilcloth, paper.

A ledger page, folded in eighths, dense with figures in a hand I know almost as well as my own reflection—because it is the hand my own slants from. Columns of names. Island names. Dates of supposed deaths. And beside them, in the margin, in my father's furious scrawl, a buyer's name repeated over and over like a man practicing an accusation until he dared send it.

And beneath the ledger page, tied with string gone soft with damp, a bundle of letters addressed in that same hand. Addressed to me.

*Freya,* says the topmost envelope. Just that. My name, in ink, in a handwriting I had spent five years teaching myself to forget.

I sat down at the kitchen table with the fire ticking and the box open in front of me, and outside the window the tide began to turn, and I reached for the letters with hands that had held the sea's whole weight that night and found they trembled anyway.
