## 10. The Father's Letters

The strongbox was smaller than I remembered.

That was the first thing that struck me, kneeling on the sand with it dripping in my hands—smaller than the thing that had lived in my imagination for three years. When you're a child and your father goes down with his boat, everything he leaves behind grows enormous. The gap he leaves is measured in the size of the absence. But here was the box itself, no bigger than a bread tin, crusted green at the hinges, light enough to carry in one arm, and I sat back on my heels in the dark and felt almost insulted by it.

Three years of grief, and it fit under one arm.

I had wrapped myself dry by the time I reached the house, though the shivering had nothing to do with cold. My mother slept in the back room, her breathing doing its wet, laboring work, and Tobias Ash sat up waiting for me with the lamp turned low, as if he'd known all along where I'd gone and what I'd come back with. He looked at the box, then at me, and asked nothing.

"Tomorrow," I said, because my voice wasn't ready yet.

He nodded and went to the lean-to with his bedroll, and I set the box on the table between the lamp and me, and we were alone together, the dead man's property and the daughter who'd stopped being allowed to grieve him properly three years ago when the company declared the debt hers.

The lock surrendered to a flat iron from the tool shed. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff with salt: a ledger page, folded twice; a document of several sheets in a hand I didn't recognize; and letters. Eleven of them. Bound with string, addressed on their faces in a slanted hand I would have known anywhere, even blind, even drowned.

*For Freya.*

Not *To* Freya. *For.* As though he'd written them the way a man buries something—for later, for whoever came digging—and hadn't decided until too late that later required a living addressee.

I didn't open them first. I want that recorded somewhere, even if nowhere keeps records of things like this. I made myself read the other papers first, because some stubborn grain in me wanted proof before feeling, evidence before grief. I had spent six years learning the company's arithmetic. I knew better than to pay out emotion on credit.

* * *

The confession was four pages, unsigned but dated, in a careful clerk's hand that belonged to neither my father nor any diver I knew. It named names. It named the *Coromandel Star*, the buyer in Macassar, the sums paid per head—the word used was not *head*, of course; the paper was too polite for that. The paper said *passengers* and *transfers* and *indentures extended*, and beneath its politeness lay the plain machinery: men listed as drowned in Meridian's books, alive and sold south and east, their rations drawn against accounts that would never complain because complaining men don't get rations, they get held.

My father had witnessed a drowning that wasn't one. That was what it came down to, reading it. A man called Weri—Tobias had spoken the name on the jetty like a debt he owed—had been put over the side of the *Ada May* with his wrists bound, in front of witnesses, because he'd asked where the transfers ended. And my father had been close enough to see it, and far enough to survive it, and had understood within a day that surviving it meant either running or joining.

He ran. He let the sea take the blame, and the sea obliged, the sea being the only honest party in the whole arrangement.

And the ledger page—I spread it flat under the lamp and there they were, the figures I'd copied a hundred times in Mr. Calloway's office without seeing them, except now I saw them with my father's marginalia alongside, tiny pencil notes in the margins like a diver's bubbles rising off the page. *Drawn 3 months after death.* *Paid to whom?* *W.—alive, Thursday Is., see letter.* My father, who could barely make his own tally marks legible, had done more auditing in one week than I had managed in a year, because he'd had the advantage of knowing what he was looking for: not numbers that didn't add up, but people who did.

I sat back. The lamp hissed. Somewhere outside a night bird said its one stupid sentence over and over.

It should have felt like triumph. Tobias had crossed two oceans for these papers; a magistrate in Sydney was waiting to turn them into justice. Instead I felt the particular desolation of a girl discovering that her family's ruin was not an accident of weather. We had not been unlucky. We had been *managed*. Every breath I'd counted underwater against my father's debt had been payment on a fiction, and the fiction had a signature, and the signature had a buyer, and the whole rotten chain led back through my kitchen table to men in offices who would never have to hold their breath for anything in their lives.

I picked up the letters because there was nothing left between me and them.

* * *

The first was dated eight days after the *Ada May* went down—or after it was said to have gone down—and I will transcribe my own reaction to it honestly: I laughed. One bark of it, ugly, into the empty room, loud enough that I heard my mother stir and froze until her breathing settled.

Because the letter began, *My dear girl, by now you will have heard I am lost at sea, and I know how you will be carrying on.*

*I know how you will be carrying on.* As though I were weeping into a hanky over a sprained ankle. As though he knew me at all. I had not carried on. I had dried my eyes inside a week because the company's man came counting, and I had learned to dive in water that frightened me, and I had stood at a ledger desk while Mr. Calloway explained, with courtesy, that grief was not deductible. That was how I had carried on. Like a mule carries on. With the load shifting onto my back.

But I kept reading, because underneath the fatuous opening there was a voice I knew, my father's actual voice, warm and quick and always slightly behind himself, apologizing for the last thing while doing the next.

*You must hate me,* he wrote. *Do, if it keeps you safe. Hatred is private. Grief gets talked about.*

Even his cowardice was instructive. Even his abandonment came with lessons attached. I could hear him saying it across the table, leaning forward, sure that logic was love.

There were eleven letters. He had written them over two years—from Thursday Island, from a pearling lugger out of Batavia, from wherever the money he sent us was earned and skimmed and sent through channels that never touched his name. Some were long. Some were three lines. In one he described the color of the water in the Torres Strait so precisely that I could see it, and my chest hurt, because he had never once described the water of our own bay to me, and I realized he had been saving his eloquence for the life after the lie.

In another: *Your mother will have told you I'm dead and she'll be right, because the man you had is dead, and what's left is a witness, and a witness is just a debt with legs.*

A debt with legs. He wrote about himself the way the company wrote about us. I wondered if he knew that. I wondered if it had ever occurred to him, in all his cleverness, that he had adopted their grammar, that he'd fled the ledger only to keep speaking in its terms—that he'd reduced himself to an instrument, a testimony, a thing that anchors a case, and left the actual living of our lives entirely to us.

The ninth letter was the worst, and I read it four times, and each time it got worse, which I hadn't known was possible.

*If you are reading these, then you found the box, which means you went down to the Ada May, which means you dove at night, alone, and I cannot tell you the size of the thing that does to me, that my daughter went into the wreck I couldn't face to bring up the truth I wouldn't carry.*

Well. Good. Let it do its size to him.

*Forgive me,* the tenth began, and I put it down unopened for an hour, because I was not going to be rushed into absolution by a dead man's schedule. Forgiveness, I had learned, was like air: you spent it according to your own depth, not anyone else's demands.

When I opened it, he didn't ask cleanly, which was somehow more honest. He circled it. He gave me excuses the way Calloway gave kindness—as instruments. He'd stayed away to protect us. He'd stayed away because the money had to keep flowing. He'd stayed away because coming back meant the company would connect him to us and then none of us would be worth anything except as leverage. Each excuse was true. That was the devilry of it. Each excuse was true, and stacked together they amounted to three years of a mother coughing in a damp room and a daughter counting her childhood out in fathoms, and the truth of each separate plank didn't stop the raft from being built on our backs.

*Tender, cowardly, full of excuses*—that was the shape of him, page after page. A man who loved us so much he calculated it, and whose calculations always concluded that the loving should be done from a distance, at cost to nobody but the loved.

Near dawn I came to the eleventh letter, the shortest. No date. His hand unsteady, as if written fast or drunk or both.

*Freya. If it comes to a choice, send the papers and keep yourself. Don't send yourself. Papers can testify. You can only drown.*

I read it, and I read it again, and then I did the thing I had not done since the night the company's man told me my father's debt was mine: I cried—not the way he predicted in the first letter, not carrying on, but quietly, furiously, with my teeth shut, the way you cry when you've been trained your whole life that noise costs money.

* * *

Tobias found me there when the window went grey. He didn't say anything at first, just stood in the doorway looking at the eleven letters laid out in rows on the table like specimens, like a catch of something hauled up from deep water.

"He wrote to me," I said. "Every few months. For two years."

"I know." He came and stood at the table's edge, not touching anything. "Daniel—that was his name aboard, the name he wore—he wrote to me too. Shorter versions. He was always editing himself for an audience."

"He was always editing himself, full stop."

"Yes," Tobias said simply. "Yes, he was."

We stood there while the grey turned to pale gold at the window's edge. Then Tobias tapped one finger, very lightly, on the confession—the clerk's neat pages naming the buyer, the ship, the sums.

"This is what the magistrate needs. This, and your father standing in front of him to say it aloud. Papers can be called forged. A living witness can't."

"And if the witness stays hidden?"

"Then the case limps. Might live, might die." He looked at me with those sea-scoured eyes, patient as tide. "There's a berth on the Southern Cross, nine days from now. Your mother needs it more than any of us need to say it aloud. But the papers go whether or not anyone does."

I gathered the letters up—all eleven—and tied the string back around them the way he had left it, and I noticed my hands were steady, which surprised me, given that everything under the steadiness had shifted overnight like a channel bottom after a storm.

"I'm keeping these," I said. "The rest—the confession, the ledger page—you can have for Sydney. But these are mine. He wrote *for* me. He doesn't get to have them back just because it's convenient for the case."

Something moved through Tobias's face that might have been respect, or relief, or recognition of a family resemblance he'd been polite enough not to mention.

"He'd agree with you," he said. "That was the trouble with Daniel. He always agreed with the people he was failing."

Outside, the morning was assembling itself over the water, flat and bright and innocent, and somewhere across it, on Thursday Island, a debt with legs was waiting to find out whether his daughter would summon him home.

I tied the strings tight, put the letters inside my shirt, against my skin, and went to wake my mother—because there were nine days left, and a decision to make about blood, and I intended to make it with my eyes open this time, which was more than anyone else in this story had ever managed.
