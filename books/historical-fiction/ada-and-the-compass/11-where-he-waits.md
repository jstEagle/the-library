## 11. Where He Waits

Tobias Ash had a way of sitting that made a room seem to lean away from him. He took up no particular space—lean as a jetty post, hands folded like a man at his own funeral—but chairs creaked under him anyway, as if they knew what he carried. Tonight, in my mother's kitchen, with the lamp turned low so the light wouldn't leak through the shutters, he sat at our table like a verdict waiting to be read.

I'd made tea because my hands needed something to do that wasn't shaking. Three cups. I don't know why I made three. Habit, perhaps. Or hope, which is just habit wearing its Sunday clothes.

"Thursday Island," he said, without ceremony, because Tobias Ash did not believe in easing into things. "He's on Thursday Island."

The teapot stopped mid-pour. A dark bloom spread across the tablecloth, and I watched it the way you watch anything when watching it means not having to speak.

"He's been there how long?" My voice came out level. I was almost proud of it.

"Two years. Maybe longer. I met him in Batavia, then lost him, then found him again in Singapore. He works the boats up there. Diving." Tobias's mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "Seems he can't leave the water alone either. It's in the family."

"It's in the *debt*," I said. "The water's just where Meridian keeps us."

My mother's cup rattled against its saucer—a small sound, a bird's-bone sound—and then went still. She had known already. She had confessed her knowing two nights past, and we were still all of us walking carefully around that fact like furniture in a dark room. But knowing he was alive in the abstract was one thing. Knowing he was alive at coordinates—that he could be reached by boat, by letter, by a girl with ink-stained fingers and a stolen ledger—was another thing entirely.

"He's waiting," Tobias said. "That's what he asked me to tell you. There's a steamer from Thursday Island every fortnight. If you send word—if you send *one word*—he'll come down and sail on the Southern Cross. His testimony plus the ledger plus the manifest, and the magistrate in Sydney won't be able to look away. He said—" and here Tobias hesitated, the first hesitation I'd seen in him, and I understood that what came next would cost him something, "—he said to tell you he'd understand if you didn't."

"He'd *understand*," I repeated.

The lamp flame stood very straight between us. Outside, the tide was coming in over the mudflats with that long, patient hiss, the sound this town made instead of silence.

"Three years," I said. "Three years we thought he was at the bottom of the channel. I used to dive over the wreck of his boat and say goodnight to it. Do you know what that does to a person? Saying goodnight to a hole full of sand and crabs?" I set the teapot down before I could do something with it that tea couldn't survive. "And the whole time he was drinking arrack in some harbor bar two thousand miles north, close enough to send letters but not close enough to come home."

"He sent money," my mother said quietly.

"He sent *money*." I laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, jagged. "Money buys flour, Ma. It doesn't buy back the funerals. We buried an empty coffin. Father Okafor said words over a box of ballast stones. I was fourteen and I held your hand while you shook, and he was *understanding* himself somewhere with a view of the water."

Maren Galloway looked at her tea as though it might contain instructions. She had burned nothing yet; that was still to come. That night she only said, "Freya. Sit down."

I sat. Because she was my mother and because my knees had opinions of their own.

"There's more," Tobias said. "There's always more with men like your father. The rendezvous isn't simple. He won't come unless the signal comes through a particular channel—the Chinese laundry on Harborside, a mark chalked on the doorframe. Calloway watches the jetty now. He watches everything. Any word you send north passes through hands that would sell their own mothers for a percentage."

"So we're to trust the mail," I said, "and the laundry, and a fortnight of luck, and a man whose last act as my father was to die beautifully and stay dead."

"That about sums it," Tobias said, and for the first time since he'd walked out of the sea and into our lives, he sounded tired. Not weary the way my mother was weary, worn down by cough and debt. Tired the way soldiers are tired, the tiredness of a man who has done the arithmetic of other people's families too many times.

I should have hated him for bringing the news. Some part of me did. He was the door through which my father walked back into the world, and doors can't help what comes through them, but you resent them anyway.

* * *

Tobias's account, later that night, by the jetty, where we went because walls in a company town have ears:

He had met my father in a hospital ward in Batavia, of all places. Not diving accident—fever. A bunk beside his, two scarred men sweating through the same season. My father talked in his sleep, Tobias said. Names, mostly. *Maren. Freya.* And numbers—debt figures recited like prayers, the catechism of a man who had fled a ledger and found it waiting behind his own eyelids.

"When the fever broke, he told me everything," Tobias said. The moon was up, half-eaten, laying a silver road across the water toward Thursday Island, which was somewhere at the end of it being lived in by a man with my chin. "How the boat didn't sink. How he paid the deckhand to scuttle her empty and report him drowned. How he'd seen something off the Pellew Islands that he couldn't unknow—a Meridian lugger taking on 'dead' divers who were very much alive, and very much chained."

"I know," I said. "We found the entries. The rations drawn for dead men. The manifest."

"You know the shape of it. He knows the names. Faces. Dates. He watched a boy go into the water off that lugger with his wrists bound, and he dove after him, and he got him out, and then he understood that getting him out was the most dangerous thing anyone had ever done in those waters. Because a living witness is worth more than pearl shell, and Meridian doesn't let its assets walk." Tobias picked at the scarred back of his hand, an old habit, I was learning. "Your father ran. I won't dress it up. He ran, and he hid, and he spent three years being a coward with excellent penmanship."

"And then?"

"And then he started writing. Letters he never sent. Evidence he never delivered. A man can only drink himself brave so many nights before the brave part starts keeping books of its own." He looked at me sideways. "When the fever convinced him he was dying—which it nearly managed—he gave me the satchel. Said: take it south, find my daughter, she has her mother's spine and her own mind, she'll know what to do. And if she wants nothing to do with me, tell her—" he stopped.

"Tell me what?"

"Tell her the tide goes out on everything eventually, and what it leaves is yours to claim or burn."

I thought about that for a long moment. The water below the jetty slapped the pylons, patient, indifferent, keeping its accounts.

"He's a poet now," I said finally. "Wonderful. Three years late and two oceans away, and he's developed a literary style."

"Would you rather he hadn't?"

I didn't answer, which was an answer, and Tobias, to his credit, let it stand.

* * *

The deciding took four days, though nobody announced that a decision was being made. Decisions in our house arrived the way weather arrived—by pressure, by signs, by the behavior of animals.

On the first day I said nothing would make me send word. On the second day I copied trafficking entries until my wrist ached and thought about the boy my father pulled from the water, alive because one coward once did a single brave thing. On the third day my mother coughed blood into a rag she thought I didn't see, and I sat in the dark doing the arithmetic: mother's lungs against father's testimony against nine days against one berth against the whole rotten architecture of the Meridian Trading Company.

On the fourth day I found Tobias mending a net on the beach—he'd taken to useful idleness, the better to look like a man with nowhere to be—and sat down beside him in the sand.

"If I send the signal," I said, "and he comes, and Calloway takes him before the Southern Cross sails—"

"Then we've handed the company its last loose thread," Tobias said, not looking up from the net. "Calloway doesn't need a trial. He needs a drowning that stays drowned. Your father resurfaces within reach of Meridian, and he goes back under permanently. That's the risk. You've seen the men on the jetty. You know what Friday looks like from where we're standing."

"So say something useful."

"The useful thing is this: it isn't your choice alone anymore. It stopped being your choice the night you opened that strongbox. There's a case now. There's a magistrate waiting. Every day we hold the evidence and don't move it, people are being sold off luggers with their wrists bound. You can hate him until the sun burns out—you'd be right to—but the question in front of you isn't whether he deserves rescuing. It's whether the truth deserves his voice."

I picked up a shell and threw it at the water, which declined to comment.

"My mother will say send for him," I said. "She'll say it gently and mean it absolutely, because she's spent twenty years loving a man the way you love a bad investment—refusing to sell at the bottom. And I'll stand there hating both of them, and hating myself most of all, because some stupid animal part of me wants my father even now. Even knowing everything. Isn't that the joke? They can make us invisible, they can drown us on paper, they can chain boys in the hold of a lugger—but they can't stop a daughter from wanting her da. That's the one thing Meridian never figured out how to tax."

Tobias set down the net. For a while he said nothing, and the tide filled the silence with its long gray patience.

"When I was dying in that ward," he said, "the man in the next bunk held my hand. I didn't ask him to. I didn't even know his name. He just did it, night after night, because that's what you do when someone's burning. Whatever else your father is—and he is a coward, and a liar, and a man who mistook disappearing for protecting—he held a stranger's hand for six nights in Batavia. People are not one thing, Freya. Not even the ones who failed us. Especially not those."

I looked out at the channel, at the wreck-marker buoy riding the swell, marking the place where a boat full of ballast stones had died so a man could live badly.

"Chalk the mark," I said.

Tobias turned. "You're certain?"

"No," I said. "I am many things, Mr. Ash, but certain has never been among them. Ask the sea—I've lied to it my whole life and it's never believed me once." I stood and brushed the sand from my skirt. "Send the signal tomorrow. Through the laundry, quiet as you can manage. And if he comes—"

I stopped. The words I wanted were *if he comes I'll forgive him*, and they were lies, and the words after that were *if he comes I'll kill him*, and those were lies too, and what remained was the truth, which was smaller and stranger than either.

"If he comes," I said, "he sleeps in the boatshed and speaks to no one until the ship sails. He gets one berth's worth of my patience and not a drop more. And he tells that magistrate every name, every date, every bound wrist—starting with the ones he ran from."

"That I can carry," Tobias said.

I walked back along the tideline as the light went copper and then violet, and behind me the sea kept saying what it always said, which was *everything ends here, everything begins here*, and ahead of me the lamps of the town were coming on one by one, and in the window of our house I could see my mother's silhouette moving, small and bent and burning her husband's history page by page in the stove—for I learned that only later, too late, the way one learns everything that matters—

—and on Harborside, in the morning, a boy from the neighbor's house stood across the lane from the laundry for a long time, watching the doorframe, and then walked, unhurried, toward the company office.
