## 12. The Informer

There is a particular quality to trouble, I have found. It does not announce itself the way a storm does, with a greenish light and a falling of the barometer. Trouble arrives the way damp arrives in a timber house—quietly, invisibly, until one morning the wall gives way and you understand it had been eating you for years.

On the Tuesday that ended everything we had managed to keep hidden, trouble arrived in the person of Danny Quill, aged eleven, with sand still on his knees and a look of self-importance so profound you might have thought he'd discovered gold rather than gossip.

I was shelling peas on the verandah because my hands needed something to do besides shake. Freya had gone down to the jetty with Tobias Ash—the stranger whose face wore its history like scars wear skin—and the house had that particular hush of a place where too many truths are stacked in too small a room.

"Mrs. Galloway," said Danny, from the gate, in the tone of a boy who has rehearsed his entrance twice on the walk over. "There's been men asking after your house."

I did not look up from the peas. This is a skill women of my generation perfected before we could read: the art of looking at exactly what is in front of us while our whole mind runs ahead like a dog let off the lead. "Have they," I said.

"Two of them. Down by the store. Asking Mr. Quill who lives at the end of the shell road." He shifted his weight. "Mr. Quill told them he didn't rightly know. But he does know. Everyone knows."

Everyone knows. In a town of four hundred souls, secrecy is not a wall; it is a curtain, and curtains are for rooms, not towns. I had known this when I burned nothing yet and hid everything. I had known it and done it anyway, because love makes optimists of the most clear-eyed among us.

"Thank you, Danny," I said, and gave him a penny, because information freely given should be paid for anyway—it teaches the giver his own value—and watched him run off down the shell road with his treasure, already composing the next telling.

Then I sat very still for a long moment, listening to the sea make its one remark over and over, and understood that the noose had finished being tied and was now merely waiting for the drop.

* * *

You will want to know what I burned, and why, and what it cost me. So let me go back, as memory insists on doing, to the beginning.

Elias wrote me eleven letters in three years. Eleven letters carried across two oceans by men paid in ways I chose never to ask about, arriving in oilcloth bundles like contraband—which is precisely what they were. The first came fourteen months after the boat went down, after I had wept myself into a kind of permanent weather, after I had taught Freya to say her father's name without her voice breaking. It arrived inside a bale of canvas from Singapore, addressed in his slanting hand—that hand I had watched across twenty years of ledgers and laundry lists and love notes passed under tables at dances where we were both pretending not to look.

*Maren,* it began, and I knew him before I knew him, the way you know a footstep on stairs.

He was alive. He was sorry. He was a coward—I can say it now; I earned the right through years of defending him even to myself. He had faked his death to escape Meridian's debt, which was growing teeth, and more than the debt: he had seen something at the company's island station that would have drowned him surer than any sea. He sent money. He sent instructions. *Never let them learn I live. If they suspect it, they will come for you and the girl—not for money. For silence.*

For silence. As though silence were a cargo that could be seized at the dock.

I read that first letter forty times. Then I did what I had always done, what the sea teaches you if you live beside it long enough: I made the truth smaller so it could fit inside a survivable life. I hid the letters in the flour tin, then beneath the floorboards, then—when Freya grew sharp-eyed and the house felt porous—in a kerosene tin buried under the lemon tree, wrapped in waxed cloth against the damp. I became a woman with two selves: the one who shelled peas and coughed politely into her handkerchief, and the one who knelt in the dark of the garden every few months to check that a dead man's words were still dry.

When Freya began making herself invisible—when I saw her learning to walk through rooms without disturbing the air, learning to be unremarkable the way her father had been remarkable and paid for it—I told myself this was wisdom. I called it protection. I built a doctrine out of fear and named it love, which is the oldest forgery in the world.

And now the boy had come up the shell road with sand on his knees, and strangers were asking questions at the store, and the doctrine lay exposed for what it had always been: a delay, not a defence.

* * *

Freya came home at dusk with that set to her shoulders I had learned to read the way sailors read cloud. Something had happened at the water. She didn't speak of it, and I didn't ask, and between us stretched the particular silence of two people keeping secrets from each other in the same small kitchen—one secret mine, one hers, and neither of us fools enough to think the other blind.

It was Freya who confirmed what Danny had begun. "Men on the jetty," she said, setting down the water bucket. Her voice was level, which frightened me more than trembling would have. "Company men. Calloway's put a watch on the landing. And there was a man walking past the house twice this evening, slow, taking his time about it."

"Calloway," I repeated. The name sat in my chest like a swallowed stone. I had met the man once, at the debt review, years ago—a soft-spoken figure with clean cuffs and the patience of a fisherman watching a line. He had thanked me for coming. He had remembered Freya's age. Men who remember your child's age are men who intend to use it.

"He knows something," Freya said. "Or suspects. Either way, Mother—" She stopped herself. My daughter had inherited my talent for making the truth smaller, and I heard her do it now, folding away whatever she and Tobias had found, whatever plan was forming behind her eyes like a storm out at sea. "Either way, we should be careful."

Careful. We had been careful for three years. Careful was a floorboard and a lemon tree and a woman kneeling in the dark with a hurricane lamp turned low.

After she slept—or lay awake performing sleep, which amounts to the same thing in a house with paper walls—I went out to the garden.

* * *

The moon was a fingernail paring, which suited me. I dug up the kerosene tin with my hands rather than a spade, because spades ring against shells and shells carry sound across a quiet town at night. The waxed cloth had held. Inside, eleven letters in their oilcloth, and beneath them the older ones—the ones from before, Elias's letters from courtship and early marriage, kept all these years in the bottom of my sewing box like pressed flowers.

I had meant, when I carried them out, to bury them again deeper, or move them, or some other rearrangement of deck chairs. That is what I told myself as I knelt there. But I had also brought the lamp, and matches, and a woman does not bring matches to a garden by accident.

Here is what I knew, kneeling in the cold sand with my husband's handwriting in my lap: papers convict. Papers hang. Every magistrate in the colonies reads ink the way priests read scripture, and somewhere in those letters—if not evidence, then implication; if not confession, then context—was enough to hang my husband and orphan my daughter twice over. The company had lawyers the way the sea has waves. And the company had grown suspicious.

And here is what I knew besides: that a man hiding on Thursday Island, waiting on word from his family, was protected only by the absence of proof that he existed. Every letter I kept was a rope with his neck in one end.

Love, I have come to believe, is mostly arithmetic conducted in the dark.

I burned the old ones first. The courtship letters, the ones where he was young and foolish and called me *my own Maren* in a hand that hadn't yet learned to slant away from things. They caught quickly, being written on thin paper by a young man with much to say. I watched the flames take his looping capitals, watched *my own* curl and blacken, and felt nothing I had expected to feel. Not grief, exactly. More like paying off a debt—a relief with sorrow folded through it, the way salt is folded through seawater.

Then the eleven.

These were harder. These I read once more, quickly, by the shielded lamp—*Maren, keep the girl from the water; Maren, tell her nothing; Maren, I was a coward and am a coward still, but a living coward can send money and a dead one cannot*—and with each reading the same sentence rose in me like a bubble through deep water: *you let us believe.* You let your daughter count your death in breaths underwater. You let me teach her grief. You sent money and instructions and not one word of apology plain enough for a child to hold.

I fed the letters to the flame one page at a time. The waxed oilcloth made the fire greedy; it roared briefly and I smothered it with sand, heart hammering, certain the whole town must have seen the light—certain Calloway himself stood behind the lemon tree with his clean cuffs and his patient hands. Nothing. Only the sea, making its one remark. Only my own breath going ragged in the cold.

The last thing to burn was the earliest letter, the one that had started it all, and as the edges browned I read the final line one last time: *Forgive me, Maren. I mean to earn it.*

Paper lies so easily. Even as it burns, it lies. I watched the words vanish and thought: he has had three years, and forgiveness is not a debt a man pays by post.

I buried the tin empty. I knelt there a moment longer with ash under my fingernails, weeping—I will admit it, since I have admitted everything else—weeping not for him but for the version of myself who had kept those letters dry and safe for three years as though they were a heartbeat, as though paper could be a man. I had preserved the past like a specimen in spirit, and now I had destroyed it to protect the living, and both acts had felt like love, and I no longer knew which of them love actually was.

Perhaps that is the honest definition. Love is whichever terrible thing you do at two in the morning in a garden, and cannot explain to anyone afterward without sounding mad.

* * *

Morning brought confirmation, as mornings do.

Tobias Ash came to the back door at first light—not the front, never the front anymore—and stood hatless with the morning behind him, and I saw in his face the news before he spoke it. A man carries bad news the way he carries a heavy load: slightly forward, braced.

"There's four of them on the jetty now," he said. "Rotating shifts. And Calloway's taken a room at the hotel—he arrived last night, personally. He doesn't send men to watch a debtors' widow, Mrs. Galloway. He comes himself when it matters."

"When it matters," I repeated. My voice sounded far away to me, as voices do at the edge of things.

"I'll be leaving within the day," Tobias said. "Every hour I'm seen near this house puts weight on the other end of their suspicion." He hesitated, and I saw the scar tissue at his jaw catch the light, and I thought: here is a man who has already survived the worst thing that will ever happen to him and walks around inside that knowledge daily. "Your daughter is braver than either of us. Keep her from spending it all at once."

He touched his hat brim and went. From the window I watched him take the shell road toward town, unhurried, a stranger again, and I thought about how the bravest people always leave quietly, because noise is for those who need an audience.

Freya emerged as he vanished from view. She had seen him go; her eyes followed the empty road a moment, then came to rest on me, and narrowed—just slightly, just enough.

"You've been digging," she said.

"Gardening," I corrected.

"In the dark?"

"The light attracts moths." I smiled, and it was the smile I had practiced for twenty years, the one that closes conversations like a door. But my daughter had spent three years learning to be invisible in a company town, and invisible people see everything, and she looked at me with her father's eyes—God help us both, with her father's exact eyes—and said:

"Mama. Where are his letters?"

And because I had burned them six hours earlier, and because a lie requires a past to stand on and mine had just gone up in smoke, I opened my mouth to answer and found nothing there but ash.

Before I could manufacture anything—before the old machinery of concealment could turn over—the gate creaked.

A boy stood there. Not Danny Quill. A smaller boy, one of the company children, holding his cap in both hands, wearing the expression of someone delivering a message he doesn't fully understand.

"Beg pardon, ma'am," he said. "Mr. Calloway's compliments. He'll call this afternoon." The boy swallowed. "He's bringing medicine. For your cough."

The sea made its one remark. Somewhere down at the jetty, four men changed shift.

I looked at my daughter, and my daughter looked at me, and neither of us said aloud what we both understood: that the company did not send medicine. The company sent invoices shaped like kindness, and the bill always came due on Friday.
