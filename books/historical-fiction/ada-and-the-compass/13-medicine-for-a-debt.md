## 13. Medicine for a Debt

He came at dusk, when the light goes soft and forgiving over the water and everything it touches looks kinder than it is. I heard the trap before I saw it—Calloway's gig was too fine for our road, and the horse stood in our yard like a debt collector's signature.

Mother was asleep by the fire, or pretending to be. Her cough had gone quiet, which frightened me more than the coughing did. A coughing woman is fighting. A silent one is losing.

I opened the door before he could knock. Some part of me understood that if Mr. Calloway knocked on my door, he would be granting himself permission to enter, and I would rather he believed he had been invited all along.

"Miss Galloway." He held up a brown glass bottle the way a priest might hold up a relic. "I'm told laudanum syrup with squill does wonders for the lungs."

"Company medicine," I said. "We've had company medicine before. It comes out of the same ledger as everything else."

"This doesn't." He stepped past me into the room without hurrying, removing his hat as though he were a guest and not an occupying force. He set the bottle on our table, dead center, where the lamplight caught it and made it glow amber, precious, impossible to ignore. "This comes from me. Personally. There's no entry against your account. Consider it—" he paused, savoring the word—"*medicine*."

Behind me, Mother stirred on her pallet and coughed, once, twice—the dry tearing sound that had become the rhythm of our house. Calloway tilted his head toward it like a man listening to music he'd paid for.

"You see?" he said gently. "I know what she needs. I've watched this house a long time, Freya. May I call you Freya? Your father always spoke of you."

The floor did not move, but something in me did—a lurch, like standing on a jetty when a swell comes through.

"You knew my father."

"I knew him better than anyone alive," Calloway said, "which is why I know he's alive. Sit down, child. This won't take long, but it will take exactly as long as it takes."

I sat. Not because he told me to. Because my knees had opinions of their own.

* * *

He talked the way water talks—patiently, wearing things down. He took off his coat, folded it over the chair back, and settled in as if our hovel were his club. The lamplight found the gray at his temples and the pleasant creases around his eyes, the ones that made mothers in town trust him with their accounts.

"Edwin Galloway," he said, tasting the name. "Best diver I ever contracted. Could read the bottom like a ledger—which I suppose he learned from me. Held his breath four minutes on a good day. Do you know how long that is, Freya? Four minutes is long enough to make a decision you can't take back."

"He drowned," I said.

"He *sank*," said Calloway. "There's a difference, and I was there for both."

The fire ticked. Mother slept on, or performed sleeping. I kept my eyes on Calloway because looking away felt like handing him something.

"The night his boat went down," he went on, "there was a man aboard who wasn't supposed to be there. An islander. One of ours—or one of the company's, which amounts to the same grammar. Your father fished him out of the water half-drowned himself. And then, instead of reporting a rescue, he reported a sinking. Do you understand what that means? A dead man can't testify. But a rescued man—"

"A rescued man gets sold," I said.

Something flickered across Calloway's face—not surprise, exactly. Recognition. Like a cardplayer seeing his own hand dealt back to him.

"Calloway," I said, and the name came out of me cold and flat as shell grit. "You were there. You said you were there."

"I was there."

"You let him go under."

"I let him go *free*," Calloway corrected mildly. "There's a difference, and it's the whole difference between us, Freya, though I suspect you're clever enough to see it already. Your father chose his grave. Most men never get the courtesy. I've spent thirty years in this trade learning that mercy is just arithmetic done quickly." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and for a moment the clubroom warmth fell away and I saw the machinery underneath. "But graves are only useful if they stay filled. And three weeks ago a stranger walked off the Brisbane boat with salt-scars on his hands and letters in his bag, asking after the Galloways. Asking after *you*."

The room got very small. I thought of Tobias's scarred knuckles, the way he'd held his satchel like a man carrying water in cupped hands. I thought: *he's known since the jetty. Since the first day.*

"What do you want?" I asked.

"Now we're doing business." Calloway smiled, and it was almost kind, which was the worst thing yet. "Here is my offer, and I'll make it only once, so attend. By Friday—the Southern Cross mails Friday—you will bring me the papers the stranger carries. All of them. And you'll tell me where he sleeps."

"And if I do?"

"Then your mother drinks her medicine every week for as long as she needs it, free and clear. Then your debt—yours, and what remains of Edwin's—is struck from the book entirely. Clean page. New start." He rose, retrieved his hat, turned it slowly in his hands. "You'd be surprised how many debts are forgiven in this world, Freya. Almost all of them belong to people who learned to point."

"And if I don't?"

He put his hat on. He looked at the brown bottle on the table, then at my mother's pallet, then back at me, and his voice dropped into something almost tender.

"Then Friday comes anyway. Ships sail. Medicines run out. Debts, as your father used to say, are patient." At the door he paused, hand on the latch, and gave me one last gift of intimacy: "Your father begged, you know. In the water. Not for his life—for yours. Said, *don't tell my girl.* Well." The latch clicked open. "Now I've told you. Good evening."

* * *

I sat by the fire a long time after the hoofbeats faded. The brown bottle sat on the table like a small god, amber and certain, holding everything we needed inside its glass walls.

Mother woke near midnight and saw it and understood—mothers always understand—and her face went through grief and fury and arrived somewhere I'd never seen before: calculation.

"How much does he want?"

"The ledger. And Tobias."

"And what will you give him?"

I turned the bottle in my hands. Inside it, the syrup shifted like something alive.

"Whatever costs him most," I said.

It came to me then, quiet as tide coming in over sand—not courage, nothing so grand as courage. Just arithmetic, done quickly, the way Calloway would approve of. If kindness was his instrument, then I would learn to play it back to him. If he measured everything in debts, I would open an account he couldn't close. Fear had sat on my chest for nineteen years like a hand; tonight it loosened, and underneath it I found something harder than either of us expected.

I looked at my mother's hollowed face, at the firelight in her gray hair, and I thought of a man begging in black water—*don't tell my girl*—and a company man who told me anyway, because cruelty is just arithmetic done slowly.

Friday. Two days.

"Tobias has to know tonight," I said, and reached for my boots.
