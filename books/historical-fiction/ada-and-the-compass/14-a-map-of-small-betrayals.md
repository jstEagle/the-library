## 14. A Map of Small Betrayals

My mother had not stood at the stove in a month, but that night she boiled water for tea as though the house depended on ritual to stay standing, which perhaps it did. The lamp sat between us on the table with its wick trimmed low—Calloway had doubled his men on the jetty, and light through a curtain is a kind of confession—and we bent over the table like conspirators in a play where everyone knows the third act ends badly.

Tobias laid out the contents of my father's strongbox the way a surgeon lays out instruments: ledger page folded in thirds, the confession naming the buyer in Sydney, the bundle of letters I had read until two in the morning and then read again because fury, like grief, does not tire of itself.

"So," he said. "We decide what goes where."

"That's a generous word for it," I said. "What goes *who*."

Maren set down three cups. Her hands shook—not from fear, I noticed, but from the effort of keeping them steady for everything else. She poured tea into each cup without asking whether anyone wanted tea, which was her way of announcing that this would be a meeting with order in it.

"Sit," she said to Tobias, and to me, "both of you," and something in her voice—the voice she'd used when I was six and wanted to swim past the second marker buoy—made us both sit.

* * *

Here is what we knew, laid out like shells on a sheet:

The Southern Cross sailed in eight days. It carried one berth to Sydney, purchased with money Tobias had crossed an ocean not to spend on himself. In Sydney waited Magistrate Ellery Voss, who had spent four years collecting testimony about Meridian's island trade and needed only the paper to make the testimony stick. My father waited on Thursday Island with his own account of the night the *Lorikeet* went down—an account Calloway had been present for, which meant Calloway's version and my father's could not both survive daylight. And here in port, in a locked records room beneath the office where I copied figures six days a week, lay the master ledger with every false death entered in a fair hand: thirty-one islanders sold north as labor while their rations continued to be drawn in their names, because a dead man's pay is the cleanest theft there is.

"We copy the ledger," Tobias said. "Freya has access. We send the original north—it can't be forged away, and Voss will want ink, not recollection. The father's testimony travels separately."

"Separately how?" I asked. "There are exactly two boats leaving this harbor before the warrant comes, and one of them is the Southern Cross."

"There's a lugger," he said. "Thursday Island run. The captain owes me."

"Everyone you meet owes you something. It's your most useful quality." I turned the ledger page toward him. "And if the lugger is searched? If Calloway's men board it looking for exactly this?" I tapped the page. "You lose both the original and my father in one afternoon. That's not a plan, Mr. Ash. That's a donation."

"Then what do you suggest?"

"I suggest we stop pretending any of us is clever enough to beat him honestly. He's been beating people honestly for twenty years. We have to be dishonest in ways he hasn't imagined yet."

My mother said nothing. She drank her tea and watched us over the rim of her cup, and I understood suddenly that she was doing arithmetic of her own, and that her sums were older and crueler than ours.

* * *

The argument about the berth lasted most of an hour, and I lost it, though at the time I believed I was winning.

"It should be me," I said. "I'm the one who found the entries. I'm the one who can explain them—Voss will need someone who understands the ration system, the indenture schedules, the way Calloway phrases things. Tobias is a courier. Couriers deliver. They don't testify."

"Thank you," said Tobias mildly, "for the character reference."

"And my mother—" I stopped. That was the flaw in the sentence and I knew it before I finished it. My mother could barely cross the room without resting. Eight days of open water to Sydney, then a magistrate's inquiry, then whatever came after. I had watched her climb the stairs to bed and count the landings like a woman counting debts.

"Your mother," Maren said quietly, "can speak for herself."

She set down her cup with a small click, precise as a ledger entry.

"You'll take the berth," she said.

"Mum—"

"You'll take the berth, and you'll carry the original ledger, and you'll put it in Ellery Voss's hands yourself, and you will not argue with me, Freya Galloway, because I buried one husband already and I am not going to spend what's left of my lungs watching them take you too."

"The berth isn't going to save you," I said, and heard the cruelty of it even as I said it, and could not stop. "The medicine Calloway brought is a leash, not a cure. You know that. If I go to Sydney and you stay here, I'm trading your life for mine and calling it strategy."

Her face did something complicated and then settled into stillness, the way water settles after a stone.

"Yes," she said. "That's roughly the shape of it."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere down the track a dog was barking at the company launch making its evening round, and I thought: even the dog keeps watch now. Even the dogs have chosen sides or been assumed to.

"Maren." Tobias leaned forward. "With respect—you're asking her to leave you behind to die."

"I'm asking her to live somewhere I'm not," my mother said. "It's the same request my husband made of me, and I hated him for it for two years, and I understand it completely now, which is worse." She looked at me. "You think I kept his secret to protect him. Partly. But mostly I kept it because the day I learned he was alive was the day I learned that Meridian doesn't punish people. It punishes *connections*. A man alone on Thursday Island is a man they have to sail to. A family in one house is a knot they can cut with one knife. So I made us invisible. I taught you to be unremarkable. I let you believe your father drowned rather than let you carry the knowing, because the knowing is a weight, and I thought—if only I carry it, maybe she stays light."

"And instead I learned to hold my breath," I said. "Four minutes at a time, under contract, counting down your husband's debt. You didn't keep me light, Mum. You just moved the weight underwater where no one could see it."

She took that without flinching, which was somehow harder to bear than flinching would have been.

"Yes," she said again. "And now we're going to spend that breath properly, all at once, instead of dribbling it out year by year until the company takes the last of it anyway. That's the whole of my strategy, love. Spend everything. Hold nothing back for the swim home."

* * *

We argued the details until the lamp oil ran low, and what we finally agreed on was this:

The original ledger page—the false deaths, the ration draws, the buyer's name in my father's hand—would go south on the Southern Cross, in the one berth, with whoever filled it. My father's written confession would go north on the lugger to Thursday Island, separate boat, separate sea lane, so that no single seizure could take both. And the master ledger in the records room would be copied—every trafficking entry, every date, every figure—in a hand that could pass for the office's own, so that even if the originals were burned, even if Calloway drowned the evidence in the harbor itself, the numbers would exist somewhere he couldn't reach.

"And who copies it?" Tobias asked, though I could see he already knew, and I could see he wished he didn't.

"I do," I said. "Six days a week I sit at that desk copying debt figures in the company style. Calloway checks my work himself. He told me once my hand was the neatest in the office—said it the way a farmer praises a beast that pulls well." I turned my wrist in the lamplight, as if the skill lived in it visibly. "I can write his clerks' hand in my sleep. I've been practicing it since I was fourteen, because anything worth writing in this port had to be written in their hand first."

"It's not enough to copy it," my mother said. "He'll count pages. He'll notice a ledger disturbed."

"Not if the disturbance wears his own signature." I let that sit a moment. "A requisition. His hand, his seal impression traced from the shipping notices he leaves on the counter. Authorizing the records room be left open overnight for inventory—his own words, his own phrasing. He signs things by the dozen without reading them. I've watched him. I know which orders he trusts and which he reads twice, and an inventory order is one he'll sign at arm's length while thinking about dinner."

Tobias looked at me the way men look at deep water they'd previously charted as shallow.

"You've thought about this," he said.

"I've thought about everything," I said, "for years. I just never had anywhere to put it."

* * *

My mother banked the fire and went up to bed—or went up to lie down listening, which was her version of bed—and Tobias and I sat on either side of the dying lamp, and he said, quietly, "You understand what happens to you if the forgery is discovered."

"I understand what happens to everyone if it isn't done."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only kind I have." I pulled the strongbox toward me and began sorting papers into two piles: north and south, testimony and proof, my father's voice split down the middle like everything else he'd touched. "Mr. Ash. When you delivered those letters—did you know what you were carrying? Did you know what he'd done?"

He considered the question honestly, which I was learning to value in him.

"I knew he'd faked his death. I didn't know why until I read further than I should have." He paused. "He made me promise to give you the choice he never gave your mother. Whether to send for him. He said—he said you'd earned the deciding, even if he hadn't."

"Earned," I repeated. "There's a word from the ledger."

"He loves you," Tobias said. "For whatever that weighs."

"It weighs nothing," I said. "Love without presence is a ration drawn in a dead man's name." Then I heard my own sentence and understood I had just described my mother's whole doctrine, and my father's whole crime, and possibly the entire Meridian Trading Company, and that I was no longer sure which of the three I despised most.

* * *

Near midnight my mother came back down, wrapped in a blanket, and stood at the foot of the table looking at our two piles of paper, and I braced myself for a final appeal—that she would ask again for the berth, or refuse it, or say something that would crack the plan open along some seam I hadn't seen.

Instead she reached into the blanket's folds and produced a small parcel, oilcloth-wrapped, tied with string gone soft with age.

"Your father's compass," she said. "From the bottom of his sea chest. He left it the night he 'drowned.'" She set it on the table between the piles. "I want it to go north with the confession."

"Why?"

"Because a man navigating by borrowed courage should at least steer by his own needle." Her mouth did something that was almost a smile and mostly wasn't. "And because if Calloway's men search the lugger and find a dead diver's compass aboard a boat bound for Thursday Island, they'll know exactly whose testimony is sitting next to it, and they'll waste their fury on a ghost instead of on the ship that matters."

I stared at her. "You want to use him as bait."

"I want to use everything," my mother said, "as bait. That's what I told you. Spend it all." She looked at the compass, and her hand hovered over it without touching, the way you hover over a stove burner to test if it's cooled. "He spent nineteen years spending us. One night of being spent back won't kill him."

Tobias cleared his throat. "The lugger sails Thursday. The Southern Cross on the ninth. Which means the copying has to happen before Thursday, which means—"

"Tomorrow night," I said. "Calloway dines with the harbormaster tomorrow. He'll sign the day's orders at six and be gone by seven. I'll bring the requisition up with the evening post."

"You'll be alone in that building," Tobias said.

"I've been alone in that building for three years, Mr. Ash. Tomorrow I'll just finally be alone *on purpose*." I gathered the north-bound pile and tied it with the oilcloth and the compass inside, and felt the weight of it in my hands—paper and brass and one coward's confession, all of it lighter than the smallest pearl shell I'd ever hauled up, and heavier than any of them too.

My mother watched me tie the knot, and when I looked up she was watching me with an expression I had never seen on her face before, and it took me a long moment to name it, because I had never once seen it in our house.

It was pride. Plain, unborrowed, unhidden pride.

"One more thing," she said. "When you forge his signature—make it slightly too heavy on the C. Calloway presses hard when he's tired, and he's always tired by evening."

I blinked at her. "How do you know that?"

"Because twenty-two years ago, before you were born, I kept books for the company's predecessor," my mother said, "and I got out just ahead of what they became. Where do you imagine you learned to count, love?"

She took the candle and went up to bed, and Tobias and I sat in the dark a moment, both of us revising everything we thought we knew about the quiet woman upstairs.

"Well," he said finally. "Now I know where you get it."

"Get what?"

"The habit," he said, "of winning arguments nobody knows you're having."

Outside, the tide was turning, and somewhere on the black water the Southern Cross rode at anchor with one empty berth, and tomorrow night I would sit down beneath my enemy's dinner table and begin, line by careful line, to steal his history back.
