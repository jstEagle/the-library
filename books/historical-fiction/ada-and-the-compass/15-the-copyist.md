## 15. The Copyist

There is a particular quality to lamplight falling on another man's handwriting. It flattens it. Makes it ordinary. Makes it something you could own.

I had been practicing Calloway's hand for eleven days before I understood that this was what I was doing. I thought at first I was simply studying him — the way a diver studies a current, learning which way a thing moves so she can move against it. He wrote his requisitions standing up, braced against the counter's edge, and the habit lived in his letters: a slight forward lean to every downstroke, as though each word were hurrying somewhere it did not wish to arrive. His *r*s were mean little things, pinched at the shoulder. His *y*s trailed off like men leaving a room without saying goodbye.

I copied them all into the back of my mother's prayer book, where no one would ever look on principle. The *r*s first. Then the *y*s. Then the peculiar way he crossed his *t*s low and hard, like a man closing a gate.

By the eleventh day I could write *Meridian Trading Company* in Calloway's hand well enough that Calloway himself would have signed it without a second glance. By the twelfth day I stopped telling myself I was only studying.

* * *

The plan, if you could dignify it with the word, had three parts, and Tobias hated all of them equally.

"The records room," he said, the night we settled it, turning his cup on Maren's table without drinking from it. "The one behind Calloway's own office. Guarded by a lock and by the fact that nobody is fool enough to try."

"Nobody has ever been fool enough," I said, "because nobody there is invisible. I am. It's the one thing Meridian ever gave me for free."

He looked at me the way he sometimes did — as though I were a chart he'd been handed in a language he almost read. "And the requisition?"

"Is how I get through the door." I laid the paper between us. *Requisition: Galloway, F., copyist, to attend records room, Thursday evening, for the purpose of transcribing outstanding debt schedules prior to quarterly audit.* Calloway's lean, hurrying hand. His gate-closing crosses. "Calloway dines with the harbor master every Thursday. Two hours, maybe more, because the harbor master drinks. The clerk locks the outer door and goes home. And the requisition says I'm meant to be there."

"And when he returns and finds you've been alone with his ledgers for two hours—"

"He won't return and find anything," I said. "Because nothing will be missing. That's the whole art of it, Tobias. You don't steal the history. You copy it. A stolen ledger can be missed. A copied page is just ink."

Maren said nothing through any of this. She sat by the stove with her blanket over her knees, coughing the small dry coughs she had learned to make quiet, and watched me with an expression I couldn't name until later, when I understood it was pride and terror wearing each other's clothes.

"You'll leave the lamp burning low," she said at last. Not a question. Mothers issue instructions even when their children are planning felonies. "And you'll sit near the door."

"Yes, Mama."

"And if you hear boots on the stairs—"

"I'll be a girl copying debt figures," I said. "Which will even be true, right up until it isn't."

Tobias laughed then, once, short and unwilling, the way a man laughs when the alternative is to say something prudent. "You know," he said, "your father used to talk like that. Right before he did whatever mad thing he'd decided on."

I didn't answer that. There was a version of me, three weeks old, who would have flinched at his name. She was gone. In her place was someone folding a forged requisition into her bodice, next to her heart, where the company had taught her to keep everything worth keeping.

* * *

Thursday came in grey off the reef, with a wind that smelled of rain that hadn't committed yet. I went to the office at my usual hour and worked my usual figures, and Mr. Calloway stood at his high desk above me doing whatever it is company men do instead of work, and neither of us knew — only one of us knew — that by midnight one of us would have rewritten the other.

"Galloway." He said it the way he always did, tasting the name like a coin he suspected of being clipped. "You've been attentive lately."

"I'm always attentive, sir."

"Mm. Attentive and accurate are different virtues." He descended the steps of his platform — he made even stairs sound like condescension — and stood over my desk, and I kept my eyes on my columns and let my heartbeat do its work somewhere below the hearing of reasonable men. "The quarterly audit approaches. Sydney will want the schedules in fair copy. You have a steady hand."

"Thank you, sir."

"Do you know what I admire about you, Galloway?" He straightened his cuffs. He had small hands, Calloway, and wore rings he'd never earned. "You don't ask questions. In this trade, that's worth more than a steady hand. Curiosity is a debt too, and debts," he smiled, "are our business."

"Yes, sir."

He left at six, whistling — Calloway whistled hymns, which told you most of what you needed to know about Calloway — and the clerk locked the outer door behind him with the ceremony of a man who believes locks are morals made metal. The building settled around me. Dust ticked down through the lamplight. Somewhere in the walls, termites went about their own quiet accounting.

I waited until the clerk's footsteps faded past the cisterns. Then I took out the requisition and set it on the desk where a searching eye would find it, and took the lamp, and climbed the stairs to the records room as calmly as if I had been born entitled to them.

The lock was a company lock, heavy and vain, but locks are made for strangers, and I was not a stranger; I was the invisible girl with a paper in Calloway's own hand, and the lock opened for the fiction of me as sweetly as a door opens for a bride.

* * *

I had expected the records room to smell of paper. It smelled of money — of ink and camphor and the faint sourness of men's ambition sealed in a hot room — and beneath that, very faintly, of the sea, because everything in this port smells of the sea eventually, even the things that think they're above it.

Shelves ran floor to ceiling. Ledgers in ranks, spines lettered in white: *Shell Accounts*, *Rations Issued*, *Advances & Recoups*, and then, on the shelf nearest Calloway's private stair, bound in green calfskin finer than anything else in the room, the volumes with no lettering at all.

I began where I had promised myself I would begin: not with the green books but with the ordinary ones, the debt schedules, copying in my own plain hand while the clock in the hall knocked out its patient quarters. If I was found, I would be found doing honest work. My hand shook for the first half hour. After that, something stranger happened.

The shaking stopped. And the exhilaration began.

Understand what it was like. I had spent four years being a figure in these books — *Galloway, F., diver, debt outstanding* — a number among numbers, entered by men who never learned to spell my mother's name. And here I sat inside the belly of the thing that had swallowed us, with a lamp and a pen, and the thing lay open under my hands, and I could read it, all of it, every entry, every fiddle, every lie dressed in double-entry. History belongs to admirals and company men — I had believed that the way you believe the weather. But weather changes. And a girl with a pen, in a locked room, at midnight, is a kind of storm no one forecasts.

I turned to the green calfskin volumes at half past nine.

They were manifest books. Island manifests — names I recognized from the jetty, from the camps, boys and men from Ambrym and Tanna and places further north whose languages I knew only as sounds across the water. And beside each name, in entries months apart, a pattern that made my skin go cold and then hot:

*Transferred — Port Moresby.*
*Transferred — Fiji, per *Lavinia*.*
*Died — fever.* Followed, three pages later, in the ration schedules, by rations still drawn in that same dead man's name. Drawn by whom? By a mark. An initials. *J.C.*

Dead men eating. Dead men transferred. Dead men sold, their deaths written here to close the account here while the account itself went on living somewhere south, in cane fields and copra boats, in places where a man bought on paper that said *deceased* cannot complain, because the dead have no standing, and the paper is the man.

My father had seen this. That was the sentence I arrived at, sitting in the yellow light with the green book open, and it came not as discovery but as confirmation, a key turning in a lock I had already picked. He had dived for these men. He had known some of their names. And one night a boat had gone down that wasn't an accident, and my father had done the arithmetic every coward does and chosen the far shore.

I hated him again, freshly, thoroughly. And then I did the only thing hate was good for.

I copied.

Page after page. Names, dates, vessels, initials. My hand grew fast and sure, faster than I'd ever written in my life, because I was not writing anymore — I was *unwriting*, striking men back into existence stroke by stroke, moving them out of the column marked *dead* into a column that had no heading yet but would, in a courtroom in Sydney, acquire one. *Exhibit.* I liked that word. I wrote it nowhere, but I carried it like a stone in my shoe.

Once, near eleven, I heard a noise above — the floorboards of Calloway's dining room, or the memory of them, the house settling its weight — and I put out my hand flat on the page and sat absolutely still and listened to my own blood. Nothing came down the stair. The clock knocked on. I lifted my hand and went back to work, and discovered I was smiling, which frightened me more than the footsteps had.

That is the truth of transgression that no sermon tells you: it feels like coming alive. Every locked door I had ever walked past politely, every question I had swallowed, every time I had made myself small and smooth and unremarkable so the current would pass over me — it all rose up now as appetite. I was not stealing. I was taking back. There is a difference, and the difference weighs exactly as much as a ledger page, and I could feel every ounce of it.

* * *

At ten minutes past midnight I heard the front door.

Not the clerk — the clerk's step was quick and apologetic. This was slow, deliberate, a man arriving rather than returning, and it crossed the floor below and stopped.

I closed the green book. I banked the lamp low. My copies — forty-one pages, folded flat — went into the lining of my bodice where the requisition had lived, and the requisition itself I left square on the table beside the open debt schedules, in case fiction needed to stand duty.

Boots on the stairs.

I arranged myself at the table with the ordinary ledgers, pen wet, shoulders curved into the shape of a girl too tired to be anywhere interesting, and the door opened, and Calloway stood in it with the dining room's candlelight behind him and his hat still on, which he would never have done in his own office, which meant he was drunk, or pretending to be, which with Calloway was often the safer bet.

"Galloway." He looked at the lamp, at the papers, at me, and the silence stretched long enough to grow things. "You work late."

I held up the requisition without a word — his own hand, his own order, *prior to quarterly audit* — and watched him take it, and watched the small pause travel through him like a fish through still water. He read it twice. I counted his breaths. Four.

"This is mine," he said slowly.

"Yes, sir. Dated the ninth."

Something moved behind his face — not suspicion exactly; suspicion he knew how to wear. This was older and less comfortable. The look of a man meeting, in a dim room, a version of events he had not authorized. "I don't recall issuing it."

"No, sir?" I let my brow do the work of a woman who has never had a clever thought. "It came down with the day's papers. I assumed—" I stopped, apologized with my whole posture, offered him the humility he fed on.

For a long moment Calloway stood in his own doorway holding his own forged signature, and I understood that I was watching a man weigh whether to believe himself. Pride argued for the requisition — pride hates to admit a slip of memory. Suspicion argued against me. And between those two advocates, exhausted from wine and hymn-whistling and the general labor of being Mr. Calloway, he chose the cheaper verdict.

"Finish the schedules," he said at last, handing the paper back. His voice had regained its varnish. "Lock up when you go. And Galloway—" at the door he paused, and the candlelight behind him made his shadow reach all the way across the floor to touch my table, "—the green volumes are audited separately. They are not part of your schedule. They are not part of anything you need to have seen."

"Yes, sir."

"Good girl."

He went. The stairs took his weight one complaint at a time. The outer door closed, and the lock turned, and I sat in the dark I had made myself, listening to the termites, feeling forty-one pages against my ribs rise and fall with my breathing like something alive I had delivered into the world tonight.

Then I finished the last debt schedule, trimmed the lamp, locked the records room behind me, and let myself out the window over the cisterns, which is a thing invisible girls can do, because no one thinks to watch for them.

* * *

Tobias was waiting in the boatshed with a shuttered lantern, and when I unwrapped the copies on the workbench his face went through several weathers in succession. At the third page he stopped reading and started counting. At the twentieth he sat down.

"It's all here," he said, in the voice of a man who has prayed for a thing and is angry to receive it. "Names, vessels, dates. Enough to hang the trade. Enough to hang *him*."

"Hang him with," I corrected. "We'll want rope on both ends."

He laughed — really laughed, this time, tired and amazed — and then looked at me with an expression I had not earned from him before and did not entirely know what to do with. "Your father," he said, "kept saying there was one person in this port Meridian never bothered to look at. One person who could walk through their own front door." He tapped the pages. "He meant you."

I gathered the copies together, squared their edges, and tied them with the bit of tarred twine divers use for everything, and felt the strange lightness and the strange weight of them at once. Somewhere across the dark harbour the *Southern Cross* rode at anchor, nine days from sailing. Somewhere north, on Thursday Island, a man who shared my eyes was waiting on word he might never get. And somewhere upstairs in the company office, Mr. Calloway was sleeping the sleep of a man who believes his own signature — not knowing that tomorrow morning he would send a telegram to Sydney asking questions, and that telegrams, like ledgers, can be read by more than the people who write them.

I blew out the lantern.

"Get some sleep," I told Tobias. "Tomorrow I learn to forge a telegram."
