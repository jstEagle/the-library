## 16. Discovery

There are two kinds of waiting in this world, and I have done a great deal of both. The first kind is the waiting of the patient man: the fisherman with his line, the sniper behind his wall, the lover at a train station who has been told *perhaps Thursday*. It is waiting that believes in its own reward. The second kind is the waiting of the man crouched in the shadow of a company storehouse at half past nine on a Tuesday night, counting his own heartbeats and finding them extravagant, while a nineteen-year-old girl forges her way through the paper heart of an empire.

I have always preferred the second kind, on the grounds that at least it keeps a man honest about his chances.

The moon was against us — a fat, indifferent coin of a moon that lit the yard between the office and the shell sheds as evenly as a magistrate's lamp. Freya had chosen it deliberately, which told me something about her I was only beginning to understand. She had reasoned that a dark night would be suspicious; nobody legitimate skulks in darkness, but plenty of people walk in ordinary light, and ordinary light was what she intended to look like if anyone looked at all. A girl walking home late from her mother's sickbed, past the office where she worked six days a week. Nothing to see. Nothing to remember.

That was her doctrine, learned at her mother's knee: be unremarkable, and you will be left alive. I had spent three years learning the opposite lesson — that being remarkable is sometimes the only shelter there is — and watching her move through that moonlit yard, shoulders down, eyes fixed on nothing, I understood that we were both wrong and both right, and that neither of us would live long enough to argue it out.

She carried the forged requisition folded inside her shirt, next to her skin, where Calloway's own handwriting could keep warm against her heartbeat. I still marveled at it. I have seen men forge banknotes and men forge passports, and all of them started with the honest confession that they were liars. Freya had started with something harder: she had started by studying a man she despised until she could reproduce his hand the way a daughter reproduces a lullaby. There is a word for that kind of intimacy and it isn't *forgery*. It's closer to *marriage*, or *murder*.

*Half past ten,* she had said at dusk, when we'd made our final plan in the smell of eucalyptus behind Maren's house. *Calloway dines late on Tuesdays with the harbor board. He takes brandy after, and he is vain enough to take it slowly where the right men can see him. He'll be above stairs until eleven at the earliest.*

*And if he isn't?*

She had looked at me with those eyes — her father's eyes, God help us both, though at that hour I hadn't yet met her father and didn't know to hate him properly — and said, *Then I'll be a girl looking for my shawl.*

It was a good answer. It was the kind of answer I should have distrusted, because it was exactly the kind of answer I gave myself before every bad decision of my life.

* * *

While she worked, I waited, and waiting is a trade like any other, so let me describe the work.

My post was the gap between the cooper's shed and the boundary fence, close enough to the office's back door to reach it in perhaps forty seconds if I ran, which I would not do, because running is a confession. From there I could see the lighted windows of the upper floor, where Calloway's silhouette moved occasionally behind the glass like a slow fish in a tank, and the lower floor, dark, where the records room lived among the other organs of the company: the ration stores, the strongroom, the little room where they kept the indenture papers of men who could not read them.

I counted. That's what I did instead of praying; I had lost the habit of prayer somewhere between Aden and Thursday Island, and arithmetic had stepped into the vacancy without complaint. I counted the minutes since Freya slipped through the door at twenty past ten. I counted the times Calloway's shadow crossed his window — four times in the first quarter hour, then nothing, which meant either that he had settled into his brandy or that he had gone somewhere, and I did not permit myself to think about where.

I thought instead about the ledger pages she would copy. We had worked out the method over two nights: she would copy the trafficking entries in her own hand onto loose sheets — no time for anything finer — and leave the originals undisturbed, so that Calloway might not know for days, maybe weeks, that anything had been taken. Knowledge leaves no footprints if you're careful. It was the one advantage invisible people have: when we steal, the world assumes nothing was ever there.

Except that I had stopped being invisible the day I walked up the jetty asking after the Galloways, and I had known it, and I had come anyway. There is a particular foolishness in that which deserves naming. A man who crosses two oceans carrying another man's secrets begins to believe the ocean owes him safe passage. The sea disabused me of that notion years ago; I kept forgetting that men are worse than water.

At forty minutes past ten, a dog began barking near the Chinese camp, and my count broke, and in the broken place I felt the first cold drop of what I would later recognize as foreknowledge. Not fear exactly. Fear is about yourself. This was about her — a sudden certainty, arriving whole and unwelcome, that the plan had already gone wrong somewhere below the surface of things, the way a hull goes wrong below the waterline, silently, and only announces itself when the water is in the cabin.

I looked at Calloway's window. Empty. His lamp burned on the desk, but the chair stood vacant, or seemed to; at this distance, shadows lie fluently.

Forty-five minutes. Fifty.

Then the back door opened, and Freya came out into the moonlight, and everything in me that had been braced went slack with relief for exactly as long as it took to notice how she moved.

She wasn't walking home. She was walking like a woman carrying something breakable — too careful, too level, her hands pressed flat against her sides where the copies were bound beneath her waist sash. And she was moving fast in the way that tries not to look fast, each step a fraction too long, and behind her, at the corner of the office, a lantern kindled.

Not Calloway's window. The veranda. Two men, then a third, lanterns swinging up out of the dark like a verdict.

They had been inside. They had been inside the whole time — below, in the dark of the lower floor, sitting in the black among the shelves with the patience of men paid by the week, letting her work, letting her finish, because a thief caught leaving with the goods is worth ten thieves suspected entering. I understood it in one sickening flash: Calloway hadn't been upstairs drinking at all. Or he had been, briefly, theatrically, a silhouette arranged for my benefit — because they had seen me too, days ago, and had simply waited to learn what shape our crime would take.

Freya stopped in the middle of the yard. One of the lanterns came forward and showed me, across thirty yards of moonlit ground, the face of a company policeman I knew slightly — a Kanaka man named Joseph whose brother had shipped out on the *Lavinia* two years ago and never written home. Joseph's face did what faces do in such moments: it apologized without moving.

"Evening, miss," said the second man, the one holding her shoulder now, not hard, not yet. "Late night for copying."

I want to record, because history will otherwise record it wrong, that my first impulse was not courage. My first impulse was arithmetic, the same cold sum I always run: *one berth, one ledger, one girl, three men, forty yards.* Every term of that equation said *stay in the shadow.* Stay in the shadow and she spends a night in a cell, loses the papers, loses perhaps more than papers, and the case dies in this yard with the moths battering themselves against the lantern glass.

But equations are how cowards dress, and I was tired of the costume. Three years I had run from Meridian — from their agents in Batavia, from their lawyers in Sydney, from the memory of a friend dying in a room that smelled of camphor and iodine, pressing a satchel into my hands with fingers already going gray. *Take it north,* he'd said. *Make it heavy for them.* I had crossed two oceans being careful, and careful had gotten me to this exact yard, at this exact minute, to watch a girl be arrested for doing the brave thing while I did the sensible thing in the dark.

There comes a point when the sensible thing costs more than the reckless one. I reached it somewhere between the third lantern and the first shout.

* * *

What I did next was not complicated, which is the nature of most rescues: by the time a rescue requires sophistication, it is already too late for one.

I walked out of the shadow between the sheds. I walked upright, unhurried, a man coming home late along a route he owned, and I let my boot strike a loose plank by the rain barrel — once, twice — so that every head in the yard turned toward the noise, and then I did the one thing guaranteed to hold them.

I ran.

Not away. Toward the fence on the far side, the long way around, making myself a second problem at maximum volume. I heard the shout go up — *there! Stop there!* — heard boots hit the ground behind me, heard Freya's voice cut sharp through the dark saying something about her shawl, her poor sick mother, playing her part even now, even mid-catastrophe, and I put on speed I hadn't used since Singapore and went over the fence with my palms singing and my ribs filing a formal complaint.

They caught me at the tideline. Of course they did. I had planned to be caught — that was the entire architecture of the thing — but planning to be caught does nothing for the moment itself, which arrived as a knee in my back and coral grinding my cheek and four sets of hands with the impersonal competence of men handling cargo.

"Easy," I said, into the sand. "Easy. I'm only one man."

"You're the man from the jetty," someone said, breathing hard. "Asking after Galloway."

"That's me." I turned my head far enough to taste salt and blood. "Tobias Ash. You'll want to spell it correctly in your report. The *h* matters."

They laughed, some of them, the way men laugh when the orders haven't arrived yet and laughter fills the space. And through their legs, across the yard, I saw Freya standing very still in the moonlight with her shawl pulled tight, a girl again, unremarkable, forgotten already — the copies flat and warm against her body, walking away step by step while every eye belonged to me.

Joseph was the last to turn. For a moment his lantern swung and the light found my face, and I looked at him and did not wink, did not nod, did nothing a man could be asked about later. But I held his eyes for the length of one breath, and I thought about his brother on the *Lavinia*, and I believe — I choose to believe — that he understood exactly what he was choosing to forget.

They marched me back across the yard. As we passed the office door, Freya was just disappearing down the track toward the divers' camp, small and grey and ordinary as smoke, and I permitted myself one glance at her, no more, and then I became entirely a prisoner, which is a discipline like any other.

* * *

The cell was new timber and old smells: tar, mildew, and the ghost of every man who had sweated fear into the boards before me. They took my boots — protocol, or spite — and my satchel, which contained nothing but spare shirts and a dead friend's spare razor, and they locked me in at midnight with the courtesy of men following instructions they hadn't written.

I sat on the bunk and took inventory. Ribs: bruised, functional. Hands: scraped, functional. Plan: intact, insofar as a plan can be intact when its author is behind a locked door and its co-author is walking free through a town that will be crawling with watchers by morning. The clock, which had been ticking since the day I stepped off the boat, now ticked audibly. Calloway had wanted to know what shape our crime would take. Now he knew. By breakfast, every jetty watch would be doubled, every departure scrutinized, and the *Southern Cross* — nine days out when we'd made our plan, eight now, seven by the time any of us slept — would be the single most examined hull in the Coral Sea.

One berth. Eight days. And me supplying the company with a name, a face, and a reason to search every passenger twice.

I had bought Freya's freedom with my own capture, and the exchange rate was ruinous, and I lay back on the bunk and laughed at the ceiling until laughing hurt, because the alternative to laughing was thinking, and thinking could wait until dawn.

Dawn came grey through a barred window high in the wall, and with it came the sound I had been listening for without admitting it: footsteps on the veranda, measured and unhurried, the tread of a man arriving at his own office as though the world were a well-run shop. Mr. Calloway, keeping early hours. Keeping hours, I would learn, that had nothing to do with me.

The window faced the front of the building, and from the bunk, if I stood on it — which I did, barefoot, palms against the frame — I could see past the bars, down the length of the veranda, to the little telegraph shed that squatted at the compound's edge like a chapel.

Calloway was there. He had come straight from his horse, coat still buttoned, and he stood at the telegraph key himself — not the operator, mind, but Calloway, sleeves folded back, sending with his own two hands. Even at that distance I knew the posture. It was the posture of a man writing a letter he intends to deny ever having written.

I watched the shutter of the shed flicker with the rhythm of the key. Long, short, short. Pause. Short, long, long. I don't read Morse — that skill belonged to the navy, and I had been cargo, not crew — but I didn't need to read it. I needed only to count the minutes it went on. Four minutes. Five. Six. A message of six minutes is not a message; it is a dossier. Somewhere under the sea, a cable was filling up with names — mine, certainly, and I would have bet the compass in my pocket that it held a second name too, a woman's name, and a third, older, belonging to a drowned man who was not drowned.

He finished. He stood a moment with his hand resting on the key as if it were a pet, then straightened his cuffs and walked back across the yard, passing directly beneath my window without looking up, because men like Calloway never look up; looking up is an admission that something above them exists.

But I saw his face, brief and tilted toward the harbour, where the masts of the town stood thin against the sunrise. And what I saw in it was not triumph. Triumph relaxes a man. What I saw was the expression of a clerk who has discovered an error in the books and is composing, in advance, the memo that will make the error somebody else's fault.

The clock had not merely run out. I understood, hanging off the bars of that little window with the sun coming up on my wrists, that it had run out days ago — that every move we'd made since the jetty had been played on a board Calloway had set before we ever arrived, and that the telegram flapping its way south to Sydney was not a warning to his masters.

It was a story. His story. And the *Southern Cross* sailed in seven days, and every soul in this town was about to find out which version of the truth arrived first.
