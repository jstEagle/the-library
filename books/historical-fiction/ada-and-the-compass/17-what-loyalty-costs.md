## 17. What Loyalty Costs

There is a particular arithmetic that drowning people do, I'm told. Not the drowning of water—the drowning of choices. You stop weighing what you want and start weighing what you can hold above the surface with two hands, and everything else goes under without ceremony.

I had become very good at arithmetic by Thursday morning.

Tobias had been in Calloway's cell for one day and one night. I knew because I had walked past the company office three times under pretenses so flimsy that even Mrs. Okada's dog hadn't believed them, and each time I had seen him through the barred window, sitting very still on the cot like a man conserving himself for something. He didn't look broken. He looked like a letter waiting to be delivered, and I was beginning to understand that Calloway knew it too—that Tobias in a cell was not a victory for the company but a hostage, and hostages are only useful when someone wants them back.

I wanted him back. That was the first term in my equation.

The second term sat across the breakfast table from me, coughing into a rag she tried to keep below the edge of the table as if modesty could disguise what the rag now carried. My mother had eaten half a piece of damper and called it a feast. The berth on the *Southern Cross*—one berth, bought with money Tobias had carried across two oceans for this purpose—was hers if I chose to spend it on her. Sydney, sea air, doctors who charged more per visit than my father's whole debt. Or it was mine, if I chose to run. It could not be both, and it could not be his, and every hour I spent not deciding was an hour the decision made itself by default toward Calloway.

The third term was the ledger. The original lay sewn into the lining of my mother's old traveling shawl, wrapped in oilcloth, warm from her lap where it had lived for three days while she pretended it was merely a shawl and she was merely cold. My copies—my own careful hand, page after page stolen out of the records room while Calloway dined above me—lay hidden in the strongbox under the floorboards, under the bed, under everything.

And the fourth term, offered to me at dusk the evening before with the courtesy of a man presenting a gift he had already wrapped in thorns: denounce my father publicly, and all debts forgiven.

* * *

Calloway had come to the house himself. He did not send clerks for important transactions; that much I had learned about him. He stood in our doorway holding his hat in both hands like a deacon, and he asked after my mother's health with such sincerity that I nearly admired it.

"The medicine agrees with her, I hope," he said.

"It agrees with her fine," I said, "considering the price."

"Ah." He smiled the way ice smiles. "Miss Galloway. May I?"

He did not wait for permission—he never did—but he took the single chair our table offered, leaving my mother standing until I pulled the crate from beside the stove for her. He arranged his hat on his knee. Somewhere outside, the tide was going out, and I found myself listening to it the way you listen to a clock when a visitor has stayed too long.

"You'll want to know the terms plainly," he said. "I find that people forgive plainness more readily than they forgive cleverness."

"I wouldn't know," I said. "No one's ever forgiven me anything."

My mother made a small sound. I did not look at her.

"Your friend Mr. Ash," Calloway said, "committed theft of company property, forgery of a company requisition—" here he paused delicately, because we both knew whose hand had forged it, and he was letting me feel the weight of what he *hadn't* said— "and obstruction of lawful business. These are serious matters. Gaol in Thursday Island is not a gentle place, and the magistrate there owes the company certain courtesies. He will be transported within the month unless charges are withdrawn."

"Withdrawn by whom?"

"By me. I have that discretion." He turned the hat a quarter turn on his knee. "In exchange, I require the ledger. The original."

"And the copies?"

"There are no copies," he said pleasantly. "That is the beauty of the arrangement. There never were any copies."

I looked at my mother then, despite myself. She sat with her hands folded over the shawl—over the ledger—her knuckles like knots in driftwood, and her face gave away nothing except exhaustion, which it could no longer help giving away.

"And the berth?" I asked. "If I give you the ledger, does my mother still sail?"

"That depends," Calloway said, "on the second matter." And he laid it before us as gently as a man lays a card he knows wins the trick.

* * *

Here is what he asked. He asked that Freya Galloway, daughter of Daniel Galloway, drowned diver, stand on the jetty on Friday morning—in front of the harbor master, in front of the divers' camp, in front of whoever cared to listen—and state that her father had not drowned at all. That he had staged his death to escape his debts. That he was a fraud and a runaway and a coward, alive somewhere north, laughing at the men who'd covered his contract.

In exchange: Tobias freed, debts erased, mother medicated at company expense, berth honored, and Freya Galloway herself restored to good standing—a girl with no debt, no scandal, and a future the company would look kindly upon.

"A fresh ledger page," Calloway said, and smiled at his own phrase.

"And if I refuse?"

"Then Mr. Ash sails for Thursday Island in irons, your debt stands—and grows, Miss Galloway, it does grow, interest is a tide that never turns—and your mother's cough continues its present conversation with her lungs." He rose, retrieved his hat, and set it on with the care of a man closing a ledger himself. "You needn't answer tonight. But I'll want the answer by Friday's tide."

He paused at the door. What he said next, he said without turning around, and it was worse for that.

"Your father, when I knew him, was a man who always found someone else to pay. I wonder where you learned your arithmetic."

Then he was gone, and the house felt smaller, the way houses do after they've been measured for a coffin.

* * *

My mother and I sat up most of the night. We did not light a second lamp; lamp oil was company store, and company store was debt, and I was done feeding that machine one drop more than I had to. So we sat in the one flame's reach and spoke in the voices people use in sickrooms.

"He wants the truth buried," my mother said finally. "All of it. The islanders, the boats, the buyer. Your father saw something, Freya. Whatever else he is, whatever he ran from, he saw men murdered on paper and he carried proof instead of running clean. That cost him something. Maybe it's the only thing about him that ever cost him something."

"He let us pay instead."

"Yes," she said simply. "He did." She did not dress it up. She never had—that was the strange mercy of my mother, that she could love a man and still name him. "And now Calloway offers to wipe the slate if you'll stand on a jetty and call him a coward in front of everyone. And the terrible thing, Freya"—she leaned forward into the lamplight, and her eyes were bright and feverish and clearer than I had seen them in months—"the terrible thing is that it isn't even a lie. He *is* a coward. He ran. You'd be telling the truth, and it would still be a betrayal. That's how Calloway builds his traps. He makes the true thing serve the false one."

I thought about that for a long time. Outside, the tide turned; I heard it change its mind against the piles.

"If I denounce him, we're free," I said. "You get your doctors. Tobias walks out. I walk through this town with my chin up for the first time in my life."

"Yes."

"And every diver in that camp watches the Galloway girl sell the last man who told the truth about Meridian, and they learn what telling the truth costs, and they never tell it again."

"Yes," she said again, softer.

"And the ledger comes to you anyway, sooner or later, because Calloway is patient, and you're sick, and I can't watch you every hour."

My mother smiled then—not bitterly, but with the tired amusement of a woman who has raised exactly one child and knows her completely. "So you've already answered him."

"No," I said. "Because answering him means choosing, and choosing means—" I stopped, because the shape of it had finally come clear to me, cold and smooth as a shell turned over in the shallows.

It had never been a question of whom to save. That was the trick of it. Calloway had framed it as arithmetic—one berth, one prisoner, one father, one ledger—as though I were balancing accounts. But accounts weren't what was being weighed. What was being weighed was me. Which Freya Galloway would walk off that jetty on Friday morning? The one who had learned invisibility so well she could perform betrayal in public and call it survival? The one who traded a dead man's name for a living debt? Or the one who had sat in the records room copying page after page of other people's erased lives and understood, finally, that history was not something admirals and company men wrote—it was something happening *to* her, right now, in a lamplit kitchen, and that the only vote she got was what she did next?

Calloway wasn't buying my silence or my testimony. He was buying my self—the visible one, the official one, the one entered in his books. And once purchased, a self stays purchased. Every day after Friday, I would be the girl who denounced her father. That was the entry. That was the line in the ledger, and ledgers are the only history the Meridian Trading Company believes in.

"Freya." My mother's voice pulled me back. Her hand crossed the table and closed over mine, dry and strong as rope. "Listen to me. If it comes to choosing between my passage and that ledger—"

"Don't."

"—then you put the ledger on that ship." Her grip tightened. "I have been quiet twenty years, child. Quiet is a kind of dying, and I've had my fill of practicing. Let me be one more thing Calloway couldn't count."

I wanted to argue. I want you to know that—I wanted it with my whole chest, the way you want air. But I looked at her hollowed face in the lamplight and understood that taking the choice away from her now would be just one more way of deciding for other people, which was, when it came down to it, the family trade.

* * *

Friday came gray and windless, the sea lying flat as poured tin, as if the whole harbour were holding its breath along with me.

I dressed in my good dress—the one without patches—and put the copied pages of the ledger inside my bodice, against my heart, where they scratched like a second conscience. Then I walked down to the jetty past the divers' camp, past Mrs. Okada and the harbor master's boys mending nets, past every pair of eyes that pretended not to follow me, and I presented myself at Calloway's office door with my hands folded and my face arranged into the blankness I had spent years perfecting.

He received me standing behind his desk, and he looked pleased, and why shouldn't he? A girl walking in alone on settlement day looks like surrender to a man who has never been lied to successfully by anyone poorer than himself.

"Miss Galloway," he said. "I confess, I hoped you'd see sense."

"I've brought an answer," I said. "As requested."

"Before you speak—" he opened a drawer and laid the key to Tobias's cell on the desk between us, deliberate as a churchman laying out communion— "know that everything discussed is available. The debt, gone. The medicine, ongoing. Your friend, released by tonight's tide. All of it, for one statement, spoken publicly, on that jetty, before this town. Denounce Daniel Galloway."

I looked at the key for a long moment. Then I reached into my bodice and drew out the folded pages, and set them on the desk, and watched his composure flicker—just once, just slightly, the way water flickers over a reef before it closes again.

"My answer," I said.

He unfolded them. I watched him read his own handwriting—no. Watched him read *my* copy of his own handwriting, the requisition, the entries, the names of men listed dead whose rations were drawn for months after their deaths, the buyer's initials in the margin. His face did not change. That was the frightening part. His face was a ledger too, and nothing was ever entered on it that he didn't choose.

"These are copies," he said at last.

"They are," I agreed. "The originals sailed yesterday."

The silence that followed had weight and temperature. Somewhere beyond the window, a gull screamed once and quit.

"You're bluffing," he said quietly. "A girl like you doesn't have the reach."

"A girl like me," I said, "is the only person in this port who has read every page of your records twice. You taught me the figures yourself, Mr. Calloway. You said curiosity was a debt. Well." I folded my hands the way he folded his. "Consider this collection."

For a moment—one long, suspended moment—I thought he might simply take them, tear them up, and have me taken too. I saw the thought move through him like a shark under a boat. And then, slowly, he set the pages down and squared their edges against the desk, precisely, compulsively, a man restoring order to a world that had tilted.

"Mr. Ash," he said, "is in a cell because he committed crimes. That remains true regardless of paper." He picked up the cell key and held it, turning it in the light. "But gaol is expensive, and trials are unpredictable, and the company prefers... tidiness." He slid the key across the desk toward me. "Take it. Collect your friend. Consider his—irregularities—resolved through the company's discretion."

"And my debt?"

"Stands." He smiled, and there was nothing in it. "You gave me nothing, Miss Galloway. Copies of copies. But you gave me something better than you know—you showed me where you keep your courage. And courage, unlike debt, cannot be transferred." He straightened his cuffs. "Enjoy your Friday. The tide waits for no one—though I should mention, the *Southern Cross*'s berth arrangements have come under review. Company review. Nothing personal."

Ice went through me, slow and complete. He meant the berth. He meant my mother's passage—his men would find some regulation, some fee unpaid, some technicality to strip the cabin from under a dying woman before the ship sailed.

I took the key. My hand did not shake; I noted that with distant surprise, as one notes weather.

"Thank you for your kindness," I said.

"It's what I'm known for," said Calloway.

* * *

I walked to the cells with the key burning a hole in my palm and the knowledge burning a hole behind it: I had won the battle and shown the enemy the map. Tobias free. Mother's berth now a target. Father's name still unsold, my own soul still unentered in any man's book—and Calloway with nine days' grace and a telegraph wire to Sydney, hunting the one paper that could ruin him.

When they brought Tobias out, blinking and salt-scarred and thinner than the sea had left him, he searched my face before he said a word. Then: "What did it cost?"

"Nothing yet," I said. "Everything soon. Come on—we've a ship to catch, and Calloway means to be standing on the jetty when she sails."

Behind us, in the office window, the curtain moved.
