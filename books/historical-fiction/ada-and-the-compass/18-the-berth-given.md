## 18. The Berth Given

There is a particular kind of arithmetic that a woman learns in a port town, and it is not the arithmetic of school. School teaches you that two plus two makes four. The port teaches you that two plus two makes whatever the company says it makes, and that if you argue, you'll be charged for the ink.

But there is another arithmetic, the private kind, done in the dark behind closed eyelids, and it was this one I was doing on the last night before the Southern Cross sailed.

One berth. One purse of coin—my diving money, seven years of breath traded for shell, enough to buy exactly one passage to Sydney and no more. One mother with lungs like wet paper. One father hiding somewhere north of Thursday Island like a crab under a rock. One stranger sitting in Calloway's cells because he had stepped into a doorway meant for me. One ledger that weighed less than my mother's Bible and was worth more than every boat in the harbor.

And one daughter, standing at the center of it all, discovering that she was the only item in the sum that nobody had counted—including herself.

I sat up in the dark and did the sums until dawn greyed the window, and when the answer came it was so simple I laughed, which frightened me more than crying would have. The answer was me. It had always been me. Every plan we'd made by lamplight, every clever division of ledger and testimony and ship, had quietly assumed that Freya Galloway would be aboard the Southern Cross, holding her mother's arm, carrying the truth south in her own two hands. And the moment I looked at that assumption straight on, it fell apart like wet paper.

Because Tobias was in a cell, and Calloway did not release men for gratitude.

Because my mother could not walk to the jetty unaided on a good day, let alone navigate Sydney alone.

Because the original ledger—the one thing that mattered, the thing with Calloway's own initials against the entries for men who had never drowned at all—had to travel with someone whose word would hold in a magistrate's court, and my father's word, as we had all learned at considerable cost, was worth slightly less than the paper it wasn't written on.

So: the berth went to my mother. The coin went to Tobias. And the word went north.

* * *

Calloway's office smelled of ink and lamp oil and the particular staleness of a man who has been awake all night composing his own virtue. He received me standing, which he had never done before. Standing, he was taller than I remembered and somehow less substantial, like a coat hung on a nail.

"Miss Galloway." He folded his hands on the desk. "You've come about your friend."

"I've come about my debt."

"Ah." A small smile, the kind you'd give a child who has said something accidentally wise. "Your debt. You know, I reduced it once. Did you ever wonder why? Out of all the divers in this camp, all the families on our books, yours is the only account I ever touched."

"I assumed it was charity."

"Calloway doesn't keep charity on his books. It depreciates." He came around the desk slowly. "Your father owed this company four hundred and twelve pounds when he died. Do you know what he owed when I first met him? Nothing. He was the best diver I ever employed. Better than you, if it comes to that, and I don't say that to flatter either of you." He stopped close enough that I could smell the coffee on his breath, gone bitter and cold. "Men like your father are expensive, Miss Galloway. Not in pounds. In attention. They ask questions. They notice things. And then one night they take a boat out and they don't come back, and everyone says what a tragedy, and I have to write *drowned* in a ledger, and do you know how many times I have written *drowned*?"

I held very still. Somewhere below us, in a cell that smelled of bilge, Tobias Ash was listening to the same silence I was.

"How many?" I asked.

"That," said Calloway, "is an excellent question, and it is precisely the sort of question that gets people noticed." He returned to his desk, opened a drawer, and set a bank draft on the blotter. My eyes went to it before I could stop them. He saw. He always saw. "Tobias Ash will be released tomorrow morning. Consider it settled accounts between us."

"And what does it cost?"

"Nothing today. That's the beauty of it. Today it costs nothing." He sat down again and picked up his pen, and I understood that I had been dismissed, and that I had been given something far worse than a threat. I had been given a receipt.

* * *

My mother was packing when I got home, which was absurd, because everything she owned fit into one carpet bag she'd had since before I was born, and she had been packed for three days already. She just kept repacking it. Taking out the shawl, folding it differently, putting it back. A woman arranging the inside of a bag because the outside of her life had come apart.

"The Southern Cross sails on the morning tide," I said.

"I know what time it sails. I've known what time it sails since before you were born. Ships are the only things in this town that keep honest appointments."

"Mama." I sat down across from her, on the crate that served as a chair, and took her hands. They were lighter than I remembered. When had my mother become a bird? "Listen to me now, and don't interrupt, because if you interrupt I'll lose my nerve and we both know I'm the brave one in this family, God help us all."

She didn't smile. But she stilled.

"You're going to Sydney. You're taking the ledger. You'll find Magistrate Whitcombe—he'll be expecting you, Tobias sent word weeks ago—and you'll put that book in his hands and you'll tell him everything, even the parts that shame us. Especially those." I swallowed. "You're going to get well there. The air is better. There are doctors who aren't paid in company scrip. And in a year, maybe two, when it's safe—" My voice did something traitorous and thin, and I pressed on. "When it's safe, you'll send for me. Or I'll come to you. Either way. Either way, Mama."

She was quiet a long moment. Outside, a gull screamed at something, or at nothing; gulls in this town had long ago given up distinguishing between the two.

"And you," she said finally. "Where does Freya go, in this arrangement? Because I have been doing sums too, daughter, and I know how much a berth costs, and I know how much a prisoner costs, and I know that you were always better at arithmetic than you pretended."

"Tobias—"

"Tobias can rot. Forgive me. Tobias is a good man and can rot politely." Her grip tightened on my hands, surprising strength in it, the old Maren surfacing through the bird-bones like a reef through low tide. "I did not bury a husband and bury myself for twenty years so that my daughter could give away her own life in installments. First the berth, then the money, then what next? Your name? Your lungs? Will you dive until Calloway owns the air itself?"

"He has Tobias in a cell because of me. Because he stood where I should have stood."

"Yes," my mother said softly. "That is what love looks like when it arrives too late and in the wrong shape. I know. I married it."

We looked at each other across the crate, and I saw that she knew. She had known since the night I told her the plan, and she had been repacking that bag ever since because her hands needed work while her heart decided whether to fight me or bless me.

"You'll send for me," she said at last. Not a question. An instruction, delivered in the voice she'd used on me when I was six and wanted to swim past the reef line.

"I'll send for you."

"Liar," she said, without heat. "But say it again anyway."

"I'll send for you."

* * *

The money changed hands at the jailer's door in the blue hour before dawn, passed to a man whose face I never properly saw, and by the time the sun came up over the water like a coin someone had dropped and couldn't be bothered retrieving, Tobias Ash was standing beside me on the jetty road looking like a man who had been pulled from the sea twice in one lifetime.

"You bought me out," he said. Not grateful. Angry, almost. It was the anger of a man who understands a gift too well.

"I bought passage," I corrected. "What you do with the ticket is your affair. Though I'm told Sydney is pleasant this time of year, if you ignore the politics."

"Freya." He caught my arm—not hard, but firm, the way you catch someone walking in their sleep toward a stairwell. "Whatever you've decided to do—and I can see from your face that you've decided something, because you have your father's face when it decides things—you should know that Calloway let me go too easily. Men like him don't release hostages. They exchange them. He thinks he's traded me for you."

"Then he's made an error in his books," I said. "He should check his figures. He's careless with them—I have it on good authority."

Something moved across his scarred face that might have been admiration, or might have been grief wearing admiration's coat. He took my hand briefly, pressed something into it—a folded scrap, a name and an address in Sydney, a magistrate's clerk who could be trusted—and walked away down the jetty road without looking back, which was its own kind of mercy.

* * *

Sending word north was the part that scared me most, and I want to be honest about why. It wasn't fear for my father, though heaven knows the man has earned fear the way other men earn pensions. It was that I could not control what the message became once it left my hands.

A note to a fisherman who sometimes ran supplies to Thursday Island. A name. A date. *Come south. Come home.* Three words I had no right to write and wrote anyway.

If the fisherman was honest, my father would learn his daughter had need of him, and perhaps—perhaps—would come down through the islands like weather, ragged and late and himself.

If the fisherman was bought—and half this coast was bought, and the other half was renting—then Mr. Calloway would learn that the Galloway girl was summoning a dead man, and he would post men at every landing from here to Cape York, and my father would step off some boat into waiting hands.

I sealed the note and gave it away and stood watching the fisherman's dinghy until it was a smudge, hating myself for the hope in it. Hope, my mother always said, is just faith that hasn't checked the tide tables yet.

* * *

The Southern Cross took on passengers at noon, and the whole town found reasons to be near the jetty. That's the way of ports—every departure is theatre, and everyone attends in disguise. The divers' camp stood along the rail of the sea wall in their Sunday clothes that weren't Sunday clothes, and nobody spoke to me, and everybody watched, and I understood that I was being witnessed, though I couldn't yet have said to what purpose.

I walked my mother down the gangway myself, my hand under her elbow, feeling the tremor in her that she would have denied under oath. At the top step she stopped.

"The bag," she said. "The blue bundle, at the bottom."

"It's there. Wrapped in oilcloth. It won't get wet, it won't get lost, and it won't leave your body except into the magistrate's hands."

"You've become competent," she said. "It's unsettling. I keep expecting the girl who used to eat sand."

"Mama—"

"No." She turned and took my face in both her hands, and her palms were dry and hot with fever, and I made myself not flinch from it. "No speeches. You've had your speech. Here is mine: you were the best thing I ever did and I was never good enough to deserve you, and I am not saying goodbye, because goodbyes are for people who believe in endings, and I raised you to know better." She kissed my forehead, once, hard, like pressing a seal into wax. "Send for me."

"I'll send for you."

"Liar," she whispered, lovingly, and went aboard.

* * *

They cast off at half past one. I stayed on the jetty because leaving felt like a thing I hadn't earned yet, and because my legs had opinions of their own.

That was when she came back up from the deck—came back down the gangway against the mate's shouted objections, moving faster than she had moved in a year, as if farewell had lent her borrowed strength—and pressed something into my hand and folded my fingers over it and said, "From the strongbox. From him. You keep it; I've carried it long enough for both of us."

Then she was gone again, really gone, waving once from the stern rail with her whole arm the way she used to wave when I swam out past the breakers and she wanted me to know she was watching.

I opened my hand.

It was the compass. My father's compass—brass, worn smooth at the edges where a thumb worries it, the glass clouded with a hairline crack like a frozen tear. I turned it over. On the reverse, scratched deep, the way a man scratches a thing when he wants it to outlast him: *F.G. — for the girl.*

For the girl. Not for his wife. Not *return to sender*. For the girl, in a handwriting I knew better than my own reflection.

I stood on the jetty with a dead man's compass in my fist and watched my mother shrink into the distance, and the strangest thing happened, which is that I did not cry. I had budgeted for tears. I had planned on them the way you plan on rain. Instead what I felt was something harder and more useful settling into place, bone-deep, like ballast.

He had marked it *for the girl*. Years ago. Before the letters, before the lies, before any of it—he had known, even then, that someday his compass would come to me instead of back to him. Maybe he'd known he didn't deserve it back. Maybe he'd simply known me. Both possibilities hurt. I put the compass in my pocket, against my heart, where it sat cold as a small verdict.

The Southern Cross cleared the headland at three o'clock, running before a sky that had begun, all unknowing, to stack its grey plates in the east. Behind me, the town bell rang the hour. Ahead of me lay a walk home past the company office, past Calloway's window, past whatever he had arranged in exchange for a freed prisoner and a departed witness.

I patted the compass once, like a horse's neck.

"Well," I said to it, and to the water, and to whoever keeps the books on girls like me, "let's see what he does next."

The wind came off the sea and pushed at my back, and far to the north, past the reefs and the islands and the waiting dark, something answered—though it would be two days and a storm before I learned what.
