## 19. Storm Over the Roads

*Freya*

The storm did not arrive so much as take up residence.

It came in on Tuesday night with the smell of hot iron and rotting weed, and by Wednesday morning the Southern Cross was still sitting in the roads like a guest who had forgotten her manners and her anchor chain both. She should have been three days gone, her mother aboard, the ledger in the captain's strongroom, Sydney two thousand miles of open water behind them. Instead she lay wallowing at her moorings while the wind walked circles around the bay and every man in port pretended this was ordinary weather.

I stood on the jetty with my collar up and my hands in my pockets and watched her masts describe small, anxious figures against a sky the colour of a bruise going bad. The rain came sideways. It always comes sideways here; even the weather has learned not to face anything directly.

"She'll clear the reef when it eases," said the boy beside me, one of Mrs. Okafor's lot, sent down to fetch kerosene and gossiping instead. "Harbour master says Thursday, maybe Friday."

The harbour master. I turned that name over in my mind the way I'd once turned shell over, looking for pearl. Silas Vane was a big, slow man who spoke perhaps forty words a day and spent most of them on tide tables. His brother had gone out with a Meridian crew in '94 and been entered in the books as lost with all hands — drowned, poor fellow, terrible business, company sends its condolences and deducts the funeral rations from his account. I had copied that entry myself, in my neat invisible hand, and thought nothing of it.

I thought a great deal about it now.

"Tobias says the magistrate in Sydney won't wait past the new moon," the boy went on, pleased to be the bearer of important things. "Says if the ledger doesn't arrive—"

"Then it arrives," I said. "It's on the ship."

It was on the ship because I had put it there myself, wrapped in oilcloth inside my mother's spare shawl, and watched the second mate stow it under lock while my mother climbed the gangway leaning on the rail as though the rail were a son-in-law. She had looked back at me exactly once. She hadn't waved. We are not a waving family; we hold things in our eyes and hope they keep.

She had pressed something into my hand before she went. The compass. My father's compass, brass worn to gold at the edges, the glass fogged from the inside like an eye that had seen too much water. *He'd want you to have it,* she'd said, which was a lie so tender I let it stand. He'd want nothing of the sort. He'd want absolution, or silence, or whichever was cheaper. But I took the compass, and I held it now through my coat pocket as the storm leaned on the town, and it felt less like a gift than like a debt being transferred between accounts.

Tobias found me at midday, or rather materialised beside me the way he does, all scar tissue and economy of motion, as if the wind had blown him there and he couldn't be bothered to object.

"Calloway's men are at the customs shed," he said without greeting. We had passed greetings some weeks ago, somewhere around the night he took my arrest onto himself, and neither of us had missed them. "Six of them. They've got a launch provisioned."

"Provisioned for what? A fishing trip?"

"For boarding." He said it flatly, but I felt the floor of the world tilt a degree. "A ship in the roads is legally nobody's ground until she clears the heads. Company charter gives Calloway inspection rights on any vessel carrying contracted labour or company freight. The Southern Cross carries both."

"Can the captain refuse?"

"The captain can refuse anything he likes from inside a locked cabin while six men walk through his hold." Tobias watched the ship roll. "Calloway will say he's searching for stolen property. He'll have a warrant by tonight if he hasn't got one already. And Freya—" he paused, and I braced, because Tobias pauses are never decorative "—the ledger isn't the only thing he wants off that ship. Your mother saw his face in your kitchen. She heard him name his price. Dead women tell no tales and neither do living ones bound for a company hospital up the coast."

The rain chose that moment to redouble, hammering the jetty planks like a creditor at the door. I thought of my mother coughing into the fold of her shawl in a cabin full of strangers, thinking herself safe, thinking the hardest part done. I thought of Calloway's voice saying *kindness is an instrument, Miss Galloway, the only one worth owning,* and how I had believed I understood him.

"He can't touch her," I said. "Not in front of witnesses. Not the harbour master's own roads."

"No," Tobias agreed. "Which is why he'll wait for dark, and why the harbour master will suddenly find himself very busy elsewhere. Men like Calloway don't break laws, Freya. they arrange for the law to be somewhere else."

* * *

*Tobias*

I had crossed two oceans and survived a cell and a beating that rearranged several of my opinions about my ribs, and I will confess that what frightened me most in all of it was watching Freya Galloway listen to bad news. Most people flinch. She goes still, the way deep water goes still, and then something moves underneath that you would swear wasn't there a moment ago.

"We need to warn her mother," I said.

"With what?" Freya's voice was level. "A rowboat? In that?"

She was right, and we both knew it. The harbour launch was chained at its mooring; the company launch was the only hull in the water besides Vane's pilot boat, and Vane's boat sat dry-docked with its seams opened for caulking, which struck me, standing there in the rain, as either the worst luck in the history of seamanship or the best arrangement Calloway's money had ever bought.

"There's another way," Freya said slowly. "The divers' camp. Forty men who owe the company money and hate it with the pure hatred of the indebted. If they were on the jetty when the launch went out—"

"If they were on the jetty, Calloway would know whose idea it was, and you've spent your whole life teaching this town not to look at you." I heard my own voice sharpen and didn't soften it. "You gave your berth away to make one point. Don't spend the camp to make another."

"And what would you do?" she asked. "Wait? You're good at waiting. You waited three years to deliver those letters."

"I'm good at surviving," I said. "It's a related skill. Waiting is what survival does when it has nothing better to do."

But the decision, as decisions in this town always did, made itself before either of us could claim credit for it.

* * *

*Freya*

They boarded at dusk, of course. Villains keep banker's hours.

I was in the customs shed — legitimately, or near enough; I still carried Calloway's forged requisition folded against my heart like a second conscience — when I heard the launch engines, low and ugly against the wind. Through the salt-fogged window I watched the long black shape push out from the company wharf, riding heavy, six men and a lantern at the bow that swung like a hanged man.

And then I watched something else.

Silas Vane came down his own jetty at a pace I had never seen him use, a pace that suggested the tide tables could go hang. He untied the lines of a dinghy that I knew — the whole town knew — belonged to no one, because it had sat upside down on his beach for eleven years since his brother's boat failed to come home. It was right side up now. It had been right side up, I realised, for some days, though I had been too busy with ledgers and love and other people's wars to notice a man quietly turning his grief over and scraping the barnacles off it.

Vane put out alone into weather that would have kept the admiralty ashore, and he steered his little boat directly across the bow of the company launch.

I want to be accurate about what happened next, because accuracy is the whole religion I converted to that year. The launch blew its whistle. Vane did not move. The launch altered course. Vane altered with it, unhurried, a man herding something rather than blocking it, his oilskin streaming, his face — even at that distance, even through rain — entirely empty of expression. The launch tried to run wide. Vane ran wider. The launch stopped. Vane stopped, ten yards off its bow, and sat there in his dead brother's boat like a sentence that would not be punctuated.

Through the rain I heard shouting, thin and furious. I heard the launch's engine rev and fall, rev and fall, a beast testing a fence. And then, one by one, lights began to come out along the shore. Lanterns in the divers' camp. Lanterns on the fishing racks. Lanterns carried down to the jetty by men and women who had spent years perfecting the art of looking at nothing, now standing in a line at the water's edge looking at everything.

Nobody shouted. Nobody moved toward the launch. That was the terrible elegance of it — there was nothing to arrest, nothing to charge, just a town standing up in the rain the way a sleeper turns over, slowly, without quite waking, until the bed itself has changed shape.

"They're not doing anything," Tobias said at my shoulder. He had come into the shed silent as ever, and his voice had an odd grain in it, like wood that's been soaked too long. "That's the whole trick. There's no crime called standing on a beach."

"It was you," I said. "You told them."

"I told no one anything. I don't have their trust. I've been here five months." He nodded at the shore, at the lanterns, at old Mrs. Okafor standing at the head of the jetty with her arms folded as though supervising weather. "You told them. Every day for nine years, by being the girl who copied their debts and never once looked away from them like they were furniture. You thought invisibility meant nobody saw you. It meant everybody did."

I didn't answer. There was a knot in my throat the size of the compass in my pocket.

Out in the roads, the launch hung on its engines a long moment, the lantern at its bow swinging, swinging. Then it came about, sullen as a cur called off, and crawled back toward the company wharf. Vane watched it all the way in. Then he rowed his brother's boat back to the beach, hauled it above the tideline, and stood beside it a moment with his hand on the gunwale — a man laying something down, or picking something up, I couldn't tell which from that distance and never asked him after.

On the Southern Cross, a lantern appeared at the rail. Small, moving, agitated. Then a second figure beside it, steadying the first, and even across black water and half a gale I knew the set of those shoulders, the way my mother carried her cough like a parcel she refused to set down.

"She's safe," I said. "Tonight."

"Tonight," Tobias agreed. "The storm holds her till Thursday. Calloway holds nothing but his temper, and tempers are the one debt that collects interest fastest." He turned to me, and the lamplight from the customs office caught the scars along his jaw, the map of everywhere he'd been and none of it willingly. "There's a telegraph office open till eight. After tonight, Calloway stops asking politely, and Sydney starts answering. Whatever happens next happens quickly, and I need to know — before it starts — whether you sent word north."

My father. Thursday Island. The word I had promised myself I would send and had not yet sent, the compass heavy in my pocket pointing, as compasses do, at whatever I least wanted to face.

"I haven't decided," I said.

"You have," said Tobias. "You decided the day you read his letters. You're just waiting to see whether you were brave enough to mean it." He glanced out at the ship rolling at her moorings, my mother's lantern steady at the rail. "Because if that man steps onto any deck in this harbour, Freya — if he surrenders himself the way he means to, to pull Calloway's dogs off that ship — then someone has to be standing on the jetty when he does. Someone who isn't me."

The wind slammed the shed door. Out in the dark, the sea went on rehearsing its oldest argument, and I put my hand around my father's compass and felt it turn, faintly, stubbornly, toward the north.
