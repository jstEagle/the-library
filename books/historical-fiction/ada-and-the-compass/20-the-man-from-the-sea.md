## 20. The Man from the Sea

The storm did not end so much as lose interest. By dawn it had spent its fury on the roads and gone sullen, trailing rain like a woman leaving an argument she intends to resume later. The *Southern Cross* still sat in the anchorage, rolling at her anchor chain, her masts swaying against the grey in long, patient arcs—nine hundred tons of Sydney-bound hope, stalled by weather and, as of last night, by the company's launch sitting squarely between her and the open sea.

I had not slept. I had stood at the window of our house watching the lightning walk across the water, holding my mother's compass in one hand and the copied ledger in the other, doing the arithmetic that never came out even. Tobias was in Calloway's lockup. The original ledger was aboard the ship, sewn into the lining of my mother's sea chest, which meant it was also, effectively, sewn into my mother's ribs. And somewhere north of us, if the message had reached him—if the message hadn't been intercepted—a man who had been dead for six years was deciding whether to come home.

He came.

I saw the boat before I understood what I was seeing: a dinghy, patched and salt-white, running down the last ragged edge of the swell with more nerve than sense. The man at her tiller wore oilskins that had given up arguing with the rain years ago, and he steered the way my father used to steer, which is to say carelessly, arrogantly, as though the water were a horse that ought to be flattered into obedience.

I went down to the jetty because my legs decided before my head did. The harbor master was already there, arms folded, watching the boat come in. Behind him, half a dozen divers from the camp had drifted out of their huts in the grey light, drawn by whatever animal instinct tells people when history is about to happen in their vicinity.

The dinghy bumped the pilings. He caught a rope, made fast, and looked up at me.

Six years is a long time for a face, but not long enough. The beard had gone grey at the jaw. There was a scar through one eyebrow I didn't recognize, and the hands on the rope were knotted like old rootwood. But the eyes were mine, or mine were his—I've never resolved whose they were first—and looking into them was like looking into a mirror hung in a stranger's house.

"Freya," he said.

"You're supposed to be drowned," I said. "It's on record."

* * *

He climbed onto the jetty with the ease of a man who has stepped off boats all his life, which he had, just not this one, not into this harbor, where every plank knew his name and his debt. The rain had thinned to a drifting mist. He stood there dripping, and I stood there dripping, and around us the divers watched with the particular silence of people who have decided to see nothing while seeing everything.

"You got my letter," he said. It wasn't a question, but I answered it anyway.

"I got three years of them. In a strongbox at the bottom of your boat. Which I dove for. At night. In the season when the sharks come in close." I let that sit. "You wrote about the weather, mostly."

His mouth worked. I had imagined this moment so many ways—in rage, in tears, in the arms of a daughter too starved to be choosy—that I was surprised to find myself simply tired. Grief, it turns out, keeps. Like salt meat. You can carry it years and it's still there, only drier.

"There were things I couldn't put in letters," he said. "Things I could only say to your face."

"You said them to Calloway's face instead, did you? That night on the water?" I saw it land—the flicker, the recalculation. "Yes. He told me. He enjoys telling me things. It's how he hunts."

"Callum." My father said the name the way you'd spit a fishbone. "Calloway now. He was a clerk then. A little man with wet hands and a big book, and he liked the book better than the men in it." He glanced at the harbor master, at the divers, at the mist-shrouded shape of the company office above the town. "Freya. We can't talk here."

"No," I agreed. "We can't talk anywhere. That's rather the family condition."

But I took him home, because he was my father, because my mother needed to see him, because there is a debt even betrayal owes to itself. I led him up through the town past the company watchman, who looked at us both and looked away again with the magnificent discipline of a man who values his pension. Word would reach Calloway within the hour regardless. Some news travels faster than gossip; it travels as duty.

* * *

My mother was sitting up when we came in, propped on pillows, thin as a winter branch. She looked at him a long time. He looked back. Whatever passed between them took perhaps ten seconds and contained twenty years.

"You look terrible, Aksel," she said.

"You should see the other fellows."

She laughed, and the laugh turned into coughing, and he crossed the room to hold the cup of water to her lips as though no time had passed at all, as though the intervening six years were a misfiled page he could simply turn past. I watched him do it and felt something in my chest go very quiet and very cold. It is a terrible discovery, learning that you do not love someone any less for hating what they did. Nobody warns you the two can live in the same house.

They talked in low voices. I sat by the window with the compass in my pocket and listened to the rain, and after a while he turned to me and said, "You have questions."

"I have a ledger page with your confession on it. I have your unsent letters. I have a mother who burned hers to keep you safe, and a friend in a cell because he carried yours." I took out the compass and set it on the table between us. Its brass face caught the grey light. "I don't need answers. Answers are for people still waiting."

He looked at the compass and then at me, and for the first time since the dinghy touched the piling, my father looked afraid.

* * *

What he proposed, he proposed standing up, the way he'd once announced we were leaving the pearling grounds early, decisions made in his body before they reached anyone else's.

"Calloway wants me," he said. "Me alive, or me drowned properly, either serves. If I walk into that office this morning and give myself up, his men come off the jetty. Every one of them. He'll want me under his own eye until the warrant comes from Thursday Island, and he'll want to search me, and question me, and gloat—" his mouth twisted— "and while he does, the *Southern Cross* takes the tide and goes."

"The tide turns at noon," I said.

"At noon," he agreed.

"And you'd hang for the drowning. For the debt. For all of it."

"I'd testify. That's the difference between hanging and mattering, these days. There's a magistrate in Sydney who wants witnesses more than corpses." He smiled, and it was my smile, the one I catch in shop windows and hate. "I faked my death once, girl. I know precisely what it's worth. Nothing. But my death, done honestly, in front of Calloway, with my name on a statement—that buys your mother clean water air for the rest of her life."

In the corner, my mother said nothing. She had said her piece in the night, apparently, in the low-voiced conference I'd pretended not to hear. Her silence now was not resignation. It was assent. That frightened me more than anything else in the room.

"And what do I get?" I asked. "In this trade."

He flinched. Good, I thought. Let him learn the exchange rates.

"You get free," he said. "You get out from under the debt and the name and the watching. You get to be a person instead of a figure in a column." He hesitated. "And you get to never see me again, if that's the price you set."

I looked at the compass on the table. Six years of brass and glass and a needle that had pointed, faithfully, at nothing in particular, because nobody had been steering.

* * *

I walked him to the jetty at half past eleven, because someone had to, and because I wanted the walk. The rain had stopped entirely. The clouds were tearing open in the west, letting down bars of yellow light, and out in the roads the *Southern Cross* lay with steam up, her crew aloft shaking canvas loose, waiting on the tide and on whatever happened ashore.

Calloway's men were on the jetty. Three of them, and the launch idling alongside, and Mr. Calloway himself standing at the head of the steps with his hat dry and his coat buttoned and the expression of a man receiving a delivery he had ordered personally.

"Well," he said, when he saw my father. "Aksel Galloway. Dead six years and looking remarkably well on it."

"Callum." My father gave him a bow that was pure mockery. "Still wearing other men's money."

"Come inside, Galloway. We have a great deal to discuss, you and I. Debts. Testimony. The disposal of certain documents." His eyes moved to me, mild as milk. "Miss Galloway. Your mother is well?"

"She's dying," I said. "You know that. It's why you brought the medicine."

Something moved behind his pleasantness—not guilt; I doubt he owned any—but accounting. Recalculating a figure. My father shifted slightly, putting himself between Calloway's gaze and me, and I realized with a strange, distant tenderness that it was the first protective thing he had done for me in six years, and it was unnecessary, and I almost forgave him for it.

Almost.

"Mr. Calloway," my father said, loud enough for the jetty, for the divers drifting near, for the watchman pretending to inspect a bollard. "Before we go in. I want it witnessed that I'm giving myself up freely. That I'm the one who's come in from the sea. And that everything I know about Meridian's island trade—every manifest, every false death, every man sold south—goes with me to whoever asks next. Starting," he nodded toward the launch, "with your good self."

The jetty went very quiet. Divers do not cheer. Cheering costs money. But I felt the silence change weight, the way water changes weight before a squall, and Calloway felt it too. His men came off the rail. They closed around my father like a ledger closing, and he went with them, upright, unrepentant, glancing back once—

I walked to him. Past Calloway. Past the launch lines. I put the compass in my father's hand and closed his fingers over it.

"That points home," I said. "Wherever you decide that is. I'm not going to be it. I'm setting you free from that too."

He stared at me. His eyes were wet, and I chose—with an effort that cost more than the night dive, more than the ledger, more than the berth—to believe they were.

"Freya—"

"Don't," I said gently. "You've spent six years writing letters you never sent. Don't start speaking one now."

I stepped back. They took him up the steps. Calloway paused beside me, and for a moment neither courtesy nor cruelty was in his face, only a kind of professional regret, the look of a man watching a well-built structure fail inspection.

"You could still have been comfortable," he said.

"I know," I said. "That's what comfort costs."

* * *

They took him into the office. Within minutes, the jetty was emptying—the launch crew recalled, the watchmen pulled inside, every eye of the company turning inward toward the man who had walked out of the sea carrying six years of their crimes in his memory. Calloway wanted him alone, sealed, controlled. Calloway had never once considered that some men are most dangerous precisely when contained, because containment makes witnesses curious.

By noon the tide had turned. The *Southern Cross* weighed anchor with the sun breaking through and her rails crowded with borrowed light, and I stood on the empty jetty watching my mother's small figure at the stern rail, one hand raised, the wind taking her hair sideways, growing smaller and smaller as the ship stood out toward the reef passage and Sydney and the magistrate who would read what we had copied and stolen and bled for.

Then the harbor master was beside me, breathing hard, having run the length of the shore road.

"Telegraph boy just came down," he said. "Warrant from Thursday Island. Signed this morning. It names the ship."

The *Southern Cross* was still inside the reef, still in the company's water, and somewhere behind us a launch was being made ready, and Calloway's voice was rising in the office above as he learned what had walked out of his grasp.

"Which ship?" I asked, though I already knew.

"All of them," the harbor master said grimly. "Yours included."
