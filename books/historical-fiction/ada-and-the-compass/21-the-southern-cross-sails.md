## 21. The Southern Cross Sails

The warrant came on the morning tide, which was the company's way of saying it had never been in a hurry about anything, least of all justice.

I knew before Calloway read it aloud. I had learned to read men the way I read water—the set of the surface telling you what moved beneath. Mr. Calloway stood on the jetty boards in his good coat with his telegram paper in his hand and his men arranged around him like punctuation, and he looked at me with something that in a better man might have been regret.

"Freya Galloway," he said, and the jetty went quiet around us. "You are detained on charges of theft of company property, forgery of company documents, and conspiracy against the Meridian Trading Company."

Theft. Forgery. Conspiracy. He made it sound like I had done something clever, when in truth I had only done what the sea had taught me: held my breath and gone down after what was mine.

"Where is my mother?" I asked.

"Gone," said Calloway. "Sailed on the dawn tide." His mouth did something at the corner—not quite a smile, not quite its opposite. "With a certain ledger, I'm told. Though of course the ship won't clear the reef before my launch can reach her. Papers travel slowly, Miss Galloway. Ships travel slower."

He thought he had her. That was the thing about Calloway—he was a man who believed the world ran on paper because paper had always run for him. He had never been under the water. He didn't know that the reef had moods, that the passage north could silt up overnight in a blow, that a harbor master with a slow boat and a long memory could make a channel lie about itself.

I said nothing. Silence is a currency, and I had learned to save.

"You look pale," he observed. "Grief, or guilt?"

"Habit," I said. "I've spent three years being careful. It takes a while to stand down."

Something flickered behind his eyes—respect, maybe, if respect were a thing he kept anywhere but his ledger. Then he nodded to his men, and they took my arms, not roughly, because Calloway's cruelty had always worn gloves.

* * *

They put me in the company office, in the room where I had copied debt figures for six years, in the chair where I had first noticed the dead men whose rations still got drawn. There was a symmetry to it that would have pleased me more if my hands weren't shaking.

Calloway sat across from me and poured tea from his own pot, as though we were colleagues settling an account.

"I want you to understand," he said, "that this needn't end badly. The ship hasn't cleared the reef. My launch is fueled. If you tell me where your father's testimony is—where Ash hid the original pages—I can recall the launch. Your mother arrives in Sydney as a witness, nothing more. A respectable woman giving evidence. And you go home to your diving."

"And if I don't?"

"Then she arrives in Sydney as the wife of a fugitive who trafficked in stolen men's names, and the daughter of a forger, and the company will have lawyers there before she has a berth ashore." He stirred his tea. "You see how it is. The truth doesn't swim, Freya. It needs someone to carry it, and carriers drown."

"The truth floats," I said. "That's what I've learned. You can weight it, and burn it, and bury it in a strongbox under thirty feet of green water, and it comes up anyway. It comes up holding its breath longer than any diver alive."

He set down his cup with a small click, precise as everything he did.

"Your father," he said, "was a coward. I want you to know I say that with some authority, having watched him run. But you—you're something else. You're the kind of trouble that doesn't stop when it's hit. I'd have advanced you far, in another life."

"In another life I wouldn't have taken the advance," I said. "I know how those work now."

* * *

They left me alone through the middle of the day, which I understood as a technique rather than a mercy. Fear does its best work in empty rooms. So I did what I do underwater: I counted. My mother on the Southern Cross with the ledger sewn into the lining of her coat. Tobias in a cell two streets away, arrested for a diversion that had cost him his freedom and bought me a night. My father somewhere on the water, carrying a compass that used to point home and now pointed nowhere I intended to follow.

And the harbor master, Dan Kellaway, whose brother had gone into a company manifest as drowned six years ago and come out the far side of the world as someone else's property. Dan, who had turned his boat across Calloway's launch in the storm without a word of explanation, as though it were the most natural maneuver in the world—as though boats had always done that here, when the cargo was wrong.

I was still counting when the light through the office window began to go amber, and I heard the sound I had been listening for without admitting it: engines. Not one engine. Several.

Boots on the jetty. Voices raised—not Calloway's measured tenor but the rougher music of the divers' camp, the pearling crews, the beach people whose names appeared in ledgers only as numbers and debts. And over all of it, one voice I knew, cracked and salt-bent, saying words I couldn't catch.

The door opened. It was not Calloway. It was Dan Kellaway, filling the frame of it, with half the camp behind him—men and women I had dived beside, shopped beside, sat beside at a hundred funerals the company had paid for with wreaths and deductions. Old Mrs. Tregenza, whose son was on the island manifest. The Vunivalu brothers, who between them had eleven years of contract left and had never once spoken to me above a murmur. Boys I had taught their letters on Sundays, because nobody else would.

"They're saying you're arrested," Dan said.

"That's accurate," I said.

"For stealing from the company."

"Also accurate."

He nodded slowly, as a man confirming a tide table. "Good," he said. "Because there's forty of us out there ready to swear we saw you on the jetty all Friday night, mending nets, and none of us saw you near any records room. And there's another twenty who'll swear the ledger Calloway's so exercised about was never in this town at all."

"That's perjury," I said. "That's a crime."

Dan smiled, and it transformed his face from weathered to ancient to young again, some trick of the light. "So is selling living men as dead ones," he said. "We've decided to be criminals in the direction that pays."

* * *

Calloway came back at dusk, and found his office full.

Not occupied—full. They had come inside without asking, the way the sea comes into a boat, and they stood along the walls and sat on the filing cabinets and leaned against the shelves of ledgers that had weighed our lives for twenty years, and every one of them looked at Mr. Calloway with the patience of people who had waited a very long time to be counted.

He stopped in the doorway. For a moment—and I will remember this moment as long as I remember anything—he looked afraid.

"This is interference with company business," he said.

"No," said old Mrs. Tregenza, in her thin voice that carried like wire. "This is witnesses. You wanted them. Here we are."

"The girl forged documents—"

"The girl," said one of the Vunivalu brothers, in English he usually saved for market, "copied what you wrote. That is not forging. That is reading."

There was a laugh at that, low and dangerous, the kind of laugh a crowd makes when it has discovered its own size. And I sat in my chair by the window and felt something happen in my chest that I had no name for—something that wasn't triumph, because triumph implies surprise, and I was not surprised. I had known these people my whole life. I had simply never known they knew me back.

Invisibility, I thought. All those years of being unremarkable, of keeping my head down and my mouth shut and my grief folded small enough to fit inside a workday. And it had never worked. It had only ever looked like it worked, the way the sea looks calm over a wreck. They had seen me the whole time. Every dive, every copied figure, every quiet kindness at the mission school—they had filed it away in the ledger they kept, the one Calloway couldn't audit.

"Calloway," I said, and he turned to me as though I had struck him—perhaps because I had never once in six years initiated speech. "Your telegram went to Sydney. Mine went too. Three days ago, from the mission wireless, in the name of the harbor master. To Magistrate Whitlam, care of the *Southern Cross*, with a copy of the manifest page. She'll have it before your lawyers finish breakfast."

I had not sent any such telegram. But Dan Kellaway, behind him, went very still in the way of a man watching a bluff succeed, and I understood in that instant that he had sent exactly that telegram, days ago, and had said nothing, and had let me believe for one whole afternoon that I had lost.

The harbor master stepped forward and laid a paper on Calloway's desk. "Warrant for the arrest of one T. Ash, on complaint of the Meridian Trading Company," he said. "Ash is in custody. Complaint withdrawn this afternoon by the complainant's own harbormaster's certification that the evidence was obtained improperly." He tapped the desk. "You'll find, Mr. Calloway, that a harbor master certifies what happens in his harbor. It's in the regulations. I've read them. All of them. Twice."

"You'd ruin yourself," Calloway said. "The company owns this port."

"The company owns the lease," said Dan. "The port belongs to the people who can swim in it."

For a long moment nobody moved. Outside, the evening came down over the water, gold going to grey, and somewhere beyond the reef the *Southern Cross* was standing out into deep water with my mother aboard, and the ledger, and the truth, riding the swell like anything else that floats.

Calloway looked at me. Whatever he was looking for, I made sure he found it: nothing hidden, nothing flinching. A woman sitting in a chair, counting.

"Take off the detention order," Dan said. "Or explain to Sydney why the whole port witnessed you refuse."

* * *

They took it off.

I walked out onto the jetty into a crowd that parted for me and then, instead of closing behind me as crowds do, closed *around* me—hands on my shoulders, my arms, old Mrs. Tregenza gripping my wrist with a strength that belied her hundred years. Someone pressed a cup of tea into my hands. Someone else was already arguing about who would row me out to the camp for supper, as though supper were a settled fact of the universe and my attendance non-negotiable.

The lights of the camp came on along the shore, one by one, the way they always did, ordinary as breathing. Beyond the heads, the last light caught a sail—or the ghost of one, the *Southern Cross* gone over the horizon hours since, carrying my mother toward magistrates and justice and a future I would hear about in letters.

I stood there a long time, tea cooling in my hands, while the camp talked and laughed and argued around me, including me in it without ceremony, without ever once making it a favor.

Six years I had believed that history belonged to admirals and company men, that people like me were what history happened *on*. Standing on the jetty with forty witnesses who had chosen, out of nothing but their own hard-won decency, to stand where I stood, I understood that I had been wrong in the way that matters most: not in the facts, but in the arithmetic. History had never been happening to me. It had been waiting for me. It had been waiting for all of us, patient as the tide, and tonight, for the first time, we had walked out to meet it.

The tide turned below the pilings. Somewhere north, on Thursday Island, a man with my father's handwriting and my father's cowardice was waiting on word that might bring rescue or ruin. And somewhere south, a magistrate was about to open a ledger and start counting.

I finished my tea, and went down to the water, and put my hand in it—cold, honest, remembering everything—to see which way it was running.

It was running north.
