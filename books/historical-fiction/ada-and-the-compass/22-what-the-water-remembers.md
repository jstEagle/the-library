## 22. What the Water Remembers

The letter came on a Tuesday, which meant nothing to anyone except me and the tide tables.

I had learned by then that the great events of the world announce themselves in the smallest ways—a steamer's whistle arriving a day late, a boy running too fast down the jetty road, an envelope with Sydney postmarks gone soft from sea air. The trial had been going for eleven weeks. We tracked it the way other towns track cricket scores: the harbor master reading aloud from weeks-old newspapers to anyone who would stand still long enough, which was nearly everyone, because everyone had a brother or a cousin or a father among the names.

*Regina versus Meridian Trading Company.* It sounded like a hymn when Mr. Okoro said it, though he said it flatly, the way he said everything, as if even the fall of empires were a matter of routine bookkeeping. Which, I suppose, it was. That was rather the point of the whole affair.

But I'm ahead of myself. The letter first. The letter came on a Tuesday, and my mother's handwriting on the outside of it was steady, which told me before I broke the seal that she was well. When her hands shook, the G's went sideways. These G's stood up straight as masts.

---

You should understand what I was doing when it arrived, because I have decided—and this is one of the few decisions I've made entirely on my own terms—that context is everything. History belongs to whoever bothers to write down what happened while it's happening. So: context.

I was sitting on the veranda of the mission schoolhouse with nine children arranged around me on upturned packing crates, teaching them their letters. Not the company's letters. Their own. There is a difference, and the difference has a body count.

The schoolhouse had been the company store once, back before the trial, back when Calloway ran his accounts upstairs and drew rations for men he'd sold south of the reef. After the warrants came and the company men left on the same boats they'd used for cargo, the building stood empty for a season like a shell after the crab has gone. Then the divers' camp got together its pooled wages—which were not much, wages never being much where Meridian had held the books—and hired me.

Hired *me*. The daughter of a disgraced diver, the girl who'd spent six years being unremarkable so thoroughly that I could walk through the company office invisible as dust. They paid me two pounds a month and all the fish I could eat, and I taught letters on crates in a room that still smelled faintly of tallow and ink, and I will tell you honestly that no berth on any ship bound for anywhere has ever tempted me since.

"Miss Galloway," said Nellie Okoro, who was seven and already better at arithmetic than half the company clerks ever were, "the postman's coming."

"The postman comes every day," I said. "That's why they call him the postman."

"This time he's stopping."

He was, too. He climbed the steps with his hat in his hand—he always took his hat off near me, though I'd stopped asking why; something about the trial, something about the photograph in the papers—and handed over a bundle. Two envelopes and a newspaper folded around a third envelope, wrapped tight against salt water, which is how you mail things when you know what the sea does to paper.

One envelope was my mother's hand. One was in a slanted, confident script I knew almost as well as my own, having spent one long night reading everything it had ever chosen not to send me. And inside the newspaper—well. Inside the newspaper was the clipping, and I'll come to that, because some things want telling in their proper order and I have earned the right to order things however I please.

---

My mother wrote four pages. She wrote about the weather in Sydney, which was cold in a way that offended her personally. She wrote about the boarding house run by the widow of another diver, a woman named Mrs. Prynne who salted everything twice and prayed over nothing, which Maren approved of enormously. She wrote that her cough had quieted—"not cured, Freya, don't fuss, but quieted, like a dog that has decided the postman is harmless"—and that Dr. Fennimore at the free clinic had listened to her chest with a stethoscope and pronounced her lungs *improved*, a word she underlined three times.

And then, on the fourth page, in writing that went smaller, the way it did when she was saying the true thing:

*They read your name out in court. Did you know that? Your name and Tobias's and the harbor master's brother's, all of it entered into the record, spoken aloud in a proper courtroom with a proper judge. A barrister—I'll tell you his name so you can be proud properly: Mr. Edmund Vale, QC—held up the ledger page and asked the court to observe the handwriting of the entries marked DECEASED, and then he held up the manifest and observed that dead men draw very regular rations. There was laughter in the gallery. Ugly laughter, the kind that comes before things break.*

*I thought of you the whole time. I thought: my daughter copied those pages. My daughter sat in that office under that man Calloway's nose and rewrote their history line by line, and here it is now, being read into the law of the land. You were there in that courtroom, love, whether you stood in it or not. More than most who did.*

*Calloway gave them everything, did you hear? Turned Crown witness to save his neck. He wept, they say. I hope it hurt him. I hope it hurt him the way kindness hurts, when you finally see what it was for.*

*Come visit when the season turns. Or don't, and I'll come home to you. Either way, I am your mother and I am alive and I intend to stay both for a good while yet, purely out of spite.*

*Yours entire,*
*Mama*

I read it twice. On the veranda, in the morning sun, with nine children watching me with the frank, unembarrassed attention of children who have learned that adults sometimes need looking after. Nellie patted my knee. I let her. There are dignities you keep and dignities you spend, and I've gotten better at knowing which is which.

---

Tobias's letter was shorter. Tobias's letters were always shorter, as though words cost him by the ounce and he'd spent his fortune getting across two oceans once and didn't care to do it again on stationery.

*Freya—*

*The magistrate offers me a position. Evidence clerk. Me, filing papers in a government office with a roof on it. I told him I'd think about it, which means yes, because a man who has crossed two oceans for a dead friend's satchel learns eventually to stop pretending he doesn't want dry ground.*

*Your father sailed north in March. Last I heard he was working a lugger out of Thursday Island, which I grant you is a strange place to hide for a man whose whole talent is hiding, but grief makes geographers of us all. He asked after you. I told him you were teaching children to read. He said nothing for a long time, and then he said, "She always could." Take that however you like. I've taken it as what it sounds like: a man discovering that his daughter was more than he ever looked at.*

*The compass found you, then? Good. He'd want that. Not because he deserves to have it matter—but because it should belong to someone who knows what it cost.*

*I remain, as ever, in your debt and glad of it.*
*T.A.*

P.S. *Mrs. Prynne salts everything twice. I have checked. She is a menace and your mother adores her. This is the kind of intelligence work I am reduced to, and I have never been happier.*

---

And then the newspaper.

I unfolded it carefully, the way you unfold things that might cut you. It was the *Sydney Morning Herald*, dated five weeks past, and the clipping—the one someone, my mother or Tobias or perhaps Mrs. Prynne herself, had folded around my father's letter like a protective shell—was set below a headline in type so large it seemed to shout:

**MERIDIAN TRADING COMPANY DISMANTLED — ISLAND LABOUR TRADE EXPOSED — MAGISTRATE'S FINDINGS "A STAIN UPON THE COLONY"**

Below it, a column of small print, and in the middle of the small print, a paragraph:

*"Central to the case was a series of documents copied in the accused firm's own offices, apparently by an employee whose identity the prosecution declined to reveal, describing her only as 'a copyist of unusual courage.' The presiding magistrate remarked that 'the history of this colony has too often been written by the men who profited from it. It is a fine thing when someone else takes up the pen.'"*

*A copyist of unusual courage.*

I sat with that a long while. Long enough that the children finished their practice rows and started drawing ships in the margins, long enough that the shadow of the veranda post crawled a hand's width across the boards.

Here is what I want recorded, since recording is the trade I've taken up: I did not cry. Or rather, I cried the way the tide comes in—not dramatically, not all at once, but everywhere at once and without permission. Nine children pretended very hard not to notice, which is the finest manners I have ever witnessed in any colony of this or any empire, and I include Parliament.

Because here is the thing. Here is the thing I had believed with my whole spine for six years, diving debt off the bottom of the sea: history belongs to admirals and company men. It gets written in London and Sydney by people who never smelled the water, and the rest of us are just what happens *to* it, the way weather happens to a coastline, wearing us down without our consent, unnamed.

And it turned out I had been wrong in the specific way that matters. History had happened to me—yes, all of it, the drowning that wasn't, the ledger, the berth given away, the warrant arriving too late on the jetty while half the divers' camp stood shoulder to shoulder beside a girl the company had measured and marked and failed to see coming. All of it had rolled over me like weather.

But weather isn't the last word. The last word is what the coastline looks like afterward. And somebody had to write *that* down.

---

* * *

Seasons turn. That is the other thing the water teaches you, if you're fool enough to learn from it: nothing stays, including the bad things, including the good ones, and the trick is to hold on loosely and mean it completely.

The trial ended in October with sentences I won't rehearse here, partly because justice served cold in a Sydney courtroom tastes different from the version we ate standing up in the divers' camp, and partly because the men who mattered most—the ones sold south of the reef, the "deceased"—were beyond any sentence a magistrate could pass. But Meridian's charter was revoked. Its buyers were named, and some of those names reached higher than anyone expected, high enough that papers in three colonies printed them and at least one government minister discovered a pressing interest in his estate in England. The trafficking routes closed. Not all of them—let's not flatter ourselves; the sea is wide and greed is wider—but these routes, our routes, the ones that ran through our jetty and our ledgers and our drowned-and-not-drowned men. Closed.

Mr. Okoro's brother came home in November. Alive. Half-starved, half-deaf from a beating, missing two fingers and all of his patience, but alive, walking up the jetty road on legs that shook, while the whole camp came out and nobody said anything because there wasn't anything to say that the sight of him hadn't already said. His daughter didn't leave his side for three days. She's in my Tuesday class now. She writes her letters like she's carving them into stone.

Calloway, they tell me, got two years and a new name to look forward to upon release, courtesy of the newspapers. I have thought about him exactly often enough to make sure I don't think about him more than that. He taught me one true thing, in his courteous way: that curiosity is a debt. He just had the direction of payment backwards. Curiosity is a debt you owe yourself, and I have been paying it in full ever since, with interest.

---

The strongbox sits under the floorboard of the schoolhouse now, in the corner where my crate-row ends. My father's old box, salvaged from the wreck he faked his death beside, holding the secret he hid from the company and then, in his cowardice and his tenderness, from us. It holds the twin of the copied ledger now—the second copy, the one we split off so no single boat would carry the whole truth, the one that turned out never to be needed because the original did its work and my mother carried it safe into a courtroom. Redundant evidence. Worthless to any magistrate.

Worthless to everyone except me.

Because it's in my hand. Every page of it, copied line by line through one stolen night while Calloway dined above me, believing—as the whole world had believed, as I had believed—that the invisible girl was furniture. It's the first thing I ever wrote that changed anything. The first sentence I put into the world that the world had to answer for. I keep it the way some people keep a first tooth: not useful, but proof. Proof that I began somewhere.

Some nights I take it out and read it by lamplight, and I can feel the water again, the black cold closing over my head during the night dive, the line fouling, the moment when I understood that the sea had been trying to make me a footnote in my father's story and I declined. I surface in memory every time. I always surface.

---

It's February now, the wet season easing, and the schoolhouse has nineteen pupils and a waiting list, and last week Nellie Okoro read a whole paragraph aloud without stopping—about a lighthouse, of all things—and the class clapped and she went red as coral and I thought: *there*. That's the whole of it. That's what the water remembers, and what it forgets, and what it waits for someone to write down.

Because here is my finding, entered into whatever record this account becomes, offered in evidence: the admirals get their monuments and the company men get their obituaries, and both are written by other admirals and other company men, and none of it amounts to much against a seven-year-old reading her first paragraph in a room where men were once counted as cargo.

History happened to me. I have made my peace with that. What I did next was mine.

Yesterday a ship came in with mail, and among it a letter in my mother's upright, mast-straight hand, and enclosed with it a second sheet in the slanted, familiar script I have decided—entirely on my own terms—to stop calling unforgivable and start calling unfinished. My father writes that the lugger wintered at Thursday Island, that he is well, that he has begun keeping a logbook of the reefs and channels as the old divers mapped them, "so the knowledge doesn't drown with us," and that he would like, someday, if I judged it fitting, to bring it to the schoolhouse and teach the children the water the way his father taught him.

He signs it *your father*, which is a claim, not a title. I notice these things now. I notice everything now; it's the family affliction, curiosity, and I intend to pass it down like an inheritance.

I have not answered yet. The letter sits on my desk beside the strongbox key and the clipping, and tomorrow there will be lessons, and the tide will come in the way it always does—everywhere at once, without permission—and I will stand in front of nineteen children and open the book.

But tonight I took out a fresh page, and at the top of it, in a hand that is entirely my own, I wrote the heading that I have been waiting all my life to write:

*Chapter One.*
