## 2. A Stranger at the Gate

The rain came in off the Weald that October afternoon like a debt being collected — sudden, total, indifferent to what it ruined. I saw it first as a grey curtain walking across the hop fields toward Marlowe House, and I had time enough to fetch the motor round to the carriage court, crank her, and get the hood up before it struck. Then it struck, and the world went to water.

I have driven through worse. I drove through the whole of that wet spring of 1912 with a cracked windscreen and a leaking hood, carrying Mrs. Pankhurst's people from platform to platform while they shouted themselves hoarse about votes and I thought about axle grease. Rain is honest work for a chauffeur. It gives you something to fight.

I was under the cedar by the stable door, smoking the cigarette I rationed myself to on Edith Marlowe's petrol money, when I heard the gate.

Not the latch — the latch is oiled, I oil it myself, and it goes *click* like a well-bred thing. This was the sound of someone leaning on it. A small, tired weight against iron. Then footsteps on the gravel, uneven, and a shape resolving out of the rain: a young woman, hatless, coat plastered to her like bark, hair dark with wet and strung across her face. She carried a canvas satchel hugged to her chest with both arms, the way you carry something you've decided not to lose even if you lose everything else.

She stopped maybe ten feet from me and looked up, blinking rain out of her eyes.

"Is this Marlowe House?"

"It is."

"I want Miss Marlowe." Her voice had an accent I couldn't place at first — English underneath, but worn smooth by other tongues, vowels rounded off somewhere foreign and fitted back in slightly wrong. "Edith Marlowe."

"Miss Marlowe doesn't see callers without an appointment." I said it in my best footman's voice, which I keep folded away like spare gloves. "You'd want to write ahead."

"Write ahead." She said it as though tasting the words and finding them spoiled. Rain ran off her chin. "I've come from a long way further than the post office reaches."

I looked at her properly then. That was my mistake, or the beginning of every mistake after — looking at her properly. There was mud to mid-calf on her skirt and her boots were wrong for this country, city boots, and her hands were raw around the satchel strap, and none of that was remarkable. Plenty of desperate girls had turned up at Marlowe House since I'd been there; the suffrage offices collected them like gutters collect leaves. What was remarkable was her eyes. They were doing arithmetic. I could see it — she was counting me, the motor, the cedar, the lit windows of the house, weighing each one and filing it somewhere behind her face. People who are merely lost don't count things. People who are hunted do.

"You're the driver," she said.

"I'm the chauffeur, miss."

"Is there a difference?"

"About four shillings a week."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth. Not quite a smile. The ghost of one, come to the edge of the grave and turning back. "Then you're the man who drives her everywhere. Who hears everything."

"I hear the engine, mostly."

"And the letters," she said.

The rain filled the silence where my answer should have gone. I felt it go through me cold, colder than the weather, because that word — *letters* — sat closer to certain secrets of mine than any stranger could know. But of course she didn't know. She meant the correspondence, the circulars, the great flapping flocks of envelopes Edith Marlowe sent out weekly to every sympathizer between here and Manchester. Everyone knew Miss Marlowe lived by her letters. It was nothing. It was coincidence. I told myself it was coincidence with the particular firmness of a man telling himself a lie he half believes.

"Miss Marlowe sees no one tonight without—"

"*Birdie.*"

I stopped.

She said it quietly, almost gently, watching my face as she did — watching it the way you watch a lock you're trying. "Tell her Birdie's at the gate. Tell her Birdie kept the letter dry. She'll understand."

Now, I have been in service and in motors and in the margins of grand houses long enough to know every kind of name a caller can produce. Cousins, creditors, sweethearts, blackmailers. I have heard duchesses called by pet names by women I could smell the gin on. A childhood nickname is currency, but it can be forged like anything else. Except — except that Edith Marlowe had been three weeks in one of her black moods, the ones that followed the anniversaries, and I had carried her letters up to the house the previous evening and seen, franked on the front of one discarded envelope, an address in a hand I didn't know, and heard Mrs. Tice say over the supper dishes that Miss Edith hadn't touched her supper and had asked — asked, mind, after eleven years — whether anyone remembered where the nursery photograph albums had been put.

Birdie. Eleven years I'd worked for the household, and I had never once heard the name.

"I'll see if she's at home," I said, and crushed out the cigarette, and left her standing in the rain because something in me — some old servant's instinct, or some newer instinct I couldn't name yet — did not want to turn my back on her longer than necessary.

* * *

Mrs. Tice answered the service bell in the hall with her sleeves rolled and flour to the wrists, and gave me the look she reserves for reports of weather, politics, and other acts of God.

"There's a girl at the gate," I said. "Wet through. Asking for Miss Marlowe."

"Then she can ask from the village. It's Thursday, Ivo, the sewing circle comes at six, and Miss Marlowe's letters are half done."

"She called her Birdie."

The flour went still. Mrs. Tice had kept the Marlowe kitchens since before I was born, and she has a face built for showing nothing, but the nothing rearranged itself just slightly, the way a room changes when a window opens somewhere far off. "Where'd she hear that?" she said, half to herself. Then, to me: "Nobody's called her that since—" She stopped, because we both knew how the sentence ended: *since Mr. Julian went away.* Since before the war, even. Since the photograph albums mattered.

"She's soaked to the skin and she's got a bag she won't put down," I said. "And she's counting the windows, Mrs. Tice."

"Counting the—"

"Like she's pricing them up. Like she wants to know how many ways out of the house there are."

Mrs. Tice wiped her hands slowly on her apron, though they were clean. In that kitchen, that gesture meant a decision was being made. "Take her to the morning room," she said. "Light the fire first. Don't let her near the study, don't let her touch nothing, and send up to Miss Marlowe that—" she paused, and something old and unwilling crossed her face, "—that Birdie's here."

* * *

The girl stood dripping in the morning room while I knelt at the grate, and she watched me lay the fire with the same arithmetic in her eyes. Up close, out of the rain, she resolved into particulars: perhaps nineteen, perhaps twenty-two; a thin white scar along her jaw like a stitch; a brooch pinned at her collar that wasn't English — some enamel bird on a branch, the colours gone soft with handling, the pin replaced with wire. Her satchel sat in her lap with both hands on it still. Water pooled around her boots and she either didn't notice or didn't care, and I found I minded on her behalf, which was unusual. I generally mind about puddles only insofar as they affect the tyres.

"Name?" I said, striking the match.

"Ada."

"Just Ada?"

"For now." She said it without coyness. She said it the way a soldier says his number.

"And where's home, Ada?"

"That depends," she said, "on whose army you ask."

I set down the matchbox. Outside, the rain went on working at the gravel, patient as a clerk. "That's an odd answer."

"It's the only true one I've got." She looked at me directly then, and the arithmetic dropped away for a moment, and what was underneath was so tired and so young that I had to look back at the fire. "Are you going to make me stand in front of a policeman before anyone listens? Because I'll do it. I've practised. But I'd rather not, and I think once she hears the name she'll rather I didn't too."

"Miss Marlowe will decide that herself."

"Good," said Ada. "Deciders. I've had a year of people who won't decide anything." And then, quieter, almost to the fire: "He said she would decide. He said if ever I reached England and reached Kent and reached this gate, the person inside would be hard as nails and twice as sharp, but she would decide, and having decided she'd never go back on it. He said that was the finest thing about her."

My spine prickled under my livery. "*He* said."

"He says a great deal," said Ada. "Mostly in writing. Mostly in handwriting nobody else alive can read properly." She shifted the satchel in her lap, and beneath the wet canvas I saw the corner of something stiff and pale — paper, thick paper, a document or an envelope of substance, worn soft at one corner as though it had been handled a thousand times on a very long road.

I should have left it there. I want to be clear about that, because everything that came after — the summons, the hearing, the whole terrible machinery of that winter — began its turning in this room, in the space of about ninety seconds, and I have gone over those seconds more times than I've gone over any engine. I should have laid the fire, taken the message upstairs, and stood outside the door like a proper servant until the grown-ups sorted it.

Instead I said: "Whose handwriting?"

Ada looked at me for a long moment. Rain ticked against the window. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard settled.

"Why do you care?" she said. Not hostile. Genuinely asking, the way she'd asked about the difference between driver and chauffeur — a person taking inventory of the world because the world had proven unreliable.

"Because I carry Miss Marlowe's letters," I said. "Every post, both ways. If there's a letter in this house that matters, it'll pass through my hands sooner or later. It always does."

Something changed in her face at that. Not trust — I wouldn't earn trust for months yet, and didn't deserve it when it came. Recognition, maybe. One reader of faces acknowledging another. Or one carrier of secrets recognizing the trade marks on a stranger.

"Then sooner," she said, and drew the satchel open, and took out the letter.

It was a plain envelope, army issue, the sort I'd seen ten thousand of, the sort that came and went through my hands all through 1914 and 15 and stopped coming the way such envelopes stop. It had been opened carefully and resealed, and resealed again, and again — layers of paste and patience along the flap, the paper gone grey with handling. Across the front, in faded pencil, someone had written an address: *Miss E. Marlowe, Marlowe House, Kent.* No stamp. No postmark. Just that, and below it in smaller letters, in a different hand — Ada's, I understood later — the words *to be delivered only into her own hands.*

"Who wrote it?" I asked. My voice came out lower than I intended.

"You'll know soon enough," said Ada. "Everyone who reads it knows soon enough. That's the trouble with it." She held it a moment longer, both hands, and I saw her thumb move over the worn corner the way you'd touch a scar, or a headstone. Then footsteps sounded in the corridor — quick, decisive, Edith Marlowe's walk, the walk that had cleared rooms in London and raised money in Manchester — and Ada closed her hands around the letter and stood up, dripping, chin lifted, like a girl presenting herself for judgement.

The door opened.

I had seen Edith Marlowe receive ambassadors' wives and hecklers and once a brick thrown through the drawing-room window, and her face had never moved more than a degree. But I was behind her, and I saw what the visitor couldn't: the moment her eyes found the girl, and above the girl the letter clutched to her chest, and above all of it — before any of it, really — the sound of the name still hanging in the house.

"Birdie," Ada said.

Edith Marlowe put one hand flat against the doorframe. Not for support. For anchor, I think, the way a sailor touches wood. She stood very straight and very still, forty-one years old and immovable in every crisis I'd witnessed, and her voice, when it came, was quiet enough that I had to strain for it over the rain.

"Child," she said, "where did you get that letter? And don't tell me who wrote it. I can see who wrote it. I knew that hand before I knew my own name." Her hand pressed whiter against the frame. "What I am asking you — what I need you to tell me, before God and before anything else walks through my door — is *when*."

Ada's fingers tightened on the envelope. The fire I'd laid crackled once in the grate, absurdly domestic, a small warm animal in a cold room.

"Six weeks ago," Ada said. "He wrote it six weeks ago. And he's written others since."

I have read a thousand letters I shouldn't have. I know the weight of paper, the way a sealed thing sits differently in the hand than an open one, the particular gravity of words that change the weather of a life. Standing in that doorway, watching two women hold a sentence between them like a live wire, I felt the first true vertigo of my comfortable, detached, well-ordered life — because I understood, in the marrow-deep way one understands a snapped axle, that whatever was in that envelope, it had already happened to me.

Edith Marlowe stepped into the room and shut the door behind her.
