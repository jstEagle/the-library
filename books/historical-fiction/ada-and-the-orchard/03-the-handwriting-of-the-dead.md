## 3. The Handwriting of the Dead

Edith Marlowe had learned to read grief the way other women read weather. It came in seasons. There was the first hard winter of it — the telegram in September 1915, the smell of the envelope's gum as though the War Office had licked its own wounds shut — and then the long grey thaw that never quite arrived, only thinned the ice enough that she could walk on it without cracking through. For two years she had kept Julian alive in the small domestic ways: his chair by the study window, his copy of Housman face-down on the hall table where he'd left it at Christmas 1914, the orchard he had planted behind the house and which now, obscenely, flourished without him. Grief was a housekeeper, she had found. It dusted everything daily.

She was in the morning room with the ledger open before her when Ivo brought the girl in.

Later — much later, in the years when the story could finally be told — Edith would try to explain what those first thirty seconds were like, and she would always fail. Words were her trade; she had stood before ten thousand people and made them feel a cause in their chests like a struck bell. But there were no words for watching your own dead walk into your morning room wearing a stranger's wet coat.

The girl was young — nineteen perhaps, twenty — and rain had gotten into everything about her: hair flattened dark against her skull, boots leaving small ruined maps on the floorboards, a canvas satchel sagging with water. She stood just inside the door as if the threshold were a border she had no papers for. And she looked at Edith — looked, and did not look away — with an expression Edith recognised from mirrors: the particular stubbornness of someone who has carried something too far to put down now.

"You knew him," the girl said. Not *do you know* — she had skipped past courtesy entirely. "You knew Julian Marlowe. When you were children. He called you—" She stopped, swallowed. "He called you Dido."

The room went very quiet. Rain ticked against the window glass. Somewhere below stairs, Mrs. Petch dropped something metal and swore with feeling.

Edith set down her pen. She did so carefully, aligning it with the ledger's edge, because her hands wanted to do something else entirely and she would not permit them. No one living called her Dido. The nickname had been Julian's invention, aged nine, delivered with the merciless precision of little brothers everywhere after she'd spent a summer reading the *Aeneid* aloud to him under the apple trees, weeping copiously over Dido while he ate unripe plums. It had been their private joke for thirty years. It had gone into the ground at Loos with him.

"Who told you that name?" Edith asked. Her voice sounded level. She was grateful to it, the way one is grateful to a servant who keeps working during a funeral.

"He did." The girl's chin came up. "Six weeks ago. In Rotterdam."

*Rotterdam.* The word landed strangely, foreign and flat, like a coin from another country dropped on English floorboards.

"I'm afraid," Edith said, "you have been deceived. My brother was killed at Loos in September of 1915. There is no ambiguity in the matter. I have the telegram. I have the effects."

She heard herself reciting the inventory of death — *the effects*, as if Julian could be reduced to a returned kit bag and a wristwatch stopped at some official hour — and hated the sound of it even as she made it. It was what one said. It was what she had said to a hundred well-meaning visitors for two years, the litany of the bereaved, and it had the great advantage of being true.

Except that the girl did not flinch. That was the thing. Bereaved people flinched when Edith performed this litany; they looked down at their hands, ashamed of having raised hope. This girl stood in her wet boots and met her eyes with something close to pity.

"He wrote you a letter," the girl said. "I promised I'd put it in your hand myself. Not post it. Hand it." She reached into the satchel, moving with the care of someone handling something that might go off, and drew out an oilcloth packet tied with string. "His handwriting," she added, unnecessarily, and held it out across the space between them. "I expect you'll know it."

And here is what Edith Marlowe would remember all her life: not taking the packet, but seeing it. Before her fingers touched it, before sense or sanity intervened, she saw the shape of the letters through the oilcloth — the long ascenders, the way Julian's capital J leaned backward like a man looking over his shoulder — and her heart, that treacherous organ, that stupid faithful muscle, leapt.

She took it. Her hands were steady until they weren't.

* * *

She did not open it in front of the girl. She would want privacy for whatever came next — hope or hoax, either deserved a closed door. She sent the girl to the kitchen with Mrs. Petch, to be fed and dried and interrogated gently, and she carried the packet upstairs alone, and she sat on the window seat in her bedroom with the rain coming down outside and the packet in her lap for a full five minutes before she touched the string.

Five minutes. She counted them by the church clock, which had been two minutes fast since 1903 and which Julian had once climbed the tower to correct, been caught, and defended himself before the vicar with a lecture on the moral importance of accurate timekeeping. Two minutes fast. She counted five minutes and thought: *if I open this and it is nothing, I will have spent five minutes alive again.*

That was the truth of it, the shameful truth underneath: for two years she had been dead in exactly the proportion Julian was dead. A portion of her had gone into the French earth with him, and she had built her life on top of the grave the way one builds on any bad ground — carefully, knowing the foundations were unsound. She had made his death useful. She spoke of him at rallies: *my brother gave everything; surely we can give a little less than everything.* His photograph, taken at Aldershot in '14, squinting into sun, had become an argument. She had folded her brother into her cause like a flag, and flags are not meant to breathe.

And beneath the usefulness, the other thing, the animal thing: *let me be wrong. Let every telegram be wrong. Let the clerks have mislaid him, let the trenches have confused him with another man of the same name — there must be a thousand J. Marlows — let him be lying in some hospital with his memory gone, writing to strangers because he cannot write to me—*

She untied the string.

The letter inside was written on cheap paper, the kind sold in continental stationers', faintly ruled, and the handwriting hit her like cold water to the face.

People imagine you recognise a beloved hand the way you recognise a face. It is not like that. It is faster and less merciful. Edith saw the backward-leaning capitals, the peculiar flat-topped *t* that Julian had never corrected despite a childhood of governesses' despair, the habit of crossing his *i*'s with a dash rather than a dot as if dots wasted ink — and she was seventeen again, and he was twelve, and he was passing her notes across the schoolroom table asking insolent questions about whether Helen of Troy had really been worth it, and she was laughing so hard she had to leave the room.

Then she read the words, and she was forty-one, and her hands shook.

*Dido,* the letter began. *If you are reading this, then Ada got through, and I owe her my life twice over, and you will want to know everything at once, and I am going to give it to you in the wrong order because that is how it comes to me.*

*I am alive. I know what the paper says. The paper is wrong, and the wrongness was mine, arranged, deliberate, and I will explain why, but not in this letter, because this letter may be read by people whose reading would cost lives. Burn this after Ada has told you the essential part. You will know the essential part because I will have told her to tell you about the Brandts, and when you hear that name you will understand everything, because you were there in the garden in 1911 when I made the promise, and you laughed at me for being sentimental, and you were right to laugh and I was right to make it anyway.*

*I am sorry. That word is too small. I am sorry in every direction — toward you, toward Mother's memory, toward the men who went over at Loos and did not come back while I lay in a ditch listening to them die and understood that I could not stand up and go with them, not because of wounds, Dido, but because of what I knew. Ask Ada. Or don't ask anyone. Come to no conclusions yet. Trust the girl. She has more courage in her little finger than I have in my whole disgraced body.*

*If you cannot forgive me, I will understand it, and I will still love you.*

*J.*

Edith read it four times. On the fourth reading she became aware that she was making a sound, a low continuous note, and that her cheeks were wet, and that the window seat's cushion had a worn patch shaped exactly like Julian's shoulder from the summers he'd sat there reading, and she pressed her mouth against the worn patch and wept like a child, loudly, gracelessly, with the door unlocked and the whole house free to hear.

* * *

An hour later she came downstairs with her eyes swollen and her spine straight, and found the girl — Ada, the letter had named her, Ada — sitting at the kitchen table with a bowl of Mrs. Petch's barley soup untouched before her and Mrs. Petch herself planted opposite like a siege engine, radiating the silent accusation that food refused was food insulted.

"She says Mr. Julian's alive," Mrs. Petch announced, before Edith could speak, in the tone of a woman reporting a claim that reflected poorly on the entire household. "Alive and abroad and writing letters. Six weeks ago, she says." Mrs. Petch had cooked for the Marlowes since before Edith could reach the stove handle; her scepticism was a family institution. "I've known that boy since he stole a whole ham off my pantry shelf aged eleven. If he were alive he'd have stolen back by now, letter or no letter."

"Petch," Edith said quietly, "would you give us the room."

Mrs. Petch gave the girl one last look — assessing, not unkind — and withdrew with dignity, pausing only to turn the soup bowl a quarter-inch toward Ada, a small act of diplomacy.

Edith sat down opposite her. Up close, in dry light, she could see the girl properly: fine-boned, travel-worn, with the watchfulness of someone who had crossed borders recently enough to still count exits. There was a faded scar through one eyebrow. Her hands, wrapped around the soup spoon without using it, were chapped red at the knuckles — work hands, or weather hands, or both.

"The Brandts," Edith said.

Ada's whole body changed. It was subtle — a settling, a loosening around the shoulders, the look of a wire relieved of tension — but Edith saw it, and understood what it meant: *the password worked; I am believed; I am, for the moment, safe.*

"You know them," Ada said.

"I know *of* them." Edith folded her hands on the table, an old debating habit. "Emil Brandt. He was — is — a merchant of Hamburg, a friend of Julian's from before the war, from the year Julian studied in Germany. He visited here. 1911." The memory surfaced with strange clarity: a tall man with a laughing wife and a solemn small son, standing in the orchard while the apples came in, speaking careful beautiful English, saying *your country and mine are too sensible for this nonsense* — and everyone at the table believing him. Everyone wanting so badly to believe him. "Julian made a promise concerning them. I laughed at him for it."

"He told me you'd say that." Something moved at the corner of Ada's mouth — not quite a smile, but the ghost of one, exhausted. "He said, *my sister will laugh, and then she will do it.*"

"Do what?"

"Honour it." Ada looked down at the soup at last, as though she needed somewhere to rest her eyes that wasn't Edith's face. "There's a family. Emil Brandt's family. There's a boy — Karl, he'd be fourteen now — and there's a trouble coming for them because of things Emil did before the war, money matters, helping people, the wrong sort of people according to his own government now. And Julian—" Her voice caught, steadied. "Julian promised in 1911 that if war ever came between the countries, he'd get them out. Get them to England, or Holland, anywhere. He made me memorise names and places instead of carrying papers, because papers can be found and a girl's memory can't. I've walked half of Europe carrying names in my head like stones."

The kitchen clock ticked. Rain still fell. Somewhere in Edith's chest, two enormous and incompatible facts were grinding against each other like millstones: *Julian is alive* and *Julian is a deserter*. She could feel the space between them widening, the terrible arithmetic assembling itself — because a man officially killed at Loos who was instead alive in Holland was not a miracle. He was a court-martial. He was a firing squad postponed. Everything that made her want to run to him made running to him the most dangerous act imaginable.

"You said he wrote six weeks ago," Edith said. "In Rotterdam."

"Yes."

"And you've come from Rotterdam to Kent, alone, through a war, to deliver a letter."

"And to ask for help." Ada lifted her chin again — that same stubborn angle — but her eyes were bright and frightened now, the fear showing through the bravery like light through a cracked shutter. "Because the promise isn't only his anymore. The boy, Karl — there's a deportation order coming. To a reprisal camp. And Julian can't cross the water without being shot, and I can't move a family out of Germany alone, and you—" She stopped. Started again, quieter, the hardest sentence of all: "He said you were the most dangerous woman in England and the only one who could help. He said you'd hate what he did and help him anyway. Was he right?"

Edith rose from the table. She walked to the window, where the rain was easing off the orchard, where the trees Julian had planted stood dripping and patient, budding toward a spring he was not supposed to see.

Was he right? She thought of the letter burning a hole in her bodice pocket. She thought of Captain Reeve's card, received a fortnight ago, a polite enquiry about postal irregularities in the district. She thought of the movement — twenty years of work, the hunger strikes, the women broken on the Home Secretary's iron — and what it would mean if it became known that Edith Marlowe's brother was a deserter sheltering under a false name, if the newspapers got hold of it, if Asquith's people used it to paint every suffragist as a traitor's kin.

And she thought of a boy of fourteen, born in England's ally's country, guilty of nothing but his father's name, waiting for a train to a camp.

"He was right," Edith said, turning back. "He usually was, damn him. Which means we have a great deal of work to do, and we begin by understanding exactly what you're carrying besides that letter. Names. Places. Everything you memorised. And then—" She hesitated, and the hesitation itself told her the answer before she spoke it. "And then I must speak to my driver. Ivo. Because if there are letters moving through this house that I haven't seen, then someone has been using my mail, and I need to know how long."

Ada blinked. "Your mail?"

"Circulars," Edith said slowly, the thought assembling itself as she voiced it, cold walking up her arms. "Suffrage circulars. We print them in London and post them all over the county." She thought of Julian's flat-topped *t*'s, of codes, of a brother who had always been three moves ahead of everyone including her. "Ada. When Julian wrote to you. Did he ever mention... circulars?"

Outside, the rain stopped altogether. In the sudden hush, from the yard below, came the sound of the motor car returning — Ivo, early, tyres crunching the gravel — and Ada looked toward the window, then back at Edith, and nodded once.

"He mentioned them every time," she said.
