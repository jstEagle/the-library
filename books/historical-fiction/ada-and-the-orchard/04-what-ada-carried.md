## 4. What Ada Carried

The fire had been built up since she arrived, and still Ada could not stop shivering. It was not the cold. The cold had soaked into her somewhere on the road between Ashford and this house, and it had been wrung out of her hours ago by the kitchen stove and Mrs. Pyle's rough towel and the tea that had been pressed into her hands like a sacrament. What remained was something else — a trembling that lived under the skin, in the place where a person keeps the things they have carried too far and too long.

They had put her in the morning room, which was not a morning room at all but a study that had surrendered to maps and pamphlets and stacks of *The Suffragist* tied in brown string. A desk by the window. A globe with a dent in it, Africa dented most of all, as though some previous owner had gripped the world there to keep from striking someone. Rain ran down the glass in ropes.

Edith Marlowe sat opposite her in a chair that might have been older than both of them together, her hands folded, her face doing what Ada had already learned it did: nothing at all. Behind her, near the door, the driver stood with his cap in his hands as though he had forgotten he was holding it. Ivo. He had a way of standing slightly sideways to a room, as if perpetually about to leave it, and his eyes went everywhere — to her face, to her satchel, to the letter lying on the desk between them like a small dead bird.

The letter. Julian's letter. Six weeks old and two years impossible, written in a hand Edith Marlowe had known before she had known her own name.

"You said," Edith began, and stopped, and began again, and Ada understood that this woman who could hold three hundred strangers silent in a market square did not know how to begin a sentence that might break her heart twice. "You said you have more than one."

Ada's satchel was on her knees. Her fingers found the buckle without being asked; they had been finding it all day, all week, all the long grey miles of the crossing and the train and the cart ride through the wet orchards. Under her touch the leather was warm now, warmed by her own body, which meant that for six weeks her secret had been sleeping against her ribs like a second heartbeat.

"Yes," she said. "I have more than one."

"Then you'd better tell me who you are," Edith said, "and how my brother came to trust a stranger with his life folded into an envelope."

"I'm not a stranger." The words came out smaller than Ada wanted them to. She cleared her throat and tried again, louder, the way one raises a voice against wind. "Not to him. My mother kept lodgings — in Antwerp, before the war. Rue de la Violette, behind the diamond quarter. Herr Professor Brandt sent us tenants when the university rooms were full. Englishmen mostly, and Dutchmen, and once a Norwegian who played the violin badly at all hours. And in the spring of 1912—" she made herself count it, made herself be exact, because exactness was the only currency she had to spend here, "—in April of 1912, an Englishman came with a trunk that had been rained on and a look like a man who has been told bad news in a language he only half understands."

"And that was Julian."

"That was Mr. Carrow," Ada said. "That is what he called himself. Mr. Edmund Carrow. He told the registry he was a surveyor for an English railway concern. He paid three months in advance and he never once, in two years, let anyone see him write at the window."

She heard herself say it and felt the old fear rise in her throat again, the fear that had ridden with her from Antwerp to Ostend to Dover like a third passenger: *they will not believe you. They will think you invented him, or stole him, or were sent.* She had rehearsed this. On the boat she had rehearsed it against the rail while the gulls screamed their opinions into the spray. But rehearsal, she was discovering, is worth exactly nothing when the person you must convince is looking at you with your brother's grey eyes.

Because they were grey. That was the first thing. When Ada had walked up through the rain to Marlowe House and seen Edith Marlowe on the step, tall and upright with rain streaming off her hat brim, the first thing Ada had thought — absurdly, horribly — was *he never told me his sister would be so like him.* Not the features, not quite. Something in the set of the mouth. Something in the way of looking at a person as though measuring them for a truth.

"Go on," Edith said quietly.

"My mother died in the winter of '12. Fever. There was just me then, keeping the lodgings, doing the books, learning to make coffee strong enough to frighten students. Mr. Carrow stayed on. He was kind to me in the way that costs a man nothing — carrying coal, fixing the loose stair, teaching me the English words for things I only knew in Flemish and German and French. He called me *meisje*, girl, but softly, the way you'd call a cat. He ate whatever I put in front of him and always asked what was in it, and when I told him he would say, *again? You are certain?* and laugh at himself for asking."

The driver shifted by the door. Ada glanced at him — Ivo — and saw that he was listening the way people listen when they have decided not to be interested and are failing at it completely.

"The others in the house thought he was dull. A dull man, punctual, home by nine, no visitors. But he wasn't dull. He was *careful*. There is a difference, and I learned to see it because I grew up in a lodging house where careful men pay better and talk less. He walked the same route every day and varied it every third day. He knew which of the neighbors watched the street. Once — this was 1913 — a man came asking after him at the door, saying he was from the railway office in Brussels, and Mr. Carrow was upstairs, and I said he was out, and he heard me say it through the floor, and that evening he taught me a word I didn't know. He wrote it on the back of my arithmetic slate. *Verifiëren.* To verify. He said, 'If anyone ever asks for me again, meisje, verify them. Ask them something only the office would know, and ask it slowly, and watch their hands.'"

Edith's hands, folded in her lap, had gone very still.

"He was hiding," Ada said. "Even then. Before the war. I didn't know from what. I thought perhaps debts. Perhaps a woman. It seemed romantic to me — I was sixteen, everything seemed either romantic or tedious. Then the summer of 1914 came, and romance packed its trunk and left."

Outside, the rain thickened against the window, and somewhere in the house a clock counted four strokes with the reluctance of an employee nearing the end of a shift.

"When the war came," Ada continued, "the Englishmen left, all except him. He couldn't leave. I understand now that he couldn't — that going home was the one direction closed to him. So he stayed, and Antwerp filled with soldiers, and then Antwerp fell, and we were inside it when it fell, and I will tell you about that another time or never, whichever you prefer. Afterward the city was grey and hungry and full of Germans, and Mr. Carrow — Julian — became a different sort of careful. And in November of 1915, after Loos—" she saw Edith flinch, a single involuntary movement like a horse feeling the whip before it falls, "—after the news came that Lieutenant J. M. Marlowe had been killed, he locked himself in his room for two days. When he came out he had shaved off his beard and he looked ten years older and he said to me, 'Well, meisje. Now I am properly dead. We shall have to be very good at it.'"

Silence. The fire ticked. In the corner of the room the dented globe held its crooked Africa.

"Why?" It was the driver who spoke — Ivo — and then seemed startled by his own voice. "Begging pardon. But why fake it? Dead men can't draw pay, can't come home, can't—" He stopped.

"Can't be shot twice," Ada finished, and watched the color leave his face. Yes. She had worked it out herself, slowly, over months, assembling it the way she assembled the lodging-house accounts. "You've thought of it too, then, whoever you are. Desertion. Or worse. He wouldn't say it outright. He never said anything outright; he spoke in weather. But I know what I know. He received letters from England that he read standing up, as though sitting down with them would be surrender. And once — only once — he said a name in his sleep. A court name. A place. I don't remember it whole and I won't guess at it, because guessing is how people get hanged."

"We don't hang them anymore," Edith said, very low. "Not for that. They shoot them instead, at dawn, and call it mercy compared to the French."

Ada looked at her. This, then, was the grief under the marble — not just a brother lost, but a brother lost to a machine whose gears she knew by sound.

"I'm telling you all of it," Ada said, "because you deserve all of it, and because if I soften it you'll catch me softening it, and then you won't believe the rest. So. The rest."

She unbuckled the satchel.

Inside, wrapped first in oilcloth and then in a petticoat and then in the pages of a Dutch newspaper dated the eleventh of September, were the letters. She laid them on the desk one by one, squaring their edges, because her hands needed work or they would shake visibly. Four packets, tied in butcher's twine. One envelope separate, sealed, marked with a single drawn symbol — a circle with a line beneath it, like an eye over a horizon.

"Fourteen letters," she said. "Written between March of this year and the sixth of October. He gave me the last ones on the ninth. Three days later I crossed."

"October the sixth," Edith whispered. Her hand had moved, without her seeming to know it, until her fingertips rested on the topmost packet, not opening it, only touching, the way one touches a sleeping child to confirm the breath. "He has been alive for nineteen months since we buried him."

"Nineteen months and eleven days," Ada said, because she had counted, because counting was the only prayer she trusted.

"And why you?" Edith lifted her eyes, and they were wet, and she let them be wet without apology. "Why give them to a landlady's daughter? Why not post them? Why not find some man, some courier, some—"

"Because of the promise," Ada said.

The word landed in the room and lay there.

"He talked about it sometimes, especially after the war began, especially when the internments started. There was a family he knew — German — friends of his from before, from university days, I think, or from some summer long ago he described once and never again. The Brandts. A professor, his wife, and a son of about my age. They had lived half their lives in England — the son was born there, he had an English accent, he supported an English football club whose name Julian refused to say without groaning. And when the war came, England turned on them like a dog that has forgotten its own household. The professor lost his position. The son was interned. The wife stood in queues and was spat at." Ada's voice hardened without her permission. "And Julian said — I am giving you his words exactly, madam, because I wrote them down that night so I would never soften them either — he said: 'I promised Herr Brandt, in 1911, in a garden, over terrible wine, that if it ever came to war I would get them out. Everyone laughed. It was a joke about the impossibility of Europe. I put my hand in his and swore it anyway, and God forgive me, I meant it.' "

The fire had burned low. Nobody moved to feed it.

"He cannot do it himself," Ada went on. "A dead man walks nowhere, asks nothing, signs nothing. Every favor he calls in is a favor spent from a grave, and every grave-spent favor draws questions — *who is paying this dead lieutenant's debts, who speaks with his voice?* So he needs hands. Hands in England. Hands that can go to lawyers and shipping offices and Members of Parliament and be believed." She spread her own hands, chapped, ink-stained at the nails, ordinary. "I am nobody. I have no country they recognize — my father was German, my mother Flemish, I was born in a city that belongs to whoever is winning. If I stand in an office and say *help these people*, they will arrest me before lunch. But you—" and here she turned fully to Edith, and the trembling in her finally stilled, because this part she was sure of, this part she had crossed a sea to say, "you are Miss Edith Marlowe. You have a voice that makes aldermen sit down. You have friends in newspapers and worse places. He said — his words again — 'My sister can move mountains with a petition and a Sunday afternoon. Tell her I'm sorry to be asking this way. Tell her the garden, the wine, the hand. She'll know.'"

Edith Marlowe did not move for a long moment. When she did move, it was to take the sealed envelope — the one with the circle and the line — and turn it over in her fingers, reading the outside of it as though the paper itself might confess.

"And your price," Edith said at last. "Everyone has one. What is yours?"

It was the question Ada had dreaded more than any interrogation, more than Captain Reeve — though she did not yet know that name — more than the sea. Because her price was shameful in its smallness next to the Brandts and their deported son and the machinery of two empires. Because her price was a person, and persons are not supposed to be cargo.

"My father," she said.

She made herself go on steadily.

"Herr Karl Weiss, though he went by Charles Wise in the English papers for twenty years, though he played cricket for a club in Croydon and sang in an Anglican choir and paid his rates on time. Interned in May of 1915 at Alexandra Palace, and then moved — we lost track — moved somewhere north, Knockaloe maybe, on the Isle of Man, maybe. He wrote us eleven letters and then the letters stopped. That was fourteen months ago." Her voice cracked on the number and she hated it and pushed through. "Julian promised me too. He said, 'When I send you to my sister, meisje, you tell her: the Brandts first, because I swore it in blood and wine, but the daughter of Frau Greet is owed the same debt, and if Edith lets a girl cross the North Sea alone in wartime and turns her away at the gate, I shall haunt her personally and be insufferable about it.' Those were his words. Nearly."

For the first time since the gate, something that was almost a smile touched Edith Marlowe's mouth, and vanished again like a fish surfacing.

But it was the driver Ada caught watching her — Ivo, cap still in his hands, standing sideways to the room — and it was his expression that frightened her afterward, remembering it. Not pity. Recognition. As though he had looked at her satchel full of other people's words and seen his own reflection in the leather.

Edith rose. She went to the window, where the rain was easing into that thin silver drizzle Kent does so well, and she stood with her back to them for the length of ten heartbeats, and Ada, who had grown expert in the silences of careful men, understood that the woman was deciding whether to hope. Hope, for someone who had buried a brother and grieved him thoroughly, publicly, with flowers and a memorial card still tucked in her writing desk — hope was not comfort. Hope was a wound reopened on purpose.

Then Edith Marlowe turned around, and she had put the marble back on, all except her eyes.

"Very well, Ada — what is your surname?"

"Groot, ma'am. Ada Groot. Though on my travel papers I'm Ada Vermeulen, and on the manifest I was Verhulst, and the man at Dover thought I said Verlinden, so I let him." 

"Miss Groot. You will stay here tonight, and tomorrow we will open these letters together, all fourteen, in order, and you will tell me everything he said and everything you saw, and I will believe you." She paused. "That is not the same as saying I will do what he asks. Believing and obeying are different animals, and I have spent forty years learning to tell them apart. But I will believe you. That much I can promise a dead man tonight."

"Thank you," Ada said, and found, to her horror, that she was crying — not elegantly, not one bright tear, but the ugly full-bodied crying of someone who has been afraid for six weeks and has only now been given permission to stop. She pressed her sleeve against her face. "I'm sorry. I told myself I wouldn't. I told myself on the boat, I told myself on the train—"

"Miss Groot." Edith's voice came from directly beside her; she had crossed the room without Ada hearing. A hand, cool and dry, settled on the crown of Ada's head like a blessing or a weight. "You carried my brother across a war zone in a petticoat. You may cry in this house whenever you please. Consider it rent."

And Ada laughed, wetly, helplessly, and the trembling finally went out of her.

It was then that the driver stepped forward — Ivo, the quiet one, the one with the sideways stance and the eyes that missed nothing — and cleared his throat, and said the sentence that changed the shape of the evening, and the year, and Ada's life, though none of them knew it yet:

"Begging pardon, miss. But there's something I ought to tell you both. About a letter I delivered back in August." His ears had gone red. "One I probably shouldn't have read. And did."

* * *

Rain again on the glass. The clock gathering itself for five. Ada looked from the driver's burning ears to the packets on the desk — fourteen letters, one promise, two debts — and felt the room tilt toward something none of them could yet name.

"August," Edith said slowly. Her hand had returned to the sealed envelope with its eye above the horizon. "What month did you say Julian gave you the first of these?"

"March," Ada said. "Why?"

Edith did not answer. She was looking at the driver the way one looks at a lock to which one has suddenly, impossibly, remembered the key.
