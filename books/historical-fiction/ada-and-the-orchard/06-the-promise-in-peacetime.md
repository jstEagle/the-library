## 6. The Promise in Peacetime

The study at Marlowe House smelled of beeswax and cold ash, and Edith Marlowe sat with her hands flat on the desk as though the wood might steady her, or she it. Rain still worked at the windows in long silver threads. Somewhere below, Ivo was putting the motor away for the night, and she could hear the small domestic percussion of him — the bonnet dropped, the chock set against a wheel — sounds so ordinary they seemed almost an insult to what lay on the blotting paper before her.

The letter. Julian's letter.

She had not opened it again since Ada left her to read it alone. She did not need to. The words were printed on the inside of her eyelids now, arriving unbidden whenever she closed her eyes: *Edie — you'll know the hand if not the name. Keep your nerve. The promise holds. Ask her about the Brandts.*

She turned her wrist and looked at her watch — a habit from committee meetings, where five minutes could be the difference between a resolution passed and a platform lost. Half past nine. Outside, the orchard was a black tangle against a bruised sky, the old trees their father had planted leaning together like conspirators.

*Ask her about the Brandts.*

She knew about the Brandts. She had known about the Brandts for six years, and until tonight the knowledge had been a pleasant thing, a small warm stone she kept in the pocket of her memory: proof that her brother, for all his teasing and his terrible puns and his maddening habit of underlining things twice, had a heart that worked exactly as she'd always believed it did.

Now the stone had turned to ice in her hand.

* * *

It had been August of 1911. She remembered the month because the plums had come in early and heavy that year, bending the boughs in the walled garden until the gardener grumbled about props and wasps, and because it was the last summer before everything — before the movement swallowed her whole, before the marches and the hunger strike and the newspaper men shouting her name through megaphones as though it were an accusation.

Julian had come home on leave from some exercise or other, looking brown and absurdly pleased with himself, and he had brought a guest.

"Herr Brandt," he announced, in the hall, dripping rain, "professor of music, amateur botanist, and the only man in the Rhineland who can fix a bicycle with a bootlace." And then, dropping his voice as the tall, grey-templed man came in behind him shaking out an umbrella: "And my friend, Edie. Mind how you go. He's smarter than both of us put together."

Karl Brandt had stayed four days. Edith remembered those days now with a clarity that felt less like memory than like evidence being entered into a record. She remembered him in the orchard, walking between the rows with Julian, touching the bark of the trees with two fingers as though reading them. She remembered him at the piano after dinner, playing Schumann while rain came down the panes, and her mother's face going soft and startled, the way faces did when beauty ambushed them. She remembered him laughing — a dry, surprised laugh, as though laughter were something that happened to him rather than something he chose.

And she remembered the evening on the terrace, the last night, when the light went amber over the hop fields and Julian sent her ahead to fetch more candles and she came back too soon and heard what she was not meant to hear.

"—if it comes, Karl, and we all know what *it* is—"

"It will not come. Men of sense—"

"Men of sense said that in 'seventy, and men of sense were wrong." A pause. The clink of glass against the stone balustrade. "Then I have your word."

"You have a promise from a soldier," Brandt said, and there was something rueful in it, something tired. "Soldiers' promises are worth little. Wars make liars of us all."

"Then let this one hold anyway." Julian's voice changed — she knew that change; he used it when he was being flippant about serious things because seriousness embarrassed him. "If it comes — war, I mean — you send word, any word, and I'll get them out. Anna and the boy and your sister's lot in Cologne if it comes to that. Papers, passage, whatever's needed. I've friends who owe me favours in ports I shan't name at a family dinner. You'd be surprised what a bored staff officer can arrange when he stops saluting and starts thinking."

"Julian—" Brandt's voice had gone quiet in a way that made Edith stand very still among the candle smoke. "You are offering to smuggle the family of a German officer out of Germany, across a frontier neither of us can yet imagine, using methods you will not name. Do you understand what that is?"

"It's a promise between friends," Julian said. "Which is the only kind worth keeping, because it's the kind nobody makes you keep."

There was a long silence then, long enough that Edith had time to feel ashamed of listening and no time at all to leave. When Brandt spoke again, his accent had thickened, the way it did when he stopped translating in his head.

"My son is seven years old. If you do this thing — if ever it is needed — then whatever happens, whatever flags we fight under, my house is yours. My children are your cousins. This I say before God, who does not care for uniforms."

Edith had slipped back down the corridor with her candles, heart thudding, and told herself she'd imagined the weight of it. Brothers made boasts in the summer dusk. It was what brothers were *for*.

Two years later the world obliged them, and the boast became a covenant, and by Christmas of 1914 Julian was dead at Loos — or the telegram said so, and the colonel's letter said so, and the small wooden cross behind the line said so, and grief had sealed all of it up like a tomb.

Until a rain-soaked girl stood in her hall tonight holding paper in a dead man's hand.

* * *

Edith rose and went to the window. Her reflection ghosted against the dark glass, superimposed on the orchard, so that she seemed to stand among the trees herself, a woman planted and rooted and — she thought, with a bitterness that surprised her — *trapped*.

Because here was the arithmetic no one else would ever be asked to do. Here was the sum she must work alone, at this desk, in this light, with no one to check her figures.

To honor Julian's promise would mean using everything she had spent eight years building. Not just money — money could be explained, money was the least of it — but the network. The women who carried circulars from village to village trusting her with their names and addresses. The printers who owed her silence. The safe houses, the sympathetic stationmasters, the whole quiet lattice of trust she had woven across Kent thread by patient thread for a cause that already called her, in the papers, *unwomanly*, *hysterical*, *a danger to her sex*. If she bent that lattice to carry a German family out of danger — if one thread snapped, if one woman were caught, if Captain Whoever-He-Was in Whitehall lifted one corner of it —

She thought of the headlines. She could see them without effort; she had a talent for imagining the worst sentence written about her. *SUFFRAGIST LEADER IN ALIEN SMUGGLING RING.* They would not distinguish. They never distinguished. Eight years of work, a thousand women's courage, the whole slow argument that women deserved a voice — and it would be reduced in a single column to a scandal, a spy story, proof of everything her enemies had always claimed. And worse than her own ruin, which she could bear — she had rehearsed bearing ruin the way other women rehearsed wedding days — worse would be the collateral. The girls. Mrs. Pankhurst's people already hunted; the local branches already watched. She would have handed the government a gift-wrapped justification for every raid they'd been itching to make.

And against all that, weighed in the other pan: a seven-year-old boy she had once seen only in a photograph Brandt carried — a solemn child in a sailor suit, holding a toy boat. Who would now be thirteen. Who faced, if Ada's frightened account held true, a camp behind wire in a country that had decided his blood was his crime.

*You'd be surprised what a bored staff officer can arrange when he stops saluting and starts thinking.*

Well. She had spent eight years arranging things, and no one had ever accused Edith Marlowe of being bored.

But duty was not the only voice in her, and she was honest enough — brutal enough, on nights like this — to hear the other one clearly. The small cold voice that said: *you are fifty-one years old, you have buried a brother and half your youth in this cause, you are tired, and here at last is a reason, a respectable reason, a reason the angels themselves would sign off on, to stop. To say the risk is too great. To grieve properly and withdraw gracefully and let someone younger carry the flag.*

Self-preservation wore such beautiful clothes these days. It called itself prudence. It called itself *responsibility to the movement*. It quoted strategy at her over breakfast.

She laughed aloud in the empty study — a short, ugly sound — because she recognized the trick. She had watched the government play it for years. Dress the cowardly thing in reasonable language and everyone nods along.

"No," she said to her reflection, or to the orchard behind it. "I don't think so."

* * *

A knock. Ivo's knock — always two raps, apologetic, like a man asking permission of the door itself.

"Come in, Mr. Underhill."

He came in carrying tea he hadn't been asked for, which was his way of speaking when words failed him, and set it on the desk edge with care, moving the letter an inch aside as though even the saucer should not presume upon it. He hovered. She studied him a moment over her spectacles. He looked, she thought, like a man standing at the edge of cold water.

"There's a train," he said, "at twenty past six tomorrow. From Ashford. If — that is — if we're going anywhere. Which I'm not saying we are."

"We're not going anywhere yet," Edith said. "First I need to know precisely what was promised, and to whom, and whether it can be honored without getting three hundred women arrested. Sit down, Mr. Underhill, and tell me everything our Miss — what is her surname?"

"She won't give one. Just Ada."

"Just Ada. How convenient. Tell me everything she told you while I was — composing myself."

He told her. He was better at it than she expected; the young man noticed everything and forgot nothing, which she suspected was the true engine beneath his dull-driver manner. The landlady abroad. The letters given into keeping. The promise Julian had spoken of — *my sister keeps her word, and so do I, and between us we are a bank that doesn't fail*. The German family. The fear in the girl's hands as she talked, wringing her cap in her lap like a washcloth.

When he finished, Edith was silent a long moment, looking at the fire she hadn't lit.

"Mr. Underhill. What I am about to say stays in this room, in this chair, and dies with me if needs be. Do you understand?"

"Yes, ma'am."

"In 1911 my brother made a promise to a German friend — that if war came, he would get the man's family out. Out of Germany, out of danger. By any means." She laced her fingers. "I overheard it. I have remembered it for six years the way one remembers a tune. And now a dead man appears to be calling in that promise, and I find myself having to decide whether to spend everything I have built — everything — honoring it."

Ivo shifted. "And will you?"

"That depends entirely on whether you ask clever questions or loyal ones."

He considered this with the gravity of a man defusing something. "Clever ones first, then. Suppose it's a trap? Suppose whoever wrote that letter knows the promise exists and is using it to draw you out?"

"Then they know Julian better than I hoped anyone in the War Office did," Edith said. "Though God knows they may. That is question one, and I shall be answering it for weeks." She held up a second finger. "Question two: can it be done quietly, through people whose discretion I'd stake my life on — because I shall be staking considerably more than mine. Question three—" her voice roughened despite her, "—question three is whether I believe my brother is alive, and that one I can't argue logically, Mr. Underhill, because logic says Loos, and logic says telegrams, and logic says graves. Logic has never once in my life accounted for Julian."

The fire caught at last — she'd lit it without noticing doing so — and its light moved on the walls, over the framed photographs, over Julian at seventeen in cricket whites, grinning like the answer to a joke no one else had heard yet.

"And the loyal questions?" Ivo asked quietly.

Edith looked at him. Really looked — at the driver who read her mail and thought she didn't know (she had known for two years; that was a conversation for another night, and she watched the color climb his neck as some flicker of it crossed her face), at this strange cast of characters the war had washed up onto her doorstep: a chauffeur with a thief's eyes, a foreign girl with a dead man's letter, and herself, a woman with everything to lose and a promise older than the war itself.

"The loyal question," she said, "is whether you're with us. Because if Julian lives — and I find that I do believe it, God help my common sense — then this is no longer correspondence. It becomes a campaign. And I do not run campaigns with spectators."

Ivo was quiet for a moment. Then he said, "I drive you where you tell me to go, ma'am. That's the job."

"That," said Edith Marlowe, "is the least convincing thing anyone has ever said in this room, and I once hosted a bishop."

Something cracked in the young man's face — not quite a smile; more like ice shifting on a pond. He picked up his cap.

"I'll check the motor," he said. "For the morning. Whichever way it goes."

At the door he paused, and did not turn around, and said, to the doorframe: "The Brandts. There was a photograph. In one of the circulars — last spring. A family group, fund-raising for refugees. I remember a boy in it. Sailor suit." He cleared his throat. "Funny the things one remembers."

He left. Edith sat quite still, the fire ticking, the rain returning in earnest against the glass, and looked at the letter until the handwriting swam.

*A boy in a sailor suit.* Thirteen now. Possibly already in a queue somewhere, tagged like luggage.

She drew a sheet of her good paper toward her, the heavy laid stock she reserved for letters that would matter, and uncapped her pen, and found her hand steady — steadier than it had any right to be. Duty versus self-preservation, and the contest had lasted barely an hour, and she was almost ashamed of how it ended.

Almost.

She began to write: *My dear Mrs. Fennick — forgive an old friend an unusual request...*

Outside, the wind got up among the apple trees, and somewhere in the black orchard a branch creaked like a door opening.
