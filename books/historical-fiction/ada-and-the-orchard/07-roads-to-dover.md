## 7. Roads to Dover

We left Marlowe House before the milk carts, in that grey hour when the lanes belong to no one but the rooks and the fog still lies in the hollows like spilled wool. Edith sat beside me on the box seat with a travelling rug over her knees and her chin set at the angle I had learned to read across three years of driving her: the angle that meant she had already argued herself past every objection and would not be argued back.

Ada rode behind us in the open body of the Daimler, wedged between a hamper of food Mrs. Tice had pressed on her and a leather valise that held, as far as I knew, nothing but letters — Julian's letter above all, wrapped now in oilcloth against the wet. She had not let it out of her hands since the night she arrived. When we stopped, she carried it. When she slept — and I gathered she did not sleep much — I suspected she slept with it under her cheek like a child with a doll.

"Left at the crossroads past Selling," she said, leaning forward between our shoulders. Her voice was still rough from crying, though she pretended otherwise, and there was something in the way she gave directions — quick, certain, half-apologetic — that told me she had given directions like these before, in another country, in another language, to men who could not afford to be lost.

"You know these roads?" I asked.

"I know the shape of them." A pause. "Julian drew them for me. Over and over, until I could draw them myself."

Edith said nothing, but her hands tightened on the rug's fringe, and I looked hard at the road ahead because some griefs are not meant to be witnessed from the front.

The mist was lifting off the hop gardens as we came down through the valley, the bines stripped and brown now, October's work done, the poles standing in their long ranks like a congregation of thin grey men. Somewhere a church bell was practising its eight o'clock. It struck me — not for the first time that week, but more sharply than before — that all this was going on regardless. The bells, the poles, the rooks arguing in the elms. The world did not know what we were doing, and would not have cared if it had, and there was a strange comfort in that and a strange insult too. For two years I had believed history happened elsewhere — in Flanders, in London, in the columns of newspapers written by men who had never smelled hop-straw — and now history was sitting on my box seat with a rug over her knees, and I could not pretend otherwise any longer.

* * *

We took the old pilgrims' way as far as Charing rather than the main Ashford road, on Ada's insistence.

"Lorries go by the main road," she said. "Military lorries. Julian said never share a road you can walk beside."

"And what else did Julian say?" Edith asked, and there was an edge in it — not anger, exactly. The edge of a woman who has spent two days being instructed in her brother's habits by a stranger who knew him better, lately, than she had.

Ada took it without flinching. "He said his sister would argue every instruction and then follow it perfectly. He said—" and here her voice did something complicated, caught between laughter and tears, "—he said you'd want to know *why* for each one, so I'm to tell you why even when he told me not to bother."

"That," said Edith, "sounds like Julian."

So we went by Charing, and walked the car a mile outside Westwell where the lane narrowed between high banks hung with traveller's joy gone to seed, and I understood the why of it well enough without being told: nowhere for a lorry to pass, nowhere for a man to hide, and anyone following would have to show themselves on foot like honest folk. Ada stood in the road with her collar up and the wind pulling at her hair and watched both ends of the lane with the patience of someone who had learned that watching is most of safety.

She was twenty, perhaps twenty-one. Younger than I'd first taken her for; the rain-soaked arrival had aged her, or the fear had. But out here in the open country she moved differently — quicker, quieter, her weight always forward like a runner at her mark. I found myself cataloguing these things the way I catalogued everything, the reader's habit turned to a new text, and what I read in her frightened me a little. Not because of what she was hiding. Because of how practiced she was at hiding it.

At midday we ate bread and cheese and apples in the lee of a barn near Hothfield, the car tucked behind it, and Ada laid out the route on the barn door with her finger in the dust of the plank.

"Tonight, Hythe," she said. "There's an inn on the canal road — the Ship, they call it, though it hasn't been a ship in any sense for years. A man there. Julian called him—"

She stopped.

"It's all right," Edith said gently. "You can tell me his name."

"He told me never to say it aloud. Not once. Only at the inn, only to him, only after—" She frowned, hunting for the phrase Julian must have used. "Only after I've said where the thrush winters."

"The thrush," I repeated.

"Coded," said Edith. "Obviously." But her mouth was dry around the word; I saw her take the flask from the hamper and drink, though she offered none to us, which was unlike her.

I kept my own counsel about codes just then. There will be a moment later in this account — several moments, God help me — where my habit of reading other people's post becomes the hinge everything swings on, and I will not pretend I didn't feel the first small turning of that hinge even then. Three weeks earlier a circular had come through my hands, one of the movement's printed bulletins, addressed to a branch secretary in Hythe, and something in the address had been wrong — a loop on the H that didn't match the others, a smudge where ink shouldn't be — and I had noticed it, and dismissed it, and delivered it. I remembered the loop now with the peculiar horror of a man who finds he has been walking on a bridge all along without knowing the river beneath him.

But that knowledge was not yet mine. That afternoon I only knew the wind off the Weald, the taste of windfall apple, and a young woman drawing roads on a barn door with her finger while the sister of a dead man watched her as though she might vanish.

* * *

It was at Aldington that we first heard the name Reeve.

We had stopped to water nothing in particular — I had invented a trouble with a tyre, which was even true in a small way — and while I knelt at the wheel a carrier's cart drew up at the cottage opposite and its driver, an old fellow I half-knew from market days, called across the lane to the cottager in the carrying voice men use when they have news worth sharing and no shame about sharing it.

"—War Office gentleman, they're saying. Staying at the Wool Pack in Ashford. Very civil, very soft-spoken, but he's been round Smeeth and Mersham already asking after letters. Asking after the post, d'you see? Who carries it, who brings it, anything come from overseas that shouldn't of."

The cottager's wife had come to her gate. "Asking after the post! Whatever next?"

"That's what I say. Captain Reeve, he gave his name. Showed a card. Said it was about security. Well, I says, our post comes regular as church, and he smiles at me — soft smile, like a cat at a mousehole — and says, *that is precisely what I am assured, and I should like to keep it so.*"

They laughed together at that, comfortably, and the cart went on, and I stayed kneeling at my tyre with my heart going like a hammer in a sack.

*Reeve.* The name meant nothing. The smile meant everything. I had driven Edith through forty villages for three years and I knew exactly what it meant when a soft-spoken man with a card began asking about the post — asking not whether letters were coming, but who carried them. They were not hunting a letter. They were hunting a courier.

And here we were: a famous suffragist whose name was in every newspaper in Kent, a foreign-born girl with papers that had already survived one inspection, and me — me with three years of opened seals folded flat again in my coat pocket at home, evidence enough to hang a man if hanging were still the fashion for eavesdroppers.

I straightened up. Ada had heard every word; I could tell by the stillness of her. She had gone to stand by the barn wall, ostensibly looking at the ivy, actually positioned where she could watch the lane both ways.

"We should not stop in villages anymore," she said quietly, when the lane was empty. "Not even for bread."

"We cannot drive to Dover without stopping somewhere," Edith said. "People will talk wherever we stop. That is the tax on being known." Then, wryly, tiredly: "I have spent ten years making sure they talk. It seems unfair to resent it now."

"Not talk about *you,*" said Ada. "Talk about *us*. About the girl in your motorcar. Captain Reeve can find you in five minutes, Mrs. Marlowe — everyone knows your face. What he needs is the connection. Me. And the moment someone describes me to him—"

She didn't finish it. She didn't need to. A dark-haired girl of twenty with a foreign accent worn thin at the edges, travelling with Edith Marlowe toward the coast, asking after boats — I could write the report myself, and I had never written a report in my life. I thought of the soft smile, the cat at the mousehole, and felt the day's light change quality, autumn gold going to something harder.

"Then we don't ask after boats," Edith said. "We are three people motoring to the coast because I am giving a speech in Folkestone on Thursday, which happens to be true, and because my doctor recommends sea air, which is a lie I shall enjoy. Ada, from here to Hythe you are my mother's goddaughter's daughter, visiting from abroad, and your English is excellent but your accent is charming, and charming accents make people lower their guard rather than raise it. Use that."

Ada stared at her. "You've done this before."

"I have spent ten years being followed by police who thought me dangerous," Edith said. "One learns the choreography." She pulled the rug higher. "Drive on, Mr. Underhill. The tyre looks much recovered."

* * *

But it was I, not Ada, who could not let the thing lie, and so it was I who made the mistake — if mistake it was; I have gone back and forth on that for years and settled finally on *not*, though it cost us a bad hour.

At Brabourne Lees we stopped at a general shop for petrol and I sent Ada to wait with the car at the corner, out of sight of the road, and went in alone. Ordinary precaution. And inside, buying my two gallons, I fell into conversation with the shopman the way one does — weather, war, the price of things — and steered it, casually, God forgive me, toward the Wool Pack at Ashford, and the War Office gentleman.

"Oh, *him,*" the shopman said, weighing my cans. "Been through here Tuesday. Sat in that very chair. Wanted to know about a girl."

My hands went quite cold on the counter. "A girl?"

"Foreign-seeming, he said. Dark. Might be staying anywhere along the Pilgrims' Road — he had it in his head she was walking the old way, of all things, like a pilgrim." The shopman laughed. "Told him the only pilgrims we see are cyclists in sandals. Paid for his tea and went. Polite enough. But he wrote down the names of everyone I mentioned, and I mentioned a good few, more fool me."

"Did he say what she'd done?"

"Said she hadn't done anything *yet.*" The shopman tore my receipt off with a flourish. "That's the War Office for you. Arresting tomorrow today."

I carried the cans out with my mind roaring. *Done anything yet.* They were ahead of us. They had been ahead of us since before Ada reached our gate — or they were following a scent from elsewhere entirely, from the coast, from whatever net Julian's letters had brushed against these two years past — and either way, the comfortable fiction of our little expedition, the doctor's sea air, the charming accent, had just acquired a shadow with a card and a soft smile and a notebook full of village names.

Ada listened to all this without a word, standing with her hand flat on the Daimler's door as though feeling for a pulse. Then she said, in a voice I had not heard from her before — level, older, almost Julian's own:

"He's working the road behind us and asking forward. Which means he doesn't know where we are. Which means we have tonight, maybe two nights, before the description catches up with the direction." She looked at the sun, low now over the Lees, gilding the airfield grass where the aeroplanes sat in their sheds like sleeping wasps. "We reach the Ship before he does, or we don't reach it at all."

"And if he reaches it first?" I asked.

"Then a man who has been dead for two years stays dead," Ada said, "and everything Julian traded his life for dies quietly in a smuggling inn, and nobody ever knows." She met my eyes, and hers were dry and fierce and terribly young. "So we drive fast, Mr. Underhill. Faster than polite."

* * *

We drove fast. I put the old girl down the canal road with the hedges streaming past and the water lying pewter-still in the dusk, and behind me I heard Edith say little, and Ada less, and the whole countryside folding itself away into blue shadow with a speed that felt less like motion than like pursuit reversed — as though the land itself were trying to outrun what was coming behind us.

Twice I stopped the car and climbed a bank and looked back down the road we'd come. Twice, nothing: mist, poplars, the lamps of some farm going up yellow one by one. The third time, near Ivychurch, there was a motorcycle standing at the crossroads ahead, engine ticking as it cooled, rider nowhere in view.

I brought the car to rest a quarter mile short and we sat in the gathering dark and watched the small machine gleam at the crossing like a pin left in a map.

"Side road," Edith murmured at last. "Right, then left. Take us round by Brenzett."

We went round by Brenzett. And as the motorcycle's lamp dwindled behind us, I heard Ada, very softly, in the dark of the car behind my shoulder, begin to hum — a tune I didn't know, foreign-sounding, the kind of song a father might have sung to a daughter in a kitchen that was no longer hers — and I understood that she was not comforting herself. She was comforting us, the only way she had.

The lights of Hythe showed at last below the hill, strung along the dark lip of the Channel like embers raked from a fire, and beyond them the sea made its vast indifferent noise. Somewhere down there was an inn called the Ship, and a man to whom I would have to say where the thrush winters, and behind us, patient as tide, a soft-spoken captain writing names in a book.

I took the hill down slowly. Some thresholds, I have learned, you cross at speed; others you give their due.

"Mr. Underhill," Edith said quietly, as the first street lamp slid over her face and off again. "Whatever happens at this inn — whatever we learn — remember that you may still turn the car around and drive home to a clean conscience and your own fireside. No one would think the worse. *I* would not think the worse."

I considered the offer honestly, the way she had meant me to. I thought of my lodgings, my neat unread-again ledgers, my philosophy of history happening elsewhere, to other people, to men with cards and notebooks.

"No," I said. "I find I've misplaced my clean conscience somewhere back on the Pilgrims' Road. Best carry on and look for it at the coast."

Behind me, in the dark, Ada laughed — the first real laugh since the gate at Marlowe House — and the sound did something to the inside of my chest that I decided, firmly, to examine at no future date whatsoever.

The Ship's sign creaked ahead, black against blacker water, and under the sign, lit by one candle in the window, stood a broad old man with his hand already raised — raised, I would swear, before our lamps had swung onto the quay — as though he had been watching the road for us all evening.

Or as though someone had told him to.
