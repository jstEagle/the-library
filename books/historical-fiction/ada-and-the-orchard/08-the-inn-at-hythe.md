## 8. The Inn at Hythe

The rain came in off the Channel sideways, the way it does on that flat stretch of coast where there is nothing to stop it — no hills, no hedgerow tall enough, only the shingle beach and the grey water beyond it and then, if you believed the maps, France. Ada pressed her forehead briefly against the cold glass of the motor car's side window and watched the droplets crawl sideways like small animals trying to escape.

"You'll fog the glass," Ivo said from the driver's seat. He did not look round when he said it. He had a way of speaking that made everything sound like a report delivered to a superior officer, even remarks about weather and windows. Ada had decided, somewhere on the long road down from Marlowe House through Tenterden and Appledore, that this was not coldness but armour; she recognised the make of it because she wore a suit of it herself.

"Good," she said. "Then you can't see me thinking."

Behind her, Mrs. Marlowe laughed — a short, surprised sound, quickly folded away again, as though laughter were a handkerchief one ought not display in public. Edith Marlowe sat with her back very straight against the seat and her gloved hands folded on the leather case in her lap. She had barely spoken for the last ten miles, which Ada understood to mean that she was composing something: an argument, perhaps, or a letter, or simply the shape of her own courage. Ada was learning to read her the way one learns to read a new language — slowly, wrongly at first, then all at once.

"The sign," Ivo said. "There."

He slowed the car. Through the rain-blurred windscreen Ada made out the shape of the inn: low, black-timbered, hunched into the lee of a sea wall like an old man sheltering from a debt collector. A painted board swung above the door, creaking on its iron bracket. *The Mermaid's Purse.* The mermaid herself had been rained on so many years that her face had run to nothing; only her tail remained, a green smear of scales, and the purse in her outstretched hand, which the painter had rendered as a plain brown sack.

"It doesn't look like a place where anything is legal," Edith observed.

"That," said Ada, "is rather the point. Mr. Fenn doesn't trust anywhere that looks honest."

* * *

They left the car behind the inn, under a lean-to where lobster pots were stacked like ribcages, and went in by the back door because Ada told them to and because, by now, they had learned to do as she said in matters of doors and strangers. The kitchen was warm and dim and smelled of woodsmoke, wet wool, and fish. A woman with forearms like a blacksmith's stood over a cauldron stirring something that did not smell like anything intended for customers. She looked up, took in the three of them — the gentleman driver, the lady in good tweed, the girl in the middle with rain flattening her dark hair to her skull — and went back to her stirring.

"Marta," Ada said. "It's me. It's Ada."

The woman's spoon stopped. For a moment the whole kitchen seemed to stop with it — the fire ticking, the kettle muttering, the rain on the slates — and then Marta set the spoon across the pot's rim and crossed the floor in four strides and took Ada's face in her two enormous hands and looked at her the way you look at a thing you have been told is lost.

"Kleines Kind," Marta said. Then, catching Edith's eye, correcting herself with a stiff Englishness: "Miss Ada. You are thin. You are wet. You are both, always." But her thumbs were gentle on Ada's cheekbones, and her eyes were shining, and Ada felt something in her chest unclench that had been clenched since Dover.

"These are friends," Ada said, in the language she and Marta had shared in another country, another life — before England, before the war, before the word *alien* had grown teeth. "Mrs. Marlowe. And Mr. Underhill. They knew Herr— they knew him. The man whose letters you carried for me."

Something closed over in Marta's face. Not hostility; caution. She released Ada and wiped her hands on her apron with great deliberation, and Ada saw her glance past them to the kitchen door, and to the window above the sink, and to the yard beyond.

"Not here," Marta said quietly. "Upstairs. And the gentleman—" a nod at Ivo, "—stays below. That is how it must be."

"He's with us."

"Everyone is *with* someone," said Marta. "That is what makes the world dangerous now." But she looked at Ivo a moment longer, weighing him — his oilskin, his competent hands, the particular stillness he kept about himself — and whatever she found there, she shrugged one shoulder as though conceding a point in an argument she'd already lost. "All right. All three. But touch nothing, read nothing, and if anyone knocks, you are lost tourists and I am a widow with no memory."

She led them up a staircase so narrow they had to go single file, along a landing that smelled of tallow and salt, and into a room at the back where a bed, a table, and two chairs had been arranged with the economy of people who move often. Rain streaked the little window. Below, grey and endless, the sea.

Marta lit a candle although it was mid-afternoon. The flame wobbled in some draught Ada couldn't find.

"Now," Marta said. "Tell me why you have come to bring ruin to my house."

* * *

Ada told it plainly, because she had learned — first from Julian, then from the hard school of the last six weeks — that a story dressed up too finely invites disbelief, while a bare one invites questions, and questions you can answer. She told about the letter in the worn envelope, the handwriting she had known for three years across a foreign boarding-house table. She told about the promise — *if war comes, the Brandts must not be left* — spoken in peacetime over coffee and cigarette smoke by a young man who had no idea how literally history would take him. She told how Julian had sent her across half of Europe and then the Channel with instructions, and how she had come at last to Kent, to a house called Marlowe, because a dead man's sister was the only address a ghost could safely write to.

Edith listened without moving. Once, when Ada said *my brother*, Edith's hands tightened on the leather case, and Ada heard the case's clasp click softly, like a held breath let partway out.

When she finished, Marta stood at the window with her back to them for a long moment. Rain ticked on the glass. Downstairs a door opened and closed; voices rose and sank; a man laughed, and the laugh stopped abruptly, as though someone had stepped on it.

"You understand what you ask," Marta said at last, without turning. "Not what you want. What you ask. There is a man who can tell you whether your Julian lives, and where, and whether the letters are truly his. His name is Fenn. He keeps the bar downstairs and half the coastline in his pocket. He knew your Julian when your Julian was calling himself by another name and paying for information with information." Now she turned. Her broad face was grave. "But Fenn has had other visitors this month. Men asking after a soldier who should be dead. Government men."

Ada's stomach dropped. "How do you know?"

"Because they asked me too." Marta smiled without any pleasure in it. "A captain. Reeve. Very correct, very quiet, gloves never off. He sat downstairs for two hours nursing one whisky and asking about smuggling routes as if he cared about brandy. He cares about men who cross water without papers. He showed me a photograph." She reached into her apron pocket and drew out a folded paper, and unfolded it, and laid it on the table between them.

Ada looked down and forgot, for a moment, how to breathe.

The photograph was military-issue, grainy, formal: a line of soldiers in front of a billet wall, and second from the left, unmistakable even at that scale, Julian — younger, clean-shaven, his cap at the angle of a man who has not yet learned what caps are for. Someone had inked a circle around his face. Beneath the photograph, in a clerk's careful hand: *Lt. J. C. Marlowe. Reported killed in action, Loos, 25 Sept 1915.*

"He showed this to every innkeeper from Folkestone to Rye," Marta said. "Asking have you seen this man. Asking, in that soft voice of his, whether we might know of soldiers travelling under false names. And here is the thing, kleines Kind — here is the thing you must carry home in your teeth." She leaned forward, planting both fists on the table. "He did not say *spy*. He did not say *traitor*. He said *deserter*."

The word lay on the table between the candle and the photograph like something dead.

"I don't understand," Edith said. Her voice was level, but Ada, watching her, saw the colour drain slowly from beneath her skin, leaving the fine bones of her face standing out like architecture. "If Captain Reeve believed Julian a spy, there would be procedure — courts, charges, the whole apparatus. But desertion—"

"Desertion is worse," Ada said slowly. She was working it through aloud, and hated each step of the arithmetic. "Desertion means he wasn't serving anyone. It means he ran. It means—" She looked at Edith. "It means the army doesn't need evidence of treachery to shoot him. Only evidence of absence."

"Cowardice," Edith said. The word came out of her like a stone from a wound. "They will call my brother a coward. And the country will believe it, because the country prefers its cowards to its traitors. A traitor requires a plot, an enemy, a reason. A coward requires nothing but a missing man." She pressed her fingertips briefly to her mouth. "And a coward cannot be bargained with. There is nothing a deserter holds that anyone wants. No secrets to trade. Nothing but himself."

"And himself," said Marta, "is worth exactly one court-martial and one morning at dawn, if they take him." She straightened. "Unless."

"Unless?" Ivo said. He had not spoken since they entered the room; Ada had almost forgotten his presence, that quiet bulk by the door. Now his voice startled her, low and even.

"Unless the truth of him is more useful than the lie," Marta said. "Fenn will explain. Fenn understands these economies better than God does." She gathered the photograph from the table and held it a moment, looking down at Julian's circled face. "You see why I show you this. Not to frighten. To measure. Look at what they have done already — a dead man's picture carried from door to door, hunting a living one. Your friend is not hiding from the Germans, Miss Ada. He is hiding from his own side. That is a different war, with different rules, and fewer mercies."

Downstairs the bell over the bar door rang. Marta went utterly still, head tilted, listening — a smuggler's instinct worn smooth as river stone. Footsteps crossed the taproom floor. A voice asked a question Ada couldn't catch. Marta's shoulders lowered a fraction.

"A farmer," she murmured. "For cider." She turned back to them, and her voice dropped to nearly nothing. "Listen to me, all three, because I say this once. Tonight Fenn will talk to you, if he chooses to, and he may choose not to, and either way you will be polite. Tomorrow you go back inland, because this coast is a net drawn tight, and every day you stand on it you shake the net." She looked at Ada last, longest. "Your Julian wrote to you because he trusted you. So trust him in this also: he has survived two years among people who would kill him for his name. He knows how to wait. Learn from him."

* * *

They were given a room — one room, narrow, with a bed wide enough for the two women and a truckle that groaned under Ivo's weight until he moved it to the door and sat against it, fully clothed, like a sentry who had appointed himself. The rain eased toward evening and the light through the shutters went from grey to pewter to a thin bruised violet. Below them the taproom filled and emptied, filled and emptied, and snatches of conversation rose through the floorboards in fragments — tide times, excise men, a horse gone lame — a language of which every sentence sounded ordinary and none of them quite was.

Ada could not sleep. She lay beside Edith Marlowe — an intimacy that would have been unthinkable a month ago and now seemed merely practical — and listened to the older woman's breathing, which was controlled, deliberate, the breathing of someone who had decided to sleep and was enforcing the decision. Somewhere below, a chair scraped. Boots on stairs, passing, fading.

"You're awake," Edith murmured, near midnight.

"Yes."

"In Switzerland. When Julian gave you the letters. Did he seem afraid?"

Ada considered the question honestly, because Edith deserved honesty and because lying to her had begun to feel like a thing Ada could no longer afford. "No. That frightened me most, afterward. He seemed — settled. Like a man who had done a difficult sum and arrived at the answer. He said—" She stopped. In the darkness the words felt heavier than they had in daylight. "He said he had already died once, and it was the most useful thing he'd ever done. I thought it was a joke. He had that way."

Silence. Then Edith said, very quietly, "He used to say that about everything. When he broke his arm falling from the orchard ladder — 'the most useful thing I've ever done, I've learned the entire alphabet of pain.' Our mother despaired of him." A pause. "I thought grief had taught me every version of my brother. It appears grief only taught me the ones I invented."

Ada reached across the narrow space between them and found Edith's hand, and held it, and Edith did not pull away. They lay like that while the inn settled around them — beams contracting, the sea keeping its slow time below the sea wall — two women holding hands across the width of a catastrophe neither of them had chosen.

It was near two in the morning when the knock came. Three soft taps, spaced evenly, like a clock deciding something.

Ivo was on his feet before the third tap. Ada sat up. Edith struck a match and lit the candle, and in its sudden small light the three of them looked at one another, and Ada saw her own fear reflected twice and squared it by refusing to name it.

Marta's voice through the door, low: "Fenn will see the lady now. Alone."

"Alone," Edith repeated.

"That is his price for seeing you at all."

Ada was on her feet. "No. Whatever he says to you, he says to us. We came together—"

"Ada." Edith turned. The candlelight moved on her face and made her look, for a moment, both older and younger than her years. "This is my brother. My family's ruin, if ruin comes. If Fenn wants a hostage, better one than three." Before Ada could argue, she added, with a flicker of dryness that was entirely Julian's, "Besides — I have spent thirty years being underestimated by men in rooms. It is the one weapon I carry everywhere."

She took the candle, lifted her chin, and followed Marta's shadow down the stairs. Ada stood listening until the taproom door closed beneath her, and then the silence of the inn closed over the sound, and there was nothing to do but wait.

* * *

They waited an hour. Perhaps more. Ivo resumed his post against the door and did not speak, but Ada noticed that he had taken out his knife and was cleaning his nails with it, which she had learned was what he did instead of pacing. She sat on the bed with her knees drawn up, watching the shuttered window as if she could see through it to France, to wherever Julian was, to the shape of the danger moving along this coast with gloved hands and a soft voice.

When the stairs finally creaked, Ada was moving before the footsteps reached the top. Edith came into the room with the candle burnt down to a thumb's width and an expression Ada could not at first read — not relief, not despair, something more complicated, a face like weather about to change.

"Well?" Ada said.

"He lives." Edith set the candle stub on the table. Her hand, Ada saw, was not quite steady. "Julian lives. Fenn has held money for him, passed messages, since the spring of '16. He is on the far coast — Fenn would not say more, and Fenn's silences, I suspect, are load-bearing. But he lives, and he knows we have come, and he has sent—" She stopped. Drew breath. Started again, and her voice changed, became careful, the voice of a woman reading a document aloud. "He has sent word that the next instruction will reach us through the usual channel. Those were his exact words. *The usual channel.*"

"What usual channel?" Ivo asked. "We haven't any channel. We've had one letter and a girl with a good memory."

"That is what I asked Fenn." Edith lowered herself onto the chair, and suddenly she looked exhausted, wrung out, forty years of a night compressed into her shoulders. "He laughed at me. Actually laughed. He said — and I quote him exactly, because the words have been turning over in my mind like a stone in a tide — he said, *Madam, your brother has been writing to you for months. You've been carrying him around Kent in your own postbag. Ask your driver what he's been delivering.*"

The room went very still. The candle guttered in its pool of wax. Outside, far off, a night bird cried over the marshes, one note, twice.

Ada turned her head slowly and looked at Ivo. He had gone pale in the way men go pale — blood leaving the face in a single tide — and his hand had closed around the knife without his seeming to notice it.

"My postbag," Ivo said. His voice was strange, stripped of its report-delivering evenness. "Your circulars. The suffrage mail." He stared at Edith, and then at the middle distance, and Ada watched comprehension arrive in him like water rising in a lock, stage by stage, gate by gate. "Oh," he said. "Oh, God. I've been reading him for months. I've been *carrying* him." He looked up, and his eyes were bright and appalled and alive. "Every week. Every village. Right past the telegraph office, past the constables, past everyone — because nobody stops a lady's circulars, and nobody watches a chauffeur reading in a lane."

"Fenn says," Edith said softly, "that my brother chose that route two years ago precisely because it was invisible. Because it was *women's business*. And that if we wish to hear from him again, we need only go home, and wait, and keep the post coming in." She looked around the room at them — at Ada, at Ivo, at the wrecked candle and the rain beginning again against the shutters — and something in her face was fierce and grieving and, impossibly, proud. "My brother has been hiding inside my own movement. Inside the one correspondence in England that no official man thinks worth opening."

Ivo sat down slowly on the edge of the truckle bed, as though his knees had been borrowed and were being recalled.

"I read everything," he said, half to himself. "Everything that crosses my hands. And it was all there, and I never—" He stopped. Ran a hand through his hair. "Which means he trusted the one person who'd never miss it."

"No," Ada said. Understanding came to her gently, the way Marta's hands had come to her face. "It means he trusted the one person who'd never *tell*."

Outside, the rain thickened against the coast, and the sea went on saying what it always said, patient and indifferent, keeping all its crossings to itself.
