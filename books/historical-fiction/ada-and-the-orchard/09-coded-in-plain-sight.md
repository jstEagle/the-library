## 9. Coded in Plain Sight

I could not sleep after the inn at Hythe, and so it was that at half past two in the morning, sitting in the kitchen of Marlowe House with a single candle burning and a cold cup of tea going colder beside me, I finally saw what had been in front of my eyes for eleven months.

It began, as these things do, with an irritation.

Ada had gone up to bed — or to her room, at least, though I doubted she slept any better than I did; the innkeeper's warning about Captain Reeve had lodged in all three of us like a splinter under the nail — and Edith Marlowe had retreated behind her study door with the letter that should not exist. That left me alone with the fire's last coals and the particular restlessness of a man who has learned too much too quickly and cannot decide which piece of it frightens him most.

I am not by nature a restless man. Restlessness implies expectation, and expectation implies investment, and for thirty-one years I had made a quiet religion of neither. History happened elsewhere — to generals, to ministers, to men whose names went into newspapers while mine went onto delivery ledgers. My job was the road between Marlowe House and the villages, the mud and the hedgerows, the letters carried sealed and unexamined.

Well. Sealed, yes. Unexamined, no.

That was my secret, the one I had confessed to Edith only days before: that I read her mail. Not out of malice — God knows there was never malice in it — but out of the small, shameful hunger of a man whose own life offered so little to read. The circulars, the correspondence, the appeals for funds and signatures; I read them all in the lamplight of way-station inns, on gateposts in the sun, once memorably upside down in a ditch with the motor steaming like a kettle. I told myself it was harmless. Paper passed through my hands; some of its ink passed through my eyes. No harm done, no harm meant. A private vice, victimless as daydreaming.

And now here was the thing that would not let me sleep: Julian Underwood-Marlowe — dead man, deserter, ghost — had been sending coded messages through this house for months. Through *me*. Ada had said it at the inn, turning her cup slowly on its saucer: *He said you'd understand eventually. He said the channel was already built.*

The channel was already built. What channel? What had he meant?

I fetched my coat from the peg by the door because the kitchen had gone cold, or because movement helps thinking, or because I wanted an excuse to be anywhere but inside my own skull. And on the hall table, where the post for the morning run was stacked and banded, I saw the circulars.

*The Kentish Sister*, Edith's monthly broadsheet — four pages, cheap paper, set in type by a jobbing printer in Maidstone who charged her less than anyone else in the county and asked no questions. I had delivered hundreds of them. I had read hundreds of them. Farm prices and meeting notices, arguments about the Cat and Mouse Act, poems by women signing themselves with initials, advertisements from sympathetic tradesmen in Ashford and Canterbury. I knew the format the way a postman knows a lane: without knowing it, the way one knows one's own street in the dark.

I took the top copy back into the kitchen and laid it flat under the candle.

Nothing. Just the usual. Page one, a leader on force-feeding at Holloway, the prose hot and hammering as Edith always wrote when she was angry, which was always. Page two, notices. Page three —

Page three, bottom corner, the small advertisement that appeared in every issue: *WANTED — Good plain cook for gentleman's household near Wye. Apply Box 14, this office.* It had run, I realized, in every issue since March. Eleven issues. An unfilled position for a cook, month after month, in a county where cooks were scarce as honest magistrates.

I sat down. My heart was doing something foolish and rapid, and I told it to be still, and it declined.

A joke, surely. Printer's filler. Except that Edith Marlowe did not print jokes in her organ, and she did not leave positions unfilled for eleven months, and she had told me — had she not? — that every line of *The Kentish Sister* passed under her eye twice.

I got the back numbers. They lived in a cupboard off the study, and I told myself I was not stealing anything, merely borrowing knowledge, which is what I had been telling myself about her mail for a year. Eleven copies, March through January. I spread them across the kitchen table like a hand of cards, weighted down with the salt cellar, a spoon, my pipe, the teapot lid.

And then I looked. Really looked, for the first time — not the skimming read of a man stealing pleasure in an inn parlour, but the flat, patient stare one gives a field of stubble when one has lost something in it.

Box 14, every month, same wording, same position on the page. But the *other* advertisements changed. In March: seed potatoes from a grower near Tenterden. April: a second-hand bicycle, Canterbury. May: lodgings in Folkestone, terms moderate. June: a piano for sale, owner leaving the district. July: hay for cutting. August: nothing — a blank space, exactly the size of a small advertisement. September: a lost dog. October: mending and alterations by a Mrs. P., address given as a shop in Hythe. November: a grey mare for sale, quiet in harness. December: a Christmas greeting from the printer himself, warm little paragraph. January: a young woman seeks situation as mother's help.

I wrote them all down on the back of a delivery chit, because writing things down makes them real and I badly needed this to be either very real or very unreal, and I couldn't tell which from looking alone.

Seed potatoes. Bicycle. Lodgings. Piano. Nothing. Dog. Alterations. Mare. Greeting. Mother's help.

Nonsense, I thought. Coincidence, filler, the ordinary static of small ads. And then I thought of the innkeeper at Hythe, a smuggler's smuggler with sixty years of coastline in his face, saying: *Your brother always was one for hiding things where folk look every day and never see.*

Where folk look every day and never see.

I took the first letter — the initial of each advertisement's headline item. S. B. L. P. — N. D. A. M. G. M.

SBLP NDAMGM. Nothing. I tried last letters. S E S O N S E R E G P. Nothing. First letters of whole lines. Initials of towns. Tenterden, Canterbury, Folkestone — no pattern held. I felt the vertigo come on properly then, the sick swinging sensation of a man on a ladder who realizes the ladder has been leaning against fog all along. Because this was my trade, wasn't it? Reading. I had flattered myself for years that I was a reader — the one man in the chain of custody who actually *saw* what passed through his hands. The colonels skimmed their dispatches; the vicars skimmed their sermons; Edith skimmed the world at a speed that frightened people. But I read. I read everything, greedily, secretly. Reading was my one possession, my one trespass, my one claim on history.

And if there had been a code running through my hands for eleven months — messages from a dead man to a living woman, threaded through the very pages I devoured in ditch-side lamplight — then I hadn't been reading at all. I'd been mouthing words. A child tracing letters it cannot sound.

The candle guttered. Outside, a fox went screaming down the orchard hedge, and I nearly went through the ceiling.

* *

It was Ada who broke it, of course. Not me. I want that recorded somewhere, even if only in the privacy of this account: I sat until dawn with my list of nonsense and my growing conviction, and it was Ada, coming down at seven with her hair unbraided and her eyes already sharp, who glanced at the table, at the eleven broadsheets fanned out like evidence, and said:

"You've found it."

"I've found nothing," I said. "I've found potatoes and a piano and somebody's lost dog."

She pulled out a chair and sat opposite me, and she did not touch the papers, just looked at me across them with that expression she had — the one that made me feel simultaneously seen through and seen, which are not the same thing and which I could not yet tell apart.

"Read me the sequence again. In order. The items."

"Seed potatoes. Second-hand bicycle. Lodgings, Folkestone. Piano. Then a gap — August was blank. Lost dog. Mending and alterations. Grey mare. Christmas message. Mother's help."

"And what does Edith say about the blank?"

"She hasn't seen it. She hasn't seen any of this. I haven't shown her." I heard how that sounded and pressed on before shame could organize itself into a sentence. "If Julian built this channel, he built it to survive scrutiny. Anyone might read the circular. Reeve might read the circular. The fewer people who know the key, the safer everyone is. That includes her."

"That's very tidy," Ada said, "for a man who's been awake all night inventing reasons not to wake her." But she was smiling slightly, and she reached over and turned the December issue toward me, tapping the printer's greeting with one finger. "Start here. Why would a printer send a personal message in a suffragist paper? He charges her less than cost. Men like that don't waste paid space on sentiment."

"'Warmest greetings to our readers at the turn of the year,'" I recited. "'May 1916 find every household brighter than the last. — Your friends at the Faversham Press.'"

"Faversham," she repeated. "Where is Faversham?"

"Twelve miles north. On the railway line to — " And I stopped, because the sentence finished itself without my permission: to Dover. To the coast. To the water.

"The others are places too," Ada said quietly. "Or they're pointing at places. Seed potatoes — Tenterden, that's south. Bicycle, Canterbury — east. Lodgings, Folkestone — southeast, the port. Piano — " she looked up at me, "pianos have makers' names. Where was it from?"

I bent over the page. "*A fine cottage upright by Broadwood, hardly used.*"

"Broadwood," she said. "Broadwood is a place?"

"It's a maker. There's a Broadwood — " I closed my eyes and let the map of Kent, which I carry in my head more accurately than any ordnance sheet, unfold itself. "There's a hamlet called Broad Oak, near Ashford. And Broadstairs is further round the coast. And — " I stopped again, because the next one arrived whole: "Hythe. Mrs. P., alterations, Hythe. That's where we met the innkeeper. That's where Julian's contact lives."

"And the mare?"

"*Quiet in harness, good in traffic.*" I stared at it. "Good in traffic. Roads. Routes."

"The dog?" Ada leaned forward.

"*Lost near Wye, answers to the name Pilot.*"

"Pilot," she breathed. "Pilot — guides. Someone's guiding him. Or he needs a guide."

We sat back. The kitchen clock ticked with the fat self-satisfaction of a clock that has known everything for hours. On the table, eleven months of a dead man's voice lay arranged in a fan, and every word of it had passed through my hands, and I had read every word of it aloud in my head in inn parlours and on gateposts and once, memorably, upside down in a ditch, and I had understood precisely none of it.

"He was talking the whole time," I said. My voice came out strange, flattened, as if someone else were using my mouth. "Route instructions. Places. Months. He's been walking himself across Kent in the classified columns of a suffrage newspaper, and I delivered him. Every month. On time. I was the courier and I never — "

"You were the courier," Ada said, "and that was rather the point. Nobody suspects the post."

But that was not the point, not the point that mattered, and she must have heard it in my silence, because her face changed and she said, more gently, "Ivo. Say it."

"He hid it from me *in the thing I steal*." I heard my own bitterness and was surprised by it. "Do you see? All those months I congratulated myself — reading what I shouldn't, knowing what nobody knew I knew, my little window into other people's lives — and there was a man writing straight through my window, in letters an inch high, and I never once — " I gestured at the papers, and my hand was not steady. "He knew. He must have known Edith used me. He counted on the driver being a man who reads everything and understands nothing. I was the security feature."

Ada was quiet a moment. Rain had started against the kitchen window, the thin persistent kind that means to stay the week.

"My father used to say," she offered at last, "that the safest hiding place in England is the inside of an Englishman's assumption. You assumed the circulars were what they said they were. So they were."

"That's supposed to comfort me?"

"No," she said. "It's supposed to teach you. There's a difference."

* *

Edith came down at nine and ended the argument by refusing to have one.

She stood at the table in her grey working dress with her sleeves pushed up, and she read the sequence I'd copied out, and then she read the pages themselves, and then she was silent for long enough that the rain filled the pause completely.

"Mare for sale in November," she said finally. "Grey mare. Quiet in harness." Her finger rested on the line without pressing. "In November he moved. Or planned to move. By road, avoiding the railway." She looked up. "The blank in August."

"A gap in the code," I said. "Something went wrong, or he had nothing to report."

"August," Edith said, and her voice did something I had never heard it do — thinned, aged, dropped ten years and a war. "August is when the Brandt boy's deportation order would have been drafted. If Julian went dark that month — if he stopped transmitting — it was because he was moving the family. Hiding them." She straightened. "Which means the promise isn't pending. It's *failing*. It has been failing since summer, and he's been shouting route after route into a newspaper, and the newspaper arrives a week late every time because the roads flood and the motor breaks and the driver — "

She stopped herself. She did not finish the sentence, and I loved her a little and hated her a little for not finishing it, because we both knew how it ended: *because the driver was asleep at the wheel of his own life.*

"There'll be a new one," Ada said. "February's proof sheets. When do they go to the printer?"

"Thursday," Edith said. "He sets type Wednesday night. Anything Julian wants in goes to Faversham by Wednesday noon." She turned to me, and the commander was back in her face, the woman who had stared down magistrates and cabinet ministers, and underneath the commander, barely visible, the sister. "Ivo. Thursday's run takes the February issue to every branch secretary in the east of the county. Which means Wednesday noon, someone drives to Faversham with copy. And whatever is in that copy — whatever Box 14 says, whatever the mare or the dog or the blessed piano says — I need it read properly this time. Before it's delivered anywhere."

"Properly," I repeated.

"With understanding." She gathered the eleven issues into a stack, squared their edges with a rap on the table, and handed them to me like a commission, like a medal, like a sentence. "You're the only one of us who's read every word he ever sent. That makes you the only one of us who can hear him. Go and learn his language, Mr. Underhill. You have until Wednesday."

She left the kitchen. Ada followed, pausing at the door to look back at me — at the table, at the candle burned to a puckered coin, at the man sitting in the wreckage of his own cleverness.

"For what it's worth," she said, "he chose you. Not the vicar, not the stationmaster. The man who reads everything. He bet his life that you'd be curious enough to steal and humble enough, eventually, to learn." A half-smile. "So far he's half right."

Then she was gone too, and I was alone with the rain and the broadsheets and the extraordinary, unbearable discovery that history had not been happening elsewhere at all. It had been happening in my hands. Every month. In eight-point type. Signed, all this time, by a dead man who trusted me precisely because I never knew what I was carrying.

Wednesday noon. Two days to learn the language of a ghost.

I picked up the February proofs' predecessor, the January issue, and read it again from the first line to the last — not skimming, not stealing, but the way one reads a letter from someone dying: slowly, twice, with the terrible attention owed to words one can never ask the writer to repeat.

Halfway down page four, in the meetings column, between Ashford and Wye, was a notice I had read a dozen times and never seen:

*A special service of thanksgiving will be held at the disused chapel outside Aldington, Sunday next, for all those separated from their families by the war. All are welcome.*

All are welcome.

Julian, I thought, staring at it while the rain came harder off the orchard, you cunning, patient, unforgivable man.

You aren't writing to her at all anymore.

You're asking us to come.
