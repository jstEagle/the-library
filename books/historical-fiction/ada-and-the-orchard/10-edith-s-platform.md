## 10. Edith's Platform

The morning of the Maidstone rally, Edith Marlowe stood at her bedroom window and watched the frost burn off the orchard grass, and thought: *I could stop.* The thought arrived the way such thoughts always did — not as temptation but as arithmetic. If she withdrew from the platform, if she cancelled the meeting, if she took to her bed with a plausible feminine complaint and let Mrs. Pankhurst's regional organisers send someone else, then no one would ever look twice at a dead man's handwriting. The cause would lose one voice in Kent and gain its safety. Julian would remain dead in every newspaper, every parish roll of honour, every careful lie the War Office had told, and Ada would carry her letters back across the water alone.

She counted the cost the way her father had taught her to count lambs — quickly, unsentimentally, and knowing that some would not survive the season — and then she rang for hot water and began to dress.

The dress itself was a kind of armour, and she chose it with a general's attention. Not white; white was for martyrs and brides, and she was neither today. She wore the dark violet that read, at forty paces, as merely respectable, with the small enamel badge pinned high on the lapel where a photographer's lens might find it or might not. In the pocket of her coat she placed her speaking notes — four cards, closely written — and beneath them, folded into eighths, the letter she had sworn she would leave behind and had not. Julian's hand. Six weeks old. Alive.

She had slept perhaps three hours. Her hands, when she held them out in the grey light, were steady. That steadiness frightened her more than trembling would have done.

Downstairs, the house was already in motion. Ivo had the motorcar out front, bonnet steaming in the cold like something alive and impatient, and Ada stood beside it wrapped in Edith's own spare coat, which hung off her shoulders as though the coat were grieving. The girl had barely spoken since breakfast. She had wanted, Edith knew, to be left at home — had argued for it in her quick foreign-inflected English, *no one will look for me if I am not there* — and Edith had refused, and refused again, and finally understood why she was refusing only when she heard herself say it:

"Because I will not spend this afternoon wondering where you are."

It was not an argument about safety. It was an argument about the shape of a family under siege, and Ada had gone quiet at it, and climbed into the car without another word, and now sat with her gloved hands folded in her lap like a woman at a funeral — which, Edith supposed, was close enough.

"You'll want the long way round," Ivo said, touching his cap. "Reeve's people were asking at the toll house yesterday. Two men, plain clothes, badly chosen boots." He said it the way he said everything, flat and competent, but his eyes went to Ada and stayed there a half-second longer than necessary, and Edith filed that away among the many things she was filing away these days, in the drawer that was becoming a cabinet, the cabinet a room.

"The long way round, then," she said, "and we shall arrive late enough to be dramatic and early enough to be useful."

He almost smiled. It was astonishing, how much of her present life was composed of almosts.

* * *

Maidstone received them with wet bunting and a hostile drizzle. The Corn Exchange had been hired through intermediaries — Edith had learned years ago never to hire halls in her own name — and by half past two the queue stretched down Week Street, women in the movement's colours and women in ordinary brown, shopgirls and farmers' wives and two elderly sisters who had come, they announced to anyone within earshot, all the way from Faversham because they had promised their mother on her deathbed that they would see the famous Miss Marlowe before either God or Mr. Asquith collected them.

Edith stood in the anteroom behind the platform and let the noise of the gathering come through the wall like weather. There was a particular sound a crowd made before a speaker it already favoured — a low, warm murmur, the sound of expectation banked like a fire ready to be drawn — and beneath it, today, she could hear the other sound, the one she had trained herself over fifteen years to isolate the way a conductor isolates a single wrong note. Chairs shifting. A voice too loud for the room. Men.

"They've let in the hecklers," said Mrs. Tarrant, the local secretary, a stout woman with the face of a tolerant spaniel. "Six or seven. We counted them coming in. One of them has a bell."

"A bell," Edith repeated.

"To drown you out. They've taken it up lately. Bells, whistles, sometimes a gramophone." Mrs. Tarrant said it with the weariness of long practice, as though reporting that the rain continued. "You needn't worry. Our stewards will manage."

"I'm sure they will," Edith said, and did not believe a word of it, and did not care. Bells she could survive. It was the other thing in the hall that concerned her, and when she stepped to the edge of the curtain to look out at the audience, she found him within ten seconds, the way one finds a wasp at a picnic.

Captain Reeve sat halfway back, on the aisle, in civilian clothes that fooled nobody — the cut of them was military even when the cloth was not, and he sat with his hat on his knee and his hands loose between his thighs, watching the platform with the patient, unblinking attention of a man who has been paid to watch and intends to earn his wage. He was not there for the cause. He was not even, she suspected, there primarily for her. He was there because a woman named Ada Brandt — or whatever name she travelled under this month — was somewhere in this building, and because a dead lieutenant's letters were moving through Kent like blood through a body, and because Captain Reeve had been given a wound to hide and had decided, sensibly, that the best place to watch a wound was wherever it was likely to bleed.

Beside her, at the tea urn, Ada went very still.

"He's here," Ada said. Not a question.

"Yes."

"Then I should go—"

"You should stay exactly where you are," Edith said, "and pour tea, and be the most unremarkable secretary in England. Can you do unremarkable?"

Ada looked at her, and something passed over the girl's face that was not quite a smile — recognition, maybe, of the absurdity, of two women conspiring to perform ordinariness while a letter written by a resurrected man burned in Edith's pocket like a coal. "I have been unremarkable my whole life," Ada said. "It is the one thing I am qualified for."

* * *

She went up to the platform at twenty past three, and the hall drew breath, and the fire of them caught.

Fifteen years of platforms teach a body things the mind forgets it knows. How to stand so that the light falls forward and your eyes can travel the room without your head turning. How to open below the pitch of the hecklers, so quiet that the hall leans in to hear you and silences them for you. How to plant a sentence's most dangerous word at its end, where it lands instead of being walked over. Edith did these things the way her hands found the teapot in the dark — without deliberation — and meanwhile the whole of her mind, the secret mind, the mind that had spent two weeks living inside her brother's impossible handwriting, ran its parallel terror beneath the performance like a river under ice.

She spoke of the franchise. She spoke of the Cat and Mouse Act — of women force-fed in prison, released to sicken, rearrested at the gate — and she felt the hall's anger rise with her own and used it, shaped it, gave it metre. She spoke of the taxpaying women of Kent, of the girls in the munitions works who were good enough to fill shells and not good enough to mark a ballot. She was good. She knew she was good; modesty about it had always seemed to her a vanity of its own kind. The words came up out of her like water from a deep well, cold and clear, and the hall drank.

And all the while, in the pocket against her hip, the letter lay against her body like a second heartbeat, and she thought: *if they search me now, it is finished.* Not arrested — searching a speaker was not yet done, though it would come, she had no doubt it would come — but seen, known, whispered into the columns of the *Kentish Gazette*, and after that every word she ever spoke would be footnoted by her brother's desertion. *The sister of a deserter asks us to trust her judgement. The family that produced one liar asks us for the vote.* She could hear the leading paragraphs already. She could hear worse than the newspapers: the quiet voices, the drawing-room voices, the ones that did not print but decided. *Of course, one understands the Marlowes. Unstable stock.*

The bell rang.

It came during her fourth card, a bright idiot clangour from the left-hand side, followed by laughter and a man's voice pitched to carry — something about ladies and their hats, something older and uglier underneath the joke — and the hall stiffened the way a horse stiffens. Edith let the bell ring. She let it finish. She looked directly at the man holding it, a pink-faced fellow with the moustache of a subaltern and the eyes of someone who had never been contradicted by anything louder than a wife, and she waited until the silence had become his problem rather than hers.

"My father kept bees," she said.

The non sequitur did what non sequiturs do; curiosity is the oldest leash there is. Somewhere near the back a woman laughed, warmly, on Edith's side of the invisible line.

"When a hive grows restless, there is a great deal of noise, and it sounds like anger, and inexperienced beekeepers are often stung trying to answer it." She folded her fourth card, unhurried, and set it on the ledge. "My father taught me that the noise is not the thing. The thing is the swarm deciding where it will live next. You may ring your bell, sir, as loudly as you please. We have already decided where we mean to live. It is called the future, and there is room in it for you, if you stop ringing."

The hall came up off its chairs.

She rode it — the cheering, the stamping, the embarrassed flush of the bell-ringer sitting down as though he had been the entertainment — and she thought, with a clarity that approached giddiness: *I could do this forever. This is the easiest hour of my life. Everything true and hard is happening somewhere else entirely.* And then, deliberately, she let her eyes travel the aisle again, to Captain Reeve, and found him not laughing, not scowling, not taking notes. He was simply watching her, head tilted very slightly, with an expression she needed a full three seconds to identify, and when she identified it her stomach dropped through the floorboards of the performance.

It was respect. He was looking at her the way one craftsman looks at another's work. And it was worse than contempt, because contempt could be survived, could even be used — but a man who respected his adversary paid closer attention, and remembered better, and forgave nothing.

* * *

Afterwards, in the anteroom, with the crowd draining away down Week Street and the stewards stacking chairs, Edith allowed herself, at last, to shake. Just the hands. She stood at the window with her back to the room and watched them tremble, fine and fast, like something caged, and she pressed them flat against the cold glass until they stilled.

Mrs. Tarrant was effusive. The Faversham sisters had fought their way backstage to grasp her fingers and announce that their dear mother would have been proud, and Edith had thanked them and meant it and remembered none of it. Ada had vanished — no, not vanished; Ivo reported her safely outside by the car, having made herself unremarkable with such success that even Edith had lost track of her, which was either a comfort or a warning. Reeve had gone before the last applause. She had watched him rise, settle his hat, and walk out with the unhurried tread of a man whose afternoon's work was complete, and she had understood, standing on the platform with her heart hammering against the folded paper, that he had got exactly what he came for.

Not evidence. Nothing so crude. He had come to measure her, and she had shown him the measure.

A woman who speaks like that, he would write in whatever report he wrote, does not abandon a brother. A woman who stands in front of bells and gramophones and six hecklers with badly chosen boots will not withdraw quietly, will not trade, will not break — unless. Unless the pressure comes not at her but around her. Through the girl. Through the driver. Through the thing she loves more than the cause, if such a thing exists, and if it exists, we shall find it.

"Ma'am?" Ivo stood at the door with his cap in his hands. He had that look he got when he had driven through weather — competent, contained, faintly apologetic for existing. "Car's ready. Miss Ada's in it. There's—" He hesitated, and Edith turned fully toward him, and saw that his hesitation had a shape to it, a weight.

"There's what?"

"There's a note been left. Under the wiper on the windscreen." He produced it gingerly, by one corner, as though it might go off. "No name. But it's—" His jaw worked. "You'd best read it yourself, ma'am."

The paper was cheap, machine-ruled, torn roughly. Three lines, block capitals, no signature:

**THE GIRL'S NAME IS ON A LIST AT THE PORT OF DOVER. IT WOULD BE A SHAME IF SHE SAILED BEFORE YOU HAD DECIDED WHAT SHE IS WORTH TO YOU.**

Edith read it twice. Outside, the drizzle had thickened toward proper rain, and the bunting dripped, and somewhere a church bell marked the quarter-hour with the same bright idiocy as the heckler's handbell, and she stood very still in the emptying anteroom with her brother's letter burning in one pocket and a stranger's threat crumpling slowly in her fist.

Ivo was watching her face. Whatever he saw there made him step forward and take the note from her hand and read it himself, and she watched him go pale, and watched him understand — faster than she had, faster than was comfortable — that the game had changed its nature entirely.

"That's not a warning," Ivo said quietly. "That's a bill. Someone means to collect."

"No," Edith said, and heard her own voice come out level, cold, the platform voice, and hated how grateful she was for it. "Bills are honest. This is an invitation. Someone wants us to run — wants us frightened and running and visible." She looked out at the rain, at the car, at the slight figure of Ada waiting under an umbrella she was holding crooked, and the terror and the fury in her rose together and fused into something she could actually use.

"Get the car, Ivo. We're going home by the long way, and on the way you are going to tell me everything you noticed today, however small. Especially the boots."

* * *

They were three miles out of Maidstone, the hedgerows streaming, the wipers keeping their tired time, when Ivo said, without preamble, in the voice of a man opening a door he cannot close again:

"Ma'am. There's something I have to tell you. About the letters. About what I've been doing with your post."

Edith closed her eyes briefly, and thought of bees, and swarms, and decisions, and said, "Drive, Ivo. And talk."
