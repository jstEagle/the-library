## 11. Ada's Own War

The rain had followed them from Dover Road all the way back to Marlowe House, and by the time the motor turned through the gates it had softened into that grey English drizzle that seemed less like weather than like a mood the country itself was in. Ada watched it bead on the windscreen glass and thought of another kind of rain entirely — warm, sudden, smelling of dust and orange peel — and of a courtyard in a town whose name she still said to herself at night, the way other people said their prayers.

She had been in England eleven days now. Long enough for her boots to dry out properly for the first time since Calais. Long enough to learn which floorboards in Marlowe House announced you and which kept your secrets. Not yet long enough to stop waking at four in the morning with her heart going like a trapped bird, listening for footsteps that were not there, in a house where no one was coming to arrest anyone.

Yet.

* * *

They gathered that evening in the small sitting room behind the library — the room Edith called the winter room, because in the coldest months she moved her correspondence there to be near the fire. Ivo had lit the lamps and then, as he always did, retreated to the periphery: the chair by the door, angled so that he could see the whole room without quite belonging to any part of it. Ada had begun to understand this about him. He positioned himself the way a man stands at the edge of a dance, watching the figures and counting the steps, convinced the music is not for him.

Edith sat with Julian's letter open on her knee. She had read it perhaps forty times since the night Ada arrived. Ada knew because she counted — the way she counted everything now, the way you learn to count when counting is all you have.

"You've told us how you met him," Edith said. "The pension in Antwerp. The landlady. How he came to trust you." She paused, and her thumb moved across the edge of the letter, back and forth, a small unconscious motion, like touching a wound to be sure it still hurt. "But you haven't told us why *you*. Why he chose you to carry all of this across a war."

Ada looked at the fire. It was easier than looking at Edith's face, which held that terrible combination — hope and grief braided so tightly together that pulling either thread would undo the whole.

"He didn't choose me," Ada said. "Not at first. I chose him. Or rather—" she laughed, and heard how thin it came out, "—I cornered him. I'd seen his handwriting on letters he posted, and I knew it wasn't the name he went by. Herr Weiss did not write like Herr Weiss. He wrote like an educated man pretending to be a clerk, and I had grown up reading men's pretences. My mother taught me. She said a woman who can't read a lie will end up married to one."

Ivo made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth.

"And when I told him what I'd noticed," Ada went on, "he didn't deny it. He just looked at me for a long time — the way you look down a road before you decide whether to walk it. And then he asked me a question I have never forgotten. He said: *What would you do if you could not go home?*"

The fire ticked. Somewhere upstairs a door closed — the house settling, or Mrs. Pym making her rounds.

"And what did you answer?" Edith asked quietly.

"I said I wouldn't do what he'd done. Faking a death seemed very grand to me. Very dramatic." Ada turned her hands over in her lap, studying the palms as though the answer might be written there. "I said I'd stay. I'd stay and make myself so useful, so quiet, so necessary, that they'd forget I didn't belong. That was my whole strategy for being alive in the world, Miss Marlowe. Make yourself furniture. Furniture isn't deported."

The word landed in the room and stayed there. Ada felt Ivo's attention shift toward her — she had learned to feel it, that particular quality of his listening, like standing near a window that has just been opened.

"Deported," Edith repeated. Not a question. An invitation.

And so Ada told them.

* * *

"My father came to England in 1881," she said. "Before I was born. Before the war, before the naval scares, before all of it. He came from Kiel with nothing but a toolbag and three words of English — *work*, *yes*, and *thank you* — and within ten years he owned a workshop off Cable Street making marine chronometers. Marine chronometers, Miss Marlowe. Instruments that keep ships from getting lost. There are captains on the Atlantic run right now navigating by clocks my father built with these hands—" she held hers up, work-roughened, ink-stained at the cuticles, "—and England gave him his papers and took his rates and let him marry my mother, who was English, from Deptford, as English as kippers and fog. I was born in Stepney. I have never in my life set foot in Germany. Until this war I had not even wanted to."

She stopped. The anger was rising in her now, the old familiar heat, and she let it, because she had decided somewhere between Hythe and home that she would tell this story hot or not at all. Cold stories could be dismissed. Hot ones left marks.

"In August of 1914 they came for him. Not soldiers — policemen, two of them, apologetic the way Englishmen are when they're doing something shameful. They said it was temporary. A precaution. Enemy aliens of military age, interned under the Defence of the Realm Act — though my father was forty-nine years old and had a bad lung from solder fumes and couldn't have fought for the Kaiser if the Kaiser himself had come round with a drum. It didn't matter. His name was Brandt-adjacent — no. Forgive me. His name was Vogel. Vogel was enough. Vogel meant pigeon in German, and apparently it also meant spy."

"Your father," Edith said slowly, "is Herr Vogel. And the family Julian—"

"The Brandts were our neighbours. In Antwerp, after we fled. Papa had sent us money through a cousin, and Frau Brandt took my mother and me in when the workshops were requisitioned and the street turned ugly — when shops with German names had their windows put in, and men stood outside shouting things I won't repeat in front of a lady who's earned better. The Brandts' son Karl is seventeen. Seventeen, and they want to send him to a reprisal camp because of where his grandfather happened to be born. Julian knows. Julian has been trying to move them for months, and he made me promise — made *me*, a girl of nineteen with a forged travel pass and a face that opens doors in two countries — that if anything happened to him, I'd bring it to his sister, because his sister was the only person he knew who could fight paper with paper and win."

Ada finally looked up. Edith had gone very still. Ivo had come forward in his chair without seeming to notice he'd done it.

"You understand," Ada said, and her voice cracked and she hated it and pressed on anyway, "that I am not telling you this to make you sorry for me. I am telling you because everyone in this room keeps treating this as Julian's war. Julian's crime, Julian's letter, Julian's ghost. But I have my own war. My whole life is a war that started without me, on terms I never agreed to, between countries that both claim I don't belong to them. England says I'm German because my father's blood says so. Germany says I'm English because my accent does. Do you know what that makes me? Nothing. Stateless. A person the map forgot. When I crossed from Holland I carried a passport that said *nationality: doubtful*. Those are the actual words. Doubtful. As if I were a banknote someone suspected of forgery."

Her hands were shaking. She folded them hard in her lap.

"Julian understood that," she said, softer now. "That's why he trusted me, in the end. Not because I caught his handwriting — plenty of people could have. Because when he asked me what I'd do if I couldn't go home, I saw in his face that he already knew the answer, because it was his answer too. He couldn't go home. Home had court-martialled him in absentia and buried him at Loos with somebody else's name on the cross. We were two people without a country, sitting in an Antwerp kitchen splitting a loaf, and he said to me — I wrote it down that night so I'd never lose it — he said: *A person without a country must build one out of whoever will stand beside her.*"

The fire had burned low. Nobody fed it. Ada had the sense that the whole room was holding its breath around her words, the way water holds the shape of a stone dropped into it.

"So yes," she said. "I'll help with the Brandts. I'll walk to Dover barefoot if it comes to that. But don't mistake me, Miss Marlowe, and don't mistake yourself. I'm not doing it for England. England interned my father behind wire at Knockaloe and lost his letters for eight months, and when they found them again half were censored into nonsense by some clerk who thought clockmaking diagrams were code. I'm doing it for a man who kept his word to strangers in peacetime, and for a boy named Karl who has done nothing except be born wrong, and for my father, who taught me that a chronometer doesn't care what flag the ship flies — it only cares that the ship gets home."

Silence. Then Edith rose, crossed the room, and did something Ada did not expect: she knelt, awkwardly, joints cracking, a woman of fifty-three lowering herself onto the rug before a girl of nineteen, and took both of Ada's hands in hers.

"Then we shall get them both home," Edith said. "Your father and the Brandts. All of them. I give you my word on it — and unlike my brother, I intend to be alive to keep mine."

* * *

Later — much later, past midnight, when Edith had gone up and the house had gone quiet in that particular way old houses do, a silence full of small creaks like a ship at anchor — Ada found Ivo in the motor shed.

He was under the car with a lamp, doing nothing that needed doing. She had learned that about him too. When his hands needed occupation more than his head needed silence, he found a bolt to polish. She sat down on the workbench across from him, and after a while his boots emerged from beneath the chassis, and then the rest of him, grease to the wrists, blinking at her in the lamplight.

"I wasn't asleep," he said.

"No one accused you."

"I was thinking about what you said. About furniture." He wiped his hands on a rag that only relocated the grease. "I think maybe I've been trying to be furniture too. Good furniture. The kind that drives you where you need to go and never asks why."

"It's not a bad strategy," Ada said. "It kept me alive for four years."

"But it's not living, is it. It's hiding in plain sight." He looked at her then, directly, which he rarely did, and there was something raw and unguarded in his long pale face. "Your father. Do you know where they're keeping him?"

"Knockaloe. Isle of Man. Last letter came in June." She hesitated. This was the part she had told no one, the part that lived under her ribs like a swallowed coal. "Julian promised to find him. After the Brandts were safe — that was always the order, Brandts first, because Karl's deadline was real — he was going to use whatever channels he'd built abroad to trace Papa through the camp records. Internees get moved, you see. Transferred, released, lost. The Red Cross lists are six months behind and full of misspellings. A man could vanish inside that system as completely as Julian vanished inside his own death."

"And if Julian can't do it now—" Ivo began.

"Then someone else must." She slid off the bench. "Which is why tomorrow I want to see every letter Julian ever sent you, Miss Marlowe's suffrage circulars included, everything. If he hid a message network inside a women's political movement, he may have hidden the Vogel inquiry in it too. He was thorough that way. He was thorough the way frightened people are thorough."

Ivo stood among his tools with the rag hanging forgotten from his fist, and she watched a strange expression cross his face — guilt and wonder fighting for the same ground.

"What?" she said.

"Nothing. Only—" He stopped. Started again. "You should know that I read the post. Have done for years. Every sealed letter that comes through this house, I've steamed and read and resealed. I told Miss Marlowe. I'm telling you, because you've just given us the truth of yourself and it seems poor payment to hold anything back."

Ada studied him. Nineteen years of reading pretences stood her in good stead now: she saw the shame sitting on him like wet wool, and beneath it something else, something almost like hope that she would not recoil.

"Do you feel lighter?" she asked.

"Lighter and filthier, both at once."

"Good. Both is honest." She moved toward the door, then paused with her hand on the latch. "For what it's worth, Mr. Underhill — I'm glad you read. A man who reads other people's letters might actually read one properly someday. Most people only look at envelopes."

She was nearly at the door when he spoke again, and something in his voice made her turn.

"There was one," he said slowly. "Months ago. A circular with a pattern in the border that I remember because it made no sense — numbers where there shouldn't have been numbers, tucked into the printer's ornament. I threw it in the basket with the rest. I thought it was a misprint."

Ada's breath went very still.

"A misprint," she repeated. "In a circular. With numbers."

"February's issue. I could probably even find it, if the baskets haven't been burnt."

Ada crossed the shed and took his greasy hand in both of hers, heedless of the smuts, and felt him startle like a horse touched unexpectedly.

"Mr. Underhill," she said, "tomorrow morning you are going to show me February's issue. And if those numbers say what I think they might say, then the first thing we ever truly read together may be the whereabouts of my father."

Behind them, out in the dark of the drive, headlights swept once across the shuttered windows of the motor shed — slow, deliberate, twice — and were gone. Neither of them saw it. Neither of them, yet, knew that Captain Reeve had already begun asking questions in the villages, and that by week's end he would come asking them of Ivo Underhill, politely, in a room with the door shut.

But the lamp in the shed burned on, and two stateless-hearted people leaned together over the shape of a clue, while the night outside drew its circle closer.
