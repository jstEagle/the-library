## 12. The First Summons

The letter came on a Tuesday, which I would later decide was the most insulting thing about it. Not its contents — those were bland as porridge, printed on War Office stationery with a crest like a boiled sweet — but its ordinariness. It arrived in the morning post among the seed catalogues and a circular from the National Union, and it sat there in the wicker basket by the scullery door for nearly an hour while I ate my breakfast and read about turnips.

*Mr. Ivo Underhill is requested to attend an interview at eleven o'clock on Friday morning at the County Police Office, Ashford, respecting certain correspondence passing through his custody.*

*Respecting certain correspondence.* The phrase had been chosen the way one chooses gloves: to cover the hands entirely. Nobody wrote that way unless they had rehearsed it, and nobody rehearsed it unless they wanted something they could not simply take.

I read it twice, then folded it along its original creases and put it in my breast pocket, where it lay against my heart like a coal that hadn't caught yet.

Edith was at her desk when I brought the morning's letters up to the study, and I set the summons down on top of the pile without ceremony, because ceremony would have told her everything. She read it once. Her face did what it always did at moments of consequence — it went very still, very smooth, like water before ice — and then she looked up at me over the rims of her spectacles.

"Friday," she said.

"Friday."

"You'll go."

"I'll go." I heard my own voice come out level and was faintly disgusted by it. "It's only sensible. If I refuse, he comes here instead."

"If you refuse," Edith said, "he learns nothing new but suspects everything old. If you go—" She took off her spectacles and polished them on her sleeve, a habit she fell into when she was buying time. "If you go, you'll be asked questions, and you'll have to decide, in the moment, which ones deserve answers."

"And if I answer wrong?"

"Then we shall know how wrong, and plan accordingly." She said it lightly, but she wasn't smiling. "Ivo. Listen to me. Captain Reeve is not a fool, whatever else he may be. He has been asking after you in Wye and Chilham and God knows where else — the innkeeper's girl mentioned it days ago. He knows you drive me. He knows you handle my mail. What he doesn't know — and must not learn from your face — is that you know more than a driver ought."

"That I've been reading your letters for six years, you mean."

"Don't be glib. Glibness reads as guilt." She put her spectacles back on and looked at me through them, and I had the strange sensation of being both seen and assessed, the way she assessed everything — a bill of lading, a hostile newspaper, a man. "You will be nervous. Be nervous slowly. Nervous men fidget; frightened men bolt. Give him neither."

* * *

The two days between the summons and the interview passed the way a fever passes — in stages, each worse than the last, punctuated by false improvements. On Wednesday I drove Edith to a committee meeting in Canterbury and sat outside in the motor with my collar damp and my hands doing arithmetic on the wheel. On Wednesday night I took out the coded circulars — Julian's circulars, the ones I had carried blind for months, the ones whose cipher Ada had cracked open like a walnut — and read them again, though by now I knew them nearly by heart. *The orchard is late this year. Ask whether the east wall can bear fruit.* Orchard. East wall. A whole geography hidden in horticulture, and I had delivered it door to door like milk.

On Thursday it rained, and I cleaned the car, which is what I do when I cannot pray. I degreased the plugs. I checked the tyres and found them sound and resented them for it. Mrs. Pask, who cooked for us and missed nothing, came out to the shed with a plate of seed cake and stood watching me work with her arms folded into her apron.

"You've got a face on you," she said.

"It's the weather."

"It's never the weather with men. Men get faces when they've done something, or when they're going to, or when someone's done something to them." She set the cake down on the workbench with a click. "Whichever it is, eat something first. No one thinks clearly hungry, and no one lies well with an empty stomach."

She left before I could decide whether she'd meant the second part as instruction or prophecy.

That night I did not sleep so much as lie in the dark rehearsing, which is its own kind of lying — practising untruths against the ceiling until they wore smooth. I constructed myself a modest man with a modest memory. A man who drove, and fetched, and signed receipts, and noticed nothing beyond the state of the roads. I gave him a flat voice and a short attention span. I dressed him in my body and sent him to bed, and in the morning I put him on like a coat, and it fit better than anything I owned.

* * *

Ashford at eleven o'clock on a Friday in November was all wet stone and horse steam. The County Police Office squatted behind its iron railings with the air of a building that had been disappointed since the reign of George III. Inside, a sergeant with a moustache like a hearth-brush took my name, looked at me a long moment longer than names require, and said, "Captain Reeve's expecting you. Upstairs, end of the corridor. Don't keep him waiting; he's particular about clocks."

The stairs smelled of floor wax and old tobacco. Somewhere below, a cell door shut with a sound like a book closing.

Captain Reeve's office held a desk, two chairs, a map of the county pinned to the wall with red thread strung between pins — I counted the threads without meaning to, and stopped counting when I recognised Marlowe House — and a clock, black and round and loud, ticking away the seconds as though it were being paid by the hour. Reeve himself stood at the window with his back to me, looking out at nothing much, and let me stand there a full half-minute before turning.

He was younger than I'd expected, and more tired. That unsettled me more than menace would have. There are men who wear authority like armour and men who wear it like a borrowed coat that doesn't quite hang right, and Reeve was the second kind, though his voice had learned to impersonate the first.

"Mr. Underhill." We shook hands. His grip was dry and brief. "Sit down. Thank you for coming promptly — I find promptness tells a man's character better than any reference."

"What does it tell you about mine?"

"That you're either innocent or well-rehearsed." He smiled as he said it, a small administrative smile, and sat, and opened a folder that had been squared to the desk's edge with geometric precision. "This won't take long, I hope. I'm investigating irregularities in the postal traffic associated with certain persons in this county. Persons of public profile. You act as driver and courier to Mrs. Edith Marlowe."

"Miss Marlowe," I said, and immediately hated myself for the pedantry, because it sounded exactly like what it was: a man reaching for solid ground.

"Miss Marlowe." He made a small note. "How long?"

"Six years. Nearly seven."

"And in that time, you've handled her correspondence."

"I drive her to meetings. I carry letters between her and the other organisers. Receipts, minutes, that sort of thing." All true. Every word true, and every word arranged like furniture in front of a door.

"You sign for registered items?"

"When they need signing."

"Have you ever signed for an item addressed to Miss Marlowe that struck you as unusual?" He looked up from the folder then, and his eyes were pale grey and perfectly patient, the eyes of a man prepared to sit in this chair until the heat death of the universe. "Unusual in origin, address, handwriting, weight, smell — I ask about smell because ink and chemicals travel oddly, Mr. Underhill. Anything your attention snagged on."

"My attention," I said, "snags on potholes and weather."

The clock ticked. Somewhere in the building a telephone rang and was answered and went quiet again, a whole unseen life happening one floor down.

Reeve turned a page. "There is a letter," he said. "Dated the fourth of September, dispatched from Folkestone, addressed to Miss Marlowe in a hand we believe to be disguised. It passed through your custody on or about the sixth. Do you recall it?"

And here is the thing I will carry to whatever grave is allotted me: I did recall it. Of course I recalled it. I recalled every letter I had ever read, filed away in the attic of my skull like a magpie's hoard, and this one — the one I had dismissed months ago as nonsense, the one with the nonsense about orchards and east walls that was not nonsense at all — rose in my memory with terrible clarity, down to the cheap paper and the stamp placed slightly crooked.

"No," I said. "September's a busy month. Harvest fares, the rally season winding down. Letters blur together."

"They do," Reeve agreed pleasantly. "Yours especially, I imagine. Six years of letters. Thousands of them." He closed the folder. Opened it again. Turned another page, unhurried, letting the silence do work that questions couldn't. "Tell me about yourself, Mr. Underhill. Where were you before Miss Marlowe?"

"Grooming stables near Tenterden. Before that, my father's cartage business. Before that I was a boy."

"No military service?"

"I was rejected. Heart murmur." This was true too, and I watched him write it down and understood that truth was the best material for lying — that a lie built entirely of true things could bear almost any weight. I felt something shift in my chest, some structural element giving way with a soft internal sound that no one else in the room could hear. It did not hurt. That was the horrifying part. It didn't hurt at all.

"The suffrage movement," Reeve said, still writing, "attracts passionate people. Passionate people make mistakes. They conceal things — not always from wickedness. Often from loyalty. Loyalty is a very human failing." He looked up. "Are you a loyal man, Mr. Underhill?"

"To whom?"

"Whoever employs you, I should think. Whoever trusts you." He laid down his pen. "Because here is my difficulty, Mr. Underhill, and I'll be plain with you, since plainness costs nothing. Correspondence connected to Miss Marlowe has been appearing in places it shouldn't. Coded material. Material relating to a matter I am not at liberty to name, involving a person officially deceased, which — you'll forgive me — makes the paperwork extremely interesting." He folded his hands on the folder. "Someone is carrying that correspondence. Someone with access, mobility, and a plausible reason to be on the roads of Kent several times a week. I have a list of such someones. Your name is on it. It is not at the top. But lists shorten."

The clock ticked. Rain had started against the window without either of us noticing, fine and diagonal, ticking a second clock of its own.

"Captain Reeve," I said, and my voice came out in the flat modest register I had built for it, worn smooth against the ceiling on Thursday night, "I drive a motor-car. I carry envelopes. If there's a code in them, it's wasted on me — I barely passed scripture at school, and that was the vicar marking generously. You say a dead man's writing is turning up in Kent. That sounds like either a cruel joke or a bad novel, and I've no opinion to offer on either, except that I'd want to read the novel." I paused, and let a little honest bewilderment leak through, because I had learned in the last forty seconds that bewilderment was a currency and I had plenty in reserve. "Is that all? Only — the car wants petrol before noon, and Miss Marlowe keeps a schedule you could set music to."

For a long moment Reeve simply looked at me. And I want to record, honestly, what happened inside me during that look, because afterwards I kept returning to it as one returns to a bruise. Nothing happened. That was what frightened me. Some inner courtroom that had operated all my life — the one that winced when I shortchanged a farmer, that made me return a dropped purse in Ashford market when I was fourteen, that had finally forced me to confess my reading to Edith with my ears burning — that court convened, considered the evidence, and adjourned without comment. The lie sat in the room between us, complete and comfortable, wearing my face, using my voice, and I felt nothing but a mild professional interest in whether it would hold.

It held.

"All right, Mr. Underhill." Reeve stood, and shook my hand again, dryly. "Thank you for your time. You'll understand if I don't apologise for the inconvenience. Inconvenience is most of my trade." At the door he added, conversationally, as though remarking on the weather: "The roads to the coast are poor this time of year. If you find yourself driving that way — Hythe way, Folkestone way — mind the checkpoints. There's been some reorganisation. New men. New questions." He smiled his administrative smile. "Drive carefully, Mr. Underhill. Men like you are rare. Good drivers are hard to replace."

* * *

I came down the stairs with my knees working on their own recognisance and pushed out through the railings into the rain, and stood a moment under the office's grim portico breathing the wet stone smell, and discovered that my hands were shaking — now, when it no longer mattered, now that the performance was over and the audience gone. My body, apparently, had been paying attention the entire time and had waited politely for the curtain.

I walked to the garage where the car was lodged, and paid for petrol, and checked the tyres, and did all the small mechanical devotions, and all the while the knowledge settled onto me by degrees, the way cold settles into a coat — not all at once but layer by layer, shoulder, back, chest — until I could not tell anymore where the coat ended and I began.

I had lied. Not evaded, not kept silent, not told a technical truth with crossed fingers — lied, fluently, with texture and detail and a comic flourish about scripture class, to an officer of the Crown conducting a lawful inquiry, and the astonishing thing, the thing I kept turning over like a coin with a strange face, was how *good* it had been. First attempt. No rehearsal beyond a sleepless night and Mrs. Pask's unsolicited coaching. Somewhere in me there had been this capacity all along, folded up like a map in a glovebox, and Captain Reeve had unfolded it in twenty minutes, and now it would never fold back to its original creases.

Edith was waiting at Marlowe House when I returned, though she pretended to be at her desk with the accounts. I stood in the doorway dripping and told her all of it — the folder, the red-threaded map, the letter of the fourth of September, the question about loyalty, the warning about the coast roads. She listened without interrupting, which is her particular discipline, and when I finished she was quiet a long moment.

"He showed you the map," she said at last. "Deliberately. He wanted you to see the threads."

"Why?"

"So that you'd carry the sight home. So that you'd know he knows where the house sits, and tell me so, and I'd feel the walls thinning." She took off her spectacles. "He's not gathering information today, Ivo. He's distributing fear."

"Well. It was accepted."

"Yes." She studied me, and whatever she saw made her set down her pen. "You lied to him."

It wasn't a question. I could have denied it — I had, apparently, the equipment — but I was tired of my own smoothness, tired of hearing the flat modest voice come out of me even in this room, with her.

"Yes."

"How did it feel?"

I thought about telling her it felt awful, which was partly true, or that it felt like nothing, which was also partly true, and which she would understand better. In the end I said: "It felt easy. That's what frightens me. It felt like a skill I've been carrying around unused, like — like a spare tyre. And now it's been used, and it's been driven on, and it isn't spare anymore."

Edith came round the desk and stood close, and for once she did not lecture or strategise. She just looked up at me with her tired fierce eyes and said, "Then we had better make sure it's used only for things worth lying for. That's the whole moral problem of war, Ivo, compressed into a motor garage. Everyone finds out what they'll lie for. The lucky ones find out early, and choose."

From the hall below came the sound of the front bell, and then Mrs. Pask's footsteps, and then — raised, careful, carrying — the sergeant's hearth-brush voice announcing a caller. Not the police this time. A woman, asking after the household, giving a name that meant nothing to Mrs. Pask and everything to me, because it was the name of the innkeeper's niece at Hythe, and we had met her exactly once, on a dark evening, and never by daylight.

Ada appeared at the foot of the stairs with the visitor's card in her hand, and her face had gone white as the card itself.

"Ivo," she said. "You need to see this."
