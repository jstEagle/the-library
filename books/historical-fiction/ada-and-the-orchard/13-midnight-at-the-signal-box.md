## 13. Midnight at the Signal Box

The message came on a Tuesday, folded inside a suffrage circular about the Cat and Mouse Act, and it was Ivo who found it — which was fitting, Ada thought later, since he was the one who had taught himself to see what other people hid.

She watched him read it at the kitchen table of Marlowe House, his thumb moving down the column of printed text about hunger strikes and prison feeding, his lips slightly parted. Then he stopped. She saw the change come over him the way weather comes over a field: first a stillness, then a darkening, then something moving through that could not be taken back.

"Here," he said. His voice was strange, as though it had traveled a long way to reach them. "The third paragraph. Read the initial letters."

Edith took the circular from him. Her reading glasses sat low on her nose; she pushed them up with one finger, an old woman's gesture she would have denied under oath. "M. N. I. G. H. T." She looked up. "*Midnight.*"

"The next line," Ada said, because she had already worked it out in her head while Ivo's mouth was still forming the word, and because saying it aloud made it more real, and because real was what she wanted now more than she wanted safety. "Signal box. Ashford line. The small box past the halt — not the junction."

"How do you know it's not the junction?" Edith asked.

"Because Julian always said the junction had eyes. He used to say it when I was twelve years old, sitting in Frau Brandt's kitchen, doing sums. *The junction has eyes, Ada, but the little boxes are blind.*" She heard herself say Frau Brandt's name and felt it land in the room like a stone dropped into still water. Edith's face changed — softened, then hardened again, grief and resolve arriving together like two trains on the same track.

"Tonight, then," Edith said.

* * *

They did not speak much on the drive. That was the thing about Kent at night, Ada thought, pressed into the corner of the motor car with her coat buttoned to her chin: the county emptied itself after dark. The lanes went narrow and black, hedgerows leaning in like conspirators, and the only sounds were the engine's effort and Ivo's breathing beside her, deliberate and even, the breathing of a man keeping himself calm by force.

He drove without lamps. He had doused them a mile out of the village, and now they moved through the dark on moonlight alone, which fell thin and silver over the stubble fields, enough to show the pale ribbon of road and nothing else. Once a nightjar called from a copse, that churring wooden sound, and once a dog barked somewhere far off and fell silent as if scolded.

Ada held the circular in her glove. She had copied the cipher key onto a scrap of paper and burned the original in the kitchen grate, watching the printed words about women's freedom curl and go orange and become nothing. It seemed to her there was a lesson in that — that all the grand printed causes in the world could be made to vanish in a second, but a message written for one person, hidden in the margins of another message, survived everything. Love letters did that too. Her mother had kept love letters through three borders and two wars, sewn into a hem. Paper hid best when it pretended to be about something else.

"You're shivering," Ivo said. He didn't look at her; his eyes stayed on the road.

"I'm not cold."

"All right." A pause. "You're shivering anyway."

She almost laughed. There was something in his flatness that steadied her — the way he stated things rather than offered them, as if the world were a ledger and every fact had its place. "I'm frightened," she admitted. "Is that what you want in the ledger?"

"I don't keep a ledger," he said. But his hands tightened on the wheel, and she understood that he did — that behind his grey quietness he was counting everything, filing everything, and that tonight the file marked *Julian Marlowe* had been opened for good.

Behind them, Edith sat straight-backed in the rear seat like a woman attending her own trial. Ada caught her reflection in the small square of the rear window: the set jaw, the hat pinned firmly against any wind, the eyes fixed forward. Twice during the drive her lips moved silently, rehearsing something. Ada wondered if she was practicing what she would say to her brother, if — if — this night proved anything at all. Two years of mourning. Two years of a grave in France and a telegram framed on the mantel like a saint's icon. And underneath it all, Ada suspected, two years of a sister's stubborn refusal to believe what the War Office told her, because Edith Marlowe had spent her whole life refusing to believe men who told her where the limits of truth lay.

"There," Ivo said.

The signal box rose out of the dark like a sentry box abandoned mid-war. It stood on stilts above the single line, wooden, lean, its windows reflecting no light because there was no light to reflect. Beyond it the rails ran straight into darkness toward Ashford, and the telegraph wires overhead hummed faintly in the wind with conversations neither of them would ever hear. Somewhere down the line a goods train passed, a long rolling thunder, and the ground carried it up through their shoes.

Ivo brought the car to a stop beneath a hawthorn and killed the engine. The silence afterward was enormous.

"No lamp," he murmured. "We'll take the torch but not light it until we must. Miss Marlowe — perhaps you'd—"

"If you tell me to wait in the car, Mr. Underhill, I shall walk to the signal box ahead of you and be standing under it when you arrive."

A silence. Then, unexpectedly, Ivo laughed — a short, surprised sound, quickly suppressed, as if laughter were another thing he filed away rather than displayed. "Understood," he said.

They crossed the field in single file, Ada in front because she knew the instructions and refused to surrender that small authority. The grass was long and wet and dragged at their ankles. The rails gleamed faintly when the moon came from behind cloud, two parallel lines of silver running toward a vanishing point, and Ada thought absurdly of perspective drawings she had done as a girl, how everything converges on a point you cannot reach.

The ladder was iron and bitterly cold through her gloves. She climbed, counting rungs, and hauled herself onto the platform outside the locked door of the box itself. Below, the levers' housing jutted; above, the roof dripped last week's rain. Everything smelled of creosote and rust and cold iron.

"The instructions said—" she began, and stopped, because Edith had gone very still.

"Under the sill," Edith said quietly. "There's a loose board under the window sill. There always was. Julian and I used to leave notes for each other here when we were children, before Papa forbade the railway. We'd cycle out from home and— " She broke off. Her voice had changed entirely; it was young suddenly, and cracked. "He'd have remembered. Even now. He'd have remembered our place."

Ada knelt. Her fingers walked along beneath the sill, finding spider silk, old bird lime, a nail head worn smooth — and then the give of a board that was not fastened to anything. She prised it up.

Inside the cavity, wrapped in oilcloth tied with string, was a parcel the size of a loaf of bread.

No one moved. Above them the wind worried the telegraph wires, humming that thin endless note. Ada became aware of her own heart, loud and stupid in her ears, and of Edith's breath, quick and shallow, and of Ivo standing at the top of the ladder like a man guarding a door that existed nowhere but in the air.

"Take it down," Edith said. Her voice had found itself again, hard and level. "Not here. Down. We read it in the car."

* * *

But of course they did not wait for the car. They got as far as the field's edge, where the hawthorn made a pocket of deeper black, and there Edith said, "Now," in a tone that permitted no argument, and Ivo unhooded the electric torch with his coat thrown over it so that only a coin of light escaped, cupped between his hands like something stolen.

Ada unwrapped the oilcloth. Her fingers shook and she let them.

Money first — banknotes, soft with handling, English notes mixed with French, and beneath them papers: a passport in a name that was not Julian Marlowe, a ration card, a travel permit stamped with officialdom's dull authority, all of it forged or bought or begged, all of it evidence of a life being lived somewhere by someone. And beneath those—

A photograph.

It was a studio portrait, the kind done cheaply in any town in any country: a plain backdrop, a plain chair, a man seated with his hands on his knees. Bearded. Grey coming into the beard at the temples. Thinner than the Julian of the family photographs Edith kept in the drawing room, the laughing young officer in uniform, the boy on the bicycle. This man's face was lined around the eyes. He looked directly into the camera with an expression that was not smiling and not sad — the expression of a man who has decided to be photographed because someone needs proof, and who understands exactly what the proof will cost him.

Ada held it out without a word. Some things should pass hand to hand, she thought. Some things should be witnessed in the flesh.

Edith took the photograph in both hands, the way one takes communion.

For a long moment she said nothing at all, and Ada, watching her, felt her throat close. She had expected weeping, or the sharp gasp of joy, some theatrical break. Instead Edith simply looked, and looked, and her face did something Ada had no language for — it aged and grew young simultaneously, grief and relief occupying the same features, arguing, merging, until what remained was not happiness but something fiercer: certainty. Proof transforming doubt into mission, thought Ada. Proof turning a mourning sister back into the woman who chained herself to railings.

"It's him," Edith said at last. Very quietly. "It's him. Look at the left ear — the scar, from the fall at Margate when he was nine. And the hands. Mama always said he had Papa's hands." She touched the photograph's edge with one fingertip, feather-light, as though it might bruise. "Two years. Two years I lit candles for a dead man and he was walking about with a beard, having his picture taken."

"He wanted you to have it," Ada said. "He said — he wrote, in the first letter — *show her my face, so she knows it is not a ghost writing to her.*"

Edith turned the photograph over. On the back, in pencil, in that handwriting Ada knew now better than her own — the sloping capitals, the distinctive g — was a single line:

*I am sorry it is this way. Tell her the promise stands. — T.H.*

T.H. Thomas Harrow. The false name. The life lived in enemy country under borrowed letters.

"He signs the false name even to me," Edith said. There was wonder in it, and something harder underneath. "Even now. Because the name keeps him alive, and the promise needs him alive." She lifted her head, and in the hooded coin of torchlight her eyes were dry and burning. "The promise stands. Do you understand what that means, Ada? It means he means to see it kept. It means whatever he asks us to do next — the Brandts, your father, all of it — he intends us to do it *now*, while he can still send messages. Before the net closes."

"The net," Ivo said.

Ada heard it too, then — or rather heard its absence. Far down the line toward Ashford, where the junction slept with its eyes open, a whistle sounded. Not a train whistle. Shorter. Sharper. Repeated twice.

Then silence, and the wind in the wires, and the three of them crouched in a hawthorn's shadow holding a dead man's face in their hands.

"That's a police whistle," Ivo said. His voice had gone completely flat, completely calm, and Ada understood in that instant that this was what he was for — that all his grey quietness had been preparation for precisely this kind of night. "Or a porter's. It could be nothing. It could be a late train being signaled through."

"And it could be a man named Reeve learning to ask questions in villages," Edith said. She slid the photograph inside her coat, against her breast, with a deliberateness that made Ada think of women hiding bread in occupied countries. "Mr. Underhill. Start the motor. Ada — the papers, wrap them, we take everything. If anyone asks, we are three fools whose motor failed on the way home from Canterbury, and I am prepared to be magnificent about it."

Ada was already retying the oilcloth. Her hands had stopped shaking; she noticed this with a kind of distant surprise, the way one notices rain stopping. Something had finished in her during those minutes under the signal box — some long argument between hope and disbelief — and what remained on the far side of it was not fear but direction. For two years she had carried letters for a man the world called dead, half-believing she was mad, half-believing she was a courier in a story too large for her. Now she had seen his beard going grey, his father's hands on his knees. Now it was not a story. It was work.

And work, unlike stories, could be finished.

The engine caught on the second try, coughing in the cold. Ivo swung the car round without lamps, easing back onto the lane, and as they pulled away Ada looked back once at the signal box standing blind and faithful over its empty rails, its little loose board already replaced, its cavity already holding nothing but spider silk and the memory of what it had carried.

Down the line, faintly, the whistle sounded again. Closer. Or perhaps only the wind.

Nobody spoke for a mile. Then Edith, from the back seat, in a voice stripped of everything but intent:

"Mr. Underhill. In the morning, drive me to Dover. I want to know what ships sail, and who owns them, and what they charge. If my brother is asking us to move a family across the water, then we shall learn the water's price — before Captain Reeve learns ours."

In the darkness Ada reached sideways without deciding to, and found Ivo's sleeve, and gripped it. He did not pull away. The road ran on ahead of them, black and narrow and full of turns, and somewhere behind them the night held its watchers, and somewhere ahead of them the sea waited with all its crossings and all its costs — and for the first time since she had knocked on the gate at Marlowe House, rain-soaked and afraid, Ada felt not like a stranger carrying other people's secrets, but like one soldier among three, marching toward the same war.
