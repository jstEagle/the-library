## 14. The Brandt Letters

The package had sat on Edith's desk all morning like a sleeping animal she was afraid to wake.

It had come out of the signal box at Wye the night before — a canvas-wrapped bundle tied with tarred string, smelling of coal smoke and river mud and something faintly sweet beneath it, like fruit going soft in a closed room. Ada had carried it in against her chest with both arms, the way one carries an infant or a wounded bird, and had set it down on the green baize without a word. Then all three of them had stood around it in the lamplight of the study, three people who twelve months ago were strangers, none of them willing to be the first to touch it.

Ivo had cut the string eventually. Of course he had — Ivo, whose hands knew knots the way a priest's hands know prayer. But even he had only loosened it, folded back the canvas at one corner, looked inside, and gone very still.

"Money," he'd said. "Papers. A photograph." A pause, long enough for the fire to shift and settle. "And letters. Quite a lot of letters."

Edith had said, "Tomorrow," and hated herself for saying it, but grief and hope between them had emptied her entirely, and she could not have read another word that night if the house had been burning around her ears. She had dreamed instead of Julian at fourteen, standing in this same study with a dead thrush cupped in his hands, asking her whether things could be un-killed if you were sorry enough. She had woken before dawn with her heart hammering and lain watching the grey climb the ceiling, thinking: *today I will find out what my brother's survival costs.*

Now the morning was half spent and the package still waited.

She made herself ready for it as though for a funeral. She dressed with care — the high-collared grey, her good brooch, her hair pinned so severely it hurt a little at the temples. Armour, her mother would have called it. She had learned young that a woman about to receive bad news should look like someone who intended to survive it.

When she came down, Ada was already in the study, sitting upright on the edge of the chair nearest the window, hands folded, spine straight as a plumb line. The girl had a way of arranging herself when she was frightened — making herself small and correct, a schoolgirl presenting homework to a headmistress who might strike her. Edith had seen her do it the first day at the gate, soaked through, clutching that impossible letter. She saw it now.

"You needn't sit like a defendant," Edith said gently. "Whatever's in there, we read it together."

Ada's chin lifted a fraction. "I've read them already. On the train. I read them forty times." Her voice caught and steadied. "I wanted you to hear them from me. In case — in case the writing is difficult. His German is much better than his English."

*His.* Edith felt the word go through her like a splinter sliding home. Two years of *Julian is dead*; two years of the black-edged note from the War Office, of the memorial service at St. Dunstan's where the vicar had spoken of *a life given*, of her mother's grave in the churchyard with *also of Julian* cut into its stone in anticipation of a death that had never happened. And now: *his German.*

"Yes," Edith said. "Tell me."

Ivo came in with the tea tray — he had taken to doing such things without being asked, another of the quiet rearrangements the past weeks had worked on him — set it down within reach of all three of them, and took his place by the cold fireplace, standing, arms folded, a witness rather than a participant. He had not slept either. She could see it in the bruised skin under his eyes, in the way he kept his gaze fixed on the middle distance as though looking directly at any of them might cost him something.

Edith drew the package across the baize and untied the string the rest of the way.

The canvas fell open. Inside: a leather wallet thick with banknotes, English and French together, soft with handling; a passport in the name of Anton Weiss that could not possibly belong to anyone named Anton Weiss because it bore Julian's photograph — bearded, older, hollowed at the cheeks but unmistakably, impossibly alive; and beneath these, a packet of letters bound with a bootlace, perhaps two dozen sheets in all, written in several hands, some in English, most in a careful, angular German script.

Ada reached forward and touched the top letter, just touched it, fingertips resting on the paper as though it might startle.

"The Brandts," she said. "Herr Brandt and his wife, and the boy, Emil. They were our neighbours — in the town where I lived, before." Before, Edith noted, was where Ada put everything unbearable. "Julian lodged with us two years. He and Herr Brandt used to play chess in the garden on Sundays, and Frau Brandt fed him until he got fat, and Emil — Emil adored him. Followed him everywhere. Julian taught him English songs with the words wrong on purpose so he'd laugh." She stopped. Started again. "In 1911, war seemed — everyone talked about it the way you talk about weather coming. Julian said to Herr Brandt, one evening, in front of me: *if it comes, you will send word to my sister, and whatever it costs, we will get you out.* He said it the way he said everything, as though it were already done. Herr Brandt laughed at him. Said England would never let them in, Germans being Germans. Julian said, *then we shall be smugglers.*" A ghost of a smile crossed her face and vanished. "He was joking. Half joking."

"And now?" Edith asked quietly.

"Now they are not laughing."

* * *

The first letter was in English, in a cramped hand that pressed so hard it had nearly torn the paper. Edith smoothed it flat on her desk and read aloud, because Ada had asked her to, and because her own voice seemed somehow safer than silence — if she was speaking, the words were happening to her in order, one at a time, and could not all fall on her at once.

> *Honoured Fräulein Marlowe,— Forgive an old man writing to you whom you have never met. Your brother spoke of you often and always with love, which is why I permit myself this liberty, and why I trust you with what follows, though it shames me to ask it.*

Edith paused. *Spoke of you often and always with love.* She had to set the letter down for a moment. Across the room, Ivo shifted his weight, and Ada watched her with those dark, patient eyes that had already read every word and were waiting, again, for the blow to land.

She took up the letter and went on.

> *You will know that since August 1914 we have lived as aliens in your country — registered, fingerprinted, forbidden the coast and forbidden the railway, obliged each week to present ourselves at the police station like criminals, though our crime is only the place of our birth. We bore it. One bears a great deal for the sake of one's children. My wife baked bread for the village until they forbade her to sell to English households. Emil grew four inches and learned to keep his mouth shut, which is a terrible thing to teach a boy of sixteen.*

> *But now the order has come. All male enemy aliens of military age are to be removed to the internment camp — and not the ordinary camp at Knockaloe, honourable Fräulein, but the reprisal compound at the new depot, where men are sent in answer to what the Germans do to *their* prisoners. Repatriation, the notice calls it. It means my son will be locked behind wire among desperate men, guarded by soldiers instructed to make his condition a message to Berlin. He is sixteen. He weighs eight stone. He stutters when he is frightened, and he is frightened now all the time.*

> *Your brother promised. I laughed at him once, God forgive me. I do not laugh now.*

> *There is a boat. There are people who, for money, ask no questions — your brother knows them, and has sent this letter to prove he knows them still. What we lack is the permission that cannot be bought: papers in a name that will bear inspection, and a hand at the top of the ladder, so to speak, someone whose word carries weight on your side of the water. Your brother says — and here I beg you to believe me, though I know how it must sound —*

Here the handwriting faltered, the pen dragging, and resumed larger, looser, almost pleading.

> *— your brother says that you are the bravest woman in England, and that if anyone can bring a family of Germans safely out of a country at war with Germany, it is you.*

> *We have eleven days. After the twelfth of December, Emil goes where no letter can follow him.*

> *I am, madam, with respect and desperation equally, yours,— Otto Brandt*

The study was silent except for the fire and, distantly, a rook arguing somewhere over the bare orchard.

Edith laid the letter down. Her hands were steady — she made them be steady — but behind her sternum her heart was going like a telegraph key, tapping out a message she could not yet decipher. Eleven days. A boy of sixteen. A reprisal camp, that phrase the newspapers used with a certain relish, as though reprisal were a form of weather rather than a thing done deliberately to human bodies.

"There are more," Ada said softly. "Frau Brandt wrote too. And Emil wrote one himself — it's at the bottom. Julian marked it. He said—" Ada's voice thinned. "He said, *read the boy's last. It's the one that decides it.*"

* * *

They read Frau Brandt's letter next, and it was shorter and worse, because mothers do not trouble with preamble. She thanked Edith. She apologised for the trouble. She described, in the flat manner of women cataloguing catastrophe, how Emil had been turned away from the bakery where he'd had work, how neighbours who had known him all his life now crossed the street, how the boy had begun sleeping with his boots on. *If they take him,* she wrote, *I do not think my husband survives it, and I say this not to move you but so that you understand what is actually at stake, since polite persons are always imagining that matters are smaller than they are.*

"She sounds like you," Ivo said, before he could stop himself.

Edith looked up sharply — then found, to her considerable annoyance, that her eyes were hot. "Thank you," she said, with asperity that fooled neither of them, and heard him exhale something that might have been a laugh or an apology.

Then the last letter. Emil's.

It was written in English — laborious, careful English, each letter formed like a child's copybook exercise, with small pencil corrections above the lines where someone, surely the mother, had corrected the spelling. Edith read it silently first, unable to speak it, and then, because Ada deserved to hear it given its proper weight and because Ivo had gone rigid by the fireplace, she read it aloud.

> *Dear Miss Marlowe. I am sorry my writing is bad. Father says you will help us and I must not hope too much but I am hoping anyway, I hope you will forgive it. I was born in your country, did Julian tell you? Not in Kent but in Hull, where father had the shipping office, till we moved. I have an accent only when I am tired. At school they called me Dutchy which is wrong twice, I am not Dutch and Holland is a different country, I used to say so and then I learned to stop saying so.*

> *I do not want to go to the camp. That is the whole of my letter. Everyone says I must write something more than that but I have thought hard and that is what it comes to. I do not want to go. I would like to see an orchard again someday. There was one behind our old house with greengages, nobody grows greengages anymore, Julian said your garden had them and I thought of it all last winter.*

> *Father says courage is not minding. But I mind. Please forgive me. Yours respectfully, Emil Brandt*

Edith set the page down very carefully, aligning its edges with the others, because her hands wanted to shake and she would not let them.

*I would like to see an orchard again someday.*

Outside the window, the old orchard at Marlowe House stood leafless and patient, its apple trees planted by her grandfather, heavy every September with fruit that went into pies and cider and sacks given away at the gate. Greengages along the south wall. She had walked under those trees ten thousand times without once thinking of them as anything but scenery, background, the given world. Somewhere a boy was memorising their existence as a reason to stay alive.

"Well," she said. Her voice came out level. She was almost proud of it. "Eleven days."

* * *

Ivo spoke first, which surprised her. "It can't be done." He said it quickly, the way a man sets down something hot. "Not legally. You know it can't. No Home Office in wartime grants exit papers to a German male of military age — they're requisitioning rowing boats off the beach, Edith, they fined a fisherman last month for taking his own dinghy past the buoy. If we apply through channels, the channels ask questions, and the questions lead to Julian, and Julian leads to—" He stopped. He didn't finish it. He didn't need to; they all carried the same map in their heads now, the same web of threads: Julian, desertion, court-martial, and Edith herself at the centre of it, her name, her platform, her movement, all of it hanging from a single cut thread.

"Not legally," Edith agreed.

"Then illegally, and illegal means smuggling, and smuggling means the kind of men who take money and ask no questions also take money and tell stories. One arrest anywhere in the chain and it isn't just Emil in the dock. It's all of us. Ada's already flagged at the ports. I'm known to Reeve. And you—" He hesitated over her name, and she watched him decide, deliberately, to use it. "Edith. If they connect you to moving an enemy alien out of the country, they won't need a trial. They'll simply end you. The movement, the newspaper access, the platforms — gone. They'd call it security. Half your own supporters would call it patriotism."

"Ivo." She waited until he met her eyes. "Do you imagine I haven't done that arithmetic? I have been doing it since before you finished speaking, and I will still be doing it tonight, and tomorrow, and every day after, because that is what conscience is — arithmetic that never balances." She rose and went to the window, to the orchard, to the boy's greengages. "But hear me. When I was twenty-two I stood on a platform in Manchester and said that a law is not the same thing as justice, and a man in the crowd shouted that I'd feel differently when I had sons, and I said — I remember it exactly — I said, *justice is not a favour I extend to my own household.*"

She turned back to face them. "This is the test of that sentence. It is easy to mean such words in the abstract. The abstract is where cowards live comfortably. Julian made a promise in peacetime, in a garden, over chess, half joking — *then we shall be smugglers* — and promises made in peacetime are the only ones worth anything, because they are made before fear arrives to renegotiate the terms." Her voice roughened despite her. "My brother is alive. Do you both understand what that has done to me? For two years I grieved a man who has been eating breakfast, growing a beard, playing chess, *living* — and I have not yet decided whether to be furious with him or grateful, and I suspect I shall die without deciding. But that is mine to carry. Emil Brandt's burden is not mine, and I will not add it to his."

Ada made a sound — small, quickly suppressed — and pressed her knuckles to her mouth.

"So," Edith said briskly, because briskness was a tool like any other and now was the moment for it. "Eleven days. We do not have legal options, therefore we have the other kind, and the other kind requires three things: a route, papers that survive inspection, and a way to keep Captain Reeve looking elsewhere while we move. Ivo — your innkeeper at Hythe. How quickly can he get word to Julian's people?"

"He said messages travel faster than goods." Ivo's jaw worked. "Edith — I'll drive wherever you want, whenever you want, that's not in question. But I want it said plainly, once, in this room: this is the road to prison if it goes wrong. For all of us."

"It is," Edith agreed. "And it is the road to a reprisal camp if we don't walk it. Those are the only two roads, Ivo. Everything else is standing still and calling it virtue."

He held her gaze a long moment. Something in his face shifted — she had watched this man for weeks now, watched the careful detachment crack and reform, crack and reform, and she recognised what she was seeing: the last of the old Ivo, the one who believed history belonged to other people, finally losing its footing.

"All right," he said quietly. "I'll saddle up the car."

"One more thing." Edith gathered the Brandt letters, squared them, and looked at Ada. "Emil writes that he hopes to see an orchard again. When this is done — however it is done — I intend that he shall see this one. Write that down somewhere, Ada. Put it with the promise. Some things must be recorded in advance so that we cannot later pretend we never meant them."

Ada nodded, blinking hard, and reached for her notebook.

It was at that moment that the bell sounded at the front door — two rings, crisp and official, nothing like the hesitant pull of a friend — and through the study window Edith saw, coming up the gravel drive in the flat grey light, a motor car she knew, and a man stepping down from it with his cap in his hand and his eyes already on the windows.

Captain Reeve had arrived. Alone, unannounced, smiling.

"Out the back," Edith said, very low, sweeping the Brandt letters into the suffrage archives with one motion — history's paperwork hiding inside history's paperwork. "Ivo, the side gate. Ada — kettle on. We are three women having tea." A beat. "Well. Two, and a driver."

She heard Ivo's breath catch — half terror, half laughter — and thought: *so that's what courage sounds like in this house now.* Then she squared her shoulders, pinned on her hostess smile like the brooch, and went to answer the door.
