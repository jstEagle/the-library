## 15. An Enemy's Face

The farm lay a mile off the Ashford road, down a lane so narrow the hedges scraped both doors of the motor like a man patting his pockets for change. I had driven that lane twice before in my life and both times had been delivering nothing more sinister than seed catalogues, and I remember thinking — as the hedges closed over the bonnet and the light went green and dripping — that this was the sort of place where nothing happened. That was my old creed, of course. History happened elsewhere. It happened in London drawing rooms with policemen at the door; it happened on the Somme, which by then was less a river than a word you learned to say without flinching. It did not happen down farm lanes in Kent where the mud was the same brown mud it had been before August 1914.

I have since made a study of being wrong.

Edith sat beside me with the Brandt letters folded into her glove, and Ada leaned forward between us from the back seat, giving directions in the low, certain voice she used when she was frightened and had decided not to show it. *Past the oast houses. Past the fallen elm. The gate with the broken top bar.* She had never been to this farm. Julian had described it to her twice, in a room in another country, and her memory held what mine would not have held in twenty years: the exact angle of the gate, the fact that the farmer's dog answered to a whistle rather than a name, the number of steps up to the milking shed. I understood then, in a small cold way, that she had memorised a man's words because those words were all she had of him, the way other people keep locks of hair.

"You'll wait by the car," Edith said to me, and it was not quite an instruction and not quite a request. We had settled into that grammar over the past weeks, Edith and I. Ten years I had driven for her and read her post and told myself I was furniture, and now I was neither, and we were both still learning what I was instead.

"I'll watch the lane," I said.

"Watch the lane," she agreed, and something passed between us that might once have been called trust and was now, after everything, simply assumed — which is either a higher thing or a lower one, I've never decided.

* * *

They went up through the yard on foot, Ada carrying a basket of eggs bought in the village for no purpose except to look like a woman who had come about eggs, and I stood by the motor with my hands in my pockets and watched the lane unspool behind us, grey-green and empty, and let myself do what I always did, which was think too much.

I want to be honest here, because honesty is the whole point of this account, and because if I am not honest about this particular hour then none of the rest deserves believing. When Herr Brandt's name first came up — in Julian's coded pages, in Edith's careful explanations — I had felt something in myself contract. I noticed it the way you notice a draught: not at once, but as a chill in a specific place. A German. An enemy alien hiding on English soil. The words arrived in my head pre-assembled, ready-made, wearing uniforms, and they were not even *my* words. They were the words off the hoardings and out of the papers, out of the mouths of men in public bars, out of the whole great chorus that had spent two years teaching us that the enemy was a species and not a set of faces. And I had swallowed them without tasting them, the way you swallow whatever's put in front of you when you're tired.

I, who read other people's letters. I, who ought above all people to know that a person is never what the outside of the envelope says.

A jackdaw dropped onto the hedge and regarded me with its white eye. Somewhere beyond the barns a man was singing — very quietly, half under his breath, in a language I did not need translated to recognise. The tune was nothing I knew, but the shape of it was the shape every lullaby has, the world over, the shape of a song sung to make a child sleep or a singer himself endure.

* * *

I did not mean to go looking for him. That must be said plainly, because what happened next will sound deliberate and wasn't. I heard the singing stop. Then I heard the particular silence of a man who has heard footsteps and gone still — a silence with edges — and I walked around the end of the cart-shed meaning only to satisfy myself that the danger Ada kept imagining down every lane was not, this once, real.

He was stacking hurdles. He had his back to me, sleeves rolled over forearms brown to the elbow, and he was working with the unhurried economy of a man who has done a thing ten thousand times and intends to do it ten thousand more. Grey in his hair. A stoop beginning at the shoulders, the kind you get from years bent over work, not from fear — though fear was there too, I saw it soon enough. He was perhaps fifty. Perhaps older. War had begun subtracting from everyone's faces, so it was hard to say.

He turned, and he saw me, and I watched the arithmetic happen behind his eyes. My clothes said driver. My cap said servant. But my face, I suppose, said stranger, and strangers meant questions, and questions meant the police, and the police meant the camp at Knockaloe or the reprisal depot or whatever new word they had invented that month for locking a man up because of the accident of his birth. He went very pale, and very quiet, and he set down the hurdle he was holding with exaggerated care, the way you'd set down something you might need to grab again quickly.

"Good morning," I said.

"Morning," he said. His English was good. Old-fashioned good, the English of newspapers and sermons, learned properly somewhere long ago. "You are — the chauffeur? From Marlowe House?"

It took me a moment to understand how he knew. Then I remembered that I was standing beside the motor, visible over my own shoulder, and felt foolish. "That's right."

"The ladies are at the house." Not a question. He knew why they'd come. Of course he knew why they'd come; he had written the letter that had brought them, or his son had, or both of them together over a kitchen table in a language they probably hadn't dared speak aloud.

"They're at the house," I agreed.

We stood there. Two men, a cart-shed, a stack of hurdles going soft with damp, and between us the entire diplomatic history of Europe compressed into forty feet of Kentish mud.

I should record what I thought, because what I thought shames me and shame recorded is at least a kind of payment. I looked at him — this stooped, greying, careful man with hayseed at his collar and a ladder in one sleeve darned with thread that didn't match — and the chorus in my head started up anyway. *Enemy. Alien. German.* And underneath it, quieter, another voice asked the question that ruined everything: *Where exactly is the enemy in what you're looking at?*

Because here is what I saw. I saw a man whose hands shook slightly, and not from age. I saw a man standing the way prisoners stand, weight back, near the wall. I saw a man who had been living on this farm — how long? Months, surely, since Julian arranged it — working, by the look of him, harder than the farmer paid him to work, because a hidden man pays his rent in usefulness. And I saw, when he glanced past me toward the lane, toward the road, toward the invisible country beyond it, an expression I had last seen on the platform at Charing Cross among the leave trains: the expression of a man looking at a door he does not expect to open.

"Your son," I said, because someone had to break the silence and because I am, at bottom, a coward about silences. "The letter mentioned—"

"He is nineteen." Said flatly, quickly, the way you hand over a hostage. As though I had come to take the boy away and he wanted it done fast.

"Nineteen." My own brother had been nineteen in 1914. My own brother had enlisted in the first flush, been wounded at Neuve Chapelle, come home with a limp and a medal and a horror of loud noises, and now worked at a bank in Tonbridge and never spoke of any of it. Nineteen. The word went through me sideways. "And the deportation order—"

"You have read our letters." He said it without accusation. Simply establishing the facts, as a surveyor establishes boundaries. "Yes. They wish to send Karl to—" he stopped, and chose, and I watched him choose, "—to a camp. In retaliation. For what our navy has done, or our government, or our Kaiser. I do not follow the logic. I have tried." He picked up the hurdle again, needing his hands occupied, and fitted it against the stack with unnecessary precision. "I came to England in 1887. I was twenty-three. I have paid rates in this county for longer than most of the men who signed the order have been shaving."

The chorus in my head tried one more time — *he would say that, wouldn't he, they all say that* — and then, mercifully, shut up. Because it was true, and truth has a way of ending arguments. He had paid rates in Kent for twenty-eight years. He had probably paid rates while I was learning to walk. Whatever nation I had decided he belonged to, Kent had owned a piece of him for longer than I had owned anything at all.

"I'm sorry," I said. It was inadequate and we both knew it. You cannot apologise on behalf of an order signed by men you've never met. But he nodded, slowly, as though the apology were a coin of small denomination that he would nonetheless accept because it was offered honestly.

* * *

Ada found us like that — standing in the cart-shed, talking about rates and camps in the careful voices of men defusing something — and I saw her take in the scene and misread it entirely, and begin to move between us, and then stop, because Edith was behind her, and Edith read the scene correctly at a glance, the way she read everything.

"Herr Brandt." Edith extended her hand. I watched him hesitate — the old etiquette warring with the new fear, a gentleman's reflex surviving inside a fugitive — and then take it and bow slightly over it, and I saw Edith register the bow, and file it away, and I knew she was thinking what I was thinking: *this man was somebody, once, in some life; this man had a profession and a parish and a pew.*

"You have my brother to thank for your welcome here," Edith said.

"I have your brother to thank for many things." His voice changed on *brother*, warmed and broke slightly at once, the way voices do around the names of the dead-or-living-dead. "When Emil — when Mr. Fenn, as I must learn to call everyone — wrote to me that he had found work for me here, I did not ask how. One learns not to ask how."

"One learns," Ada murmured, and there was a whole continent in the way she said it.

There was business then — papers, the photograph of Karl, the question of whether the boy could be moved and when and to where, conducted in the low urgent register that had become, without my noticing, the ordinary language of all our lives. I stood apart, as was my function, and watched Herr Brandt listen to Edith lay out what she intended to do for his son — the Member of Parliament she would write to, the Quaker network she would invoke, the sheer patient machinery of respectable influence she proposed to bring to bear — and I watched the hope arrive on his face and hurt him coming, the way hope does when it's been away too long. Hope is cruel to men who have learned to live without it. It comes back like blood into a numb limb, all pins and needles and pain.

* * *

Afterward — after the papers were copied, after the promises were made in the formulaic language that all of us now understood could bind nobody legally and everybody morally — the farmer's wife appeared with tea. She was a broad, weathered woman named Mrs. Pettit who plainly knew exactly who her hired man was and had known from the first day, and had decided, in the sovereign manner of Kentish farmwives, that it was no business of hers nor of anyone's, and that a man who milked well and asked little was a man worth the risk. She set the tray down on a trestle by the shed door and said, "There's no sugar, so don't ask," and departed, having thereby communicated more moral philosophy than most pamphlets manage.

We drank tea in the yard. There were five of us: Edith, Ada, Herr Brandt, Mrs. Pettit's dog, and myself. And here is the part of the afternoon I have turned over most often since, the part I would set down even if this account needed nothing else set down.

We did not talk.

Understand — it was not an awkward silence, nor a hostile one, nor the silence of people with nothing to say. It was the silence of people for whom speech had become expensive. Every sentence spoken aloud on that farm was a sentence that could be repeated, reported, twisted. Herr Brandt could not safely discuss Germany; we could not safely discuss Julian; Ada could not safely discuss herself; Edith could not safely discuss anything, being who she was. So we sat in the thin October sun with our enamel cups and we drank tea and said nothing, and the nothing was — I reach for the word and find it — *communal*. We were silent together, in the way that people who have shared a funeral are silent together, and it bound us more tightly than conversation would have.

At one point he refilled my cup. He had noticed it was empty — I hadn't noticed myself — and he leaned across and poured, and said, quietly, "You drive for Miss Marlowe a long time?"

"Ten years."

"Ten years." He considered this with the seriousness he seemed to bring to everything. "Then you have driven her through all of it. Through the stones and the windows broken and the prison and the feeding." The suffrage violence, he meant; the whole ugly catalogue. He pronounced the facts gently, without judgement, the way you'd recite a patient's case history. "She must trust you very much."

I looked at my tea. Ten years ago I would have said *she trusts me to drive*. Six weeks ago I would have said *she trusts me more than she knows*, with the smugness of a man concealing a theft. Now — now that I knew she had known about my reading all along, now that the humiliation had cooked down into something else — I said:

"She trusts me more than I deserve."

He nodded slowly, as though I had said something wise, though I had only said something true. "My father drove horses," he said. "In Württemberg. He said you can tell everything about a man by how he sits behind a horse. Whether he blames the animal or himself." A pause. "I think it is the same with motors. And with men."

"I blame the motor," I said, and he laughed — a short, surprised bark of laughter, quickly suppressed, glancing instinctively toward the lane even as he laughed, because that was his life now, laughing in instalments, checking the horizon between syllables. But he had laughed. And I had made him laugh. And some small fortified city in me, garrisoned since 1914, lowered a gate.

* * *

We left in the middle of the afternoon, while the light was still good, because Edith was rigorous about such things and rigor had kept us free thus far. At the gate Herr Brandt shook hands with each of us in turn — Edith gravely, Ada with both his hands around hers, murmuring something in whatever language they had shared abroad that made her eyes shine and swim — and then he came to me last, and stood a moment, and did not offer his hand, and I wondered with a falling heart whether I had offended after all, whether the whole tea had been theatre and the verdict unfavourable.

Then he said, in German, two words I did not understand, and seeing my face, translated them himself, carefully, as though the translation mattered:

"It means — *come back safe*. It is what we say. To soldiers." He almost smiled. "I say it now to civilians also. There are so few others to say it to."

I drove them home with the hedges scraping and the light failing and none of us speaking, because there was nothing that needed saying yet, and because we were all three of us busy with the same private labour: carrying a face away with us. Not the poster face, not the spiked-helmet face of the recruiting hoardings, but a tired man's face, grey-templed, hopeful, afraid, holding an enamel cup of unsweetened tea in a Kentish yard, saying *come back safe* to a chauffeur.

I had spent two years knowing what the enemy looked like. It had taken one afternoon to learn that I had never seen him at all.

We were three miles from Marlowe House when Ada, who had been watching the mirrors the way she always did now, said suddenly: "Ivo. Stop."

I stopped. Behind us, far back down the Ashford road, a black motorcar stood at the crossroads where we had turned off. It was not moving. It was not turning. It was simply sitting there, the way a spider sits, and even at that distance I could see the glint of field glasses catching the last of the light.

"That car," Ada said quietly, "was behind us outside Hythe yesterday."

I put the motor in gear, and drove the rest of the way home by lanes I had never used before, and began — for the first time in my comfortable, detached, well-upholstered life — to plan.
