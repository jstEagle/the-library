## 16. Reeve Closes In

The telegram came at half past seven on a grey Tuesday, when the mist had not yet lifted from the orchard and the apples hung like small pale moons in the fog. Edith read it twice at the breakfast table before she understood what it was saying beneath what it said.

*REPRESENTATIVE OF MILITARY AUTHORITIES WILL CALL MARLOWE HOUSE TEN O'CLOCK RE: POSTAL SECURITY REVIEW. YOUR COOPERATION EXPECTED.*

No signature. No courtesy. *Expected*, not requested. She set the telegram down beside her toast and found that her appetite had gone somewhere and would not be called back.

"Reeve," Ivo said. He stood by the sideboard with the coffee pot in his hand, and she watched the steam rise past his face. He had grown thinner since September, she thought. They all had.

"It doesn't name him."

"He wouldn't need to." Ivo set the pot down with a care that told her his hands were not as steady as his voice. "It's his phrasing. He writes the way a man walks through a room he already owns."

Ada was not at the table. She had risen early — earlier than any of them now, Edith had noticed; the girl slept badly and worked before dawn to disguise it — and was in the morning room with the ledgers, being usefully invisible. That was the arrangement they had all drifted into without ever speaking of it: Ada existed in this house the way light existed in it, everywhere and unremarked upon, so that no visitor's eye would ever fix on her.

But today there would be a visitor whose whole profession was fixing his eye.

Edith buttered nothing further. She rose, and the two men at the table — Ivo, and Julian's absence wearing Julian's chair — seemed to understand that she was going somewhere and that following her would be a mistake.

She went first to the study, where the safe was, where the letters were. Three months of them now, or nearly: the slow impossible correspondence of a dead man, arriving in cipher inside suffrage circulars and hymn-book wrappers and once, absurdly, inside an advertisement for corsets. She had burned some, kept others, and could not have explained even to herself the arithmetic by which she decided which was which. The ones she kept she kept because burning them felt like killing him again.

They lay in the bottom drawer of her writing desk, beneath the household accounts, wrapped in oilcloth. She took them out and held the bundle against her chest for a moment, feeling the paper's dry warmth — or imagining it; paper has no warmth, only the memory of hands — and then she carried them down the back stairs, through the scullery where Mrs. Petch was already boiling water and pretending not to see anything, which was another of the house's arrangements, out into the wet grass, and across to the long brick building at the garden's end that had been a potting shed when her father was alive and had been, since 1909, the archive of the Kent and Weald Women's Social and Political Union.

* * *

The archive smelled of dust and ink and cold brick, and underneath those things, faintly, of apples — because the shed still held a few crates of last year's russets in its darkest corner, forgotten by everyone but the mice. Edith lit the lamp, set the letters on the trestle table, and stood looking at the shelves.

The shelves ran the length of both walls. Minutes. Membership rolls. Treasurer's books going back to the founding. Bundles of correspondence tied in pink tape — pink, because when the union had been founded someone had observed acidly that if the world expected women to be decorative they might as well make their bureaucracy decorative too. Pamphlets by the thousand. The great wooden box marked PRESS CUTTINGS that weighed as much as a coffin.

And here, on the third shelf, the thing she had thought of in the night while lying awake listening to the rain: eleven years of *The Weald Sisterhood*, the monthly circular, every issue from the first duplicated hectograph sheet to the present, bound in annual volumes by Miss Hartley, who had been a librarian in a former life and brought to the movement a passion for order that Edith had once teased her about and was now prepared to fall on her knees and thank God for.

She pulled down volume after volume. 1913. 1914. The war years, thinner, printed on worse paper, the ink going grey as the men went away. And in these pages — thousands of them — committee reports and subscription notices and appeals for funds and obituaries of members' sons, columns of type so dense and so dull that no man alive had ever willingly opened one.

She untied the oilcloth. Julian's letters — his real letters, the uncoded originals Ada had memorized and destroyed abroad, reconstructed here from Ada's recitation and Edith's own hand — she could not risk. Those she would deal with separately. But the coded sheets themselves, the circulars with his cipher threaded invisibly through the text about jumble sales and membership dues — Reeve had seen such things before, perhaps; he suspected such things existed. If he found one loose, one anomalous sheet among her papers, he would know what he was holding.

But if he found it sewn into a bound volume of the *Weald Sisterhood* between a report on the Ashford whist drive and a recipe for economical marmalade —

Edith began to laugh, quietly, alone in the cold shed, and the laugh frightened her a little because it sounded like her brother's laugh, and because she could not stop it for a full minute.

Let him look. Let Captain Reeve, with his War Office manners and his memory like a customs ledger, open the 1915 volume of a women's suffrage circular and search it line by line. Let him read about the Faversham branch's cake stall. Let him check the treasurer's figures against the subscription lists. Men did not read these documents. Men had never read these documents. She had sat across from cabinet ministers who could recite troop movements from memory and who visibly, physically glazed when presented with a page of women's organizational minutes — because they believed, with the serene unexamined confidence of their entire sex, that nothing important had ever been written in such a hand, on such a subject, by such people.

For eleven years that contempt had galled her like a stone in her boot.

This morning she took it out, and looked at it, and found it was not a stone at all. It was a key.

* * *

By nine o'clock the transformation of Marlowe House was complete, and it had been accomplished with the efficiency of a household that had staged bazaars, rung doorbells on four hundred doors in one afternoon, and once smuggled a speaker out of a hostile meeting in a laundry basket.

Ivo had driven the motorcar around to the coach-house and taken out the dog cart instead — the motor was too modern, too memorable; a groom with a dog cart was furniture. He had also, at Edith's instruction, removed from the car's tool compartment the packet of papers he had been carrying for Julian's network, and those now lay in a biscuit tin buried under seed potatoes in the cellar, a hiding place so humble it had taken Ada thirty seconds to devise and Edith a full hour to stop admiring.

Julian himself — the fact of Julian, the living weight of him — had been distributed. His razor was in Ivo's washstand drawer. His spare spectacles were in the case with Edith's reading glasses, which no one had ever opened but her. The photograph of him bearded and older, taken at the signal box in October, was folded inside the cover of Edith's Bible, on the page with the genealogies, in the one book in England no searcher would open uninvited.

And Ada sat in the morning room with the union ledgers spread around her, a pencil behind her ear, looking exactly like what she appeared to be: a secretary. Youngish, plain-ish in the way that busy women are permitted to be plain, foreign only if you knew how to look — and Edith had coached her, the night before, in the small performance of it. *If he asks your name, give it plainly. Ada Brandt is a name half of Kent carries now; don't flinch from it, flinching is what he hunts. And if he looks at you longer than politeness allows, look back with the mild boredom of a woman interrupted at her work. Nothing infuriates a certain kind of man more, and nothing convinces him faster that you are beneath his attention.*

"I'm to be boring," Ada had said, and something in her face had flickered — the old wound, the stateless girl, the woman whose existence was a paperwork error — and then she had smiled. "That I can do. I have been practicing all my life."

Now, at ten minutes to ten, Edith stood in the hall and looked at herself in the tall mirror. She wore the dark grey dress, the one with the high collar, the one she called her deputation dress because she wore it when she intended to be reasonable and immovable in equal measure. Her hair was pinned with severe competence. She looked, she hoped, like exactly what she was: a respectable widow of forty-six, mistress of a modest estate, honorary secretary of a regional suffrage society that the newspapers had mostly stopped finding interesting since the war made everyone's anger unfashionable.

A person of no consequence whatsoever.

She practiced the thought the way a singer practices a note. *I am a person of no consequence.* And underneath it, steady as a bass string: *and you will believe it, because you have always believed it, because believing it costs you nothing and has cost me everything, and today, Captain, today it will cost you.*

The knock came at ten precisely. Of course it did. A man who arrived early might seem eager; a man who arrived late might seem careless. Captain Reeve arrived at the stroke, like a train, like a sentence.

* * *

He came in with the rain-smell still on his coat and a leather portfolio under his arm, and behind him — Edith noted this at once — a sergeant, young, red-eared, holding his cap in both hands like a schoolboy at a headmistress's door. Two of them, then. Not a social call.

"Mrs. Marlowe. Thank you for receiving us." His eyes went around the hall with a speed that pretended to be casualness: the umbrella stand, the stairwell, the doors. Counting exits. Counting doors that might hide other rooms. "Captain Reeve. This is Sergeant Dunn. As the telegram indicated, we're conducting a review of postal security arrangements in the district. There have been — irregularities."

"Irregularities," Edith repeated, and let the word sit in the hall air until it grew uncomfortable. "In the post? Good gracious. Has someone been steaming open Christmas parcels?"

Something crossed his face — quickly, professionally suppressed, but she saw it land. He had expected either fear or indignation and had received instead the tone of a woman discussing a problem with the milk supply. "Letters have gone missing. Or rather—" he corrected himself with visible distaste, "—letters have been *found*, that should not exist. Correspondence from persons believed deceased. The War Office takes a serious view."

"How extraordinary." She moved ahead of him toward the drawing room, unhurried, forcing him to follow at her pace, in her house, along her route. "Do sit down, Captain. Sergeant, there's a chair by the window. May I offer you tea? It's scarcely drinkable since the rationing tightened, I warn you, but hospitality is hospitality."

"No tea, thank you." He sat on the edge of the chair, portfolio on knees, and produced from it a printed form. "The review requires an inspection of correspondence facilities. Incoming mail, outgoing mail, any franking arrangements, any persons employed in handling letters. And—" the pause was surgical, "—we may require to look at whatever papers the household keeps. Routine."

"Routine," said Edith.

"Entirely."

"Captain Reeve." She folded her hands in her lap. "I am the honorary secretary of the Kent and Weald WSPU. Eleven years. I receive approximately sixty letters a week, most of them from women telling me other women have died in prison or given up the movement or lost sons at the front. My late brother's regiment wrote to me twice after Loos — once with condolences and once, eight months later, with an apology because they had sent his effects to a Captain Marsh of the same surname by mistake. I have buried more correspondence than you have filed. If there is something irregular in my post, I promise you it has escaped my notice amid the general catastrophe, and you are welcome to satisfy yourself. But you will do it here, in this room, with me present, and you will not go wandering through a private house on the strength of the phrase 'routine.' I've had enough men tell me what's routine to fill a parish churchyard."

The silence stretched. Beside the window, Sergeant Dunn shifted his weight and looked desperately at the garden.

Reeve inclined his head a fraction — acknowledgment, not surrender. "With me present, then. Perhaps you'd send for whoever handles your mail."

"That would be myself, my secretary, and my driver. My driver is exercising the horse. My secretary is working. You may speak to either when they're free; they work, Captain, unlike the Army."

* * *

He was good. She granted him that, watching him over the next ninety minutes as he worked through the hall table and the morning room and finally — inevitably — asked to see her study. He read fast, and he read everything, and he had a way of lifting a letter with two fingers as though it were evidence already, as though guilt were a property of paper. Twice he paused on a sheet for a long moment and Edith's heart performed a small controlled detonation, and both times it proved to be a bill from the coal merchant and a furious letter from the Maidstone branch about conference accommodation.

Then he asked about the archives.

"The society's records. Where are they kept?"

"In the old potting shed. It's dry and it locks, which is more than can be said for the county record office."

"I'll want to see them."

"You'll want to see eleven years of minutes?" She let genuine astonishment into her voice — and it was only half manufactured, because part of her still marveled that he could not see the absurdity. "Captain, there are perhaps forty thousand pages. Committee minutes. Subscription lists. Arguments about whether the Sevenoaks banner should be purple or green that occupied the executive for three meetings in 1911. I have read them all and I am the only person in England who has, including the women who wrote them."

"All the same."

"Of course. Though I wonder—" and here she turned, deliberately, and gave him the full mild weight of her gaze, "—whether your time might be better spent. These are women's organizational papers, Captain. Accounts, arrangements, resolutions. I only mention it because officers usually find such material rather — well. Dry."

There it was. She watched it move across his face like weather: the flicker of assent, the reflexive agreement that yes, women's minutes were dust, that a man of his training had better things to search than cake-stall receipts — watched him almost yield to it, and then watch himself yielding, and harden against it, because he was clever, damn him, and cleverness was the enemy of the plan. He knew that contempt was a weapon. He simply didn't believe it could be aimed at him, by her, using his own ammunition.

"I've learned not to assume, Mrs. Marlowe," he said quietly, "what's worth searching."

"No," she agreed pleasantly. "I imagine that's why they gave you the job."

* * *

He searched the archive for two hours. Edith sat in the doorway on a kitchen chair she had Mrs. Petch bring out, ostensibly supervising, actually knitting — she had taken up knitting again that autumn for precisely this species of occasion; needles clicking communicate patience, and patience communicates innocence — while Captain Reeve went shelf by shelf through the history of her life's work.

Sergeant Dunn helped at first, hauling down the press-cutting box and staggering under it, and then was dismissed to hold the dog cart's horse, which was sulking, which was true, and which got him out of the way.

Reeve worked the way she imagined he did everything: completely, joylessly. He opened the treasurer's books and checked sums. He unbound the 1913 volume of the *Sisterhood* — her heart held quite still — and turned perhaps twenty pages with his jaw tight, and Edith understood that he was fighting his own conviction that this was waste, that somewhere in his professional soul a voice was telling him a man of his rank did not spend his mornings reading about the Ashford whist drive, and that the voice was winning. He skipped. He skimmed. At page forty of 1913 he closed the volume with a small decisive thump and moved to the next shelf.

He never reached 1915.

The coded sheets lay in the 1915 and 1916 volumes, sewn in along the inner margins by Ada's neat hand, disguised as continuation pages, their cipher text sitting in plain sight between a memorial notice for a Faversham member's son killed at Gallipoli and an appeal for knitted socks. Julian's messages, in Julian's own chosen hiding place, guarded by the accumulated contempt of a century.

Reeve went through the correspondence bundles with more attention — those were letters, and letters were his trade. Pink-taped bundles from 1908, 1909, arguments with Members of Parliament, a furious exchange with a bishop. He read three or four entire letters, and Edith watched him read and saw the moment his shoulders changed, the moment he registered that this correspondence was better argued, better informed, and more dangerous than anything he read in his own office — and saw him file that realization somewhere he did not intend to examine.

"Your society is thorough," he said at last, standing, brushing dust from his cuffs.

"We are a democracy, Captain. It's astonishing what that obliges you to keep."

He looked at her then, directly, and for the first time since the hall she saw the courtesy drop away entirely, just for a second, the way a mask shifts when the wearer forgets it. What was underneath was tired and sharp and very much awake.

"Mrs. Marlowe. Do you know a family named Brandt?"

The needles clicked. Once. Twice.

"Brandt." She frowned, the small frown of honest effort. "There were Brandts at the hop farm near Marden. Decent people, German-born, been there since before I was married. The son used to deliver apples. Why?"

"They've applied to emigrate."

"Half of Kent wants to emigrate, from what my correspondence tells me. Can't think why, with the weather." She closed her knitting bag. "Have you finished with my archives, Captain? Only I have a committee at four, and the Faversham ladies do not forgive lateness. They've begun keeping minutes of my failings."

He almost smiled. Almost. "One more question. Your brother. Captain Julian Marlowe. You've received no communication — nothing at all — since his death?"

And there it was, laid on the table between them, naked at last: not postal security, not routine. *Julian.* Edith let the silence last exactly as long as grief lasts when it is true and old and worn smooth by two years of handling. She looked at the middle distance, at the wall above his head where a spider's web hung between beam and beam, silvered with damp.

"My brother died at Loos," she said, "on the twenty-fifth of September, nineteen fifteen. I received the telegram on the third of October, in this hall, standing where you're standing. Since then I have received his medals, his watch, a form asking whether I wished his body repatriated — which was a cruel joke somebody's clerk made without thinking — and a letter from a chaplain who spelled his name wrong. If you have found a letter from my brother, Captain Reeve, then you know something the War Office does not, and I suggest you take it up with them. I suggest you take a great many things up with them." She rose, smoothing her skirt. "Now. I'll show you out."

* * *

From the upstairs window, later, she watched the dog cart — no; she watched Reeve's staff car pull away down the drive, the sergeant driving, the captain in the rear seat not looking back, which was itself a kind of looking back. The mist had come down again off the orchard and took the car within fifty yards, and the sound of it lasted a little longer than the sight.

Behind her, in the room, nobody spoke. Ada stood by the cold fireplace with her arms folded. Ivo leaned against the doorframe, still in his stable coat, having come in the moment the car cleared the gate.

"Well," said Ivo.

"He didn't find them." Ada's voice was flat with exhaustion. "The volumes. He stopped at 1914."

"He stopped because he was bored," Edith said. She heard her own voice come out harder than she intended, and did not soften it. "Because eleven years of women's committees taught him, in the first twenty pages, that there was nothing in them worth a clever man's time. That is the only reason my brother's life is not in a cell tonight. Not our cleverness. His contempt."

She turned from the window. On the desk below it lay the day's post, delivered after the car left — ordinary-looking, circulars and a bill — and atop it, unremarkable, a copy of the November *Weald Sisterhood*. She had not yet opened it. She had not dared, with Reeve's hands in the house.

Now she picked it up. The paper was thin and cheap and smelled of the printer's ink, and somewhere inside it, threaded through some paragraph about a winter fundraiser or the price of duplicating paper, was either nothing at all — or her brother's hand, reaching again across whatever distance he endured, asking for whatever he needed next.

Her fingers were steady. She was almost surprised to find they were.

"Light the lamp," she said. "It's getting dark, and I think we'll want to see clearly."

Outside, the fog thickened over the orchard until the trees were only shapes, and the shapes were only suggestions, and the road down which the car had gone held its silence like a held breath.
