## 17. The Quarrel

The storm came up from the Channel that evening, the way storms do in Kent — not arriving so much as assembling, gathering its grey arguments over the orchard and letting them loose against the windows of Marlowe House one by one, like a creditor knocking at every door.

Ada stood in the morning room with the Brandt letter open on her knee and watched the first drops hit the glass. Behind her, Edith was dictating to herself — a habit she had when she thought no one listened, composing speeches aloud in fragments, *the vote is not a gift, it is a wage* — and the fire ticked and settled, and everything in the room seemed arranged, ordered, civilised, as though a boy of seventeen were not lying in a reprisal camp barracks in Rouen waiting for papers that would never come through any legal channel before winter closed.

"We could be in Boulogne in nine hours," Ada said.

Edith's pen stopped mid-word.

"I've been counting," Ada went on, turning the letter over though she knew every crease of it by heart now, every water-stained word of Frau Brandt's careful English. "The night boat from Folkestone, if we can get aboard it. The smuggler's channel — Mr. Cobb said — runs twice a week when the moon is dark. There is a dark moon Thursday."

"There is also a war," Edith said mildly, which was how her anger always began: mildly, like frost forming on a pane you didn't notice until the whole window had gone white.

* * *

They had been three weeks back from Hythe, three weeks since the photograph of Julian — bearded, older, his eyes squinting against some foreign sun but unmistakably, impossibly alive — had transformed their errand into a mission. Three weeks since the package from the signal box had given them money, forged papers, and the Brandts' plea, and in those three weeks Ada had learned what it was to live inside a house where everyone spoke carefully and no one slept well.

She had thought they were agreed. That was the terrible thing; she had thought it was settled. Ivo drove the roads and mapped the checkpoints. Edith wrote letters to people whose names were spoken only in lowercase, half-whispered. And Ada — Ada had let herself believe, for the first time since Rotterdam, since her father's arrest, since the word *alien* had been stamped across her life like a brand across a crate — that she belonged somewhere, that these two strangers were becoming the shape of a family, and that families pulled in the same direction even when they argued about the harness.

"Thursday," she said again. "The dark moon. After that we wait a fortnight, and Herr Brandt's son is deported to—" She stopped. The letter did not say *reprisal camp* in so many words; it said it in the way frightened people say things, in circumlocutions and dashes, in a sentence that simply ended: *and then we do not know what, and that is the worst, that we do not know.*

"Sit down, Ada."

"I'd rather stand."

"Then stand." Edith set down her pen with exaggerated care, aligning it parallel to the blotter's edge, and folded her hands. In the lamplight her grey hair looked silver, and her face had the stillness Ada had come to dread more than shouting — the stillness of a woman who had been thrown off platforms, force-fed through iron tubes, dragged by her wrists through hostile crowds, and who had learned that composure was a weapon and she never left home without it. "Tell me your plan. All of it. Not the crossing — the part after. You arrive in France carrying forged papers for a German national. How does he travel? How does he eat? What happens when a gendarme asks him for his documents?"

"He won't ask. Cobb's man—"

"Cobb's man is a smuggler. Smugglers are reliable right up until the moment they are arrested, at which point they become extremely talkative." Edith's voice remained level, almost gentle, which made it worse. "You would be taking a boy of seventeen into occupied-adjacent territory on the word of a man who moves brandy for a living. And if it goes wrong — if he is taken — do you know what they will call it? Espionage. A German spy ring moving through Kent. Do you know what happens to this house, to me, to Ivo, to every woman whose name appears in my correspondence, if that story takes root?" She gestured at the walls, at the filing cabinets, at the years of work stacked in neat acid-free boxes. "They are already looking for an excuse. Captain Reeve is already sniffing at my mail."

"Then let him find nothing because there IS nothing," Ada said, and heard her own voice crack upward, too loud, too fast. "Because the boy will be safe in Holland and the papers will be ash and—"

"And Julian will still be a deserter in the eyes of the Army," Edith said quietly, "with no bargain, no guarantor, no terms. We abandon the thing that protects all of us — the negotiation, the leverage — and we gamble everything on a boat and a dark moon."

"He is SEVENTEEN."

"Yes."

"They are sending him to a camp where they—" Ada's throat closed. She pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "My father has been interned for two years on an island in the middle of the sea, and he did nothing, NOTHING, except have been born in the wrong country after thirty-one years of paying rates in Deptford. Thirty-one years, and they took him anyway, because nobody negotiated, nobody wrote letters to people whose names are whispered. Letters are what you write while the ship is sinking, Mrs. Marlowe. Somebody has to row."

The silence after that was enormous. Rain filled it, drumming on the glass, running in bright threads down the panes.

Ada realised, distantly, as though watching from outside her own body, that she had never raised her voice in this house before. That in five months she had been careful, grateful, quiet — the landlady's daughter, the girl Julian had trusted, the guest who washed dishes without being asked — and that all that carefulness had just torn along its seam.

Edith had gone very pale. But she did not shout back. Ada almost wished she would.

"You think I don't know about ships sinking," Edith said at last, and her voice had changed — lower, rougher. "You think I have spent twenty years writing polite letters to Cabinet ministers because I enjoy the stationery? I have been beaten unconscious in Westminster. I have been fed through the nose until my throat bled, and I have come out of Holloway and written MORE letters, because—" she stopped, breathed, started again. "Because fury is fuel, child, but it is a filthy navigator. It will run you onto rocks and tell you the rocks are the shore."

"Don't call me child."

Something flickered in Edith's face — hurt, quickly mastered, quickly filed away. Too late. Ada saw it land.

* * *

It was Ivo who brought the tea, because Ivo always appeared with tea at the exact moments when the house needed someone ordinary in it. He came in with the tray balanced on one forearm, took in the room — the letter on Ada's knee, Edith's white knuckles, the rain — and set the tray down with the unhurried precision of a man defusing something.

"The dark moon is Thursday," he said.

Ada looked up. So did Edith.

"I've been thinking about it," Ivo went on, addressing the teapot rather than either of them, which was his way. "Folkestone. The military checkpoint at the harbour gates checks drivers' passes, not passengers' faces, if the driver has a War Office transport sticker. Mine's current. The Daimler reads as requisitioned. If a party of civilians were travelling under my authority—" He glanced up, finally, and Ada saw that his long, melancholy face had resolved itself into something she had rarely seen on it: decision. "Cobb's boat. Thursday. I'll drive."

"No," Edith said.

"Mrs. Marlowe—"

"I said no, Ivo." Now she did rise, and the mildness was gone entirely, stripped away like paint off a hull. "Do you understand what you're proposing? Forging authority. Impersonating military transport. If you're caught, you aren't fined — you're shot. Or worse, you're questioned, and you tell them everything, because everyone tells them everything eventually, and then it isn't smuggling, it's a conspiracy, and my brother's name goes into a file beside yours and Ada's and mine, and whatever slim chance there is of getting him home alive burns with it."

"Or," Ada said, standing too, "we do it your way, and Karl Brandt spends another winter in a camp, and maybe he comes out and maybe he doesn't, and meanwhile we are SAFE, and RESPECTABLE, and we can live with it because we tried, didn't we, we wrote such beautiful letters—"

"That is unworthy of you."

"It is TRUE of me!" The words tore out of her before she could weigh them, and once they were out she could not gather them back. "You want to know why? Because respectability is a coat I put on when I came to this country and it has never once kept me warm. My father taught mathematics. He taught ME mathematics, at this kitchen table, in a house four miles from here, and when they took him, his colleagues — good men, kind men, men who signed petitions — they wrote letters. And he is still ON THE ISLAND. So forgive me if I have stopped believing in letters!"

The rain slammed against the window as if applauding.

Edith stood very straight. When she spoke, each word came out flat and separate, like stones laid end to end. "If we cross illegally and it fails, we lose everything — the movement, the archive, Julian, the Brandts, all of it, in one stroke. If we proceed as I propose, we risk less and move slower, and slower means a boy suffers longer in a place I would not wish on a dog. I know that. I carry that arithmetic every hour of every day, and I will carry it whether or not you forgive me for it." Her eyes moved to Ivo. "And you? You would drive them into that. Knowing what it costs if the wheel comes off?"

Ivo was quiet for a long moment. Outside, the storm found a loose shutter somewhere and worried it, a small persistent rattling like teeth.

"I read your mail," he said.

The non sequitur hung in the air. Edith's eyebrows drew together. Ada stared at him.

"For two years," Ivo went on, still in that low, steady voice, looking at the middle distance as though reading from something held just beyond the wall. "Every letter through this house, I opened with steam and sealed it again after. Circulars, correspondence, all of it. Because history happened to other people, and I wanted — I told myself I wanted — to be near it." He finally turned to look at Edith fully. "I am telling you this now because it's the truth, and because you deserve to hear it before you trust me with anything. And because the reason I'm siding with Ada is the same reason I read: I cannot stand outside anymore. I've tried it. It doesn't hold. If we take the slow road and the boy is lost, I will know — the way I know the weight of other people's words — that I stood in the doorway holding a teapot."

Nobody moved. The fire collapsed softly inward, sending up a small galaxy of sparks.

"Steam," Edith said at last, faintly. "Two years."

"Ma'am."

"You sealed them properly? No one ever—"

"No one ever knew. Until now."

Edith pressed two fingers to the bridge of her nose. Whatever passed behind her hand, she kept it there, private. When she lowered it, her face had reassembled itself, but Ada — who had spent months learning to read this woman the way she read weather — caught the fracture lines beneath.

"You are both determined," Edith said. "Against my judgment. Against my experience. Against every hard lesson I have paid for in prison cells." She looked from Ivo to Ada and back, and something in her expression was not anger at all — it was grief wearing anger's clothes. "Then hear me. I will not stop you. I will not help you, either, and I will not lie for you when Reeve comes asking, and he WILL come asking, because he always does. If you cross on Thursday, you cross as three fools alone on a cold sea, and you had better pray Cobb's man is worth his price."

"Mrs. Marlowe," Ada began.

"Get out of my morning room. Both of you. I have letters to write." A bitter half-smile touched her mouth and vanished. "Beautiful ones."

* * *

Ada found Ivo later in the coach-house, where he was checking the Daimler's tyres by lantern light with the thoroughness of a man who needed his hands busy or he would start thinking. The rain had slackened to a fine salt-smelling drizzle. Somewhere down the valley a dog was complaining at the weather.

"She'll come round," Ivo said, without looking up. "She always comes round. She just needs to lose the argument loudly enough first."

"She thinks we'll kill us all."

"She thinks we might. It's not the same thing." He straightened, wiped his hands on a rag, and leaned against the car's mudguard. In the lantern light his face was tired and younger than his years. "For what it's worth — I meant it. In there. About the mail. You didn't need to learn it that way."

"Everyone in this house keeps confessing to me," Ada said. "Julian. You. It's like living in a church that leaks secrets instead of rain."

Ivo almost smiled. Then the smile went out, and he was quiet a moment, working the rag between his fingers.

"Ada. If Thursday goes wrong — if we're stopped at the harbour — you let them take me first. You say I forced you. Say I lied about the pass, said you believed it was legitimate. They'll believe it. Nobody suspects the girl of anything; they'll search my pockets and wave you through." He said it lightly, arranging his own arrest like a dinner reservation.

"No," Ada said.

"It's sensible—"

"I have been spared by other people's arrangements my whole life." Her voice came out low and shaking. "My father arranged my passage to England. Julian arranged my route to Kent. Everyone arranges, and I am always the parcel. Not this time. This time I arrange, and if the boat goes down, we go down in the same water."

Ivo looked at her for a long moment, lantern-light and shadow moving on his face, and whatever he saw there made him nod slowly, the argument leaving him like air from a tyre.

"Together, then," he said. "Thursday."

"Together."

He went back to the tyres. Ada lingered in the doorway of the coach-house, looking back at the house — at the one lit window of the morning room, where Edith's silhouette bent over her desk, motionless, writing her beautiful useless necessary letters into the small hours — and felt the strangest sensation, like a bone setting wrong but setting: that they were still a family, all three of them, even broken like this. Perhaps especially broken like this. Families, she thought, were not the people who agreed with you. They were the people who stayed in the house after the shouting.

She was halfway across the wet cobbles when she remembered the note.

It had come with the evening post — she'd tucked it unopened into her apron pocket during the argument and forgotten it entirely. Now, under the coach-house eaves, by the spill of Ivo's lantern, she unfolded it. No signature. No address beyond *Marlowe House* in a clerkly hand. Inside, a single line, printed in block capitals:

**THE HARBOURMASTER AT FOLKESTONE HAS YOUR DESCRIPTIONS. TRAVEL AS FOUR.**

Four.

Ada read it twice. Behind her, Ivo was humming tunelessly over the spare wheel, and the lantern threw his shadow huge against the far wall, and out over the darkening orchard the last of the storm was dragging itself seaward toward the very coast they meant to cross — toward a harbourmaster who knew their faces, and a fourth traveller whom nobody had named.

"Ada?" Ivo called. "What is it?"

She folded the paper slowly, her mouth suddenly dry. "We need to talk about Thursday," she said. "There's been a change in the passenger list."
