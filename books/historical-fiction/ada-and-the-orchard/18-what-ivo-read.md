## 18. What Ivo Read

The house was quiet in the particular way Marlowe House was quiet after dark — not silent, but breathing. Floorboards ticking as the day's warmth left them. The clock on the landing that ran four minutes fast and had run four minutes fast since before the war, because Edith said a clock that lied was still useful if you knew how it lied. Somewhere below, Ada's lamp went out; her footsteps crossed the hall and climbed, unhurried, to the blue room at the back where she slept with the window cracked even in November.

Ivo sat in the study with a ledger open on his knee and read nothing at all.

It was the third night of the quarrel's aftermath. Three days since Ada had stood in this same room with her jaw set like a keel and said, *Then I'll go by myself*, and Edith had said, *You will not*, and Ivo had heard his own voice say something no one had asked him to say — *She shouldn't have to* — and understood, standing there between them, that he had chosen sides without ever deciding to. The fight had ended the way fights end in houses where people love each other badly and well at once: doors, silence, tea nobody drank.

Edith had gone out that evening. A committee in Ashford, she'd said, though the hour was late for committees, and her coat had gone on with a certain grim ceremony Ivo had learned to recognize. She would be hours. And she had left behind, stacked on the corner of her desk where she never left anything, a bundle tied in green ribbon.

Her private letters. The diary-letters she wrote monthly to her friend Constance in Edinburgh — the ones Ivo had carried to the post himself, dozens of times, sealed and addressed and, every time, steamed open over the kettle in his room above the garage, read, resealed with a borrowed iron, and delivered with a steady hand.

They were sitting there. Untied. Waiting.

He should not have touched them. He knew it the way you know the depth of water by the cold coming through your boots. Whatever was in those pages, whatever reason Edith had left them out — and surely she hadn't; surely it was carelessness, the first carelessness he'd ever known from her — reading them now would be theft twice over. Once when they were sealed. Once when they were offered.

His hand went out anyway. His hands had always gone out anyway. That was the whole trouble with him, the whole shape of his life: a man built like a door that opened from the wrong side.

---

He took the top letter only. October, two years ago. He told himself he would read one page, put it down, and spend the rest of his life being a better man about it.

*Ivo drove me to Lenham today,* Edith had written, in the upright, unornamented hand that looked, on paper, exactly like her voice sounded when she was keeping her temper on a leash. *The roads were flooded and he complained about nothing, which is his way of complaining about everything. He waited three hours in the rain outside the hall rather than come in, because he says crowds make him itch, but I noticed he arranged the car so he could see the platform.*

Ivo's face went hot. Two years ago. Rain at Lenham. He could feel it still, the wet working through his collar while Mrs. Pankhurst's sister-in-law or some such spoke about the franchise to forty women and a dog, and he had told himself he was watching the road, watching the crowd, watching for trouble — and had been, partly, but mostly he had been watching Edith, because watching Edith was the closest thing to religion he practiced.

*Here is what I want you to understand about Ivo Underhill,* the letter went on, and his thumb stopped dead on the page. *You will think me careless when I tell you, so let me be careful. My post has been read. Not all of it — he is too good for that — but mine, regularly, for going on two years. I know because I tested him. I began including small falsehoods in my sealed correspondence, details that existed nowhere else, and watched to see where they surfaced. They surfaced nowhere. He reads, and he keeps what he reads, and he tells no one. Do you see? In an organization full of women who cannot keep their own secrets past the second glass of sherry, God bless them, I have a man who reads everything and repeats nothing.*

*I have thought long about whether to dismiss him. I am supposed to be furious. A woman in my position is supposed to guard her correspondence like a dragon on eggs. But consider what he actually does. He carries my letters through villages where half the households would cheer to see me tarred, and he does it knowing — he must know — that if any of it were traced to him he would lose his position, perhaps worse, under this wretched Cat and Mouse business. He reads because he is lonely, I think, and because other people's lives are the only ones he believes he is permitted to have. And having read, he protects. Every letter he has ever steamed open, he has also, without knowing it, guarded.*

*I do not excuse him. I employ him. There is a difference, and it matters.*

Ivo set the letter down on the desk with both hands, carefully, the way you set down something that might go off.

The fire had burned down to a red seam along the log. He didn't remember the room getting dark. Outside, wind moved through the bare apple trees behind the wall, and the sound of it was the sound the orchard made all winter, a low dry rattle like applause from a very long way off.

She knew. She had known for two years. She had tested him — laid false trails through her own heart like a woman salting a mine — and he had walked every one of them without ever once looking down.

Humiliation came first, and it came thoroughly. It came up through the floor and into his teeth. Every careful resealing, every borrowed iron heated just so, every morning he had handed her a letter and met her eyes and said *post's been, ma'am* with the smoothness of a man committing a crime he believed was invisible — all of it had happened in front of an audience. He had been a conjurer performing for the one person in the hall who knew where the cards were palmed, and she had clapped politely for two years.

And underneath the humiliation, arriving slower and settling deeper, was the other thing. The thing that did not go away when he stood up and paced to the window and came back.

*Trusting him.* Her word, in the letter. *In an organization full of women who cannot keep their own secrets, I have a man who reads everything and repeats nothing.* She had taken the worst thing about him — the furtiveness, the loneliness dressed up as curiosity, the whole grey economy of a life lived on other people's words — and she had weighed it, and found it load-bearing. Not forgiven. Employed. *There is a difference, and it matters.*

Nobody had ever done that to him. His father had died calling him a bystander. The army medical board had stamped him unfit and meant it as a verdict on his whole character. Women in villages took his cap and called him *the driver* as though the car arrived on its own. And here was Edith Marlowe, writing to Edinburgh in October of 1915, describing him as though he were a fact worth recording — a fixed point, a thing the movement could stand on precisely because it was crooked.

He picked the letter up again and read it a third time, and this time he wept, briefly and furiously, standing in another man's employer's study with green ribbon under his fingers, and was ashamed of that too, and then was not ashamed, because the crying felt less like weakness than like a debt being paid into the right account at last.

---

There were more letters beneath. Of course there were. He untied the ribbon fully now — the transgression had already swallowed him whole; there was no sense pretending to wade — and worked backward through them the way you work backward through a tide.

November 1915, after Loos. *Julian's effects have come. I cannot write what I expected them to contain, because what I expected was him, folded up small. Instead: a watch that doesn't run, a hymn book, a sock with a hole in the heel that I knitted myself and that he apparently carried through training and France and everything else rather than throw away. I keep thinking of that sock. Grief is very stupid and very specific.*

Ivo remembered carrying that letter to the post. Remembered standing in his room over the garage with it open on his knees, feeling like a burglar in a cathedral, reading her grief at arm's length because he could not stop and could not look directly at it either. He had resealed it and driven to Ashford in fog and thought, *this is as close as I will ever come to mattering to anyone: I carry their sorrow and they don't know my name.*

January 1916. *Constance asks why I don't replace him with someone trustworthy. The question assumes trust is a sealed envelope. I trust Ivo precisely because he opens them. A man who knows everything and can repeat nothing is the safest repository I possess. Also — and write this to no one — he has begun to look at Ada when he thinks no one sees, and looks are the one correspondence he hasn't learned to steam open. It does me good to watch it. The boy is learning to belong to something by accident.*

He laughed. It came out of him sideways, startled, a bark in the empty room — the first real laugh since the quarrel. *By accident.* As if belonging were weather that happened to you. Well. Perhaps it was. Perhaps that was the whole discovery of his thirty-one years: that you didn't join the human race, you got caught in it, like rain.

March 1916. *Reeve has written again. Third time this year, each more courteous than the last, which in a War Office man is the equivalent of a raised fist. He wants to discuss "postal security" in the movement. I have replied with equal courtesy and no information. I begin to wonder what he thinks he will find in our mail, and then I remember Julian, and wonder instead whether he already knows what I only suspect — that my brother's death was too neatly documented, too promptly closed. Bureaucracy grieves quickly. It is its one mercy and its one confession.*

So she had suspected Reeve even then. Had been fencing with him in polite ink while Ivo ferried the blades. He read on, faster now, hungry, two years of her interior life unspooling under his hands — the doubt she never showed, the fury at the Cat and Mouse Act, the nights she wrote *I am afraid* in three words and moved on as though fear were a chore on a list — and with every page the humiliation changed its chemistry. It was turning into something else. Something with weight and direction. He didn't yet have a name for it, but he recognized its cousin: it was what soldiers described, men like Ellery would describe, when they spoke of a comrade who had seen you at your worst and stayed in the trench beside you anyway.

Devotion. That was the name. An old-fashioned word, a servant's word, and he wore it gladly.

---

The last letter was recent. September. Six weeks old, or near enough — the date mattered, and his eye caught the date the way a hand catches a nail.

*Ada asked me tonight whether I trusted her. I said yes, and it was true, and it frightened me how easily it was true. Then she asked — in her roundabout foreign way, which is to say bluntly — whether I trusted Ivo. I said yes again. She looked at me as if I had confessed to something. Perhaps I had.*

*Here is what I have never told him. I leave things out for him to find. Nothing important — never anything that would endanger us — but I leave the drawer unlocked, the ledger open, the letter on the desk, and I wait to see whether he takes the bait, and when he does, I know two things. First, that he is still Ivo: curious, furtive, faithful. Second, that he is still mine. A man who stops reading my letters has stopped caring what happens to me. I would sooner lose the cause than learn that.*

Ivo read it four times.

The study was very cold by then. The fire was ash and one orange eye. He became aware, gradually, of the sounds a house makes when someone comes home: the gate, gravel, the front door with its particular complaint about its hinges, footsteps that did not hurry and did not need to.

Edith came into the study still wearing her coat, smelling of night air and coal smoke from the Ashford train, and stopped in the doorway.

She saw him. She saw the letters, spread across the desk like a hand of cards played face-up. She saw the ribbon, untied, lying in a loose green loop beside the lamp. And Ivo saw her take all of it in — the whole tableau, himself included, arranged and illuminated — and understood, with a clarity that felt almost like falling, that the desk had been bait, and the study had been bait, and perhaps the entire evening, the committee in Ashford that convened at nine o'clock at night, had been constructed for this exact moment, because Edith Marlowe did not do carelessness, and had never once in her life left anything anywhere by accident.

"You've read them," she said. Not a question. She began, unhurriedly, to unbutton her gloves.

"Yes." His voice came out lower than he intended. "All of them."

"All?"

"The sock," he said, and had to stop, and start again. "The sock with the hole in it. You wrote about the sock."

Something crossed her face — grief, and under the grief, satisfaction, and under the satisfaction, a tenderness she would deny to her dying day. "Two years ago," she said. "Lenham. The rain. You watched the platform from the car."

"I told myself I was watching for trouble."

"Were you?"

"Some of the time."

She hung her coat on the stand. She came to the desk and gathered the letters, tapping them square against the wood with the brisk competence of a woman shuffling a deck she had already won with, and retied the ribbon, and then she did not pick the bundle up. She left it lying there between them, within reach of his hand, deliberately, unmistakably, an offer renewed in plain sight.

"I'm not sorry," Ivo said. "I ought to be. I'm not. You wrote—" He gestured at the letters and found that English, which had served him adequately for thirty-one years of other people's sentences, failed him now for the first sentence of his own. "You wrote that I was yours."

"Did I?" said Edith, and looked at him with the level grey gaze that had stared down magistrates and hecklers and Captain Reeve himself. "Well. I don't recall sealing that particular sentiment, so I can't accuse you of theft." She turned toward the door, then paused. "Ada leaves for the coast road Thursday, whatever the three of decided Tuesday. The innkeeper Julian trusted — Halloran, at Hythe — will want paying, and paying in favors, and favors are the only currency that appreciates in wartime. Someone must drive her."

"I'll drive her."

"I know," said Edith Marlowe. "That's why I mentioned it." And she went out, taking the lamp, leaving him the letters and the dark and the green ribbon, and Ivo sat alone in the cooling study with his heart going like a engine at full throttle, understanding that he had just been given everything he had spent his life stealing — and that Thursday, the road to Hythe would begin collecting payment for it.
