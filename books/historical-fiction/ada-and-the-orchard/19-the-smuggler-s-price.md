## 19. The Smuggler's Price

The rain had followed us all the way from Marlowe House, and by the time we turned down the lane to the Smack and Anchor at Hythe it had worked itself into a proper grey fury, flung sideways off the Channel like a man shaking water from his coat. The Dymchurch road had been a river in places. Twice I'd had to get out and lay my coat over the wheels' chains, and twice Edith had insisted on helping despite Ada's protests, so that by the time we arrived all three of us were soaked to the skin and nobody speaking much.

I want to record that I minded. I don't think I did. There is a peculiar cleanliness about being wet through on an errand that matters — the body occupied, the conscience given something honest to do. In the old days, delivering Mrs. Pankhurst's circulars to Ashford or Mrs. Marlowe's letters to the Folkestone postmistress, I had kept myself dry as a ledger and twice as blank. Now my boots squelched and my hands were stiff on the wheel and I felt, for the first time in my life, like a man inside history rather than outside its window.

The inn sat low against the shingle, its timbers black with two centuries of salt, its sign creaking on iron brackets that sang in the wind. A single window glowed amber above the door. Smoke stood up from the chimney and was torn away before it had climbed ten feet.

"Same arrangement as before," Edith said quietly, as I brought the car round behind the building, out of sight of the coast road. "Let me do the talking."

"Mrs. Marlowe." Ada leaned forward between the seats. Her hair had come loose from its pins and hung in wet ropes against her collar, and her eyes were very bright. "Mr. Cobb trusts me. He told you so himself last time."

"He told me he trusted no one," Edith said, "which from a smuggler is nearly the same thing. But very well. We go in together."

* * *

Josiah Cobb was not what strangers expected of a man who had run contraband across Romney Marsh for thirty years. He was small and neat and grey, with reading spectacles pushed up into thin white hair, and he greeted us with the unhurried courtesy of a bank manager. Only his eyes moved constantly — to the door, to the window, to our hands, to our shoes.

"Miss Ada." He took her hand briefly. "You're wetter than the Marsh."

"The weather's improved since we last spoke, then," Ada said, and something passed between them — a flicker of real warmth under all that caution — that made me wonder how long she had known this man, and through whom.

Cobb looked past her to Edith, then to me, and back again. "Kitchen's warm," he said. "Not the taproom. There's a revenue man drinking porter by the fire out there and asking friendly questions of everyone who comes in soaked."

My stomach dropped. "Reeve's men?"

"No. Worse. An honest one." Cobb said it without irony. "This way."

The kitchen was low-beamed and copper-brightened, with a fire big enough to roast an ox and a kettle already singing. He set bread and cheese before us unasked, poured tea thick enough to stand a spoon in, and only when we were all seated and the door shut did he take off his spectacles, polish them on his apron, and say:

"You've come about the crossing."

"We have," Edith said.

"And you'll be wanting it soon, and quiet, and with papers that hold." He set the spectacles down. "I can do it. Weather breaks Tuesday; there's a boat out of Dungeness that runs dark on a falling tide. But I'll be plain with you, ma'am, because Miss Ada vouched for you and I don't throw away thirty years on a vouching lightly. What you're asking has got heavier since you were here last. They've doubled the watch at Folkestone. There's a list of names posted in every port office between here and Margate, and half the fishermen on this coast would sell their grandmothers for the reward money being offered."

"What reward?" I asked. "For what?"

Cobb looked at me for a long moment, and I understood I had asked a question whose answer he was deciding whether I could bear.

"For a deserter," he said. "And for persons assisting one."

Ada's cup stopped halfway to her mouth. "Julian's not—"

"Not your gentleman, no. Another matter entirely." Cobb rose, went to the dresser, and drew out a bottle of brandy without offering any. He poured a measure into his tea and drank it like medicine. "But it comes to the same door. Here's the shape of it. Three nights ago a boat came in short-handed, running cargo as usual. Among the cargo was a young man. Soldier. Shot through the shoulder, fevered, half-dead of cold. My lads carried him up from the beach because leaving him there meant a coroner's court and questions, and questions are the one cargo I never carry."

Edith set down her cup with great care. "You're sheltering a deserter."

"I'm sheltering a boy of twenty-two with a hole in him," Cobb said, "who cries out for his mother in French and English both, which tells you he's been out there long enough to forget which tongue he dreams in. The army doctor at Lydd would ask his name. The army doctor at Lydd would hang him, ma'am — they're not hanging deserters this month, but they'd transport him, or worse, and either way my boys who carried him up the shingle would be answering questions too." He turned the cup slowly on the table. "He has to go inland. Tonight, if he lasts. There's a woman at Ruckinge — a widow, discreet, used to nursing men who don't give their right names. She asks nothing but payment and silence. After that, in a week or three, when the fever's broke and the search's moved on, he goes on to London, and after London I neither know nor care."

"That's not our business," I said. It came out harder than I intended. "We came for a crossing. For three passengers and papers."

"Yes." Cobb smiled without any humour in it. "And I'm telling you the price of the crossing. You want my boat, my skipper, my papers, my silence. All of those are worth more now than they were a fortnight ago, because every man on this coast knows there's a reward out and a boy bleeding in my back room. So the crossing costs what the boy costs. You move him inland for me tonight, and on Tuesday you sail. That's the bargain."

The fire cracked. Rain drove against the little window like gravel. I looked at Edith and saw her doing sums I couldn't see — not in money, but in risk, in time, in the distance between Ruckinge and Dover and the hours left before dawn.

"Why us?" I said. "You've got men. Lads who carried him up the beach."

"My lads are known," Cobb said simply. "Every face on this Marsh that moves at night is known to somebody. Yours aren't. A motor car, a lady in furs, a chauffeur — that's gentry going about their lawful business, even at midnight. Especially at midnight." He almost laughed. "The best disguise on this coast in 1916 is respectability, Mr.—"

"Underhill."

"Mr. Underhill. Nobody stops a motor car with a lady's maid and a chauffeur unless they already suspect it. And nobody suspects it, because the whole apparatus of this war is built to look downward, never up." He glanced at Edith. "Begging pardon, ma'am."

"None taken," Edith said dryly. "It happens to be the truest thing anyone's said to me all year."

Ada hadn't spoken. She was staring into the fire, and I watched her jaw tighten, and I knew — I had learned to know it by then, the way you learn weather — that she was thinking of her father. A man carried inland in the night by strangers. A man moved like cargo because the law had decided what he was. Whatever she was going to say next, it wasn't caution.

"I'll drive," she said.

"No," I said, before I knew I was going to say it.

She turned on me. "You don't decide for me."

"He's shot and fevered and probably delirious, and if he starts screaming in the back of a car at a checkpoint—" I stopped. "Ada. Let me carry this one. Please."

The *please* landed between us like a dropped coin. She held my eyes a moment longer, then nodded, once, and looked away, and I found my heart was beating oddly, and made myself attend instead to Cobb.

* * *

They kept the boy in a room above the stable, reached by an outside stair that Cobb's men had nailed a loose board across to look disused. Cobb went up first with a lamp; the rest of us followed in a line, Edith's hand light on my shoulder in the dark, and the smell hit us halfway up — carbolic and sweat and the sweetish reek of a wound gone bad at the edges.

He lay on a cot against the wall, a blanket to his chin, and I will try to describe him honestly because everything that came after began with looking at him properly. He was very young. Brown hair cut short and growing out ragged. Hands that had been soft once and were hard now, knuckles split. His face was flushed high on the cheeks with fever, and his lips were grey, and he breathed in shallow quick pulls that made his whole chest shudder. The bandage on his left shoulder had been changed recently — someone competent, Cobb's widow-mother perhaps, or Cobb himself — but the stain had already bloomed through again, rust-brown, the size of a saucer.

There was a service cap on the floor by the cot, turned upside down. Nobody had touched it. It seemed to lie there like something washed ashore.

"Private Ellery," Cobb said quietly. "That's what he gave us. Might be his name. Might not. Men in his state give you whatever name is nearest." He set the lamp down. "Wound's a week old at least. He walked. God knows how far. Somebody got him over the lines, somebody put him on a boat at Flushing, and somewhere between there and my beach every friend he had ran out."

Edith knelt beside the cot. She didn't touch him. She studied his face the way she studied a document — thoroughly, without mercy, looking for the truth under the ink.

"Deserters don't walk a week on a shot shoulder," she said at last, softly. "Men run from cowardice in the first hour. This one endured something first. Long enough to break him."

"You'd know better than me, ma'am."

"I know my brother," Edith said, and stopped herself, and Cobb, to his credit, pretended he hadn't heard.

Ada fetched water. I stood by the wall with my arms folded like a fool, because some part of me still wanted to be a chauffeur, a carrier, a man who moved things and asked nothing about them — and that part of me had died somewhere on the Dymchurch road, and I hadn't yet learned what to do with my hands.

Ellery opened his eyes.

They were unfocused, glassy, fixed on the rafters, and for a moment I thought he'd merely surfaced and would sink again. Then his gaze slid down and found Edith kneeling there, and he made a sound — not a word, a sound, the noise a man makes when a rope he's hanging from goes suddenly slack.

"Sister?" he whispered. "Is that— no. No, she's dead. You're not—" His breath caught, whistling. "Don't tell her. Don't write and tell her. Tell her I was taken ill. Tell her it was quick."

"She isn't dead," Edith said gently. "Whoever she is, she isn't dead, and you're going to tell her yourself, when you're well."

He stared at her. Fever logic assembled itself visibly behind his eyes, sorting her face into some private file. Then his eyes rolled toward me, and widened, and he struggled up on his good arm with a strength that alarmed us all.

"Tom," he gasped. "Tom, they said you were killed. They sent your kit home. Mother's got your kit on the bed, she won't move it, she sleeps—" He swayed. I caught him without deciding to, one arm behind his shoulders, careful of the wound, and he gripped my lapel with a hand that shook like a leaf in wind. "Tom. Tom, listen. Listen to me. It wasn't the shelling. Everyone thinks it was the shelling. It was the wire. He went out to the wire and he didn't come back and I heard him, Ivo, I heard him calling all night and we couldn't go, orders, they said no one goes, and in the morning—" 

His voice failed. His grip on my lapel did not.

"In the morning there was nothing left to bury," he finished, barely audible. "Nothing. And I stayed eleven more days, Tom, I stayed and I did everything right, and then one morning I just — walked. Out past the wire. Toward where he'd been. And I couldn't stop walking. I couldn't ever stop walking again."

He sagged against me. His forehead pressed into my shoulder, hot as a stove-lid, and he wept without sound, the way men weep who learned to do it in trenches, silently, economically, ashamed even of grief.

Nobody in the room moved. Cobb had gone still by the door. Ada had both hands pressed over her mouth. And Edith — Edith had closed her eyes, and two tears stood on her cheeks, unmoving, as if she dared not let them fall lest the boy see and be shamed.

And I held him. A stranger. A criminal by the law of the land. A boy who had mistaken me for his dead brother, and I did not correct him, because correcting him would have been cruelty, and because some treacherous part of me wanted to hear the rest — wanted, God forgive me, to understand what had broken Julian too, what my employer's brother had fled from that no court-martial would ever write down in its neat columns of charges.

"He called me Ivo," I heard myself say stupidly, to no one. "He said Ivo. But he thinks I'm Tom."

"Fever mixes names," Cobb murmured. "Means nothing."

It meant everything. It meant nothing. I could not yet tell which.

* * *

We loaded him at half past midnight, wrapped in blankets, laid across the back seat of the car with his head in Ada's lap because she insisted and I hadn't the heart to fight her twice in one night. Cobb provided a flask of laudanum-and-brandy mixture, dosed to keep the boy quiet, and directions to the widow's cottage at Ruckinge — third turning past the church, lantern in the east window, knock twice and wait.

"One more thing." Cobb stood at the car door, rain streaming off the brim of his hat. "Tuesday's tide waits for no argument. Be at the Dungeness track-end by eleven, night side. If you're late, you're late till the following week, and the following week the world may not be so kind." He paused. "And Mr. Underhill."

"Yes?"

"The boy talked, you said. Delirium talk." Cobb's eyes were steady and unreadable in the lamplight. "Whatever you heard up those stairs — it stays up there. Not because I'm threatening you. Because if Reeve's people ever get hold of what that boy says in his sleep, they won't just hang him. They'll pull this whole coast apart thread by thread looking for everyone who helped him, and your Tuesday boat drowns with the rest." He touched his hat brim. "Goodnight, ma'am. Goodnight, Miss Ada. Mind the third turning — the signpost's been down since August."

We pulled out onto the coast road with the rain coming at us sideways and the wipers useless and the boy moaning softly in the back, and for six miles nobody spoke.

Then, from behind me, Ada's voice, very low, half to herself: "Eleven days he stayed. He did everything right for eleven days after losing his brother, and no one calls that a wound." A pause. The rain drummed. "What do we call it, when a man breaks doing everything right? What did we call Julian?"

"Alive," Edith said from beside me, so quietly I almost lost it under the engine. "However he managed it — however wrong the manner — we call him alive. And we bring the Brandt boy out on Tuesday, and we pay every debt along the way, because that is what promises are. Debts you pay when paying has become expensive." Her hand came to rest briefly, lightly, on my sleeve — a touch so unlike her that I nearly swerved the car. "Thank you for carrying him, Mr. Underhill."

"It's Ivo," I said. "When he said it, I — it seemed a shame to waste it."

Ahead, the road bent toward Ruckinge, and a church tower stood up black against the rain, and somewhere beyond it a lantern burned in an east window, waiting.

I did not yet know that Private Ellery would spend the whole drive mistaking me for his brother Tom — that he would talk, and talk, and that everything he said in that fevered voice would lodge in me like shrapnel, working deeper for years. I only knew that the car smelled of carbolic and brandy and rain, that Ada's fingers were in the boy's hair keeping the blankets snug, and that the debt I had come to Hythe to arrange had grown a second head while I wasn't looking.

Debts, I was learning, always did.
