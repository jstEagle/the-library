## 20. Moving Mr. Ellery

The favor came due on a Tuesday of low grey cloud, the kind of day when Kent seemed to hold its breath between rain and the memory of it.

The innkeeper — Hobbes, though everyone at the Smack Inn called him nothing at all, simply nodded in his direction as one nods to a load-bearing wall — appeared at Marlowe House before eight, standing in the yard with his cap in his hands like a man come to ask for a daughter's hand rather than a body's transport.

"He can't stay," Hobbes said. "Army doctor's been through Rye asking after wounded men not on any register. My cellar's no place for a lad bleeding slow. You've a motorcar, Mr. Underhill, and you've already got the look of a man who's done things he'll want forgiven someday. One more won't tip the scale."

I had wanted to say no. Let me be honest about that, since honesty is the one currency I came late to and have been hoarding ever since. I wanted to say no the way a man wants to keep his hand out of a fire. But Ada was watching from the kitchen window with her arms crossed, and Edith stood behind me in the hall, and neither of them said a word, which was worse than argument. The house had developed a way of deciding things without deciding them, and I always found myself already agreed before I'd consented.

"Where?" was all I asked.

"Ashford way. A cottage past the hop gardens. There's a woman there — his aunt, or near enough." Hobbes turned his cap once, twice. "He talks, mind. Fever-talk. Don't believe half of it and don't forget any of it."

* * *

They brought him out wrapped in sacking that smelled of hops and blood, and laid him across the back seat of the Talbot like cargo, except cargo doesn't moan when the wheels find a rut. He was younger than I expected — twenty-two perhaps, twenty-three, with the kind of face that hadn't finished deciding what it would be when it grew up. A dressing bound his shoulder where a bullet had gone through and someone field-stitched the exit wound with thread better suited to boots. His name, Hobbes said, was Ellery. First name Thomas, though he answered to nothing now but the weather inside his own skull.

Ada insisted on coming. She climbed into the passenger seat before I could formulate an objection, tucking her skirt beneath her with the brisk economy of someone who has ridden in worse vehicles on worse roads.

"You shouldn't be seen," I told her. "Reeve's men are still working the villages."

"So are yours," she said, which wasn't an answer, but it settled the matter the way most matters were settled in that year: by whoever spoke last with the most certainty. "I'll sit on the floor of the back if we're stopped. A girl and a driver. Nothing in the world more ordinary."

Edith watched us from the steps. She did not offer to come, and I understood why — her face was known now from Hythe to Canterbury, photographed for the papers beside words like *unruly* and *hysteric*. Her presence would turn a smuggling run into a spectacle. Instead she pressed something into my hand as I cranked the engine: a small flask.

"Bromide and brandy," she said. "For the pain, not the nerves. And Ivo—" She paused, and the morning light caught the grey at her temples, and for a moment she looked every one of her fifty-odd years and none of her reputation. "Whatever he says, remember he is saying it to someone else."

I didn't understand her then. I do now. That is often how Edith worked on me — planting instructions in my ear that took root weeks later, blooming without my noticing the season pass.

* * *

We went the back ways, because the front ways had grown eyes. Down lanes so narrow the hedges wrote on both flanks of the car in wet green script; across a ford where the water ran loud over flint; past a churchyard where the new graves of 1916 had begun to outnumber the old ones of centuries, the mounds still raw as wounds that hadn't scabbed.

Ellery talked almost from the first mile.

It began as fragments — numbers, mostly, map references, the names of villages in France that sounded like hymns sung backwards. Vermelles. Cuinchy. Givenchy. Ada leaned over the seat and held his good hand and murmured nonsense to him, gentle syllables, the kind of sound a mother makes that means nothing and everything. I drove and listened and tried not to listen, and discovered those are the same act.

Then, somewhere on the long straight between the ford and the hop gardens, he opened his eyes — fever-bright, unfocused, looking straight through the glass at the back of my head — and said:

"Charlie?"

I said nothing. One does not correct the delirious; Hobbes had warned me, and besides, some instinct older than sense told me the correction would cost more than the lie.

"Charlie, they said you'd come." His voice had changed. Underneath the rasp of fever there was something young in it, something that made my hands tighten on the wheel. "You got my letter then. You got it and you came."

"I came," Ada said softly, playing along, her eyes finding mine in the mirror. Hers were wide and sorry.

"They told us you was dead." A laugh cracked out of him, terrible, delighted. "Dead! Charlie Ellery, dead of a blighty one in Blighty itself. Mum'll have your photograph on the mantel with the black ribbon and everything. She'll have to take it down now. She'll grumble about the pins."

The road unspooled ahead of us, brown and glistening. Rain began again, fine as sifted flour, ticking on the hood.

"Tom," he said then — to himself, or to the air, correcting his own drift the way a man walks himself back along a path in fog — "Tom's here too, Charlie. Tom's here." And then, lower, with a shame so thick it seemed to choke him: "Tom's the one what ran."

* * *

We stopped once, at a spinney off the road, to give him water and check the dressing. Ada did the checking; she had steadier hands than I did, and less imagination about what lay under bandages. I stood with my back turned like a gentleman of the old school, listening to the rain work the leaves, and Ellery gripped my sleeve — when had he reached me? — and pulled me down until his mouth was at my ear.

"Charlie, listen. Listen, because they'll ask and I'll lie to them, but I can't lie to you."

His breath was hot and sour against my cheek. I should have pulled away. I didn't.

"It weren't the shelling." Each word came out deliberate, spaced, like stones laid across a stream. "Everyone thinks it's the shelling what breaks you, or the mud, or seeing your mates go. It ain't. Or — it is, but it's slow, like water on stone. What broke me was Tuesday." A pause. The rain filled it. "There was a Tuesday. Ordinary as bread. We'd been in the line eleven days and nothing happened. Nothing happened so hard I thought I'd go mad from the nothing of it. And I looked down the trench and saw Jenkins reading a letter, laughing at it, folding it away, and I thought —"

His grip tightened. Through the thin stuff of my coat his fingers felt like a bird's feet.

"— I thought, *he's got somewhere to put it all.* All this. He's got a door it goes out through. And I hadn't. Not since you stopped writing, Charlie. Not since March. I wrote and wrote and nothing come back and I thought, well, he's found out what I am. He knows now what I done over there, or what I couldn't do, and he's ashamed of me, and Mum's ashamed, and the whole world's just a trench with better weather."

"Tom," Ada said gently, from behind us. "That's not —"

"And so I ran." He said it flatly now, the confession spent, only the fact remaining. "Not from the guns. From the silence. Does that make sense, Charlie? Tell me it makes sense. Tell me anyone would've done the same."

I looked at his face — the flushed cheeks, the cracked lips, the eyes swimming somewhere ten years and four hundred miles from that spinney — and I thought of a man I had never met who had faked his death to escape a court-martial, and whose reasons nobody in our household had yet spoken aloud, because speaking them might make them true, or forgivable, or ours.

"It makes sense," I heard myself say. "More than you know."

* * *

The cottage, when we found it, sat beyond the hop gardens with its back to a wood, smoke rising from a chimney crooked as a question mark. The woman who answered the knock was sixty and built like a gatepost, and she took one look at the sacking bundle in my arms and said, "In the back room. Mind the cats," with such utter lack of surprise that I understood she had done this before, and would again, and that England in 1916 was stitched together everywhere by such women while the official country looked elsewhere.

We laid Ellery on a truckle bed. The aunt — Mrs. Pankhurst she was not; her name was Cobb, and she pronounced it with a defiance that dared comment — set to work with scissors and boiled linen and a competence that made my own hands feel ornamental. Ada fed him broth. Outside, the rain eased, and light came slanting through the window, catching the dust, catching the steam off the kettle, catching everything ordinary and making it briefly luminous.

Ellery surfaced once more from the fever as we prepared to leave. He found Ada's hand this time, and then mine, holding both like a man gripping two ropes over deep water.

"You'll write?" he asked me — asked *me*, still wearing his brother's shape in his swimming eyes. "When I'm well. You'll write and tell me it's forgiven? Because that's the thing, see. That's the whole thing. A man can stand anything if there's a letter coming."

I said yes. I lied to a dying-shallow boy in a smuggler's safehouse and felt the lie settle into me not like shame but like weight — the particular weight of a promise I intended, God help me, to keep.

* * *

We drove back in the long gold light of late afternoon, the lanes running with the day's rain, the hedgerows dripping. Neither of us spoke for miles. It was Ada who finally broke the quiet, not turning from the windscreen.

"You knew," she said. "What he meant. About the letters."

I kept my eyes on the road. "I read other people's post. It's a vice. It never seemed —" I searched for the word. "It never seemed to *matter*. Words going from one warm room to another. I thought history happened somewhere else and these were just the crumbs it dropped."

"And now?"

The Talbot took a bend; a pheasant exploded from the verge in a clatter of bronze. Ahead the land fell away toward the weald, all shadowed wood and pale pasture, beautiful in the disinterested way the English countryside manages even in wartime, perhaps especially then, as if beauty itself were an accusation.

"Now I think a letter is the difference between a man standing and a man running," I said. "Now I think Julian Marlowe ran from exactly what that boy ran from, and nobody has once said it out loud in that house, because Edith needs her brother to be a martyr or a spy, and either one can be honored. But a man who broke because he was alone in the dark — that man you can only forgive."

Ada was silent a moment. Then: "She'd forgive him. She has, probably, in every way except the one that counts."

"The one that counts being saying so to his face."

"To his face," she agreed, "when he comes home."

*If*, said the old Ivo, the detached Ivo, the man who believed history belonged to other people. But that man had been left somewhere on the road between the ford and the hop gardens, and what rode home in his place was someone still unnamed, holding a stranger's secret in each pocket.

* * *

Marlowe House, when we returned, had acquired a visitor.

A motorcar stood in the yard — military grey, mud-flecked, government plates. A captain's insignia glinted at a figure on the steps, talking with Edith, hat in hand, all courtesy and steel. Even at a hundred yards I knew the set of those shoulders. Reeve.

Ada's hand closed on my arm, hard enough to bruise.

"He's early," she breathed. "He wasn't due till — Ivo, he's *early*."

And I understood, with the clarity that arrives only after the chance to act has passed, that we had spent the day ferrying a deserter across half of Kent while the man hunting ghosts walked unchallenged through our empty house — and that whatever Ellery had confessed in his fever, we were about to learn what it costs to be caught holding secrets of one's own.
