## 21. Reeve's Offer

The frost came early that November night, silvering the yew hedge along Marlowe House's drive so that by the time Captain Reeve's motorcar turned in from the lane, the whole approach glittered like a thing cut from tin. Edith watched from the drawing-room window, a cup of tea cooling forgotten in her hand. She had known he would come. Men like Reeve did not spend three weeks asking questions in villages without eventually coming to sit down across the table from the woman they suspected, and to do it smiling, hat in hand, with all the courtesy of an age that believed politeness and menace were compatible — indeed, that they were married.

"Miss Marlowe." He stood in the hall shaking rain — no, melting frost — from his coat, and Ivo took the coat with the wooden face of a servant who had learned, these past months, to be made of oak. "I hope you'll forgive the hour."

"The hour is nine o'clock, Captain. Farmers keep later ones." She led him into the study because it was the room with the fewest windows and the most books, and because she wanted him surrounded by spines and paper — wanted him to feel, if only dimly, what he was up against when he came to bargain with a woman who lived among arguments the way other women lived among china.

He declined whisky. He accepted coffee. He sat with his knees together and his cap on his knee like a schoolboy called before a governor, and for four full minutes they spoke of nothing — the roads, the price of apples, the lamentable state of the Folkestone road since the military lorries had begun chewing it up. Edith let him. She had spent twenty years learning to wait out men who needed silence more than she did. Silence, for them, was a kind of itching.

At last he set down his cup. The porcelain clicked against the saucer, once, precisely, and she understood that this was the sound of a man closing one door and opening another.

"Your brother," Reeve said.

Edith kept her hands still in her lap. Outside, a rook called from the bare orchard, one dry note, like a stick snapped. "My brother is dead, Captain. At Loos. You will find his name on the memorial at Ashford, third panel from the left. I paid for the lettering myself. It cost eleven shillings."

"Yes," said Reeve mildly. "It does rather, doesn't it. Costing money, I mean, to have a name carved in stone. One wants to be sure of the name first."

The fire ticked. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard settled, and Edith thought of Ada — Ada asleep above them, or pretending to sleep, with Julian's worn letter hidden where she hid everything, in some seam of herself that Edith had not yet been permitted to see. Three weeks now since the stranger had come through the gate with rain running off her chin and a dead man's handwriting folded against her heart, and the house had never been the same. It was fuller. Fuller and more dangerous.

"I don't follow you," said Edith, who followed him perfectly.

"No," Reeve agreed pleasantly. "You wouldn't. Let me put it another way." He drew a paper from his breast pocket but did not open it — merely held it, the way a card player holds cards he has already won with. "There is a matter before the War Office concerning certain correspondence passing through this district. Circulars. Pamphlets. Printed material of a political nature, which is of course entirely legal—" he inclined his head toward her, a small ironic bow, "—and which is also, in certain instances, carrying rather more than ink."

"You've been reading my post."

"I've been reading *the King's* post," said Reeve. "A different animal altogether, though it bleeds the same." He unfolded the paper at last, smoothed it on his knee, and read aloud in the flat voice of a clerk: "'*The orchard is unguarded in autumn. The west ladder still stands. Tell them the fruit keeps.*'" He looked up. "Lovely. Almost pastoral. Do you know, Miss Marlowe, there are men in Whitehall who have stared at that sentence for six weeks and got nowhere. And then there are men — fewer men — who understand that an orchard is not always an orchard."

The room seemed to tilt very slightly, the way a boat tilts. Edith heard her own breathing and made it slow. *The west ladder.* She had read that circular herself, had passed it across this desk to be folded and sealed, had thought it one of Mrs. Pankhurst's milder metaphors about opportunity. All those months of paper through her hands, and threaded through them like a scarlet wire, Julian. Julian alive, Julian writing, Julian hiding inside the very movement that had already cost her so much — as though he had known, even from wherever he was, that the only postal system in England the authorities were too contemptuous to search properly was the one run by women in sashes.

She would not look at the ceiling, toward Ada's room. She kept her eyes on Reeve with the steadiness she had learned at a hundred platform edges, facing crowds that had come sometimes to listen and sometimes to throw things.

"If you have an accusation to make," she said, "make it. If you have a charge, prefer it. But if you have come to my home at nine o'clock at night to recite poetry about ladders, I will ring for Ivo to show you out, and we shall both have wasted an evening."

Something moved behind Reeve's eyes — respect, possibly, or its close cousin, disappointment. He refolded his paper.

"No accusation," he said. "No charge. What I have come to make, Miss Marlowe, is an offer."

* * *

Afterward, she would remember the shape of the next quarter hour more clearly than almost any conversation of her life — remember the way the lamplight lay along the edge of the desk, the smell of the cold coming off the windowpanes, the rook calling again somewhere in the dark orchard as though keeping time.

"Your brother is alive," Reeve said. "We believe he is alive. We believe he deserted before Loos — that the death report was convenient to more parties than one — and we believe that for two years he has been living abroad under another man's name, corresponding with persons in this country through channels that I am looking at right now, sitting in a warm study, drinking your coffee." He paused. "Desertion in the face of the enemy carries one penalty, Miss Marlowe. You know what it is. The newspapers know what it is. Your movement's enemies would know exactly what it is, and they would print it in letters two inches high, and beneath it they would print your name, and the words *sister of*."

Edith's hands had gone cold. She folded them anyway. There was a trick to it — she had learned it from watching her mother at their father's funeral — folding the hands so they had nothing to do but be folded, so the trembling had nowhere to go.

"And your offer?"

Reeve leaned forward. Up close, in the lamplight, she saw that he was younger than his manner — thirty-five perhaps, with the beginnings of grey at his temples and a tiredness around the mouth that no amount of discipline could quite iron away. It occurred to her, with a strange detached pity, that he was a man being used by his superiors and probably knew it.

"Julian comes back quietly," he said. "Through a side channel. No arrest at the dockside, no photographs. A medical board — his nerves, his health, there are ways of framing it — and a discharge. Quiet. Clean. He lives. Perhaps in Canada. Certainly not here, certainly not under his own name for some years. But he lives, Miss Marlowe, and he grows old, and there is no firing party, and no inquest, and no scandal."

"And in exchange."

"In exchange," said Reeve, "you withdraw."

The word sat in the room between them.

"Not forever. Not — publicly disgraced, nothing so crude. A letter to your federation. Ill health. Family circumstances." His voice softened with something that might have been genuine embarrassment. "Everyone understands family circumstances. You retire from the platform. No more rallies, no more speeches, no more — " he gestured vaguely at the bookshelves, at the pamphlets stacked in drifts on the side table, at the whole loud argumentative architecture of her life — "of this. Eighteen months. Two years, perhaps. By then the war will have changed everything anyway, and no one will remember, and you may return to whatever the world looks like on the far side of it."

"And if I decline?"

He spread his hands. "Then we proceed as we would proceed with anyone. We find him, Miss Marlowe. We are finding him already — every week the circle tightens, every village, every inn, every signal box along the coast line. And when we find him, none of it is quiet. None of it is clean. He faces a tribunal and the tribunal faces a war that needs examples, and you — " and here, for the first time, his voice lost its official polish and became simply a tired man's voice — "you face the newspapers. I have seen what they do. I would not wish it on my own sister."

*His own sister.* Edith looked at him and felt the terrible vertigo of recognizing oneself in an opponent — the same stubbornness, the same willingness to spend other people's peace for a principle. She wondered what cause had made him, and whether he had ever stood at a window at midnight wondering what it had cost.

"I shall need time," she said.

"One night," said Reeve, rising. "I'm authorized to give you until tomorrow evening. After that the decision passes upward, and you may trust me that the people above me are less pleasant to bargain with than I am." At the door he paused, cap in hand, and said — not officially, not as a soldier, but as one human being to another across a gulf neither of them had chosen — "For what it is worth, Miss Marlowe, I hope you say yes. Whatever else you are, you are the finest speaker I have heard in this county, and it would be a poor thing, this war taking both a brother and a voice."

And then the door closed, and the motorcar ground its gears on the frosted gravel, and Edith Marlowe stood alone in her study with the fire burning low and the word *withdraw* hanging in the air like smoke that would not rise.

* * *

She did not light the lamp again. She sat in the firelight with Julian's last true letter open on her knee — the real one, the one Ada had carried across half of Europe sewn into a coat lining, not the coded circulars but this: six pages in the handwriting she had known since he was eight years old and printing his name wrong on the flyleaf of his books.

*I know what I am asking,* he had written. *I know it better than you can, because I know what they will say about you, and I know that you have spent your whole life making yourself into someone they cannot dismiss, and I am asking you to stand next to a deserter while they try. If there were any other road, Edie, I swear to you on Mother's grave I would take it.*

*Edie.* No one living had called her that. Her throat ached with it.

She rose and walked the room, touching things the way one touches things when thinking — the spine of Mill, the cold glass of the window, the brass latch. Through the panes the orchard showed as a congregation of black shapes, leafless, patient, their branches holding up nothing but frost and starlight. Their father had planted those trees the year Julian was born. Forty years the apple trees had stood there, giving what they gave without argument, asking nothing but the pruning. She had stood beneath them as a girl and believed the world was a place where things grew because you tended them, and she had spent fifty years discovering how much tending the world required, and how little of it was ever done.

If she withdrew, Julian lived.

She made herself write it plainly, in her mind, the way she made speakers in her federation write plainly: *If I stop speaking, my brother does not die.* Eighteen months. Two years. He would be in Canada, alive, perhaps planting something — he had their father's hands, Julian, he had always been happiest with grafting wax under his fingernails. And the movement? The movement would lose her voice at the moment the franchise fight reached its crisis; the women in the manufacturing towns who wrote to her, the girl in Maidstone who had been imprisoned twice and fed by force and written to Edith afterwards in a hand that shook and said *you were the one who made me believe I was a person* — that girl would march on without her, yes, but Edith knew how much difference one voice at the right meeting made. She had built two decades of it. She knew the weight of a single voice the way a mason knows the weight of a single stone.

And if she refused — if she stood on platforms with her brother's name waiting in the newspapers like a loaded gun — then everything she had built would be turned into ammunition against itself. *The sister of a coward.* They would say he had run while she urged other people's sons forward; the logic was obscene and it would work, it would work beautifully, she had watched them do it with lesser women. Her silence would buy nothing then. She would lose him *and* the cause, both, and the losing would be public, and it would be used to bury a hundred other women's voices after hers.

Unless —

She stopped by the fire.

Unless Reeve's offer was not what it appeared. Unless the quietness he promised was the quietness of a man burying something. He had said *the death report was convenient to more parties than one*, and he had said it lightly, but Edith had spent a lifetime listening to men speak lightly about heavy things, and there had been a hairline crack under the lightness, a fault in the stone. Who at the War Office had processed a death report? Who had signed it? Why would a captain spend weeks haunting Kentish villages over one deserter when deserters ran like rabbits from a battue and were nobody's priority?

There was something underneath this. Something Reeve wanted buried along with Julian, or along with her.

She pressed her palm flat against the cold window glass until it ached, and thought: *they tie it together deliberately, the blood and the cause, they always do, they make you choose as if the choosing were simple, as if either thing alone were bearable.* Her mother had been given the same choice in a different key — husband or self — and had chosen the husband and been quietly hollowed by it, and had told Edith once, drunk on sherry at Christmas, *never let them offer you a choice between two parts of yourself, Edie, that's how they win, they make you cut yourself in half and call it duty.*

But Julian was not a part of herself. Julian was Julian. Julian aged seven teaching her to whistle through a blade of grass. Julian aged nineteen arguing politics across this very desk until their father threatened to confiscate the decanter. Julian going white and silent in August of 1914 and saying, in the garden, holding an apple he never ate: *if it comes, Edie, I've given my word to the Brandts, and a man is nothing but his word once the shooting starts.* She hadn't understood then. She understood now. The promise he had made in peacetime was pulling him through wartime like an undertow, and it had pulled him past the point where any flag could hold him, and he was alive, he was *alive*, and the whole machinery of the state was closing on him like a hand.

One night. He had given her one night.

Edith returned to her chair and took up her pen — not to decide, but to think, for she had always thought best in ink. She wrote at the top of the page, in her small upright hand: *What does withdrawal actually purchase?* And beneath it, listing, the way she listed arguments for a speech:

*1. His life — IF they keep their word. IF the discharge holds. IF "quiet" means what he says and not what it suits them to mean.*

*2. Nothing else. The cause loses. The girls lose. And I live the rest of my life knowing I bought one man's safety with a hundred women's voices, and that the purchase was witnessed, and will be collected upon.*

She looked at the two lines for a long time. Then she turned the page over and wrote on the back:

*What does refusal purchase?*

*1. My honour, such as it is.*

*2. His death, such as it will be — shot at dawn, or worse, dragged through the mud of a tribunal first.*

*3. And whatever it is Reeve is burying stays buried, and buried things have a way of growing.*

She put down the pen. The fire had sunk to a red heart of embers; the cold was coming up through the floorboards now, the honest Kentish cold that got into everything by December. Somewhere in the walls the house ticked and settled, doing what old houses do at night, keeping its own counsel.

There was a third road. There was nearly always a third road, if one refused both doors offered — that was the whole lesson of her life, that the menu was written by the enemy and one was under no obligation to order from it. Reeve wanted her choice between blood and cause because a woman divided was a woman defeated. Very well. Then she must find the move that kept both — that saved Julian *without* her silence being the coin that purchased it. Which meant finding what Reeve was hiding. Which meant, tomorrow, setting Ivo — quiet, watchful, unreadable Ivo, who noticed everything and confessed nothing — onto the scent of why a captain of the War Office cared so much about one dead man's paperwork.

And it meant telling the others. Tomorrow she would tell Ada and Ivo everything — the offer, the deadline, the arithmetic. A household that kept secrets from each other would hang separately. She had learned that much from the movement, if nothing else: you did not send women out to face the police without telling them what the police had.

Edith banked the fire with the poker, carefully, the way her father had taught her, coaxing the embers together so they would hold till morning. A night's work yet, a night's thinking. She would sit up with Julian's letter and with the memory of every woman whose letters she had ever answered, and by dawn she would know — not the safe answer, not the tidy one, but the true one, the one she could live inside for the rest of her life.

She climbed the stairs past Ada's door, where a thread of candlelight still burned beneath the wood, and paused there a moment, hand raised to knock — then let it fall. Let the girl sleep. Tomorrow would come soon enough with its hard arithmetic.

But in the grey hour before dawn, when Edith finally slept at last in her chair, the house below her stirred with a different kind of waking — a door eased open, a bag taken down soft as breath from a wardrobe shelf, and Julian's borrowed cipher scratched one last time onto a scrap of paper left weighted on the hall table, glimmering pale in the dark like a moth that had landed and meant to fly.
