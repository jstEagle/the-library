## 22. Ada Disappears

The morning she left began like any other, which is the cruelty of it. If the sky had been wrong — bruised, or too bright, or holding its breath the way Kent skies do before a storm — I might have felt it coming through the walls of Marlowe House the way you feel a train passing a mile off, in the soles of your feet and the fillings of your teeth. But it was an ordinary November morning, grey as dishwater, smelling of wet leaves and coal smoke, and I came down to the kitchen at half past seven expecting toast and Ada's argument with the marmalade.

She had opinions about marmalade. She thought English marmalade was a form of apology for everything else the country had done to her, and she said so every morning, scraping the pot with a knife as though she could find the good year at the bottom. Edith would answer that at least it was *our* bad marmalade, and Julian — who was not there yet, who was still two counties away behind his borrowed name — would have laughed, we all knew, because Ada told us he used to eat it straight from the jar with his finger when he thought nobody was looking, even at nineteen, even in front of the Brandts.

That was the texture of those weeks: a household rehearsing itself around an empty chair.

I noticed the first wrongness at the gate. Ada's boots were gone from where they always stood, tipped against the stone to drain, laces tucked inside so the birds couldn't take them for nesting. In their place were her good shoes, the buttoned ones she hated, set down neatly side by side. Neatly. That word should have stopped my heart then and there. Ada did nothing neatly. She crossed rooms like weather. She left cups on windowsills and books face-down and her hairpins in patterns only she could read. A neatness from Ada was a message, the way a folded flag is a message.

But I was thinking about the car. The Talbot had been running rough since Hythe — a fuel line, I suspected, or water in the petrol — and I went out into the yard with a spanner in my hand and the ordinary greed of a man who would rather fix an engine than face what was waiting in the house. I jacked up the near-side wheel. I let the cold get into my knuckles. I gave myself forty minutes of bolts and gaskets and the honest language of machinery, and all the while the house sat quiet behind me, quieter than it had been since Ada arrived soaked through its gate in the rain, and I called that peace.

It was Edith's voice that broke it. Not loud. That was worse. She stood at the scullery door with a piece of paper in both hands, held flat, the way you hold something that might be venomous, and she said my name — just "Ivo," just once — in a voice I had heard her use exactly twice before: once at the news of Julian's death, and once when she read the Brandt boy's name off the deportation list.

* * *

The note was written on the back of a page torn from a parish almanac, in pencil, in letters that meant nothing at all unless you knew the key.

Julian's cipher. The one Ivo had spent three nights over a candle cracking open back in October, after the signal box, after the photograph of the bearded stranger who was and was not dead — the method built on the suffrage circulars themselves, on the printed columns of a newsletter whose typesetting Julian had helped design in another life. Every fifth character of the body text, taken by a rule that shifted with the date. It was elegant. It was the work of a man who had learned to hide in plain sight because hiding anywhere else got people shot.

And Ada had learned it. Of course she had. He'd had months in that foreign lodging house, months of teaching the landlady's daughter how to carry his words without knowing — no. Not without knowing. Ada had known everything, eventually. She had chosen to be the vessel, and now she had poured herself out of it and gone.

I sat at the kitchen table with the almanac page under my hands and decoded it the way other men pray: mechanically, desperately, mouthing the counts. *Fifteen November. Take the nine o'clock from Ashford. Tell E. the chapel terms stand. Do not follow. This is mine to finish.*

"Do not follow." Four words, and beneath them, in smaller pencil, pressed so hard the paper had nearly given way, as if she had needed the graphite to break before she could believe herself: *Please.*

Edith sat opposite me with her hands wrapped around a teacup she wasn't drinking. Her hair wasn't fully pinned; there was grey at the temple I had never seen because she never let it show, and this morning, apparently, she had run out of the strength it took to hide things.

"She took the eleven-forty last night," Edith said. "Cook saw her go down the lane with her bag. Cook assumed she was posting a letter." A pause. "Ada hasn't posted a letter in her life that she didn't want someone to read."

"The chapel terms." My voice sounded far away to me, a voice on a wire. "Reeve's terms. Julian surrenders; the Brandts get their passage; Ada gets immunity. Those terms need all of us. They need her especially — she's the one they'll want to prosecute. She's the one carrying papers."

"Yes."

"So if she goes alone—"

"If she goes alone, she is a stateless young woman of enemy-associated birth attempting an unauthorised channel crossing with forged documents, caught between a War Office captain who wants a scandal buried and a port authority that has already flagged her once." Edith said it flatly, item by item, like a clerk reading an inventory of a burned house. Then her composure did something I will remember until I die: it didn't crack, it *thinned*, the way ice thins — you could suddenly see all the black water underneath. "She's twenty-two years old, Ivo. Her father is in a camp on the Isle of Man because of a surname. And she has decided that the cheapest thing in this whole affair is herself."

* * *

I don't remember deciding to go to the garage. I remember the smell of it — petrol and leather and the cold iron breath of the pit — and I remember kneeling by the Talbot with the wheel still jacked up, staring at a brake shoe I had absolutely no interest in, and understanding, in one long vertical drop, something I had been refusing to understand since a rainy afternoon at the gate.

I loved her.

Not the way I'd loved the idea of her — the mystery at the gate, the girl with the dead man's letter, the puzzle that made my dull life luminous. That had been real enough, but it had been safe, the love of a man for a story. This was different. This was the knowledge that the kitchen would be silent in the mornings. That the marmalade would sit untouched, going sugary, and no one would insult it. That there was a person alive in the world who said *please* in cipher because she couldn't bear to say it aloud, and she was on a train, or soon would be, walking toward a port where men with stamps could erase her with a signature.

History happened elsewhere. That had been my creed for thirty-one years. Other men fought, other men died, other men's letters carried the century's blood past me while I drove it through the lanes and read it furtively and put it back sealed. And here was history — small, warm, furious, wearing buttoned shoes she hated — climbing onto the nine o'clock from Ashford, and I understood that it had never been elsewhere. It had been in the passenger seat the whole time.

"Her bag," I said. I was on my feet without remembering standing. "Did Cook say how big?"

"A valise. Small."

"Not the big one. Not the one she packed for Dover." I was already moving, taking the stairs two at a time, taking them wrong and catching myself on the rail. "Then she doesn't mean to come back. You pack light for a crossing you mean to make alone and disappear on the far side. Edith—" I stopped in her doorway. She had followed me into the hall, and up close I could see she hadn't slept either, that the whole house had been a held breath for days and we had all pretended otherwise. "When does Reeve's man do his rounds?"

"Tuesdays and Fridays. Today is Thursday."

"Then we have until tomorrow before anyone official knows she's gone. And the nine o'clock gets into Folkestone at ten-twenty." I looked at my watch, that little inherited watch with the cracked crystal that I wound every morning of my life without thinking about time. "If I leave now, I can be in Folkestone by noon."

"We," Edith said.

"The roads—"

"I have spent four years being told to stay home by men who meant well." Something came up through her thinning composure that was not fear. It was the thing underneath the fear, the ore the fear was dug from, and I had heard it in her voice at rallies through a megaphone and never expected to hear it across a hallway at seven in the morning. "Get the car, Ivo."

* * *

The Talbot ran rough all the way to Ashford and I drove her hard anyway, because a fuel line can be forgiven and a train cannot. Edith sat beside me with the almanac page smoothed on her knee, reading it again and again as if the pencil might revise itself. The lanes streamed past — bare hedgerows, rooks lifting off ploughed fields, a church clock saying ten minutes to some hour forever — and all of it looked exactly as it had looked on a thousand deliveries, and none of it was the same, because now I was driving toward instead of past.

At Ashford station I went in alone, Edith keeping low in the motor with her hat down, and showed a porter sixpence and asked, casual as sin, whether the London boat train had been busy that morning. He scratched his ear. Busy-ish. One young woman, dark-haired, foreign-seeming, asked him which platform for the Folkestone boat connection — yes, he remembered, because she'd asked whether the crossing tickets could be bought at the harbour or only in advance, and that wasn't a question people usually asked, was it, and he'd told her at the harbour, cash, no questions.

No questions except hers, I thought. She'd been asking the questions that mattered, working it out alone in a station hall with her heart going like mine was going now.

"She bought a single?" I asked.

"Couldn't say, sir. Didn't see."

A single. I walked back to the car with the sixpence sweating in my palm and told Edith, and she closed her eyes for one second — one second, no more — and said, "Then we are not bringing her home. We are catching her before the water does."

We drove. The Weald went by, golding into winter. Somewhere past Elham the engine coughed and I talked to it under my breath, coaxing, threatening, and Edith said, "You know, she isn't doing this to spite us."

"I know."

"She's doing it because everyone she has ever belonged to has been taken away by committees and stamps and men with lists. Her father. Julian, in his way. Her country, which never agreed to have her." Edith watched the road. "She thinks if she removes herself from the equation, the equation resolves. That the rest of us — Julian's surrender, the Brandts, your testimony — can proceed without her becoming the price."

"And you think differently."

"I think she has spent her whole life believing she is the expendable clause." Edith's voice dropped, and for a moment it wasn't Edith the platform speaking, it was a sister. "I know what that belief costs, Ivo. I've paid instalments on it myself. But it is a lie, and lies like that kill quietly, and I refuse — I *refuse* — to let her pay for our promise with the only coin she believes she has."

I said nothing. There was nothing to say that wasn't a confession, and the confession was enormous and shapeless and had no business being shouted at fifty miles an hour over a failing engine. So I drove, and the confession rode with us, unnamed, filling the car like fog fills a valley.

* * *

Folkestone rose out of the coast haze the way it always did — chimneys, gulls, the smell of fish and funnel smoke — and my stomach went over as we came down Leas Cliff and saw the harbour below, the swing bridge, the boats, the little stone rooms where men sat behind ledgers with the power of kingdoms.

Because there, at the harbour steps, talking to a ticket clerk, her collar up and her hat pulled low and her bag gripped in both hands like a rail at the edge of a height —

There was a woman. Dark-haired. Small. Standing very straight in the way people stand when standing straight is all the armour they have left.

I brought the car to the top of the slope and stopped dead, engine ticking, and Edith put her hand on my arm. "Wait," she breathed. "Look."

And I looked, and my relief curdled, because fifty yards along the quay, leaning against the customs shed with a newspaper he was not reading, stood a man in a good coat with the stillness of a man on duty. I had seen him twice before, once outside a village post office and once across a rally crowd, and both times Captain Reeve had worn exactly that expression: patient, courteous, and utterly immovable, a door that had decided you were not going through it.

He hadn't followed us. He hadn't needed to. He had simply reasoned the way Ada reasoned — that she would go alone, that she would go cheap, that she would go by the harbour — and he had come down ahead of her and waited, reading yesterday's news, letting her walk all the way to the water before he moved.

Ada finished at the ticket window. The clerk shook his head — papers, the gesture said, papers first — and she turned, and I saw her face full on for the first time in three days, and it was not frightened.

It was resolved. And that frightened me more than anything else that morning, because a frightened girl can be talked out of a boat, but a resolved one has already said goodbye to everyone she loves, and Ivo Underhill, driver of motors, reader of other people's letters, coward by long habit and philosophy, understood that in about ninety seconds she was going to walk directly past that customs shed, and that Captain Reeve was going to unfold himself from the wall, and that whatever happened next would happen to her — to *her*, to the person history had finally, irrevocably happened to —

"Drive down," Edith said, and her hand was iron on my arm. "*Now.*"
