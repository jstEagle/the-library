## 23. The Chase to Folkestone

### Ivo

The note was in Ada's hand, but it wasn't in her voice.

That was the thing Ivo understood first, standing in the cold hall of Marlowe House with the paper trembling slightly between his fingers — that handwriting could lie the way faces lied, that a neat, careful script could say *Gone to Folkestone. Don't follow.* while the woman who wrote it screamed. He had spent years reading other people's letters, learning the difference between what people wrote and what they meant, and this note meant *come after me.*

"She's gone," he said, and his voice came out flat and strange, as though someone else were using it.

Edith came down the stairs two at a time. She had not undressed; he'd heard her pacing past midnight, the floorboards above his room keeping her own restless time. She took the note without asking, read it once, and handed it back.

"The seven-twelve from Ashford connects at Folkestone Junction with the boat train," she said. "If she caught it."

"If." The word sat in Ivo's stomach like a stone swallowed whole.

"Then we drive."

"Miss Marlowe — Edith — Reeve's men are on the coast road. The landlord at Hythe said—"

"I know what the landlord said." She was already pulling her coat from the hook, already pinning her hat at an angle that admitted no argument. "Ivo. She went alone because she thought she was sparing us. That is the single most foolish and the single most generous act I have witnessed in this house all year, and I have witnessed a great many of both. We are going. Now."

He went. Of course he went. He fetched the key from its nail and the rug from the boot and cranked the engine until his shoulder burned and the Hupmobile coughed itself awake into the grey hour before dawn, when the mist lay on the orchard like a sheet drawn over something sleeping. Edith climbed in beside him and did not fasten the screen, and the cold came through the gap between their shoulders and made them allies of one another against it.

The gates swung open onto a road the colour of pewter. Somewhere behind them a thrush began, absurdly, to sing.

"Left or right?" Ivo asked.

"Right. The main road is watched, so we take the main road."

He glanced at her. "That's — forgive me — mad."

"It's exactly what they'll expect us not to do. Reeve's men watch lanes and back roads for people who are hiding. We are not hiding. We are a lady and her chauffeur motoring to the seaside before breakfast, which is eccentric, but only the sort of eccentricity England expects of women like me." A pause. "Besides. If they stop us, I intend to be extremely annoying about my rights as a ratepayer, and while they're suffering through it, you will think."

It was such a Edith thing to say that Ivo laughed — one short, startled bark of a laugh that surprised them both — and then the laughter died, because ahead of them the road unspooled white in the fog and somewhere down its whole grey length Ada was walking or waiting or being questioned, alone, with papers that would not hold.

He drove faster than he had ever driven. The hedges became ribbons. Rooks lifted off the ploughed fields like scraps of torn letter-paper, black marks scattered across a page, and Ivo thought: *I have spent my whole life reading other people's mail, and the one letter I ever needed to answer is twenty miles ahead of me and I cannot reach it fast enough.*

At Stone Street a motorcar stood pulled up on the verge, bonnet raised, a man in a civilian cap bent over the engine with a military straightness to his back that fooled nobody. Ivo slowed to a crawl, touched his cap, asked after the road to Hythe in his best village voice — slow, pleasant, half-witted — and the man waved him on without looking up, and it was only a mile later that Ivo let out the breath he'd been holding and found his hands aching from gripping the wheel.

"He'll report a car passing," Edith said quietly.

"Not ours. He never saw the number."

"He'll remember your face."

"Then by the time he does," Ivo said, "we'll be where we're going."

He did not add what both of them knew: that where they were going might be nowhere good at all.

### Edith

Edith had faced hostile crowds, magistrates, hunger, and her father's disappointment, and she had learned in each case the same discipline: fear was fuel, and terror, properly rationed, could burn a very long way. But there was no proper rationing terror now. It came in floods, between the hedgerows, at every bend of the coast road, and she sat with her hands folded in her lap and let it come and refused, absolutely refused, to show it.

Ada. The girl had been with them scarcely three months, and Edith could not find the seam in herself where Ada ended and the rest began. It alarmed her how quickly the seam had vanished. Julian had always been the quick-bonding one, the brother who collected stray dogs and lost causes and foreign friends; Edith had prided herself on judgment, on distance, on the long view. And then a rain-soaked stranger had stood at her gate holding a letter in a dead man's handwriting, and within a season Edith had rearranged her entire architecture of risk around the girl's safety without noticing the scaffolding go up.

*I am forty-six years old,* she thought, watching the fog tear open over Romney Marsh, *and I have just discovered I love like my brother does.*

The Marsh spread out below them, silver-green and drowned-looking, sheep drifting through the mist like thoughts not fully formed. Royal Military Canal glinted along its ancient line, dug against a French invasion that never came, and Edith thought bitterly that England had always been digging canals against the wrong enemies. The enemy was not across the water. The enemy was Captain Reeve's tidy conviction that he served order, and the machinery behind him that ground up strangers and called it security, and her own government, which would intern a girl's father on the strength of his surname and lose him in the files.

"We should have gone with her," Ivo said suddenly. "The night she talked of crossing. We should have—"

"You wanted to cross. I forbade it. If blame is being distributed this morning, I'll take the largest share." She said it more sharply than she intended and saw him flinch, and softened. "No. Listen to me. Ada chose this. She left a note written in Julian's cipher — did you notice? Not her usual hand. She used the borrowed cipher because she knew we'd read it correctly, and because she wanted us to know she'd learned it well enough to leave. That wasn't a farewell, Ivo. That was a message. *Don't follow* written by someone who taught herself our codes — that's *follow me carefully.*"

He was silent for a moment. Then, grudgingly: "You've been reading letters too long yourself."

"I learned from the best bad example in Kent."

They came down into Hythe with the church tower rising out of the mist and the sea-smell strengthening, salt and weed and tar, and Edith's heart contracted at the sight of a khaki figure outside the Town Hall — but the soldier was only a recruiting poster come to life, collecting signatures from shopkeepers, and they passed through the town unmolested, along the canal, past the inn where everything had begun, and turned toward the hills.

It was at Dymchurch that they met the second car.

It came toward them slowly, crawling along the sea wall, and Edith saw the man in the passenger seat lean out to look at them — a lean she recognized, the particular forward tilt of a man checking a face against a description. Reeve's men had descriptions now. Of course they did. She fixed her gaze on the middle distance with the serene, faintly bored expression she wore on platforms when hecklers started, and Ivo drove past at exactly the speed of a man with nothing to hide, and neither of them spoke for a full minute afterward.

"They're sweeping the coast road," Ivo said at last.

"Then they haven't found her yet. If they had, they'd have stopped looking." She hated herself for the arithmetic of it — *not-found-yet* as good news — but arithmetic was what she had. "Which means she's either still traveling or already at the port. Either way, Folkestone. Faster now, Ivo. Take the risk on the bends."

He took the risks on the bends. The little car flung itself along the ridge above the Channel, and below them the sea appeared and vanished, appeared and vanished, grey-green and enormous, and Edith thought of Julian crossing it in some other direction under another man's name, and of Herr Brandt's son who would soon cross it if they failed, and of how much of all their lives had come to hang upon that narrow water.

Her hands, folded in her lap, had gone white at the knuckles. She unfolded them deliberately and pressed them flat against her skirt.

*Hold on, Ada,* she thought, to the fog and the miles and whatever listening God presided over coastal roads. *We are coming. Be clever a little longer.*

### Ivo

Folkestone assembled itself out of the haze in stages: first the smell of fish and coal smoke, then the gulls' arguments overhead, then the town climbing its cliff in terraces of wet brick, and finally the harbour, where the steamer lay against the quay with steam already feathering from her funnel, and the whole waterfront seethed with soldiers home on leave and soldiers going out and families clinging on the pavement and porters shouting and everywhere, everywhere, uniforms.

Ivo brought the car down Harbour Street at a walk, eyes raking the crowd. Looking for a small figure in a dark coat. Looking, he admitted to himself, for the particular way Ada held her head — chin up, eyes doing the work her height couldn't — that he had somehow memorized without ever deciding to.

"There," Edith said.

But it wasn't her. It was a policeman at the foot of the gangway, and beside the policeman, a second man in plain clothes with a ledger under his arm, and they were speaking to every third person who approached the boat train passengers queuing at the customs shed, turning papers over, asking questions. Papers. The word went through Ivo like cold water. Her papers were flagged; the landlord had warned them; Reeve had had days.

He parked badly, half on the cobbles, and walked fast, Edith matching him stride for stride, and the crowd closed around them — a good crowd, a kind crowd, full of mothers and kitbags and one band playing somewhere — and then he saw her.

She was sitting on a bench beneath the harbour wall, between a stack of mail sacks and a mooring bollard, with a constable standing over her and her carpetbag on the ground at her feet, and her hands were folded in her lap exactly the way Edith's were folded, and she was talking — he could see her talking, calm, reasonable, smiling even — and Ivo felt relief hit him so hard it was nearly pain, and then, a half-second later, the true shape of the scene arrived: the constable was not listening. The plain-clothes man from the gangway was coming over with his ledger open. And Ada's smile, seen close, was the smile of someone holding a door shut against a flood.

"—entirely a family matter," she was saying as they reached her. "My aunt is traveling and I was to bring her a parcel she forgot, only the trains were delayed, and if you'll just—"

"Miss—" the constable checked his notebook, and Ivo's blood stopped. "*Voss.* You gave the ticket clerk the name Voss, but your bag has initials that don't match, and the clerk remembers you asking twice about sailing times. So we're going to step into the office and sort out who you actually are, and—"

"That won't be necessary."

Edith's voice. Ivo had heard her use it perhaps four times in all the months he'd known her: the platform voice with the warmth stripped out of it, the voice that had faced magistrates and made them feel like schoolboys. Both the constable and the plain-clothes man turned.

"Mrs. Marlowe," the plain-clothes man said, and something in the way he said it told Ivo he'd been given her name too. Everyone had been given everyone's name.

"This young woman is my secretary," Edith said. "Ada Brandt — yes, that name, officer, write it down, I'm sure you have instructions about names. She is carrying documents for me. My documents. Which makes any question about her identity a question about mine, and I will answer those questions in the presence of my solicitor, whom I shall telephone from the Harbour Office directly, unless you'd prefer I telephone the newspapers instead? They do so enjoy a story about women detained at ports without charge."

The constable looked at the plain-clothes man. The plain-clothes man looked at Ada, and then at Edith, weighing something — and Ivo watched the scales tip, watched the man decide that Mrs. Marlowe of Marlowe House was precisely the sort of troublesome gentlewoman whose arrest would cost more paperwork than it bought, and hated him, and blessed him, all in the same breath.

"A misunderstanding, no doubt," the man said stiffly. "But I'll need the lady's true particulars before she boards anything. There are regulations."

"Then you shall have them, properly, through the proper channels." Edith took Ada's arm. "Come along, Miss Brandt."

Ada rose. For just a moment — a moment no one else could have seen, a flicker measured in eyelashes — her composure cracked, and Ivo saw underneath it: the exhaustion, the terror banked like a fire carried all night in a bucket, and something else, something that looked unbearably like shame at being found, at being rescued, at needing it. Their eyes met. Hers said *you shouldn't have come.* His tried to say back: *of course we came. What else was there?*

They walked away from the bench together, three abreast, unhurried, a lady and her secretary and her chauffeur, and only when they reached the shadow of the harbour arches did Ada's knees give — just slightly, just enough that Ivo got a shoulder under her arm without appearing to move at all.

"You followed me," she whispered. "I wrote *don't follow.*"

"You wrote it in cipher," Edith said grimly. "In our cipher. Never leave instructions to a household in a language the household invented. Sit down before you fall down. Ivo — the car."

But Ada caught his sleeve as he turned. Her fingers were ice through his cuff.

"The boat," she said. "Ivo, listen — the boy. Emil Brandt. He's here. On this quay. I didn't come to cross alone — I couldn't get him passage without help and I couldn't ask for help without putting you all— He's waiting by the fish sheds with the smuggler's man, and the sailing is at noon, and that man with the ledger has been checking every young man of the right age." Her voice frayed at the edge, and she pulled it straight again by sheer will. "Reeve isn't looking for me anymore. He's found something better. He's looking for *him.*"

Out beyond the harbour arm, the steamer's whistle sounded, long and low, and the sound rolled across the water and came back changed, and Ivo understood that the day had only just begun deciding what it would take from them.
