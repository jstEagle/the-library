## 24. Interrogation Room

The room they put me in smelled of cold tea and lamp oil and something underneath both — old fear, soaked into the walls the way damp soaks into plaster. A single window, high and barred, let down a grey rectangle of light that fell across a table scarred by generations of men who had drummed their fingers on it while deciding what to do with people.

They had taken my papers. That was the first thing I noticed about myself in that room: how much of me lived on paper. Ada Kessler-Brown, twenty-three years of age, born Rotterdam, resident of England since 1909, daughter of Heinrich Kessler, naturalised British subject (suspended), landlady's daughter, secretary, courier, liar. Without the papers I was just a girl with wet boots and salt-stiff hair sitting in a chair too tall for her, watching the door.

I made myself count things. The wall had forty-one cracks visible from where I sat, if one counted the fine ones branching off the coarse. The lamp above the table hissed at intervals of roughly eleven seconds. My hands were steady. I looked at them lying flat on my knees, palms down, as though pinning two small frightened animals, and I was surprised — genuinely surprised — to find them still. Somewhere between the dock gates and this chair, terror had burned itself down to coals, and coals give light, and light was what I needed now.

*You are a governess seeking employment in Boulogne,* I told myself. *You were turned back because your references are abroad. You are annoyed. You are tired. You are not afraid.*

The door opened without a knock. Captain Reeve came in the way weather comes in — no drama, just a change in pressure. He carried a leather folder under one arm and a chipped enamel mug in the other hand, and he set the mug on the table in front of me before he sat down.

"You haven't been offered anything," he said. "But shouting through a door is undignified for both of us."

I looked at the tea. It was the colour of strong regret. "Thank you," I said, because my mother had raised me to thank people even when I suspected them of everything, and because courtesy, I had already learned in twenty minutes of waiting, was a kind of armour. They could search a spy's luggage. Courtesy gave them nothing to seize.

Reeve sat opposite me and did not open the folder. He folded his hands on it instead, which told me the folder was theatre, and that the real examination would be conducted with nothing but his face. He had a good face for it — patient, faintly amused, the face of a man who has watched a great many liars and has come to enjoy the variety.

"Miss Brown," he said.

"Miss Kessler-Brown." I said it mildly. "Both names are mine. I'd hate for you to have an incomplete file."

Something flickered behind his eyes — not anger; interest. "Kessler. German name."

"My father's. He came to England in 1887, before I was born. He managed a shipping office in Rotterdam, then a warehouse in Maidstone. He has paid English taxes for twenty-six years, which I'm told makes him more English than half the House of Lords, though nobody has thought to tell the Home Office."

"And yet," Reeve said, "he was interned in May."

The word went through me like a fishhook. I kept my hands flat. "He was," I agreed. "Which is why I am going to France, Captain. There is work for an English-speaking governess in Boulogne, and there is no wage in England for the daughter of an enemy alien, whatever she calls herself. You've read the same arithmetic in my file that I live in."

"Mm." He leaned back. The chair creaked its opinion. "Tell me about your crossing. You presented yourself at the pier office at six this morning. The boat doesn't sail until noon. Why so early?"

"I was told to be early. I have been told all my life that people like me should be early, should be quiet, should be grateful, should be invisible. This morning I simply obeyed the habit." It was true, every word of it, and I let the truth do the work, because a lie built on truth holds weight the way a keel holds a mast.

"And when the clerk found a flag on your papers—"

"A flag?"

"Don't play innocent, Miss Kessler-Brown. It doesn't suit you; you're far too intelligent. Your file was marked. Suspicious correspondence received at this address in Rotterdam. Letters in cipher."

"In cipher," I repeated, and laughed — I actually laughed, a short helpless sound that surprised us both. "Captain, you've read letters written by my aunt Wilhelmina. She writes to me in Dutch, which I grant looks like cipher to an Englishman, but it is only Dutch. She spells *butter* four different ways in one letter because her eyes are bad and her pride is worse. If that's the cipher that brought me in, then your codebreakers should be reassigned to counting her spelling errors. It would keep them busy until Christmas."

The lamp hissed. Eleven seconds. Reeve watched me the whole time without blinking, and I understood that the laughter had been a risk and possibly a mistake, and that he was now recalibrating me from *frightened girl* to *something else*, and that *something else* might be worse.

"Your aunt," he said. "Wilhelmina Kessler. And your correspondent at Marlowe House? Mrs. Marlowe. Edith Marlowe."

Here it was. Here was the door with the fire behind it. I had known it would come; I had rehearsed it on the train, in the queue, on the wet stones of the pier, rehearsed it so many times the words had worn smooth like sea glass. But rehearsal is one country and the interrogation room is another, and between them runs a strait wider than the one outside these walls.

"She is my employer," I said. "Or she was, briefly. I wrote to her last spring asking after a position. She took me on as a temporary secretary while her regular girl was ill. When the position ended, we corresponded. She is kind. She recommended me for the Boulogne post. Is kindness now a suspicious correspondence?"

"Mrs. Marlowe," Reeve said, as though I hadn't spoken, "is a woman who stands on platforms and tells crowds that the government of this country is illegitimate because it excludes half its population. She corresponds with pacifists in Holland. She receives parcels of printed matter that would take your aunt's spelling errors and raise them to an art form. And three weeks ago, one of those parcels contained a circular with a marginal annotation that our analysts believe may be a book code." He paused. "Do you know anything about codes, Miss Kessler-Brown?"

"No," I said.

It was true. God forgive me, it was even true — Ivo had done the deciphering, not I. I knew the method now, could recite it, could probably reconstruct it, but I had never once worked a cipher with my own hands. Truth is a strange country; it has borders everywhere and none of them hold.

"No?" Reeve said softly.

"I know about letters. I know about waiting for them, and fearing them, and reading them eleven times because the eleventh time they'll finally say what they meant. Codes are for people with secrets worth the machinery. I have only ever had news."

"And what news does a governess carry that gets flagged at two ports?"

"One port," I said. "This one. I was waved through Rotterdam without a question. Perhaps your Dutch colleagues are careless. Or perhaps," and here I leaned forward, and let my voice drop into the register my mother used for telling the butcher he was wrong, "perhaps someone in England decided a girl with a German name ought to be stopped, and the flag went out ahead of me like a dog sent to bite before anyone asked whether I'd done anything at all. Has anyone asked, Captain? Has one person in this building asked me a single question about what I have actually done? Or only about what I might be?"

The silence stretched. Outside the high window a gull cried, once, twice — the sound of the sea reaching inland to touch the place where they held people, and I thought absurdly that the gull had freedom of movement I envied.

Reeve opened the folder.

What was inside, I couldn't see. But he turned pages slowly, letting me hear them, letting me understand that he had a document for every answer I might give, and that documents, unlike girls, never get tired.

"Ada Kessler-Brown," he read. "Born Rotterdam, 1890. Mother, Constance Brown, English, deceased 1911. Father, Heinrich Kessler, interned Knockaloe, Isle of Man, category B. Educated at the English school in Rotterdam. Employed Maidstone, 1909–1912, household of—" he glanced up— "shall we say, various families. Then a gap."

"A gap," I said.

"Two years, Miss Brown, with no employment record. From autumn 1912 to spring of this year. Where were you?"

And there it was. Not the door with the fire — the floor itself opening. Because the gap was Julian. The gap was a lodger called Mr. Ashworth who had come to my mother's house with quiet manners and a cough he hid and books in three languages, and who had stayed two years and taught me grafting and taught me patience and gone away leaving a promise and a bundle of letters. Every true thing I could say about those years pointed at him like a compass needle, and every false thing I could build around him would collapse the moment anyone pulled one thread.

I had perhaps four seconds. In interrogation, I was learning, you live your life in four-second instalments.

"I kept house for my mother until she died," I said. "Then I kept house for myself, which pays nothing and therefore appears nowhere. Then I took in lodgers. One of them was a Mr. Ashworth, a commercial agent for a Rotterdam firm. He can confirm my character, if you can find him — though I'd be obliged if you wouldn't trouble him; he was ill for much of his stay, and illness is tedious to explain twice."

"Where is Mr. Ashworth now?"

"On the Continent somewhere. His firm moved him. He wrote to me until the summer." *Until the summer.* Until Loos, until the telegram that never came, until the letter in a dead man's hand arrived in Kent and turned my quiet life into a boat with a hole in it.

"Convenient," Reeve said, "a witness on the far side of a war."

"He's not a witness, Captain. He's a man who owed me rent." I said it dryly enough that he almost smiled, and I saw — this was the strange thing, the thing I would remember all my life — I saw that he *wanted* to believe me. Some small part of that patient grey face wanted very much for the story to be simple: a girl, a dead mother, an interned father, a governess's arithmetic. I think it was because he had spent two years hunting ghosts across paperwork, and a simple story would have been a rest.

But wanting is not believing, and he closed the folder.

"Here is what troubles me," he said conversationally, folding his hands again. "Not your aunt's spelling. Not the governess post, which is genuine — I telephoned the agency while you were cooling your heels, and they do expect a Miss Brown. What troubles me is this." He drew a single sheet from the folder and laid it face-down on the table, keeping two fingers on it like a man holding a lid on a pot. "Three days ago, a motor car registered to Mrs. Marlowe passed through Hythe. Its occupants included a young woman matching your description. The innkeeper at the Rose and Anchor remembers her particularly. He says she asked questions about tides."

The lamp hissed. Eleven seconds. Forty-one cracks.

"Tides," I said. "Yes."

He waited. It was a good technique and I hated him for how well he used it — the silence shaped exactly like a trapdoor.

"I asked whether the crossing would be rough," I said. "I have been seasick on the Dover packet and I did not wish to repeat the experience. The innkeeper told me the winter tides run hard in the Sound. He also told me his wife makes a passable mutton pie, and that the Germans will be beaten by Easter, and that young women travelling alone want watching. Two of his three opinions were nonsense. I judged the third might be accurate, given present company."

For a moment — a real moment, I would swear to it — Captain Reeve's mouth twitched at one corner, and I understood that I had scored, however lightly, however temporarily, and that scoring against this man was like scratching paint off a ship's hull: satisfying, cosmetic, and ultimately irrelevant to seaworthiness.

"You're quick," he said. "That's not a compliment in my trade. Quick people are either innocent and clever or guilty and clever, and the paperwork looks identical from this side of the table." He stood, taking the sheet with him, and buttoned his jacket with the unhurried air of a man closing shop for the day. "You'll be kept overnight. There's a cot and a blanket and a jug of water, and a matron who will pretend not to listen to anything you say aloud. Tomorrow we continue. I'd advise you to spend the evening remembering your two missing years in more detail. Memory is like tide, Miss Brown — it comes in whether you invite it or not."

At the door he paused, hand on the latch, and did not turn around.

"For what it's worth," he said, "the tea is better in the morning. Fresh pot. And the girl who brings it has a brother at the front and no opinions about Germans at all. You might find her company less hostile than mine."

Then he was gone, and the lock spoke its one dry syllable behind him, and I was alone with the forty-one cracks and the cold tea and the enormous whiteness of the walls.

* * *

They do not tell you, when they lock you up, how loud a locked room is. There was the lamp, hissing its eleven seconds. There was the water in the jug, ticking as it cooled. There was the sea, faint but constant, working at the harbour wall like a patient animal. And under all of it there was my own blood, which sounded, in that silence, exactly like someone saying my name over and over in a language I was trying to forget.

I lay on the cot with the blanket to my chin and I took stock, as Papa taught me to take stock of a warehouse — shelf by shelf, crate by crate, everything accounted for or written off.

Assets: a cover story with real bones in it. A governess post that existed. An aunt whose spelling was, for once, useful. A temper I had mostly kept leashed, except twice, and both times the leash had held. A face, apparently, that could make a professional hunter want to believe in innocence.

Liabilities: Hythe. The Rose and Anchor. Tides. Ivo's motor car seen by a man who sold gossip along with ale. And the two missing years, which I had papered over with *Mr. Ashworth*, and which would tear like wet paper the moment Reeve found one thread — a photograph, a landlord's ledger, a neighbour in Rotterdam who remembered the quiet Englishman with the cough and the foreign books.

And beneath the assets and liabilities, unentered on any ledger, the thing I could not write down even in the privacy of my own skull: that I was not only protecting Julian. I was protecting Ivo, who had driven that conspicuous car through Hythe like a lighthouse on wheels; protecting Edith, whose name alone would hang her; protecting the Brandt boy, who was at this hour, God willing, in a barn outside Aldington with straw in his hair and a forged passport in his coat. I had walked into this room carrying five people inside my coat like smuggled brandy, and every question Reeve asked was a customs officer patting the seams.

A spy, I thought, staring at the ceiling, is a person whose inner life is bigger than her papers. And I was beginning to suspect the reverse was also true, and worse: that a person could be made entirely of papers, and that mine — Rotterdam-born, alien-blooded, stateless in practice if not yet in law — had been written by other people before I ever learned to hold a pen. My whole life was a file in some office, marked and flagged and shelved. *Enemy's daughter. Suspicious correspondent. Girl asking about tides.* The cover story and the true story ran side by side like rails, and I could no longer always say which train I was riding.

Somewhere past midnight — I judged it by the lamp being refilled, the matron's boots on the stair, the sea changing its voice with the turn of the tide — I heard footsteps stop outside my door. Not the matron's; lighter, quicker. A pause long enough to mean deliberation. Then the scrape of something pushed under the door: a folded paper.

I waited until the steps receded. Then I crossed the cold boards, knelt, and picked it up.

It was a page torn from a signal-register pad, railway stock, the cheap blue-lined sort Ivo would know by touch in the dark. On it, in a hand I did not recognise — a woman's hand, quick and slanted — three lines:

*They are watching the Marlowe house. R. has orders from above. Hold your story. Help is coming from a direction you don't expect.*

No signature. No cipher. Plain words, meant to be read fast and swallowed — and indeed, standing there in my shift with the draft licking my ankles, I did the only sensible thing with contraband: I put it in my mouth, chewed, and washed it down with the cold tea, which tasted of paper and tannin and, faintly, unbelievably, of hope.

*Help is coming from a direction you don't expect.*

I stood a long time in the dark, listening to the sea work the wall, turning the sentence over the way Julian had taught me to turn a graft, looking for the join. Watching the Marlowe house meant Ivo and Edith were still free, still moving, still mine. Orders from above meant Reeve was not the top of this tree — that somewhere over his head sat a man with more to lose than a case. And help from an unexpected quarter—

Outside the high barred window, the sky had begun, very slightly, to change. Grey toward pearl. The gulls were up already, screaming their ownership of the morning. And down the corridor a telephone rang, and rang, and was answered, and I heard — muffled, but unmistakable — the matron's voice saying, "Yes, sir. Yes, he's here. For *her*? Well, I'm sure I couldn't say, sir, but there's a letter just come, special delivery, addressed to Captain Reeve personally, and it's signed—" a pause, paper unfolding— "it's signed *Julian Marlowe*."
