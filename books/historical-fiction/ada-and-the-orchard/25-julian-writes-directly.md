## 25. Julian Writes Directly

The letter came on a morning so cold the garden had turned to glass.

Edith saw it first through the drawing-room window — Ivo coming up the drive with his collar up and his breath smoking, carrying not his usual satchel of correspondence but a single envelope held in both hands, the way one carries a bird with a broken wing. She knew before he reached the door. She knew by the set of his shoulders, by the careful slowness of his walk across the frosted gravel, that this was it. That after weeks of cipher and half-message and the terrible arithmetic of what they could not say aloud in rooms they suspected of listening, her brother had stopped whispering.

He had written to her directly. Openly. Under his own name.

She was standing at the fireplace when Ivo entered, and she watched him take in the scene — Ada curled into the window seat with a mending basket she never actually touched, the tea going cold on the tray, the fire built high because Edith had been unable to get warm for three days — and she watched him decide, without a word being exchanged, that whatever the envelope held would be read together or not at all.

"It came with the morning post," he said. "Mixed in with the *Votes for Women* circulars. No cipher. Nothing hidden." He held it out. "It's addressed to you. Plainly. 'Mrs. Edith Marlowe, Marlowe House, Ashden.' His name is signed on the back flap."

"His name," Edith repeated.

"Julian Marlowe. In full."

Ada had risen from the window seat. She stood now with her hands pressed flat against her skirt, and Edith saw that the girl's knuckles had gone white, and that she did not come closer, and that her whole body was straining toward the letter like a compass needle finding its north.

Edith took the envelope. It was heavier than an ordinary letter — there were pages inside, several of them — and the paper was good stock, the kind Julian had always used, bought from the stationer in Wye who charged a penny more than anyone needed to pay because he believed good pens deserved good paper. Her brother's handwriting on the front was as she remembered it: upright, deliberate, each letter given its full measure, the hand of a boy who'd been made to copy out sermons until they improved and had instead simply learned to write beautifully out of spite.

She broke the seal. The wax cracked with a sound like a small bone breaking.

*My dear Edith,*

*I have been a coward in most things, but I will not be one in this.*

She sat down. She did not remember deciding to sit; her legs simply folded beneath her, and Ada moved instantly to the chair beside her, and Ivo stayed standing by the door like a sentry, though she noticed his eyes were fixed on the page over her shoulder with an hunger he no longer troubled to disguise. Let him read it too, then. There were no more secrets worth the keeping. There hadn't been since the night Ada arrived soaked through at their gate with a dead man's handwriting in her pocket.

*You will have wondered why I hid behind codes and couriers for so long, and I owe you the truth, which is this: every month I delayed was a month in which I told myself the situation might resolve itself without my ruin. It will not. Captain Reeve has found the thread, and he is pulling it, and when he reaches its end he will find not only me but you, and Ada, and the Brandts, and everyone whose kindness has kept me alive these two years. I will not permit that.*

*So here are my terms, which I ask you to carry, because you are the only person in England with the standing to carry them.*

*I will surrender myself. Voluntarily, publicly, at a time and place of your choosing — though I would ask for Ashford, where the cells are dry and the governor owes my old commanding officer a favor. In exchange I require three things, and I will accept no partial fulfillment.*

*First: safe passage for the Brandt family. All four of them, including the boy, out of England and to Holland, with papers in order and no detention en route. You know what waits for German nationals in the camps now. You know what the newspapers call them and what the mobs do when the papers give them permission. Ernst Brandt carried me on his back for eleven miles after Loos — do not look surprised, Edith, you know already that I was there, and you know why I left — and I swore to him in 1911, in peacetime, in his own garden, that if war ever came I would see his family safe. A promise made in peace is the only kind that means anything. It must be honored first, before anything is asked of me.*

*Second: immunity for Ada Weiss for everything she has done in carrying my letters. She is twenty-three years old and she has risked a prison cell for a man who is not even her countryman, because her father taught her that gratitude is a debt that does not expire. If she is prosecuted, I withdraw my cooperation and we may all go down together in the newspapers, which Reeve's superiors will enjoy about as much as we will.*

*Third — and here, Edith, is the part I write with the greatest difficulty — I require that the guarantee come not from Reeve but from his superior. Colonel Hargreaves. You will find the name distasteful; so do I. But understand: Reeve cannot guarantee anything, because Reeve is not hunting me for justice. He is hunting me to bury something. I know what it is. I have proof of it. And if his superior wants this affair ended quietly — and he does, I am assured of it — then Hargreaves must put his signature to the terms himself, knowing that my testimony dies with my cooperation.*

*Tell him I remember the winter of 1909. He will understand.*

*I do not ask for pardon. I ask for terms. I deserted; I will not insult you by pretending otherwise. But I deserted a thing that had stopped deserving the name of army, and I did it to keep a promise, and if England cannot tell the difference between those two things anymore, then England has a problem larger than one lieutenant from Kent.*

*Give my love to the house. Tell it I'm sorry about the orchard wall — I was fourteen and it seemed funny at the time.*

*Your brother,*
*Julian*

The room was very quiet. Outside, a robin was working the frozen border below the window, hopping and striking, hopping and striking, industrious and absurd against all that glittering frost.

Edith became aware that she was gripping the pages hard enough to crease them, and made herself loosen her fingers, one by one.

"He's alive," Ada said. Not a question. She had read the letter over Edith's shoulder — of course she had — and now she said it again, louder, as though volume could make it more true. "He's alive and he's writing and he has a plan. Mrs. Marlowe, he has a *plan*."

"He has a surrender," Edith said.

Her voice came out steadier than she felt. She looked down at the pages again, at the familiar loops and strokes, and felt the two feelings rise in her together, inseparable, like roots and stones pulled up in the same spadeful. Relief — vast, dizzying relief, the relief of a woman who has been holding her breath for two years and is finally permitted to exhale — and beneath it, shadowing it entirely, the knowledge of what the exhale would cost.

Because she understood immediately what the letter demanded of her, beyond the negotiations and the colonels. It demanded that she help deliver her brother to a cell. It required her, Edith Marlowe, who had spent fifteen years teaching women to refuse the cages built for them, to walk her own brother to the door of one and hold it open.

"I can't," she said — and then caught herself, because Ada was looking at her with those wide frightened eyes, and because the sentence was untrue, and she had built her entire life on never saying untrue sentences. "I mean — I can. Of course I can. I only need a moment to be a sister before I become a negotiator."

"Take all the moments you want," Ada said softly.

Ivo shifted by the door. "Colonel Hargreaves," he said. "Do you actually know how to reach a man like that? They don't exactly list themselves."

"They don't," Edith agreed. "But I know his wife."

She let that sit. Ivo raised an eyebrow; Ada looked between them.

"Hargreaves' wife is Margaret Hargreaves," Edith said. "Before her marriage, Margaret Ellery-Smythe. She was at Girton two years ahead of me, and she marched with us in 1908 before her husband's career made such displays inconvenient, and she still sends a donation every January under her maiden name because she thinks a bank clerk won't connect it." Edith almost smiled. "Margaret has been waiting fifteen years to be useful. She simply hasn't been asked."

"And the winter of 1909?" Ivo asked. "What happened in 1909?"

"That," Edith said, "is precisely the question we are not going to answer in this house, because I have begun to assume every room in Kent contains a listening ear." She rose, folding Julian's letter along its original creases with hands that trembled only slightly, and was gratified to find that the trembling felt less like weakness than like voltage. "Ada — pack a bag. Small. We're going to London on the afternoon train. Ivo — the car. Check the tyres, and take the long way around Ashden when you bring it round, past the church, slow enough to be seen. If Captain Reeve is watching us, let him watch us go to London for suffrage business, which is after all what women like me do."

"And what will you do in London?"

"Have lunch with an old friend," Edith said, "and ask her to arrange a meeting between her husband and a ghost."

* * *

London wore its war like a dirty scarf. The train had been delayed twice by troop movements, and by the time they came up out of Charing Cross the lamps were lit against a sky the color of dishwater, and the pavements were crowded with men in uniform and women in mourning and posters, everywhere posters, exhorting and accusing and recruiting. Ada walked close at Edith's side — closer than the difference in their ages warranted — and Edith realized the girl was afraid of the crowds in a way she hadn't been six months ago, that the city had changed for her since her father's internment, that every street corner now held the memory of some official door closing.

They ate at a modest restaurant near Russell Square, because Edith refused to conduct serious business on an empty stomach — her mother's dictum, and one of the few inheritances she'd kept — and it was there, over omelettes and weak coffee, that Ada finally voiced the thing that had been sitting in her chest since the letter.

"You'll really do it," Ada said. "Hand him over."

"I'll really do it."

"But—" Ada set down her fork. Her face, always so expressive, was working through several arguments at once, and Edith waited with a patience she'd learned from thirty years of hostile public meetings to let people arrive at their own conclusions. "He survived. Two years. He got out, he built a life, he found ways to help people — he helped *my* family, he's trying to help the Brandts — and you're going to give him back to the machine that tried to kill him. For what? For rules?"

"For the Brandts," Edith said. "And for you. And because he asked."

"He asked because he thinks it's the only way—"

"He asked because it *is* the only way, Ada, and he knows it, and what wounds me is not that he's surrendering." Edith turned her cup slowly on its saucer. "What wounds me is that he's right. I have spent my life believing that stubbornness is a virtue, that if you simply refuse hard enough the world rearranges itself. Sometimes it does. But sometimes refusal just means the people around you absorb the blow instead. Julian has been refusing for two years, magnificently, and the cost has been paid by Ernst Brandt's son, by your father, by you, by Ivo, by everyone who loves him. The bravest thing he has ever done is stop running, and I will not cheapen it by pretending there's a cleverer option hidden somewhere in my handbag."

Ada stared at her. Then, unexpectedly, she laughed — a wet, broken sound — and pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "You sound like him."

"I should hope so. We had the same upbringing and the same obstinate mother."

"And if they shoot him?" The question came out flat and fast, the way one pulls off a plaster. "Desertion in wartime. They can. You know they can."

Edith had known it since she'd first read the word *court-martial* in connection with her brother's name, and she had carried the knowledge the way one carries a stone in the boot — constantly, privately, adjusting one's gait around it. She would not lie to this girl. Lying to Ada would be like lying to a mirror.

"They can," Edith said. "But they won't. Not if the terms hold. A public execution of a surrendered officer who saved a German family would be a scandal even this government couldn't digest — and Julian knows it, and that knowledge is armor. He has arranged matters so that killing him would cost them more than sparing him. That is his genius, Ada. It always was. Even as a boy, he never once won a fight. He simply made losing too expensive for the other fellow."

* * *

Margaret Hargreaves received them in a townhouse in Kensington that smelled of beeswax and lilies, and she was exactly as Edith remembered her: tall, silvering, dressed with expensive restraint, wearing the expression of a woman who has spent decades being intelligent in rooms designed to pretend she isn't.

"Edith Marlowe." Margaret took her hand and held it a moment longer than convention allowed. "You've grown more dangerous-looking. I approve."

"Margaret, this is Miss Ada Weiss, who works for me. Margaret is an old friend," Edith added, for Ada's benefit, "and a subscriber to the cause, under an assumed name, which tells you everything you need to know about the state of her marriage."

"My marriage is excellent," Margaret said placidly, pouring tea. "My husband is a colonel, which is a different matter entirely. Now. You did not cross from Kent to discuss the franchise, much as I wish you had. What does Edward's command want with you, Edith? Because I have watched that man come home for three years looking like a man who is being slowly poisoned, and last week he came home looking worse, and this morning he telephoned to say he may receive 'a communication of a delicate nature' regarding 'a matter in Kent.'" She set down the pot and looked at them both directly. "So. Before you say anything I may need to deny knowing: shall I ring for fresh tea, or sherry?"

Edith laid Julian's letter on the table between them, face down, unopened.

"Sherry," she said. "And then I'm going to tell you a story about a man named Ernst Brandt, and a promise made in a garden in 1911, and a grave in France that has been empty for two years. And then, Margaret, I'm going to ask you to do the single most dangerous thing you have ever done for this family of yours — which is to believe a woman's word about a man's war."

Margaret Hargreaves looked at the letter for a long moment. When she lifted her eyes, there was something in them Edith had not seen since before August 1914 — something bright and reckless and young.

"Ring for the sherry myself," she said, rising, "and lock the door behind me. Whatever else happens, Edith Marlowe, nobody is walking in on this conversation."

* * *

They came back to Kent after dark, through fog that lay in the valleys like spilled milk, and Edith drove the last miles herself while Ivo dozed badly in the passenger seat and Ada kept watch on the mirrors. At the gates of Marlowe House the frost had returned, and the bare apple trees stood along the drive with their arms up, patient as prisoners awaiting sentence.

There was a light burning in the study that Edith had not left lit.

Her heart performed a full revolution before reason caught up: Ivo had left it on, or Ada, or the maid. But when she stepped into the hall, shaking the fog from her coat, she saw the study door ajar and heard, from within, the scratch of a pen.

On the desk lay a second letter. It had not come by post — there had been no time for post. It had been left by hand, sometime during the day, by someone with a key or a ladder or simply the nerve of the devil, and it bore no address at all, only a single line on the outside, in that upright beautiful hand:

*For my sister, in case the first letter frightened her more than it reassured her.*

Edith sat down in her own chair, in her own study, with her household asleep above her and her brother alive somewhere within twenty miles, and opened it.

*Edith,* it began. *You will have done as I asked, because you always do what is right even when it costs you, and I want you to know that I know the price. You are giving up the brother you mourned and accepting the brother who exists, faults included, cell and all. No one has ever loved me so expensively. When this is finished — however it is finished — plant something. You always said grief wants a garden. Yours has waited two years. So has mine.*

*Three days, Edith. Give me three days to settle the Brandts' papers in motion, and then send Hargreaves his answer.*

*J.*

Outside, the fog pressed against the windows, and somewhere down in the valley a night train went crying toward Dover, and Edith Marlowe folded her brother's second letter beside the first and sat for a long while in the lamplight with her hands open in her lap — empty of him, full of him — while three days began, quietly, to fall.
