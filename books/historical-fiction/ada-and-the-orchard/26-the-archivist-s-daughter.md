## 26. The Archivist's Daughter

The name came to me the way most useful things did that autumn — sideways, half-heard, carried in on somebody else's business.

I had driven Edith into Canterbury on a grey Tuesday, the kind of day when the sky sat low over the cathedral like a lid, and while she argued tariffs with a printer I waited with the motor in a side street off Palace Street, reading the notices pasted to a board outside the Records Office. That was where I saw it. *Retirement of Mr. A. Fenwick, Registrar and Archivist, after thirty-one years' service.* Thirty-one years. The dates ran back far enough to swallow 1915 entire. Far enough to swallow a death report from Loos.

I had been thinking about that report for eleven days. Since Julian's letter had arrived addressed openly to Edith — since the word *surrender* had stood up out of the page and refused to sit down again — one question had been walking beside me like a dog that would not be shooed: *how does a dead man write letters?* Not why. How. Somebody, somewhere in the great grey machine of the War Office, had written down the words *Killed in action* about a man who was not killed, and had signed it, and filed it, and let it travel home to Marlowe House in its official envelope to break an official heart. Paperwork did not lie on its own. Paperwork was lied *to*, by people. And people could be found.

I copied the name into my notebook without letting myself think too hard about what I was doing. Thinking too hard was how you talked yourself out of things. I had learned that much about myself by now.

* * *

Finding Mr. Fenwick took three days and two lies, which by my current standard was almost honesty. The first lie I told Edith — that I needed the Austin for a delivery to Wye — and it came out smooth as oiled leather, and I hated it, and I made it anyway. The second lie I told a landlady in Ashford who kept lodgings for retired civil servants, and that one was barely a lie at all: I said I was researching the records of a fallen officer for his family. Every word of it true except the word *fallen*.

He lived in a narrow brick house behind the goods yard, where the trains went through all night dragging their chains of trucks, and when I knocked he opened the door himself — a small, dry man, seventy if he was a day, with spectacles thick as jam-jar bottoms and hands stained faintly yellow, as though thirty-one years among old documents had dyed him the colour of their pages.

"Mr. Underhill," I said. "About Lieutenant Julian Marlowe."

Something happened to his face. It is hard to describe unless you have watched a man's whole profession pass across it in a moment — the archivist's instinct, which is first and always to know what has been touched and by whom. He looked at me the way he might have looked at a document discovered out of order in a locked drawer.

"You'd better come in," he said. "Mind the cat."

* * *

The front room smelled of tea, pipe smoke, and dust that had been loved rather than neglected. Books everywhere, but not arranged — *curated*, I would say now; every pile a decision. On the mantel stood a photograph of a young woman in a nurse's apron, and beneath it a smaller frame holding a pressed sprig of something white.

"You'll take tea," said Fenwick, not asking. "My daughter poured tea for officers all through '15 and '16. She'd never forgive me if I let a caller stand."

"She isn't here?"

"France." He said it flatly, and I understood the flatness, and did not ask further. He measured leaves into a brown pot with the exactness of a man weighing evidence. "Now. You said the Marlowe name. Which Marlowe?"

"Julian. Second Battalion, Royal West Kents. Reported killed at Loos, September of '15."

The teapot paused mid-pour. One drop darkened the saucer.

"And you are?"

"Their driver," I said. It sounded absurd. It was absurd. "And a friend of the family."

He set the pot down and took off his spectacles and polished them very slowly on his sleeve, which gave him time, I think, to decide something.

"I processed that notification," he said. "Not the field report itself — that comes up from the regimental channel — but the confirmation. The record copy. The entry in the roll." He put the spectacles back on and became, before my eyes, a younger man sitting at a high desk with a green-shaded lamp. "It came through in the second week of October. Casualty confirmations were coming through in sheaves by then, whole harvests of them, and Loos was the worst we'd handled. But that one I remember."

"Why?" My voice cracked on it.

"Because of the daughter." He nodded toward the photograph. "Margaret was engaged to a Kent man in the same brigade. I checked every Kent name twice that month, against hope and against dread both. Yours I checked because the surname matched a family I knew of — the Marlowes of Marlowe House, the suffrage woman. My wife read her speeches aloud to me and called her either a saint or a firebrand depending on the week." He smiled briefly, and then the smile went out like a lamp. "So yes. I remember the Marlowe entry. And I will tell you what I have told no one, because until this moment there was no one to tell it to and no reason on God's earth to tell it."

The clock on the mantel ticked. Somewhere beyond the wall the goods train clanked past, hauling its endless freight.

"There was a discrepancy," said Fenwick. "Small. The sort ninety-nine men in a hundred would sign straight through, because the war was killing faster than we could count and nobody wanted to be the clerk who slowed the harvest. But I was trained to notice discrepancies. It is the whole of the trade."

"What discrepancy?"

"The field report named the officer killed. But the accompanying list — the battalion nominal state, the muster correction — still carried him present. Both papers crossed my desk the same morning. A man cannot be struck off the strength and left upon it in the same hour. One of those documents was wrong. I flagged it. I wrote a memorandum — a polite one, the way you do — and sent it back up the chain." His hands, around his teacup, were quite steady. "The memorandum came back within the week. Withdrawn. Marked *resolved, administrative error, no further action*. Signed off by a staff major whose signature I knew, because I had seen it on a thousand clean papers. Only this time—" He stopped. He drank his tea. The cat got onto his lap and he permitted it, staring over its grey head at a middle distance full of filing cabinets.

"This time," I said quietly, "the ink was wrong."

Fenwick looked at me with real respect. "You've handled documents."

"I've read a few I shouldn't have," I said, and found I meant it as a confession, and found also that it cost me less than confessions used to.

"The ink was fresher than the paper's handling," he said. "That's the phrase I'd use now, forty years of hindsight later. The paper was October. The ink behaved like December. Someone had gone back into a settled file. And the signature — Major Hollis, it was — was correct in every particular except speed. Men sign their names ten thousand times and it becomes a reflex, a signature like weather. This one was drawn. Copied. Careful." He set the cup down. "I raised it once more, informally, with a friend upstairs. Two days later I was transferred to the Rochester annex to catalogue ordnance ledgers. Very quiet work. Very final."

"You thought it was a mistake being covered up. An embarrassment."

"I thought it was a *clerk's* mistake being covered up," said Fenwick. "A misfiled report, a hasty signature, some staff officer protecting his own neck. It happens. It happened often enough that I told myself the story and believed it, mostly, for two years. Because the alternative—" He looked at the photograph of his daughter. "The alternative was that a man's death had been manufactured on purpose, and if a death can be manufactured, then everything I had spent my life keeping honest was only a very tidy room built over a cellar. Old men prefer the tidy room, Mr. Underhill. We tell ourselves the files are the truth. We build our whole lives on that."

"And now?"

"Now a young man sits in my parlour saying the dead officer writes letters." He removed his spectacles again. Behind the thick glass his eyes were wet and furious. "And the alternative comes walking up my garden path with mud on its boots, and I find I am *glad*. Do you understand? Glad. Because if that boy lives, then the lie was not the world's whole nature. It was one man's signature. One man's careful, copied, cowardly hand." His voice dropped. "Major Hollis. Remember the name. He was mentor to a Captain Reeve — they were spoken of together, the clever pair, Hollis and his protégé. If your Captain Reeve is hunting this matter even now, ask yourself whom he serves. Dead men make convenient martyrs, Mr. Underhill. Living ones make inconvenient witnesses. There is a whole career to be built on knowing the difference."

* * *

He would not give me copies — "I keep nothing here, nothing, they taught me that much" — but he wrote from memory, in a fair clerk's hand, the file reference numbers, the dates, the sequence of memoranda, and folded the sheet into my breast pocket himself, patting it once like a man burying something alive.

At the door, the cat winding round his ankles, he stopped me with a hand on my sleeve.

"My Margaret writes that the hospitals are full and the clerks are full and everyone is full," he said. "She asks me in every letter whether the work I did mattered. Whether the paper was true." His jaw worked. "Tell the Marlowe woman — tell her brother, if he breathes — that one clerk noticed. That's all any of us are, in the end. One clerk noticing. Tell them the system itself was lying, and one small dry man caught the smell of it, and was sent away for his trouble, and never stopped being certain."

I drove back through the dark with the headlights hooded per regulation, the lane unspooling black under the mask-light, and the folded paper over my heart like a second, secret heartbeat. Hollis. Reeve's mentor. Reeve, who had searched our house with soft apologies; Reeve, who interviewed me with courteous eyes; Reeve, who was not hunting a deserter at all, but a scandal — burying his teacher's signature in whatever grave he could dig for it.

I turned in at the Marlowe gates as the church clocks were striking nine, and saw lights burning in the study, and Ada's silhouette crossing the window, and felt the whole shape of it come together in my chest like a knot pulled tight.

Edith met me in the hall before I had my coat off. She read my face the way Fenwick read documents — instantly, wholly, missing nothing.

"Well?" she said.

"It wasn't a mistake," I told her. "It was a signature. And I know whose."

Her hand went to the banister. From the study doorway, Ada had appeared, and behind her, framed in lamplight, lay Julian's letter with the word *surrender* waiting in it — waiting, I understood suddenly, not for the War Office at all.

For us. For whoever we chose to show it to. And above all, for Reeve — who had two days ago requested, in writing, a private meeting with Edith Marlowe, alone.
