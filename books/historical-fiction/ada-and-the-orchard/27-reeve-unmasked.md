## 27. Reeve Unmasked

The archivist's papers lay on my desk like something freshly exhumed — which, in a sense, they were. Ivo had brought them back from Canterbury in a brown paper parcel tied with butcher's string, and he set them down with the particular care of a man handling evidence he does not yet trust himself to interpret.

"Colonel Ambrose Fenn," he said. "That was the name on the file. He processed the casualty confirmations for the Second Battalion in the autumn of '15. And here—" he turned a flimsy sheet toward me, its edges soft with age and handling, "—is the amendment."

I did not touch it at first. I have learned, these two years, that paper can wound as deeply as shrapnel, only slower. The telegram confirming Julian's death had arrived on a Tuesday in October; I remembered the day of the week because I had been addressing envelopes for the *Suffragist Circular*, and my pen had stopped mid-word, leaving a small ink blot shaped like a bird. I had kept that envelope. Grief makes archivists of us all, whether we intend it or not.

"Read it aloud," I said. "I want to hear it in someone else's voice. Mine would shake."

Ivo cleared his throat. "'Amendment to Casualty Report U-4417. Confirmation of death withdrawn pending verification. No further action. Filed by order of Col. A. Fenn.' Dated the ninth of November, nineteen fifteen." He looked up. "Three weeks after they told you he was dead. Someone knew, Mrs. Marlowe. Someone knew before the ground had even settled at Loos, and they buried the truth instead of your brother."

"And Fenn," I said, "was Captain Reeve's—"

"Mentor. Patron. The man who got him his commission in the first place. The old clerk at the archive remembered them together — Fenn bringing the young Reeve round, showing him off like a protégé. 'The boy has an instinct for discretion,' the old man said. His words." Ivo's mouth twisted. "An instinct for discretion. That's what they call it when a man learns early which truths are convenient."

I rose and went to the window. The orchard behind Marlowe House stood bare-limbed against a sky the colour of pewter, the January light lying thin and cold across the frozen grass. Somewhere beyond the yew hedge, Ada was teaching herself to drive the motor in second gear, and the engine coughed and caught and coughed again — a small stubborn heartbeat in the grey morning. I thought of Julian, wherever he was, living under another man's name, growing a beard to hide a face I had loved since it was toothless and furious in a cradle. Two years. Two years of wreaths and condolence cards and my mother going quietly mad with a grief that had been, all along, a clerical fiction.

"So," I said, when I could trust my voice again. "Let us follow the thread to its end. Julian fakes his death — or allows it to be believed. Fenn discovers it within weeks. And instead of reporting it—" 

"He buries the amendment," Ivo finished. "Why? That's what I can't make sit straight. If Fenn was honest, he'd have reported the desertion. If he was corrupt, what did he gain by silence?"

"Think, Mr. Underhill, as a man who has spent years reading other people's correspondence. What is the one thing a bureaucrat fears more than scandal?"

He considered. Then I watched it arrive in his face, slowly, like dawn coming up over the Weald.

"Being *connected* to it. Fenn signed the original confirmation. If Julian turned up alive, the question wouldn't be why did the army lose a soldier — it would be how did the paperwork lie, and whose signature was on the lie? Fenn didn't hide the desertion. He hid his own error. Or worse." Ivo's voice dropped. "Or worse — he knew more than an error. What if the false death report wasn't Julian's doing alone? What if someone inside helped him vanish, and Fenn was that someone?"

The fire spat. Down in the lane, the motor stalled and Ada swore with admirable fluency in three languages.

"And now," I said, "Fenn is dead or retired — Ivo, which?"

"Retired. Cheltenham. But his files passed to his protégé. Which means Captain Reeve has spent this entire hunt not looking for a deserter. He's been looking for the loose end that could unravel him and everything Fenn built. He isn't serving justice, Mrs. Marlowe. He's serving the reputation of a dead man's career — and his own place in its shadow."

I turned from the window. Something had shifted in the room, some alteration of pressure, the way air changes before weather. For two months Captain Reeve had moved through our lives like a slow blade — polite, patient, appearing at village post offices and parish meetings with questions dressed as courtesies. He had searched my house under the pretext of a security review and stood in my drawing room handling my brother's letters wrapped in suffrage pamphlets, never once thinking to look there, because to Captain Reeve a woman's movement was furniture, not architecture. I had wagered on his contempt and won. Now the wager changed shape entirely.

"He thinks we know nothing," I said. "And tomorrow he comes to tea."

* * *

Reeve arrived punctually at four, stamping snow from his boots on the step like any respectable caller, his driver waiting in the lane with the engine running. He was a tall man with the kind of face that photographs well and remembers poorly — all administration, no weather. He accepted tea. He praised the marmalade. He remarked, with studied lightness, on the number of letters passing through Marlowe House for a private residence.

"We are a correspondence hub for the movement," I said pleasantly. "You know that. You've read half of it yourself, I imagine, through your friends at the postal censor's office."

"One does one's duty." He set down his cup with a small precise click. "Mrs. Marlowe, I will be candid, since candour seems to be our currency. There is a matter touching your late brother. Irregularities. I have reason to believe certain persons at this house are in possession of material relevant to my inquiry. I would take it as a mark of loyalty — to the Crown, and indeed to your brother's memory — if such material were surrendered."

"His memory," I repeated. "Yes. Let us speak of his memory, Captain. Let us speak of the ninth of November, nineteen fifteen."

Something crossed his face — not guilt, not yet, but the alertness of a man hearing a floorboard groan overhead.

"I don't follow."

"No," I said. "You follow very well. That is rather the trouble." I drew the amended report from my writing case and laid it on the table between us, facing him. "Withdrawn pending verification. Filed by order of Colonel Fenn. Three weeks after the army told my family my brother was dead. So either the Crown killed a man twice — once at Loos and once on paper — or somebody knew Julian Underhill was alive and chose silence. Which was it, Captain?"

For a long moment the only sound was the clock on the mantel and, faintly, the wind working at the ivy on the east wall. Reeve did not reach for the paper. I noted that. An innocent man snatches at evidence; a guilty one lets it lie, as though stillness might starve it.

"That document," he said at last, evenly, "is a forgery."

"Is it? Then you'll have no difficulty explaining why your own hand appears on the routing slip attached to it. The archive clerk in Canterbury has an excellent memory for faces. Yours, particularly. He says you came asking after the Fenn files in September. Before you ever came asking after us."

I confess I took a certain cold pleasure in watching the administration drain out of his face and leave the weather behind at last. Fear looks different on every man. On Reeve it looked like recalculation — the rapid, silent arithmetic of a man tottering on a ledge and measuring the drop.

"You've been to Canterbury," he said.

"I have been to Canterbury," I agreed, "as you have been to Hythe, and to Ashford, and to the post office at Wye, where poor Mrs. Tilling was so frightened she gave notice. We have both been busy, Captain. The difference is that I have been pursuing my brother, and you have been pursuing the man who could hang your mentor's reputation — and yours with it — on a hook."

"You presume a great deal."

"I presume nothing. I *read*." I leaned forward. "You were Fenn's creature. Everyone says so. He made you, and you inherited his drawers and his secrets and his liabilities. Julian Underhill's resurrection would not merely embarrass the War Office — it would expose how a confirmed dead man walked out of the machinery of the British Army without one signature being questioned. It would expose Fenn. And it would expose the young officer Fenn trusted with the tidying up. Tell me I'm wrong. Go on. Tell me, and then explain why you've spent months harassing a widow's household over a deserter you could have forgotten."

The clock ticked. Outside, Ada's voice floated up from the yard — she had abandoned the motor and was arguing cheerfully with the gardener's boy about grafting wax — and absurdly, that ordinary domestic sound steadied me more than any argument could have.

Reeve rose. He walked to the window with his hands clasped behind him, a man buying time by admiring frost. When he spoke again, his voice had shed its officiousness and acquired something rawer underneath.

"Do you imagine," he said, "that I wanted any of this? Fenn died owing money and owing favours, and I inherited both ledgers. The Underhill file was the worst entry in it. He left instructions — *instructions*, Mrs. Marlowe — that if the matter ever surfaced, it was to be managed quietly. Managed. Do you understand what that word costs a man of my position? I have spent two years sitting on a falsified death report because a dying colonel asked it of me, and now there is a woman abroad carrying letters in a dead man's handwriting, and a chauffeur who reads sealed mail — yes, we know about Mr. Underhill's little habit — and a sister who publishes pamphlets calling for peace while her own flesh and blood is technically liable to be shot at dawn. Every one of you is a fuse, and I am standing on the powder."

"Then we are in agreement about the danger," I said. "We differ only on where the spark should fall."

He turned. "What do you want?"

Ah, there it was. Four words, and the whole balance of the room tipped like a scale. I made myself pause — not for effect, but because I genuinely needed the moment to decide what I wanted, and because a demand made too quickly smells of desperation.

"I want my brother to come home alive and unshot. I want the Brandt family passage out of this country before their boy is shipped to a reprisal camp. I want Miss Brandt — Miss Ada — freed of any charge or suspicion, now and permanently. And I want all of it done without one further search of my house, one further interrogation of my household, and one further frightened old woman in a village post office."

"And in return?"

"In return, the Fenn amendment stays in Canterbury, and I stay quiet about what it proves. Not withdrawn from the movement, Captain — you may save that bargain for a weaker woman. My silence about your mentor, purchased by my brother's life. Nothing more and nothing less."

"It's blackmail," he said flatly.

"It is *leverage*," I said, "which is what blackmail becomes when the cause is just. You have been wielding the law as a weapon against people whose only crime is loyalty. I am merely picking up the same weapon and finding it fits my hand surprisingly well. You taught us how, after all — every summons, every search, every threat. Consider this the lesson returned."

For the first time since I had known him, Captain Reeve sat down uninvited. He looked suddenly tired — not defeated, not yet, but a man who has seen the far side of his own power and found it thinner than advertised.

"Fenn's instructions were clear," he said quietly. "If the file ever surfaced, the family was to be discredited first. Your suffrage work made that simple — a traitor's sister, a hysterical cause, the press would have done half the work for nothing. That was the plan I was given. Discredit, isolate, then manage." He met my eyes. "You should know that I drafted the memorandum. In March. It recommended surveillance of this house."

"I know," I said. "It arrived at the district office in April. A sympathetic clerk showed it to a friend of the movement. We have known since spring, Captain. Why do you think we hid Julian's letters where we did?" I let that settle. "You assumed we were hiding things from you. We were hiding things from your *assumptions*. There is a difference, and it has kept us free until now."

He laughed then — short, humourless, almost admiring. "You are the most formidable woman I have ever been sent to dismantle."

"How kind. Most people say stubborn." I gathered the amendment from the table and slid it back into my writing case, deliberately slowly, letting him watch it disappear. "Here is what happens next. I will send terms to you in writing. You will find the venue agreeable or propose another. And Captain — the moment anything happens to my brother, or to Ada, or to the Brandts, the copy goes to a journalist I trust and a Member of Parliament I trust better. Fenn's shadow is long, but mine is longer, because unlike your colonel, I am still alive and still angry."

He stood, smoothed his tunic, and became once more the photographable administrator. At the door he paused, hand on the frame, and did not turn around.

"The chapel on the Ashford road," he said. "Disused since the vicar died. Neutral ground. Thursday, at eleven."

Then he was gone, boots ringing on the frozen gravel, and the door closed behind him with a sound like a period at the end of a long sentence.

* * *

They were all three waiting in the kitchen when I came through — Ivo by the range, Ada perched on the scrubbed table itself, manners abandoned somewhere around November, Julian's photograph face-down beside the bread crock as though it might overhear. They read my face before I said a word. I must learn, someday, to school it better; then again, perhaps I shall not bother. A household that cannot read you is no household at all.

"Well?" Ada demanded, sliding off the table. "Did he fold?"

"He creased," I said. "Which is the beginning of folding. Thursday, eleven o'clock, the disused chapel on the Ashford road. Terms in writing before then."

Ivo let out a breath he seemed to have held since Canterbury. "You threatened a captain of the War Office with a piece of paper."

"I offered a captain of the War Office a piece of paper. The threat was implied. Threats land harder when the other man writes them himself." I untied my apron strings and sat, suddenly aware of how much the afternoon had cost me; my hands, I noticed, were not quite steady on the tabletop, and I folded them to hide it, and Ada saw, and reached over and covered them with her own without a word, which steadied them better than any resolve.

"He'll bring conditions," Ivo said. "There's always a condition."

"Of course there is. There always is, with men of his kind — they cannot yield without charging rent on the yielding. Whatever it is, we weigh it Thursday." I looked round the kitchen — at Ivo's careful anxious face, at Ada's fierce bright one, at the photograph lying face-down like a sleeping thing — and felt the strange vertigo of a woman who has just gambled her brother's life on a folder of stolen bureaucracy and won only the right to gamble again. "But hear me, both of you. From tonight until Thursday, nobody travels alone, nobody posts anything, and nobody — Ivo, I am looking at you — reads anything not addressed to them."

A ghost of his old sheepish smile. "Not even the terms letter?"

"*Especially* not the terms letter. If Captain Reeve learns his correspondence is as unsafe with us as ours is with him, we lose the only advantage God and a Canterbury archive clerk have given us."

Ada laughed — the quick, startled laugh that had become, over these hard weeks, the truest sound in Marlowe House. Then it faded, and she looked at the photograph, and turned it face-up at last: Julian, bearded, older, squinting into some foreign sun, alive.

"Thursday," she said softly. "Two days. He's waited two years. Can he wait two days?"

Outside, the light was already failing, blue shadows pooling in the orchard furrows, and somewhere down the Ashford road a chapel stood dark and empty, waiting to become the most dangerous room in Kent.

"He has waited two years among strangers," I said, and touched my brother's photographed face with one finger. "He can wait two days among family. But we must not waste a single hour of them — because I promise you, Captain Reeve will not waste his."
