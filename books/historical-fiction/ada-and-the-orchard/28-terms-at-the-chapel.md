## 28. Terms at the Chapel

The chapel stood a mile from any road that mattered, down a lane gone green at its centre where cartwheels no longer wore it bare. It had been a Methodist chapel once — Edith's grandmother might have sung in it, though she'd have denied the scandal of it — and then a granary, and now nothing at all but a roof that mostly held and windows of milky glass through which the afternoon came down like light through pond water.

Edith walked the last quarter-mile alone. Ivo had wanted to wait with the motor at the lane's mouth; she had told him that a negotiation attended by a nervous chauffeur would go badly, and that Captain Reeve would count noses before she did. She had not told him what she was actually afraid of, which was not Reeve at all, but herself — the temptation, when the moment came, to ask for too much or to give away too much out of sheer weariness. Power was like water pressure in an old pipe: you could feel it wanting to burst somewhere, and the whole art was to keep it moving, useful, directed.

She pushed open the door. The hinges had been oiled recently. That small courtesy told her Reeve had arrived early and intended to be comfortable, which meant he considered himself in control, which meant he was not.

Inside, dust hung in the slanted light like something suspended mid-decision. The pews had been sold off years ago; three chairs stood in a rough semicircle near where the pulpit had been, and on one of them sat Captain Reeve with his hat on his knee and his hands folded over it, a man visiting a church he did not belong to and knew it.

"Mrs. Marlowe." He rose. He was younger than his reputation — forty perhaps, with the kind of face that would still be called boyish when it was sixty — and he had the particular stillness of men who had learned to be watched. "You came alone."

"You counted my driver from the hedge," Edith said. "I counted your corporal from the gate. Shall we call ourselves even and sit?"

A flicker at the corner of his mouth — not quite amusement, but the ghost of a man who might once have been amusing. They sat. Between them on the third chair lay a leather folder, squared to the edge of the seat as precisely as a place setting. He had brought documents. So had she, folded into her reticule, though hers were only three pages of her own handwriting, because the best terms are the ones you can recite without paper.

"I'll be plain," Reeve said. "This is not an official interview. There is no record of this meeting, and there will not be."

"No," Edith agreed. "There wouldn't be, would there. Not if what my brother's letter implies about the Loos paperwork is true."

The stillness in him changed quality. It was the difference between a man being patient and a man holding something shut. She watched him decide whether to deny it, and watched him decide against denial, and understood that this was the real beginning of the conversation: they had both stopped pretending, which in her experience was rarer than love and considerably more useful.

"My mentor," Reeve said, "processed the casualty returns for that sector. Colonel Ainsworth. A good man. Decorated twice." He looked up at the pale window. "He falsified a death report for a captain who had walked away from his battalion rather than face a court-martial on charges that would have—" He stopped. Recalibrated. "The charges themselves were never proven. But the report was falsified, and a widow received a pension on the strength of a lie, and the Army does not survive that kind of thing becoming known. Not now. Not with France the way it is."

"And so you have spent two years hunting a dead man who isn't dead," Edith said, "not to bring him to justice, but to bury him somewhere quieter than France."

"To close the file." His voice stayed level, but his thumb moved along the brim of his hat, one small revolution. "Yes. If you prefer it said plainly."

"I do prefer things plainly. It saves such a lot of time for lying later, when it's needed."

* * *

She made him wait while she took stock of the room — the old habit of her father, who had taught her that you negotiate the room before you negotiate the man. The chapel smelled of dust and birdlime and, faintly, of apples; someone had stored fruit here last autumn and the sweetness had soaked into the plaster. Through the milky glass she could see the tops of the hedges going gold. Somewhere beyond them Ivo was waiting with the motor, and Ada was waiting at Marlowe House, and Julian was waiting God knew where with whatever courage it took to walk into a police station and hand himself over.

Everyone in this affair was waiting on her. She found she did not hate it. She found, more uncomfortably, that she was good at it.

"Before we discuss terms," Reeve said, "you should understand what I can offer and what I cannot. I am not the Secretary of State. I am a captain with a great deal of delegated authority and very little of it written down."

"Yes. I've met your kind of authority before." She let that sit. "It is the kind that can ruin a woman's life on a Tuesday and be unable to un-ruin it by Friday."

Something moved behind his eyes — recognition, maybe, of how much she knew about the machinery. Of course she knew. She had spent a decade watching men of exactly his description certify that women were fit for nothing but the home, and she had learned their grammar the way one learns a language by living in the country.

"The terms I'm instructed to propose," Reeve went on, "are these. Your brother surrenders himself voluntarily, at a time and station of our choosing. He pleads to desertion. In exchange — given the medical evidence, which I am assured exists — he receives a reduced sentence and a discharge on grounds of health rather than dishonour. And the file regarding Loos closes permanently."

"And me?"

He hesitated. This was the hinge, and she felt him know it was the hinge, and felt him proceed anyway, because he had been sent to proceed.

"You withdraw from public work. The WSPU platforms, the meetings, the circulars. Quietly. Illness is the usual formula. You've earned a rest, the papers will say. Nobody will press it further."

There it was. The price underneath all the other prices. She had known since the chapel door that it would come to this, and still hearing it aloud produced in her a strange cold clarity, like the first sip of very strong tea.

"You want my silence," she said.

"I want the appearance of your retirement. It's not the same thing."

"It is exactly the same thing, Captain, and we both know it. A woman who stops speaking has not retired. She has been silenced. The difference between those two words is the entire history of my life." She set her reticule on her lap, not opening it yet — the gesture itself a kind of punctuation. "And I notice the terms make no mention of Miss Brandt."

Reeve's stillness again, held a half-second too long. "Miss—"

"Ada. Who has been detained, questioned, and had her papers flagged at Folkestone by men answering to you. Who is twenty-two years old, carries no allegiance to any government but this one's cruelty, and whose only crime is keeping a promise made to her by my brother." Edith heard her own voice drop into the register she used on platforms, the one that carried to the back of a hall without effort, and made herself soften it, because softness here was not weakness; softness was the scalpel. "Your terms cover Julian. They cover me. They do not cover the child who carried the letters. Which tells me either that you haven't thought of her, or that you have, and mean to leave her dangling as insurance."

For the first time, Reeve looked away from her. He looked at the pulpit's empty socket in the floorboards as if the room itself might supply him with a better answer than he had.

"She is a complication," he said.

"She is a person," Edith said. "That is the whole of my counter-proposal, and everything else follows from it. So let us begin again, properly, with persons instead of files."

* * *

They negotiated for two hours, and afterward Edith could never entirely reconstruct the order of it, only the shape — the way a long argument leaves a groove in memory like a wheel-rut, and the individual stones along it blur.

She gave him Julian, first of all, because Julian had already given himself. The surrender would stand: voluntary, public enough to be legal, quiet enough to avoid the newspapers. She extracted in return the word *medical* — discharge on grounds of health, with the examining board drawn from a list not controlled by Ainsworth's friends — and she made Reeve write the phrase *reduced sentence* in his own hand in the margin of his own document, because a man writes differently when he knows the paper may someday be read aloud.

Then the Brandts. This was harder, because the Brandts touched the machinery of internment and deportation, which belonged to the Home Office and not to Reeve's little empire of closed files. But here she played the card she had been carrying since the letter arrived — the card she had hated carrying, because it was built out of exactly the kind of leverage she despised in others.

"You need me silent," she said. "But silence is a perishable commodity, Captain. It spoils. What keeps it fresh is my continued goodwill, and my goodwill has a price list. Item one: the Brandt family passes. Not the son alone — the whole family, papers processed, passage arranged, no reprisal camp at the far end. Item two: Ada Marlowe—" she saw him start at the name, and permitted herself the smallest satisfaction — "yes, she will take my name, and yes, that is a statement — Ada receives immunity in writing for anything done in the carrying of those letters, dated before her surrender of the documents, signed by someone whose signature outranks yours."

"And item three?" Reeve asked, because he was not a fool, and fools would have stopped counting at two.

"Item three is not a demand. It is a fact." Edith leaned forward. The apple-sweetness of the air seemed thicker here, as if the room itself had ripened toward the moment. "I do not withdraw. I go quiet — genuinely quiet — for as long as the terms hold. No platform speeches. No rallies. I shall discover, publicly, a passion for my garden. But I do not sign anything renouncing the work, because the day you or your successors break these terms is the day I stop being quiet, and I will not have given you the instrument of my own gag beforehand. My silence is a loan, Captain, not a sale. You may borrow it. You do not own it."

He studied her for a long moment. Outside, a rook crossed the window and its shadow slid over the milky glass like a hand.

"You understand," he said slowly, "that from where I sit, that is worse than withdrawal. A withdrawn suffragist is a closed account. A silent one is an overdraft."

"Yes," said Edith. "Now you understand how it feels to be a woman in England."

* * *

The last hour was the hardest, and it surprised her that it should have been, because it concerned no clause at all. It concerned the manner of the thing.

Because somewhere in the second hour, as they argued over the wording of the immunity — *acts performed in ignorance of their military significance*, she insisted, knowing that the ignorance clause was the only wall between Ada and a cell — Reeve put down his pen and rubbed his eyes, and for a moment he looked not like an officer of the War Office but like a tired man in a cold church, and she understood suddenly that he had been sent to do this by people above him who would never stand in the room, and that some part of him knew the file he was closing was a coffin lid laid over living men.

"Ainsworth was good to me," he said, abruptly, to the floor. "When I was a subaltern he—" He stopped himself. Straightened. "It doesn't matter."

"It matters enormously," Edith said. "It is the only honest thing anyone has said in this chapel today, including me. Loyalty to the man who lifted us is the most respectable chain there is, and the hardest to break, and breaking it costs more than the law ever admits." She paused. "I am not asking you to betray him. He betrayed himself, years ago, when he falsified the return and let a pension office pay a widow for a death that didn't happen. You are merely declining to carry his coffin for him. There is a difference, and you will sleep better once you can feel it."

"Do you always talk like a sermon?" he asked. It was almost — almost — a joke.

"Methodist grandparents," said Edith. "It's congenital."

And then, because power wielded without cruelty was the only kind she could live inside, she offered him the one thing that cost her nothing and might cost him everything: she told him she would not use the Loos scandal publicly. Not against Ainsworth, not against the Ministry, not in the pamphlets and not in the memoirs she fully intended to write someday under another name. The file would close because it deserved to close, not because she was bought.

Reeve looked at her for a long time across the dust-bright air.

"You're giving that up for nothing," he said. "It's your strongest card."

"It's not nothing. It's the difference between winning and merely beating someone." She gathered her reticule. "Besides, I have learned that the truth keeps better than blackmail. Blackmail spoils the moment it's spent. Truth improves with storage, like cider."

* * *

They walked out together as far as the gate, which neither of them had planned and both permitted. The evening had come on gold and cold; rooks were going over toward the woods in loose black handwriting. At the lane's mouth, far off, she could just see the motor, and Ivo leaning against it with his arms folded, watching the lane the way a man watches a door.

"Mrs. Marlowe." Reeve stopped at the stile. "Your brother. When he comes in — I'll be the officer receiving him. I thought you should know, so you could prepare him, or prepare yourself."

"That is either kindness or tactics."

"In my experience they're usually the same thing wearing different coats." He settled his hat. "The Brandt papers will take ten days. Perhaps twelve. After that, no one above me can guarantee anything."

"Ten days," Edith repeated, and felt the words land in her chest with weight, like coins into a well. Ten days in which a family in Kent and a promise made in peacetime and a boat somewhere on the coast all had to find each other. "Then we had better not waste a single evening of them."

She watched him go up the lane until the hedge took him. Then she stood a while longer in the cold gold light, letting the chapel settle behind her — the dust, the apple smell, the place where two people had sat and divided a human life into clauses and both, somehow, left with their souls mostly intact.

Then she lifted her skirts past the wet grass and walked toward the motor, and toward Ivo, and toward the news she had to carry home: that Julian must give himself up within the week, and that every hour between now and the Brandts' sailing was a coin balanced on its edge.

She was halfway down the lane when she began, quietly and without shame, to run.
