## 29. The Crossing

The fog came off the marshes at dusk like something poured rather than risen — a grey broth that swallowed the hedgerows first, then the oaks, then the last of the light itself, until the world beyond the windscreen of the Humber was a pale nothing with our own headlights swimming in it like two drowned coins.

I had driven in fog before. Every Kentish driver has. But I had never driven fog like this, carrying what we carried.

Herr Brandt's son sat in the back seat with his knees drawn up and his arms wrapped around a leather satchel he would not let out of his reach even to take the tea Ada had pressed on him at the farm gate. He was seventeen. His name was Emil, and he spoke English with his father's careful, old-fashioned precision, and when Ada had introduced us in the yard an hour earlier he had shaken my hand as though it were a ceremony he'd rehearsed and feared failing.

"Thank you for driving," he'd said. "Thank you very much."

As though I were a hired man doing a job. Which, in a sense, I was. Only the wage was not money.

Ada sat beside me in the passenger seat with the road map open on her knees, though the map was useless now — you cannot read a map against fog any more than you can read the future against it. She held it anyway. I think she needed her hands occupied the way other people need their hands held.

"The lane forks in half a mile," she said. "Left at the ruined barn."

"I know the lane," I said. "I've driven it since before you could walk."

"That's exactly why I'm telling you," she said, and there was a tremor under the dryness of it, and I understood that she was frightened and managing her fright by managing me, and that I loved her for it, and did not say so. There is a whole language of things I did not say that autumn. It may be the truest thing I ever wrote in anyone's letters.

* * *

We had agreed on silence past Ashford. Before Ashford there had been talk — low, practical, the kind of talk that is really a form of holding one another's coats. Edith had stood at Marlowe House door in the grey morning and given instructions like a general who has learned that generals mostly give instructions they cannot follow themselves.

"You will not speak at checkpoints unless spoken to," she said to Emil. "You are Mr. Ellery's nephew, convalescing from shell shock. You do not speak because shell-shocked men do not always speak, and no one will press a man who does not answer." She looked at him a long moment. "I am sorry to make your wound into a costume."

"It is a good costume," Emil said gravely. "It is nearly true. My father says we have all been wounded by this country, only some of us quietly."

Edith had smiled then — the first true smile I'd seen on her in a week — and touched his shoulder, and said, "Your father is a wise man, and I intend to tell him so when this is done, in whatever language pleases him best."

Then she had turned to me, and whatever she saw in my face made her drop the general entirely.

"Ivo." Just my name. "Bring them back — no. Bring him across, and bring her home. That is all. That is everything."

And Ada had kissed her cheek, and Edith had held her a moment longer than efficiency allowed, and then we were rolling down the drive and the fog took Marlowe House behind us as if it had never been built at all.

* * *

The first checkpoint was where the Canterbury road crossed the military railway line. We heard it before we saw it: a lantern swinging somewhere in the white blindness, and the flat call of a voice — "Halt! Who goes there?" — muffled by fog into something almost gentle.

My hands went cold on the wheel. Not shaking. Cold, as though the blood had been requisitioned elsewhere for some more urgent front.

"Soldiers' convoy," I called back, pitching my voice into its duller register, the one I used for tradesmen and officials. "Two men and an escort. Medical transfer to Folkestone."

A shape resolved out of the murk — young, rifle slung, greatcoat collar up, a corporal's stripes lost somewhere in the wet wool. He came to my window and the lantern light found my face first, then Ada's, then lingered on the back seat.

Emil was magnificent. He stared through the corporal with the thousand-yard vacancy I had seen on actual soldiers coming back from France, his mouth slightly open, his eyes fixed on the middle distance where nothing lived. The satchel lay across his knees and he rocked it faintly, faintly, the way men rock who have forgotten they are rocking.

"Who's this, then?" the corporal asked.

"Nephew," I said. "Mr. Ellery's boy. Came back from the Somme with his nerves gone. We're taking him down to Dr. Trentham's place outside Hythe — sea air."

"Sea air," the corporal repeated, without inflection. He leaned in. The lantern swung; shadows ran over Emil's face like water. "Bad?"

"Gone bad," I said. And then, because the truth is the best disguise when you can find the right piece of it: "He doesn't know where he is most days. Don't mind him."

"No," the corporal said softly, and something moved behind his eyes — recognition, or dread, or memory of a brother or a mate. He straightened up. "Right you are, sir. On you go. Mind the crossing — there's a train due and you won't hear it till it's on top of you in this."

I thanked him. My voice did not shake. I remember being surprised by that, the way you're surprised by anything you dreaded and survived.

We rolled across the rails, and a hundred yards on, in the solid white silence, Ada let out a breath she must have been holding since Ashford.

"He believed you," she whispered.

"He believed himself," I said. "That's better. A man who believes himself doesn't wonder later whether he was fooled."

Emil surfaced from his trance and looked at us both, and said, in his careful English, "I am sorry. I did not know how to be mad, so I thought about arithmetic. Long division. It makes the face go empty, I think."

Ada laughed — a small, cracked sound, half sob — and reached back and gripped his knee once, hard, and said, "Emil Brandt, you are the finest actor in England and if we live through tonight I will tell everyone, consequences be damned."

"Please don't," he said. "I would make a terrible famous person. I would have to be shot twice."

* * *

The second checkpoint was worse, because it was not a checkpoint at all.

It was near the top of Stone Street, that ancient paved way that runs down from the heights toward the coast, and the fog there was moving — sliding down the hill in slow ropes — and out of it came headlights. Two sets. Stationary, angled across the road, forming a gate of light we could see the beams of long before we could see the vehicles.

I pulled up under an elm whose black bulk was the first solid thing in twenty minutes, and we sat with the engine idling and watched.

"Not army," Ada said at last. Her voice had gone very level, which I knew meant very afraid. "Army uses lanterns. Look — those are motor cars, and there's a man walking the verge. That's police, or that's Reeve."

Through the slow-tearing fog I counted shapes: two motorcars, four men, one of them pacing. One carried a lamp and swung it along the hedgerow with the methodical patience of a man looking for something dropped — or someone hidden.

"They're not stopping traffic," I said. "They're watching the road. Which means they think someone will come down it tonight, and they don't know who."

"The innkeeper's man said midnight from the quarry track," Ada breathed. "This isn't the quarry track. Unless—"

"Unless Reeve guessed there'd be a decoy run and covered both roads." I looked at the fuel gauge, the clock, the fog. Eight minutes past eleven. The boat waited off Littlestone at one; the smuggler's skiff would come to the groyne at half twelve, and it would come once, and it would not wait, because men who run boats at night in wartime do not wait.

"We can't go through," Ada said. "Four men. They'd stop us for certain — a night convoy on Stone Street? They'd want papers, and Emil's are—" She stopped. Emil's papers were good for lanterns and boys and doctors' errands. They were not good for a policeman with time and a lamp.

"There's the old drover's road," I said slowly, working it out even as I said it, feeling the plan assemble itself out of things my grandfather told me and things I'd learned hauling contraband apples as a boy, though I had never once thought of that education as preparation for anything. "West off Stone Street, above the quarry. It's rutted hell and it adds forty minutes and if we break an axle we sleep in a field. But it comes down to the coast north of the lights, and it isn't paved, and no one watches a cart-track in fog because no one in a motorcar can follow it."

"In the Humber?" Ada said.

"In the Humber," I said, "if God forgives the suspension."

She looked at me across the dark of the cab, and the fog-light from the distant lamps caught just enough of her face to show me she was grinning — terrified, furious, alive.

"Ivo Underhill," she said, "you are full of surprises."

"I'm full of my grandfather," I said, and put the car in reverse.

* * *

What followed I will not easily forget, and could not honestly describe if I tried, because much of it happened in a white blindness broken by hedges lunging out of nowhere like the shoulders of giants. The drover's road was a trench between earth banks, and the Humber took it the way a horse takes water it distrusts — reluctantly, deeply, with much complaint. Stones cracked under us like pistol shots. Twice the near-side wheels left the ground entire. In the back, Emil held his satchel and recited, very quietly, the seventeen-times table, and I understood it was not for fear of the driving but for fear of the waiting, and I drove faster.

Once — only once — the fog opened, as fog does, like a curtain twitched aside by a bored hand, and we saw below us the whole coast laid out: the Channel invisible, the shingle gleaming faintly, and far to the south along Stone Street the two points of lamplight burning like the eyes of something patient. Then the curtain fell again and we were alone in our grey world with the engine roaring and the gorse scraping the doors.

"Half past twelve," Ada said at last, consulting her watch by feel. "The groyne should be — there. There! Lights!"

No. Not lights. A single shuttered lantern, opened and closed twice, three times, down at the tideline where a wooden groyne ran out into the muttering water. And beside it, a boat, and beside the boat, men.

I stopped the car above the shingle and my hands, which had been iron all night, chose that moment to begin shaking — the delayed bill for every mile of it. I sat and let them shake and was ashamed and did not care.

"Go," I said. "Both of you. I'll hold here and turn the car ready for a fast start, in case."

"In case of what?" Ada said.

"In case of whatever comes," I said. "That's been the whole job description lately."

She hesitated — one heartbeat, two — and then did the thing that undid me and remade me, all in the same instant. She leaned across and kissed my cheek, quick and hard, salt-wet from the fog, and said, "Come down after. Come down after or I'll come back up for you, and I'll be cross about it, and you know how I am when I'm cross."

Then she was gone, down the shingle with Emil's arm in hers, two small figures descending into the lantern-light and the sound of the sea, and I sat in the ticking, cooling car with my hand against my cheek like a schoolboy, and understood finally, completely, without any possibility of further argument, that history had not been happening elsewhere. It had been happening in this car, in this fog, to me, and its name was Ada.

I turned the Humber. I started down the shingle.

And that was when the headlights came over the ridge behind us.

* * *

They came fast — too fast for the drover's road, which meant they hadn't come by the drover's road, which meant while we crawled the hills they had taken Stone Street and swept the coast road and found nothing and doubled back along the shore track, and now their lamps pinned my mirror full of white fire and an engine note I knew. Big Wolseley. Official tyres.

Below me, the lantern winked its warning — three sharp flashes — and I saw figures scatter from the boat like startled birds, Ada among them, pulling Emil down behind the groyne timbers.

There was no decision to make. That is the strange mercy of such moments: the choice has already been made by everything you are, and all that remains is the doing.

I gunned the Humber straight down the beach — not toward the road, not away, but toward the boat, engine screaming in low gear, shingle spraying, and as I passed the groyne I flung the door wide without stopping and shouted, "Boat! Now! All of you!" and heard Ada scream my name as I roared past, drawing the headlights after me like a bull by its nose-ring.

I ran the car a quarter mile down the strand, wheels sinking, until the shingle became soft sand and the sand became the sea's own territory, and then the engine died drowning-deep and I got out and stood in the wash with the waves at my knees and the Wolseley slewing to a halt far up the beach, doors banging, torches stabbing the fog.

"All right!" I shouted, arms high, the oldest surrender gesture in the world. "All right! One man alone! One man!"

Torches converged. Boots crunched shingle. And out of the fog-ribbons ahead of the torches came a voice I knew, clipped and cold and, beneath it, running like groundwater, something that might have been relief.

"Underhill." Captain Reeve stopped six feet away, lamp raised, two constables flanking him with rifles that wavered between readiness and embarrassment. He looked at the drowned car, the empty beach, the sea. "Where are they?"

"Where are who, sir?" I said.

And far out in the fog, faint as a church bell underwater, I heard oars — muffled, wrapped, pulling hard — and the sound going away.

Reeve heard it too. His head turned a degree. The lamp trembled, a hair's breadth, and steadied.

"You sent them out," he said quietly, "and stayed to be caught. That is either very brave or very stupid, Underhill, and I have not yet decided which."

"It's usually both, sir," I said. "In my experience."

He looked at me for a long moment across the lamplight, and the sea sucked at our boots, and somewhere behind his eyes a ledger was being balanced that I could not see — the scandal he served, the mentor he protected, the terms Edith had wrung from him in a disused chapel, weighed against a boy on an open boat in a fog-bound channel.

"Take his name," Reeve said at last, turning away. "Interference with military operations. He'll answer for it Monday."

But he did not order a boat launched. And as the constables walked me up the shining strand, I kept my face forward and my heart out on the water, halfway across, with her — praying to a God I mostly ignored to keep the fog thick, the patrol blind, and the forged papers good by inches.

Because inches, I had learned that year, were all that history ever really turns on.
