## 30. Halfway Across

The fog did not lift with the dawn; it simply thinned, the way grief thins — never gone, only made bearable in layers.

Ada crouched low between the boat's thwart and its gunwale, one arm around Karl Brandt, who was seventeen years old and shaking so badly she could feel his tremors through her coat, through her own ribs, as though their bodies had agreed to share the fear between them. The boy smelled of engine oil and terror-sweat. The smuggler — a man called Tice, who had introduced himself at Hythe with the gravity of a vicar christening a child — worked the tiller in small, patient movements, reading the fog the way Ivo read letters: closely, hungrily, for what was hidden inside them.

"Keep down," Tice had said when they cast off from the shingle beach below the inn. "Down means alive. Up means questions." And then he had grinned, showing teeth stained brown by forty years of pipe smoke, and added, "Not that questions ever killed anybody. It's the answers."

Ada pressed her cheek to the cold wood of the gunwale and looked up at nothing. Salt spray had soaked through her skirt at the knees; her boots were ruined; her hands were two blocks of ache where they gripped Karl's sleeve. She should have been afraid of drowning. She found she was not. Drowning would be simple. It was everything above the water that had become complicated.

They had been under way perhaps an hour. Perhaps two. Time on the water refused to behave itself; it pooled and stretched like spilled ink. The engine — a muffled, smothered thing wrapped in sacking to keep its noise down — ticked beneath her feet like a second heartbeat, and over that she could hear the sea working against the hull, the endless indifferent shrug of it, and somewhere behind them, England dissolving into grey. Somewhere behind them, Ivo was driving back through the fog-bound lanes with the Brandt father beside him in the motorcar, and Edith was waiting at Marlowe House with a telephone she could not use and a silence she must keep, and Julian — Julian was walking toward a police station in Canterbury with his hat in his hands and his whole false life folded away like a letter no longer needed.

He is giving himself up for me, Ada thought, not for the first time, not for the hundredth time. He is walking into a cell so that I can walk onto this boat.

The thought did not make her feel saved. It made her feel spent, like a coin passed too many times to know whose face it bore.

Karl stirred against her shoulder and whispered something in German. She caught only the word *Vater*. Father.

"Soon," she whispered back, in German, because it seemed cruel to answer a homesick boy in any other tongue. "Soon you'll see him."

It was a lie, or near enough. His father was on a farm outside Ashford with a forged labour card and a name that wasn't his own, waiting for word that would come, if it came at all, weeks from now, by channels Ada half understood. But the boy needed the lie the way a drowning man needs driftwood — not because it will hold, but because holding it keeps your head above water.

She knew about needing lies. She had been living inside one since Boulogne.

* * *

The fog had been Tice's idea. "Best cover God provides free of charge," he'd said, squinting at the weather glass in his parlour above the inn. "Patrols don't like it. Engines cough in it. Men get lost in it and go home to their tea rather than admit it." But even Tice had hesitated at the strength of it tonight, standing on his doorstep looking out at a world erased, and Ada had seen the hesitation and understood: the fog protected them, and it endangered them equally, because a boat that cannot see is also a boat that cannot be seen from, until it is upon you.

Now the fog thinned further, and Ada saw the water for the first time — a long slow swell, iron-coloured, moving with the patient authority of something that has outlasted every empire. Gulls hung overhead like scraps of paper caught in wind. And far off, or perhaps not far off at all — distance meant nothing here — there was a sound.

At first she took it for the gulls. Then for her own pulse. Then Karl went rigid under her arm, and she knew he had heard it too, and that he had heard such sounds before, in places she tried not to imagine.

An engine. Not theirs.

Tice heard it too. She watched his shoulders change — the loose fisherman's slouch tightening into something coiled and quick — and his hand lifted, flat, downward: *stay*. The engine note dropped to almost nothing. The boat began to drift, wallowing, and the great grey swells took them up and set them down again like a mother handling a sleeping child, except that no child had ever been handled by a sea so vast and so unbothered.

The other engine grew louder. Then it stopped.

In the silence that followed, Ada heard voices carry across the water — distorted, directionless, the fog playing tricks with sound the way it played tricks with everything. A hail, in English. Unhurried. The voice of a man doing his job on a cold morning, expecting nothing but cold and routine.

Tice answered it. His voice came out changed — slower, broader, the voice of a Channel fisherman with a hold full of legitimate catch and no interest whatsoever in conversation. Ada understood, with a lurch, how many men this man had been. How many men *anyone* could be, if the water gave them room.

Karl's hand closed on her wrist hard enough to bruise.

"They'll board us," he breathed. "They always board."

*Yes*, she thought. *And when they do, they will find a fisherman, his mate — me, in a borrowed sou'wester, my hair stuffed up under it — and a boy with papers that say he is Dutch, papers bought from a man in Folkestone for more money than Karl's family had ever seen at once.* Papers that would hold, maybe. Papers that were good work, Tice had said, admiring his own supplier the way a carpenter admires another's joinery. But paper held by inches, and inches were all the sea allowed anyone.

The patrol boat materialized the way things materialize in dreams: gradually, then all at once. First a darker smudge within the grey. Then the suggestion of a rail, a mast, a figure. Then it was simply *there*, thirty yards off, a grey cutter with a Lewis gun mounted amidships and four shapes in naval coats along its side, and one of those shapes raised a hand and shouted across the water:

"Heave to! Stand by for inspection!"

*Stand by for inspection.* As if the sea were a railway platform. As if their lives were luggage to be opened and rummaged through and repacked carelessly, straps left undone.

Ada felt something rise in her that she recognized with horror as calm. It was the same calm that had come over her in the interrogation room at Folkestone, when Reeve's junior officers had fired questions at her and she had held to her story the way a drowning woman holds to a spar — except that this time the calm said something different. This time it said: *You know how to do this. You have done it before. You are, God help you, practised.*

And beneath the calm, quieter, colder, a second voice — the voice that had been growing in her since the night Julian burned his false names in the study fire and said, *I will not let them take you, Ada. Whatever happens, I will not let them take you.*

That voice said: *Give yourself up.*

* * *

Here was the arithmetic she could not stop performing, even as Tice shouted back his compliance and began, with theatrical slowness, to furl canvas he hadn't been using:

If the boarding party found Karl, the boy would be taken — not merely sent back, but taken, questioned, held. An enemy alien of military age attempting clandestine departure from wartime England was not a nuisance; he was a *category*. He would go to a camp, or worse, and the reprisal camps — Ada had read the reports Julian had smuggled to her, had read them twice and then put them face-down on the table because she could not look — the reprisal camps were places where boys like Karl went in and older, broken men came out, if they came out.

But if she stood up — if she said, *the boy is nothing, I am the one you want; I am Ada Kessler, daughter of Heinrich Kessler, interned on the Isle of Man these two years; I am the courier; I am the spy you've been hunting; take me and let the fisherman and his nephew go* — then Karl might pass. Tice's papers were built around a crew of two. If the suspicious element removed herself, the remaining pair might read as exactly what the papers claimed.

It was clean. It was almost elegant. One life offered up, and the arithmetic balanced.

She had learned arithmetic like this from the war. Everyone had. It was the only subject the war taught thoroughly.

Her hand moved to the collar of the borrowed coat. Under it, against her skin, hung the little tin locket — Julian's parting gift, absurdly sentimental, containing a scrap of orchard blossom pressed in paper and a slip with Edith's address in London, the safe house, the lawyer's name. Evidence, if she was searched. Everything she carried was evidence. That was the trouble with being a courier: you became the letter.

"Whatever happens," she murmured to Karl in German, "you say nothing. You understand? Nothing. Let me speak. Fishermen's nephews are shy. Play the shy one."

His eyes were enormous. "Fräulein Ada—"

"*Nothing*," she said, gentler, and squeezed his wrist, and began to count the yards between the boats as the patrol cutter's dinghy splashed down and started across the water toward them. Four oars. Two sailors in the stern sheets, an officer with a peaked cap and a lantern he didn't need yet but carried anyway, because authority liked to carry light whether or not it intended to shine.

Twenty yards.

Fifteen.

Ada drew a breath, tasting salt and diesel and the iron tang of her own fear, and prepared — truly prepared, the way one prepares for a dive into cold water — to stand up and end it.

*Julian gave himself up for me. It's only fair that—*

"No," said Karl Brandt.

She blinked. The boy — trembling Karl, weeping-for-his-father Karl, seventeen-year-old Karl who had crossed half of occupied Europe on borrowed courage — had shifted his grip from her wrist to her arm, and his grip was surprisingly strong, and his eyes, still enormous, had gone steady.

"You sit," he said, in English, carefully, each word laid down like a stone in a stream. "You taught me the words. *Hold the line.* That is what you say to me. So now I say to you." He swallowed. "No."

"Karl—"

"If you give yourself up, they search everyone anyway. Everyone. My cousin told me — once they smell one spy, they tear the whole boat apart looking for the rest. Your sacrifice buys nothing." His voice cracked on *sacrifice* and he pushed through it. "It buys *nothing*, Ada. It only spends you."

She wanted to tell him he was wrong. She wanted to believe he was wrong, because if he was wrong, then her plan was cowardice dressed as courage, and if he was right — if surrender bought nothing — then the only thing left was to sit in this rocking boat with her heart hammering and trust a forged Dutch passport and a fisherman's lies and a fog that was thinning by the minute.

Ten yards. The dinghy's bow ground against their hull with a wooden knock that Ada felt in her teeth.

The officer climbed aboard. He was young — younger than Julian, younger than Ivo — with a chapped face and the particular tiredness of a man in his third winter of watching empty water. He looked at Tice. He looked at Ada, taking in the sou'wester, the bound chest, the work-roughened gloves. He looked at Karl, curled small and pale against the thwart.

"Papers," he said.

Tice produced them from an oilskin wallet with the weary patience of a man who had done this a hundred times and resented it mildly every time. "Fisherman's licence. Registry for the *Doris May*, out of Hythe. Crew of two." He spat over the side, expertly, disrespectfully. "Third time this month you people have stopped me. There's no herring running, son, and there's no Hun in my hold. There's fish guts and bad weather and a fool's errand, and you're welcome to all three."

The officer was not listening to Tice. He was looking at Karl.

"Your nephew doesn't look well," he said.

"He's always sick on the water," Tice said. "Useless lad. His mother sends him to toughen him up and he spends the trip feeding the fish." He nudged Karl with his boot, affectionately, brutally. "Go on, boy. Show the officer how green you are. Don't spare him."

And Karl — Ada would think about this moment for the rest of her life, would return to it the way you return to the place where you once saw lightning strike — Karl leaned over the gunwale and vomited into the Channel with the full, convincing, miserable conviction of a boy who was genuinely terrified, which he was, and seasick besides, which he also was, and the officer stepped back with distaste and said, "Papers for the crew, then."

Karl handed them over with shaking hands. Shaking hands. Ada's stomach turned over. No forger could account for shaking hands.

The officer unfolded the passport under his lantern, though the grey morning light was sufficient, and read it, and Ada sat absolutely still and listened to the sea and counted — one, two, three — the way she had counted through the interrogation at Folkestone, and thought, wildly, *if he asks me one question I will stand up, I will do it now, I will—*

"Kessler," the officer said.

Ada's blood stopped.

"The name on this passport. Karl Kessler." He looked up. His eyes, pale and red-rimmed, went from Karl to her and back. "There was a Kessler on our list last month. Internee family. Man of that name on the Isle of Man."

The sea filled the silence. The gulls cried. Somewhere behind them, England was a rumour; somewhere ahead, France was a promise; and between them, in thirty feet of wooden boat, four people waited on the turn of a young officer's suspicion.

"My brother-in-law's name," Tice said easily. "Half of Holland is named Kessler or Van Dam or De Vries. You want to pull in every Kessler in the North Sea, you'll need a bigger boat." He jerked his thumb at the cutter. "That one looks big enough. Go on, then. Haul us both to Dover, lose me a day's fishing, and explain to your captain why the *Doris May*'s hold smells the way it does."

The officer looked at Karl a long moment. At the vomit drying on his chin. At his shaking hands.

Then he folded the passport, handed it back, and said, "Keep clear of the shipping lane east of the Varne. And if you see anything — any lights, any signals from the French side — you report it. That's an order, even for neutrals."

"Aye," said Tice. "We'll report the seagulls too, shall we?"

The officer did not smile. He climbed down into the dinghy, and the oars bit, and the cutter withdrew into the fog the way it had come, gradually, then all at once, until it was a smudge, then a memory, then nothing, and the engine sound faded, and the sea resumed its long indifferent breathing, and Ada discovered that she had been gripping the gunwale so hard her fingers would not straighten.

* * *

Karl slept, finally, an hour later, curled in the bilge space under the tarpaulin, exhausted past fear into the dreamless black sleep of the young. Tice steered and smoked and said nothing, which was his version of kindness. Ada sat at the stern and watched France assemble itself out of the mist — first a paleness at the edge of the world, then a line, then dunes, then the dark stitch of pines, then, faintly, a church spire, and bells. Actual bells. Carrying across the water the way voices carry, the way names carry, the way a promise made in peacetime had carried across two years of war to land, at last, in a wet boat off a Kentish beach.

She thought of Ivo's face when she'd left him at the crossroads — the way he'd held her hand a half-second longer than drivers hold passengers' hands, and said nothing, because Ivo said nothing; he carried things. Letters, secrets, other people's histories. He had carried hers without dropping it once.

She thought of Edith, who would be pacing the study right now, wearing the carpet thin between the desk and the window.

She thought of Julian, in a cell tonight, sleeping — if he could sleep — with the strange freedom of a man who has finally told the truth.

*I nearly gave myself away today,* she wrote in her head, composing the letter she would send through Tice's channels, the letter that would travel slower than she had travelled and arrive after she was safe. *And a boy of seventeen refused to let me. I taught him the English for "hold the line," and he used it against me. I have never been so furious and so grateful in the same breath.*

The boat grounded on the shingle with a long grinding sigh. Tice jumped down into the shallows and held the bow. Ada gathered up the sleeping boy's bag and her own small bundle — the locket, the letters, the photograph of Julian alive and bearded that she still could not look at without her throat closing — and stepped down into the cold water of France.

On the beach, a figure detached itself from the pines and came toward them, raising one hand in a signal Ada had been briefed on and had rehearsed until her fingers knew it in her sleep.

But it was the second figure, smaller, hanging back at the tree line, that stopped her breath — a figure in a woman's shawl who did not move, did not wave, only stood and watched the boat come in with an intensity Ada felt across fifty yards of cold sand like a physical weight.

Tice saw her too. He frowned. "That's not mine," he muttered. "Nobody was to be on this beach."

The figure in the shawl took a step forward. The dawn light, breaking at last through the ragged fog, caught her face — a face Ada knew from a single photograph, carried for two years, kissed nightly like a relic: thinner, greyer, older, unmistakable.

"Madre di Dio," Ada whispered — her mother's Italian surfacing, as it always did, at the edge of wonder.

Her mother was supposed to be in Holland. Her mother was supposed to be safe, three hundred miles north, beyond the war's reach. Instead she was standing on a smuggling beach in Picardy, and behind her, at the tree line, Ada could now see others — men shifting uneasily among the pines, and the dull gleam of something metal that was very much not a fishing knife.

Tice's hand closed hard on her shoulder. "Get back in the boat," he said quietly.
