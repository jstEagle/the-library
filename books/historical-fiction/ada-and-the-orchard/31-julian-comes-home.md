## 31. Julian Comes Home

The morning of the surrender began, of all things, with bread.

Edith Marlowe stood in the kitchen of Marlowe House at half past six, her hands buried to the wrist in flour, because sleep had proved impossible and kneading was the only prayer she trusted. The dough resisted her, then yielded, then resisted again — a conversation without words, the kind she had always preferred to the ones that required courage. Through the window above the basin the orchard showed grey and dripping, the old trees holding their October leaves like letters no one would collect. She had looked at those trees every morning for two years and seen a grave in them. This morning she could not decide what she saw.

The letter had come the evening before, delivered not by Ivo but by a boy on a bicycle who did not know what he carried. It was addressed openly — *Mrs. Edith Marlowe, Marlowe House, near Wye, Kent* — in handwriting she would have known blindfolded, in the dark, at the bottom of the sea. The loops of the *l* like little ladders. The crossbars on the *t* struck through with a soldier's economy, left to right, never twice.

*My dear Edith,* it began, as though two years were a weekend. *I am coming home on Thursday. I will walk into the police station at Ashford at ten o'clock and give myself up. I have arranged everything with Reeve's people and with mine. Do not try to see me before. Do not try to speak to me after. Let me do this one thing cleanly, since I have done so much else badly.*

*I have thought about what to say to you for two years, and all of it is useless on paper. Tell Ada the Brandt papers are where she knows. Tell Ivo he read better than any of us deserved.*

*You used to say I would break my neck showing off. You were right, but not in the way you meant.*

*Your brother, J.*

She had read it eleven times. She had held it over the stove flame until the paper warmed and smelled faintly of toast and of nothing else, testing it for invisible ink out of pure superstition, though there was none. There was only the plain terrible fact of it: Thursday. Ten o'clock. Her brother, alive, walking into a building full of policemen to hand them the ghost they had mourned.

Ada had cried when she read it — great silent tears, the crying of someone who had learned young that noise attracts attention — and then had gone out to the kitchen garden and pulled weeds with a violence that cleared three rows in an hour. Ivo had said nothing at all, which from Ivo was a speech. He had polished the car until it gleamed like a beetle, checked the tyres twice, and stood in the yard looking at the road to Ashford as if he could see all the way to Thursday down it.

Now it was Thursday, and the bread would not rise properly, and Edith's hands would not stop.

* * *

They drove in convoy, in a sense: Ivo at the wheel of the Hupmobile with Edith beside him and Ada pressed against the far door, and behind them the long wet ribbon of the Pilgrims' Way unspooling eastward under a sky the colour of pewter. Edith had insisted on driving herself once, years ago — Julian had taught her, shouting instructions across the paddock while the engine backfired and the hens scattered — but today her hands were needed elsewhere, folded in her lap, keeping each other company.

She dressed for it the way she dressed for a platform: deliberately, armour chosen with care. The grey wool suit with the collar that made her look, Ada had once said, like a portrait of somebody's formidable grandmother. A hat that shaded her face. Gloves, so that when the moment came her hands would not betray her. She had spent twenty years teaching women that their bodies were instruments of argument, and now she sat in the back seat of a motorcar trying to make her own body hold still.

"You don't have to watch," Ivo said. His eyes stayed on the road; his knuckles were white on the wheel. "From outside the station. If it's too—"

"I have spent two years not watching," Edith said. "I intend to spend the rest of my life doing otherwise."

The rain came sideways off the fields, needling the windscreen, and the wipers kept their small metronome time. Ada, in the corner, had Julian's first letter — the one she had carried through two borders and a war — open on her knees, not reading it, just resting her fingers on the paper the way one rests a hand on a sleeping animal to be sure of its breathing.

"They'll let you see him," she said suddenly, not as a question but as a decree, in that flat foreign-tinctured voice that hardened when she was most afraid. "Afterwards. They must."

"Must," Edith agreed, "is a word I've spent my life arguing with governments about." And was surprised to hear herself almost smile.

Ashford assembled itself around them gradually: the gasworks, the goods yard, the terraces with their curtained windows, the market square where a man was selling chestnuts from a brazier and the smoke rose straight up in the windless air. Ordinary Thursday things. Somewhere in this town men were going about the business of arresting her brother, and the chestnut seller did not know, and the curtains did not twitch, and history — that vast indifferent animal — walked through the streets wearing an ordinary coat.

Ivo drew up in the square opposite the police station, a squat brick building with a blue lamp over the door and a noticeboard of faded placards — recruiting posters from '14, gone soft and grey as old fruit. He cut the engine. In the sudden silence the rain sounded enormous.

Ten minutes to ten.

* * *

There was a room. Edith had not known there would be a room — some sergeant, bribed by nothing more than Captain Reeve's signature and the sheer weight of official boredom, had unlocked the magistrates' antechamber that adjoined the front office, separated from it by a partition of wired glass, the kind installed to protect clerks from angry debtors. From inside, one could see the whole of the entrance hall: the desk, the bench along the wall, the door to the cells, and beyond it the street door with its frosted panes.

Reeve was there already. He stood by the desk in civilian clothes — a brown tweed suit that fit him like an apology — and he looked smaller out of uniform, diminished, a man whose architecture had been removed overnight. When Edith entered with Ada and Ivo he did not greet them, only moved slightly aside, granting them the window, and there passed between himself and Edith a look she would remember all her life: not hostility, not quite shame, but the recognition of two people standing on either side of a transaction both wished had been unnecessary. He had lost. He knew she knew. Neither of them would say so.

"The terms are as agreed," he said quietly. "He gives himself up. He is processed here and transferred under escort. No photographs. No press — I've spoken to the *Kentish Express* myself." A pause. "You may observe. That is all. If you attempt to communicate with him, or he with you, I cannot protect the arrangement."

"I understand," Edith said, and found that she meant it. There was a strange mercy in rules, she thought. They told you exactly how much heartbreak you were permitted, and no more.

The room smelled of cold tea and floor polish and, beneath it, something older — the smell of buildings where decisions are made about other people. Ada took the chair nearest the glass and put both palms flat against it, as if warming herself at a window in winter. Ivo remained standing, hat in his hands, positioned slightly behind Edith's shoulder like a shadow that had learned loyalty. Through the wired glass the hall lay before them, empty, lit by a high window that turned the rain on the cobbles outside into falling silver.

At four minutes past ten, the street door opened.

* * *

Later, Edith would try to reconstruct the moment honestly, and find she could not. Memory, she wrote afterwards in the diary she kept locked in her writing desk, is not a photograph; it is a painting done from a description given by a witness who has fainted. But certain details burned themselves in with terrible clarity.

He was thinner. That was the first thing — thinner, and bearded, the beard greying at the jaw, so that for one vertiginous second she saw their father in the doorway instead of her brother. He wore a good coat that hung on him like a borrowed opinion, and he carried nothing, absolutely nothing, no bag, no bundle — a man arriving at the end of a journey having already set down everything he owned. Rain stood on his shoulders. His boots were wrong, city boots, polished too carefully, the boots of a disguise rather than a life.

And then he looked around the hall — once, quickly, the surveying glance of a man who had spent two years entering rooms and measuring them for exits — and his eyes found the wired glass, and found her.

Edith had rehearsed this. On the drive she had composed herself into composure the way she composed a speech, clause by clause: she would be calm, she would be dignified, she would not weep in front of Captain Reeve, she would give her brother the gift of a steady face at the end of his long road. She had believed it, the way one believes a timetable.

What happened instead was that grief and joy arrived simultaneously, from opposite directions, and met somewhere behind her sternum with a concussion she felt in her teeth. Joy — *he is alive, he is alive, the world contains him* — and grief, black and enormous, for the two years, for Loos and the lie of Loos, for the telegram and the memorial service and the empty chair at Christmas, for her mother dying not knowing, for every night she had lain awake inventing his death in new varieties. Both at once. Neither yielding. She gripped the windowsill and made a sound she was grateful the glass could not carry.

Julian's face changed. The careful blankness — the fugitive's mask she had seen described in a hundred wanted notices without ever seeing the thing itself — cracked clean through. He lifted one hand, halfway, a gesture begun toward the glass and arrested by Reeve's presence, by the law, by two years of discipline. He let the hand fall. His mouth shaped something.

She read it through the glass, through the rain, through the whole obstructive apparatus of the world: *Edie.*

No one had called her Edie since 1908.

Then the desk sergeant came out of the inner office wiping his moustache, saying something procedural, and the moment folded itself away like a letter returned to its envelope, and Julian turned to the desk, and took out his papers, and surrendered.

* * *

It was, Edith thought, the quietest catastrophe she had ever witnessed. No handcuffs — Reeve had arranged that much, at least. The sergeant read from a printed form in a voice flattened by repetition. Julian answered questions in a voice she barely recognized, pitched low and level, each word placed like a stone in a wall. Yes. Underhill — no, that was another matter. Yes, Julian Edward Marlowe, formerly Second Lieutenant, 9th Battalion. Yes, he understood the charge. He had come of his own accord. He wished to state, for the record, that he had written to the War Office giving his intentions, and that correspondence would confirm the date.

His voice did not shake. His hands, resting on the desktop, did not shake. Only once — when the sergeant asked him to sign — did he pause, pen hovering, and look at the name he was about to write as a man looks at water he is about to dive into. Then he signed *Julian Edward Marlowe*, in the looping ladder-legged hand she had known since his school copybooks, and the signature seemed to cost him something physical; Edith saw his shoulders take the weight of it, saw him straighten under it, and understood that for two years he had signed other names, false names, dead men's names, and that writing his own was a kind of resurrection performed in front of witnesses.

Ada's palms were still flat against the glass. Tears ran freely down her face and she made no move to stop them, and her lips moved silently, shaping words in — Edith realized with a small shock — German. A prayer, perhaps. Or a promise. Beside her, Ivo had gone very pale and very still, and when Edith glanced up at him she found him not watching Julian but watching Ada, with an expression he would have denied under oath and which Edith, who had spent her life reading faces across hostile rooms, read perfectly. She said nothing. Some knowledge is a gift you hand over later, wrapped.

The sergeant disappeared with the papers. Julian stood alone in the middle of the hall, waiting, the way men wait in stations — that suspended patience, belonging nowhere. And in that interval of nothing-to-be-done, he turned again to the glass, deliberately this time, knowing it was forbidden, doing it anyway.

He raised his hand — the arrested gesture completed at last — and touched two fingers to his brow. Not a salute. Something older than that. Their father's gesture, at the gate, leaving for London on the early train, every weekday of their childhood: *mind the house, mind your sister.* And Edith, forty-one years old, a woman who had faced hostile crowds and hunger strikers' funerals and the specific fury of men told no by a woman, pressed her gloved hand to the glass exactly where his fingers would have been if glass were not glass, and held it there.

The wired glass was cold. Of course it was cold. It was October, and the room was unheated, and the pane divided her from her living brother by a quarter inch of diamond-latticed security and two years of the dead.

Through it, faintly, she heard him laugh — one breath of laughter, broken in the middle, the sound of a man laughing at a funeral because the alternative was worse. Then the cell door opened at the back of the hall, and a younger constable appeared, and Julian squared his shoulders with a little roll like a man settling a pack, and walked toward it without hurry, and at the threshold he stopped, and turned, and looked at her one last time.

What passed between them in that final look Edith never attempted to describe, even to herself. It contained, she thought, the whole of their childhood — the orchard, the arguments, the summer he'd taught her to swim in the Stour and nearly drowned doing it, the promise he'd made in 1911 over brandy and chess about a German family whose name he would not yet say aloud. It contained forgiveness — she could not have said who was forgiving whom, and suspected the direction changed twice. It contained goodbye, and it did not contain goodbye, because he was alive, and the dead say goodbye but the living say *later*, and his mouth, before the door shut, very clearly formed the word *later*.

The door closed. The lock turned with a sound like a knuckle cracking.

* * *

For a long moment nobody in the antechamber moved. The rain continued its silver work on the cobbles. Down in the hall the desk sergeant began, unbelievably, to whistle — eight bars of some music-hall tune, tuneless and contented, a man whose morning's work was done.

"Well," said Reeve.

Edith turned from the glass. Her face was wet; she discovered this without surprise, as one discovers weather. She removed her glove — the right one, finger by finger, unhurried — and wiped her cheeks with the bare hand, and looked at Captain Reeve with eyes she could feel were red-rimmed and utterly, dangerously clear.

"He kept his word," she said.

"He did."

"Then you will keep yours. All of it. The transfer arrangements. The medical examination — he is not well, whatever he pretends; look at his wrists when they process him, he has been ill and hiding it, I know the signs, I ran a hospital ward in '15. And the rest." She stepped closer, and dropped her voice to the register she used for closing arguments, the one that made cabinet ministers check their notes. "Because I have spent two years believing my brother's bones were in a French churchyard, Captain, and I find I am no longer inclined to be reasonable about anything. The tribunal will hear the truth — all of it, including what your department buried at Loos. My counsel has the papers. My friends know where I keep the copies. Am I understood?"

Something moved behind Reeve's eyes — weariness, chiefly, and under the weariness something that might, in a better man, have been relief. "Understood, Mrs. Marlowe."

"Good." She drew the glove back on. Her hands, she noticed distantly, had finally gone still.

Ada rose from her chair. She crossed to the glass and laid her forehead against it, briefly, the way one touches a headstone or a lover's shoulder — a farewell to the room where Julian had stood, to the quarter inch of winter-cold wire between promise and performance. Then she turned, and her chin came up, and she was, Edith saw, entirely Ada again: the girl who had crossed a continent on a forged pass, who had stared down port officials, who had a father somewhere in an internment camp and a family in Germany waiting on a promise made in peacetime by a man now sitting in a cell thirty feet below them.

"When can we see him?" Ada asked.

"That," said Reeve, "is not in my gift."

"No," Edith said. "But it will be in someone's."

They went out together into the square, the three of them, into the smell of rain and roasting chestnuts. The Hupmobile waited where Ivo had left it, beaded with water, patient as a horse. Somewhere behind them, down a corridor and through a locked door, Julian was being measured and recorded and entered into the machinery of the state that had pronounced him dead; and here in the open air the town went on buying chestnuts, and a church clock struck the quarter hour with its usual indifference, and Edith Marlowe stood in the rain in her best grey wool with grief and joy still lodged together behind her sternum like two passengers sharing a compartment, neither willing to get off.

"We should go home," Ivo said gently. "There's nothing more—"

"No," Edith said. "There's a great deal more. Get in."

She settled onto the leather seat and took out the letter — Julian's letter, Thursday's letter, the plain terrible wonderful thing — and unfolded it on her knee, and read again the last lines, the ones she had read eleven times and now read a twelfth with different eyes:

*Tell Ivo he read better than any of us deserved.*

"There is a hearing," she said, as Ivo started the engine and Ada climbed in beside him, shaking the rain from her hair. "A tribunal. Within the fortnight, Reeve says. They will weigh two years of desertion against whatever it is they're burying, and they will decide what my brother is worth." She looked up through the streaming windscreen at the road ahead, running west out of Ashford, running home through the dripping orchards. "Which means, between now and then, we are going to teach me how to testify — and Ivo, my dear, it appears that after all these years of carrying other people's words—"

She folded the letter closed.

"—you're finally going to be asked what you read."
