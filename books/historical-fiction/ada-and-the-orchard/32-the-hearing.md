## 32. The Hearing

The tribunal sat in a room above a drill hall in Maidstone, and the room had been chosen, Ivo thought, for its power to make a man feel small. It was long and narrow, lit by high windows set with frosted glass that turned the January light the colour of skimmed milk. The floorboards had been scrubbed until they were pale as bone, and they creaked under every boot in the room, so that even stillness seemed to confess something. At the far end stood a table behind which three officers sat — a colonel in the middle, flanked by a major and a chaplain whose collar was white as a wound — and before them, at a smaller table, a clerk with a fountain pen that scratched like a mouse in a wall.

Ivo had driven Edith to Maidstone through fog so thick the hedgerows existed only as sound. She had not spoken the whole way. Her hands had been folded in her lap around a small black book — her diary-letters, the ones he had read without her leave, or so he had believed until November — and when the car stopped she had looked at him once and said only: *Whatever happens in there, Ivo, you are the only one of us they cannot frighten. You have already given them your worst secret.*

He hadn't understood what she meant then. He understood it now, standing in the milk-white light with his cap in his hands.

Julian Marlowe sat at a plain table in the centre of the room, in uniform — his old uniform, brushed and mended, the buttons polished by Ada's hands the night before until they threw back small coins of light. Two years of another man's life had weathered him; the beard was gone now, shaved off two days ago in a barbershop in Tunbridge Wells while Edith watched through the window with her breath fogging the glass, and without it his face looked younger than his years and older than his photograph, both at once. His hands lay flat on the table. They did not move. Ivo had seen men keep their hands still like that on the drive up from Dover — men who had learned, somewhere bad, that movement was noticed.

The charges were read aloud in a voice like a train announcement: desertion in the face of the enemy, absence without leave, falsification of identity, conduct prejudicial to good order. The words fell into the room one after another and lay there, heavy as wet sand.

*How do you plead?*

*Not guilty to desertion, sir.* Julian's voice was quiet but it reached every corner; the room's very emptiness helped him. *Guilty to the rest.*

The colonel leaned forward. *You understand, Captain Marlowe, that the court is bound by the Army Act. Desertion carries—*

*I understand what it carries, sir.* A pause. *I also understand that I am not the man this court believes deserted at Loos, and I intend to show you why.*

* * *

They began, as such things always begin, with paperwork. The clerk read the death notification from September 1915. He read the regimental returns. He read the telegram sent to Marlowe House and the receipt signed by Edith Marlowe, and at that signature Ivo saw Edith's jaw tighten, once, and release.

Then the president called the first witness: Captain Reeve.

Reeve came in through the side door and Ivo studied him with a kind of grim appetite. This was the man who had sat across from him in a tea shop in Ashford and asked about suspicious mail with a smile that never touched his eyes; the man who had turned over Marlowe House's papers with gloved hands and the particular gentleness of someone looking for exactly one thing. Reeve wore his uncertainty well. But Ivo had spent two years reading what people sealed inside envelopes, and he could see the seal on Reeve had been broken. He glanced at the president only when spoken to. His answers were precise. And twice — only twice — his gaze slid to the far door, where his superior, General Sir Harcourt Vane, would enter later in the day, and both times it came away quickly, like a tongue finding a chipped tooth.

Reeve testified to the investigation: the coded circulars, the signal box at Westenhanger, the woman detained at Folkestone pier. He described Ada in flat administrative language — *the female person known as Ada Brandt-Keller* — and Ivo felt his jaw ache and realized he had been clenching it.

*Did your inquiries establish,* the major asked, *whether Captain Marlowe was engaged in espionage?*

Reeve hesitated. It was a small hesitation, half a second, but in that scrubbed room it rang like a dropped coin. *My inquiries established that Captain Marlowe was living under a false name in Holland, sir. The purpose of his residence remained unclear to me.*

*Unclear,* the colonel repeated. *For eighteen months of investigation.*

*Some matters, sir, are clearer at certain distances than others.*

It was the nearest thing to a confession anyone would get from him that morning, and Ivo understood it for what it was: a man laying down cards face-up, one by one, waiting to see who blinked first. Reeve knew about Vane. Had perhaps known for weeks — since the archivist Mr. Pell, since the falsified death report with its impossible ink, since the letter in which Julian named his guarantor. He was hunting his mentor's scandal, yes, but he was also, Ivo realized with a strange disloyal pang of sympathy, a man who had discovered too late that the case he'd built was standing on a lie told before the war began, by a superior he could not touch except through this room.

*Thank you, Captain Reeve.* The colonel made a note. *You will remain available.*

* * *

They called the archivist in the afternoon. Mr. Pell was seventy-one and deaf in one ear and he answered questions with the unhurried courtesy of a man who had outlived everyone who might have intimidated him. He produced the death report — the original, carried in a leather folder against his chest like a grandchild — and laid it before the court.

*Item four, sir,* he said, tapping one line with a blunt finger. *Date of entry. The ink. If you'll permit me.* He took out a jeweller's loupe, absurd and wonderful in the grey light. *This report was typed on the twelfth of September 1915. The date of death recorded is the twenty-fifth. Yet the date line has been altered — struck through and re-entered — in an ink containing an aniline dye not issued to clerical staff until the spring of 1916. Someone amended this document no earlier than eight months after it was supposedly written. And the amendment was initialled — here — with initials belonging to Lieutenant-Colonel A. Vane.*

The room went very quiet. Somewhere below them a bugle sounded the half hour, muffled by floorboards.

*In your professional opinion, Mr. Pell, how did such an error arise?*

Pell folded his loupe with great care. *It was no error, sir. I processed four hundred death reports that autumn. One learns the rhythm of them. This one was brought to me completed — pre-typed, pre-signed, pre-witnessed — by an officer who did not wish me to examine the particulars. I examined the particulars anyway. I wrote to the War Office. My letters were returned unopened. Then I retired.* He looked up, and his milky eye and his sharp one fixed on the president together. *A record, sir, can be murdered just as surely as a man. Only more quietly, and by better-dressed hands.*

* * *

General Sir Harcourt Vane entered at four o'clock and left at twenty past, and in between he said almost nothing that mattered. He confirmed that he had known Captain Marlowe before the war. He confirmed that he had authorized *certain irregular arrangements* in 1915 regarding intelligence channels through Holland. Asked directly whether he had falsified the death report, he replied that the question insulted the service, and the president, who had received — Ivo saw the paper passed up the table, and saw the colonel read it and go carefully blank — a certain document that morning, chose not to press. Not yet. The leverage Edith had gathered in the chapel at Charing was being held like a stone under a cushion: invisible, and shaping everything beneath it.

But Vane's departure marked the turn of the day, because the president then said:

*The court will hear from the witness Underhill.*

* * *

Ivo had sworn the oath with his hand on a Bible he privately suspected of having witnessed worse, and now he stood in the frosted light with his cap damp in his grip, and the president said: *State your name and occupation.*

*Ivo Underhill. Chauffeur and courier to Miss Edith Marlowe of Marlowe House, Kent.*

*You are acquainted with the accused?*

*No, sir. Not before October.* A breath. *I know his sister. I know the letters.*

*Letters.* The colonel's pen stopped. *Explain.*

And here it was — the moment he had rehearsed on the fog-bound road and abandoned eleven times, because every rehearsal sounded like a defence and this must not be a defence. It must be a confession. He had promised himself on the drive, watching Edith fold her hands around her black book, that he would give this court nothing laundered. He had spent two years opening other people's seals. Today he would open his own.

*I read the mail, sir.*

The scratching of the clerk's pen stopped.

*Miss Marlowe's correspondence. Suffrage correspondence, mostly — circulars, subscriptions, angry letters from vicars' wives. Sealed, all of it. I steamed the envelopes open over the kettle in the garage and read them, and sealed them again, and delivered them, and told myself it didn't count because history happened to other people and I was only the man who carried their words.* He heard his own voice steady itself, which surprised him. *I am telling the court this because it is the reason I recognized what I was holding, and the reason I nearly destroyed it.*

*Go on.*

*In March of last year a circular came through the network — the ordinary suffrage circular, printed in London, distributed by post. Except this one had been composed by hand first and typeset after, and the composition followed a pattern. Every seventh word, counting from the salutation, spelled a message if you wrote the words down in order.* He looked at Julian. Julian was watching him with an expression Ivo had never seen on a soldier's face — the look of a man hearing his own voice come out of someone else's mouth, faithfully, across two years and a false grave. *The message was in a cipher I didn't know yet. I transcribed it anyway. I kept it in my toolbox for five months, sir, because I thought it was nonsense, and I was ashamed to ask anyone, because asking meant admitting I'd opened the envelope.*

*And the shame,* said the chaplain, mildly, *was the greater burden?*

*The shame was the lock, sir.* Ivo turned back to the bench. *Everything I've watched happen since October happened because I finally broke it. And everything I'm about to say, I say badly, because I am a driver, and I have spent my whole life believing that words belong to other people. So I'm going to read you the letters instead. All of them. In order. Because I have read every word of them already — honestly, this time, with permission — and because I don't trust my own sentences to carry what his do.*

He took the packet from inside his jacket. Eleven letters, worn soft as cloth at the folds. Ada had sewn them into his coat lining for the journey; he had taken them out himself, in the corridor, wanting them where the court could see them.

*May I, sir?*

The colonel nodded slowly. *Read.*

Ivo unfolded the first letter, and the room's silence changed texture — became the silence of people listening rather than waiting.

*"'Dear Edith,'"* he read. *"'By the time you hold this you will have hated me for two years, and you will be right to. I did not die at Loos. I ran. I want that stated plainly, because everything else I write depends on it being understood that I am not asking you to excuse a coward but to employ one.'"*

His voice found its footing on the second paragraph. He read the account of Loos — the smoke that ate its own artillery barrage, the order to advance into ground no runner had scouted, the moment Julian understood that following orders that morning would spend forty lives buying a map-maker's error, and that refusing would buy a firing squad instead. He read how Julian had gone over the top anyway because the alternative was worse than dying, and how, in the confusion of the gas and the broken wire, carrying a wounded German sergeant toward the aid post, he had simply kept walking — past the post, past the line, into the fog, with a man on his back whose name he did not yet know. How the sergeant's family, the Brandts, were the same family he had sworn a promise to in 1911, in peacetime, at a garden table, laughing. How he had crossed into Holland with false papers the sergeant's cousin provided, and lived under the name Cornelis Verhagen, and begun writing home in cipher hidden in the only channel he trusted: his sister's own movement, its circulars passing through hundreds of innocent hands, unreadable to any censor because no censor imagines a dead man writes suffrage propaganda.

*"'I chose your war, Edith, because it was the one place in England where a dead brother's name would pass through the post untouched. Forgive me for using your cause as my camouflage. Forgive me more for being right that no one would look. They did not look, Edith. For two years, no one looked — except a chauffeur who wasn't supposed to read, and couldn't help it, and saved my life by being unable to mind his own business.'"*

Someone in the gallery — Ivo did not dare look — made a small sound.

He read the Brandt letters next: the plea for the son, Karl, marked for deportation to a reprisal camp; the dates; the names; the promise made in 1911 and invoked in 1916. He read Ada's testimony into the record — she had already given it, but he read Julian's references to her, the landlady's daughter who carried letters across borders in a hatbox, *"'braver than the entire British Expeditionary Force and paid in eggs.'"* A rustle went through the officers; the chaplain's mouth twitched and was mastered.

He read the last letter, written six days before Julian walked into the police station at Ashford. *"'Tell the court the truth in whatever order hurts most. Truth in order of comfort is just lying with good manners. And tell the chauffeur — tell Ivo — that history has been happening to him all along. He was never the man outside the story. He was the story's only honest reader.'"*

Ivo lowered the pages. His eyes were hot and his voice had gone rough near the end and he did not apologize for either.

*That is everything I have, sir. I stole these letters first and earned them after. I leave it to the court which act mattered.*

* * *

The cross-examination lasted forty minutes. The major wanted to know why Julian hadn't surrendered sooner; Julian answered, evenly, that the Brandt boy's deportation order had a date on it and surrender did not. The major wanted to know who else had handled the ciphered circulars; Julian declined to answer, and the president — glancing again at the paper before him — allowed the decline. When the major asked whether the accused considered himself above the law of the army, Julian said:

*No, sir. Below it, and underneath it, and buried by it, for two years. I am asking the court to dig me up, not to crown me.*

At seven o'clock the president announced that the court would deliberate overnight and reconvene at ten. Bailiffs moved through the room. Edith rose, and Ada rose beside her, and for one moment — one only, in the emptying chamber, under the eyes of a bored military policeman — Edith crossed the floor and put her hand flat on her brother's shoulder, and Julian covered it with his own, and neither of them said anything at all, because everything had already been read aloud.

Outside, the fog had lifted. The stars over Maidstone were hard and close, and Ivo stood by the motorcar breathing air that tasted of coal smoke and frost, and Ada came to stand beside him, her shoulder just touching his arm.

*You were wonderful in there,* she said.

*I read aloud. That's all.*

*That's all,* she repeated softly, as though the words were a spell. *Two years of ghosts, undone by a man reading out loud.*

They drove back through the dark with the headlamps carving a yellow corridor out of the night, and none of them slept, and at half past nine the next morning the summons came: the court had reached its findings, and would pronounce sentence at noon.

Ivo drove them in. The frost lay silver on every hedge between Maidstone and the drill hall, and Edith sat straight as a mast, and Ada held Julian's spare gloves in her lap as though holding his hands, and Ivo thought: whatever they give him, whatever they take — I was there. I was in the room. It happened to me.

The bailiff opened the door at noon precisely, and the president rose, and the room came to its feet, and the colonel's voice began the words that would decide whether the ghost of Julian Marlowe was buried forever or sent home.
