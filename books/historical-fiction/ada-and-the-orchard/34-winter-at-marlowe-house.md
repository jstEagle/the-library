## 34. Winter at Marlowe House

The first frost came on the third of December, silvering the orchard grass so that at dawn the whole slope behind Marlowe House looked dusted with sugar, every blade of grass holding its own small light. Ada stood at her bedroom window with her breath fogging the glass and watched Ivo cross the yard to the garage, his collar up, his breath pluming after him like a thought he couldn't quite catch. He stopped halfway, as he did every morning now, and looked up at her window, and she raised one hand against the pane, and he raised his in answer, and that was all. That was everything. Two years ago such a gesture would have meant nothing to her; now it was the whole grammar of her mornings.

Julian's room had been moved downstairs, into what had once been Edith's morning parlour, because the stairs were still beyond him. His lungs had not forgiven France, nor Belgium, nor the false names he'd breathed through for two years, nor the tribunal itself, which had taken something out of him even as it gave him back his name. The doctors said *convalescence*, that lovely long word that meant nothing and everything: time, rest, warmth, patience. It meant, in practice, that Marlowe House had rearranged itself around a convalescent the way a river rearranges itself around a stone.

Ada had meant to stay a fortnight. That had been in October, when Julian came home and the house stood shocked and half-lit, everyone moving carefully, as though the rooms might bruise. Then the fortnight became a month, and the month became a position — *secretary*, Edith had announced over breakfast one morning, in the tone she used for things already decided — and by December Ada could no longer remember what it felt like to live anywhere else. She had a desk now, in the corner of the library, under the window where the light came longest. She had a drawer with her own key. She had a chair pulled close to the fire that everyone else had tacitly agreed was hers, and when Julian tried to sit in it one evening, three separate voices told him no before he'd even finished sitting down.

He laughed about it afterward, propped on pillows, grey-faced and pleased. "I've been shot at, starved, court-martialled in absentia, and interrogated by Captain Reeve himself," he said. "And this is the first place in two years where anyone tells me what to do."

"That's called a family," Edith said from the doorway, without looking up from the letters in her hand, and then went pink to the roots of her hair, and left the room rather quickly, and Julian and Ada looked at each other for a long moment, and neither of them said anything at all, because there was nothing that needed saying.

* * *

Winter settled in properly, and the house found its rhythms.

Mornings belonged to work. Edith's correspondence had grown monstrous since the affair with Reeve — quietly resolved as it had been, publicly invisible, its cost paid in private currencies — because word travels in strange channels, and other women in other counties had begun writing to ask how she'd held her ground. Ada answered most of them. She had learned Edith's voice the way you learn a piece of music: the firm opening, the refusal of apology, the closing sentence that always turned toward action. *We do not ask permission to be useful,* Edith liked to write, and so Ada wrote it too, forty times some weeks, until her wrist ached and the phrase felt like a prayer worn smooth by handling.

She kept the files now, too — the great suffrage archives that had hidden Julian's letters through the search in November, buried among minutes and circulars where Captain Reeve's men had never thought to look, because why would they, because it was women's paperwork, because contempt made them blind. Ada had reorganised the whole system since, and taken a certain fierce pleasure in it. Every document cross-referenced. Every name indexed. Somewhere in those drawers lay the evidence of everything they'd survived, filed between a circular on the Cat and Mouse Act and a subscription list from Tunbridge Wells, and Ada touched the drawer handles sometimes just to feel the weight of it.

Julian slept late and read later. He worked his way through the books on Edith's shelves like a man eating a meal after a famine, and argued with them, out loud, alone, until someone came to find out who he was talking to. His hands still shook on bad days — the tribunal doctor called it nerves, called it shell shock in the newer papers, wrote it down in Latin — but on good days he sat at the little table by the window and wrote letters of his own, real ones, signed with his own name, which Ada understood was a pleasure she could only imagine. For two years every signature he'd written had been a lie. Now he practised his own name in the margins of newspapers, over and over, *J. Marlowe, J. Marlowe*, like a schoolboy learning penmanship, and if anyone noticed, nobody teased him, which was its own kindness.

Afternoons, if the weather allowed, they got him outside. This was Ada's campaign, conducted with the gentle ruthlessness she'd learned from her mother. Blankets, a rug over the knees, a thermos of coffee that Ivo carried out balanced on a tray like a waiter in a hotel he disapproved of. They'd sit him in the garden chair facing the orchard, and the three of them would keep him company in the thin gold light, breath steaming, talking about nothing — the price of coal, the vicar's sermon, whether the rooks were plotting something, which Julian maintained they always were.

It was on one of these afternoons that the arguments started in earnest, and Ada understood, listening to them, that the house was healing, because families are known by their arguments the way rivers are known by their bridges.

* * *

The first argument was about grafting.

"It can't be done in winter," Ivo said. He'd come out to report on the car — the Austin needed antifreeze and a new fan belt and, in his opinion, an exorcism — and had stayed to dispute horticulture with a woman ten years his junior, which Ada noted he did with increasing frequency and decreasing resistance.

"Apples, no," Ada agreed. "But pears, yes — quince stock especially, you can bench-graft in the shed, out of the frost. My father—" she stopped, the way she still stopped, a hitch in the sentence like a stair with a missing step. "A man I knew abroad taught me. You take the scion wood now, while it's dormant, and heel it in sand till spring."

"A man you knew," Julian said, from under his blanket, with the delicacy of a man walking on ice.

"Mr. Weiss. He ran the nursery outside Cologne." She said it deliberately, watching their faces, and both faces did exactly what she expected: Ivo's flickered with the old reflexive stiffness — the word *Cologne* still landed somewhere uncomfortable in English chests, and she saw him catch it and master it, the way he'd learned to over the autumn — and Julian simply nodded, because Julian had lived under German names for two years and had no illusions left about what nationality proved.

"He taught me grafting and I taught him English insults," Ada said. "A fair exchange. He used to say a grafted tree is a letter written between two trees that takes years to read." She paused. "I'd like to plant here. In spring. If Edith agrees."

"Edith will agree," said Edith's voice from the kitchen window, where she'd been standing for some minutes pretending to look at the thermometer. "But we'll discuss the orchard properly, at table, like civilised people, and not while Julian is turning blue."

"I am not turning blue," said Julian, turning slightly blue.

That evening the orchard was discussed at table like civilised people, which is to say the discussion lasted two hours, involved three maps drawn on the backs of envelopes, one genuine quarrel (Ivo and Julian, over drainage, escalating with astonishing speed to a dispute about the entire concept of Kent), and ended with everyone laughing so hard that Julian had to be given his drops early and sent to bed, still laughing, protesting, wheezing happily, a man being fussed over against his will and loving every second of it.

Ada washed the wine glasses afterward and heard them through the wall — Edith reading aloud to Julian, something dull and improving, her voice going soft at the ends of sentences the way it never did anywhere else — and she dried the same glass twice, three times, letting herself have the moment entire. A year ago she had been a stranger standing in rain at a gate, carrying a dead man's handwriting, afraid to be believed. Now she owned a chair by the fire and a drawer with a key and a place at a table where people argued about drainage.

* * *

There were harder evenings too. Nobody pretended otherwise.

Some nights Julian woke shouting, or worse, woke silent, lying rigid with his eyes open, back in a trench or a border office or the long grey corridor of his own deception. Ada heard him through the floor more than once — the house was old and honest about what happened inside it — and more than once she heard Edith's door open, and Edith's footsteps go along the landing and down the stairs, and then nothing, just the murmur of a voice reading something, anything, psalms or shipping forecasts or the suffrage circulars, whatever words were nearest to hand, read aloud into the dark until the breathing below evened out.

Once, near midwinter, it was Ada who found him. She'd been up late with the correspondence and came down for a book and found him sitting upright in the dark by the cold window, blanket around his shoulders like a man wearing his own ghost.

"The moon's wrong," he said, when he knew it was her. "In Flanders the moon came up over the water. Here it comes up over the wood. I know it's nothing. I know where I am." His voice was steady and terrible. "But some part of me is still checking. Some part of me has been checking for two years and doesn't know it's been dismissed."

Ada didn't tell him it was nothing. She dragged her chair to the window instead, sat down beside him, and looked at the moon with him, properly, until it had come up far enough over the wood to be a Kentish moon again, ordinary, indifferent, theirs.

"My father used to say homesickness isn't wanting a place," she said, after a long time. "It's your body keeping the time of another place. Like a clock that won't reset." She wrapped her hands around her mug. "You'll reset. Slowly. Badly. With bad nights. But you will."

"And you?" he asked. "Whose time does your body keep?"

She thought about it honestly, because he deserved honesty and had earned it at rates no one should have to pay. "Half a dozen places," she said at last. "None of them quite. I think that's what I am — a grafted thing. Scion wood from everywhere, heeled in sand, waiting to see what takes."

"It'll take here," Julian said. It wasn't comfort; it was pronouncement, Marlowe tone, the family voice. "I'm telling you as a man who knows about living under false names. Yours fits you. I've watched you wear it all winter."

They sat until the fire burned down. Neither of them mentioned that Christmas was coming, or the anniversary of Loos behind it, or any of the dates that lay ahead like stones under snow. There would be time to walk over those stones together. That was the whole point of together.

* * *

Christmas itself was small and loud.

Edith produced a tree from a source she refused to disclose ("There are advantages," she said mysteriously, "to knowing every blacklegged smuggler between here and the coast," and refused to elaborate) and Ivo, discovering that none of them owned decorations, spent an entire afternoon in the shed making paper chains with a concentration he usually reserved for carburettors, tongue between his teeth, glue on his knuckles, producing chains of such structural magnificence that Julian pronounced them over-engineered and Ivo threatened to walk to Dover and enlist.

"You can't," said Ada placidly, threading cranberries. "You're compromised. You lied to a War Office captain. You're ours now entirely."

"I am aware," Ivo said, with a gravity that was almost entirely a joke and entirely true underneath, and hung his magnificent chain across the ceiling beam.

They ate too much. They pulled crackers and wore paper crowns with the unselfconscious solemnity of people who had all, in one way or another, spent recent Christmases believing there might not be many more. Julian fell asleep in his chair at four o'clock, mid-sentence, crown askew, and nobody woke him; they just talked quieter around him, and Edith put a blanket over him with the tenderness of a woman handling something recovered from a wreck, salvaged, salt-stained, hers.

Afterward, in the kitchen, washing up while Edith dried — an arrangement that had become ritual without anyone announcing it — Ada said, "You should send him away somewhere warm. Switzerland. The south of France. The doctors all say—"

"The doctors all say what they said last year about men who are dead," Edith said. Her hands kept moving, plate to cloth, plate to cloth. "No. He stays where I can see him. Where we can all see him." She set a plate down with a click. "Besides. He wouldn't go. Have you noticed? He asks about the post twice a day like a boy at school. He reads the circulars cover to cover. Last Tuesday I caught him correcting my punctuation in a draft speech." She almost smiled. "Two years of silence, Ada. He's making up for it in punctuation."

"He's happy," Ada said. "Underneath all of it. He's — I don't know the word. Convalescing isn't it."

"Mending," Edith said. "It's a good old word. Mending. Things mend slower than you want and stronger than you expect." She handed Ada the last glass, and for a moment their hands stayed on it together, and Edith said, not looking at her, in the voice she used for the sentences that mattered most, the ones she never raised: "I'm glad the gate was where you chose to stand, that day in the rain. Of all the houses in England."

"So am I," Ada said. And that was the whole conversation, and it was enough.

* * *

January came in hard, with a wind straight off the marshes that made the chimneys sing and drove everyone indoors and made the house feel less like a refuge than a ship — a good image, Ada thought, a family under sail, everyone with their station, everyone hauling on their rope.

The arguments continued, as arguments should. Edith and Ivo fought about the car (he wanted to sell the Austin and buy something sensible; she regarded this as a personal insult to an old soldier). Julian and Ada fought about grafting technique with a ferocity that concealed the fact that they agreed completely. Ivo and Ada fought about nothing at all, magnificently, frequently, over card games and crossword clues and whether Beethoven was better than Bach (he was wrong, and enjoyed being wrong), fights that ended in the particular silence of two people standing very close together having forgotten what they were saying, and Edith developed the habit of leaving rooms during these silences with a tact that fooled no one.

On the coldest night of the month the pipes froze in the east wing and the household convened in the kitchen like survivors of a shipwreck, all four of them plus Mrs. Tice the cook, who had opinions about frozen pipes and expressed them at length, and they played cards by lamplight until midnight, cheating cheerfully, Julian winning spectacularly through methods he declined to explain ("two years of false passports teaches a man to shuffle"), and when Ada finally went up to bed past one o'clock, she paused on the stairs and looked back at the kitchen doorway — lamplight, laughter, the rattle of cards, Edith's head thrown back mid-laugh, Ivo arguing a point with a playing card held aloft like counsel at trial — and she stood there in the cold hall and let it hurt her in the way that happiness hurts, the way that things you thought you'd lost forever hurt when they come back.

History happened elsewhere. She'd thought that once, or something like it — that the great currents moved through other lives, other houses, and washed past people like her without stopping. Standing on the stairs of Marlowe House in the small hours of 1917, she knew better. History had walked into this house in the shape of a letter, and a deserter, and a captain with a buried scandal, and four frightened people who decided, one decision at a time, to be brave in company. History happened here. It was happening still — smaller now, gentler, happening as mending, as planting plans, as a man practising his own signature in newspaper margins.

* * *

The letter came on the last Saturday of January.

Ada saw the post first, as always — she sorted it now, that was the secretary's privilege and burden — and she was standing in the hall with the day's stack in her hand when she drew out the envelope at the bottom and went still.

Foreign stamps. Two of them, overprinted, unfamiliar. The postmark smudged but legible: *ROTTERDAM*. And the handwriting — she knew the handwriting before she knew anything else, knew it the way you know a voice through a wall, because she had spent months decoding it, that careful slanted script with the peculiar crossed sevens, the handwriting of a man who had learned to write in three countries and belonged properly to none of them.

Her father's handwriting.

Her father, interned eleven hundred miles away on the Isle of Man, then deported, vanished into the war's machinery — her father, whom she had traced through the same smugglers' channels that had carried Julian home, sending inquiries into the dark for half a year with no answer, no answer, no answer—

The envelope was addressed to her. Not to Miss E. Marlowe. To *Miss Ada Voss*, in full, correct, in the handwriting she had last seen on a luggage label in another country, in another life.

From the library she heard Julian laugh at something Ivo had said. From the kitchen came the smell of bread and the sound of Edith dictating a letter to no one yet, composing aloud as she did. The house hummed with its ordinary morning, its mended, argumentative, improbable life.

Ada sat down on the hall chair, the one by the coat stand, and held the letter in both hands, and found she was shaking.

She broke the seal.
