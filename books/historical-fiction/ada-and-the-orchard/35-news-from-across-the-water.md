## 35. News from Across the Water

The letter came on a morning so cold that the panes of Marlowe House wore ferns of frost, and Ada stood before them with her breath held, watching the postman's bicycle wobble up the drive through a mist that seemed to have risen out of the ground itself rather than fallen from any sky.

She had learned to read the postman's posture in these months. There was a particular way he slowed at their gate when he carried something official — the squared shoulders, the leather bag gripped like a shield — and another way entirely, loose and almost apologetic, when he brought nothing but circulars and bills. This morning he was slow. Squared. And there were two letters, one thin and grey as a winter reed, the other thick, soft-cornered, travel-worn, its paper gone the colour of weak tea.

The thin one bore the crest of some ministry. The thick one had crossed water.

Ada took both from him without a word — she had learned that too, that silence made men hand things over more quickly than questions did — and stood in the drive until the bicycle's bell faded down the lane, and then she did not go inside at once. She turned the foreign envelope over in her hands. The stamp was Dutch. The postmark was Rotterdam. The handwriting was neither Julian's careful scholar's script nor Edith's upright, unforgiving slant, but something looser, rounder, written by someone accustomed to writing in haste and in fear and latterly, perhaps, in neither.

She knew the name before she opened it, because she had written it herself a hundred times in her head: *van der Meer*. The smuggler's contact, the man with the boat and the fog-clever eyes who had taken Ivo, the Brandt boy, and her own desperate hope across the Channel in November. He would not write unless there was news worth the risk of writing it.

Her fingers were too cold to work the seal properly. She tore it, and hated herself for tearing it, and read standing in the frost with the mist beading in her eyelashes.

*To the lady at Marlowe House, for Miss Ada —*

*The family arrived whole on the twelfth. All four. The mother was sick on the crossing and laughed about it after, which the son says is how you know she is well. They are lodged with cousins in Delft and the boy has been enrolled at the school on the Buitenwatersloot; he sent me a drawing of our boat, which is a better likeness than I deserve. The father asks me to tell the English lady that he remembers an orchard, and a promise made under it, and that promises kept are rarer than peace and heavier to carry. He says this gratefully.*

*There is more. When your friend asked me — as I believe he asked every man between here and Harwich — whether anyone had come through my hands asking after a Czech-born printer named Novak, I told him no. That was true in September. It stopped being true in October. A man answering that description passed through Flushing on the third of October, travelling on papers issued in Amsterdam, going north. He was older than your description. He had lost most of his hair and gained a stoop, and he spoke Dutch with the accent of a man who learned it late and in bad circumstances. But he paid his passage with money earned setting type, and when the mate joked about the cold he answered in three languages, and one of them was the language your friend said he'd hear if ever he heard it.*

*I cannot swear it was your father. I can only say that a man matching him exists, alive, moving freely, and last known bound for Groningen, where there is a printing house run by exiles that takes in men of his trade. If you write to me care of the address below, I will ask further. I do not ask what he is to you. It is enough that he is someone's.*

*The winter is hard here too. The sea does not care whose war it interrupts.*

*— v.d.M.*

Ada read it twice. Then a third time, aloud, in a whisper, because saying the words seemed the only way to make them hold still long enough to be believed.

*Alive. Moving freely. Bound for Groningen.*

The mist moved around her. Somewhere behind the house a rook argued with the cold, a raw grating sound. She became aware, gradually, that her cheeks were wet, and that she could not remember beginning to cry, and that she was smiling anyway — the two together feeling less like contradiction than like weather, one system passing over another.

*It is enough that he is someone's.* Her father. Papa. Jan Novak, who had set type in London for thirty-one years, who had printed hymn sheets and trade bills and, in the end, one foolish pamphlet arguing that workingmen of all nations had more in common with each other than with their generals — a pamphlet that had cost him his shop, his liberty, and his daughter's nationality in a single stroke of bureaucratic ink. *Enemy alien.* As if thirty-one years could be unmade by two words. As if a country could take back everything it had given a man and call it justice.

They had interned him on the Isle of Man and then, in the reshuffle of 1915, shipped him — she had never learned where exactly, the letters had simply stopped — and for over a year she had carried the not-knowing like a stone sewn into her coat. Every port, every queue, every official face had been the same face: the face that looked through her, that saw a name on a list instead of a woman standing in front of it. She had learned to expect nothing from such faces. Expectation was a wound you opened yourself.

And now a stranger on a boat in Holland wrote to say that a stooped, half-bald man who spoke three languages had passed northward in October, alive, free, setting type somewhere among exiles in Groningen.

Not certainty. She would not let it be certainty. Certainty was a thing that broke. But hope — cautious hope, the kind you fed sparingly, the kind you kept in a shed like a lamp you daren't leave burning unattended — hope she could manage. Hope she had practised.

* * *

She found Edith in the morning room, at the big table where the suffrage archives lay in their ordered battalions — folders, press cuttings, the great ledger of correspondence that Ada now kept, having inherited the duty along with the desk. Edith was reading the ministry letter already; Ada had left both letters on the tray in the hall, and Mrs. Petch had done what Mrs. Petch always did, which was deliver everything upstairs with the discretion of a woman who had seen too much in this house to be surprised by envelopes.

Edith looked up, and Ada saw at once that she had read the wrong one first — or the right one first, depending — because her eyes had the flat shine they got when she was holding something very still inside herself.

"The Brandts," Edith said. "All four arrived. The boy is at school." She said it the way she said few things: simply, without architecture, without argument. Just the fact, laid down like a stone on a cairn.

"I know." Ada sat down across from her. "I've had mine too."

"The van der Meer letter." Edith pushed the ministry letter aside — a summons of some kind, a routine acknowledgment, the machinery grinding on indifferently now that it had gotten what it wanted — and reached across the table. For a moment Ada thought she meant to take her hand, and braced herself, but Edith only smoothed the Dutch envelope flat between them, as though preparing a surface for something to be built on. "Tell me."

So Ada told her. Not reading — she had the words by heart already — but telling, watching Edith's face change as she came to the part about the printer. She watched the caution come into it first, the old magistrate's caution, the *we must not build on sand* look; and then watched the sand turn slowly, against Edith's will, into ground.

"Groningen," Edith said at last. "There *is* a printing house there. De Vrije Pers — the Free Press — run by exiles. Belgian, French, some German socialists who got out before the border closed. I've had their pamphlets through the movement. Someone sent us one on conscientious objection in '16 and it was the sanest document produced anywhere in Europe that year." She paused. "If your father is setting type anywhere on this earth, Ada, it would be there."

"You know this how?"

"Because I've spent ten years learning where the honest printers went." Something moved at the corner of Edith's mouth, not quite a smile. "Governments lie fluently. Printers have to be accurate or they lose their trade. It makes them a different class of witness."

Ada laughed — a short, cracked sound, surprised out of her. "You're saying we should trust a pamphlet."

"I'm saying we should trust the trade." Edith drew a sheet of paper toward her, and then stopped, pen hovering, and looked at Ada directly — that look of hers, level and unhurried, that made you feel examined and then, strangely, approved. "But we should be careful what we ask for. You understand that. If we write to Rotterdam asking after a specific man, that letter passes through hands. Postal censorship hasn't relaxed just because the Brandts are safe. If your father is living quietly under new papers, a clumsy inquiry could undo in a week what two years of silence preserved."

"I know." Ada's voice came out harder than she intended. "I've been careful for two years, Mrs. Marlowe. Careful is the only thing I'm good at."

"That isn't true," Edith said mildly, "but I take the point." She uncapped the pen. "Then we write carefully. We don't ask for a man. We ask the Free Press — as a suffrage society making a donation, which we genuinely shall — whether they accept foreign correspondents for their bulletin. And in the covering note we mention, quite innocently, that our society has members of many nations, including, let me see—" the pen moved, "—*a compositor's daughter, who learned her letters at the case-side and misses the smell of ink.*"

Ada stared at her. "That's —"

"A sentence a censor will pass without a glance and a printer will read like a telegram." Edith finished the line and blotted it. "Julian taught me cipher. He thought I wasn't paying attention. I was always paying attention." She slid the page across. "Read it. Change anything you like. It should be yours."

Ada read it. It was perfect — bland, courteous, forgettable to anyone who hadn't spent her childhood standing on a stool beside a composing frame while her father's quick fingers walked the type, explaining *this is a lower-case e, ada, see how it sits in its box, everything has its box, everything has its place.* The smell of ink. The smell of the shop on Cloth Fair, oil and lead and the vinegar tang of the dampening water, her father's sleeves rolled, his forearms black to the elbow, singing something Czech and off-key while the press clacked its patient rhythm behind him.

"Everything has its place," she whispered.

"What?"

"Nothing. He used to say — never mind. Yes. Send it. Send it today." She pressed her palms flat on the table to stop their shaking. "And Mrs. Marlowe — thank you. I know what a letter costs now. I've seen what they cost. I won't forget that you're spending one on me."

Edith looked at her for a long moment, and whatever passed behind her eyes was old and deep and not offered up for inspection.

"My brother crossed a continent on the strength of a promise made in peacetime under an orchard," she said. "I am merely posting a letter. The proportion of courage is not comparable." She stood, gathering the ministry letter with brisk distaste, folding it into thirds. "Now. Ivo is in the yard with the Daimler, doing something unsanctioned to the carburettor, and Julian is attempting to walk to the gate and back without assistance, which Dr. Loxley has forbidden and Julian has therefore scheduled. One of us should supervise both. I nominate you. I have correspondence."

"And the summons?" Ada asked, nodding at the folded grey sheet.

"Bureaucracy acknowledging its own digestion." Edith's tone was light, but Ada caught the flicker beneath it — the tribunal was coming, the hearing that would weigh Julian's desertion against Reeve's buried scandal, and even with the leverage shifted their way, even with the archivist's evidence safe in Ivo's keeping, nothing was settled until it was settled. "Nothing for today. Go and watch your brother-in-all-but-name drop a wrench on his foot."

* * *

The yard smelled of petrol and frozen mud. Ivo was indeed elbow-deep in the Daimler's engine, sleeves pinned back, muttering in the private liturgy of drivers everywhere, and Julian stood nearby wrapped in two coats and a blanket despite insisting he needed none of them, leaning on a stick cut from the orchard — from the old orchard, from the tree under which the promise had been made, though Ada suspected Ivo had chosen the branch deliberately and Julian pretended not to know it.

"— and if you keep advancing the jet like that she'll flood the moment the temperature drops below—"

"She won't flood. She's temperamental, not stupid. Unlike certain carburettors I could mention."

"You're insulting the carburettor and complimenting yourself in the same breath," Julian observed. "That's economy."

"It's efficiency," Ivo said, without looking up. Then he saw Ada and straightened, wiping his hands on a rag that was filthier than his hands. "Postman came?"

"He came." She held up the Dutch letter. "I've already told Mrs. Marlowe. I wanted—" She hesitated. These two. She had not expected, arriving rain-soaked at their gate a lifetime ago with a dead man's letter in her pocket, to acquire a family. Families were things that happened to other people; she had put herself in that category years ago, alongside people history happened to. And yet here she was, wanting to say the thing twice, once for each of them, the way you fed a fire log by log.

Ivo took the letter from her and read it fast — he was a faster reader than anyone credited, a fact with its own long history in this household — and when he finished he didn't say anything clever at all. He just nodded slowly, twice, and handed it to Julian, and stood close while Julian read, so that the old man could steady himself against the car's wing if he needed to, which after a moment he did.

"All four," Julian said. His voice had gone rough. "Karl is at school."

"At school in Delft," Ada said. "Drawing boats."

Julian laughed — a real laugh, startled out of him, that bent him over the stick. "He drew boats when he was nine. Terrible boats. All funnel and no hull." He wiped his eyes with the back of his glove, an awkward gesture, half hiding it. "Two years I carried that family in my head like ballast. Two years wondering if I'd traded my own life for theirs and whether it was worth — and now Karl is drawing *terrible boats*, and the answer is yes, obviously it was worth it, obviously, and I shall never doubt it again out loud." He steadied. "And the rest of it? Van der Meer writes more than one page. What else?"

So she told them that too. About the stooped man with three languages. About Groningen, northbound, October.

The yard went very quiet. Even the rook had stopped. Ivo was looking at her with an expression she had learned to read in recent months — the expression of a man recalculating, again, the borders of what was possible.

"Your father," he said.

"*Possibly* my father." She heard her own caution and hated how it sounded, and loved it too, because it was armor she had forged herself. "Van der Meer can't swear it. I can't swear it. But Papa went north when they moved the interned men — we lost the trail at Hull — and Hull is north, and ships go north from Hull, and men who print pamphlets governments hate go where presses will have them." She swallowed. "It fits. It fits the way truth fits, loosely, with gaps."

"With gaps," Ivo agreed softly. "Everything true has gaps. It's the lies that come seamless." He glanced toward the house, then back. "Mrs. Marlowe's letter — the one about the compositor's daughter?"

"You've seen it?"

"I read everything," he said, and for once there was no shame in it, only the plain fact of himself, the man made of other people's words who had finally been granted his own. "It'll pass. Censors skim the second paragraph of anything with 'society' in the heading. And if it reaches the right eyes—" He stopped. Recalculated. "When it reaches the right eyes, a compositor in Groningen will know exactly who is asking."

"Not asking," Ada corrected. "*Missing.* There's a difference. Asking demands. Missing just — stands at a window." She looked away, toward the bare orchard rows, the black trees stitched against the white sky. "He used to stand at the shop window when it rained and watch people run for the bus, and he'd say, *they think the rain is an interruption. The rain is the day, ada. The bus is the interruption.*" Her throat tightened. "I want to tell him he was right. I want to tell him I finally understand it."

Julian put his free hand on her shoulder — lightly, the touch of a man still relearning his own weight. "You will," he said. "Men who set type survive. It's the nature of the trade. You spend your life putting other people's words in order; you develop a certain stubbornness about endings." He smiled, tired and crooked. "Look who's talking."

* * *

The answer, when it came, came faster than any of them dared expect — eleven days, carried in the same soft tea-coloured envelopes, in the same loose hand — and it came on the last afternoon of the year, while the whole house was already half-dressed for the vigil Edith insisted on keeping every New Year's Eve, candles and quiet and the old orchard walked once in the dark.

Ada opened it alone in her room, because some doors you knock on before you open them and some you must open alone first.

*Miss Ada —*

*My cousin in Groningen is not a romantic, which is why I trust him. He says: the Free Press employs a compositor called Herr Braun, engaged autumn 1916, references Amsterdam, age approximately fifty-five, speaks German and Dutch and one other language he declines to name, sets a clean line and sings badly. He keeps a photograph above his bench that he will not discuss. My cousin describes it thus: a girl of perhaps twelve, standing on a stool at a composing frame, squinting.*

*He was twelve when the photograph was taken. I am twenty-three. But I stood on that stool, and I squinted, and I was wearing the blue pinafore Mama made me, and there is no one else on God's earth it could be.*

*Herr Braun, my cousin adds, receives no mail and sends none, and asked no questions when a letter arrived addressed simply to "the compositor who knows why the rain is not an interruption." He read it four times. Then he went to the window and stood there a long while. Then he wrote — my cousin took this down verbatim, being a man who understands documents —*

*"Tell her the case is still set. Tell her I kept the forme of her name in type, locked in the chase, these five years, and I will not distribute it until she comes to lift it out herself. Everything has its place. Everything waits. Tell her: the rain is the day."*

*There is no more to report. Whether passage can be arranged, and under whose flag, and when — those are questions above my station, and below your patience, and I leave them between you and whoever in that house arranges such things. From what I saw of your driver, he arranges most things.*

*— v.d.M.*

Ada sat on the edge of her bed for a long time with the letter open in her lap and the December light failing slowly across the floorboards.

*A girl of perhaps twelve, standing on a stool at a composing frame.*

He had kept her photograph above his bench through two countries, three names, and a war. He had kept her name locked in type — *locked in the chase* — the metal words of her, Ada, Ada, Ada, set and tied and waiting, undistributed, unbroken back into the common stock, held apart from all the other letters the way you hold a thing sacred: by refusing to melt it down into usefulness.

*Until she comes to lift it out herself.*

Downstairs, faintly, she could hear the household assembling — Mrs. Petch's footsteps, Edith's voice giving some instruction about candles, Ivo laughing at something Julian said, that low easy laugh that had become, without her marking the moment it happened, one of the sounds her happiness lived in. Through the window the first stars were out over the black orchard, and beyond it the lanes and the downs and the coast and the cold bright water, and beyond the water, in a city of canals and exiles, a stooped man with a locked chase and a photograph of a girl in blue stood — she was sure of it, sure the way you are sure of gravity — at his window, watching the same stars, missing the same missing.

She rose. She put the letter in the wooden box where she kept everything that mattered — Julian's first letter, the signal-box photograph, the scrap of Brandt embroidery Karl's mother had pressed into her hand at the dock — and she went downstairs to the candlelight and the voices, carrying the news like a coal cupped in both hands.

At the foot of the stairs she stopped. Tomorrow the year would turn, and behind the turning year stood the tribunal, and the verdict, and whatever imperfect shape justice would take; but tonight there was this: a full house, a locked chase waiting in Groningen, a name in type that would not be melted down.

"Ada?" Ivo appeared in the hall doorway, candle in hand, lighting her more than the stairs. "You're crying."

"I'm not," she said, and found that she was, and laughed. "Come outside. Everyone. Bring the candles. I have something to tell the orchard."
