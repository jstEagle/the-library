## 36. The Century Turns

The last day of 1916 arrived the way so many of our days had come that winter — grey, low-ceilinged, smelling of woodsmoke and wet gravel — and it seemed to me, as I carried in the morning post, that the whole year had been holding its breath and was only now beginning to let it out.

I had been at Marlowe House eleven months by then. Long enough that Mrs. Tice no longer asked whether I'd take breakfast but simply set my place; long enough that the Daimler knew the lane to Wye without my thinking about it, and my hands turned the wheel the way a man's hand finds his own door-handle in the dark. And long enough that when I came through the back door with frost still on my collar, Ada looked up from the kitchen table where she was sorting seed catalogues and said, "You're late," in exactly the tone her father might have used, and I felt — as I had been feeling for some weeks now, in a way I did not examine too closely — that being late to her was a thing worth being.

"Roads are glass," I said. "The hill above Charing's like skating."

"Then you'll drive carefully tomorrow." She said it lightly. Tomorrow. The year turning over. There was to be a vigil — Edith had announced it three days earlier in the voice she used for decisions already made: *We will keep watch until midnight. All of us. In the drawing room. If this country can spend a night making war, we can spend one night making something else.*

Nobody had argued. Nobody ever argued with Edith when she used that voice, though Julian had begun to learn how, in his slow convalescent way, and their arguments now were a music the house had almost forgotten how to hold — two voices circling each other, sharp and fond at once.

Julian was in the conservatory that morning. He spent his mornings there now, wrapped in a blanket like an old Indian civilian, doing nothing more strenuous than watching the frost burn off the glass. His sentence had bent him before it released him; he walked with a stick and a slight list to starboard, as though part of him were still leaning into a wind only he could feel. But his eyes had come back from wherever they had gone during those two years under borrowed names. That was the thing I noticed most, watching him from the doorway with the post still under my arm: a man's eyes coming home.

"Ivo." He didn't turn. "Come here a moment."

I went. The conservatory smelled of cold earth and geraniums surviving on principle. On the table beside him lay a small tin box — tobacco-tin size, dented at one corner — and on top of it, squared neatly, a little stack of papers.

"What have you there?" I asked, though I suspected, because I had learned by then that everything in that house eventually told me what it was if I waited.

"My names." He touched the stack without picking it up. "Karl Weissman. Anton Verhoeven. A third one I won't trouble you with. The men I had to be while the man I was couldn't be found." He looked up at me then, and there was something in his face I couldn't read — not guilt exactly; more like a man standing at the edge of a cleared field, looking back at the forest he'd walked out of. "Edith says I ought to keep them. For evidence, she says. For the record."

"And you don't mean to."

"No," Julian said. "I mean to burn them."

* * *

The day went about its business the way days do when everyone in a house is pretending the evening doesn't matter more than usual. I made two runs — parcels to Ashford, a committee woman home from Canterbury who talked the whole way about coal prices as though coal prices could save anyone. The light began going early, as it does at that hinge of the year, and by half past three the lanes were blue with dusk and every window in every cottage held its lamp behind net curtains, small yellow squares stitched onto the dark.

I thought, driving back, about the letters. It was impossible not to think of them, these days. Since the tribunal — since I had stood up in that close green room with its smell of polish and fear and read aloud, honestly, at last, the words I'd once stolen in private — my whole strange apprenticeship with other people's mail had been turned inside out and shown to the light. I had confessed it to Edith in the spring, expecting ruin, and found instead that she had known all along, had let me go on reading, trusting me with her secrets before I'd earned them. I still did not entirely know what to do with that knowledge. Some gifts are too heavy to carry gracefully; you just learn to walk with the weight.

The post that evening was thin — a circular from the Federation, two bills, a Christmas card from someone in Tunbridge Wells whose handwriting I did not know, which felt faintly scandalous after everything. I set it on the hall table, squared the edges out of habit, and stood a moment in the quiet of the house, listening to it breathe. Fires ticking. Ada humming in the kitchen, something with a German lilt to it that she probably didn't know she was humming — her father's songs, I'd guessed once, and never said aloud, because some things you learn are not yours to repeat.

At seven we gathered.

* * *

Edith had transformed the drawing room in her particular way — not with festivity, for festivity would have been a lie in that winter of the Somme's aftermath, but with intention. Candles instead of lamps. The good chairs drawn into a circle. On the sideboard, a cold supper laid out as though for travellers: bread, cheese, apples from the old orchard, a bottle of the blackcurrant cordial Mrs. Tice made, and one bottle of claret that had survived the war by being too humble to confiscate.

"We're not celebrating," Edith said, when Julian raised an eyebrow at the claret. "We're keeping watch. Even watchmen eat."

Ada laughed — that quick, surprised laugh of hers that always seemed to escape before she'd decided to allow it — and the circle settled: Edith in her high-backed chair, upright even at rest; Julian opposite her, stick hooked on the chair arm; Ada curled sideways on the window seat with her feet tucked under her, a posture Edith had given up correcting around Easter; Mrs. Tice, invited against all precedent, perched on the edge of a straight chair like a bird that might yet fly off, though she stayed. And me. I took the chair nearest the fire because nobody assigned it to me and because I had learned, this year, that a household will assign you your place if you wait quietly enough.

The clock on the mantel ticked. Outside, the dark pressed against the windows, and somewhere down in the valley a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

"Five hours," Ada said. "What do people do at vigils? Pray?"

"Some pray," Edith said. "Some remember. Some plan." She smiled — that dry, quick smile that was her armour and her honesty in one. "We shall do all three, I expect, whether we intend to or not."

So we remembered. That was the shape the first hours took: talk that circled and returned, the way talk does among people who have been through something together. Julian spoke of Holland — carefully, in the way he spoke of the years abroad, giving us the weather of it and the safe houses and never quite the fear. Ada told us about the canal at Delft in winter, how the ice rang like a bell when the boats broke through, and her face did something complicated when she said *Delft*, because her father was alive in Rotterdam now, traced through the same channels that had saved the Brandts, and she had not yet seen him, and would not for months, and hope that has to wait is a hard thing to hold gently.

"He writes that the herring is good," she said. "That's all. Herring, and the weather, and — *be patient, mijn kind.* As if patience were a thing you could decide to have, like buying boots."

"It is, rather," said Edith.

"You've never had to be patient about anything in your life."

"I have been patient about England," Edith said, "for forty-nine years."

And Mrs. Tice, who almost never spoke in company, said into her cordial: "Patience is what you've got left when you've used up everything else," and we all looked at her, and she stared back defiantly, and Ada reached over and squeezed her wrist, and the old woman pretended not to notice while visibly glowing.

The clock crept toward ten. The candles burned down and were replaced. Somewhere in those hours the conversation went quiet, the way water goes still, and we sat in the circle not talking at all, each of us alone with the arithmetic of the year. Fourteen million dead across Europe — they printed the figures now in the papers as casually as grain prices, and the figures had stopped meaning anything, had become wallpaper behind the actual griefs. I thought of Mr. Ellery, the deserter we'd moved inland a lifetime ago last spring, who had mistaken me for his brother in his fever and told me what broke him at the front; I thought of Herr Brandt's tired face over tea in silence; of Reeve, retired into disgrace, whom I sometimes saw in nightmares wearing his immaculate gloves. I thought of the boy Brandt, crossing in the fog with Ada's hand on his shoulder, and how the patrol boat's lantern had swept over us and passed on, and how I had understood in that moment — really understood, for the first time — that history was not happening elsewhere.

That had been my creed, once. My comfortable catechism. History happened to statesmen and generals, to people in newspapers, to other people. I was a driver; I carried words; I watched. Eleven months ago I had believed it the way you believe the floor will hold you.

The floor had held. But it had also opened.

* * *

At quarter past eleven, Julian got up.

He crossed to the fire slowly, stick tapping, and fetched the tin box from the conservatory — he must have set it ready on the hall table earlier, for it appeared in his hands without explanation, the way important things had begun appearing in that house. He set it on the low table in the middle of our circle and stood over it.

"I promised myself," he said, "that if I ever got home, I'd do this. It seems a foolish promise now. Papers are only papers." He looked round at us, and his gaze rested longest on Edith. "But a name is not only a name. Every one of these, I wore like a coat. And in each coat there was a man who did things the real Julian Underhill couldn't have done — things I'm not proud of, and a few I am, and none of them mine to keep. Karl Weissman kept his head down in Antwerp while people were arrested. Anton Verhoeven lied beautifully in French and Dutch and once in Flemish, which nearly got him killed. They served their purpose. They kept me alive to come back to—" his voice caught, briefly, on nothing visible— "to this. To you. And I don't want them in the record. Let the record have the truth, ugly as it is. These can go."

Edith said, "Julian—" and stopped herself. I watched her hands close on the arms of her chair. The archivist in her — the woman who had built her life on documents, on testimony, on the patient preservation of what actually happened — wanted those papers the way a drowning woman wants a plank. I saw the wanting. I also saw her master it.

"Burn them," she said finally. "But tell us their names first. Say them aloud. Then they'll be in a record that matters — ours."

Julian looked at her for a long moment. Whatever passed between the two of them in that look, I was not meant to read all of it, and I didn't try. He nodded once.

"Karl Weissman," he said. "Anton Verhoeven." A pause. "And Thomas Ackerley. The third one. He was the worst of them — he was the one who enjoyed it."

He knelt at the hearth — kneeling cost him something, and Ada half-rose, and he waved her back — and fed the papers into the flames one at a time. The fire took each sheet with a small greedy rush. I watched Karl Weissman curl and blacken. I watched the ink lift off Anton Verhoeven's careful signatures and vanish upward in a breath of smoke that smelt of iron and vanishing. Thomas Ackerley burned last and fastest, as though the fire knew.

Nobody spoke. The clock said twenty to twelve. Through the window, beyond the garden, the orchard stood black against a sky clearing from the west — the clouds tearing open, stars coming out hard and cold, the kind of sky that promises frost by morning.

Ada got up and put another log on, and sparks went up the chimney like a reversal of falling snow, and she said, "There. Now the new year starts clean," in the practical voice she used to keep herself from saying anything else.

* * *

The last hour I barely remember as time. It was more like a held note. We refilled glasses nobody drank from. Mrs. Tice fell asleep sitting up and woke herself with dignity twice. Edith and Julian talked low in the corner — about spring, I think, and the planting, and the trees Ada wanted to teach him to graft — and their voices had the rhythm of two people rebuilding a bridge they'd both assumed destroyed. Ada sat beside me on the window seat, our shoulders not quite touching, and we watched the fire together, and neither of us said anything, and it was the best conversation I'd had all year.

Somewhere in that silence I made my resolution.

I want to set it down exactly, because I never said it aloud and because a thing unsaid needs writing somewhere, even if only here, even if only in the privacy of a page no one may ever see. I resolved this: that I would stop waiting. All my life I had waited — waited to be noticed, waited for history to finish with other people, waited outside doors in cars with the engine running. I had read other people's words because I thought my own weren't worth the paper. Well. I had testified. I had driven through fog and checkpoints and my own fear. I had tea with an enemy and found a neighbour. The floor had held.

So: this year I would write. Not carry — write. Letters of my own. Perhaps more than letters. I did not yet know the shape of it; I only knew the direction, the way you know at dawn which way east is. And I knew one letter in particular that needed writing, and to whom, and roughly what it must say, and roughly how badly I would say it, and that I would say it anyway.

The clock gathered itself. Ten strokes. Eleven.

Edith rose, and we rose with her, and she stood with her back to the fire like a general on a hill she intended to hold. "Whatever comes," she said — and her voice was steady but her eyes went round the circle, touching each of us, "whatever this year brings, we know how to keep watch now. That's worth more than any of us knew a twelvemonth ago."

Twelve.

The clock struck, deep and unhurried, and outside — faintly, from the direction of the village — a single church bell answered, then another further off, the sound passing along the valley like a rumour. Ada grabbed my sleeve. Julian put his hand on Edith's shoulder and she covered it with her own. Mrs. Tice cried a little and pretended it was the smoke. The year 1916 died without ceremony, and 1917 came in on frost and candlelight, carrying its unknown weight.

"Happy New Year," Ada said to me, close, under the bells, her hand still on my sleeve.

"Happy New Year," I said. And inside, silent, where resolutions live: *This year I write.*

It was Julian, later — much later, when the candles had guttered and Mrs. Tice had gone up to bed and the rest of us stood reluctant in the hall, unwilling to end the night — who changed the shape of everything, as he had a gift for doing. He had gone out to the porch for air and come back in holding an envelope that had lain on the mat since evening, unnoticed under the festive clutter, snow-damp at one corner.

"This came for you, Edith," he said slowly. "No stamp. Hand-delivered." He turned it over. "Typed address. No sender."

Edith dried her hands and took it, and slit it with the letter-knife, and read it standing there in the hall with the dead year still warm behind us. I watched her face go through three expressions in as many seconds — puzzlement, recognition, and something fierce and settling, like a woman deciding to pick up a weight she'd been resting from.

"Well?" Ada asked.

Edith folded the letter along its existing creases, very precisely. "It appears," she said, "that someone thinks our story ought to be told. And has offered to help me tell it — anonymously, under changed names, to the whole country." She looked up, and the firelight caught the beginning of a smile. "Someone, it seems, has been reading my mail."

She looked directly at me when she said it.
