## 38. Ivo's Letter

The stationery had been in the drawer of the writing desk in Marlowe House's back parlour for as long as Ivo could remember — a full box of it, cream-coloured, edged with a thin line of blue that Edith had probably ordered before the war and never touched since, because everything worth saying since then had been said on whatever paper came to hand. He took the box out on a Tuesday evening in February, set it on the desk, and looked at it for a long while without opening it.

The fire was small. The wind had come round to the east again, finding every gap in the old sash windows, and somewhere upstairs Julian was coughing — not badly, not the wet terrible cough of the winter's worst weeks, but enough that Ivo paused each time to listen, counting the intervals between fits the way he'd once counted the seconds at level crossings. Ada had gone up an hour ago with a tray: beef tea, bread and butter cut thin, the little jar of quince preserves Mrs. Petch made from the last of the orchard's fruit before the frost got into it. He'd heard her footsteps cross above him, heard the low murmur of her voice through the floorboards, heard Julian answer — two voices, easy, unhurried, the sound of people who were no longer afraid in their own house.

And Ivo sat alone with a box of cream-coloured paper and understood that he was going to have to do the hardest thing he had ever done, which was not driving through fog or lying to a captain of the War Office or testifying before a tribunal with his hands shaking under the table. It was this. Writing a letter. Writing *his own* letter, in his own hand, about his own heart, when he had spent thirty-one years perfecting the art of being nobody's business.

He opened the box. The paper smelled faintly of lavender — Edith kept sprigs of it in every drawer against the moths — and he drew out a single sheet and laid it flat, and the sound it made against the blotter was so quiet it might have been nothing at all.

*Dear Ada.*

He wrote the two words and stopped.

Outside, down in the kitchen garden, the ground was still iron-hard, but the light was changing — he'd noticed it that morning, driving the Daimler back from Ashford with a consignment of pamphlets wrapped in brown paper on the rear seat, noticing how the shadows lay differently across the fields, longer in one direction, thinner in another, the way they did when the year turned over in its sleep without telling anyone. February was lying to March already. Soon there would be mud instead of frost, and then green, and then — if Edith had her way, and she always did, eventually — spades and young trees and Ada kneeling in the cold earth behind the house with her sleeves rolled up, teaching a convalescent soldier how to graft scions onto rootstock as though she'd learned it in some orchard heaven rather than in a German town from a landlady who became a second mother.

He dipped the pen. He crossed out *Dear Ada*. He wrote *Ada* without the *dear*, because the *dear* felt like a door he wasn't sure he had the right to open, then stared at the word *Ada* standing alone at the top of the page like a man on a platform, and put the *dear* back, and then crossed out both and started the sheet over, which meant he had now wasted two sheets of paper belonging to Edith Marlowe, and she would know it, because she knew everything that happened in that house, including — and here his ears went hot even sitting alone — including the fact that for six years he had read her post.

That was the thing about this household. You could hide nothing in it. Not because anyone pried, but because the rooms were too close together and the silences too well-mannered, and because Edith Marlowe had spent a lifetime reading what people didn't say, and Ada had grown up in a house where being believed was never guaranteed and so had learned to notice everything, and Julian — Julian simply had a soldier's eye for what was out of place. Ivo had lived among them five months now as something more than a driver, and every week he discovered another layer of himself peeled back and examined and, astonishingly, found acceptable. They had accepted the letters-reading. They had accepted the lying to Reeve. They had accepted him at the tribunal, standing up in his best suit with his collar too tight, reading aloud from a smuggler's cipher until the room went silent — *his* voice, doing the work, the words he'd carried for other people all his life finally carrying something of his own.

But this was different. This was not testimony. There was no tribunal that compelled it. Nobody had asked.

He tried again.

*Dear Ada — I am writing this by the fire in the back parlour on Tuesday night, and I have thrown away three sheets already, which you must never tell Mrs. Petch or she will lecture me about waste, and rightly.*

Better. That was almost a joke, and jokes were safe ground; he and Ada had built whole conversations out of them, a kind of scaffolding they both pretended was the building itself. He kept going, the pen scratching, the fire ticking.

*You will wonder why I am writing at all, since we sit twenty feet apart at breakfast and you have heard my voice more these five months than any living person except perhaps the horse. But there are things I cannot say out loud in that kitchen with Julian's door open and Edith's ears — which miss nothing — tuned to the kettle, and I have decided that after a lifetime of carrying other people's words I ought to try making some of my own, even if they come out crooked.*

*Here is the crooked truth, then.*

He stopped. The wind pushed at the window. Upstairs, silence now — good silence, sleeping silence.

Here is the crooked truth. And what was the crooked truth? That he loved her? He'd known that since Folkestone, probably, or since the fog-bound drive to the boat, her hand on his shoulder from the back seat, gripping harder at each checkpoint, neither of them speaking because speaking would have broken something fragile as ice on a puddle. Or maybe since Hythe, since the inn with its smell of tar and spilled beer, when she'd stood up to the innkeeper half her size and twice her fear and he'd watched her do it and felt something rearrange itself permanently in his chest. Or maybe — be honest, Underhill, you've finally learned honesty is cheaper than lying — maybe since the first morning, the rain-soaked stranger at the gate with a dead man's letter inside her coat, looking up at him with eyes that said *I know you read the post, and I don't care*, and him feeling seen for the first time in his life and hating it and wanting more of it forever.

Whenever it started hardly mattered. What mattered was that it had survived the quarrel, the disappearance, the port, the interrogation room, the tribunal, the winter. It had survived Julian coming home. That was the part that frightened him — that it hadn't needed the crisis to keep burning. Crises were easy. Crises gave you something to do with your hands. It was the ordinary mornings that tested a thing like this: passing the marmalade, arguing about whether the Daimler needed new plugs, watching her laugh at something Julian said and knowing that the laughing belonged to the house and not to him and being glad of it anyway.

He wrote on.

*I have watched you this winter learn our roads the way you learn everything — once, thoroughly, and then as though you had always known them. You know where the lane floods below the church and where the signpost lies about distance. You argue with Edith about commas. You have taught a decorated-invalid soldier to graft apple wood without letting him once feel clumsy about it, which is a kindness more people should practise. You fit, Ada. That is the plain word for it. You fit this house and these people and this grey corner of Kent as if the place had been waiting for you, and I find I cannot bear the thought of spring arriving and your trunk being packed.*

*Because it will be, won't it. Once the trees are in. Once the Brandts' letter has been answered with good news and your father's passage is arranged and every promise kept that can be kept — there will be nothing holding you here but us, and I know better than most that *us* is not a thing a woman like you owes anybody. You came here carrying a dead man's letter and a live man's hope, and you paid for both in terror, and the world does not owe you lodging for that. But I am asking anyway. Not for lodging. For more than lodging.*

His hand shook. He set the pen down and pressed his palm flat on the desk until the trembling ran out of it, the way he calmed the gearstick on a steep hill. Through the window he could see the bare black lines of the old orchard against the sky, the trees Edith's father had planted, most of them older than everyone now living in the house except perhaps the house itself. In a few weeks, weather permitting, there would be new holes dug along the south wall, and young trees with their roots bound in sacking, and Ada on her knees in the earth showing Julian how to make the cuts. One tree for each person lost, saved, forgiven — that had been her plan, announced at breakfast in December with such matter-of-fact certainty that Edith had simply nodded and begun a list. There were eleven names on the list now. Eleven trees. He had privately resolved to dig every hole himself, so that no one else's back should suffer, and had told no one, and understood even as he kept the resolution secret that keeping secrets in this house had become a habit he could no longer afford.

So: tell her. Tell her in ink, where it couldn't be taken back, couldn't be softened by a change of subject or the arrival of the post.

*What I am asking, badly, on Edith's good paper, is whether you will stay. Not as secretary — Edith would keep you employed till Doomsday and you know it. Not as lodger, not as Julian's rescuer or the Brandts' advocate or the girl who saved us all, though you are every one of those. As yourself, on the ordinary days, with no crisis to justify your presence. I am asking because I love you, Ada, and I have loved you since roughly the moment you lied to me about your name at the gate, which I think tells you something about my judgement, but there it is. I am thirty-one years old and I have driven ten thousand miles of other people's errands and I am done pretending I have no destination of my own.*

*You need not answer tonight. You need not answer kindly. Only answer.*

*I. U.*

He read it through four times. Each time, some sentence made him wince — *there it is*, God, who talked like that — and each time he reached for the pen to strike it and stopped, because underneath the clumsiness it was true, all of it, and he had learned this winter that true things delivered crooked beat false things delivered smooth. Reeve had been smooth. Reeve had been polished and plausible and rotten through. Let his own letter be a cart with a squeaking wheel. It would still arrive.

He folded it. Then he sat with it folded in his hands for the better part of an hour, the fire burning down to a red lacework, the clock in the hall striking eleven and then midnight, and did not move. Because there remained the final problem, the one no amount of drafting solved: delivery. He could not hand it to her at breakfast — his hands would shake, she would read his face before she read the page, and everything would happen too fast, in front of marmalade and Julian's raised eyebrows and Edith's terrible gentle omniscience. He could not leave it on her pillow; that was a liberty. He could not post it through the village box like a coward — or could he? Was posting it cowardice or mercy? A day's delay. Time for her to read it alone, in her own order, without an audience. Time for him to sweat. He thought of all the letters he'd ever carried, sealed and unsealed, urgent and idle, and how the envelope itself was a kind of kindness — it let the reader meet the words on their own terms, in private, where truth could be taken in slowly instead of all at once like cold water.

Yes. He would post it. Tomorrow, in Ashford, with his own hand, with a stamp bought with his own money. His first posted letter in thirty-one years of posting everyone else's.

He slept badly and woke early, and the morning was one of those hard bright February mornings that made the whole county look scrubbed, frost silvering the hedgerows, smoke rising straight from the cottages along the Ashford road. He drove in with the rear seat empty for once — no pamphlets, no passengers, just himself and a letter in his breast pocket that weighed, he was convinced, several pounds. At the post office he bought the stamp, licked it, pressed it down with his thumb, and held the envelope over the slot for what the postmistress later described, to great local entertainment, as "a good minute," before letting go.

The sound it made falling in was very small. It sounded like a door opening.

* * *

The next two days were the longest of his life, and he had once spent nine hours in a fog-bound queue outside Dover. He drove. He stacked firewood. He sat through a committee meeting at which Edith and three other women planned the spring rallies with a precision that would have shamed the General Staff, and answered "yes, ma'am" to questions he did not hear, and caught Ada watching him once across the table with a slight crease between her brows — concern, or suspicion; with her the two were neighbours — and smiled in a way he hoped read as *nothing wrong, only tired*, and knew perfectly well it read as *something wrong, hiding it badly*.

On the third evening, coming in from the garage after dark with his cap still on, he found her waiting in the back parlour. Alone. The fire lit. On the desk beside the lavender-scented stationery box lay a single sheet of paper, folded once, his own handwriting visible on the outside where she'd turned the address away from herself out of some scruple he found unbearably moving.

She was standing with her hands clasped in front of her, the way she stood when she was frightened and refusing to show it, and he knew that stance from ports and checkpoints and interrogation-room doors, and his heart went into free fall.

"You posted it," she said. Not a question. "Through the actual post. Three days ago."

"The stamp cost a penny," he said. "It seemed the least I could do."

"Ivo." She unfolded the paper — his letter, returned to him unread, or so he thought for one vertiginous second until she said, "I read it four times," and the floor steadied beneath him. "I read it in my room the morning it came, and I read it again at lunch, and again after, and I have been trying for three days to decide whether you are the bravest man in Kent or the biggest fool, and I have concluded—" She stopped. Started again. He watched her do it, watch her, the woman who had lied to captains and faced down innkeepers, fumble for words, and he thought: *so it costs her too. Good. Let it cost us both.* "I concluded that you left one thing out."

"Several things, likely. My spelling—"

"You left out," she said, stepping closer, close enough that he could smell the cold air still on her coat, woodsmoke and frost and the ghost of quince preserves, "whether you asked me to stay *because* of what we went through, or in spite of it. Because if it's because of it — if I'm a habit the winter left behind — then I'd rather go, Ivo, I'd rather go tomorrow, I have been someone's consequence before and I will not be it again."

"Ada." His voice came out lower than he intended. "I asked you before the tribunal. Before Reeve. I'd have asked you in October, in the fog on the Dover road, if either of us had dared open our mouths. You are not what happened to me. You're what I chose after it stopped happening."

The silence that followed was long enough for the fire to settle, for a log to shift and throw up a small fountain of sparks. Then she reached into her pocket and brought out a sheet of her own — cream-coloured, blue-edged, stolen from the same box — and handed it to him without ceremony, the way she did everything that mattered.

"Then answer's yes," she said. "But I'm not having you say I never put anything in writing. Read it properly. I worked on it two nights, and if you tell a soul I used Edith's paper I'll deny it to the tribunal."

He opened it with hands that were not steady. Her writing was quick and slanted, foreign-taught, and it began without any greeting at all, straight into the middle of things, the way she entered every room:

*Ivo — Yes. With conditions, because you know I deal in conditions. One: I keep my own money and my own name. Two: we plant the trees first, all eleven, and you dig the holes and I choose the varieties, and Julian learns grafting whether he likes it or not. Three: you never again carry a letter for me. Whatever you have to say, say it yourself, out loud, however crooked. I have had enough of messages. Four—*

But condition four he never finished reading that night, because she took the page gently out of his hands and set it face-down on the desk, and showed him instead.

* * *

Later — much later, the fire burned to embers, the house asleep around them — he lay listening to the east wind ease off toward the south, and thought about envelopes. All those years he had believed history was a thing sealed and addressed to others, delivered through his hands to people who mattered, and he had been the road and not the journey. Now the drawer of Edith's desk held two letters that were nobody's business but theirs, and downstairs in the hall hung Ada's list of eleven names, and in three weeks' time, weather permitting, there would be spades in the frozen ground behind Marlowe House and the smell of turned earth and sap, and Julian standing in the cold with his coat over his pyjamas insisting he was well enough to plant, and Edith supervising holes as though inspecting troops, and Ada on her knees teaching them all how a wound in a tree could be grafted shut and grow over and bear fruit.

He had written his letter. She had answered. And the last thought he had, drifting off with the embers ticking, was that condition four had better be a reasonable one, because something in her voice as she'd pocketed the page suggested she'd saved the best clause for last — and that come morning, with frost on the orchard grass and the whole household due in the field with spades, he was going to find out exactly what she intended to plant in him.
