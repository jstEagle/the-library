## 39. Spring Planting

The ground behind Marlowe House had been a rubbish heap for as long as anyone could remember — broken crockery and cinders, the skeleton of a cold frame, rusted bed-springs that had been there so long the nettles had grown through them and made a kind of green upholstery of the whole disgraceful arrangement. Ada had looked at it that first week, when she was still a guest and did not yet know she was becoming something else, and thought: *this is what happens to things no one claims.* She had been an unclaimed thing herself for most of her life. She understood the nettles.

Now it was March, and the ground had been turned twice, and Ivo had hauled out the bed-springs with such theatrical disgust — holding them at arm's length like a serpent he'd been asked to kiss — that Edith had laughed until she had to sit down on the upturned wheelbarrow, and Julian, watching from the step with his stick under his arm and his convalescent thinness wrapped in one of his old sweaters, had said, "You've found your vocation at last, Underhill. Salvage."

"I prefer *rescue*," Ivo said. "It sounds less like work."

Ada knelt in the cold soil with her sleeves pushed back and her hands black to the wrist, and felt — not happiness exactly, happiness was too loud a word for it — but something like the moment after a long illness when you notice, without ceremony, that you are hungry.

* * *

They had agreed on the arithmetic of it in February, at the kitchen table, with the rain coming down like a decision already made.

"One tree for each," Edith had said, tapping the table with her forefinger. "That's the whole of it. Not a memorial garden — I won't have yew trees and urns, we are not a cemetery. An orchard. Things that bear fruit. You plant a memorial tree and you look at it; you plant a fruit tree and you wait for it, year after year, and every autumn it asks something of you. That's better. Grief ought to ask something of you."

"Lost, saved, or forgiven," Julian said. He said it lightly, but Ada had learned by then to hear the weight he hid inside lightness, the way he had hidden himself for two years inside another man's name. "Which is the largest category, do you think?"

"Forgiven," Edith said, without looking at him. "By a distance."

He took it. He was learning to take things from her — the rebukes along with the soup — because taking them was the only penance she would accept. The tribunal had taken two years of his life and given him back a medical discharge and a reduced sentence served mostly in a hospital ward; what it could not take or give was his sister's forgiveness, which arrived, as all her gifts arrived, disguised as practical instruction: *you will chop the onions this way; you will write to the Brandts on Sundays; you will be at this house when you are well enough to be anywhere.*

The list, when they finally wrote it out, ran to fourteen names.

Fourteen trees. There had been some argument about species. Ivo lobbied hard for apples on the grounds that Kent was apple country and a Kentish orchard of anything else was a sort of treason. Edith wanted plums — *Victoria plums, because sentimentality is only unbearable when you pretend not to feel it.* Ada, who had grown up among other people's gardens in three countries and belonged to none of them, asked for almonds, and was told by everyone at once that almonds could not possibly survive in Kent, and planted the almond pits anyway, in a corner, secretly, the way she did everything that mattered.

Julian asked for quinces, because their mother had made quince paste and cut it into diamonds, and none of them had tasted it since before the war, and no one said anything after he said it, and the silence was its own agreement.

* * *

Ada taught grafting on a Thursday morning, in the potting shed, with the door open to let out the smell of the paraffin heater.

"It is very simple," she said, "and it never feels simple. You take the scion from the tree you want — this is the future, this piece here — and you cut it so the living green layer matches the living green layer of the rootstock underneath. Cambium to cambium. Then you bind them together and leave them alone, and if you have done it honestly, they grow into each other and forget they were ever separate."

"And if you haven't done it honestly?" Ivo asked. He had appointed himself her student with an eagerness he tried to disguise as irony, and failed, because his hands gave him away — they were careful hands now, she had noticed, hands that had learned what it cost to handle other people's words carelessly.

"Then it dies," she said. "Quietly. In June usually. It looks fine for weeks and then the leaves go brown at the edges and there's nothing to be done." She held up the knife. "So you learn to make one good cut instead of four hopeful ones. That's the lesson. One honest cut."

Julian sat on an upturned crate with his coat still on, though the shed was warm. He watched her hands with the particular attention of a man relearning how to watch anything without flinching. His own hands were steadier than they had been in January, but they still had a tremor at rest, the legacy of whatever those two years abroad had done to him — he had never told them all of it, and Ada suspected he never would, and had decided this was his right. A person who had lived under false names for two years earned the right to keep one true thing private.

"You learned this abroad," he said. It wasn't quite a question.

"In Holland, the first time. My father's landlord kept cherries — sour ones, for kirsch — and I was underfoot all summer so he put a knife in my hand to keep me quiet." She fitted the scion into the notch she'd cut in the stock, the two green seams kissing. "I did forty that summer. Thirty-one took."

"That's a good rate."

"It was a good teacher. He'd been grafted himself, do you see? Born in Germany, forty years in Holland, married to a Dutch wife, children who spoke Dutch and dreamed in Dutch. He used to say—" she paused, finding the memory's shape again after the winter it had spent in storage— "he used to say that a grafted tree is more loyal than any citizen. Because it has chosen where it stands. Nothing about it is accidental."

She bound the joint with raffia, wound it tight, sealed it with wax from the little pot on the heater, and handed the finished thing across to Julian.

"Your quince," she said. "Quince onto quince — an easy one, for your first. Hold it."

He held it. He held it the way he might have held a child, both hands, slightly too carefully, looking down into the top of it as if expecting it to say something.

"It's very small," he said, "to have caused so much trouble."

"They always are," said Ada. "Trees and people. The smallest cut does the most."

* * *

They planted over three weekends, when the moon was right — Edith insisted on the moon, having read something authoritative somewhere, and no one argued with Edith about moons — and the weather behaved itself in that grudging, provisional way Kent weather behaves in spring, sun and squall alternating like siblings.

Ivo dug the holes. This was not assigned to him; he simply annexed the work, because digging was the one job that required no decisions and no words, and Ada understood that he needed a certain number of wordless hours per week the way other men needed tobacco. He dug fourteen holes in the old rubbish-ground and the spade rang sometimes on buried brick and he swore cheerfully at the ground and the ground forgave him, being Kentish.

For each tree there was a small ceremony, invented as they went, because none of them were churchgoers anymore except Edith, who attended for intelligence-gathering purposes and admitted it freely.

The first hole was for Tom Ellery, the deserter whose fevered confessions Ivo had carried inland in a cart two years ago and never fully put down. They planted a Bramley for him, cooking apple, sharp and honest. Ivo tamped the earth with the flat of the spade and said, "He wanted to go home to Shropshire," and that was the whole eulogy, and it was enough.

There was a tree for the Brandt boy's grandfather, who had died in Hamburg the previous autumn without knowing whether his grandson had reached safety — a plum, sweet, because the man had reportedly loved sweets and been denied them by rationing and everything else. There was a tree for the schoolmaster at Hythe who had vouched for papers that were forged and gone to prison for it smiling. There was, after a long argument, a tree even for Captain Reeve — "forgiven is forgiven, or the category means nothing," Edith said, "and besides, he is retired to Tunbridge Wells, which is punishment enough" — a crab apple, small and sour and ornamental, and Ivo planted it last in that row and gave it the least water, and everyone pretended not to notice.

And there was a tree for Ada's father.

She had not asked for it. She would not have dared to ask. But she came out one April morning and found a hole already dug in the best position — southern slope, sheltered from the east wind — and beside it a cherry tree in a pot with a paper label in Edith's upright hand: *for H., wherever he is rooted.*

Her father was alive. That was the letter that had come in the winter, traced through the same channels that had carried the Brandts to safety: alive in Holland, older, working again at his old trade, keeping a room above a shop and asking, in the careful formal English he had never lost, whether his daughter might write to him at the address provided. She had written eleven drafts. The twelfth was in her desk drawer, sealed, waiting for courage or for spring, whichever came first.

She stood at the edge of the hole with the cherry in her arms and could not move, and Ivo — who missed nothing now, whose blindness had been cured by shame into a terrible attentiveness — quietly picked up the spade and went to dig another hole somewhere else, loudly, giving her the field.

She planted it alone. She pressed the earth down with her bare hands, palms and fingers, feeling the cool crumb of it, thinking: *rootedness is not where you were born. Rootedness is where you dig.* Her father had been torn up twice — once by war, once by panic — and still he had sent roots out sideways in foreign soil, patient, unkillable, like the cherry that comes back from the stump no matter how you cut it.

She talked to the tree while she worked. Her mother had done that; she had sworn it made the roots braver.

"Growth," she told it, in the mixture of English and Dutch and German that was her true native language, the one no flag owned. "That's all I'm asking. Just grow."

* * *

By May the orchard stood complete: fourteen young trees in their straight lines, staked and tied, absurdly thin against the wide sky, looking less like an orchard than like the idea of one — a promise made in wood.

But the grafts were waking. Ada walked the rows each morning before anyone else was up, with her tea going cold in her hand, and checked the wax seals, and there — yes — the first buds breaking on the Ellery Bramley, tight green fists unclenching; and on Julian's quince, the scion she had cut and fitted with her own hands, three leaves unfurled like small flags of a country not yet named. Cambium to cambium. Two trees agreeing to be one.

She taught him through that month, patiently, correcting his cuts. He was a slow learner and a thorough one — he wanted the reasons for everything, the botany beneath the technique, why this angle and not that — and she realized, watching him frown at a bud, that he was teaching himself something larger than grafting. He was teaching himself to make one honest cut. To join himself to living wood and stay joined.

"The thirty-one out of forty," he said one evening, as they washed the pots. "The ones that took. What happened to the nine?"

"Some died," she said. "Some I cut wrong. And some—" she dried her hands slowly— "some took beautifully, and then the tree grew so fast it snapped its own new joint. Too much vigor too soon. You have to pinch it back. Let it strengthen before it reaches."

He nodded slowly, looking at his own hands, and she saw him translating it into whatever language his two years had left him, and did not interrupt.

* * *

On the last evening of May, the whole household ended up in the orchard without planning it, the way they now ended up everywhere together — Edith with the evening paper she wasn't reading, Julian on his bench with a blanket he didn't need, Ivo leaning on the gate, Ada in the middle of it all with dirt permanently under her nails.

The light came low and gold across the Weald. Somewhere a cuckoo was still going, late and persistent. The fourteen trees threw shadows far longer than themselves, reaching toward the house like fingers.

"Next year," Julian said, "there'll be blossom."

"The year after, maybe fruit," said Ada.

"Quince paste in diamonds," Edith said. "I still remember the recipe. Mother wrote it in the back of her prayer book, which tells you everything about Mother."

They were silent together, comfortably, the silence of people who had survived being strangers to one another. And Ada thought about the drawer upstairs, and the twelfth letter, sealed and waiting, and the address in Holland written in her father's careful hand.

Tomorrow was the first of June. The post left Marlowe House at eight.

"Ivo," she said, before she could stop herself, before the coward in her could file the impulse away with all the others. "How are you with letters these days?"

He looked at her. She watched him understand — watched it arrive in his face, that slow dawn she had come to depend on more than she would ever admit aloud.

"Sealed ones?" he said. "I've developed principles."

"Good." She took a breath that hurt pleasantly, like cold air after fever. "Because tomorrow I'm posting one, and I'd rather you didn't know what it says until it's answered. Some things want to grow in the dark first. Like roots."

He smiled — that rare full smile, the one that rearranged his whole skeptical face — and touched two fingers to his cap in a salute.

"Then it will travel," he said, "*unread*, madam, on my honor as a reformed sinner."

Above them the first swifts of summer came screaming low over the young trees, and in the shed the last of the wax cooled in its pot, and the quince scion held, and held, and did not let go.
