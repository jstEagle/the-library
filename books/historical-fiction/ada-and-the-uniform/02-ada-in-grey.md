## 2. Ada in Grey

The letter had sat on Beatrix's writing desk all night like a sleeping animal she was afraid to wake.

She had placed it there deliberately—face down, government frank uppermost—and then she had done the thing she never did with correspondence: she had left it alone. For ten years her discipline had been a kind of religion. Every letter that crossed this house passed through her hands within a day; every seal was lifted and re-lifted with her kettle's breath and her bone folder's edge; every decision—to pass on, to soften, to burn—was made before the clock in the hall struck ten. She was, by her own private accounting, the most reliable woman in England whom nobody knew existed.

But this letter she had not opened.

Now it was morning, grey and thin through the lace curtains, and Lady Constance would ring for her at half past eight, and the letter could not sit unopened past nine without becoming a fact rather than an omission. Beatrix stood over it in her dressing gown, hair pinned but not yet dressed, and looked down at the frank as though it might explain itself.

*Home Office.* Not the department itself—the frank of a private secretary's office, the sort of envelope that arrived when someone official wanted to seem unofficial. Addressed not to Lady Constance but to *The Secretary, The Hale Household*, which was itself an oddity. Nobody wrote to the household. People wrote to Lady Constance Hale, or to Mrs. Fairweather for engagements, or to the cook about eggs. This envelope had been addressed by someone who knew how the house actually worked and wished to pretend otherwise.

She made tea first. She always made tea first; ritual was armor. Then she filled the shallow pan, set the kettle singing, and held the envelope over the steam the way she had done perhaps four thousand times—wrist steady, fingers light, the paper warming until the gum surrendered its grip with a small sigh no one else in the world would ever hear.

The flap came free. She slid out two sheets, and read.

It took her three readings to believe what she was reading, and even then belief came reluctantly, the way one accepts bad weather.

The letter was unsigned. That alone told her it was genuine; signed letters were performances, and performances were safe. It was written in the flat, economical hand of a man who drafted documents all day and had forgotten how to write anything else. It said that certain parties at the Home Office had prepared a file against Mrs. Ashcombe of Ashcombe Court—her suffrage donations traced, exaggerated, re-dated where necessary; her name to be linked in the press, by spring, to a financial irregularity that did not exist yet but soon would, because the paperwork was being built the way one builds a hedge: planted early, cut to shape, allowed to grow into something that looks natural.

It said further that the Ashcombes' patronage had been useful to the movement, and that the movement's friends should consider whether continued association would prove—here the phrase, in its bureaucratic delicacy, made Beatrix's stomach turn—*injudicious*.

And it said, in a postscript that felt like a hand closing around her throat: *The daughter is at present a probationer with the Voluntary Aid Detachment. She is young. Young women can be spoken to.*

Beatrix set the pages down on her desk very carefully, as though they were wet.

She had spent ten years reading other people's secrets, and she had developed a taxonomy. There were love letters, which were mostly weather. There were money letters, which were mostly fear. There were political letters, which were chess moves wearing perfume. And once or twice a year there came a letter that was none of these—a letter that showed her the machinery underneath everything, the gears turning behind the wallpaper of ordinary life. Those she destroyed. Not because destruction hid them from others, but because after reading them she could not bear to know they existed in the house, physical, findable, capable of falling into the wrong hands. She burned them and carried them instead, weightless, in the place behind her ribs where she kept everything.

This one she could not burn. Not because of what it said about the Ashcombes—though that was terrible enough—but because of what its existence proved. Someone inside the Home Office had written to warn them. Someone inside the machine had put his hand against the gears. Which meant the machine could be fought, if one knew where to press.

Which meant silence was no longer merely her duty. It might be her weapon.

She heard the hall clock strike eight, and downstairs the front door bell rang—early, urgent, twice.

* * *

Mrs. Pym answered it, of course; Beatrix was still upstairs, and by the time she descended, buttoning her cuffs, the visitor was already in the morning room and Mrs. Pym was already disapproving of her in the particular silent way Mrs. Pym disapproved of anyone under thirty who arrived before ten.

"She wouldn't wait," Mrs. Pym murmured. "Says it's a matter of urgency. Says—" and here her voice dropped to something almost embarrassed, "—she says you'll know why she couldn't write ahead."

*I'll?* thought Beatrix. But she only nodded and went in.

The girl standing at the window of the morning room wore grey.

That was Beatrix's first impression and it stayed with her: grey dress, grey apron, the red cross on her breast the single point of color in the whole muted figure, like a drop of blood on ash. A VAD probationer's uniform, worn correctly—collar buttoned, belt squared, hem regulation length—which told Beatrix the girl had been drilled and had submitted to drilling, had learned to make herself neat and obedient and small. Her cap was still on her head though she stood indoors, as if she'd come straight off a night shift, or as if removing it would feel like removing armor.

She turned when the door opened, and Beatrix saw her face for the first time.

Ada Ashcombe. She had seen photographs, of course—Lady Constance kept the society pages longer than she admitted to keeping them, and the Ashcombe engagement notices and charity lists had passed through Beatrix's hands often enough. In photographs the girl had been decorative: a pleasant oval face, good teeth, the smile of someone who has never needed to think quickly. The living version had the same face, but the photograph had lied about everything behind it. The eyes were red-rimmed and dry—that particular dryness that comes after weeping has exhausted itself and found nothing left to give. She stood the way soldiers stand in photographs of men waiting to go over: upright because collapsing is not permitted.

"Miss Ashcombe," Beatrix said. "You're up early."

"I haven't been to bed." The girl's voice was low and level, and Beatrix recognized the levelness instantly—it was the voice of a person holding a door shut from inside. "Is Lady Constance awake?"

"She takes breakfast at half past eight."

"I know her schedule." Then, catching herself, a flicker of apology: "I mean—I've met her. Twice. At my mother's. I don't imagine she remembers me particularly."

"Lady Constance remembers everyone," Beatrix said. "It's part of her method." She moved to the tea tray, poured a cup, and held it out—an offering, and also a test. "Sit down, Miss Ashcombe. You look as though you've walked here."

"I took the tram as far as the square." The girl took the cup but didn't drink. Her hands were steady around it, which surprised Beatrix; the rest of her trembled somewhere beneath the uniform, in the way a house trembles before anything visible falls. "I need to speak to Lady Constance. Alone. Before nine o'clock."

"It's not quite twenty past."

"Then I'm in time." Something in her face almost relaxed, and was immediately re-secured. "I've been rehearsing what to say since midnight and I've got it down to four sentences. I'd like to say them to her while I still remember the order."

Beatrix studied her over the rim of her own cup. Ten years of reading people through their letters had taught her things faces alone never taught her, but the training ran both ways now; she could not stop reading even when reading was rude. And what she read in Ada Ashcombe was so familiar it hurt: a young woman who had discovered that the adults around her—her parents, her employers, the entire ornamental apparatus of her life—were engaged in managing her, and who had come to the one person in London rumored to be dangerous enough to help.

But it wasn't only that. It was the way the girl held the teacup without drinking. The way she had said *you'll know why I couldn't write ahead* to Mrs. Pym—as though she assumed the household's letters were watched. As though she already knew.

"You said four sentences," Beatrix said quietly. "Perhaps you'd better try them on me first. If they survive me, they'll survive her."

Ada looked at her then—really looked, the first time their eyes had properly met—and Beatrix saw the calculation happen, quick and frightened and brave. The girl set down the untouched cup.

"They're going to ruin my mother," she said. "There are papers being made. False ones. About money she gave to your cause. And they're going to use me to do it—they've already spoken to someone at the hospital where I train, someone offered me a position in London, and I understood, halfway through the interview, that it wasn't an offer. It was a placement. Like a piece on a board." Her voice stayed level; only her hands, folded now in her lap, betrayed the pressure beneath. "My mother doesn't know. My father doesn't know. If I tell them, they'll do exactly what whoever wrote these papers wants—they'll withdraw the donations, they'll go quiet, they'll be frightened into obedience. So I came to the one person who frightens back."

The morning room was very quiet. Somewhere above them a floorboard creaked; Lady Constance's maid, drawing curtains.

"How do you know about the papers?" Beatrix asked. Her own voice sounded strange to her—too calm, too curious, the voice of a woman buying time.

"Because the man who interviewed me made a mistake," Ada said. "He mentioned a date. A donation made in March. My mother gave nothing in March. Whoever built his file guessed wrong, and I noticed, and he saw me notice." She swallowed. "After that, I stopped pretending to be flattered."

Beatrix set down her cup. Behind her ribs, the place where she kept her burned letters, something shifted—a crack, hairline, running through ten years of careful masonry. Because she knew about March. She knew precisely what had happened in March; she had steamed open the acknowledgment herself, two springs ago, and decided it was harmless, and filed it in the archive under the stairs where a decade of harmless acknowledgments slept in their boxes.

The girl across from her did not know that. Could not know it. And yet here she stood, having caught the same error, drawn the same thread, arrived at the same door.

They were, Beatrix realized, the same kind of creature—two women whose usefulness lay in being overlooked, who had each spent their lives inside other people's secrets. The difference was only that Ada Ashcombe had cracked first.

Above them, the floorboards announced Lady Constance's descent: the measured tread, the pause at the landing rail. Twenty minutes early. Beatrix rose, smoothing her skirt, feeling the letter's weight behind her ribs as though it had migrated from her desk into her body.

"Miss Ashcombe," she said softly, "when you speak to her—tell her about the man and the false date. Tell her everything except how you got here. Leave the rest to me."

Ada frowned. "The rest?"

"How I intend to prove it," Beatrix said, and heard, in her own voice, the sound of a lock turning from the inside. "I keep the household's correspondence, Miss Ashcombe. All of it. For rather longer than you'd believe."

On the stairs above, Lady Constance Hale began to descend.
