## 3. What the Lady Doesn't Ask

The letter lay in Beatrix's writing desk for two days before she could make herself decide anything at all.

She had put it beneath the false bottom of the stationery drawer, under the reams of cream paper and the spare nibs, where it sat like a coal that had not yet decided whether to burn. Twice she took it out. Twice she held it to the window light, as though the government frank might have changed its mind overnight, as though the crest might have dissolved into something harmless — an invitation to a garden party, a bill from a fishmonger with pretensions.

It had not. The frank was still there, crisp and official, the sort of envelope that arrived at Hale House perhaps four times a year and always made Lady Constance's jaw tighten before she'd read a word.

On the third morning, Beatrix carried it down to breakfast on the silver tray with the rest of the post, and set it on top, face up, and watched.

Lady Constance Hale took her breakfast standing, which she said kept her arguments sharp. She was fifty-eight years old and built like a keel, all ballast below the waterline and sail above it, and this morning she wore violet — the movement's colour, though she claimed she simply liked it — pinned at the throat with the small gold brooch of scales that her late husband had given her when she first spoke from a platform in 1906. She sorted the post without looking at it properly: bill, bill, circular from the WSPU, a note from Millicent Fawcett's secretary. Then the sealed letter with the government frank.

She turned it over once. Turned it back. Set it down beside the marmalade as if it were something that had crawled out of the garden.

"Well," she said.

"Shall I open it?" Beatrix asked, in the flat voice she used for such offers — the voice that said *I am a function, not a person, and functions do not read what they open.*

"No." Lady Constance looked at the envelope the way one looks at a dog known to bite. "No, leave it. I'll deal with it after."

"After" was a country Lady Constance visited rarely. Beatrix collected the tray at half past ten, and the letter still sat unopened by the cold toast, and Lady Constance was at her desk drafting a speech about the Conciliation Bill with the particular ferocity of a woman who has decided not to think about something.

Beatrix understood the shape of that silence. She had lived inside it for years.

* * *

At noon, driving Lady Constance to a committee meeting in Kensington, Beatrix listened to her employer rehearse.

"They want us frightened," Lady Constance said to the back of Beatrix's head. "That is the entire strategy now, you understand. Not argument — fright. Asquith's people have learned that we can out-argue them, so they will stop arguing. They'll whisper instead. Scandal is cheaper than debate."

"And if they whisper about us?" Beatrix asked, because it was permitted, just barely, for the driver to ask a question through the little hatch.

"It won't be about me. I'm too useful as a monster already; I come pre-discredited." A dry laugh. "They'll go for the money. They always go for the money. Half our patrons are women whose husbands would rather see them laughed out of drawing rooms than out of their inheritances, and the Government knows precisely where every soft seam lies."

The carriage turned onto Cromwell Road. Lady Constance fell silent for a moment, and then said, in a different voice, lighter, deliberately careless:

"The Ashcombes gave again last month. Five hundred pounds. Sir Edmund signed it himself, which surprised me — he's been cool since the window business."

"Since the window business," Beatrix repeated neutrally.

"Young Ashcombe girl threw a brick through a Home Office pane in March. Ada. You drove me to the court, don't pretend you weren't there." Lady Constance's voice warmed slightly. "Sir Edmund was furious for a fortnight and then quietly doubled his subscription, which tells you everything about that family that matters. He is furious and loyal. It's the most English thing about him."

Beatrix said nothing. In her breast pocket, folded very small, was a copy — her own hand, made at midnight — of a letter that suggested Sir Edmund's loyalty was about to be tested in ways fury could not survive.

She should have said something. She knew even then, turning the corner into the committee rooms' cobbled yard, that there had been a moment — perhaps thirty seconds wide — in which she might have said, *Ma'am, there is a letter; ma'am, they mean to smear the Ashcombes; ma'am, we should move first.* Thirty seconds, and the hatch between the driver's seat and the lady's conscience stood open, and she let it close.

Because Lady Constance would have done exactly what she always did: seized the thing, acted on it loudly, written three letters and made a speech, and in doing so confirmed to whoever had sent that envelope that Hale House read its mail. And the letter did not say who had sent it. That was the poison in it. A smear named no author, offered no proof, only a shadow shaped like a Home Office clerk and a sum of money and the word *Ashcombe* repeated until it curdled. If Lady Constance cried fire and there was no fire, she'd be the hysterical woman they already said she was. If she cried fire and there *was* fire, she'd have told the arsonists she could smell smoke.

So Beatrix said nothing, and helped her ladyship down with her papers, and sat in the carriage on Cromwell Road for forty minutes with the rain starting, thinking about the difference between loyalty and cowardice, and being unable, entirely unable, to find the line.

* * *

The archive lived under the stairs.

Officially it was the wine cellar, and indeed there were racks along one wall holding bottles nobody drank. Officially Beatrix went down there to inventory the claret. Unofficially, behind a screen of dust sheets, stood four filing cabinets, a locked deed box, and eleven years — no, ten years, eleven felt like boasting — of the correspondence of Lady Constance Hale, sorted, cross-referenced, and in some cases curated with a firmness their authors never suspected.

Beatrix had built it the way a spider builds, without deciding to, one necessary strand at a time. The first year she had kept copies of letters because Lady Constance's memory for promises was conveniently selective. The third year she had begun keeping the letters themselves — the originals she judged dangerous — because burning them felt like murder and returning them felt like suicide. By the sixth year there was a system: green folders for correspondence concerning money, blue for the movement's internal quarrels, red for the letters Beatrix had altered, and black — a single black folder, thin as a hymn book — for the ones she had destroyed outright, represented now only by her own sworn summary of each, written in a hand so unlike her usual script that even she found it faintly sinister.

She unlocked the deed box and took out the black folder and set it on the wine crate that served as her desk, and did not open it. Not yet. First, the new letter.

She laid the government envelope beside the folder and read it again, the fourth reading, slowly, letting each sentence settle like sediment.

It was unsigned but not anonymous — that was the cleverness of it. The paper was Crown stationery. The phrasing was clerkly: *it has been represented to this Department*, *matters touching the propriety of certain subscriptions*, *the name of Ashcombe has been mentioned in connection with irregularities*. No accusation. Only the announcement that an accusation was *available*, like a weapon lying on a table, and the clear implication that the writer could either pick it up or set it down, depending on what came next in the post.

And beneath that, in a paragraph of exquisite blandness, the hook: *We understand that certain private papers passing through your household may illuminate these matters. Their careful custody would be appreciated. Their careless dispersal would be regretted.*

They knew. Whoever wrote it knew that Hale House had a reader — or strongly enough suspected it to bait a trap. *Their careless dispersal would be regretted.* Regretted by whom?

Beatrix sat down on the crate and became aware that her heart was going quickly, quickly, like a sewing machine.

Because here was the thing she had never let herself think in ten years of steaming envelopes over the spirit lamp: they were afraid of her. Of *her* — of the woman who poured the sherry and knew which brake squeaked. Somewhere in a warm office a man had written a sentence addressed, obliquely, tremblingly, to her existence. *Certain private papers passing through your household.* Your household. Hers.

All those years she had thought of her knowledge as a weight — sacks of other people's secrets carried up and down stairs, growing heavier, hers alone to haul. But a weight only presses on the one who carries it. This was not a weight. This was leverage. Somewhere in the cabinets behind the dust sheets were letters that could unmake a Home Office clerk, and he knew it, and he had written to her — not to Lady Constance, whatever the envelope said — *to her* — asking nicely.

The intoxication of it went to her head like brandy taken on an empty stomach. For one long minute Beatrix Delacroix allowed herself to feel it: power, real power, held quietly in two chapped hands in a cellar that smelled of cork dust.

Then fear arrived, punctual as ever, and pointed out the arithmetic. A man who wrote such letters did not stop at writing them. A man who suspected a reader inside Hale House would next send a reader of his own — a caller, an agent, a friendly policeman — and the first thing such a man would want was the archive itself, and the second thing would be the archivist.

She pulled the green folders. Money, then. Ten years of the movement's finances passed through her hands: subscriptions, bequests, the desperate quarterly scrambles. She worked until the cellar grew cold and the single bulb overhead buzzed like a trapped fly, pulling every letter that touched the Ashcombe name, and by eleven o'clock she had a stack of nine — donations, thank-yous, one extraordinary note from 1909 in which Lady Constance had promised, rashly, in writing, to "stand between the Ashcombes and any consequence" of their generosity.

*Any consequence.* Lady Constance had promised protection and was now declining to read the letter announcing the attack. There was a speech in that, if anyone survived to give it.

Beatrix copied the relevant lines, returned everything, locked the box, and sat a moment longer in the dark with her hands flat on the crate.

There was one more thread to pull, and she had been avoiding it. In March — the week of the broken window — a letter had come to Hale House marked personal, from a name she had not seen before or since: a solicitor's clerk in Gray's Inn, writing to warn, obliquely, that "certain enquiries were being made at the instance of persons at the Home Office" regarding suffrage funds. She had filed it blue, internal quarrels, because it had seemed like factional gossip. It did not seem like gossip now. She fetched the blue folder and found it in four minutes, and read it standing, and felt the cellar tilt very slightly around her.

The warning had come in March. The smear had been assembled since at least March. Which meant the machinery had been running for months — patiently, clerkishly — while everyone above stairs slept.

And which meant, Beatrix thought, climbing the stairs with the lamp out and her heart still going like a machine, that the letter in her desk was not the beginning of anything.

It was a courtesy call. The beginning had happened long ago, quietly, in offices she would never see — and someone else, somewhere, already knew far more than she did.

The question that followed her up into the light was simple and unbearable: if the Government had spent months building a case against the Ashcombes, what, precisely, did they already know about the daughter?
