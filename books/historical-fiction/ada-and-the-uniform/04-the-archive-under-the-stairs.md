## 4. The Archive Under the Stairs

### Beatrix

The cellar door had been hers alone for ten years.

It sat beneath the back stairs of Lady Constance's house in Kensington—a door painted the same drab green as the pantry, with a lock so old it looked ornamental and a key Beatrix wore on a ribbon at her waist, hidden under her driving coat where no one thought to look. Servants assumed it was a coal cellar. Lady Constance assumed—Beatrix had made sure of this over the years—that whatever papers were down there were dull ones: receipts, subscriptions, the administrative sediment of a busy political life. Nobody asked about sediment.

Tonight she took Ada Ashcombe down the stairs by lamplight, and the key turned in the lock with the sound of something long held finally exhaling.

"Mind your head," Beatrix said. "And mind what you touch. The order matters more than you'd think."

The lamp found the room slowly. Shelves ran floor to ceiling along three walls—not rough carpentry but proper joinery, oak that had been bought second-hand and oiled until it glowed. Boxes in ranks, each labelled in Beatrix's small upright hand. *Correspondence 1903–Q2. Correspondence 1905–Q1. Financial, Hale. Financial, allies. Press. Legal.* And along the far wall, apart from the rest, a single shelf of grey boxes with no labels at all, only dates.

Ada stood very still in the middle of the stone floor. She was still in her nurse's uniform—the grey dress, the white apron folded away somewhere, the cap that made her look ten years older and entirely unremarkable, which Beatrix suspected was the whole point of the thing. Girls in uniforms could stand in doorways unobserved. Beatrix knew this from the inside.

"You built all this," Ada said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I tidied it," Beatrix said. "Lady Constance is a great woman and a catastrophic filer. Someone had to know where things were." She set the lamp on the work table—a plank across two trestles—and pulled off her gloves finger by finger. "What she doesn't know is *what* things were."

She heard Ada breathe in. In the yellow light the girl's face was pale and steady, and Beatrix thought: *she will not flinch. Good.* She'd decided on the drive back from the station, watching Ada's reflection in the carriage glass while pretending to watch the road, that if anyone was to see the archive, it should be someone who already understood what it cost to keep other people's secrets. A girl who wore a uniform as camouflage was someone who understood.

"The letter you saw," Beatrix said. "The one with the government frank. I'm going to show you why I couldn't put it down."

She went first to the grey boxes. Ten years of them. Inside each, letters—not copies but originals, or near-originals; some were the steamed-open envelopes themselves with their contents refolded wrong, some were pages Beatrix had copied out in her own hand before returning the originals altered or not at all. She laid them out on the table in a chronology she had carried in her head for a decade but never once assembled flat, never let it become a thing with edges.

"This one, 1906. A note from a Mr. Ellery Voss—he signs himself a private inquiry agent—to a man at the Home Office. You'll want the name." Her finger stopped on the signature. "R. G. Pellwyn. Third clerk, Home Office, per his own description of himself. Note he does not write to his superior. He writes *around* them. That's the habit of a man building something privately."

Ada leaned close. Beatrix watched her read, watched the girl's lips move slightly over the phrases—*the Ashcombe subscription… the Hale connection… a matter best handled without ministerial knowledge*—and saw the exact moment the words stopped being words and became a picture.

"There are eleven of these," Beatrix said quietly. "Spread over six years. Voss writes, Pellwyn answers—or rather, Pellwyn instructs and Voss executes. Payments here—" she fanned three bank drafts, "—drawn through a solicitor's client account in Lincoln's Inn. And here, 1909, the first mention of the word we now have reason to care about."

She put the newest letter beside the others: the sealed envelope with the government frank, opened last night against the kettle spout, its contents copied into her notebook and the original resealed with a stick of gum arabic and a warm knife, as she had done a thousand times.

"*Fabrication of correspondence implicating Mrs. Ashcombe's donations in irregular party funds,*" Ada read aloud, and her voice did not shake, which cost Beatrix something to hear, because she could hear what the steadiness cost. "*The daughter's position affords access. Voss to prepare material for press use by spring.*"

"The daughter," Beatrix said, "is you."

"I gathered." Ada straightened. For a moment she looked at the grey boxes as though they might bite. Then, in a voice gone flat and practical—the voice, Beatrix guessed, of a girl who spent her days among doctors who spoke over her—she said: "Show me how you know it's him and not Voss acting alone. Anyone can forge a frank."

Good. Better than good. Beatrix almost smiled. "Because Voss is a hired pen. Look at the drafts—they stop whenever Pellwyn's section has an audit. Here, March 1908, nothing moves for five weeks, then resumes the day after the audit report. A man running his own scheme doesn't pause for another man's paperwork. A servant does." She tapped the page. "Pellwyn is the hand. Voss is the glove. And gloves get changed."

Ada was silent a moment. Then she said the thing Beatrix had been waiting for without knowing she waited:

"My mother's solicitor already knows."

### Ada

She had not meant to say it yet. She had meant to hold it, the way she held everything—behind the teeth, in the place where she kept the things that would upset Mama if spoken aloud. But standing in this cold bright cellar among ten years of a stranger's careful handwriting, holding a lamp over proof that men in Whitehall had spent six years arranging her family's ruin like flowers in a vase they intended to knock over—it came out of her the way blood comes from a pricked finger. Without permission.

Mr. Trenchard. Papa's solicitor for twenty years, a man who smelled of church and called Ada *Miss Ada* and had once, when she was nine, shown her how a fountain pen worked as though conferring a knighthood.

"I found it by accident," she said. Both women's eyes were on her—Mrs. Delacroix's grey, level, unhurried eyes, and she understood suddenly why Lady Constance trusted this woman with everything, because those eyes made you want to tell the truth just to fill them properly. "Three weeks ago. I'd gone to Lincoln's Inn to collect a deed Mama needed signed—she thinks I can't manage such errands, so I do them. Trenchard wasn't expecting me. His clerk let me wait in the outer office, and through the door—I wasn't listening, I want that recorded, I wasn't *listening*, but the voices carry—"

"Recorded," Mrs. Delacroix repeated, with the ghost of dry amusement. "You've been down here four minutes and you're already deposing yourself."

Ada felt heat climb her neck. "I'm being precise. You're precise. It seemed polite."

"It seemed correct," Mrs. Delacroix said. "Continue."

"Trenchard was speaking with a man I didn't know. About Papa's accounts. And Trenchard said—" she closed her eyes; the sentence had lived behind her teeth for three weeks and came out whole, worn smooth from handling, "—he said, *'If it goes to press in the spring, the settlement must be drawn before Lady Constance's people start asking questions.'*" She opened her eyes. "Settlement. As in compensation. As in a man being paid to look the other way while my family is destroyed in the newspapers. He's their solicitor. He christened the firm that handled our donations. And he is *theirs*."

The lamp flame stood very still between them. Somewhere above, the house ticked and settled, ordinary sounds, the sounds of a world that did not know what was in its cellar.

"And you told no one," Mrs. Delacroix said. Not accusing. Weighing.

"I told no one." Ada's hands had found each other in her lap without her noticing; she made herself separate them. "Who would I tell? If I tell Mama, she confronts Trenchard, and then the man knows we know, and whatever they're building gets moved somewhere I can't see. If I tell Papa—" she stopped. Started again, quieter. "Papa believes that unpleasantness can be managed by gentlemen. He would take Trenchard's word over mine. He takes everyone's word over mine. I'm the daughter who plays piano and wears a uniform and is therefore furniture." She heard her own bitterness and was startled by it, and then was not startled, because it was true and had been true a long time. "So I kept it. Behind the teeth. And then Mama began receiving the anonymous notes—you've seen one, presumably—and I understood that the thing I was keeping was going to happen whether I held it or not."

"Yes," Mrs. Delacroix said simply. "That is generally the discovery. That silence is not a container. It's only a delay."

They looked at each other across the table, across the spread of evidence, and Ada felt something shift in her chest—a door unlatching. This woman had known. Not everything, perhaps, but enough; she had stood in hallways and read faces and kept her mouth shut for years, exactly as Ada kept her mouth shut, except that Mrs. Delacroix had done something with her silences. She had *filed* them. She had turned the invisible woman's only weapon—attention—into a room full of oak shelves and dated boxes and a case that could hang a Whitehall clerk if anyone were ever permitted to see it.

"You could have destroyed all of this years ago," Ada said. "Any night. No one would ever have known it existed."

"No one would ever have known I existed either," Mrs. Delacroix said. "There's a certain symmetry in that, isn't there." She began stacking the letters back into their sequence, briskly, her hands sure even in bad light. "But destruction is a luxury for people who expect the future to be kind. I have never expected the future to be kind. I have only expected it to arrive. So I kept the record, on the chance that someday—" she paused, a paper halfway to its box, "—someday someone would come down those stairs who needed it."

"Someone like me."

"Someone like you." Mrs. Delacroix looked up, and for the first time since Ada had met her there was nothing guarded in her face at all, and it was like watching a locked house with every window suddenly lit. "Listen to me, Miss Ashcombe. What we have here proves orchestration—Voss, Pellwyn, the money trail, the timing. What you have just given me proves complicity on the inside of your own walls. Between us we have the shape of the whole machine. But proof in a cellar is proof in a coffin. It needs to get out. It needs to reach a man of law who cannot be bought, before whoever runs Mr. Pellwyn realizes that two women neither of whom officially exists have assembled his entire career in a basement."

"How long do we have?"

"That depends," Mrs. Delacroix said, rising, taking up the lamp, "on how soon they count their letters—and discover one is missing."

Above them, faintly, the front doorbell rang.
