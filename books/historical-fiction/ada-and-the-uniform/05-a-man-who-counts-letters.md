## 5. A Man Who Counts Letters

He came on a Thursday, which Beatrix would remember afterward as the first cruelty of it, because Thursdays were the day the silver was polished and the house smelled of beeswax and Mrs. Petch sang hymns off-key in the morning room, and nothing wicked had ever before chosen to arrive on a Thursday.

She saw him first from the landing window, where she had gone to shake out a duster she did not need. A man standing at the gate in the thin rain, not coming up the path but waiting at it, hat brim beaded with water, hands clasped behind him like a man admiring architecture. He did not ring. He did not knock. He stood and looked at the house the way an assessor looks at a house—not at its windows or its garden but at what it held.

Beatrix's stomach performed a slow, deliberate turn, the way a key performs a turn in a lock one has not meant to lock.

She went down. She always answered the door when Lady Constance was at her writing; that was the arrangement, unspoken these ten years, that Beatrix was the membrane between the world and the woman inside it. She opened the door only as far as the chain allowed, though there was no chain, because old habits of caution are worn like chains whether or not they are fastened to anything.

"Good afternoon," the man said. He was perhaps fifty, grey at the temples, with the smoothed, unhurried face of a man who had spent his life being underestimated on purpose. "Mr. Ellery-Swift. I wonder if I might have a word with the—housekeeper?"

"Secretary," Beatrix said, before she could stop herself correcting him, and hated the small vanity of it even as she said it.

"The secretary, then." His eyes moved over her face with a thoroughness that felt like gloved fingers turning over spoons. "Miss—?"

"Fairweather."

"Ah." And there it was—the small brightening, the flicker of a man who has found the page he was looking for in a very long ledger. "Miss Fairweather. Yes."

* * *

She did not ask him in. She stepped out onto the step instead, pulling the door to behind her, and stood with her arms folded in a manner she hoped read as a servant's impatience rather than a conspirator's dread. The rain had nearly stopped. Water dripped from the lion's mouth of the drainpipe in a steady count: one, two, three.

"I represent," Mr. Ellery-Swift said, "a party interested in certain correspondence that has passed through this address. Correspondence of a political character. Letters received by Lady Hale over the past—" he consulted nothing, but paused anyway, delicately, "—oh, some years."

"I'm afraid I couldn't say anything about the lady's post," Beatrix said. Her voice came out flat and bored, a servant's voice, a voice with no floorboards under it that might give way. Ten years of practice. Ten years of being unreadable, and here at last was the examination, and she found her training held. "The lady deals with her own correspondence."

"Does she." He smiled without warmth. "I understand she is much occupied. Speeches. Committees. The cause." He pronounced *the cause* the way a customs officer pronounces *duty-free*: knowing exactly how much of it there was and what it was worth. "A busy woman cannot read everything herself. There is usually someone who sorts. Who arranges. Who—" he chose the word with visible pleasure— "*manages* the flow of paper."

"Then you understand more about this house than I do," Beatrix said.

"I make it my business to understand houses." He took a step closer, and now she could smell him: wet wool and something faintly chemical, like a photographer's darkroom. "And I make it my business to understand people who handle paper. It is remarkable, Miss Fairweather, how many such people there are, and how few of them are ever noticed. A man counts letters the way other men count money. In and out. What arrives, what leaves. And when a letter arrives at a house and never leaves it—or leaves it altered, or not at all—a man who counts begins to wonder where it has gone."

The dripping of the drainpipe counted on. One, two, three.

Beatrix let a full three seconds pass, letting boredom settle over her face like a cloth over a birdcage. "If you've come to sell me something, you've wasted a walk in the rain. We buy our ink from W. H. Smith like everyone else."

Something moved behind his eyes—not anger; she would have preferred anger. Interest. The interest of a man adding a column and finding the total larger than expected.

"Of course," he said mildly. "I'll leave my card." He produced one from a card case of good leather, and she noticed his gloves were kid, damp at the fingertips, and that he held them out from his body slightly, as though the rain were beneath his notice. "If correspondence of the kind I mentioned should happen to find its way into your keeping—and I say *if*, Miss Fairweather, purely as a matter of form—there are parties prepared to be generous. Very generous. Generosity, in my experience, is rarely refused twice."

"And if it is refused once?"

He smiled again, and this time the smile reached all the way to his eyes, which was worse. "Then one assumes the material is valuable enough to keep. Which tells us what it is worth, doesn't it? Either way, the counting continues." He touched his hat brim. "Good day, Miss Fairweather. I expect we shall meet again. Men like me always do."

He walked away down the wet street without hurrying, and Beatrix stood on the step holding his card between finger and thumb as if it might go off, until the maid came to the door behind her and asked if she'd catch her death standing out there, and Beatrix said no, no, just air, and went inside and locked the door and then, absurdly, stood with her back against it.

* * *

The card said: *H. ELLERY-SWIFT, Agent & Commissioner. 14 Chancery Row.* No telephone number. No affiliation. Agents and commissioners were the cockroaches of official London—everywhere, deniable, fed from a dozen different kitchens. But she knew whose kitchen this one ate from. She had known since the letter with the government frank, the one that lay at the bottom of the tea caddy among the Lapsang Souchong, wrapped in oilcloth, naming the Ashcombes as the next course in a meal that had been cooking for months. A Home Office clerk named Pell. She had learned his name the way one learns the name of a tumor: reluctantly, and with a horrible clarity.

*Someone inside has read too much.*

That was what Ellery-Swift's visit meant, stripped of its politeness. Not suspicion—suspicion they had carried for weeks, perhaps. Certainty. Someone in the house had opened what should have stayed sealed, kept what should have been destroyed, or destroyed what should have been delivered. A man who counted letters had counted one too many discrepancies, and now he had come to offer the discrepancy a price, because offering a price was cheaper than forcing a door, and because the offer itself was an instrument. If she took the money, she was bought and could be squeezed forever. If she refused, she confirmed value. If she reported him, she explained why he had come. Every road out of the visit led back through the fact of her.

She made tea. Her hands were steady; she noted this with a kind of detached pride, watching them measure the leaves, warm the pot, perform the whole liturgy of calm. Underneath the liturgy something else was happening, something that had begun the night she sat in the cellar archive with Ada Ashcombe and watched the girl's face change as page after page proved the smear was manufactured, signed, filed, paid for. That night something had shifted in Beatrix's chest like ballast shifting in a hull, and she had thought it was fear.

It was not entirely fear.

It was the discovery—intoxicating and appalling in equal measure—that ten years of stolen knowledge was not only a burden. It was power. She knew things about men in Whitehall that their own wives did not know. She knew which alliances were real and which were letters written for the file. She knew that Pell's little machine required silence to run, and that silence was the one commodity she had spent a decade manufacturing, and that she could, if she chose, stop producing it.

They had come to buy her silence. They had come to the wrong woman's door for that. Or possibly—she set down the teapot and stared at the rain running down the kitchen window—the right one, because no one on earth knew better than Beatrix Fairweather what silence cost, having paid for it daily for ten years in the coin of her own invisibility. They assumed she would protect it out of habit, the way a miser protects a hoard. They had not considered that a woman who had spent ten years keeping other people's secrets might have developed opinions about which ones deserved keeping.

*Generosity is rarely refused twice,* Ellery-Swift had said.

Let him come again. Let him bring his card case and his counting. She had a card of her own to play, and it was not printed by any stationer in Chancery Row. It was a decade long, kept in boxes under the stairs, indexed in her own hand, and every page of it was a debt the government did not know it owed.

But the price of survival was rising, and she could feel it rising hourly now, like water in a lock. They knew the house. They knew her name. They knew, or soon would, that Ada Ashcombe had called twice. And somewhere across the city, a man was counting letters, and his arithmetic was patient, and patient arithmetic had ruined better women than her.

Beatrix washed the teapot, dried it, put it away. Then she went down to the cellar, unlocked the archive, and took out the box marked *1911 — Oct–Dec* — because if Ellery-Swift wanted to know what she was worth, she intended to find out first.

It was while she sat among the boxes with a lamp turned low that she heard Ada's knock at the service door, quick and doubled, their agreed signal—and heard, behind it, slower and heavier, a second set of footsteps on the area steps that did not belong to Ada at all.
