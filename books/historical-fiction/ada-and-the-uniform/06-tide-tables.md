## 6. Tide Tables

Ada found out on a Thursday, over the cold remains of luncheon, in the voice her mother used for discussing the weather or the price of coal.

"The *Ophelia* sails Saturday morning," Lady Ashcombe said, not looking up from her correspondence. "Tide's at half past six. You'll want to pack light—steamers are cramped things, and you'll be buying most everything in Cape Town anyway."

Ada stood very still, the way one does when the floor has shifted and the body has not yet agreed to fall. The dining room went on being itself: the silver epergne catching grey October light, the footman refilling her mother's glass with the particular reverence he reserved for the claret, the clock over the sideboard ticking with its fat, self-satisfied patience. Somewhere in this house, Ada thought, there was a version of her that would simply say *yes, Mama* and go upstairs to fold linen into a trunk. That girl had been raised here, watered like a hothouse fern. Ada had only lately begun to suspect she was a different species entirely.

"Cape Town," she repeated.

"Your aunt will chaperone. The sea air will do you good." Her mother signed a letter with a flourish, blotted it, and at last lifted her eyes—the blue-grey Ashcombe eyes that Ada had inherited and could never quite read. "You've been strange lately, Ada. Distracted. Running about to London without telling anyone where. A voyage will settle you."

*It will remove you,* Ada thought. *That is what it will do.* She understood now—or was beginning to—with the slow, sick clarity of a woman reading a letter meant for someone else. The scandal being stitched together about Papa, the donations to the cause that were suddenly an embarrassment, the solicitor Mr. Penhale who had stopped answering his telephone—all of it arranged like flowers in a vase, and she was the bloom they'd decided to cut and ship somewhere the rot couldn't reach her. Not exile for punishment. Exile for tidiness. They weren't sending her away because she knew too much; they were sending her away so that whatever she came to know later would arrive too late, softened by distance, deniable.

"When did you decide this?" Ada asked.

"Your father and I discussed it some weeks ago."

Weeks. Before she had ever knocked on Lady Constance Hale's door in her nurse's uniform, her heart hammering under the stiff white apron. Which meant—and this was the thought that made her grip the back of her chair until her knuckles ached—that Miss Delacroix had known, or guessed, or smelled it coming the way animals smell weather. All those careful questions in the cellar archive. *Who else knows you're here? Does your mother watch your post? When do you next dine at home?* Ada had thought it was caution. It had been cartography. The woman had been mapping the walls of Ada's life to find where they were thinnest.

"I'll begin packing tomorrow," Ada said.

Her mother smiled, satisfied, and returned to her letters, and Ada climbed the stairs to her room on legs that felt borrowed, and sat down at her writing desk, and allowed herself—for exactly four minutes by the mantel clock—to be frightened.

Then she took out paper and wrote a note in the small, square hand she'd learned from her father, who believed a lady's handwriting should look like accounting: *I sail Saturday, 6:30 tide. I intend to make them regret putting me on board. —A.*

She addressed it to Miss B. Delacroix, care of Mrs. Pankhurst's old lodgings, which she knew from the archive was one of three dead drops Beatrix still serviced, and gave it to the scullery maid with sixpence and instructions involving a chemist's shop and a great deal of aimless walking.

* * *

Beatrix read the note twice, then held it over the gas ring in her sitting room and watched it curl into ash, because that was what one did now; the habit had become a religion.

*Sail Saturday.* She had half expected it. Men who built scandals the way the Home Office clerk—Harrow, his name was, a small man with ink-stained cuffs and a talent for forgery—built them always wanted the loose threads shipped overseas. But expectation was not preparation, and preparation was not yet forty-eight hours old. She spread her tide table on the desk and ran her finger down the column: Saturday, high water at Portsmouth 6:41 a.m., the *Ophelia* a Union-Castle mail steamer, berthed at Southampton, gangway up at dawn. Two days. Less, really—a night and a morning.

The problem assembled itself in her mind with the terrible neatness of a chess position. If Ada sailed with nothing but a trunk of summer clothes, she was a silenced witness, and silences could be maintained indefinitely from the far side of the equator. But if Ada sailed *with* the evidence—if the testimony crossed the water inside the very vessel meant to carry it beyond reach—then the ship became not a prison but a courier. Harrow's own plan turned against him. There was even a barrister: Mr. Josiah Fenwick of Cape Town, whom Lady Constance had corresponded with in '09, whose letters sat in the cellar archive in a folder marked *SAFETY* in Beatrix's own hand, because ten years ago she had judged him the one man outside England whose discretion and courage could both be relied upon. She had filed him the way one files a fire extinguisher—hoping never to need him.

Now she needed him, and needed him fast, and needed papers that could survive a customs inspection and a search by whoever Harrow sent aboard.

There was a knock. Ada stood in the doorway of the little house off the Euston Road, still in her VAD uniform, having come across London in it like armour—which, Beatrix reflected, it was. Nobody searched a nurse. Nobody questioned a nurse hurrying on official business. The uniform was a kind of invisibility that Ada wore better than Beatrix had ever worn her own.

"They're sailing me on the morning tide," Ada said, without preamble. "Saturday. My mother told me over the fish course."

"I know. Your note arrived an hour ago." Beatrix moved a stack of files off the second chair. "Sit down. We have less time than you think."

They worked through the afternoon as the light went copper and then grey against the window. Beatrix laid it out plainly: the copies made during the night of lamplight and burning fingers—the affidavits, the ledger pages in Harrow's hand, the deposition Ada had signed, the letters tracing the smear from its first poisonous draft to its distribution—would be sealed in oilcloth and packed inside a medicine chest. Nurses travelled with medicine chests; customs officers respected them with something close to superstition. Ada would carry them aboard openly, calmly, the way she carried thermometers and lint.

"And when we dock?" Ada asked. "*If* we dock."

"Fenwick meets the ship. I'll wire him tonight from three different offices, in three different hands, so no telegram clerk can flag it. He'll have an affidavit ready for you to sign on arrival, witnessed, dated, before anyone can meet you at the quay with your aunt's carriage." Beatrix paused. "There's a risk. Harrow knows someone inside our house has read too much. If he guesses the evidence is moving, he won't stop it politely. Papers get seized at docks all the time. People get detained."

"Then don't let him guess."

"It isn't only the papers." Beatrix looked at her hands for a moment—small, capable hands that had steamed open a thousand secrets and kept every one. "It's that a satchel is only paper. Paper can be confiscated, burned, disbelieved. What Harrow truly fears isn't paper. It's a witness who cannot be bought and cannot conveniently vanish. He wants you in Cape Town because Cape Town is far. So we give him Cape Town—" her eyes came up, and there was something in them Ada had not seen before, a banked ferocity, "—and we also give him a record that arrives whether or not the ship does. Three copies. One in the medicine chest with you. One posted separately, in parcels, through three different ports of call. And one—" she tapped the table, "—stays here, hidden where not even I know the whole of it, split between two places, so that destroying any one cache proves nothing and saves nothing."

Ada stared at her. "How long have you been planning this?"

"Since Tuesday." A ghost of a smile. "Planning is the only part of my work I've ever been permitted to enjoy."

* * *

They met once more before the sailing, on Friday evening, in the one place neither Harrow nor the Ashcombes would think to look: the chapel of the hospital where Ada trained, empty between services, smelling of candle wax and cold stone. Ada came in uniform; Beatrix came dressed as a hospital visitor, in a plain coat and a hat with no feathers, carrying the medicine chest as though delivering it.

"Say it back to me," Beatrix murmured, as they knelt side by side in a pew, two women praying to no god in particular.

"The chest goes in my cabin, not the hold. I sign nothing aboard. If anyone boards to search, I am fetching quinine for the voyage and I have a headache to prove it. At Cape Town I ask for Mr. Fenwick of Wale Street before I speak to anyone else, including family. And if the chest is taken—" Ada's jaw tightened, "—the record survives without me."

"And?"

Ada hesitated. Then, quietly: "And whatever happens, I testify. Under my own name. No more anonymous daughters of embarrassed houses."

Beatrix was silent a moment. In the stained-glass dusk, her face seemed carved from something older than either of them. "Ten years I've kept other people's names safe by keeping them secret," she said. "You make it sound simple."

"It isn't simple. It's just necessary." Ada reached out and touched the medicine chest between them, its canvas strap cool under her fingers. "My mother thinks she's shipping me away to keep me quiet. Let her. By the time the *Ophelia* drops anchor, the quiet will be the loudest thing in Cape Town."

Outside, the London evening closed around them—cabs, gaslight, the distant shriek of a train—and somewhere across the city a small man with ink-stained cuffs was counting letters, and a night porter was weighing coins in his pocket, and the tide was already beginning to think about turning.

Neither of them knew yet that the agent had bought the house.
