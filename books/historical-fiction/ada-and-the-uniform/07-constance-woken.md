## 7. Constance, Woken

Beatrix chose the hour the way she had once chosen which letters to burn: deliberately, with an eye to consequence.

It was half past ten on Thursday night, and Lady Constance Hale was at her desk in the blue drawing room, correcting proofs of a pamphlet by lamplight, her spectacles low on her nose, her fountain pen scratching in small furious strokes. She worked late now more often than she slept well; Beatrix had observed this for years without ever saying so. The house was quiet around them—the maids abed, the fire down to its red arithmetic—and Beatrix stood in the doorway holding a tea tray she did not need and a confession she could no longer carry.

She set the tray down. She did not pour.

"Constance." Not *my lady*. She had earned the first name a decade ago and spent it rarely, like coin kept against famine.

Lady Constance looked up over her spectacles. "You've the face of a woman about to tell me the motor's dead again."

"The motor is fine."

"Then it's worse than the motor."

Beatrix sat—uninvited, which was itself an alarm—and folded her hands in her lap so they would not betray her. Ten years of unreadable hands. Tonight they felt like other people's hands, borrowed and clumsy.

"I have something to tell you," she said, "and when I have finished it, you may dismiss me, or you may call the police, or you may do neither. But you will want to hear all of it before you choose, because part of what I have to say is that I have already chosen for you. Many times. Over many years."

The pen stopped scratching. Lady Constance removed her spectacles with the unhurried precision of a surgeon laying down instruments, and Beatrix understood that she was no longer speaking to her employer but to the woman who had once stood on a platform in Bristol while eggs flew and did not blink.

"Go on," said Constance Hale.

* * *

Beatrix began at the beginning, because a confession told out of order is a defence, and she refused to defend herself.

"In 1902, your correspondence began arriving opened before it reached you. Not by me—not then. There was a footman, Peters, who was paid by someone—I never learned whom—to steam envelopes and copy contents. I discovered him in the pantry with a kettle and a stack of your letters, and I dismissed him on the spot and told no one, including you, because telling you would have meant telling you that your post was compromised, which would have meant changing how you wrote, which would have meant the watchers knew we knew."

"You read my letters." It was not yet an accusation. It was a hand feeling along a wall in the dark.

"From that week onward, yes. All of them. Before you saw them and after. I steamed them open at night and sealed them again with a bone folder and a flat iron, and I made a rule for myself, and the rule was this: I would never alter meaning. Only exposure. If a letter named names carelessly—a donor, a member of Parliament who met us privately—I held it back a day until I'd judged whether it would survive being found. If it wouldn't, I destroyed it and told you it never came. Some letters I rewrote. Softened. A phrase here, a signature there. Twice I invented letters entirely, from allies who had gone cold, to keep others warm, because warmth is contagious in politics even when it's counterfeit."

"How many?" Lady Constance asked. Her voice was level, which frightened Beatrix more than shouting would have.

"Of your letters? Thousands. Of mine—of my interventions—" Beatrix calculated honestly, because honesty was the only currency left to her tonight. "Four hundred and six. I counted once. In 1908, after the Manchester arrests. Counting seemed necessary then."

"Four hundred and six decisions about my life, made in secret, by my chauffeur."

"By your driver, your censor, and—for ten years—the only person in England besides yourself who knows everything this movement has risked."

The silence that followed had weight and grain. The fire ticked. Somewhere upstairs a pipe knocked twice, the house settling into its bones.

"Say the rest," Lady Constance said. "There is a *rest*. You don't confess arithmetic at midnight unless there's blood under it."

So Beatrix told her about the letter with the government frank. About the Ashcombes. About Ada in her grey uniform standing in the hall that morning with rain still beaded on her cape, asking for help her mother didn't know she needed. About the Home Office clerk whose name ran through a decade of Beatrix's archived correspondence like a thread through cloth—present at every coincidence, adjacent to every ruin. About the solicitor bought and the smear commissioned and the agent who had come to the door offering money with a smile like a ledger.

And about the archive under the stairs: ten years of originals, copies, and ashes, catalogued in Beatrix's own shorthand, waiting.

When she finished, Lady Constance rose. She crossed to the window and stood looking out at the dark garden as though the answer might be posted to her from the night. When she turned back, her face had gone very still—the stillness Beatrix had seen her summon before hostile crowds, before judges, before husbands of suffragists begging their wives home.

"Do you know what you have done to me?" she said quietly. "Not the movement. *Me.* Every confidence I ever put in the post, every unguarded sentence I wrote to my sister, to Millicent, to men whose discretion I believed in—you read them. I have been naked in my own house for ten years and called it privacy."

"Yes."

"And you let me thank God, aloud, at this very table, for correspondents who were discreet—when it was *you*, it was only ever *you*, deciding what I was allowed to know about my own life."

"Yes," Beatrix said again. She did not look away. Looking away now would be the true betrayal; the reading had merely been the crime.

"Why?" And here Lady Constance's voice finally cracked, one hairline fracture across the marble. "Was it vanity? Power? Did you enjoy it?"

Beatrix had prepared answers to this—had prepared them for years, in the cellar, among her boxes—but standing in the lamplight they all sounded like lies, so she gave the true one instead.

"I enjoyed none of it. I did it because I loved you, and because I loved the cause, and because I was invisible enough to protect both, and invisibility is the one gift this world gives women like me without asking what we'll pay for it." She heard her own voice go rough and was too far in to smooth it. "I destroyed a letter in March of 1907 that would have ended us. You remember the WSPU split, the money question, the accusations flying. There was a note from a treasurer admitting he'd moved funds to shield a woman who'd been arrested under a name that wasn't hers. If it had surfaced—if the *Standard* had printed it—we'd have lost the Ashcombe donation, the Grey sisters, half the northern federations, all inside a fortnight. I burned it. I told you the post had been delayed. You built your speech that spring on foundations I had quietly laid in ash, and you never knew, and the movement lived, and I went back downstairs and locked the empty box."

Lady Constance sat down slowly, as if the chair had moved.

"That letter," she said. "I wondered for years why the attack never came. I thought we'd been lucky."

"We were never lucky, Constance. We were *kept*. That is what I am trying to tell you. Not excused—*kept*. There is a difference and I will not insult you by blurring it."

* * *

They did not make peace quickly, and Beatrix was glad of it. A forgiveness granted too easily would have been another kind of lie between them, and they had enough of those to last a lifetime.

For a long while Lady Constance simply looked at her archive of questions, and Beatrix watched her sort them the way she herself sorted mail: what must be answered, what may wait, what burns.

"The Ashcombes," Lady Constance said at last. "If the smear lands, we lose them anyway—worse, we lose every family weighing whether patronage of us is worth the price. If we expose the clerk, we expose methods—*your* methods—ours, the movement's. We will be handing the *Times* the word 'forgery' to hang around our necks forever."

"Unless it reaches the record properly," Beatrix said. "Affidavit. Barrister. Testimony taken down in fair copy, witnessed, dated. Not a leak—a *record*. They can smear a rumour. They cannot smear a sworn document sitting in a barrister's chambers, copied, distributed, beyond seizure."

"And the evidence requires transport. And the Ashcombe girl requires transport. And the government's man is circling this house like a cat around a mousehole." Lady Constance pressed two fingers to the bridge of her nose. "You have a plan. Of course you have a plan. You've had plans for ten years and told me nothing."

"There's a ship," Beatrix said. "Saturday's tide. And there are papers that must reach Mr. Ellery-Swan before the gangway rises, or not at all. And there is one thing more, and it is the reason I'm telling you all this tonight instead of simply going." She leaned forward. "I cannot do it alone anymore. The copying, the timing, the household—it needs hands I can trust absolutely, and I have just spent an hour teaching you every reason you shouldn't be one of them."

Lady Constance laughed—one short, incredulous note, aimed at the ceiling. "You confess treachery and then ask me to conspire."

"I ask you to do what you've always done. Break the law in service of the truth. I'm only asking you to break it indoors this time."

The silence stretched. Then Lady Constance stood, walked to her desk, and drew out the bottom drawer—the deep one Beatrix had never once opened in ten years, though she had dusted around it a thousand times. From it she took a bottle of brandy and two glasses, and poured, and pushed one across the desk toward her driver, her censor, her betrayer, her keeper.

"My husband used to say a household is only as strong as its worst secret," she said. "He meant it as a warning. Tonight I find it reads more like a promise." She raised her glass, and her eyes over it were dry and fierce and awake. "Very well, Miss Delacroix. You will tell me everything I have forgotten knowing. Every letter, every name, every ash. We begin now, and we do not stop until Saturday's tide—and Beatrix—"

"My lady?"

"If we are to hang together for this, you will damned well call me Constance while we work."

Beatrix lifted her glass. Outside, far off toward the river, a ship's bell rang the quarter-hour, and somewhere in the dark a man who counted letters was counting theirs.

They drank to the morning tide, and then Lady Constance pulled a fresh sheet of paper toward her, and said, "Start with 1902. Leave nothing out."
