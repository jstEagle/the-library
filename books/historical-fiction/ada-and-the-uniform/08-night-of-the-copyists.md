## 8. Night of the Copyists

The house had never been awake at this hour, not like this. Beatrix stood in the cellar doorway and watched the lamplight move among the shelves like something living—six flames in glass hurricanes, borrowed from every room that had a mantel, their oil smell mixing with the cold stone breath of the archive. Ten years of her silences lay in those boxes, and tonight they would be spoken aloud, or copied at least, in triplicate, by the hands of women who had no idea what most of them said.

"You'll work in pairs," she said. "One reads aloud, one copies. If you come across anything with a name you recognize—a name from the newspapers—you mark it with this." She held up a slip of blue ribbon. "Blue means it goes in Ada's satchel. Everything else stays."

Mrs. Petch, the cook, had rolled her sleeves to the elbow as though preparing for a particularly stubborn roast. "And if anyone asks why the kitchen's cold tomorrow because we've burned all the coal down here?"

"Tell them the pipes froze," Beatrix said.

"The pipes don't freeze in April, Miss Delacroix."

"Then tell them nothing. You're all very good at that. I've watched you for ten years."

Something passed around the circle then—not quite laughter, but its cousin: the grim, complicit warmth of women who had spent their lives being unseen and were about to make use of it. Ellen the parlormaid, who could copy a hand so exactly she'd once forged Lady Constance's signature on a laundry list without thinking twice. Two of the typists from the office above the mews, Miss Hartree and young Miss Odom, who arrived with their Remington between them wrapped in a blanket like an infant. And Ada, still in her VAD uniform, grey wool and white apron, the red cross on her breast catching the lamplight.

"Why the uniform?" Beatrix had asked her at nine o'clock, when Ada came down the area steps instead of the front door.

"Because if anyone looks through a window, they see a nurse calling on a sick household," Ada said. "No one questions a nurse at any hour. It's the one disguise that's also the truth—I am here tending to something ill."

Beatrix had looked at her a long moment. "You'd have made a good censor."

"I'd have made a good conspirator. There's more call for it."

* * *

Ada took the boxes marked *A–C* and settled at the small table beneath the stairs, where Beatrix had sat a thousand evenings with her paste pot and her razor blade. The chair was worn to the shape of another woman, and sitting in it felt like trespassing into someone's private country. She opened the first folder and began to read aloud, quietly, her voice pitched for Ellen's ears alone.

*"Dear Mr. Ashcombe—I write regarding the matter we discussed, and assure you the sum named will be sufficient to secure the cooperation of—"*

"Stop," Ellen said. "That one. Blue ribbon."

They worked out a rhythm within the hour. Ada read; Ellen copied in her beautiful, untraceable hand; when Ellen's wrist tired, Miss Odom took the pen and Miss Hartree fed paper into the Remington, whose clatter they had muffled by standing it in a drawer lined with flannel. The archive gave itself up slowly, grudgingly, the way a long-held secret does—one letter at a time, each one a small detonation.

It was Ada who found the clerk's name repeated like a watermark through three years of correspondence. *Hollis. Hollis. Initialled H., countersigned H., "speak to Hollis about the Ashcombe subscription."* She laid the letters side by side on the table and felt the shape of it, the whole architecture of the smear, assembled at last from fragments she had only ever held singly.

"He built it like a wall," she said. "One brick at a time, and each brick innocent on its own."

"That's how men like him build everything," Ellen said, not looking up from her copying. "My father laid bricks forty years. He always said you can carry one stone anywhere and nobody blinks. It's the pile that gets noticed."

Ada looked at the parlormaid—at her chapped hands moving steadily across the page—and understood suddenly what her mother had never grasped in thirty years of committees: that the movement lived or died not in Albert Hall but in rooms like this, among women whose names would never be printed. She copied out Hollis's name herself, in her own hand, and put it under blue ribbon.

* * *

Above them, the house slept with one eye open. Beatrix made her rounds on the hour—the front door chained, the study window latched, the curtain in the morning room left open two inches because from the street it showed lamplight and a woman's silhouette at a desk, which is precisely what a house should show at midnight if it wished to be believed ordinary. She had left the real work below stairs and arranged the theatre above it: her own shadow, playing herself.

At half past one she saw the cigarette.

It was on the far side of the square, under the plane tree opposite the mews entrance—a small red point that glowed, went dark, glowed again. A man who waited did not smoke continuously; he smoked in intervals, conserving, and this man was conserving. She watched for ten minutes until the glow moved off toward the corner, unhurried, a man taking his ease, a man being paid by the night rather than the errand.

Hollis's agent. Or Hollis himself. It hardly mattered which; what mattered was that someone had counted her comings and goings for longer than she had credited, and now stood outside counting the lights in the windows.

She went down to the cellar. "We may have less time than we thought. How many boxes remain?"

"Four," Mrs. Petch said.

"Then we skip nothing, but we summarize where we can. Names, dates, sums. The letters themselves are proof, but if the letters are seized, the summaries survive in six heads and three satchels." She looked around the lamplight at their faces. "From this moment, nothing leaves this room in one place. Not even us."

* * *

By three o'clock the oil in the hurricanes was low and the air in the cellar had gone thick with tallow and breath and the particular exhaustion of sustained attention. Miss Odom's fingers cramped on the keys; Mrs. Petch brewed tea on a spirit lamp, strong enough to stand a spoon in, and they drank it scalding from saucers because there weren't enough cups. Ada's voice had gone rough from reading. She had reached the box marked *F–H* and found, folded inside a letter dated 1909, a note in Beatrix's own hand—a marginal annotation, tiny and precise: *"Destroyed original. This is my fair copy. The original named C.H. directly."*

C.H. Constance Hale. Ada looked up and found Beatrix watching her from across the room, and neither woman said anything, and everything was said.

At twenty past four, Ellen set down her pen. "That's the last of them."

They counted. Three satchels of summaries in three different hands, none signed. One bundle of blue-ribboned originals. One typewritten index. Six women who could testify to what they had seen, if it came to that, though Beatrix prayed it would not, because testimony required standing in a courtroom and these women had employers and reputations and, in Ellen's case, a mother in Bethnal Green who could not afford scandal.

"We sleep in shifts," Beatrix said. "Two hours. Then at dawn—"

The bell at the area door rang.

Once. Softly. The night porter's ring—or rather, the ring the porter used when he wanted to wake the household without waking the street.

Every lamp in the cellar seemed to gutter at once, though none had. Mrs. Petch pinched out the nearest flame with wet fingers. In the sudden half-dark, Beatrix crossed to the foot of the stairs and listened.

Boots on the gravel above. Two sets. One heavy and familiar—Jennings, the porter, who had served the house for six years and whose rheumatism she could chart by the sound of his walk. The other lighter, quicker, stopping and starting like a man reading a house by its windows.

Then Jennings's voice, pitched low, apologetic, the voice of a man doing something for the first time and hating it: "Begging your pardon, miss, but this gentleman says he's lost a document of great value, and he has reason to think it come ashore here."

A second voice, smooth as banked coals. "I needn't come in, Miss Delacroix. But I'll want an answer before the light does."

Beatrix stood in the dark at the bottom of the stairs with four o'clock in her bones and three satchels at her back, and understood that the night's work had bought them everything except time.
