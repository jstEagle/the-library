## 9. The Morning Tide

The fog on the river had a taste to it, coal and brine and something older, like the water remembered everything it had carried and forgave nothing.

Beatrix drove the little Austin through it with the lamps unlit until the last turning, because lit lamps could be followed, and because her hands needed the small discipline of darkness. In the seat beside her Ada sat in her nurse's uniform, grey wool and white apron, cap pinned with the two brass pins that had belonged to her grandmother. The uniform was the whole of their plan's cleverness: no one stopped a VAD. No one questioned a nurse carrying a medical satchel at half past five in the morning. The third satchel — the real one, the one that weighed like sin — rode under Ada's feet, beneath a folded blanket and a copy of *The Nursing Times*.

"You're breathing wrong," Ada said.

"I am breathing correctly."

"You're breathing the way Mother breathes when she's decided not to be frightened yet." Ada turned in the seat to face her. In the grey light her face was very young and very steady, which Beatrix found more alarming than tears would have been. "In through the nose, Miss Delacroix. Four counts. I do it before injections. It works on other people too."

"I have driven Lady Constance through three heckled meetings, a stone-throwing in Bristol, and one actual fire," Beatrix said. "I know how to breathe."

"Then breathe."

The docks rose out of the fog in stages: first the smell, tar and fish and rope; then the sound, winches groaning somewhere invisible, gulls arguing over nothing; then the shapes, masts and derricks and the great black shoulder of the *Calypso*, bound for Lisbon and thence anywhere that wasn't here. Her berth papers said she sailed on the seven-forty tide. The tide tables in Beatrix's pocket — she had checked them four times — agreed.

What the tide tables did not say was whether a man who counted letters would be waiting.

He was.

He stood at the foot of the gangway with his hat set at an angle that suggested he had dressed by gaslight and been satisfied with the result, and beside him stood the night porter from Cavendish Row, hands clasped, face arranged into the expression of a man who has been paid and is trying to look surprised anyway. A third man leaned against a bollard some twenty yards off, doing a poor job of not watching them.

Ada saw him first. Beatrix felt her go rigid in the seat, the way a horse goes rigid before it decides between bolting and fighting.

"That's him," Ada whispered. "That's the man who came to Father's office. Mr. Quill." She almost laughed, bitterly. "His name is actually Quill. He thinks it's funny."

"He would."

"What do we do?"

Beatrix looked at the gangway, at the fog, at the satchel under Ada's feet. Ten years of practice told her: turn the motor round, drive home, burn what couldn't be buried, survive. Ten years of survival. She thought about the archive under the stairs, all those drawers of other women's silences, and how she had opened every one of them except her own.

"We get out of the motor," she said, "and we walk toward him. Slowly. You are a nurse taking a colleague aboard for the voyage; I am your driver. We are nobody. We have never read anything in our lives."

"And if he doesn't believe it?"

"Then I'll think of something," Beatrix said, and was surprised to find it was true. "That's rather my profession."

* * *

Quill let them come within six feet before he spoke.

"Miss Delacroix." His voice was exactly as it had been through the door — smooth, banked, patient. "And Miss Ashcombe. How convenient. Two birds, one fog."

"I'm afraid you have me confused with someone," Beatrix said. "I'm a chauffeur. This young lady is a nurse sailing to join her aunt in Portugal. If you'll excuse us—"

"There were forty-one letters," Quill said, conversationally, as though discussing the weather. "Copied last night in a house in Cavendish Row. The porter tells me the lamps burned till four o'clock. He tells me there was a typewriter. He tells me—" he smiled, showing teeth like piano keys, "—that you personally carried three satchels down to the cellar and up again. Now. I can't search a nurse's kit without cause, and causing a scene at this dock would inconvenience everyone, including my employer, who dislikes inconvenience even more than he dislikes you. So let us be civilized. Give me the copies, keep the originals or burn them or frame them, I genuinely don't care, and walk away rich. There is a version of today where you are on a train to Scotland by noon with four hundred pounds and no history."

Beatrix heard herself answer in the flat, pleasant voice she used for parking disputes and difficult tradesmen. "I don't know anything about any letters."

"No," Quill agreed softly. "You wouldn't. That's rather the tragedy of your sort. You spend so long being unreadable that when the moment comes to be read, you've forgotten how."

Something in her chest went very quiet and very cold, the way it did before she steamed open a seal.

"You misunderstand my employment," she said. "I don't forget things. I keep them. And I have kept ten years of Lady Constance Hale's correspondence — every letter, every draft, every indiscretion committed to paper by every fool in this movement, including Lady Constance herself. Including things she wrote in anger at your employer's friends. Including things that would end three marriages, two careers, and quite possibly the entire parliamentary alliance your office spent four years cultivating." She stepped closer. Fog beaded on Quill's lapels. "You want the copies because they prove the smear against the Ashcombes. But understand what else is in my head, Mr. Quill. If anything happens to that girl, to that satchel, or to me — if we are arrested, detained, delayed, or drowned — the rest of the archive goes to a barrister named Hartley with instructions and a schedule, and your employer learns what it costs to make an enemy of a woman whose entire profession is knowing what must never be repeated."

The dock sounds went on around them — winches, gulls, a mate shouting numbers — but within the six feet of fog between them there was a silence like held breath.

"You'd ruin her," Quill said. "Your own lady. That's not loyalty."

"It's testimony," Beatrix said. "It's the only currency I have ever had, and I am spending all of it this morning."

* * *

Ada watched the exchange the way she watched a surgeon's hands: terrified, fascinated, unable to look away.

She had not known Miss Delacroix could speak like that. She had known her for a week — really known her, since the cellar, since the drawers, since the night of lamplight and copying — and in that week she had catalogued the woman the way she catalogued patients: competent, guarded, kind in practical ways, allergic to praise. She had not seen the steel underneath. Or perhaps she had; perhaps that was why she had come to Cavendish Row instead of going to her mother. Invisible women recognized each other. It was the one advantage invisibility conferred.

Now Quill's eyes flicked to her, and Ada made herself stand straighter in her uniform.

"And you," Quill said. "Miss Ashcombe. Your father signed papers yesterday morning. Your passage was arranged to keep you quiet and comfortable. You could have had Switzerland."

"My father signed what your man put in front of him after your man bought our solicitor," Ada said. "I've read the correspondence. I know how bought solicitors behave. My mother doesn't know yet, and when she does, she will write to every newspaper in England, because unlike my father she has never once mistaken comfort for safety." She lifted her chin. "You fabricated the scandal, Mr. Quill. I merely declined to carry it."

A ship's bell rang somewhere above them, muffled by fog. Seven o'clock. Forty minutes to the tide.

Quill was silent for a long moment. Then he took off his hat, which somehow was more frightening than anything he'd done with it on, and looked at Beatrix with something that was almost professional respect.

"Four hundred pounds," he said. "Or a cell. Those remain the options."

"One option remains," Beatrix said. "You walk away, and in eighteen months, when this is settled and your clerk has resigned and the Ashcombe name is clean, you discover that I kept my word — that I never breathed a syllable of the rest of it. Because I don't break seals, Mr. Quill. Not even yours." She paused, letting the fog carry it. "Unless you give me reason to."

Ada saw the calculation move behind Quill's eyes like a fish under ice. He was a man who counted letters; he understood arithmetic. Four hundred pounds bought silence once. Testimony, sworn, scheduled, distributed, bought it forever — and cost his employer everything either way. The copies existed; the porter had seen to that certainty. What remained negotiable was only the shape of the disaster.

"You're bluffing about the schedule," Quill said.

"Yes," Beatrix agreed pleasantly. "But you can't afford to find out."

Another bell. Closer now. The fog was thinning at the edges, going from wool to gauze, and Ada could see the gangway clearly, and the purser at its head checking tickets, and beyond him the rail of the *Calypso* with its wet rope fenders knocking gently against the hull, patient as everything nautical, indifferent as the tide itself.

Quill put his hat back on.

"The Home Office," he said, "will deny everything, and the papers will print 'anonymous correspondents,' and no one will ever know your name, Miss Delacroix. That is your victory. I hope it keeps you warm."

"It's not my victory," Beatrix said. "It's the record's. I was never the point."

Quill looked at her a moment longer — and Ada, watching, saw him decide. Saw the exact instant the arithmetic finished. He inclined his head a fraction, the smallest possible concession, turned on his heel, and walked into the fog toward the bollard where his colleague waited. The porter melted away after him, already composing his apology to Mrs. Jennings.

Ada discovered she was shaking. "You threatened to destroy Lady Constance."

"I threatened to tell the truth about her, at the right moment, to the right people, which is nearly the same thing and considerably more effective." Beatrix's voice was level, but her hand, when it closed around Ada's arm to steer her toward the gangway, trembled like a plucked string. "Come along, Nurse. You're wanted aboard."

* * *

The purser barely glanced at them. Ada's uniform worked its magic a final time — a nod, a stamp, a gesture toward the second-class companionway — and then they were on the gangway itself, the satchel heavy between them, carried jointly, neither woman willing to surrender her grip on it.

Halfway up, the fog tore.

It came apart all at once, the way it does on rivers, and the sun came through low and hard, laying a path of hammered gold across the water, and Beatrix stopped dead in the middle of the gangway because below them, on the quay, a motorcar had just drawn up — the Austin's twin in everything but colour — and out of it stepped Lady Constance Hale, hatless, coat buttoned wrong, moving faster than Beatrix had ever seen her move in ten years of service.

"Lady Constance," Beatrix said, uselessly, to no one, to the air.

"She knows," Ada breathed. "How does she *know*?"

Constance reached the foot of the gangway just as a steward began to lift the rope. She looked up — past the sailors, past the purser's protest, past the whole machinery of departure — and found Beatrix's eyes with the terrible accuracy of a woman who had been lied to expertly for a decade and had, sometime in the last four hours, understood precisely what that meant.

"Beatrix Delacroix!" Her voice cracked across the water like a whip. "You will stand there and explain yourself, or so help me—"

The gangway shuddered. Began to rise.

Behind them the ship's engines woke, a vibration climbing up through the plates into their shoes; ahead of them the gold path on the water ran all the way to the horizon; below them Constance stood at the edge of the quay with the rising gap widening between them inch by inch, shouting words the wind was already stealing.

Ada gripped the satchel. Beatrix gripped Ada. Neither moved.

"Well?" Ada shouted over the engines, half laughing, half weeping. "Do we jump, or do we sail?"

Beatrix looked down at the woman she had served, and protected, and deceived, and saved — all four, always all four — and tightened her hold on the rail as the deck tilted toward the sea.
