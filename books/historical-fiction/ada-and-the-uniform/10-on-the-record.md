## 10. On the Record

The affidavit arrived on a Tuesday in March, folded inside a solicitor's envelope that smelled of pipe smoke and sealing wax, and Beatrix read it standing at the window of her rented room while the gas lamp guttered and the rain wrote its own longhand against the glass.

*In the matter of Ashcombe v. The Crown,* it began, and went on for eleven pages in the barrister's careful hand—Mr. Ellery Voss of Lincoln's Inn, who had once been the recipient of a letter Lady Constance had never known was written, because Beatrix had read it first and judged him safe. Ten years of reading had taught her which men could be trusted with paper. It had also taught her that trust was a currency best spent quickly, before fear could devalue it.

The affidavit named the clerk. Mr. Ambrose Pell, Home Office, second registry, seventeen years' service—a man who counted letters the way misers counted coins, who had understood long before anyone else in Whitehall that the suffrage movement's true weakness was not its marches or its window-breaking but its correspondence: the delicate web of patrons and subscriptions and whispered alliances that held it together. Pell had fabricated the smear against the Ashcombes with nothing more than a forged subscription record, a bribed solicitor, and the unassailable authority of an official frank.

And he might have succeeded, Beatrix thought, folding the pages along their creases until they were small enough to hold in one fist. He would have succeeded, if a driver had not steamed open an envelope at midnight and decided, for the first time in ten years, to break her silence.

* * *

The papers got it wrong, of course. They always did; she had learned that too, over a decade of watching her mistress's speeches transcribed into something softer and more palatable than they'd been spoken. The *Times* ran three paragraphs on page five beneath the headline ASHCOMBE SMEAR EXPOSED — HOME OFFICE CLERK RESIGNS, and attributed the evidence to "anonymous correspondents close to the movement." The *Daily Mail* gave it four paragraphs and a photograph of Ada Ashcombe in her nurse's uniform, looking grave and beautiful on the steps of some hospital or other, and did not mention correspondents at all.

Anonymous. Beatrix read the word and felt something turn over in her chest—not bitterness, exactly, though there was a grain of that, sharp as a seed coat. Something older than bitterness. Recognition. She had spent ten years being anonymous by design. She had made an art of it: the unreadable face behind the wheel of the Daimler, the invisible hands sorting post, the woman no one looked at twice because she had trained them not to. Invisibility had been her uniform, her armour, her wages.

But anonymous correspondents. Plural. As if the truth had simply condensed out of the air, out of the collective conscience of a nation, rather than out of one woman's cellar archive and another woman's sworn testimony and a third woman's willingness to stand in a solicitor's office in Southsea and state her name aloud for the record—*Ada Constance Ashcombe, voluntary aid detachment nurse*—while her mother sat white-faced beside her, hearing for the first time what her daughter had done and nearly what it had cost.

Beatrix set down the newspaper and looked out the window at the wet street. Somewhere south of here, on a ship that had docked in Cherbourg and then gone on to safer harbours, the original archive sat in oilcloth and a steamer trunk, copied four times over, lodged with barristers and a sympathetic MP and, at Ada's insistence, one copy sealed and addressed to *the future*, whatever that meant. The girl had a talent for the dramatic gesture that her mother would have denied and demonstrated daily. Beatrix found she missed it.

She missed more than that, if she were honest, and honesty had been the whole lesson of the winter.

* * *

Lady Constance had dismissed her in February, in the morning room, with the curtains open—which was itself a message, because Constance Hale conducted painful business behind drawn curtains as a rule, privacy being to her what water was to fish.

"I can't keep you," she said. "You understand that I can't."

"Yes," Beatrix said.

"What you did—" Constance stopped, pressed her lips together, started again. Beatrix watched her do it and thought: *ten years, and I have never seen her search for words.* It was oddly moving. It was also, she suspected, the closest thing to an apology the woman possessed.

"You read my letters for a decade," Constance said. "You destroyed at least two that I know of now—you told me yourself. You let me believe I was answering men who had already been answered by you." Her voice didn't rise. It lowered, which was worse. "I don't even know which of my convictions are mine."

"Both of the letters you know about were blackmail," Beatrix said. "You'll remember the movement survived both senders."

"That is not the point."

"No," Beatrix agreed quietly. "It isn't. The point is that you're dismissing me, and I'm going, and we both know why, and we also both know that without the cellar you'd be giving this interview from Holloway."

Constance's chin came up. For a moment she looked every inch the platform speaker, the woman who could hold three thousand strangers in her palm—and then the look faltered, cracked, became something smaller and truer.

"The affidavit," she said. "Your name isn't on it."

"No."

"Why not? Voss offered. Ada told me he offered."

Beatrix had asked herself the same question for six weeks, turning it over like a stone in a pocket. The truthful answer was complicated. Part of it was habit—the reflex of concealment worn so long it had grown into the bone. Part of it was protection: an unnamed source could not be prosecuted under the Official Secrets Act, could not be sued, could not be made into a cautionary tale at suffrage meetings across three counties. And part of it—she admitted this now, alone, to the rain-streaked glass—was that putting her name on the document felt like a door she didn't yet have the key to. She had been Beatrix-in-relation-to-Constance for so long that *Beatrix Fairweather, correspondent* sounded like someone else entirely. A stranger she hadn't been introduced to.

"She wanted me to sign," Beatrix said. "Ada. Very fiercely."

"And?"

"And I told her the truth keeps better in duplicate." Which had been only half evasion. The girl had looked at her with those grey Ashcombe eyes—so like her mother's and nothing like them at all—and said, *One day you'll want your name on something, Miss Delacroix. When you find it, don't hesitate.* Seventeen years old, and issuing instructions like a dowager duchess. Beatrix had laughed for the first time in months.

Constance stood, and Beatrix rose too, and for a suspended moment the two women regarded each other across the morning room—across ten years of driven miles and sorted secrets and the particular intimacy of people who have kept each other's dangers.

"You were the best servant I ever had," Constance said finally.

"Madam," said Beatrix, "I was never your servant."

A pause. Then—almost imperceptibly, so slightly that a lesser observer would have missed it, but Beatrix had made a profession of the imperceptible—Constance smiled. "No," she said. "I begin to see that you weren't."

At the door, Beatrix paused. "There's a copy of everything," she said. "Voss has it. If you ever need it—if the movement needs it—it will surface. That much I can promise from outside the house as well as inside it."

Constance looked at her for a long moment. "That," she said, "is the most loyal disloyal thing anyone has ever said to me."

* * *

The office was on the first floor of a narrow building off Chancery Lane, above a stationer's who sold ink by the pint and complained cheerfully about the smell of carbolic from the chemist next door. Nine pounds a quarter. One window, north-facing, good light. A second-hand Remington that Beatrix had stripped, cleaned, and re-ribboned herself, because a decade of driving and archiving taught a person that machines reward attention and punish neglect.

She had painted the door herself, on a Sunday, in a grey-green that the stationer swore was fashionable. She had cut the lettering from gold leaf with a scalpel borrowed from Ada—who had appeared that weekend unannounced, still in her nurse's cloak, smelling faintly of hospital soap, and taken over the whole operation with the breezy tyranny of the young.

"Taller," Ada commanded. "The F is crooked. Honestly, Miss Delacroix, you'd think a woman who forged a Home Office cipher could paint a straight stem."

"I never forged anything," Beatrix said, repainting the F. "I only ever read."

"You altered a letter to Sir Gerald Pemberton in 1908."

"You've been talking to Voss."

"Voss talks to everyone. That's rather his gift." Ada sat back on her heels, gold leaf clinging to her fingers like pollen, and studied the door. "Fairweather &—no. Just your name. Good." Then, quieter, in the voice she saved for things that mattered: "Mother wants you to come back, you know. Not as staff. She said—as a colleague. She used the word *colleague*. I thought the house would fall down."

Beatrix kept her eyes on the lettering. "And what did you tell her?"

"That you'd opened your own office and were far too grand for the likes of us." Ada grinned. "Also that you'd say no, and that saying no would do her good. She's spent thirty years being indispensable. Someone should teach her how it feels to be refused."

"You are a vindictive creature, Miss Ashcombe."

"I'm a witness," Ada said serenely. "It's different."

They finished the door as the light went amber. BEATRIX FAIRWEATHER — TYPEWRITING, COPYING, CORRESPONDENCE UNDERTAKEN. Ada insisted on adding DISCREET, and Beatrix, after a struggle, allowed it, because discretion had after all been her trade, and there was no shame in advertising honestly.

* * *

On the first Monday of April she unlocked the door at half past eight and found the morning's first letter waiting under it—slipped through overnight, the envelope cheap and smudged with coal dust. No frank. No crest. A woman's handwriting, hurried, the kind of hand that writes by candlelight after the household sleeps.

*Dear Madam,—I am told you are a person who understands letters. I have ones I dare not burn and dare not send. They concern my husband's brother, who is a magistrate. I am told you helped the Ashcombes when no one else would touch it. I have nowhere else.*

Beatrix stood on the pavement a long moment, the letter in her gloved hands, the street waking around her—a milk cart, a boy with newspapers, the stationer dragging up his blind overhead. Anonymous correspondent, indeed. There were more of them out there than Whitehall had ever dreamed: all the unread, the steamed-open, the silently edited, keeping other people's histories in cellars and hatboxes and the locked drawers of other women's houses.

She had spent ten years learning that knowledge was a burden. It had taken one winter to learn it was power. Now, standing before her own name on her own door, she understood the third lesson, the one neither burden nor power had prepared her for: that testimony shared was neither of those things at all, but something rarer. Company.

She unlocked the office, lit the lamp, and began to write back.
