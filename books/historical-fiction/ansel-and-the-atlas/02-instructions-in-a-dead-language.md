## 2. Instructions in a Dead Language

The safehouse was a cobbler's shop on Vessel Street with a false wall behind the lasts and a landlady who asked no questions because she had learned, somewhere in her long life, that questions were the first thing to bleed you.

Sable arrived at dusk, when the lamps were lit but the shutters not yet closed—the hour when a woman carrying a satchel looked like any other woman going home. She had walked the last two kilometers instead of taking the tram, doubling back twice through the fish market, and she stood for four full minutes in the doorway of the chandler's across the street watching the shop's front window before she crossed. Nothing moved that should not move. The chalk mark above the doorframe—a small crescent, half-erased by rain—was still the old mark, which meant no one inside had been made to draw a new one under someone else's eyes.

She let herself in with the key hidden in the drainpipe, as if she lived there, because for these purposes she did. Living somewhere was only a matter of what you could leave behind without grief. She could leave behind everything in this room. That was how she knew it was safe.

Marta was in the back, boiling something that smelled of cabbage and stubbornness. She glanced up, took in the satchel, the mud on Sable's boots, the particular set of her shoulders, and said nothing except, "There's bread."

"I ate."

"You didn't." Marta turned back to her pot. "There's bread anyway."

Sable took it. This was their entire ritual: an accusation of hunger, a surrender, no names exchanged beyond the ones they both knew were false. Marta had called herself Marta so long that perhaps it had grown true, the way a shoe grows to a foot. Sable understood this. She had been Sable for six years now, and some mornings she woke and reached for that name before any other, the way a hand reaches for the bedpost in the dark.

The room upstairs was narrow and cold and perfect. One cot, one table, one lamp with a shade of green glass that turned the light the color of pond water—good light for reading, bad light for being read. She bolted the door, wedged the chair beneath the knob out of habit rather than fear, and sat.

Then she took out the book.

It was a child's primer, *First Words for Little Hands*, printed eleven years ago by a press that had since been melted down for shell casings. The occupation's censors had examined it a hundred times in a hundred satchels and found nothing but the alphabet, because there was nothing but the alphabet—unless you knew that the republic's cipher bureau had chosen its dead language carefully, and that the marginalia in this edition, the little practice sentences in Old Vessic, were not practice sentences at all when read against the correct page of the correct primer held at the correct angle beside a window at the correct hour.

Old Vessic had been dead for two centuries. No one spoke it. No one dreamed in it. It existed now only in church inscriptions and in the heads of perhaps forty people, most of whom were dead or had fled, and in the cipher bureau's conviction that a language nobody living remembered was a language nobody living would think to break. So far they had been right. Sable did not love the republic enough to call it wisdom, but she respected it the way she respected ice on a river: it held until it didn't.

She opened the primer to the ribbon she kept knotted at page thirty-four, laid yesterday's intercept beside it—a greasy fold of paper bought off a laundry cart in Halven Bridge—and began.

The work itself was simple once you had done it a thousand times. The Old Vessic words in the margins were numbered by their position; the numbers keyed to letters; the letters, read in threes, became instructions. The difficulty was never the mechanics. The difficulty was keeping your hands steady while your mind ran ahead of them, because your mind always ran ahead, and sometimes what it found there was worse than anything the checkpoint soldiers could have done to your body.

*Vessel. Vessel. Anchor.* Three words in. Her hands stopped.

She read it again, thinking she had miskeyed, thinking the laundry man's paper had been smudged, thinking a hundred small technical things, because technical things were comfortable and the alternative was to understand what she was reading.

*Vessel. Vessel. Anchor.*

In the bureau's vocabulary, *vessel* meant the capital. Everyone knew the capital's code name; it was a joke older than the war, from the days when the city still thought of itself as a ship that weather could not touch. And *anchor*—

*Anchor* meant fallen. Not threatened, not besieged, not holding. Fallen. There was a separate word for *holding*, and a separate word for *lost*, and the bureau used them with a miser's precision, because every word in a dispatch was a word that could kill someone if intercepted. They did not spend *anchor* lightly. In six years of carrying, Sable had decoded it exactly once before, for a garrison town whose name she had made herself forget within the week.

She forced herself through the rest. It was short. Short dispatches were either good news or news too large for elaboration, and this was not good news.

*Vessel anchor. All stations proceed to extraction protocol. Courier 7 to port city on receipt. Manifest authority transferred. Hold berth allocation pending final tally. Burn all prior instructions. Trust the tide, not the chart.*

She read it four times. On the fourth reading her hands were steady again, which surprised her, and then did not surprise her, because steadiness was the thing she had trained into herself the way other women trained in needlework or prayer. Steadiness was the whole of her craft. A courier who trembled died at the first checkpoint; a courier who trembled later, alone, in a locked room, simply trembled, and no one ever knew.

But underneath the steadiness, something had shifted—not broken, not yet, but shifted, the way a house shifts on its foundations in a flood, settling into a new and worse geometry.

The capital had fallen.

She had known it would. Everyone had known it would, the way you know a dying man will die; knowledge is not the same as readiness. For six years the capital had been the fixed point of her world, the place the orders came from, the place the money came from, the place the war was supposedly going to turn around. She had never loved it. She had never even seen it. But she had built her life on the assumption of it, the way a compass needle builds its meaning on the pole, and now the pole was gone and the needle swung in a slow, stupid circle, pointing at nothing.

*All stations proceed to extraction protocol.*

Extraction. The word the bureau used when a network was being folded up like a map after the campaign was lost. Extraction meant ships. Extraction meant lists. Extraction meant that the republic, which had spent six years asking her to carry its secrets forward, was now asking her to help carry its people out.

She got up and stood at the window, looking down through the green-tinted gap in the shutter at Vessel Street, where a lamplighter was making his rounds and a dog was worrying something dead in the gutter and the ordinary evening went on being ordinary, because the street did not know yet. Streets never knew first. The news came to streets last of all, arriving like weather, unexplained, and by then it was simply the condition of life.

*Manifest authority transferred.* That was the part that snagged her, turning it over like a stone with something alive under it. Manifest authority meant berths. Berths meant the evacuation ships—the *Atlas* among them, if the rumors from the port were true—and the lists of names that determined who left and who stayed. She had carried manifests before, in the early years, when they were cargo manifests, bolts of cloth and crates of rifle oil. Now the cargo would be people, and the paper in her coat hem would decide, in its small indifferent way, who breathed free air next month and who breathed whatever came after.

*Hold berth allocation pending final tally.* Pending. The bureau loved that word. Pending meant the decision had not been made, which meant the decision would be made by whoever held the paper last, which meant her.

She sat back down at the table and made herself look at the sentence she had been avoiding.

*Trust the tide, not the chart.*

It was a bureau idiom, old as the service. The chart was the plan, the doctrine, the beautiful architecture of the operation. The tide was what actually moved. When the bureau told a courier to trust the tide, it was an admission, dressed as advice, that the chart was finished—that the plans had come apart somewhere above her head and the only honest instruction left was: adapt, survive, don't wait for us to tell you how.

They had never sent her that sentence before. In six years, never once. The bureau would send a courier into a burning building with a complete set of diagrams before it admitted the building was burning. And here it was, in a dead language, on a scrap of laundry paper: *we are done planning; do what keeps you alive.*

Sable discovered that she was angry. It came up in her slowly, like water rising in a locked hold, and she examined it with professional interest because anger was data. She was not angry that the capital had fallen—she had priced that possibility years ago, folded it into her calculations along with her own probable death. She was angry at the tone of the dispatch. Its calmness. Its tidiness. Somewhere in a cellar or a ship's cabin, some clerk of the collapsing state had composed these lines with the same care he had composed victory bulletins, and had sealed them, and had sent them out to forty couriers in forty cities, and each of those couriers would sit tonight in a rented room decoding the end of everything, and the clerk would sleep.

Or maybe the clerk was dead already. Maybe the dispatch had been written by a machine of procedure running on after its operators stopped, the way a heart can beat for minutes in a body that has otherwise finished. Maybe no one had decided anything. That was somehow worse.

She did what the instructions said. She burned the prior orders—one year of them, folded into the lining of her spare blouse, each a small fossil of a mission that had mattered enormously at the time and now mattered not at all. The primer's pages took the flame grudgingly; she fed them to the stove in Marta's kitchen downstairs, feeding the fire bit by bit so the smoke stayed thin, and Marta watched her from the doorway with the pot spoon in her hand and said nothing, because Marta had seen couriers burn paper before and knew the ritual required silence the way a funeral required it.

When it was done, Sable washed her hands. There was no ash on them, but she washed them anyway, thoroughly, up to the wrists, and dried them on the rough towel, and stood a moment with her palms flat against the basin's rim.

"You'll be going to the port, then," Marta said. Not a question. Landladies of safehouses read dispatches in the way a person's shoulders burn paper.

"When I'm told."

"You've been told."

Sable almost smiled. "You decode very well for a woman who asks no questions."

"I ask plenty." Marta gathered her pot and her spoon. "I just don't expect answers. There's bread on the board. Eat it before the mice do, they've got more sense than either of us."

Upstairs again, the green light, the cold room. Sable took out a fresh sheet of the thin paper she used for copies and began, in her small round hand—the hand of a schoolteacher, a widow, a woman named Verena Kolb who sold notions and had no history—to draft her cover story for the port. A notion-seller traveling to restock. Papers in order, transit stamp expiring soon, a legitimate reason to be moving toward the harbor district. She built the woman piece by piece, the way she always did: a limp acquired from a childhood fall, a sister in the port city, a habit of complaining about her feet. Verena Kolb was easy to wear. Verena Kolb had no past heavier than a bad knee.

And yet, halfway through the second page, Sable stopped writing.

Because she had caught herself doing something new. In the margin of her draft, without deciding to, she had written three words in Old Vessic—the old alphabet, the churchyard letters—and they were not part of any cover story. They were a question, addressed to no one:

*Who stays?*

She looked at it for a long time. Then she struck it through, once, hard enough to tear the paper slightly, and told herself it was fatigue. Couriers did not ask questions; couriers carried answers they were not permitted to open. That was the covenant. That was the armor. You carried the weight and never weighed yourself, and in exchange you survived, and survival was the whole of the ambition, and she had believed it for six years with the clean, total belief of a woman who has burned every other option.

But the dispatch had changed the terms. *Extraction protocol. Manifest authority. Berth allocation.* These were not the words of carrying. They were the words of choosing. Somewhere ahead of her, in the port city, on the decks of a ship named for a god who held up the world, there would be a list, and the list would have names on it, and she would be the one holding the paper.

She blew out the lamp and lay on the cot in the dark, listening to the wind work at the shutters, and found that her certainty—which had served her through checkpoints and interrogations and the deaths of three colleagues whose faces she had also made herself forget—had developed its first crack. Small. Hairline. Running from the word *anchor* to the word *berth*, straight through the middle of everything she had built.

Outside, far to the south, where the capital had been, the sky glowed faintly, and she could not tell whether it was fire or just the last light of a country refusing, for one more night, to admit it was over.

Two days later, on a morning of iron-gray frost, someone knocked at the safehouse door—three soft knocks, a pause, then none—and when Sable opened it, there was no one there at all.

Only a bundle on the step, wrapped in a man's coat.

And it was breathing.
