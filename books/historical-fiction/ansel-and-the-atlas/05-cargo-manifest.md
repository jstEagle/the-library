## 5. Cargo Manifest

The road to Vessengard smelled of salt and diesel, and beneath both, faintly, of burning—something far inland, something the wind had carried a hundred miles to die against the harbor. Sable rode in the back of a fish truck between crates of herring packed in ice, her breath fogging, her hands folded on the dispatch case strapped flat against her ribs like a second sternum. The driver had not spoken to her. She had paid him not to, which was, she had found, the most honest transaction available in the occupied territories: money for silence, no pretense of friendship, no debt that could later be called.

She had learned the port city the way she learned everything—before she needed it. Three years ago, bored on a winter layover in a safehouse with nothing but a smuggler's atlas and a bad lamp, she had memorized Vessengard street by street: which alleys dead-ended at garrison walls, which taverns sold passage papers along with beer, which customs clerks took bribes and which took notes for the occupier's intelligence bureau instead. Most couriers trusted their legs. Sable trusted her memory. Legs got tired. Memory only betrayed you if you fed it lies, and she had been careful about what she let herself believe.

The truck slowed at the harbor checkpoint. Through a gap in the canvas she watched the sentries' lanterns swing, saw the driver pass across his papers, heard the wet slap of a bayonet testing the tarpaulin. The guard's shadow fell across the herring crates. Sable did not hold her breath; holding your breath made your face go strange. She thought, instead, about the price of cod, a thing so banal it could not be mistaken for fear.

The bayonet withdrew. The truck lurched forward into the city.

* * *

Vessengard had once been beautiful in the way that ports are beautiful—gulls and rope and the great bronze cranes standing over the quay like patient herons. Now the cranes were hung with searchlights, the customs house flew a flag she refused to look at directly, and every wall within a mile of the water bore fresh orders in two languages, the occupying tongue printed larger, as though the city itself were hard of hearing.

EVACUATION BY PERMIT ONLY, the posters said. THE SHIP ATLAS DEPARTS ON THE TWELFTH. UNAUTHORIZED PERSONS FOUND ABOARD WILL BE REMOVED.

*Removed.* Sable appreciated the word. It was honest, in its way. Everyone in the queue outside the shipping office knew what removal meant at a gangway with soldiers at either end. They queued anyway. That was the thing about desperation—it did not make people clever. It made them patient in long lines, visible, catalogued, each one pressing a paper or a story or a bribe toward the clerk behind the grille like flowers offered to someone who had already chosen his favorites.

Sable did not join the queue. She walked past it, around the corner into Rope Lane, up three stairs to a door marked HALLORAN & SON, MARINE SURVEYORS, and knocked twice, paused, then once.

The man who opened the door was sixty if he was a day, with a surveyor's squint and hands stained permanently with ink and tar. Halloran—or whatever his name had been before the republic gave him a new one—looked at her for exactly as long as it takes to count money, then stood aside.

"You're late," he said.

"The roads are late," Sable said. "I'm precisely on time."

He grunted. Inside, among charts and brass instruments and a smell of old varnish, he poured tea neither of them drank and laid out the situation with the economy of a man who billed by the word.

"Atlas sails on the twelfth. Nine hundred tons, registered grain carrier, refitted last month for passengers they'll call *technical staff*. Four hundred berths on paper." He tapped the manifest with one tarred finger. "Two hundred and sixty berths in fact. The difference went to officers' families, dock administrators, and anyone whose cousin knows the harbormaster's mistress. You understand the arithmetic."

"I understand arithmetic," Sable said.

"Your name is on the list. Third revision." He slid a paper across the desk: a typed line amid dozens, *Vera Kessler, cartographic technician, priority B-2.* Her false name looked strange and small, a coat she'd worn so long it fit better than her skin. "But a name on a revised manifest is worth what a promissory note is worth from a government that's losing the war. There will be more revisions. Every revision cuts. The question is whether you're still standing when they stop cutting."

"How do I keep standing?"

Halloran leaned back. "You misunderstand how this works, courier. The manifest isn't a document. It's a currency. Favors owed, favors called. The harbormaster's signature is worth ten berths, and everyone who helped him rise is collecting. The purser sells 'lost' places to whoever can pay and forgets them for whoever can pay more. The military liaison stamps priority on whomever the garrison commander dined with last. Nobody aboard that ship has a right to be there. They have a *price*, and the price keeps rising as the departure gets closer."

Sable turned the paper over, as if the reverse might contain something truer. "And mine?"

"Yours came through the ministry chain. Which means it's real today and rumor on Thursday." He studied her with the frank appraisal of a man estimating tonnage. "You want my advice? Don't be a name on a list. Be useful to someone who writes lists."

"I don't make friends," Sable said. "It's a policy."

"I'm not suggesting friendship. I'm suggesting leverage. Friendship is what people call leverage afterward, when it's gone soft." He pushed the tea aside. "There's a reception tomorrow night. Harbormaster Duvall. He collects rare maps—the old empire surveys, pre-partition. Flatters himself a scholar. Half the officers attending will be angling for evacuation priority themselves, which tells you everything: even the men running the docks intend to leave. Find him a map he doesn't have. Become the woman who found him a map. Names get cut from manifests, courier. Debts don't."

"And where does one find a pre-partition imperial survey in a country that's being eaten?"

Halloran smiled for the first time, showing teeth stained the color of strong tea. "One asks the right thief."

* * *

She spent the afternoon becoming Vera Kessler completely. It was a discipline, an actor's discipline, and she performed it in the public rooms of the Harborlight Hotel because the hotel was where a cartographic technician would stay, and because visibility in the correct place was the best camouflage she owned. She sat in the lobby with survey folios and made marginal notes in pencil. She ate dinner alone and left a tip calculated to be remembered without being remarkable. She asked the concierge—one question, mildly, the way a tired professional asks—whether Herr Duvall's collection might be accessible to a visiting specialist. The concierge's eyes flickered with the small greed of all gatekeepers, and Sable noted it, filed it, moved on.

In her room, she unlocked the dispatch case and checked the codes out of habit, counting seals the way other people counted spoons. All present. All silent. The case held the republic's remaining secrets and weighed almost nothing, which had always struck her as the central obscenity of her profession: entire districts, whole networks, lives arranged in columns—and it all lifted with one hand.

She caught herself thinking of the safehouse. Of Marta's kitchen. Of—

She stopped the thought the way she'd been trained to stop thoughts, cleanly, at the root. But it came back anyway, wearing a different shape: the boy, Tomas, sitting at Marta's table that morning with bread and milk he didn't touch until she nodded at him, watching Sable pack with those gray, unblinking eyes. He hadn't spoken. Not once in nine days. Marta said trauma, said wait. Sable said nothing, but she had begun, without deciding to, to narrate her departures aloud in front of him—*I'll be back Thursday, three days, watch the blue coat for me*—as though a mute child were a colleague who required briefing. As though he were hers to brief.

That was the danger. Not attachment—attachment she could refuse—but *habit*. The slow formation of duties she had never agreed to. She had built her life on the principle that you carry only what you can abandon, and now there was a boy in her ledger who had never signed anything, and she could feel the weight of him already, light as paper and just as hard to put down.

*Three days,* she told him in her head. *I said three days.*

She closed the case.

* * *

The map cost her four hundred crowns, a favor owed to her by a forger named Ilse who preferred repayment in blank occupation permits, and one hour of genuinely unpleasant negotiation in the back room of a bookbinder's shop that smelled of glue and fear. The seller was an archivist who had looted his own institution during the fall of the capital and now dealt in the republic's scattered memory. The map he produced—a hydrographic survey of the northern coast, 1898, hand-tinted, the empire's crest stamped in faded gold—was, he claimed, the finest thing left in Vessengard.

"It's theft," Sable said.

"It's salvage," he said. "Everything now is salvage. The only question is who gets to the wreck first." He wrapped it in oilcloth with the tenderness of a man handling a relic. "Duvall has the 1901 edition. Incomplete. This one has the soundings the navy expunged after the partition. He'll know what he's looking at. Men like him always do—that's the tragedy of them. Connoisseurs of a country they helped bury."

At the reception the next evening, in the harbormaster's residence with its looted chandeliers and its orchestra playing waltzes slightly too fast, Sable wore the blue coat and the face of Vera Kessler and felt the old machinery of her craft turning smoothly, gear after gear. She presented the map to Duvall in a lull between toasts, watched his hands go still over the oilcloth, watched the connoisseur wake behind the bureaucrat's eyes.

"Where," he said quietly, "did you find this?"

"In a drawer," Sable said, "belonging to a man who no longer needs drawers."

Duvall laughed—a real laugh, brief, startled out of him—and something in his face aged and softened at once. "Cartographic technician," he said, reading her off the guest list in his mind. "Kessler. Yes, you're on my revised manifest. Priority B-2." He rolled the map closed with reverent fingers. "I'll tell you what nobody tells you, Fräulein Kessler. This ship leaves on the twelfth whether the list is finished or not. And the list will never be finished. So between tonight and the twelfth, that paper is going to bleed names. Mine won't be among them." He met her eyes, and for one unguarded moment he looked less like a functionary than like a drowning man describing the water. "Make sure yours isn't. Come see me again before Thursday. Bring me something else. I find I enjoy owing you."

*I find I enjoy owing you.* Sable turned the sentence over all the way back to the hotel, past the sentries, past the poster on the customs house wall—THE SHIP ATLAS DEPARTS ON THE TWELFTH—while the orchestra's tempo chased her down the dark street like a pulse.

A berth. A real one, anchored in a powerful man's gratitude rather than a ministry's fading ink. Ambition, she reminded herself, was just desperation with better posture, and she had never been ashamed of either. She had a map-man's debt, a false name, a deadline, and a plan.

It was only when she reached her room and found the hotel clerk's message slip waiting on the tray—a single line, unsigned, in handwriting she did not recognize—that the evening's smooth machinery jammed.

*Your landlady says the boy talks now.*
