## 6. A Face She Burned

The house on Verlaine Street had a staircase that sang.

Sable knew this before she knew most things—before she knew the names of rivers or the shape of the republic on a schoolroom map, she knew that the fourth stair from the bottom groaned in a minor key if you stepped on its left edge, and that Livia could always avoid it, moving through the dark like water finding its level, while Sable seemed constitutionally incapable of silence. Even as a child, even before the training, before the codes and the coat hems, her body betrayed her. She breathed too loudly. She laughed at the wrong hours. She wanted things too visibly to be trusted with them.

Their mother used to say: *Livia survives; Sable insists.*

It was meant as a compliment to Livia. Sable took it as a diagnosis.

* * *

The winter of the marriage proposal was the coldest in a decade, so cold the harbor froze gray and solid and men walked out onto it for sport until one went through the ice and the city remembered itself. Sable was nineteen that winter. She spent it in the back room of a bookbinder's shop on Corvus Lane, learning the trade her father had arranged for her—an honest trade, a quiet trade, glue and thread and bone folders, the smell of paste and leather. It was meant to keep her hands busy and her head down. Her father had two daughters and a modest fortune and a clear plan for each: Livia would marry well; Sable would work with paper.

He did not know yet how literally she would take him.

The Aldermans came to dinner on a Thursday in late February, when snow stood knee-deep in the gutters and the light died by four o'clock. Their son was named Emmerich—a broad, decent, unremarkable young man with his father's shipping interests and his mother's habit of looking slightly past whoever spoke to him. He was not cruel. That was the thing Sable could never make anyone understand later, when she tried to explain why the whole affair had felt like a betrayal. Emmerich Alderman was not cruel. He was not stupid or coarse or drunk. He was simply a door that closed, a room without windows, a life already furnished by other people's taste. When he smiled at Livia across the soup course, he smiled the way a man smiles at a purchase he has been assured is sound.

And Livia—Livia smiled back.

Sable watched from the foot of the table, seventeen months younger and invisible in the way that second daughters are, and she watched her sister perform the entire courtship in miniature over three courses: the lowered lashes, the laugh pitched just below full volume, the hand turned palm-up on the cloth as though offering something. Livia had always been beautiful the way certain buildings are beautiful—symmetrical, load-bearing, designed to be looked at and lived in. Men saw her and saw shelter. What they never saw, what almost no one saw except Sable, was the flicker behind her eyes when the conversation turned to the harbor routes, or the war taxes, or the new censorship office on Merchant Row—the flicker of a woman keeping count of everything she was agreeing to forget.

Afterward, in the bedroom they still shared, Livia sat at the mirror taking the pins from her hair, and Sable lay on her bed watching the ceiling and hating her.

"You didn't have to say yes," Sable said finally. "You haven't said yes. You can still say nothing."

"He hasn't asked yet."

"He will ask Thursday."

Livia's hands paused over her hair. In the mirror her face was composed, unreadable, which was itself an answer. "Father has asked me to consider it seriously."

"Father has asked you. Has anyone asked you what you want?"

"What I want." Livia said it softly, tasting the words, and set down a pin with a small precise click. "You want to know what I want? I want to stop being afraid of winters like this one. I want a house where the stove doesn't smoke and a husband whose debts aren't ours and a table with enough on it. Is that so contemptible to you?"

"It isn't enough."

"It's more than Mother ever had." The pins stopped entirely now. Livia turned on the stool, and in the mirror their eyes met, dark on dark, the family eyes, the same eyes they would spend the next decade pretending were not identical. "And it's more than you'll have, running messages for people who will use you up and bury you under some name that isn't yours."

Sable sat up. "I don't know what you mean."

"You think I don't see it?" Livia's voice stayed low, stayed even—that was her gift and her curse, she could fight in whispers. "The man who comes on Tuesdays asking Father to vouch for your 'apprenticeship.' The way you disappear on Sundays and come back with river mud on your hem when you told us you'd be at the archive. You think because I'm marrying a shipping clerk I'm blind?" She shook her head slowly, and something in her expression cracked—not anger, Sable realized. Fear. "They shoot couriers, Sable. Or worse. And no one ever knows what happened to them, because that's the entire point of them."

"I'm careful."

"You're nineteen."

"So were you when you started lying to me about Emmerich."

That landed—Sable saw it land, saw the hurt go in below the armor—and she was ashamed of it immediately and would remain ashamed of it for years, and she said it anyway, because she was nineteen and because the alternative to saying it was admitting what it actually meant: *don't leave me here alone with them.*

Livia turned back to the mirror. For a long time neither of them spoke. Downstairs their father coughed, the particular wet cough that had frightened Sable since childhood, and somewhere on the frozen street a patrol's boots rang out in the iron rhythm of the occupation's third year.

"He'll ask Thursday," Livia said at last, quietly. "And I'll say yes."

"Why?"

"Because someone in this family has to survive," Livia said, "and it was always going to be me. You made sure of that years ago, the first time you climbed out that window. There's only room in Father's heart for one daughter who breaks it. I decided it wouldn't be me."

She said it without accusation. That was the worst part. She said it like arithmetic, like weather, like a fact about ice on the harbor. And Sable lay back against the cold wall and understood, with a clarity she would carry into every safehouse and every checkpoint for the rest of her life, that her sister was choosing to be buried alive and calling it survival—and that Livia thought exactly the same thing about her.

* * *

Emmerich Alderman proposed on a Thursday in March, under the chandelier, with both mothers present and the good silver out, and Livia said yes in a voice like still water, and everyone wept appropriately except Sable, who stood by the sideboard holding a tray of glasses and feeling the floor tilt beneath her.

Later—years later, in another country, under another name—she would try to reconstruct that evening honestly, without the varnish of grievance. She would admit that Livia had hesitated. That between the question and the answer there had been a silence of perhaps four seconds, four seconds in which their father's knuckles whitened on his chair arm and their mother's smile froze into porcelain, and Livia's gaze crossed the room and found Sable's—found it, held it, pleaded with it, Sable saw that now, she had seen it then but had refused the plea because refusing it felt like winning—and that only after Sable had looked away, deliberately, coldly, down at the silver, did Livia say yes.

Four seconds. Sable had spent four seconds being her sister's last window, and she had shut it.

But that night, standing at the sideboard with the glasses ringing faintly against each other in her trembling grip, all she felt was fury—fury with a shape, a direction, an address. Fury at her father for selling safety as if it were an honor. At Emmerich for buying. At the occupation for making safety worth selling. And underneath all of it, in the sealed cellar of herself where she kept the things she could not look at directly: fury at Livia for proving that the world was exactly as narrow as their mother had always said, that there was no third option, no door marked otherwise, that a woman born on Verlaine Street chose between the cage and the grave and called whichever one she picked *sense*.

There was a third option. Sable had found it. It had a name, though she wasn't supposed to know it yet, and it smelled of lamp oil and wet wool and the particular chalky ink used on documents that never officially existed.

The man who came on Tuesdays—he had a different name each month; that year he was Mr. Ferrant—found her the following Sunday at the back of the bookbinder's shop, ostensibly checking the stitching on a repaired atlas. He did not ask if she was ready. He had learned, as she would learn, that readiness was a fiction; you were either already gone or you weren't.

"There's a route opening up," he said, turning a page of the atlas with one finger, not looking at her. "Northern line. Cold work. Bad roads. You'd carry things you can't read and meet people you'll never see twice. Your father would be told the apprenticeship fell through and you took a post abroad. He'd grieve privately. It's cleaner that way."

"My sister gets married in June," Sable said.

Ferrant turned a page. "Yes."

"If I'm gone by then, she'll know why."

"Yes."

"She'll hate me for it."

"She'll understand it eventually," Ferrant said. "Or she won't. That part isn't yours to manage anymore. That's the first lesson of the work, girl: once you cross, what people feel about you stops being information you're given. You give it up. All of it. Are you listening?"

She was looking at the atlas under his finger. An old plate, outdated borders, a coastline the cartographers had redrawn twice since the war began. Countries like faces; faces like countries; everything drawn and then redrawn by whoever held the pen that season.

"I'm listening," Sable said.

"The northern courier who holds the route now is being extracted. She lasted eleven months. The average is seven." Now he looked at her, and his eyes were the flat gray of the frozen harbor, and she understood that he was giving her the truth as a courtesy and a test at once. "You want to tell me why a bookbinder's daughter with a married sister and a warm bed wants that arithmetic?"

Because my sister is burying herself alive in June and calling it sense, and I would rather die in a ditch with my own name in my mouth than be interred upright in a drawing room with someone else's on the stone.

What she said was: "I'm careful."

Ferrant almost smiled. "So was she."

* * *

She left on the last night of May, three weeks before the wedding.

Her father believed she was traveling to a printing fellowship in Vellmar; the letter confirming it, forged in a hand indistinguishable from the ministry registrar's, sat on his desk where he would find it in the morning. Her mother believed the same, because her mother had long ago stopped believing anything strongly enough to check. The lie was a gift, in its way. It let them grieve a career instead of a corpse.

Only Livia would know. Only Livia would stand at the altar in white silk with the whole street watching and understand that the empty pew on the family side was not an accident, was a sentence, was the last argument of a fight they'd been having since February—and that Sable had chosen to deliver it on the one day Livia could not answer.

Sable packed one bag. This was the discipline she was proudest of, the one Ferrant had praised without praise: she owned little and carried less, and what she left behind she left without ceremony. The books she'd loved went into a crate for the shop. Her winter coat, the good one, stayed on its hook—she'd be issued another, anonymous, army-surplus, smelling of someone else's smoke. On the desk she left her copy of the household ledger, balanced to the last coin, because she could not help herself; order was the one inheritance from her father she couldn't burn.

At midnight she stood in the bedroom doorway and looked at Livia sleeping.

Her sister slept on her right side, always, one hand curled near her mouth, and in sleep her face lost all its architecture—all the symmetry, the composure, the beautiful load-bearing calm—and became simply a girl's face, young and undefended, the face from before the pins and the mirrors and the arithmetic of survival. Sable stood in the doorway for a long time, memorizing it. That was the training beginning already, though she didn't know it: *notice everything; keep what matters; let the rest go.*

She thought: *she'll wake and find the note and she'll know, and she'll hate me, and someday—one day, maybe—when the war does whatever the war is going to do to both of us—someday she'll understand that I didn't leave because I stopped loving her. I left because staying was starting to feel like dying, and I couldn't watch her call that living.*

She thought it, and did not write it. The note on the pillow said only:

*Liv — the apprenticeship ended early. Don't look for me. Burn this. — S.*

Two sentences. Everything else was cargo she couldn't afford.

On the stairs, she stepped on the fourth step from the bottom, deliberately, left edge, letting it sing its small minor note into the sleeping house—so that Livia, who slept lightly, who had always slept lightly, who could avoid that stair in perfect darkness, would hear it and know that her sister was passing below her window forever. Let her wake. Let her come to the glass and see the figure crossing the frozen garden with one bag. Let her have that much of the truth, since the rest was forfeit.

Whether Livia woke, Sable never learned. The garden gate clicked shut behind her; the street took her; the northern road took the street. By morning she was a name on a freight manifest, and by summer she was a rumor in a border town, and by the following winter she was no one at all—which had been, she told herself, the entire point.

* * *

Eleven years later, kneeling in a rented room above a bakery with coded dispatches spread across the floorboards, Sable Pemberton—who had been four other women since Verlaine Street—held a match to the corner of a decoded telegram and watched her old life's logic curl and blacken: *the capital has fallen; prepare evacuation logistics.* The state she'd chosen over her sister's wedding was choosing its own funeral now, and the arithmetic she'd fled north to escape had followed her the whole way down.

One berth, the new orders would say soon. One berth, and more claimants than the number allowed.

She thought of Livia asleep on her right side, hand curled near her mouth. She thought of the empty pew. She thought of the note she had actually written versus the one she had left unwritten, and of all the notes since, unsent, hidden beneath the floorboard where Marta's broom might one day find them.

Then the knock came at the safehouse door—two, pause, one—and Sable rose to answer whatever the dying republic required of her next, telling herself, as she had told herself for eleven years, that leaving had been the same thing as living.

She was beginning, at last, to doubt it.
