## 8. Occupier's Dinner

The invitation arrived on Thursday, tucked inside the renewal notice itself—a single card of heavy cream stock, edged in the gray of the Provisional Administration, with a time and an address and nothing else. No name. No reason. Just *seven o'clock*, in handwriting so controlled it might have been printed.

Sable held it over the stove flame for a long moment out of habit, then lowered it. You could not burn an invitation without burning the renewal notice beneath it, and without renewed transit papers she was a dead woman carrying her own warrant through the streets.

*Seven o'clock,* she thought. *They want me rested.*

She put the card face-down on the table and went to check on Tomas, who was asleep in the alcove off the kitchen where Marta had made him a bed of quilts. He slept the way children sleep in occupied cities—not sprawled, not abandoned, but gathered, arms around his own knees as if holding himself together against a wind only he could feel. Three weeks now, and he had spoken exactly once. Three weeks, and she had already learned the particular rhythm of his breathing that meant nightmare, the small hitch-and-hold before he woke.

She had catalogued it the way she catalogued everything. That was the problem. A courier noticed things; a mother memorized them. She did not permit herself the second word even in the privacy of her own skull.

"You're staring again," Marta said from the doorway, arms folded, spectacles pushed up into her gray hair. "You do that when you're frightened."

"I'm never frightened."

"No," Marta agreed mildly. "You're never frightened. You're going to be late for your dinner."

"It's not a dinner. It's an audit with candles."

"Then wear the blue dress." Marta's voice softened by a degree, which from Marta was a great deal. "Men like him expect a woman to arrive looking like something they've already won."

Sable looked at her. "And what does Marta expect?"

"I expect you home by ten." She turned back toward the kitchen, then paused. "I'll leave the lamp in the window. The one on the left. If it's moved to the right, don't come in—go to the church on Vasil Street and wait under the bell."

It took Sable a full second to understand what she was being given: a signal system. An escape route. Marta had built it sometime in the last three weeks, quietly, the way she did everything, without ever once acknowledging aloud that the courier who lived above her was anything other than a traveling saleswoman in textiles.

"Marta—"

"The blue dress," Marta repeated, and closed the door between them.

* * *

The address was a house, not an office—that alone told Sable how much the evening would cost her. The Administration conducted business in requisitioned buildings with marble floors and clerks behind grilles; but an invitation to a private residence meant an audience. It meant someone who wanted to watch her across a table instead of across a desk.

The house stood on Kestrel Row among the villas the occupiers had confiscated in the first month, its garden walls hung with bare winter vines, its windows glowing amber against the dark. A soldier at the gate checked her papers, looked at her face, looked at the papers again—and here was the first cold drop of the evening, because he did not look at her face the way soldiers looked at faces. He looked at it the way clerks looked at signatures. Comparing.

"Second floor," he said. "The colonel is expecting you."

Not *an officer*. Not *the commandant*. The colonel.

The staircase smelled of wax and woodsmoke and, faintly, of lilies—the occupiers' flower, shipped in from their own country at ruinous expense while the city queued for flour. Sable counted doors as she climbed because counting was prayer, arithmetic was armor, and she needed every piece of armor she owned tonight.

He received her in a room lined with books that had been someone else's. She recognized the spines—Vessik's histories, the Aldmere editions, a complete Corvane in green morocco that she'd have sold a year of her life for—and understood the gesture precisely. This room had been chosen to be recognized. *We know what you are,* the shelves said. *Educated. Careful. One of ours, once, before we were theirs.*

"Sable Merrin," the colonel said, rising from his chair. He was perhaps fifty-five, silver at the temples, wearing civilian clothes—a charcoal jacket, no insignia—which was its own kind of uniform, the uniform of a man who wished to appear as though this were merely dinner. "Thank you for coming. I'm Colonel Havel Rostek. I hope the walk wasn't unpleasant. They tell me the curfew checkpoints have been improved."

"They've been made slower," Sable said. "Slower isn't the same as better."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth—not quite a smile, more the place where a smile would go if a man like him kept such things on the premises. "So I've been told. Wine? It's from the southern valleys. The vintage is older than the war."

"Everything in this room is older than the war," she said, taking the glass anyway. Refusing wine was refusal, and refusal was information. "That's rather the point of looting."

"That's rather the point of most things." He gestured her to a chair opposite his, a low table between them set for two, candles burning with the steady indifference of good beeswax. "But we didn't come here to discuss theft. Or rather—we came here to discuss nothing at all, and everything will be discussed anyway. That's how these evenings work. You know that, I think. You've sat through enough of them."

"I sit through very few evenings, Colonel. I'm a saleswoman. Textiles. Evenings are for inventory."

"Of course." He poured himself wine and settled back, and for a while—long enough to become deliberate—he simply looked at her. Not leeringly, not cruelly. With the patient attention of a man reading a document he has read before and is checking for the forgery. "Tell me about the textiles."

"Linen, mostly. Some wool in the autumn. My supplier is in Harrow Fen."

"Harrow Fen." He nodded slowly. "Good country. Flat. Easy to cross at night, I imagine, if one were the sort of person who crossed fields at night."

"If one were," Sable agreed, and sipped her wine, and let the silence do her lying for her.

This was the old game and she knew all its moves: the feint toward her cover, the counter-feint toward her real work, each of them pretending the whole time that there was no game being played. She had played it at border tables and consular offices and once, memorably, in a customs shed with a dog panting two feet from a hem full of codes. She knew how to keep her hands quiet, her eyes modest, her answers two degrees duller than the truth.

What she had not accounted for was the soup course.

A servant brought it—chilled sorrel, a dish from her own country's kitchens, unobtainable now except through confiscation—and Rostek watched her taste it with an expression of mild, terrible kindness.

"My wife used to make this," he said. "Before. She was from your side of the border, actually. From Callow Bay. She used to say the sorrel had to be picked before the frost or it turned bitter." He stirred his spoon in a slow circle. "She died three years ago. Before any of this. Which my colleagues consider a mercy and I consider—" he paused, "—an incomplete sentence."

"I'm sorry," Sable said, because the word was free and cost nothing.

"Are you?" He looked up. "That's an honest question, Miss Merrin, not a rhetorical one. I find that people in your position are sorry in the abstract, the way one is sorry about weather. And I confess I've been curious about you long enough that I'd like to know whether you're capable of the specific kind."

"And what position is that?"

"The position of a woman whose papers were issued in Vellisport by a registrar's office that burned down fourteen months ago, along with its records." He said it pleasantly, the way one comments on the soup. "Which means your documents cannot be verified. Only renewed. And renewal requires judgment. My judgment."

The candle flames did not move. The room was too well-built for drafts. Sable felt her heartbeat settle into the slow, deep cadence she had trained into herself on frozen borders: *you are a woman eating soup; you are a woman discussing linen; the rest is weather.*

"Clerical fires are common in wartime," she said.

"Extremely common." He smiled at last, fully, and it transformed his face into something almost gentle, which was the most frightening thing it had done all evening. "Almost as common as couriers."

The word landed on the table between them like a dropped coin.

Sable did not reach for it. She let it lie there, gleaming, and lifted her spoon, and ate another mouthful of soup that tasted of nothing at all. *If he says the word twice,* she thought, *he knows. If he says it once, he suspects. If he lets it lie, he wants me to pick it up myself.* She had been trained by a man who had since been shot—she'd read the dispatch, coded, four lines long—to believe that silence was the strongest card in any hand, and she played it now, and watched Rostek play it too, and for nearly a minute the two of them sat in the candlelight eating confiscated soup in a stolen house while neither of them conceded a single thing.

"Your transit route," he said finally, "takes you through the eastern districts. Weekly, according to your filings."

"My suppliers are in the east."

"There's fighting in the east."

"There's fighting everywhere, Colonel. That's why the linens are cheap."

He laughed then—a short, genuine bark of amusement that seemed to surprise even him—and set down his spoon, and for a moment the mask slipped far enough that she saw the tired man underneath, the widower, the administrator of a dying occupation in a country that hated him, and she understood with sudden clarity that he had not invited her here to trap her. Trapping her would require paperwork, warrants, procedures. He had invited her here because he was lonely, and because loneliness in powerful men is more dangerous than malice, and because some part of him wanted—needed—her to be exactly what her papers claimed.

Or that was the performance. She had seen officers perform weariness before. She had seen one perform it while signing deportation orders with the same hand that held his brandy.

"The renewal will be granted," Rostek said, rising to fetch a leather folder from the desk in the corner. "Through the spring quarter. Eastern districts included." He laid the fresh transit paper on the table between them, stamped, signed, official, and did not push it toward her. "There's only one matter outstanding. A formality."

"Formalities," Sable said, "are the heaviest cargo in the world."

His eyes came up sharply at that—sharper than anything else in the evening—and held hers for a beat too long before softening. "Yes," he said quietly. "I've found that too." He slid the paper across. "The formality is this: quarterly renewals now require a countersignature from the Port Authority in Callow. Your next trip east will take you near enough. You'll present yourself at the Harbor Office, window nine, and collect the endorsement. Routine. Everyone does it."

Window nine. Sable filed the detail away with the rest—the burned registry, the flat fields of Harrow Fen, the word *courier* spoken once and never reclaimed—and reached for the paper.

Rostek's hand came down over hers. Lightly. Barely touching.

"One more thing, Miss Merrin." His voice had changed. The civility remained, wrapped around the words like cloth around a blade. "Whatever you carry—linens, samples, invoices, whatever it is that keeps a textile saleswoman crossing a war zone every week—I want you to consider what happens to it after the spring. This administration is leaving. Everyone in this city knows it, whatever the broadcasts say. The ships are being scheduled now. Manifests written. Berths allocated." He released her hand and stepped back, and resumed his pleasant expression as though setting down a tool. "When the time comes, there may be room on those manifests for people who were useful. People who were *reasonable*. I mention it because I dislike waste. Of ships. Of people."

"Is that an offer, Colonel?"

"It's a formality," he said. "Like the stamp. Like the soup." He walked her to the door himself, past the dead man's books, past the soldier at the landing who did not quite meet her eyes. At the threshold Rostek inclined his head, courteous as a dancing master.

"Give my regards to Harrow Fen," he said. "Lovely flat country. So easy to see anyone coming."

* * *

The streets were curfew-quiet, and Sable walked them fast, papers against her ribs, breath smoking in the cold. The lamp in Marta's window stood at the left. Safe. She stood in the doorway a long moment before going in, letting the night drain out of her shoulders, and it was only then that her hands began to shake—the delayed tremor, the body settling its accounts after the mind had finished its performance.

Marta was awake, of course, sitting in the kitchen with tea going cold beside her and a mending basket untouched in her lap.

"Well?" she asked.

"He renewed the papers."

"That's good."

"He knows what I am. Or he knows enough. He said the word *courier* like a man laying a card face-up and daring me to match it. And then—" Sable stopped. Her voice had gone thin. "Then he offered me a berth. On one of the evacuation ships. For services rendered. Services yet to be rendered, I should say."

Marta was silent for a moment. In the alcove, Tomas slept on, gathered around himself like a fist.

"And what did you say?"

"Nothing. There was nothing to say. That's what frightens me, Marta—he didn't press. A man who suspects and doesn't arrest you isn't merciful. He's *investing*. He let me walk out of that house because a live courier is worth more than a dead one, and because—" she heard herself say it, and could not stop, "—because letting me go was worse than stopping me. Stopping me would have ended the game. Letting me go means the game goes on, on his terms, and I don't know the rules anymore."

Marta rose, warmed the tea, and pressed the cup into her hands. "You'll sleep," she said, in the tone of a woman stating physics. "And tomorrow you'll write your letter."

Sable looked up sharply. "What letter?"

"The one you write every night," Marta said, gathering her mending basket, not looking at her, "to your sister. The one you seal and never send. The one that's been sitting in the bottom of your trunk since November, growing into a book." She paused at the kitchen door. "I dust that trunk, dear. I dust everything in this house. Tomorrow, you should write to her properly. Because men like that colonel keep manifests—and manifests have a way of deciding who gets to say goodbye."

Upstairs, later, in the dark, Sable took out the packet of unsent letters and read the last line she had written to Livia, weeks ago, in a hand steadier than she felt now:

*If you are reading this, it means I failed to burn it, which means I failed at everything else first.*

She sharpened her pencil and began page thirty-one.
