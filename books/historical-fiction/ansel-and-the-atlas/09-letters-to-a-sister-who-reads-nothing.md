## 9. Letters to a Sister Who Reads Nothing

Marta Voss had kept the house on Kessler Lane for nineteen years, and in that time she had learned to read her lodgers the way other women read weather. The ones who stayed a night were rain—brief, noisy, gone by morning, leaving nothing but wet boots and a tip pressed into her palm as though payment could erase them. The ones who stayed a week were fog. The rare ones, the ones who stayed months, were something else entirely: a pressure system, a low gray weight that settled over the second-floor room and never quite lifted.

The courier was a pressure system.

She had come in late autumn with a forged transit card that was almost too good—almost, because whoever made it had gotten the ink right and the name wrong, choosing *Ilse Brenner*, a name Marta happened to know belonged to a girl who'd died of fever in '39. A forger who didn't check the parish registers. Sloppy in one small way, flawless in every other way, which meant careful, which meant professional, which meant trouble walking up Marta's stairs wearing a coat too thin for the season and an expression like a locked drawer.

Marta had said nothing about the name. That was the arrangement she'd made with herself long ago, after Henrik died and after the first soldiers came and after she understood that the house on Kessler Lane would fill with people passing through history whether she invited them or not. Her part in it was simple: beds, bread, silence, and the discipline of not knowing what she already knew.

But knowing, she had also learned, is not the same as understanding. And it took her until the third week of December to understand the courier at all.

* * *

It began with candle wax.

Marta rose before dawn every morning of her life, even now, even when there was no market worth rising for, because her mother had taught her that a woman who sleeps past the light is asking the world to make decisions without her. So she was in the kitchen at half past five, coaxing the stove awake, when she saw the light under the courier's door. Second floor, end room, facing the yard. Burning at five-thirty in the morning.

The courier came down an hour later, dressed, composed, her face washed clean of whatever the candle had been for. She ate her bread and jam standing up, thanked Marta in a voice pitched to be forgettable, and left for the port district before the milk cart passed.

That evening the candle burned again. And the next morning. And the next.

Marta told herself it was none of her business. She repeated this to herself while sweeping the stairs, while salting the soup, while pulling the dead geraniums from the window box so the soldiers on their patrols wouldn't see a house that had stopped trying. None of her business. But she was seventy-one years old, her knees ached, her mother was eleven years dead and still talked to her from the chair by the stove, and she had discovered that the only vice left to her was curiosity—the slow, patient kind, the kind that keeps its hands folded in its lap.

On a Tuesday, while the courier's room stood empty, Marta climbed the stairs with an armful of linens she did not need to change.

She did not snoop. She wanted that understood, if anyone ever asked, if God kept accounts of such things. She changed the pillowcases and straightened the blanket and turned the mattress the way you're supposed to turn a mattress, and if her eyes happened to fall upon the writing desk, well, a person cannot sweep a room blindfolded.

There were letters on the desk. Eleven of them, fanned slightly, weighted at the corners with two brass buttons and a spoon. Each envelope bore an address in careful script—a street in a city far to the south, a name: *Livia Ashford.* Each envelope had been sealed. Not one of them bore a stamp.

Eleven letters, sealed and stamped with nothing, going nowhere. Letters to a sister who would never read them.

Marta stood in the cold room holding a pillowcase against her chest like a shield, and felt something crack open in her own ribs, some old door she'd painted shut years ago. Because she knew about letters like that. She had written them herself.

* * *

Henrik had been dead nine years when the war came properly to their city, and in those nine years Marta had written him four hundred letters. She knew the number because she'd counted once, drunk on plum brandy at New Year's, sitting where the courier sat now. Four hundred letters to a man who could not read them, sealed and stacked in a hatbox under her bed. She'd written about the roof leaking, about the price of coal, about the neighbor's dog, about how she'd dreamed of his hands and woken reaching across cold linen. She wrote them because the alternative was speaking aloud to an empty kitchen, and speaking aloud was madness, but ink was private. Ink could keep a secret even from yourself.

When the hatbox got full enough to lift with both hands, she burned them in the stove, one by one, reading each aloud in a whisper as it curled, so that somewhere—if anywhere—Henrik might hear.

She looked at the courier's eleven letters and thought: *there is a hatbox in that girl too.* Something she carries because putting it down would mean admitting it exists.

Marta finished changing the linens. She did not touch the letters. She went downstairs and started a pot of coffee she hadn't planned on making, and waited for the front door to open at dusk.

* * *

The courier—*Ilse*, though Marta's tongue refused the name; in her head she called her *the girl*, though the girl was probably thirty-five and had killed men, or carried things that got men killed, or both—came in at six with frost in her eyebrows and the boy asleep against her shoulder.

The boy. Tomas. That was another thing Marta was pretending not to know about, in the sense that she baked for him, mended his coat, carved him wooden animals from scrap, and had begun, without deciding to, keeping the good butter for his bread. He'd appeared on the doorstep in November with a note that said *forget this face*, and he had not spoken a word since, and Marta—who had raised no children and buried no children and therefore considered herself an expert on neither—had decided within a week that the boy's silence was not damage. It was strategy. She recognized strategy. She'd used it herself for nineteen years.

The courier laid Tomas on the settee in the parlor, pulled off his boots with a gentleness that contradicted everything else about her hands, and covered him with the gray wool blanket.

"He ate?" Marta asked from the doorway.

"Half the roll."

"I'll make soup."

"You don't have to—"

"It's already made." It was not. "Sit down. You look like a fence post someone leaned against a wall."

The courier sat. She always obeyed instructions quickly; it was the one way you could tell she'd been trained by someone hard. She folded her hands. She watched Marta chop leeks with the wariness of a woman watching someone handle a weapon.

"The boy spoke today," Marta said, not looking up. This was a lie, and she delivered it smoothly, the way you salt a pot. "Not words. But he laughed. At my cat. The cat knocked the flour over and he laughed."

Something moved through the courier's face—fast, buried immediately, but fast—and Marta understood that she had just handed the woman a gift and that the woman knew it was a gift and resented needing it. Good, Marta thought. Resentment is honest. Gratitude can be performed.

"Thank you," the courier said.

"Mm." Marta swept the leek ends into her hand. Then, casually, as though mentioning the weather, as though this entire conversation were happening to two other women in another house: "You write to your sister every night, I see. Through the ceiling. Candlelight comes down between the floorboards like a little river."

The silence that followed had texture. Marta let it sit.

"She doesn't get them," the courier said finally. Her voice was flat, but flatness is also a tone; it takes effort. "They're not—they don't go anywhere."

"No," Marta agreed. "I didn't think they did."

"You looked at my desk."

"I changed your linens. Your desk was sitting there being itself." Marta dropped the trimmings in the compost pail and wiped her hands on her apron. "I'm not going to ask what's in them. I'll tell you why I'm not going to ask, and then we'll both feel better. I wrote four hundred letters to my husband after he died. Sealed every one. Burned them when the box filled up. So I know exactly what those letters are, and I know they're not for me, and I know that if you stop writing them, something in you will die, and I need you alive, because you're the only person in this house besides me who knows how to keep a fire burning quietly."

The courier stared at her. Somewhere upstairs a pipe knocked, twice, the old house clearing its throat.

"That's—" the courier began.

"Don't thank me," Marta said. "I hate it. Eat your soup."

* * *

After that, something loosened, though neither of them named it. Naming things, Marta had found, was a luxury for peacetime; in wartime you simply began leaving the kitchen lamp lit later, and setting out two cups instead of one, and the other person either came down or didn't.

She came down.

The first real conversation happened on the longest night of the year, when the snow came down so thick the patrol trucks gave up and went home, and the whole street went silent in that particular way snow imposes, a silence like held breath. They sat at the kitchen table with the stove ticking and a bottle of plum brandy between them—the same brandy, Marta noted privately, that she'd been saving for news of liberation, which seemed increasingly likely to arrive after they were both dead.

The courier drank like a soldier—one swallow, jaw tight—but by the second glass her shoulders came down from around her ears.

"My sister chose wrong," she said abruptly. It was the first time she'd mentioned Livia unprompted. "Eighteen years ago. There was a boat. There's always a boat, isn't there, in stories like ours. A boat and a choice and a man. She married the man and I took the dispatch bag, and we each thought the other one was the coward." She turned the glass on the table, slowly, making a wet ring. "I've written to her every night for three weeks. She won't ever read them. She might be dead. She might be worse than dead—comfortable, collaborating, telling herself stories." Her voice caught on nothing, recovered. "I don't even know why I write them. Habit. Punishment. I don't know."

"Do you want to know why I wrote mine?" Marta said.

The courier looked up.

"Because a letter is the only place where you can say a true thing all the way to the end of it." Marta refilled both glasses. "In conversation you stop halfway. You watch the other person's face and you edit. In a letter nobody stops you. You find out what you actually think around paragraph three, and by paragraph seven you've said the thing you'd die before saying out loud. My Henrik—I told him I was angry at him. Forty years married and I never said it once while he lived. Died with a clear conscience, the fool, and me with forty years of anger and love mixed together like cement in my chest, and nowhere to pour it except paper." She shrugged. "Your sister will never read yours. Fine. You're not writing to her anyway. You're writing to the version of her that lives in you, and that one reads every word."

The courier's eyes were bright and furious about it. "You're a very strange landlady."

"I'm a very strange many things." Marta tapped the table. "Tell me about her. Not the argument. Her. What was she like before?"

And the girl talked. Hesitantly at first, then in a flood—Livia at fourteen, Livia singing in the stairwell of their building, Livia brazen and brilliant and afraid of thunder, Livia who could argue a fishmonger down to half price and then give him back the difference out of guilt. Marta listened the way she did everything: thoroughly, silently, filing each detail into the room in her chest where she kept people. And she noticed—because noticing was her profession now, her pension plan, her life's work—that the courier never once said *was* where she could have said *is*. Present tense kept slipping in. *She does this thing where.* *She always says.*

Alive, then. Probably alive. Or at least unproven otherwise, which in this country passed for hope.

* * *

Tomas began coming down to the kitchen in the evenings, and this changed the geometry of everything. The boy would sit at the table with his wooden animals—Marta had made him a horse, two birds, and a lumpy bear that he slept with—and arrange them in silent dramas while the two women talked above his head. He never spoke. But he watched Sable—I'd finally learned her name, or a name, or the shape of one—with an attention that Marta found frankly unnerving in a child of six. He watched her the way she watched checkpoints.

One night in mid-January, Marta came downstairs for her knitting and found them asleep in the parlor: the boy curled against the courier's side, the courier upright but unconscious, head tipped back, one hand resting on the boy's shoulder in sleep—the hand having apparently defected from policy without authorization. The lamp was still lit. On the side table lay the night's letter, unsealed, and Marta, moving quietly to turn down the flame, could not help seeing the first line in the courier's sharp handwriting:

*Livia — the boy laughed today. I don't know what to do with that.*

Marta turned down the lamp and stood a moment in the dark, listening to the breathing of the two sleeping people, and felt her own loneliness—the great silent animal she'd housed for nineteen years—stir and settle, not gone, but quieter, as if it had smelled company.

* * *

It was near the end of January when the girl came down the stairs two steps at a time, which she never did, coat already buttoned, and Marta knew before she spoke that something had broken the rhythm of their small world.

"There's a ship," Sable said. "The Atlas. Out of the harbor. Evacuation. I have to go to the port office tomorrow—there are papers, a manifest—" She stopped, pressed her lips together, visibly reassembling herself. "I may be gone two days. Watch the boy."

"Of course I'll watch the boy." Marta set down her knitting. "Sable." She almost never used the name; it felt like handling something hot. "A berth on that ship is worth more than gold right now. Men have killed for less than a place in a queue."

"I know what it's worth."

"And you have one? For certain?"

The pause lasted half a heartbeat too long. "I'm a courier of the republic," Sable said. "My passage is guaranteed."

Later—much later, after everything—Marta would remember that pause, and the way Sable's hand had gone briefly to her coat hem, the way a gambler touches his last coin without meaning to. But that night she only nodded and said, "Take bread. And take this." She pressed into the girl's palm her own transit card, the real one, the one with Marta Voss's photograph and Marta Voss's tired face, useless to her now and possibly not useless to anyone.

"I can't—"

"You can and you will. Papers are like prayers, girl. You carry more than you think you'll need, and you never say them out loud." Marta closed the girl's fingers over the card. "Go. Come back. Both of those are orders."

At the door, Sable hesitated—the first hesitation Marta had ever seen in her—and said, very quietly, "If anything happens to me, the letters on the desk. Burn them unread."

"I'll burn nothing," Marta said. "If anything happens to you, I'll deliver them myself. To the south address. However far that is."

"They're not—"

"However far," Marta repeated, "that is."

The door shut. The snow outside swallowed the sound of footsteps. Marta stood in the hallway with the boy's hand in hers and the house suddenly enormous around them, and upstairs, through the floorboards, she could see the faint line of the candle under the closed door—burning, as always, for a woman who would never read a word.
