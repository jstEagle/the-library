## 10. The Manifest Changes

The harbor city of Vesseny had a smell in winter that I never forgot: tar and fish rot and coal smoke frozen mid-air, so that breathing felt like swallowing something already dead. I came down from the hills on the third morning of December with frost in the seams of my boots and a boy's name stitched into my silence—Tomas now lived behind my eyes the way a splinter lives under skin, small, foreign, impossible to ignore.

I had left him at the safehouse with Marta four days earlier. I had told myself it was logistics. Couriers travel light; couriers travel alone; couriers do not explain themselves to children who do not speak. I had crouched at the door, adjusted his collar for no reason except that my hands needed occupation, and said, "I'll be back before you learn to miss me."

He had looked at me with those gray, patient eyes—the eyes of someone cataloguing *me* now, God help us all—and said nothing, because he always said nothing, and I walked out into the snow feeling like a woman fleeing a debt.

Now, standing above Vesseny where the road bent and the whole gray bowl of the harbor opened below me, I let myself think of him for exactly one minute. I counted it out against my heartbeat, the way I'd been taught to count danger. Sixty seconds of Tomas's chapped hands and the way Marta had begun humming to him when she thought no one listened. Then I folded the minute up and put it away, and I went down into the city to buy my life back.

* * *

The shipping office of the Atlas Line stood on Quay Street behind a façade that had once been white and was now the color of old teeth. The republic's flag still hung over the door, but someone had half-lowered it weeks ago and no one had bothered to raise it again—a flag at half-staff for a country not yet dead enough to bury.

Inside, the office smelled of ink and wet wool. A clerk sat behind a grille of iron bars, a narrow man with the face of someone who had learned early that his survival depended on remembering which lies he had told to whom.

"Pemberton," I said, and gave the number. "Manifest confirmation."

His eyes flicked up, flicked down, flicked sideways—the little dance they all did now. "Step aside, please."

There were six people ahead of me and I used the time the way I use all waiting time: reading the room. A woman in a fur collar that had been good ten years ago, clutching a document folder like a hymnal. Two brothers who kept touching each other's sleeves as if confirming the other still existed. An old man asleep upright, his ticket pinned to his coat because his hands shook too badly to hold anything. Everyone in that room was doing arithmetic. I recognized it because I was doing it too: berths against bodies, money against mercy, one life weighed against another on scales too small to hold them.

When my turn came, the clerk pulled my file and his expression did something terrible. It softened.

That's how I knew, before he said a word.

"Miss Pemberton." He lowered his voice as though the room might be bugged, and perhaps it could be; the occupiers had ears everywhere, and some of them were human. "Your passage has been—amended."

"Amended." I kept my voice flat as ice on a well. "I hold a confirmed berth. Number forty-one. Paid in full, per the agreement."

"The Atlas lost thirty-two berths this week to military priority." He said *military priority* the way priests say the names of saints, without inflection, because inflection is dangerous. "Artillery units are being evacuated ahead of civilian passengers. Your berth has been reassigned."

"To whom? A gun?"

"To the 114th Coastal Regiment." He didn't blink. Blinking was probably forbidden too. "However." And here his voice dropped further, down into the register men use when they're about to hand you something that will cut you. "A supplementary arrangement exists. Certain individuals with proven reliability and established transit credentials may have their civilian status restored through service to the Provisional Authority."

I understood immediately, and understanding felt like cold water finding every seam in my coat. "You mean the courier routes haven't been evacuated. Only everyone else."

"I mean," he said carefully, "that the Authority requires one final dispatch run inland. Kolverath sector. Documents of state significance. The courier originally assigned is—" a pause, the smallest shrug—"no longer available."

*No longer available.* Three words with a body buried under them somewhere.

"And if I carry these documents," I said, "my berth is restored."

"Restored and guaranteed. Priority status. You would board before the military cargo." He slid a paper through the grille. The route map was drawn in a hand that trembled slightly at the river crossings, or perhaps that was just how cartographers drew rivers now, knowing each one was a place where people stopped being alive. "Departure of the Atlas is fixed for the twenty-third. The dispatch must be delivered and receipted by the twentieth."

Three days' travel inland through occupied country in December, and three days back, with no margin for snow, checkpoints, or bad luck. They weren't offering me a berth. They were offering me a wager, and they held the dice, and the house always—

"Where do I collect the dispatch?" I asked.

His eyebrows rose a fraction. I think he had expected argument. Everyone argues; arguing is free, and desperate people spend freely. "Sector office. The address is on the routing slip. You'll need your transit papers renewed—I understand you've an appointment Thursday with Colonel Brandt's office."

"I do."

"Then you'll want to mention nothing about this." His eyes met mine through the bars, and for one unguarded second I saw what lived behind the clerk: a man watching another woman walk onto the same ice that had already taken the last one. "The colonel's office does not look kindly on independent arrangements. Officially, there are no more courier runs. Unofficially—" he spread his dry hands— "there is only what keeps the ships moving."

I took the routing slip. Paper feels different when it carries your death inside it—heavier, though it weighs nothing—and I folded it into the lining of my sleeve where the other papers lived, all my little folded lives, and I walked out into the street.

The flag over the door hung at half-mast. I thought: we've started mourning ourselves while we're still breathing. Then I thought: forty-one. My number. Somewhere below decks on the Atlas, a soldier of the 114th Coastal Regiment was sleeping in a hammock that should have been mine, and he didn't even know he'd won it, and I hated him completely and irrationally and then forgave him, because he hadn't chosen either. None of us chose any of this. That was becoming the national prayer.

* * *

I stayed the night at a flophouse near the fish market where the beds were rented by the hour and nobody asked names. I didn't sleep. Sleep is a currency too, and I was saving.

Instead I sat by a window with frost ferns climbing the glass, and I did the thing I never let myself do in daylight: I took out the minute I'd folded away, and I unfolded it, and I let Tomas fill it and then overflow it.

He had been at the safehouse eleven days. Eleven days, and already Marta reported—through the coded phrasing of her weekly market notes, hidden in talk of flour prices—that the boy followed her through the house like a shadow with a shadow of its own, that he ate everything set before him with the desperate thoroughness of a child who has known hunger professionally, that he slept curled against the kitchen door as though guarding it, or waiting for it to open. That he had begun, Marta wrote, to stack kindling without being asked, arranging it by size with a precision that broke her heart, because only children who have been punished for disorder stack wood that way.

And underneath her report, in the margin, in her blunt carpenter's hand, she had written one line that I had read forty times since:

*He watches the road from the window. He is waiting for you.*

I put the note away. Waiting for me. What a catastrophic misunderstanding. I was a woman made of aliases; there was no one at the center to wait for. I had built myself that way deliberately, brick by brick, name by false name, until the architecture could stand without a person inside it. That was the entire design. That was the genius of it.

Except the design had a flaw, and the flaw was eleven days old and stacked kindling by size.

Here is what I decided that night, in the dark of the flophouse, with the fish smell rising through the floorboards and the frost closing its white fingers over the window: I would take the dispatch. Of course I would take the dispatch. Not for the republic—the republic had stopped being a country months ago and become merely an argument between armies—but for the berth. For the guaranteed seat on the deck of the Atlas with the wind off open water, where no one's name meant anything and every border was a horizon.

And then, because I am honest with myself in the dark and a liar by daylight, I admitted the rest: I would take the dispatch because the alternative was to sit in a safehouse with a mute child and a loquacious landlady until the soldiers came, and waiting to be found is a slower way to die than walking toward danger, but it is death all the same, and I had never learned how to do it.

The price of survival, I wrote in my head, is always paid in advance. That was the lesson of the manifest. Nothing in this country was given anymore; everything was traded, berth for blood, bread for silence, a child's future for a woman's courage. The republic was dying, but its arithmetic remained in perfect health.

* * *

I reached the safehouse at dusk two days later, coming in through the orchard rather than the road because a courier who stops being careful dies of it eventually. Snow lay on the apple trees like linen laid out for corpses. Through the kitchen window I saw firelight, and Marta's broad silhouette moving at the stove, and a smaller silhouette sitting on the floor beside the stove, cross-legged, perfectly still, sorting something.

Kindling. By size.

I stood in the cold a long moment, longer than caution allowed, watching him through the glass. There is a particular pain that comes from seeing a thing you refused to want, wanting you anyway. It goes through the armor like water through stone—not violently, not all at once, just patiently, season after season, until one day the stone is sand.

Marta saw me first—of course she did; the woman missed nothing, which was precisely why she frightened me—and she lifted her chin toward the door without stopping her stirring. *Come in, then, out of the cold,* the gesture said. *You foolish girl. You frozen, stubborn, half-dead girl.*

Tomas heard the latch. He turned, and for the first time since the night on the doorstep, his face moved. Not a smile. Something older than a smile—something from before language, when recognition was the whole of speech.

I stepped inside, stamped the snow from my boots, and hung my coat on its nail with hands that almost didn't shake. Almost. Progress of a kind.

"You're thinner," Marta said, ladling soup without looking at me. "Sit. Eat. Then tell me what the city took out of you, because it took something—I can see the hole from here."

"There's a dispatch run," I said. "Inland. Kolverath sector."

The ladle stopped. In our private vocabulary, *Kolverath* meant the interior, the deep country, the places where the occupiers shot first and invented reasons after. Marta knew the geography of dying better than any mapmaker.

"When do you leave?"

"Day after tomorrow. Before dawn."

She set the ladle down with exaggerated care, the way she did when she was angry and refusing to show it. "And the ship?"

"Guaranteed, if I return by the twentieth."

"*If.*"

"If," I agreed, and took the bowl she thrust at me, and ate, because the body insists on its own business even while the mind is signing contracts in blood.

Tomas rose silently from his kindling, crossed the floor, and sat down beside my chair—not touching me, never touching me, but close, close as a held breath. After a while, without looking at me, he picked up a stick of kindling from his pile and set it, very deliberately, on my knee.

I looked down at it. A straight piece of pine, sanded smooth at one end where he had worried it with his thumb. A gift. The first thing anyone had given me in this war that wasn't a bill.

"Thank you," I said.

His gray eyes came up to mine, and he nodded once, gravely, a magistrate acknowledging testimony.

Later, when the dishes were done and Marta had gone up to her mother's room, I sat at the kitchen table with the routing slip and the map of the Kolverath sector spread out under the lamp, memorizing rivers, bridges, the names of villages that would be checkpoints by the time I reached them. And beneath the map, hidden in my sleeve, the other papers pressed against my wrist like a pulse: the transit documents, the manifest confirmation, the small printed promise of berth forty-one—*if*.

Outside, the wind came down off the hills carrying the smell of snow, and somewhere far inland, past all those rivers I was memorizing, a dead man's replacement waited with a package that would cost me three days and might cost me everything else.

I blew out the lamp. In the dark, Tomas breathed slow and even by the stove, and Marta's house creaked around us like a ship at anchor, and I lay awake doing the arithmetic I swore I'd stopped doing, counting the days backward from the twenty-third, subtracting the roads, the snow, the checkpoints, the luck I did not believe in.

Nine days. Nine days to carry the republic's last secret through the country that had swallowed it, and come back alive enough to claim the seat that was mine.

I closed my eyes and told myself it was simple.

It was the worst lie I ever told, and I had told so many.
