## 11. Livia at the Gate

The city had taught Livia Pemberton—no, that name was burned; the city had taught *her*, the woman with no papers worth the ink they were written on—that grief was a form of luggage. You could not put it down. You could only learn to carry it in such a way that no one noticed the weight.

She carried it now through streets that smelled of coal smoke and wet stone, past shuttered bakeries and the occupation's gray flags hanging like wet laundry from every public building. Her boots were someone else's boots, bought from a woman in the market district who had not asked questions because Livia had paid in a wedding ring, and the woman had looked at the ring a long time before taking it. Not greed. Recognition. Everyone was selling something they had sworn to keep.

Three days since the house on Vell Street burned. Two days since she had stopped looking over her shoulder for soldiers and started looking instead for the particular kind of silence that meant she was already being followed. One day since she had heard, in a queue outside the ration office, a name spoken low and careful, the way one speaks of the dead or of saints: *the courier*. A woman who moved through occupied territory like smoke through a keyhole. A woman with a berth on the *Atlas*.

Livia had known before the whisperer finished speaking. She had known the way one knows one's own handwriting.

*Ansel.*

Not her sister's true name—Sable had shed that along with everything else twelve years ago—but Livia had spent her childhood reading her sister's disguises, and she recognized the architecture of them still. The courier was Sable. The courier had to be Sable. Because the world, which had taken Aldric and taken the house and taken the child she might yet have been mother to, would not be so cruel as to leave Livia only strangers to beg from.

She found the safehouse by accident, which is to say she found it the way you find anything in an occupied city: by following a thread too thin to see until it breaks. The landlady's network—the old women who traded news under the cover of trading eggs—had spoken of a boarding house on a street whose name changed twice in five years. Livia walked it in the last gray hour before curfew, counting doorways, watching windows, and there: a light in an upper room that went out the moment she slowed her step beneath it.

Someone inside had seen her shadow pass the glass. Someone inside had been watching the street.

Only one person Livia knew watched streets like that—counting footsteps, timing lamplighters, building a map of every exit without ever appearing to look. Only one person had ever made vigilance into a way of loving the world, which was another way of saying never touching it.

She stood in the cold and let the realization settle over her like snow. Then she climbed the steps, and knocked.

* * *

The door opened four inches. The chain caught. And through the gap Livia saw a face she had prepared herself for across two hundred miles of road, and discovered that preparation is a currency that buys nothing at the actual till.

Sable had their father's jaw and none of his softness. Her hair was cropped short as a soldier's, streaked early with gray at the temples, and her eyes did the thing Livia remembered from girlhood: they took inventory. Collar. Hands. Boots. The bruise on Livia's cheekbone where a rifle butt had introduced itself on the second night of her flight. Sable catalogued all of it in the time it took a candle flame to gutter once.

"You're dead," Sable said quietly. "Aldric Pemberton's widow died in the Vell Street fire. It was in the occupation gazette."

"The gazette prints what they're told to print." Livia kept her voice level. Twelve years of marriage to a man who negotiated with grain merchants had taught her that whoever stays calmest sets the price. "I'm told there's a ship leaving this harbor. I'm told there are berths on it. I'm told one belongs to a courier named Ansel."

The chain did not come off. Behind Sable, deeper in the house, Livia heard a small sound—a floorboard, a breath, something quick and young—and filed it away without letting her eyes move toward it. Never show them where your treasure sleeps. Another lesson the city had given her free of charge.

"Who sent you?" Sable asked.

"No one. That's the whole of my misfortune. No one sent me. There's no code sewn into my coat, no handler waiting in some neutral port. I'm just a woman standing on your step in borrowed boots, and in—" she checked the sky, theatrical, because she knew it would infuriate her sister, and infuriating Sable had always been the surest way to prove she was still alive— "in perhaps forty minutes, the patrols change shift, and a woman alone on a doorstep after curfew becomes a problem that requires paperwork neither of us has."

Something moved behind Sable's eyes. Not softness. Calculation, Livia thought, and hated how much the distinction mattered to her, and hated more that she could not stop making it.

"You should have gone east," Sable said. "Through the mountain passes. People do it."

"People die doing it. In ditches, unnamed, and the gazette doesn't even bother to lie about them." Livia lifted her chin. The bruise pulled at the skin and she welcomed the small pain; it kept her voice from shaking. "I didn't cross half the country to be lectured about routes by a girl who left home because the roads were safer than our kitchen table."

The chain slid free. The door opened.

It was not forgiveness. Neither of them pretended otherwise. It was arithmetic—the same cold sum Livia had watched her sister perform her whole life—and she stepped across the threshold grateful for it, because arithmetic, unlike love, could be relied upon to hold still.

* * *

Inside, the safehouse smelled of lamp oil and boiled potatoes and, beneath it, faintly, of a child—not the sour smell of sickness but the warm animal scent of a boy recently asleep. Livia clocked the second cup drying on the rack. The small shoes by the stove, mended with wire. A chair drawn up close to the fire with a folded blanket on its seat, arranged with the particular care of someone who had learned, late and against her nature, to tend something small.

"So," Livia said, keeping her back to the stairs, giving Sable the courtesy of pretending not to have noticed. "The rumors were true. The great invisible courier keeps a boarding house and mends children's shoes."

"He's not mine." Sharp. Immediate. Too fast, which meant it was a wound, which meant Livia had just been handed a knife she would not use tonight but would certainly keep.

"I didn't ask if he was yours."

"You were going to."

"I was going to ask," Livia said, sitting down at the kitchen table because her legs had finally informed her that three days of walking were done being negotiable, "whether the ship is real. Whether the berth is real. And whether either of them survives contact with the people who run them."

Sable did not sit. She stood by the stove with her arms crossed, and in the lamplight Livia could see the toll the years had taken—not in lines or gray hair, those were nothing, but in the way her sister held herself, coiled around some central absence, a woman built like a wall around an empty room.

"The *Atlas* sails in nine days," Sable said at last. "My passage isn't certain anymore. The military has been cutting berths. What's left goes by priority, and priority goes by favors, and favors go by—" she gestured vaguely at the whole ruined apparatus of the world— "by things I'm still arranging."

"But there is a berth."

"There may be a berth."

"That's not a no."

"It's not a yes. It's not anything. It's weather." Sable's voice cracked on the last word, just barely, and then smoothed itself flat again with the practiced ease of someone who had been smoothing herself flat for twelve years. "You walk three hundred miles on rumor and want me to hand you certainty? I don't deal in certainty. I deal in paper, and most of the paper I carry these days tells people they can't come."

Livia looked at her sister across the table—at the sharp face, the guarded hands, the whole armored architecture of her—and felt something rise in her chest that was not quite anger and not quite pity and hurt worse than either.

"Do you know what I thought about," Livia said, "on the road. At night. In the barns and culverts and the backs of carts, when I couldn't sleep for the cold." She turned her cup slowly on the tabletop, watching the candlelight swim in the tea's dull surface. "I thought about the day you left. Not the argument—we've both replayed the argument enough times to wear grooves in it. The morning after. You'd already gone, and Mother sat at the table with your place still set, because she hadn't had the heart to clear it, and Father stood at the window for an hour like a man waiting for weather that had already passed. And I thought: she's out there. My sister is out there in the wide world being brave and necessary, while I stay here and become my mother."

"Livia—"

"Let me finish. I earned the finishing." The cup stopped turning. "I stayed, Sable. I married Aldric because staying was the braver choice in my arithmetic—he needed me, the estate needed someone with a spine, and running away to spite our parents seemed like the coward's version of courage dressed up in a courier's coat. I told myself that for twenty years. And then the soldiers came, and my husband died defending a house that burned anyway, and every single thing I stayed for is ash, and here is the joke, the magnificent, obscene joke: the ones who ran are alive. You're alive. Your friends in the passes are alive. Staying was not bravery. Staying was just slower dying with better furniture."

The words came out harder than she had planned, and once begun they would not be called back, and she watched them land on her sister one by one like stones dropped down a well.

Sable said nothing for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice was quiet enough that Livia had to lean forward to catch it, and understood even as she leaned that the quiet was deliberate—a hook, baited with intimacy.

"Aldric," Sable said. "How?"

"What does it matter?"

"How."

Livia closed her eyes. She had told the story to border guards, to priests, to no one. Each telling cost something different. "He stayed at the gate when they came for the harvest records. He had a shotgun older than both of us. They made him kneel in the yard and he—" Her throat closed. She opened her eyes and found Sable watching her with an expression Livia had never seen on that guarded face, and could not name, and distrusted completely. "They made him wait eleven minutes. He counted aloud. He wanted us—I was in the cellar—to know exactly how long we had. He gave us eleven minutes and then he stopped being a man I was married to and became a number in an occupation report."

The fire ticked. Upstairs, the child stirred—a small creak of bed slats, a murmur, silence again. Sable glanced at the ceiling, involuntary, instinctive, and Livia saw it happen and said nothing, and added it to the growing ledger between them: *she loves him already, and she thinks no one can tell.*

"I'm sorry," Sable said. It sounded like it cost her.

"I'm not here for apologies."

"No. You're here for a berth." Sable straightened, and the armor came back up plate by plate, and Livia watched her sister become the courier again, the professional, the woman who dealt in weather. "So let's talk about what you actually bring to the transaction. Papers?"

"Burned with the house."

"Skills?"

"I can forge a merchant's hand, keep double ledgers, negotiate with men who think women are furniture, and lie to officials in two languages. Also I can cook for twelve and shoot a pistol adequately, which Aldric insisted on and I resented and am now grateful for."

For one unguarded half-second, Sable almost smiled. Livia saw it—the ghost of the girl who used to laugh at funerals, inappropriate and bright—and then it was gone, banked like a fire at curfew.

"And what you need," Sable said, "is passage. Out. Away. Somewhere with different weather and a flag nobody in this country recognizes."

"Yes."

"Then hear me clearly, because I'll only say this once." Sable leaned forward, hands flat on the table, and her eyes held Livia's with a force that had nothing of warmth in it and everything of urgency. "There is one berth. Possibly. If the military doesn't take it first, and if the man who controls the manifest decides my remaining useful outweighs my remaining suspicious. One. And I cannot promise it to you, and I will not pretend to, because pretense is a luxury and we have none left. But you will sleep under this roof tonight, and tomorrow we start building you a new name, and after that—" she paused, and something flickered behind the armor, brief and human and quickly smothered— "after that we'll see what the arithmetic allows."

Arithmetic. Always arithmetic. Livia nodded slowly and felt, beneath the exhaustion and the fear and twelve years of settled accounts, a treacherous flicker of the old sibling fluency—two girls at a kitchen table, dividing anything into shares, each convinced the other was cheating and each secretly delighted to be playing.

"One condition," Livia said.

Sable's jaw tightened. "You're not negotiating from strength."

"I'm negotiating from experience. You'll find I always have conditions. First: you don't lie to me about the odds. Not kindly, not cruelly, not at all. I've buried enough of the truth to last me."

"And the second?"

Livia looked toward the ceiling, toward the small sounds above, and then back at her sister, and let the silence stretch just long enough to make her point.

"When you're ready to tell me whose child that is upstairs," she said, rising, taking her cup to the basin because old habits of housekeeping survive even the fall of republics, "you'll tell me. And until then, I'll mend those shoes properly, because whoever did it used wire, and wire chafes, and children have enough chafing in this world without us adding to it."

She climbed the narrow stairs to the offered room, feeling her sister's eyes on her back the entire way—inventorying, calculating, cataloguing—exactly as she had at the door. Some things did not change. Some things, God help them both, were the closest thing to constancy the century allowed.

In the small cold room, Livia sat on the narrow bed and listened to the house settle. Below her, faintly, she heard Sable moving—writing, always writing, the scratch of a nib like a small animal gnawing. Above the harbor, beyond the black rooftops, somewhere out in the dark water, the *Atlas* rode at anchor with its berths and its lists and its lies, holding in its steel belly the shape of everything Livia had left to want.

Nine days. One berth. Two sisters who had spent twelve years perfecting the art of surviving each other's absence.

She lay back and closed her eyes, and the last thought she had before sleep took her was not of Aldric, nor of the burned house, nor of the road behind her—but of the shoes by the stove, mended badly with wire, and the strange, terrifying suspicion that the smallest person in this house might turn out to matter most of all.

Downstairs, the nib scratched on. And in the morning, Livia knew, the real work would begin—forging a woman out of paper, a future out of arithmetic, and whatever fragile, unwilling truce the night had left them into something that might, against every precedent of their shared history, hold.

She did not yet know about the final dispatch sewn into her sister's coat hem. She did not yet know what names the republic had chosen to save, or chosen to spend.

But she would.
