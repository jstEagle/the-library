## 12. Two Names, One Coat

### Sable

The kitchen table had been cleared of everything but the lamp, the ink, and the dead man's passport, and in the yellow circle of light my sister and I sat down across from each other like surgeons about to open a chest.

Livia turned the passport over twice, studying the watermarks the way she used to study sheet music—silently, lips moving, committing it to some inner ledger she never let anyone read. "Whose face is this?"

"A customs clerk from Vellin. Died of fever in October. His papers were buried with his pride." I laid out the tools between us: the fine brush, the acid bath Marta kept for removing photographs, the gum arabic, the two sheets of good stock I'd bought off a printer who owed me a favor and would deny it to his grave. "We have until dawn to make him into a woman."

"That's ambitious."

"We've done more with less. You once turned me into your husband for a weekend so you could cross the Serrat Bridge without your father's men asking questions."

Livia's mouth did something complicated—a memory trying to become a smile and being stopped at the border. "You were fourteen. You complained the entire time that husbands didn't complain."

"Husbands don't," I said. "That's how we knew you were doing it wrong."

She laughed then, briefly, one note, and looked away as if embarrassed by the sound. Outside, the wind worried the shutters. Tomas slept upstairs in Marta's spare room, and Marta herself was in her parlor pretending not to listen through the floorboards, which was her way of guarding us.

I soaked the photograph loose while Livia read the clerk's particulars aloud in a low voice, learning him: Aldous Renner, born Vellin, occupation civil service, a scar on the left brow he did not actually have. The acid worked under the photo like patience working under grief. In four minutes the image lifted free, leaving a pale rectangle where a man's face had been.

"Now the hard part," Livia said.

"The hard part is done. The hard part was you knocking on that door three days ago."

Her eyes came up fast, sharp. "Sable—"

"You want passage. Fine. You get passage. But we do this properly, which means we do it honestly, which means you tell me what they know about you before I build you a new life on top of whatever they've already built a file on top of the old one."

For a long moment she said nothing. She picked up the fine brush and set it down again, and I understood that she was arranging her answer the way she'd always arranged her answers, in order of what would hurt least.

"They have my name," she said finally. "They have my name and my late husband's name and the fact that I sold the house in Corren two weeks before the order came down freezing sales. Someone talked. Not me. But someone did, and now 'Livia Ashgrove' is a woman who cannot board a ship or buy bread in three provinces."

"Then Livia Ashgrove stays dead tonight too. We bury them both." I slid the blank stock toward her. "What were you called before Father decided we needed respectable names?"

She blinked. "What?"

"Before. When Mother was alive and drunk on plum brandy every solstice and called us by whatever came into her head. What did she call you?"

A silence. Then, quietly, wonderingly, as if retrieving something from a house long sold: "Vesna. She called me Vesna. Spring, because I was born in March and wouldn't stop crying until the thaw."

"And she called me—" I almost couldn't say it. It had been thirty years locked away, and saying it felt like breaking a seal that had been holding something valuable together. "Pike. Because I swam upstream and bit everything."

Livia laughed for real this time, and it cracked something open in the room, some old sealed window letting in cold clean air. "Pike. Gods, I'd forgotten. You bit the postman."

"He deserved it. He read our letters."

"He read everyone's letters. That was the job."

"Still deserved it."

And just like that we were working. That was the thing about sisters, I thought, watching her hand steady over the stock, mixing ink to match the clerk's faded blue-black by eye alone—that was the thing no one told you. You could be strangers for fifteen years, wounded strangers, proud ones, and the moment you sat down at the same table with a task between you, all the old fluency came back like a language you'd pretended to forget. She mixed; I knew when to say *more copper*. I cut the stencil; she knew to turn it a half-degree against the grain because official stamps were always cut lazy, always slightly off true, and a perfect forgery was the most suspicious thing in any inspector's drawer.

"How do you know that?" she asked, watching me angle the stamp. "About the stamps."

"Because I've forged four hundred of them and been caught zero times, and the only reason is vanity. Forgers fall in love with perfection. Inspectors are looking for someone in love with perfection."

"You sound like Father's ledgers."

"I sound like nothing of his."

"You do, though. The way you check things twice." She said it without malice, which somehow made it land harder. "He checked things twice. He counted the silver every night even though no one had stolen anything in forty years."

I pressed the stamp down and held it for the count of five, breathing slowly, and did not answer, because she was right and because being right about Father was still, after everything, a kind of trespass.

* * *

### Livia

My sister had become a person made entirely of procedure, and I loved her anyway, which surprised me more than the forgery did.

I watched her work—the economy of her hands, the way she never reached for a tool twice—and I thought: *she learned this the way I learned to smile at tax assessors.* We had both built armor, hers out of competence, mine out of charm, and here at the table the two armors fit together like they'd been cast from the same mold, which of course they had. Same house. Same father counting silver. Same mother naming us after fish and seasons because the truth of our names was too heavy to say aloud.

"Vesna Pemberton," Sable said, testing it. "It doesn't scan. Pemberton isn't a spring name. It's a bank name."

"So change the surname too. Widows remarry. It's practically mandatory."

"To whom?"

"To a merchant. Dead, obviously. Everyone I love ends up dead, so we may as well make him useful." I heard my own voice go flat and was sorry for it, but not sorry enough to take it back. Grief that has walked nine hundred miles earns the right to be flat.

Sable's brush paused above the paper. "Corren," she said carefully. "Your husband. The officer's people took him?"

"They took him the night the garrison changed hands. They asked him questions for eleven days. On the twelfth they returned what was left and billed us for the interrogation." I kept my eyes on the ink. This was the part of the story I had rehearsed for the road, and rehearsed stories come out smooth, and smoothness is its own kind of lie. "So yes. Make me a widow twice over if it helps. I'm fluent."

"Sable—"

"Don't. Don't do the voice. If you do the voice I will put my head down on this table and ruin the document, and then neither of us sleeps."

She did not do the voice. That was mercy, of a sort—the Ashgrove kind, which is to say mercy administered silently and never acknowledged afterward. Instead she said, "Merchant. Grain, maybe. Grain merchants die well; there's always a plausible fortune and a plausible enemy." And she began writing the history of a man who had never lived, giving him a birthplace, a trade route, a fever, and I sat beside her adding details the way we used to add verses to songs in the cart on market days, each of us trying to out-invent the other.

By midnight we had built Vesna Marchetti, widow of Orlan Marchetti, grain factor of Corren, fleeing the collapse of her husband's estate with one son and no prospects. The son was the problem. Documents for children are scrutinized hardest, because everyone knows children are how smugglers move contraband and how mothers move themselves.

"Tomas needs papers that predate him," Sable said, frowning at the stock. "He looks six. Six-year-olds have baptismal records, vaccination scars, school registers—"

"He doesn't look six. He looks small for eight, which is different, and it gives us room. And his records burned." I said it simply, because it was simple. "Half the records in the river provinces burned. No one will question a burned parish."

"Everyone will question a burned parish. That's the first thing they teach inspectors to pity." She rubbed her eyes with the heel of her hand, and I saw how tired she was, tired in the marrow, the kind that doesn't sleep off. "We give him a mother instead. A nursemaid's boy, registered under the household name, orphaned with the household. Orphans travel on their guardian's papers. It's thinner, but it bends."

"It bends exactly right. Do it."

We bent it. Somewhere past two o'clock the lamp burned low and I trimmed the wick, and Sable—without looking up, without any change in her voice—said, "You hold the stencil steadier than I remembered."

"You remember nothing about my hands. You left before you could have."

The words came out sharper than I intended, and I watched them hit, saw her shoulders take them the way our father's mule used to take the whip: without surprise, without protest, just a settling deeper into the weight. I should have taken it back. Fifteen years of silence had earned her the right to that wound, and I had just reopened it to watch it bleed, because hurt people hoard their hurts like coin.

But she only said, "No. I suppose I left early," and passed me the drying sand, and we went back to work, and that—God help us both—that was forgiveness, Ashgrove-style: administered silently, never acknowledged, worth more than the spoken kind because it cost the speaker visibly more.

* * *

### Sable

Toward the end we stopped talking altogether, and it was the best conversation we'd had in years.

There is a thing that happens when two people work with total trust on a single object. Your breathing synchronizes. You hand tools before they're asked for. Livia would tilt the page toward the lamp and I would already have the blotter ready; I would reach for the second pen and find it already uncapped in her fingers. We had done this as girls with homework and lies, and apparently the skill had survived intact beneath everything else, fossilized, waiting. I found it almost unbearable—not the work, but the ease of it. All those years apart, and the machinery still meshed on the first try. What else had survived? What had I thrown away that had merely been sleeping?

The last document was the transit pass, the dangerous one, the one that required an actual occupied-territory seal rather than a republic one. I had the seal. Getting it had cost me the dinner with Major Voss and the whole slow poison of that evening, and I did not tell Livia what it had cost, and she did not ask, because she had read the shape of my silence the way she used to read my fevers when we shared a bed as children.

"Three days," she said, checking the dates. "This pass expires in three days."

"Four. The date's smudged. Smudged dates get generously interpreted."

"By whom?"

"By clerks who want to go home."

She laughed softly and blew the sand from the finished pages, and in the lamplight, for one unguarded moment, she looked sixteen again—before the marriage, before the house, before the choosing that had split our road in two. I looked away first. Some things you can't look at directly, like suns and sisters.

We laid the documents out in a row to dry: Vesna Marchetti, widow; Tomas Marchetti, ward; the pass that would carry them both to the harbor. One coat hung on the door behind us—it was mine, the courier's coat with the false seams, and I caught Livia looking at it with an expression I couldn't parse.

"What?" I said.

"When we were girls," she said slowly, "we had one winter coat between us. You remember. Father's arithmetic. I got it Mondays, Wednesdays, Fridays; you got the rest, and you cried about it every single week."

"I did not cry."

"You wept, Pike. You wept like a professional." She smiled, and it wobbled at the edges. "And then one Monday I wore it to the river against the rules and fell through the ice up to my waist, and you gave me your days for a month and never once mentioned it, and Mother found out and named you Pike for the second time, for biting your tongue about it."

I stared at her. "I forgot that."

"I didn't. I've carried it for thirty years. It was the last kind thing you did before you started hating me." Her voice stayed level, but her hands, I noticed, had gone very still on the table, the way hands go still around something breakable. "So. One coat. It seems fitting that we end the way we started."

"It wasn't the last kind thing."

"No?"

"No." I stood, took the coat off its hook, and put it around her shoulders, because the room was cold and because some debts you settle at 3 a.m. with a garment and no speech. "It was just the last one I let you see."

She pulled the coat closed. She didn't thank me. I hadn't expected her to, and the not-thanking was itself a fluency, ours, private, complete.

Upstairs, a floorboard creaked—Marta, or the boy, or the house settling—and then, faintly, the sound that undid me: Tomas's voice, low and sleep-slurred, saying a word I couldn't catch. My false name, probably. The one I'd given him weeks ago, the one he'd broken his silence to speak, the one that meant I existed somewhere outside my own head now, in a child's mouth, in a sister's memory, in a landlady's careful kindness.

Invisibility had been my religion for fifteen years, and in one night two people had converted me without ever once arguing doctrine.

Livia gathered the dry documents and squared their edges, brisk again, competent again, and I loved her for the briskness too, for knowing that tenderness can't be sustained past dawn without becoming a wound.

"Dawn," she said. "When does the harbor road open?"

"At first light. There'll be a checkpoint at the tollhouse—there always is."

"Then we walk through it," she said, tucking the papers inside the coat, inside my coat, against her heart, "and we don't stop walking until the water."

She was asleep within the hour, sitting upright in the chair with her chin dropped to her chest, still wearing the coat. I covered her with the blanket from my own bed and stood a moment in the dark, listening to her breathe, memorizing the sound against the day I might need to live without it.

I did not yet know that in the morning, before the tollhouse, before the harbor, the dispatch case in the cellar would be opened, and the final orders I carried would show me the price of everything we had just built—and that the list of names inside those codes would include, in the republic's neat and merciless hand, my own.

I only knew that the lamp had burned out, that my sister slept in my coat, and that for the first time since the border, I was afraid of losing something more than my life.
