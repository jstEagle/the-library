## 13. The Dispatch

The inland road to Vesseny took two days by cart and half a day more on foot, and Sable spent all of it rehearsing the shape of her own obedience.

She had done this before—the final delivery, the last link in a chain she'd been forging for eleven years. There was always a final delivery. The republic ran on them the way a dying man runs on morphine: one more, one more, and the pain recedes just far enough to let you believe the next dose will be the one that saves you. She knew better. She had carried enough dispatches through enough checkpoints to understand that the state she served was a body already cold, still twitching with orders because no one had thought to stop writing them.

But the Atlas waited in the harbor at Corvane, and her berth waited on the Atlas, and so she walked.

The cart driver who took her as far as the river crossing was a beet farmer named Hulik who talked about soil the way other men talked about women—with a tenderness that embarrassed them both. Sable let him talk. Talking drivers were useful; they watched their own mouths instead of their passengers' hands. She sat among the sacks with Tomas's mended coat folded under her head—she'd taken to carrying it on long trips, though she would have denied it under torture—and thought about the arithmetic of distance. Two hundred kilometers inland. Two hundred kilometers between her and the ship that meant survival, and every one of those kilometers bought with a lie she told herself each morning: *this is duty, this is duty, this is only duty.*

The occupation had thinned here, away from the coast. Vesseny was too far from anything worth occupying, and so the occupiers had left behind only the architecture of control: a requisitioned schoolhouse flying a flag she refused to look at directly, a curfew bell rung by a boy no older than fourteen who rang it with the apologetic rhythm of someone doing a chore he hated. The town smelled of coal smoke and cabbage and the particular sourness of places that have been waiting a long time for news that never comes.

The drop was the old granary on Kettle Street, third door from the corner, beneath the loose brick where the downspout met the foundation.

Sable went at dusk, when the light made everyone a stranger and strangers were invisible. The codes rode against her spine this time, sewn into the lining of her coat in a strip of oilcloth—her old habit, the border habit, the habit from Chapter One of her life, if lives could be said to have chapters. She had carried paper across frozen borders and through burning cities. She had carried paper while men died around her for carrying less. Paper had never once betrayed her. It was the one relationship in her life that had held.

She knelt at the granary wall, worked the brick loose with a fingernail, and found the hollow empty except for a tin matchbox and inside it a slip of pasteboard bearing a single printed line:

*DELIVERY CONFIRMED — PRESENT THIS AT THE CHANCERY — 14 MULBERRY ROW*

Not the usual protocol. The usual protocol was silence, a clean handoff, and a new set of instructions arriving by channels she wasn't meant to question. This was different. This was an invitation.

*Present this.* As though she were a guest. As though eleven years of service culminated not in another sealed envelope but in a room where someone wanted to look at her face.

She stood in the alley with the matchbox in her palm and felt the first cold thread of something she couldn't yet name. Fear came later. What came first was the courier's instinct, honed across a decade: *when the pattern breaks, the danger is real.*

She went anyway. Of course she went anyway. That was the whole of what she was—a woman who went anyway—and it would take her years to learn whether that had been courage or simply training wearing courage's clothes.

* * *

Mulberry Row had been a merchant's street once, before the war reorganized the city's priorities. The chancery occupied a narrow townhouse with a green door and a brass plate polished to a modest gleam—modesty itself a kind of camouflage, she noted, the same trick she'd used for years. No guard at the door. A woman in a gray cardigan opened it before Sable knocked, which meant there had been eyes on her since the bridge.

"Courier Pemberton," the woman said. Not a question.

"Not my name," Sable said automatically.

"It is tonight." The woman stepped aside. "Everyone in this house is exactly who they say they are. You'll find it disorienting."

The room beyond the green door was small and warm and lit by a single lamp with a green shade, and it contained a desk, two chairs, and a man Sable recognized from photographs she had memorized seven years ago and never expected to see outside of them: Aldous Renk, Deputy Secretary of the Interior, or what remained of an interior for anything to be deputy secretary of.

He looked older than his photographs. He looked like a man who had been awake for a month.

"Courier." He gestured to the chair opposite the desk. "Sit. Please."

She sat. The cardigan woman closed the door and remained beside it, arms folded, a fixture rather than a presence.

"You delivered the codes," Renk said. "You should know they arrived intact. Your work has been—" he paused, searching, and Sable understood he was not searching for the word but deciding whether to spend it, "—impeccable. Eleven years. Four hundred and some dispatches. Do you know how many couriers survived four hundred dispatches?"

"One," Sable said.

Renk almost smiled. "One."

"I'd like my papers renewed. And confirmation of my berth on the *Atlas*. Those were the terms of the delivery."

"They were." He opened a drawer. Did not remove anything from it. "Before I give you what you've earned, you've asked—I know you have, they all ask eventually—to know what you've been carrying. All these years. You've earned that answer too, I think. Though I warn you, most people who receive it wish they hadn't."

Sable kept her hands still in her lap. Her heartbeat, traitorously, did not. "I carry what I'm given. That's the work."

"That's the discipline." Renk drew a folder from the drawer—thin, gray, unmarked—and laid it flat between them but did not open it. His hand rested on it the way a man rests his hand on a dog he may have to put down. "You've wondered why the final dispatches increased in weight. Why the ciphers changed twice in three months. Why your routes moved inland, away from the ports, in the last year of a war whose only remaining theater *was* the ports."

"I assumed logistics."

"You assumed correctly. Evacuation logistics." He opened the folder and turned it toward her. "This is the master manifest. The evacuation priority list for Operation Lantern—the *Atlas*, her sister ships, everything leaving this coast. Every name the republic intends to save."

Sable looked down.

She had expected weapons schedules. Supply chains. The coordinates of buried archives, perhaps—the state's silver and its secrets being ferried out ahead of its people. She had prepared herself, in the way she prepared for everything, to learn something hard.

What she saw was names. Columns of them. Typeset in the same plain font as ration cards and death notices. Names with numbers beside them—berth assignments, vessel designations, priority classes. And she read them the way she read everything now, with the automatic cataloguing eye she could not switch off, the eye that had catalogued a mute boy's eyelashes and Marta's flour-dusted hands—

And stopped.

She knew the name in the third row. Not personally. Knew *of* him—Vesek the railway man, Vesek whose network had run her north along the coast line for two years, Vesek who had taught her which stationmasters could be bribed with coffee and which needed tobacco. Priority Class A. Berth confirmed.

She scanned lower. More names she knew. The printer's widow from Corvane. The surgeon who had once stitched her forearm without asking questions. Names, names, names—four hundred of them, maybe five hundred, the republic's chosen, its essential, its beloved—and then, at the bottom of the second page, in a section headed *SECOND TIER — SUBJECT TO CAPACITY*, a list that was longer, and looser, and written in a hand less certain of itself.

Her finger moved down it without her permission.

She found Livia's married name before she understood she was looking for it—found it because Renk had said *master manifest* and because some part of her had known, since the night her sister appeared at the safehouse gate with frost in her hair, that the republic's ledgers touched everything she loved. But Livia's name was not there. Livia had never been registered; Livia existed nowhere in the state's records, which meant Livia existed nowhere in the state's mercy either. The absence of the name was worse than the name would have been. Her sister was not even worth abandoning on paper.

And then, in the third tier—a section with no heading at all, just a continuation of columns, typeset smaller, as if the typesetter himself had wanted to make these names easier to lose—she found hers.

*Pemberton, S. — Courier Corps (ret. classification pending) — Status: NON-VIABLE.*

Non-viable. The phrase had a bureaucratic elegance to it, a tidiness. Not *condemned*, not *expendable*—those words belonged to armies. *Non-viable* belonged to accountants. It was the word you used for a shipment gone rotten, a loan unrecoverable, a pregnancy that would not hold. It was the word that meant: we have calculated, and the sum of you is less than the cost of your passage.

She heard herself speak. Her voice sounded like someone else's, level and dry, the checkpoint voice. "Define non-viable."

Renk closed the folder. "It means what you think it means."

"The manifest is coded into the dispatches I've carried for the past year." Not a question. She was assembling it as she spoke, the whole machine laying itself bare in her mind: the heavier envelopes, the changed ciphers, the routes pushed inland. "I've been distributing the evacuation list. Name by name, cell by cell. I've been telling the republic who to save."

"You've been telling the republic who *it chose* to save. Yes." Renk leaned back. In the lamplight his face was the color of the paper he governed. "Someone had to move that information, and it couldn't travel by wire—it doesn't exist by wire, it exists only in pieces, in code, in the hands of people who don't know what they're holding. You knew what you were holding, Courier. You just never asked. And we never offered to tell you. That was the arrangement. That was always the arrangement."

"And my berth. On the Atlas. That was the payment."

"Your berth was conditional on completion of service." He said it gently, which was the cruelest available register. "Your service isn't complete. There's a final dispatch—one last circuit, north, into the occupied zone proper. After that, your status would be reviewed."

"Reviewed." She tasted the word. "By whom? The men on the ship?"

"By the process."

"The process." She almost laughed. It came out as breath, a ghost of a laugh, nothing. "There are nine hundred people in that city, Minister. Maybe a thousand. And this list holds five hundred. I've spent a year delivering the arithmetic of who lives, and I never once appeared in my own sums until tonight."

"No courier appears in their own sums. That's what makes a courier useful."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere below them a clock struck eight, and the sound seemed to come up through the floorboards like water rising. Sable became aware of details with terrible clarity: the green shade of the lamp, a chip in the desk's edge, the cardigan woman's hands folded at the door, the way Renk's thumbnail had a split in it, a human flaw in a man she was rapidly converting to architecture. Cataloguing. Always cataloguing. It was the only thing keeping her upright.

"You let me believe," she said slowly, "for eleven years. Through checkpoints where they shot couriers in the street. Through winters. You let me believe the state I bled for intended to bleed back. To carry me out. That was the wage."

"The wage was purpose," Renk said. "And you were paid in full."

For a moment she saw it with perfect clarity—what she must have looked like to them, all these years. Not a person. A route. A pair of legs attached to a memory good enough not to ask questions, moving through occupied country with the state's mercy sewn into her hem, distributing salvation to strangers while assuming, with the modest vanity of the dutiful, that her own name must be somewhere in the ledger too. She had believed her invisibility protected her. It had. It had protected her right up until the moment it made her weightless—easy to use, easy to omit, easy to classify as *non-viable* by men who had never seen her face and never wanted to.

Marta had seen her face. Marta fed a boy Sable hadn't asked for. Marta had known her name within a week, had probably known it from the first night, had watched her write letters to a sister who would never read them—and had said nothing, had simply set out another plate. Visibility, warmth, witness: the things Sable had spent a decade treating as fatal exposures were the only things in her life that had ever treated *her* as real.

The state had finally seen her clearly tonight, and what it had seen was a number it could subtract.

"I'll need the final dispatch," Sable said.

Renk blinked. Whatever he had prepared for—tears, rage, a drawn weapon—he had not prepared for the checkpoint voice continuing to function. "You'll take the circuit?"

"I'll take the circuit." She stood. Her legs held. She was distantly proud of them. "Where is it?"

The cardigan woman brought it: a slim envelope, heavier than it should have been, sealed with the black wax of the Interior. Sable took it and felt its weight settle against her palm like a stone dropped into still water.

"For what it's worth," Renk said, and for the first time he sounded tired rather than administrative, "the review would have gone in your favor. I'd have seen to it. The corps doesn't leave its best behind by choice. Only by arithmetic."

"Then burn your arithmetic," Sable said, "and count faces instead."

She walked out through the green door into the dark of Mulberry Row, and the cold air hit her like a slap, and she welcomed it. Behind her the lamp glowed in its window, the republic's last warm room, and she did not look back. Looking back was for people with something to return to.

She walked to the edge of town, to the stable where she'd paid a boy to keep a horse, and she did not mount it. She stood in the straw-dark doorway with the envelope in both hands and turned it over, and over, and over.

Inside was a cipher she could break in twenty minutes. Inside was the last piece of a list she had spent a year assembling without knowing it. Inside, somewhere in the columns, were more names—names of people like Vesek, like the surgeon, like the printer's widow, people who trusted the republic the way she had trusted it, who had traded their invisibility for a promise written in a dead language by living liars.

And somewhere in those columns, or in the margins, or in the space where a margin should be—there would be more classifications. More tiers. More arithmetic. Perhaps even, this time, a name she loved.

Tomas's name was not in any ledger. That was the one mercy. The boy existed nowhere, officially—which meant, she understood suddenly, with a clarity that felt like falling, that the boy could exist *anywhere*. The lists bound only the listed.

The horse shifted behind her. The envelope crackled softly in her grip.

Sable Pemberton, courier of a collapsing republic, holder of four hundred and some impeccable dispatches, stood in the doorway of a stable in Vesseny and realized she was holding the state's entire moral authority in her two hands, in wax and oilcloth and cipher—and that no one on earth could stop her from doing with it whatever she decided.

She thought of fire. She thought of the harbor. She thought of one berth.

Then she untacked the horse, and swung up into the saddle, and turned north—not toward the coast, not toward the *Atlas*, but toward the final circuit, because some part of her still needed to see the whole of the lie with her own eyes before she answered it.

The last dispatch of her career rode against her spine, and for the first time in eleven years, Sable Pemberton was not certain whose side it was on—or whose side *she* was.
