## 14. What Marta Cannot Carry

Marta had counted the rooms of her house so many times she no longer needed to walk them. Eleven, if you included the pantry and the room behind the stove that her father had built for smuggled tobacco in a war older than any of his children. She could tell you what each one held with her eyes shut. The chipped basin on the second-floor landing. The water stain above the kitchen door shaped like a kneeling woman. Her mother's bed beneath the east window, where the light came first and stayed longest.

She did not need to count anymore. But she did it anyway, every morning, the way other people prayed.

The knock came at dusk—two short, one long. Sable's knock. Marta wiped her hands on her apron out of habit rather than need; they were already clean. She had spent the afternoon rendering fat and boiling linen, and the whole house smelled of lye and tallow, of keeping things going.

"Come in before the street sees you," she said, opening the door herself.

Sable stepped inside with Tomas's hand in hers, and the boy came in after her like a small tide following the moon. He was speaking more now—not much, but words arrived some mornings like birds that had decided, against all sense, to winter here. *Bread.* *Cold.* *Look.* Marta treasured every one of them the way she treasured the buttons in her mother's sewing tin: not useful, exactly, but proof of something that had once been whole.

"You look terrible," Marta told Sable, which was true. "Sit down. There's soup."

"I can't stay long."

"You said that the last three times, and each time you ate two bowls." Marta ladled the soup without waiting for an answer. This was, she had learned, how one handled Sable Pemberton: not by asking but by simply placing things in front of her until she surrendered. A bowl. A chair. A reason to stay five minutes longer than she intended. It was like anchoring a boat that wanted desperately to drift.

Tomas climbed onto the bench beside Sable and put his hands around the bowl before she'd even set it down, warming his fingers against the clay. Sable watched him do it with an expression Marta had come to know well—the look of a woman checking her own heart for damage and finding, to her irritation, that it still worked.

"The ship," Sable said quietly, when the boy was occupied. "The Atlas. I have news."

Marta kept stirring though there was nothing left to stir. "You've found berths."

"I've found possibilities. It changes daily. The military keeps taking cabins back. But yes—I think I can get Livia passage. And Tomas." She paused, and Marta heard the pause the way one hears a floorboard creak: knowing precisely which weight is about to come down on it. "And you."

*And you.*

There it was. Marta set the ladle down and folded her hands, giving herself a moment, because a woman who had spent forty years caring for others knew that her own moments had to be stolen deliberately or they would never come at all.

"Mama can't travel," she said. "You know that."

"There are medical transports—"

"There are rumors of medical transports. There were rumors last month too, and the month before, and the people who waited for them are buried behind the church." Marta's voice stayed level. It always stayed level; her mother used to say she'd been born with a lid on her pot. "Even if a transport existed, even if it stopped at our door, she wouldn't survive the road to the harbor. You've seen the harbor road. Carts overturned, people trampled. She weighs nothing now, Sable. A strong wind at the docks would take her."

"She would want you to go."

"She has never once said so, and neither will you say it again." Marta sat down across from her, folding herself onto the hard chair she kept by the stove—the caretaker's chair, worn to the shape of one body. "My mother carried me out of a burning village when I was six years old. She ran three kilometers with me on her back and my brother in her arms, and my brother died anyway, of fever, on the road, and she never once let me see her cry about it. Not once in fifty years. Do you understand what I'm telling you? That woman paid everything she had to keep me alive. I am not going to balance that account by abandoning her in a bed at the end of the world because the arithmetic got difficult."

Sable said nothing. Tomas looked up from his soup, sensing weather in the room, and Sable put a steadying hand on his shoulder.

"I'm not refusing out of stubbornness," Marta went on, gentler now, because she could see the guilt assembling itself behind Sable's eyes like troops at a border. "I want you to hear that. People think staying is what you do when you're too frightened or too tired to run. Sometimes it is. But this—" she gestured upward, toward the east window and the bed beneath it, toward the small sounds her mother made in her sleep, sounds like pages turning in a book no one read aloud anymore— "this is not surrender. This is the work. Someone has to be the ground. Everyone talks about the ones who escape. Nobody writes songs about the ones who hold the door."

"You could come with us as far as the city," Sable tried. "See the doctors there—"

"And leave the house? Leave the hens, the stores, the cellar?" Marta shook her head. "No. Besides, someone has to stay who knows faces. The occupation officers come through here twice a week now. They ask questions at the well. If they come asking about a woman named Sable Pemberton—or whatever your real name turns out to be—and find an empty house with cold ashes, what does that tell them? But if they find an old landlady with a sick mother and nothing to hide..." She smiled, thin as paper. "I'm better armor empty than full. You taught me that yourself, though you don't remember doing it. The night you first came, three years ago. You stood in my doorway and lied to me beautifully, and I fed you anyway, and I thought: *there is a woman carrying so much weight she's forgotten she's allowed to set any of it down.*"

"I never lied to you," Sable said.

"You lied to me constantly and competently, dear. It's why I trusted you." Marta reached over and squeezed her wrist—once, briefly, the way one touches a hot thing one loves. "Now. Before you go. Give me your hand."

Sable hesitated, then held out her right hand, palm up, the way one does for coins or handcuffs. Marta ignored it and took both of Sable's hands in hers instead, holding them over the table between the soup bowls, in the lamplight, in front of the child.

"I want you to take something of mine," Marta said. "Not for safekeeping. Don't insult me with *safekeeping*. For keeping. Do you understand the difference?"

"Marta—"

"A locket. My mother's mother's. Silver, worn thin, with a chip in the back where my grandfather pried it open during a famine to sell the chain—he sold the chain and kept the locket, and we've laughed about it for three generations, because he was a fool and the chain was worth nothing and the locket was everything." Marta's voice caught only slightly, and she was proud of the *only slightly*. "It's upstairs in my room. Inside there's a scrap of cloth from my grandmother's wedding dress and a curl of hair that might be mine and might be my sister's; Mama mixed them up years ago and refused to correct the error, said God would sort it out. I want it to leave this country. I want it on a ship, on the water, past the mines and the guns. I want it somewhere the soldiers can't reach."

"Why are you telling me this now?"

"Because tomorrow you'll argue with me again, and the day after that, and by then there won't be time for goodbyes done properly. Goodbyes done properly take preparation. Like anything else." She released Sable's hands. "Also because I'm a coward about crying and I'd rather do it in front of you while there's still soup to blame my red eyes on."

For a long moment Sable didn't move. Then Tomas, who understood more than anyone gave him credit for, slid off the bench, crossed the kitchen, and put his arms around Marta's waist—a sudden, fierce pressure, his face pressed into her apron, smelling of lye and tallow and soup steam. Marta closed her eyes and rested one hand on his head, on the fine, uncombed hair, and thought: *this is the account. This is the payment. Not silver. This.*

When he let go, Sable was standing too, and her face had the terrible nakedness of a mask half-removed.

"I'll get you out," Sable said. "I'll find another berth. I'll—"

"No, you won't, and you shouldn't, and if you waste a single hour trying, I will haunt you so thoroughly you'll long for ordinary ghosts." Marta kissed her then, on both cheeks, the old way, the way her mother had kissed her in the burning village before setting her down and turning back toward the smoke. "Go up. Second door on the left. The locket's in the blue box under the window, wrapped in a handkerchief. Take it and don't thank me. Thanking me makes it charity, and this isn't charity. It's freight. You're a courier. Carry it."

Upstairs, Marta listened to the floorboards announce Sable's crossing—the third board still squeaked; she'd never fixed it, some superstition about houses needing to speak—and then the soft scrape of the blue box, and then silence, and then footsteps coming back down heavier than they went up, because grief adds weight even to the lightest cargo.

They stood together in the doorway. The street beyond was dark and quiet, the kind of quiet that was never truly quiet anymore, just listening.

"When this is over," Sable said, "when there's an *over*—"

"If there's an over, you'll write me a letter. A long, tedious letter, with the weather in it. That's all I ask." Marta smiled. "Now go. And take the boy somewhere warm tonight. His ears are red."

She watched them go down the street—two small figures, the tall one bent slightly toward the small one, as though leaning into wind—and she stood in the doorway until they turned the corner, because watching people safely out of sight was the last duty of anyone who stays behind.

Then she closed the door, banked the fire, climbed the stairs, and settled into the chair beside her mother's bed.

Her mother's eyes opened, pale and clear as winter water.

"The courier woman," her mother whispered. "She took it?"

"She took it."

"Good girl." A pause. The wind moved the curtain. "And you, Marta? Will you be a good girl too, and stop pretending you aren't afraid?"

Marta took her mother's hand, all bones and history, and answered honestly, because lying to the dying was a debt she refused to owe anyone.

"I'm terrified, Mama," she said. "But I'm staying."

Outside, far off toward the harbor, a ship's horn sounded—long, low, patient, like something counting.
