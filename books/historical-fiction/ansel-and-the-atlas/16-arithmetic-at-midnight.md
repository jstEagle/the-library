## 16. Arithmetic at Midnight

The house held its breath after Livia went to bed.

Sable sat at the kitchen table with the lamp turned low, a single candle guttering beside her because Marta rationed the oil like everything else now—oil, bread, hope. The stove had gone cold hours ago, and the cold came up through the floorboards and settled into her bones as though it had been waiting for her all evening for exactly this: to sit alone with numbers.

She was good at numbers. She had always been good at numbers. It was the first thing they taught you in courier training, before cipher work, before route memorization, before the courses on how to walk so that no one's eyes snagged on you like a nail on cloth. They taught you that a courier's life was arithmetic. Weight of paper against weight of risk. Distance against daylight. What you carried against what you could afford to lose. Every mission was an equation, and the couriers who survived were the ones who solved it honestly—who did not flatter themselves, who did not round down the danger or round up their own luck.

She took out a sheet of scrap paper, the kind Marta saved from wrapping parcels, and sharpened a pencil stub with a knife until the shavings curled into the flour dust on the table.

*One berth.*

That was the fixed quantity. Everything else moved around it like planets around a dying sun. The *Atlas* sailed in four days—three now, if you counted honestly, and she counted honestly; it was the one virtue she had never managed to shed. One berth remained under the name she had forged, in the hand she had practiced for eleven years until her false signature flowed more naturally than her true one. One berth, guaranteed by nothing but ink and the desperation of a shipping clerk who owed his own escape to a favor Sable had done him two winters ago, when favors were still cheap.

*Three people.*

She wrote the names. She wrote them small, as if smallness might make the problem smaller.

*Tomas.*

The boy slept in the alcove off the kitchen, in the bed Marta had made up with quilts that smelled of lavender and other people's lifetimes. He had been with her since autumn. He had spoken once—a single word, her false name, *Vessa*, offered up to her in the dark like something he'd found and thought she'd lost—and since then he had not spoken again, but he watched her with eyes that catalogued everything, and she recognized the habit because it was hers. He had learned invisibility the way children learn language: by immersion. Someone had taught him to be unnoticeable before he was old enough to know there was any other way to be. And then someone had set him on a doorstep and told a stranger to forget his face, and the stranger—the coward, the saint, whoever he had been—had failed to forget it, and so now Sable knew the shape of his ears and the way his left thumb hooked into his sleeve when he was frightened and the exact pitch of silence that meant he was awake and pretending not to be.

He was eight years old. She had done the arithmetic on him months ago, without meaning to, the way you do sums on anything that crosses your path. Eight years old meant he had spent nearly half his life inside the occupation. Eight years old meant he would remember almost none of what was being erased, unless someone carried it out for him. Eight years old meant that if he stayed, his odds were the worst of anyone's—worse than hers, worse than Livia's—because the occupation did not conscript boys his age; it simply absorbed them, the way water absorbs salt, until nothing distinguishable remained.

Tomas needed the berth. That was not even a question. That was a premise, like the existence of the ship itself.

She wrote it and looked at it and felt nothing move in her chest, which frightened her more than grief would have. Grief would have meant she was already counting him as lost. This flat certainty—*the boy goes*—was just the old arithmetic doing its cold work, and she distrusted herself enough to keep checking it.

*Livia.*

Her sister slept upstairs, in the room under the eaves, behind a door that no longer needed to be locked because there was nothing left inside worth stealing and both of them knew it. Livia had arrived nine days ago at the safehouse gate with frostbite blooming on two fingers and her dead husband's papers sewn into her coat lining—the same trick, the same seam, the same needlework their mother had taught them both at the same kitchen table in another country that no longer existed either. Widowed in the spring purges. Hunted since summer. She had stood at the gate with snow in her hair and said, *I'm not asking you to forgive me. I'm asking you to count me*, and Sable had understood, with a clarity that felt like a blade going in sideways, that Livia had known about the arithmetic too. Had always known. Had chosen her words the way Sable chose routes: precisely, with full knowledge of the terrain.

They had spent the last nine days forging documents together, and it had been—there was no other word for it—*easy*. Their hands remembered each other. When Sable mixed the ink too thin, Livia thickened it without being asked. When Livia hesitated over a date, Sable supplied it. They argued about margins the way they had argued about everything twenty years ago, furiously and briefly and without residue, and somewhere in the middle of the third forged transit pass Sable had caught herself laughing, actually laughing, at Livia's impression of their father's disapproving sniff, and the laugh had died in her throat because laughter was a luxury item now and she could not account for it.

Livia needed the berth. Her name was on lists—Sable had seen the lists, had carried some of them. Stayed behind, Livia lasted a season. Perhaps less. She had her husband's enemies and none of his protections, and she had a face that people remembered, which in this country was a death sentence wearing a pretty mask.

But—

Sable pressed the pencil hard enough to dent the paper.

*But I need the berth.*

There it was. The term she kept trying to strike from the equation and couldn't. Because it wasn't vanity, wasn't fear—or it wasn't only those things, and the arithmetic refused to work if she lied to herself about her own weight. She was the courier. She was the one with the working papers, the transit passes, the contacts in the harbor office, the face that no one had ever bothered to remember. If the berth went to Tomas alone, he arrived at whatever waited on the far side of the water as a child traveling on documents he couldn't explain, attached to a name no one would come to claim. Children alone on arrival lists became wards. Wards became labor. She had seen the manifests. She knew what the receiving countries did with unaccompanied minors when the ships came back empty and the quotas stayed full.

And if the berth went to Livia—if Sable put her sister on that gangway with Tomas's hand in hers and a story stitched between them—then perhaps. Perhaps. Two travelers, aunt and nephew, plausible, portable, survivable. Livia was quick. Livia had talked her way past harder checkpoints than the harbor road with less convincing papers. Livia would lie beautifully, because Livia had spent twenty years practicing a marriage the way other people practice an instrument, and lying beautifully was a transferable skill.

So: Livia and Tomas. The equation solved itself, didn't it? Two lives where one berth seemed to allow only one, solved by the simple expedient of folding the child into the woman's paperwork. She had already drafted it. It was sitting in the drawer three feet from her elbow, the finished forgery, the elegant solution, the thing she should have burned weeks ago if she intended to do the arithmetic honestly.

The problem was the third line. The problem was the word she had written at the top of the page and kept circling like a moth around a lamp it could not stop loving.

*Me.*

Because she had run the scenarios. All night, all week, every hour that Livia slept and Tomas pretended to sleep and Marta sat downstairs listening to her mother's ragged breathing through the wall. She had run them the way headquarters ran supply projections, coldly, completely, and every scenario ended the same way.

If she gave up the berth: Livia and Tomas crossed. She stayed. She was useful here—she knew the routes, the safehouses, the faces of forty couriers whose survival depended on her discretion—and she would be useful until the day she wasn't, and then she would be a file in an occupier's cabinet, or a name on a wall, or a lesson taught to the next courier by example. She had accepted this outcome eleven times this week. She had written her own name last on the list every time, in the smallest letters, and had felt the strange clean relief of it, the martyr's discount, and then—every time—she had noticed the relief and been ashamed of it, because relief was not sacrifice. Relief was just another form of escape, available only to people who could afford the fantasy of their own nobility.

And if she kept the berth: if she walked up the gangway alone, with her forged pass and her blank face and her coat hem empty of dispatches for the first time in eleven years—what exactly was she saving? A body. An employee of a state that had stopped existing except as a passenger manifest. She had opened the final dispatch three nights ago. She knew what was in it. She knew that the republic she had served with her anonymity and her ankles and her whole careful invisible life had already decided, in its last functioning hour, who was worth carrying out and who was ballast, and her name was not among the saved. The state had done its own arithmetic, and in the state's ledger she was an expense.

So why was she still solving for herself?

The candle guttered. She trimmed the wick with the same knife that had sharpened the pencil, and in the recovered light she saw the page again, really saw it, the way you see your own face unexpectedly in a shop window—as something foreign, something you'd been carrying around without examining.

Three names. One berth. And underneath, the terms she hadn't let herself write until now, the real coefficients:

*Marta stays.* Not because she must—Marta had been offered a place, had refused it with a gentleness that was worse than refusal, because her mother could not travel and Marta did not believe love could be divided from duty. Marta had pressed her locket into Sable's hand and said, *carry this instead of guilt, it weighs less,* and Sable had carried it ever since against her sternum like a second heartbeat, and it weighed nothing and it weighed everything.

*I stay.* Or I don't. The pencil hovered.

The truth—the thing beneath the arithmetic, the thing the numbers existed to hide—was that Sable Pemberton had built her entire life on the principle of carrying only what could be abandoned. Light load, fast feet, no attachments, no debts, no names. She had left her sister at a train platform twenty years ago because staying would have meant becoming someone's wife, someone's daughter-in-law, someone's *expected*, and she had chosen the road instead and called it freedom. She had taken a child off a doorstep because leaving him there was the one abandonment even her armor couldn't absorb. And now the road had narrowed to a gangway, and the light-load philosophy had arrived at its final audit, and the audit was asking one question:

*What have you been saving it all for?*

Eleven years of invisibility. Eleven years of forgetting her own face in mirrors, of answering to six names, of sleeping with her shoes laced. If she stepped onto the *Atlas* alone, she would arrive on the far shore as a woman with no family, no state, no mission, no name that belonged to her—and she would finally, at the end of everything, understand what she had suspected on the frozen border crossing that began this whole long unraveling: that survival was never the same thing as living, and that she had confused the two so thoroughly, for so long, that she no longer knew which one she wanted.

Whereas if she stayed—

No. She put the pencil down. Staying was not a gift she could give; that was the trap the officer had dressed up as mercy at dinner, the temptation wearing compassion's coat. Staying was just another outcome, and outcomes didn't redeem anyone. The question was never *who deserves the berth*. The question was *who can use it*, and the answer was a child who could not yet defend the memory of his own face, and a sister who had finally, after twenty years, learned to ask for help in exactly seven words.

The solution was in the drawer. It had been in the drawer for days. Livia and Tomas, folded together, plausible, portable. All Sable had to do was accept it, sleep, and spend her remaining days making the harbor road safe for them.

Except.

Except the dispatch. The final codes, still sewn into the lining of her winter coat, still warm from her body, still humming with the republic's last instructions. She had delivered the copy inland; she had kept the original, against protocol, because something in her had wanted proof—proof of what, she hadn't let herself finish the thought. Proof that the betrayal was real. Proof that the list was real. Names to be saved, names to be abandoned, and hers among the latter, typed in a clerk's indifferent hand.

And the list had power. That was the terrible thing she had realized tonight, sitting alone with her cold arithmetic. The list was not just a record. It was a currency. In the wrong hands it was a hunting ledger. But destroyed—destroyed, it was mercy. Burned, the names on it became merely people again, unsorted, unranked, uncondemned. Burned, and the state's last act of judgment dissolved into smoke, and everyone still standing in this occupied city stood equal outside the ledger, which was the only equality the republic had ever actually delivered.

She looked at the drawer. Then at the stairs, where Livia slept. Then at the alcove, where Tomas breathed in the particular rhythm of a child who had finally, after months, begun to trust the dark.

Then she picked up the pencil one last time and wrote, beneath the three names, not a solution but a sentence, the kind of sentence that appears in ledgers only once:

*The arithmetic refuses every answer except the one I am afraid to read.*

She blew out the candle. And in the dark, with the cold rising through the floorboards and the locket ticking against her heart like a clock running out, Sable Pemberton reached for the coat with the dispatches in its hem, and her fingers found the matches.
