## 17. The Burning

The stove in the safehouse had not been lit since October. Marta kept it cold because coal was rationed and because her mother liked the house to feel like winter felt when they were girls—honest about itself, Marta said, unlike everything else in the country. So Sable knelt on the kitchen floor at three in the morning with a cold iron stove and a bundle of paper sewn into her coat hem for eleven years, and she thought: this is what treason looks like. It looks like a woman on her knees before an empty stove.

She had imagined the moment differently. In the versions her mind rehearsed during long walks between cities—the courier's habit of rehearsing disaster, then rehearsing deliverance—there was always fire already burning, always a dramatic wind, always some line of scripture from the republic's founding catechism rising unbidden to her lips. *We carry the light so others may see.* She had recited it at her induction at nineteen, hand flat against a flag, meaning every word the way the young mean things: completely, and without understanding what the words would cost.

Now the light was a problem of logistics, like everything else. No flame. No coal. She had matches—she always had matches—but a stove that cold needed more than a match. It needed paper first, and kindling, and patience, and patience was the one supply she had never learned to carry.

She sat back on her heels and looked at what she'd brought up from the cellar where she'd hidden it behind a loose brick two days ago, after the port, after the manifest, after Livia's face in the lamplight asking for nothing except the one thing Sable could not yet give.

Three packets. The oldest was soft as cloth from years against her body, its wax seals cracked to lacework. The second was the dispatch from Vellmar, carried through two checkpoints in her boot sole. The third was the one she'd delivered inland ten days ago—or half delivered, because delivering it had meant reading it, and reading it had meant knowing.

*Names to be saved. Names to be abandoned.*

Her own name among the abandoned. Not by accident. By arithmetic. The same arithmetic she'd performed at midnight with Livia asleep in the next room and Tomas curled against her back like a small warm comma—the state had performed it first, in some ministry office smelling of ink and cold coffee, men she'd never met deciding with a pencil stroke whether August Ashgrove's daughter lived or became a footnote in occupied territory. They hadn't even used her real name. They'd used her cipher designation. *Kestrel-9. Expendable. Retain for inland relay until final week.*

Retain. Like a coat left at a checkroom.

She unfolded the third packet again, though she knew its contents the way she knew the scar on her palm. The list ran to four pages in the republic's tightest field cipher, the one reserved for personnel, for people rather than materiel. Page one: names bound for the *Atlas*, berths numbered, priorities assigned. Ministers first, naturally. Then the officers of the evacuation board. Then merchants whose contributions had purchased their places in the currency of favors she'd watched trade hands at the harbor office. Then—

Then page three, where the handwriting changed slightly, a different clerk or a colder hour, and the tone shifted from allocation to triage. *Berths reduced by military priority: 214.* And beneath the number, columns. Names struck through. Some with annotations in the margin—a single letter, D, for detained; another mark she eventually understood meant *useful in place*. Couriers. Nurses who knew too many faces. Printers. A schoolteacher from Corvane whose only crime appeared to be literacy.

Sable Pemberton, struck through. Kestrel-9, retained for relay.

And below her name, in the margin, in that second clerk's hand, a note she had read perhaps forty times now and which still did its small work of murder each time: *Asset unaware of status. Maintain.*

Maintain. Keep her carrying. Keep her believing. A mule does not need to know the destination; it needs only the load and the road.

She folded the pages along their old creases and put them on the floor beside the stove, squared like correspondence awaiting reply.

 upstairs, the house ticked and settled. Livia slept badly—Sable could tell by the rhythm of the floorboards' complaint, the way their mother's old house in Corvane had told them both, through the ceiling, when the other was crying. Tomas slept the way children sleep in wartime: totally, suspiciously, like a soldier grabbing rest between watches. He had begun sleeping pressed against Sable's back without asking, and she had stopped pretending to mind around the fourth night, and stopped noticing around the tenth, which frightened her more than either.

Tomorrow they would go to the harbor road. Tonight she had one thing to do, and it required fire.

* * *

She found kindling in the scullery—Marta's hoard, split thin and dry, stacked with the precision of a woman who had survived three occupations by keeping small accounts perfectly. Sable allowed herself a handful. She owed Marta more than kindling and would repay in ways that couldn't be burned, but tonight she took it without asking, which was itself a kind of education: how easily theft comes to the desperate, how the republic's catechisms dissolved not in fire but in a handful of borrowed sticks.

The match caught. The kindling caught. The stove, offended by years of neglect, smoked and spat and finally, grudgingly, drew breath.

Sable fed it the oldest packet first.

She had expected ceremony to overtake her—the trembling hands, the tears, some grand internal reckoning. Instead there was only the physical truth of it: the wax seal bubbled and went dark; eleven years of her life curled at the edges and browned and lifted upward as flame, and the heat came back against her face with a smell like libraries burning, like her own history rendered to ash and carbon. The dispatches she'd bled for. The border crossings where she'd held her breath while soldiers' gloved fingers walked along her coat hem an inch from discovery. The night in Vellmar she'd slept in a drainage culvert with the codes wrapped against her sternum like a second heart.

All of it, fuel. All of it, warmth enough to boil water, if she'd wanted tea.

The strange thing—and she would remember this later, in the country of the staying, when she told the story and Marta laughed her low disbelieving laugh—was how light she felt. Not relieved exactly. Lighter. As if the papers had weighed more than paper, as if eleven years of carrying had calcified into something structural, a spine made of sealed wax and duty, and now that it was smoke her body didn't know how to stand.

She fed it the second packet. Vellmar. The orders that had described an ending. The flame took them faster, hungry now, and through the grate she watched words she'd decoded by lantern light in a frozen room become unreadable, then illegible, then nothing—no longer instructions, no longer betrayal, no longer anything anyone could torture out of her, because torture finds nothing in ash.

That was the point. That was the whole point. She understood it fully only now, kneeling there: the codes were not information anymore. They were liability. Every name on those lists was a person the occupation would hunt if the pages surfaced—names to be saved were names worth interrogating, names to be abandoned were names worth disappearing. The republic had lost the power to save anyone. What remained of it was a weapon pointed at its own people, still clutched in the hands of its last believers.

She had been its hand. Kestrel-9, maintained.

The third packet she held longest. Not from hesitation—she'd finished hesitating somewhere around midnight, in the arithmetic, when every solution failed and the failure itself had been the answer. She held it because she wanted to read her own name one last time. There is a particular discipline in looking directly at the thing that has discarded you. Her mother had taught her that, actually, in a different context, over a different loss: *Look at it, Sable. Things you won't look at own you.*

Sable Pemberton. Struck through.

She said it aloud, once, to the empty kitchen—her true name, the one no checkpoint had heard in eleven years, the one Tomas had somehow spoken in his sleep last week with the terrible accuracy of children—and then she fed the pages to the fire one at a time, watching each column of the saved and the damned rise together, indistinguishable, equal at last in the only court still sitting.

The margins burned last. *Asset unaware of status.* The little letters swelled, blackened, and went.

* * *

The fire had settled to a red murmur when the floorboard above her creaked.

Sable went still, hand moving automatically toward the knife taped under the table—a reflex so deep it preceded thought. But the tread was wrong for an intruder and right for a sister: uneven, favoring the left knee Livia had ruined falling from a horse at seventeen and never admitted to.

Livia came down in Marta's borrowed shawl, hair loose, face sleep-swollen and sharp-eyed all at once, because their family had never produced anyone who could be surprised without immediately calculating.

"I smelled smoke," Livia said.

"Stove."

"At three in the morning."

"The house was cold."

Livia crossed to the stove and stood with her palms out, absorbing the heat the way she'd once stood before their father's hearth, and for a moment the years fell away and they were girls again in Corvane, arguing about nothing, warm for no reason. Then her eyes dropped to the hearth—to the drift of pale ash, to the corner of a page still curling, to the fragment of wax seal glowing its last among the coals—and Sable watched her sister understand everything, because Livia had always read her faster than any cipher.

"You're burning your orders." Not a question.

"The orders burned themselves," Sable said. "I just provided the draft."

Livia looked at her for a long moment. Then she sat down on the floor beside her, shawl pooled around her, close enough that their shoulders touched—their old position, the one they'd taken during thunderstorms as children, the one neither of them had ever acknowledged as comfort because acknowledging it would have ended it.

"Do you know what I thought," Livia said quietly, "when I saw you at the gate? I thought: she'll have a plan. Sable always has a plan. Sable carries the plans of a whole republic in her coat hem." She turned her head. Her eyes were dry but very bright. "I didn't think: she'll burn them for us."

"I'm not burning them for you."

"No?"

"I'm burning them because they're lies. Because the state that wrote them sold my berth to a colonel and wrote *maintain* next to my name. Because—" She stopped. Started again, lower. "Because survival isn't the same thing as living, Liv, and I spent eleven years confusing them, and those papers are the difference written in cipher."

Livia nodded slowly, and did not say *I told you so*, which was its own kind of forgiveness, since she had in fact told her so, seventeen years ago, on the night Sable left—*you're not choosing freedom, you're choosing spite with good boots*—and had been right in the way older sisters are right: completely, and unforgivably.

They sat together in the stove's red light while the last fragments went gray. Outside, the occupation's patrol passed on the street, boot-heels in frost-rhythm, and both women went silent without looking at each other, two bodies remembering at once how invisibility worked. The boots faded. The silence held a moment longer, and then broke, gently, into something else.

"Tomas woke up," Livia said. "He's standing at the top of the stairs pretending he isn't listening."

"He's bad at it."

"He gets that from you."

Sable closed her eyes. From you. As if it were decided, as if it had always been decided, as if the boy at the door with the note saying *forget this face* had instead been a message saying *remember this one*. She opened her eyes and looked at the stove—at the clean ash, the dead ledger, the space in her coat hem where eleven years of weight had been and now was nothing but a seam.

"Marta keeps flour behind the false panel in the pantry," she said. "And she has a locket she wants someone to carry. We leave at first light, before the market carts, while the checkpoint changes shift."

Livia stood, pulled her up by the hand, an old motion, automatic, theirs. At the bottom of the stairs she paused.

"And the ship?" she asked. "The *Atlas*? You've given up your berth entirely?"

Sable looked at the ash and did not answer—not because she was hiding the decision, but because the decision had changed shape in the last hour, quietly, the way ice changes under snow, and she had not yet said even to herself what the new shape was. One berth. Three souls. A boy who had called her by her false name in his sleep, and a sister who had crossed a country to reach her, and herself—herself, who had just discovered that a woman can survive the loss of everything except the wish to matter to someone.

"We leave at first light," she said again.

Upstairs, small feet retreated across the floorboards, pretending, badly, that they hadn't heard.

* * *

She did not sleep. She sat by the dying stove until the sky outside the window turned the color of wet slate, and in that hour she wrote the last of the unsent letters—the ones Marta had found and never mentioned, the ones addressed to a sister who now slept thirty feet away—and this one she addressed differently. Not to Livia. To no one. To the girl of nineteen with her hand on the flag, reciting catechisms. She told her that the light she'd volunteered to carry had been a fuse. She told her that the republic had died years before its flag came down, and that its last act had been to draw up a passenger list. She told her that treason, done for the right reasons, feels exactly like coming home, and that no one warns you because no one who knows it survives to warn anyone.

Then she folded the letter into quarters, held it over the coals, and let it go—watching the words lift off the page as they blackened, rising on the stove's last warmth like something set free from a cage too small to have ever really held it.

By the time the ash was cold, the market carts were beginning to groan past in the street, and Marta was downstairs lighting the lamp, and the day they would walk to the harbor road had begun.

Sable buttoned her coat. Empty hem. Full pockets: matches, knife, Marta's flour-bought bread, and a folded paper bearing three names in a child's careful hand—Tomas insisted on writing them, though he gave Sable only her false one, and she found she preferred it that way, for now. For one more day.

The door opened onto frost and the smell of coal smoke and the long gray road east, and somewhere at the end of it, a gangway, a bell, and the arithmetic she had not yet finished.
