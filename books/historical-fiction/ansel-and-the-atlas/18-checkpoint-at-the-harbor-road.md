## 18. Checkpoint at the Harbor Road

The fog came off the harbor before dawn, and Livia thought it was a mercy. Fog meant fewer eyes, and fewer eyes meant that the three of them—Sable in front with the cart, Tomas between them, and Livia at the rear watching the road behind—might pass through this last mile of occupied country as nothing more than poor people moving early, which was what they were pretending to be.

She had dressed for it in Marta's kitchen by candlelight: a widow's gray coat with mended elbows, a headscarf knotted low, boots two sizes too large stuffed with newspaper so they wouldn't slip. She had looked at herself in the blackened window and seen a stranger who was also, somehow, more honest than the woman she had been. The woman she had been wore silk to her husband's funeral. The woman in the glass had nothing left but a sister's forged papers and a child's cold hand.

Tomas had not let go of her hand since they left the safehouse. She kept expecting him to reach for Sable instead—it would have made sense, after everything—but he didn't. He held on to her as if she were the fixed point, the thing the world turned around, and Livia found she could not decide whether this was love or simply arithmetic: Sable carried the papers, and children learned quickly whose hands held the documents that mattered.

*No,* she corrected herself, walking. *He chose you. Let it be that.*

The harbor road ran flat and straight between the fish-oil warehouses, and along its length the occupation had planted lamps every fifty paces, each one burning behind wire cages like something the light itself was trying to escape. Beyond the last warehouse she could see the masts of the ships in the basin, a forest of bare poles against the graying sky, and among them—the largest, the closest to the sea wall—the funnels of the *Atlas*, painted the dull red of dried blood, steam already rising from her stacks in slow white columns.

She was real. She was there. Livia felt her breath go strange for a moment, the way it did when hope arrived without permission.

"Don't look at the ship," Sable said quietly, not turning her head. "Look at the road."

"I know how to do this," Livia said.

"I know," Sable said. "I'm saying it for me."

That was the nearest thing to an admission of fear Livia had heard from her sister in twenty years, and she treasured it the way you treasure a crack in a wall that lets you see the room beyond. All night, in the safehouse, Sable had drilled her—name, birthplace, reason for travel—and all night Livia had answered until the answers stopped being memorized and became true. *Marta Voss.* No—that had been the rehearsal name, abandoned when Sable decided the landlady's own identity was too dangerous to lend even to fiction. In the end Sable had built her out of a dead woman's papers, a cousin twice removed who had died of fever before the war and whose documents still lived in a drawer in the port district. *Ilse Brandt. Widow. Born in Kessel, forty-one years ago. Traveling with your son to your mother's people in the north.*

"And if they ask why the boy doesn't speak?" Sable had asked.

"He was hurt," Livia said. "Everyone will understand that. Everyone has seen a boy who was hurt."

Sable had looked at her across the candle for a long moment, and whatever she saw there had made her nod once, sharply, and say: "Good. That's better than anything I'd have written."

It was the first time in their adult lives that Livia had been better than anything Sable wrote. She held onto that too.

* * *

They heard the checkpoint before they saw it—a voice calling numbers into the fog, the wet slap of a leather ledger being turned. The road narrowed where two barricades of stacked railway iron pinched it down to a single lane, and beside the gap stood the guard hut with its stovepipe smoking, and beside the hut stood four soldiers and an officer in a gray greatcoat, and beyond them, Livia knew without looking, would be the list.

There was always a list now. Names permitted to leave; names required to stay; names to be taken aside and never spoken of again. Her husband's name had migrated down such lists in the last year of his life, she'd learned afterward, from the second column to the third, while he waited in a corridor outside an office in the capital believing himself merely delayed.

"Stop here," Sable murmured. "Cart first. I'm cargo. You're the traveler. Let them come to you."

Livia nodded. It was good tradecraft and she hated it—hated standing still while soldiers' eyes moved over her son—but she obeyed, because obedience to her sister's competence was the whole architecture of the morning.

The officer detached himself from the hut and came toward them unhurriedly, hands clasped behind his back, a man taking his morning constitutional rather than conducting a search. Up close he was younger than his coat, with the soft, well-fed face of someone whose war had been conducted entirely on paper. Behind him, two soldiers drifted wide to flank the cart, and a third stayed by the barricade, and the fourth—barely more than a boy, rifle slung backward the way tired men carry them near dawn—began, without any order she could see, to turn the pages of the ledger.

"Papers," the officer said. Not unkindly. That was the worst register of all, Livia thought. Cruelty you could fight. Courtesy you could only endure.

She produced the folder from inside her coat—slowly, deliberately, the way Sable had taught her, as though the documents were precious and slightly annoying—and held it out. The officer opened it with two fingers.

He read. The fog pressed in around them. Somewhere out in the basin a ship's bell rang twice, muffled, like a heart heard through a wall.

"Ilse Brandt." He pronounced it carefully, testing the weight of it. "Kessel."

"Born there," Livia said. Her voice came out steady, which astonished her. "Left when I married. My husband is dead. I'm taking my son north to my mother's family before the winter closes the passes."

"The passes closed in October."

"Then we'll wait in Thalberg until they open. We have cousins there. Anyone has cousins everywhere, these days." She heard herself say it—light, a little weary, a woman making small bitter jokes because the alternative was crying—and saw the officer's mouth twitch at one corner. Good. Be bored of me. Be mildly amused and move along.

"You understand," he said, turning a page, "that travel to the northern districts requires a transit endorsement. Your papers show the endorsement of the regional office at—" he paused, and Livia's whole body went cold, because she knew what came next, Sable had warned her what came next, "—Vesseny. But Vesseny fell on the fourteenth. Its seal is no longer valid."

Here it was. The hinge. Sable had spent half the night arguing with herself about it, Livia remembered, pacing the kitchen with the folder open under the lamp, muttering: *they'll either know Vesseny's fallen or they'll pretend they don't, and if they pretend they don't, then the endorsement stands, and if they don't pretend, then—* and she had stopped there, unable to finish, and finally written the endorsement anyway, in the hand of a clerk who had probably been shot or fled or both, using ink mixed with tea to age it, pressing the page under a hot iron to give it the faint damp-warp of a document carried through rain.

Livia lifted her chin. "Then I suppose you'll have to tell me whether my papers are worth anything, Officer. I can't read the war. I only read what's stamped."

For a moment—two seconds, three—the officer said nothing, and the fog filled the silence, and Livia became aware of everything at once with terrible clarity: the boy soldier's finger resting on the edge of a ledger page; Tomas's hand tightening around hers, small and burning hot; Sable ten feet away by the cart, perfectly still, wearing the face of a woman waiting to be waved past, and beneath that face Livia could feel her sister thinking at the speed of falling water.

Then the officer smiled—a small, administrative smile—and took out a pencil.

"Vesseny's endorsement stands until superseded," he said, mostly to himself. "Regulation eleven. Nobody reads regulation eleven." He wrote something on the folder's back page—a date, a stamp-number copied from the ledger, initials—and handed it back. "You're in luck, Frau Brandt. Or in bureaucracy. They feel the same, some mornings."

"Thank you," Livia said, and meant it with a ferocity that frightened her.

But he didn't step aside. He glanced instead at Tomas—really looked at him for the first time—and something in Livia's chest went tight as wire. "And the boy? Papers?"

"Listed under mine," she said. "He's seven."

"They grow fast in wartime." The officer crouched slightly, bringing his face level with Tomas's, and said with the bright false cheer adults deploy at children they suspect are damaged goods: "And what's your name, young man?"

Silence. The fog dripped off the wire cage of the nearest lamp. The boy soldier licked his thumb and turned another page.

"He doesn't speak," Livia said, and her voice cracked—not by design, not performed, but truly, the sound coming up out of some floorless place in her—and she let it crack, let the officer hear it, because it was the truest thing she owned. "Since the shelling. Since his father. The doctors say he may again someday. They also say not to press him." She pulled Tomas against her hip, hard, proprietary, a mother's arm like a gate closing. "So please don't ask him again. Please."

Something moved behind the officer's eyes. She watched him decide—watched the decision happen in real time, the way you watch weather change—and understood that he was choosing not to see them. Choosing boredom. Choosing the path of least paperwork on a cold morning when the ship was leaving and his tea was getting cold in the hut.

Perhaps that was mercy. Perhaps it was laziness. On the harbor road at dawn, she suspected, they wore the same coat.

"All right," he said, standing. He waved a gloved hand at the barricade. "Frau Brandt. The boy. Through."

* * *

Later—much later, when her knees had stopped shaking and they were standing in the shadow of the customs shed with the *Atlas* towering over them, close enough now that Livia could hear the groan of her gangway cables—Sable said quietly, "You were extraordinary."

"I wasn't. I cried."

"That's what made it work. I'd have given them facts. Facts invite questions. You gave them grief." Sable was looking at the gangway as she spoke, at the thin line of passengers already queuing at its foot, wrapped in every coat they owned. "Grief just gets endured."

"It's what I have the most of," Livia said. "I've learned to spend it."

Sable turned and looked at her then—really looked, the way she used to when they were girls, before the quarrel, before the marriage, before the long silence that had calcified into a kind of mutual exile. And Livia saw in her sister's face something she had not seen since childhood: not suspicion, not the appraising distance Sable wore like armor, but plain, undisguised trust. *You held the line I drew. You held it better than I could have.*

Twenty years. A wedding refused, a door slammed, a life chosen out of spite on both sides. And it had come down to this: a forged endorsement, a regulation nobody read, a widow's tears that happened to be true.

"We're not finished," Sable said, and her voice changed—flattened, went careful. "The berth list is checked again at the gangway. By the ship's purser and an occupation liaison. Together."

"How many berths?"

"One."

Livia stared at her. "One. For three of us."

"One," Sable said again. She reached out and straightened Tomas's collar with two fingers, an oddly tender gesture, her eyes never leaving Livia's face. "I've known since midnight. I did the sums a hundred ways. There's no way that ends with all of us on that ship." She paused, and the fog seemed to gather in the pause, and when she spoke again her voice was almost gentle, which from Sable was more alarming than shouting. "So before we take another step toward that gangway, you need to tell me—who goes aboard?"

Out in the basin, the *Atlas* sounded her whistle—one long note rolling over the water, over the warehouses, over the frozen fields beyond—and began, very slowly, to raise steam.
