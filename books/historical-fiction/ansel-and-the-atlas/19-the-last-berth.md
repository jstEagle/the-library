## 19. The Last Berth

The harbor smelled of coal smoke and salt and the particular sourness of ten thousand frightened people pressed into a space built for two thousand. Sable stood at the mouth of Pier Four with Tomas's hand locked in hers and Livia's shoulder touching her own, and she understood, with the clarity that had carried her through eleven years of checkpoints, that the arithmetic was over. The numbers had finished their work. What remained was the choosing.

The *Atlas* rode at anchor beyond the pier like a country unto herself—grey hull, black funnel, her rail already crowded with faces looking down at the crowd with the particular blankness of people who have made it and cannot afford to feel anything about those who haven't. A gangway descended to the quay, narrow as a gallows rope, and at its foot stood a table. Behind the table sat a clerk of the evacuation office with a manifest bound in blue leather and a stamp the size of a fist, and flanking him were two soldiers of the occupation authority who looked bored in the way that only armed men can afford to look.

Sable had spent the last three days learning everything there was to learn about that table.

She knew the clerk's name—Vessick—and that he took no bribes because his own wife and daughter held berths forty-one and forty-two, and a man whose family is already aboard has nothing left to sell. She knew that the military priority orders had gutted the civilian list from three hundred names down to ninety, then sixty, and that this morning's revision—the one she had burned in Marta's stove before dawn, along with every code she'd ever carried—had cut it again. She did not know how many names remained. That was the cruelty of it. The list lived in Vessick's blue book and nowhere else now, not even in her own memory, and so the number could be anything. It could be one.

She had done the arithmetic at midnight and again at four, and both times it had refused every solution she fed it. One berth. Three people. The equation did not balance. It had never been going to balance. All the forging, all the fluency she and Livia had recovered over those long nights at Marta's kitchen table—Livia's steady hand drawing the seal flourishes while Sable inked the names, the two of them arguing about watermark patterns the way they'd once argued about whose turn it was to fetch water—all of it had bought them exactly this far: past the roadblock on the harbor road where Livia had claimed Tomas as her son without a flicker of hesitation, her voice carrying the weary boredom of a mother who has done this a hundred times, and the soldier had waved them through because the papers were flawless and because grief, real or performed, is the best disguise there is.

But papers got them to the pier. Papers would not get them up the gangway.

They joined the line. There was nothing else to do; everyone in the crowd was doing it, shuffling forward by inches toward the table where their fates were stamped in violet ink. Around them the noise was enormous and strangely wordless—not screaming, not weeping, but a low continuous murmur, the sound of a city holding its breath. Somewhere behind them a man was reciting names, over and over, like a prayer: his wife's, his daughters', his own. Somewhere a woman had simply sat down on the cobblestones and would not get up, and the crowd flowed around her the way water flows around a stone, without malice, without stopping.

Tomas tugged her hand.

He had spoken twice since the burning. Once, at four in the morning, watching the ash of the dispatches curl in the stove: *"Why?"* And once, an hour ago, when Livia had knelt to straighten his collar before they left the safehouse and told him, quietly, that whatever happened today, he was to do exactly what Sable said—he had said Sable's false name. Her false name, in his small cracked voice, pronounced perfectly, the way you pronounce the name of someone you trust.

That was the thing about children. They couldn't be fooled with paper. They saw through every alias to the person underneath, which was why Sable had spent eleven years avoiding them, and why she had failed, utterly, with this one.

"What is it?" she asked.

He pointed. Not at the ship—at the rail above the gangway, where a woman had lifted a child onto her shoulders so the boy could see the water. The child was waving at the crowd below, delighted, oblivious, waving at ten thousand strangers as though they had all come to watch him sail.

Tomas watched him for a long moment. Then he looked up at Sable, and his eyes asked the question he would not ask aloud, because he was seven years old and he had already learned that some questions are too heavy to carry in the voice.

*Am I going?*

Sable looked away first. It was the first time in her life she had ever lost a staring contest, and she lost it to a mute child on a dock, and she would remember that for the rest of her life, however long the occupation let that life run.

* * *

The line moved. It always moved; that was its horror. You could stand in it for hours dreading the moment of decision, and the moment came anyway, carried toward you on the patient feet of strangers, and there was no way to make it come slower and no way to make it pass you by.

Livia walked beside her in the coat they had shared the stitching of—their old sibling joke, *two names, one coat*, except it wasn't a joke anymore, it was just true. Under the coat hem, sewn into the lining where Sable had once carried dispatches, there was now nothing at all. She kept reaching down to check anyway. Eleven years of habit. Her fingers found only fabric, and every time they did, something in her chest turned over like a boat taking water.

"You're doing the thing," Livia said quietly. They were close enough that she didn't have to raise her voice; the crowd made privacy out of noise. "Where you go somewhere else in your face."

"I don't do that."

"You've done it since you were nine. Papa called it your customs-house look. Everything inspected, nothing admitted." Livia glanced at her sidelong. "Whatever you're planning, plan it out loud. I'm not letting you decide alone again. Not after the last time."

"The last time," Sable said, "was twenty-two years ago, and you chose the marriage."

"And you chose the road, and we both chose wrong, and we've spent our whole lives paying interest on it." Livia's hand found hers, above Tomas's head, and gripped hard. "So. Out loud. What are you thinking?"

Sable looked at the table. They were thirty people from it now. Twenty-five.

"I'm thinking," she said, "that Vessick checks documents against the manifest. I'm thinking my name isn't on any manifest anymore, because I burned it. I'm thinking Livia Ashgrove—" she used the married name deliberately, the name on the flawless papers, "—is on the list. Priority class, widow of a port engineer, dependent minor child. I'm thinking the papers will hold. I checked them against every forgery tell I know, and you drew the seals better than I could have."

"But?"

"But there's one berth." She said it flatly, the way she'd once read casualty figures off coded dispatches, back when detachment was armor and armor worked. "The revisions cut the list to bone. I counted the stamps this morning—Vessick confirmed fourteen boarding parties before ours. Even if every party took two, the book runs dry before we reach that table. The mathematics doesn't care what we deserve. It never has."

"Then we find another way—"

"There is no other way. That's what the arithmetic taught me. You can't solve it. You can only choose what it costs, and who pays."

Twenty people. Fifteen. The ship's horn sounded, long and low, a sound that went through Sable's bones like cold water, and the whole crowd flinched as one animal.

"Sable." Livia's voice broke its own composure for the first time since the gate. "Listen to me. If it comes down to it, the berth goes to you. You're the one who knows the networks, you're the one who can—"

"—I'm also the one they're hunting. My description went out on occupation wire eight days ago. Yours didn't. You're a widow with clean papers and a child, and grief makes you invisible in ways my kind of invisibility never was." Sable heard her own voice and marveled at it: calm, precise, already decided. She had decided, she realized, sometime in the night, maybe at the stove, maybe earlier, maybe the day a stranger set a boy on her step and said *forget this face*. Maybe she had been walking toward this gangway her entire adult life. "You'll stay with Marta's network. You'll survive. And you'll hate me for it, and you'll be right to, and I'll be dead or gone either way, and none of that changes what's true."

"What's true?"

Sable looked down at Tomas. He was holding her hand with both of his now, the way small children hold on when the world gets loud, and his chin was trembling, and he was not crying, because someone had taught him—not her, not anyone she knew—that crying cost too much.

"That I love him," she said. It was the first time she had said it to anyone, including herself. It came out of her like a splinter leaving the skin. "That's what's true. Eleven years I built a life out of things I could abandon—papers, names, routes, codes. Carry only what can be dropped. That was the whole philosophy. And then this—" she touched the top of Tomas's head, lightly, the crown of soft hair, "—and he can't be dropped. He won't fit in any coat hem. So the philosophy dies today, on this pier, and good riddance to it, because I think it was killing me slowly for a decade and I mistook the numbness for peace."

Ten people ahead. Five.

The soldier on the right shifted his rifle and yawned. Vessick stamped a document—a family of three, Sable saw, a father and two girls, and the father was weeping as he gathered the paper, and the girls didn't understand yet, they thought the stamp meant they'd won—and the blue book closed, and opened, and closed.

Then they were at the table.

* * *

Up close, Vessick had the face of a man who had stopped sleeping around the same time the capital fell, and Sable recognized the face because she wore its twin. He looked at the papers she handed across without ceremony—she had learned long ago that ceremony invites scrutiny—and ran his finger down the manifest page.

"Livia Ashgrove," he read. "Dependent minor." His eyes came up, flicked from Livia to Tomas, back. "Papers are in order."

The relief that went through Sable was so violent she nearly swayed. Then his finger moved to the next column, and stopped.

"One berth remaining under this classification," Vessick said. His voice was neutral, procedural, the voice of a man reading a death sentence he had read a hundred times today. "One. I'm required to inform you that the party must select a single traveler. The others' documents are hereby voided." He set his stamp down beside the book, deliberately, like a judge laying down a gavel. "Select now, please. There are people behind you."

The crowd's murmur rose around them. The horn sounded again, shorter this time, impatient. Somewhere far off down the quay, glass broke and no one even turned to look.

Sable felt Livia's hand close on her arm—felt the argument gathering in her sister like weather—and spoke before it could break.

"Tomas," she said.

And then she crouched, down to his level, in full view of the soldiers and the clerk and the ten thousand strangers and the grey ship and the cold sky, and she took both his hands, and she forgot every rule of tradecraft she had ever learned, because there was no longer anything left to protect. Anonymity was a currency, and she was spending the last of it here, in public, buying one future with it.

"Look at me," she said.

He looked at her. His eyes were wet and furious and brave, seven years old and already fluent in loss, and she thought: *whoever left you on that step loved you enough to give you away, and I finally understand them, and I hate them, and I am them.*

"You're getting on that ship."

His lip shook. His grip tightened on her fingers until it hurt, and she let it hurt.

"When the stranger brought you to my door," Sable said, and her voice was steady, God help her, her voice was courier-steady, "there was a note. It said *forget this face*. That was an instruction for me—to keep you safe by staying a stranger, by being nobody to you, by carrying only what I could drop." She swallowed. "I obeyed it badly. I disobeyed it completely. I memorized the way you take your bread, and how you line your shoes up by the door, and the exact sound you make when you almost laugh. You made me somebody, Tomas. For eleven years I survived by being no one, and you ruined it in five weeks, and it is the finest ruin of my life."

A tear went down his cheek. He didn't wipe it. Neither did she dare.

"So here is your instruction, and you will keep it better than I kept mine." She leaned close, so the words belonged to him alone, though the whole pier might hear them. "*Remember this face.* Not forget. Remember. Remember it when the water gets wide and the language changes and the years pile up. Remember that you were loved here, in the country that fell, by a woman who was never anybody at all until you made her someone. Remember it, and grow up, and be free, and one day—if the world is ever kind—write it down. Tell someone. That's all I ask. That the face survives somewhere besides my mirror."

He threw himself against her. She held him—held him the way she had never held anything, because everything else she had ever held had been designed to be released—and over his shoulder she saw Livia standing rigid with her hand pressed to her mouth, tears running freely down a face that had held composure through checkpoints and forgeries and twenty-two years of estrangement, and Livia nodded once, small and terrible, and Sable nodded back, and between them, without another word, the treaty was signed.

Vessick cleared his throat. When Sable looked up, the clerk's eyes were wet too, and his stamp hovered, and his voice when it came was rough. "The berth is for the boy?"

"For the boy."

"Name for the manifest."

Sable hesitated one heartbeat. Then she gave it—his true name, the name the stranger's note had tried to bury, the name she had guarded in the safehouse ledger of her heart for five weeks. Let it travel. Let it live abroad, in freedom, attached to a living boy instead of a dead file.

Vessick wrote it. Stamped it. Tore the stub free and held it out—not to Sable, but to Livia, because he understood too, everyone on this pier understood, this was the whole grammar of the place. "Boarding party six. Go now, madam. Take him up." His gaze met Sable's for half a second, and in it she read the only mercy the bureaucracy contained: *I see what you did. I will lose sleep tonight on your behalf.*

Livia scooped Tomas up. The boy wrapped his arms around her neck but his eyes stayed fixed on Sable over her shoulder, wide and streaming, and he mouthed something—no sound, his old silence returning one final time, but she read it as easily as any code she'd ever broken.

*I'll remember.*

"Go," she said aloud, and her voice finally broke, on the last syllable, and she let it. "Go, my love. Run."

They went. Up the gangway, into the press of bodies at the rail, into the smoke and the noise, Livia climbing with the boy on her hip and her jaw set like the prow of a ship. At the top, a sailor took Tomas's hand to pull him aboard, and the boy twisted around for one last look—

And Sable raised her hand. Not waving. Palm out, fingers spread, held high and still, the gesture that says *I am here, I am here, I am here*—and she held it until the crowd swallowed them both.

* * *

The mooring lines came off at dusk.

Sable stood at the end of Pier Four, alone among hundreds of the abandoned, and watched the *Atlas* gather herself against the tide. Searchlights swept the blackening water. The funnel breathed its first fat plume of smoke. On the deck, tiny with distance, passengers lined the rail, and Sable searched the row of small faces for one in particular and could not find him, and then she could: a small figure lifted up, held aloft by a tall shape beside him—Livia, raising him so he could see—and the small arm went up, palm out, fingers spread, held high and still.

Sable raised her hand again and held it until her arm shook, until the gap of dark water widened past shouting distance, past recognition, past anything but faith.

The ship's horn sounded one last time, long and mournful, and the *Atlas* turned her bow toward the open sea, toward the free coast, toward the rest of his life, and Sable stood on the dying pier of the dying city with nothing in her pockets, no codes, no cover name, no mission, no armor, and felt—for the first time in eleven years—entirely, dangerously, unbearably alive.

Behind her, boots on cobblestones. A lantern bobbing through the thinning crowd, coming toward the pier with purpose, and beside it a second light, and a third.

Sable did not turn around. She watched the ship until her eyes blurred, and said, quietly, to whoever had come for her:

"You're late. She's already gone."
