## 20. The Atlas Departs

The whistle sounded three times, and each one went through me like a nail driven into green wood.

Tomas stood at the rail of the *Atlas*, small against the iron bulk of her, his hands wrapped around the bars the way he had once wrapped them around mine in the dark of the safehouse when he couldn't sleep. He was not crying. I had braced myself for crying—for the tearing, animal grief of a child being left—but he did not cry, and somehow that was worse. He simply looked down at us, at Livia and me pressed together in the crowd on the quay, and he lifted one hand from the rail and held it there, palm out, fingers spread.

*Remember my face,* I had told him. Not forget it. I had turned the stranger's words inside out like a glove, and now the boy wore them, and so did I.

"He can't hear you," Livia said quietly. "Whatever you're about to shout."

"I wasn't going to shout."

"You were. Your whole body was."

She was right. My whole body was. There were words stacked in my throat like cordwood—*be brave, be clever, don't trust anyone with kind eyes, eat before they feed you, lie first and apologize never*—twenty years of courier's wisdom compressed into a single breath that could never hold it all. So instead I raised my hand too, palm out, fingers spread, and held it there until my arm shook, and Tomas held his, and between us stretched forty feet of cold harbor air and the whole irreparable distance of a life.

The mooring lines came off the bollards one by one, heavy as snakes, coiled by sailors who did not look up at the people on the dock because looking up was how you learned things that got you shot. A gap of gray-green water opened between the hull and the quay. First an arm's width. Then a cart's width. Then something no voice could cross.

The crowd around us made a sound I will hear until I die—not wailing, not cheering, but a long collective exhalation, like a city letting go of something it had been gripping for years. Some people wept openly. Some stood rigid as fence posts. An old man near me removed his hat and held it over his heart, though whether in mourning or salute I could not say, and perhaps neither could he.

"They're going," Livia said. It was not a question. She said it the way you say *it's raining*, the way you name a weather that has been coming for days.

"They're going."

The *Atlas*'s engines turned over beneath the water, a vibration I felt through the soles of my boots, through the stones of the quay, through the bones of my feet as if the harbor itself had begun to speak. Smoke rolled black from her funnel and flattened against the low clouds. Slowly—so slowly it seemed less like departure than like the dock sliding away from the ship—the vessel began to move.

I found Tomas in the crowd of faces at the rail. He had not moved. His palm was still out. And then, as the gap widened past shouting distance, past throwing distance, past any distance I could bridge with anything but looking—he did something I did not expect.

He smiled.

It was small. It was crooked, lopsided, the smile of a boy who had spent months not speaking and had only just remembered what his mouth was for. But it was aimed directly at me, unerringly, through all those strangers, as if some thread ran taut between us still, and I understood suddenly that he was not saying goodbye. He was saying *I have your face now. You told me to keep it. I am keeping it.*

I broke.

Not loudly. Not the way the women beside me broke, keening into their shawls. I broke the way ice breaks on a river in late spring—one silent fissure running the whole length of me, everything shifting and groaning beneath a surface that still looked solid. Livia's arm came around my waist. I do not know when she put it there. I leaned into her without deciding to, the way you lean into a wall when the floor tilts, and she took my weight without comment, without ceremony, the way family does the things that need doing.

"She'll make landfall in four days if the weather holds," Livia said. Her voice was steady, and I knew the steadiness cost her; I could feel it in the tension of her forearm against my back. "The captain owes Marta's network more than he'd admit in daylight. He'll see the boy fed."

"You've been talking to Marta's people."

"I've been awake since Tuesday, Sable. I talk to everyone."

A laugh came out of me—short, cracked, half sob—and it startled us both. Livia looked down at me with an expression I hadn't seen on her face since we were girls: astonishment, quickly folded away, replaced by the dry almost-smile that was our mother's before it was hers.

"There she is," she said. "There's the girl who laughed."

"The girl who laughed died at a border checkpoint in—" I stopped. Recalculated. "No. She didn't. That's the thing, isn't it. She didn't die. She just stopped answering to her name."

The *Atlas* cleared the breakwater. Her bow swung toward the open sea, and the last of the morning light caught her hull, and for one moment the whole ship blazed white-gold against the gray water like something holy, like something out of the illuminated books our father used to show us, saints and ships and burning cities all rendered in the same impossible gold. Then the light passed off her, and she was just a ship again, getting smaller, getting grayer, carrying my heart in a third-class berth under a name that wasn't his.

The crowd began to thin. This was the part no one warned you about—the aftermath of departure, the terrible ordinary minutes after the thing you dreaded has actually happened. People turned away from the water in ones and twos, gathering children, gathering bundles, gathering themselves. Some would walk home to houses that would be searched within the week. Some would walk to other docks and wait for other ships that would never come. The old man with the hat put it back on his head and shuffled away, and the space where the *Atlas* had been filled with gulls and fog and nothing.

"We can't stand here," Livia said.

"I know."

"Sable. We cannot stand here." Her grip tightened. "You're the professional. Tell me where we go."

And that was the question, wasn't it. Where does a woman go who has just burned her country's last ledger, refused an officer's mercy, surrendered her own berth, and watched the evidence of all three crimes steam over the horizon? Every safehouse I knew was burned or burning. Every contact was a liability now, every route a corridor with a closed door at the end of it. I had spent twenty years knowing exactly where to go next—that was the whole architecture of my life, the next dead drop, the next forged seal, the next border—and standing on that quay with the gulls screaming over empty water, I felt the architecture come down.

Then I felt the locket.

It hung against my sternum on its thin chain, warm from my skin, Marta's locket with Marta's mother inside it, the two faded portraits facing each other in their little brass room like conspirators. *Take it,* Marta had said. *So you remember that staying is also a choice, and some of us make it with our eyes open.* I had carried dispatches sewn into coat hems across three borders. I had carried codes that decided who lived. I had never once carried anything that was given to me freely, expecting nothing back.

"Harbor Road," I said. "There's a chandlery halfway up with a blue door. Third shutter from the left stays boarded. Behind it—"

I stopped. Because behind it was a room, and in the room was a woman named Ilse who owed me nothing anymore, whose debt I had canceled along with everyone else's when I fed the list to the stove. The map in my head was drawn in ink that had dried up.

Livia watched me work through it. She had always been able to watch me think—it used to infuriate me, when we were young, the way she'd sit across the table while I struggled with sums, waiting, patient as a cat at a mousehole. Now she waited again.

"Marta," she said, when I'd been silent too long. "You're thinking about Marta."

"Marta stayed."

"Marta has a network. Marta has a bedridden mother and a chandlery full of friends and a lifetime of knowing which shutters stay boarded." Livia turned to face me fully, and the crowd flowed around us, and I saw that she was afraid—truly afraid, for the first time since she'd arrived at the gate with her dead husband's name in her mouth—but that she had decided to be afraid in motion rather than in place. "We don't have a network. We have each other and a set of papers good for maybe six more hours. So we find Marta's people, or we find Marta's people's people, and we do it walking, because standing still on this quay is how we get put against a wall."

"When did you become the tactician?"

"When my little sister gave away our boat ticket to a nine-year-old." She said it without bitterness. That was the miracle of it—no bitterness at all, only a kind of fierce, wondering pride, as if I had done something magnificent instead of something mad. "Come on. Walk. Left hand in your pocket, right hand through my arm. We're two widows going to market. We've done this before."

We had done it before—two days ago, at the checkpoint on the harbor road, when she had claimed Tomas as her son with such flawless, boring conviction that the soldier had waved us through and gone back to picking his nails. Two names, one coat. Our old fluency, resurrected in forgery and argument and silence. I took her arm. She was thinner than she should have been. We all were.

* * *

We walked.

The city received us the way occupied cities receive everyone: indifferently, suspiciously, with its collar up. Troops at the corner of the fish market, bored, their breath steaming. A queue outside the ration office already fifty deep in the cold. Posters on the customs house wall, layered three deep, the newest one showing the republic's broken seal above a word I refused to read. A child sold roasted chestnuts from a brazier and watched us pass with eyes far too old for the trade.

Livia kept up a low stream of commentary—prices, weather, the scandalous state of the cobbles—widow-talk, harmless-talk, the audible wallpaper of two unremarkable women. Underneath it, barely moving her lips, she said: "Blue door. Which street?"

"Salt Lane. Off the ropewalk. If the door's marked—if there's chalk—we keep walking and try the netmender's on the quay."

"And if there's no one at either?"

"Then we keep walking until someone finds us. In a city like this, the staying side always finds the stayers eventually. It's the leaving side they can't track."

"That's either very comforting or very grim."

"It's both. Most true things are."

At Salt Lane the blue door stood exactly as I remembered it, paint blistered, hinges rusted, the third shutter from the left still boarded with the same gray planks. No chalk. No mark. I walked past it once, counting my own heartbeat, then doubled back through the alley behind the ropewalk and knocked—three slow, two fast, one slow—on the rear door of the chandlery, my knuckles raw with cold.

Nothing.

I knocked again. Somewhere inside, a floorboard settled. Then footsteps, unhurried, and the door opened the width of a hand, and one eye appeared in the gap—a woman's eye, sixty years old and sharp as a gutting knife.

"We're closed," the eye said.

"Ilse. It's Ashgrove. Pemberton. It's—" I faltered on my own false names, that litany of aliases I had worn like coats, and heard myself say, plainly, stupidly, wonderfully: "It's the woman who burned the list."

The eye widened. The door opened.

Ilse was smaller than I remembered, wrapped in a man's coat, her gray hair pinned up with what appeared to be a pencil. She looked at me, then at Livia, then up and down the alley with a speed that belied her age, and pulled us both inside by our sleeves.

"Off the streets," she hissed. "Both of you. Do you have any idea what's being said? Half the harbor watched that ship leave. The other half informed on the first half." She bolted the door behind us and turned, and in the dim lantern light of the chandlery—rope coils and tar pots and the smell of hemp and pitch—I watched her take in our faces, our exhaustion, whatever it was we were radiating like heat off a stone. Her expression softened by perhaps one degree. "The boy?"

"On the ship. Safe berth. Fed, if the captain keeps his word."

"He'll keep it. He has daughters." She paused. "And you gave him yours."

It was not a question. News traveled faster than ships in a city like this; it traveled faster than light. I said nothing. Livia said nothing. Ilse studied us both for a long moment, and then she did something I did not expect: she laughed, low and rough, shaking her head.

"Marta was right about you," she said. "She told us—months ago, when you first started coming through—she said, *that one pretends she's paper, but she's blood, and one day she'll find out.*" She jerked her chin toward the back of the shop, toward a stair descending into darkness. "There's soup. There's a cellar with a false wall. There are eleven people in this city who'll hide you and forty who'd sell you, and I'll teach you the difference by tomorrow. But tonight—" her voice caught, just slightly, on the edge of something older and sadder, "—tonight you grieve. Both of you. Whatever you left on that dock, you grieve it properly, because tomorrow we work."

* * *

The cellar was low-ceilinged and candlelit and smelled of turnips and lamp oil, and it held, besides Ilse and ourselves, a printer with ink-stained fingers, a schoolteacher, and a boy of perhaps sixteen who watched Livia with the hopeless devotion of the very young. Soup was produced. Bread was produced. A bottle of something amber and medicinal was produced, and Ilse poured three fingers into tin cups and pushed one at each of us.

"To the ones who left," she said, raising her cup.

"To the ones who left," we echoed.

"And to the ones who stayed," she added, "because somebody bloody well has to."

We drank. The liquor burned a clean line down my chest and pooled warm in my stomach, and for the first time since the gangway, since the whistle, since the small crooked smile through forty feet of impossible air, my hands stopped shaking.

Livia sat beside me on a crate, our shoulders touching. She smelled of coal smoke and my soap and the particular cold of the harbor, and when I turned my head I found her already looking at me, and neither of us looked away.

"Do you remember," she said softly, "the summer Father taught us to swim? Off the rocks at Corren's Cove. And you wouldn't jump. You stood on that ledge for an hour calculating."

"I wasn't calculating. I was assessing risk."

"You were calculating. And then I jumped first, and you were so furious at being shown up that you jumped after me with your eyes shut, and you came up spluttering, and Father laughed until he cried." Her hand found mine on the wet wood of the crate, cold fingers closing around cold fingers. "You've been standing on ledges your whole life, Sable. Calculating. Today you jumped."

"I didn't jump. I fell."

"Same splash," she said.

I laughed again—that same cracked, unfamiliar sound—and this time it turned into tears before I could stop it, and Livia gathered me in the way she had on the quay, without ceremony, without comment, one hand cradling the back of my head like I was nine years old and had skinned my knee. Around us the candle flames bent in some draft from the stairs. The printer pretended to read. The schoolteacher studied the ceiling. The boy with the hopeless devotion discovered a sudden fascination with his boots. And I wept into my sister's shoulder in a cellar under a chandlery in a fallen city, for the boy on the ship, for the country in the stove, for Marta and her locket and her mother upstairs in the dark, for the woman I had been who could carry anything except being known.

When it was over—when the storm had passed through and left that strange scraped-clean quiet behind it—Livia held me at arm's length and examined my face with a gravity that made me nervous.

"What?" I said.

"Your eyes are red. Your nose is red. You look like a widow at a funeral." A ghost of the dry smile. "Which is perfect. Nobody looks twice at a grieving woman. Come on. Ilse says there's a false wall, and I intend to sleep for twelve hours behind it, and tomorrow—"

The cellar door opened above us.

Footsteps on the stairs, fast, wrong-fast, and Ilse was on her feet with a candle snuffer in her fist like a dagger, and the printer blew out two of the three candles in a single practiced breath. Darkness dropped over us like a lid. Through the narrow slit of the false wall's vent, up the stairwell, I could see the rectangle of the outer door, and a shadow falling across it, and I heard Ilse's voice go flat and pleasant and utterly transformed:

"Yes? The shop's closed, officer."

And a man's voice answered, courteous and cold and horribly familiar—the voice from the dinner table, the voice that had let me leave, the voice that was worse than any accusation:

"My apologies, madam. I'm not here for the shop." A pause, long enough for my heart to stop entirely. "I'm looking for two sisters."
