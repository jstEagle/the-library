## 1. The Weight of Small Gears

The summons came on a morning when the frost had not yet surrendered to the sun, delivered by a boy in the livery of the Royal Observatory who would not come past the threshold of the shop. He held the letter out at arm's length, as though the paper itself might be contaminated by the smell of solder and oil that hung in Montrose & Son, Clockmakers.

Silas wiped her hands on her leather apron before she took it. She had learned long ago that a craftsman's hands told stories, and hers had been carefully taught to tell only one: that they belonged to a young man of two-and-twenty, competent, unremarkable, and utterly without history worth investigating.

*To Master Silas Montrose, clockmaker, from the Office of the Director, Royal Observatory.* The seal was heavy wax, pressed with the observatory's mark—a star above an open eye. The handwriting beneath was precise, cold, and brief. *The master clock has fallen into irregularity. You will attend upon it this day. Come alone. Speak of this to no one.*

No signature beyond the office's stamp. No mention of payment, which meant payment would be generous—generosity was how men like the Observatory's director bought silence, and everyone in the quarter knew it.

"Tell your master I'll attend within the hour," she said to the liveried boy.

"He knows," the boy said, and left at a trot, his heels striking cobbles that still rang with last night's cold.

Silas stood in the doorway a moment, watching him go. The street smelled of coal smoke and yesterday's bread, and somewhere down toward the river a crowd had gathered around a news-sheet seller, their voices pitched in that new, restless key she'd been hearing for weeks now—the sound of a city clearing its throat.

She closed the door and turned the lock out of habit more than fear. Fear was a thing she rationed like lamp oil. Too much and you froze; too little and you burned.

Her father had built this shop's reputation over thirty years, gear by gear, and when he died it had taken Silas exactly eleven days to become his son. Eleven days of binding her chest, lowering her voice into its working register, letting her hair grow no longer than any apprentice's. Eleven days of practicing the way men looked at clocks—with ownership rather than wonder—and the way they spoke of them, as though time were a beast they'd tamed rather than a river they stood in.

The guild had never questioned it. Why would they? A dead man's son, shy but skilled, keeping the family trade alive. The city approved of such things. It required so little imagination to approve of Silas Montrose, and imagination was a luxury the city did not extend to girls.

She took down her tool roll—the good one, with the escapement files and the tweezers fine enough to lift a hair-spring—and wrapped it in oilcloth. Then she paused, and after a moment took also the loupe with the brass fitting, and the small mirror she used for seeing into cases where the light would not reach.

A voice in her, quiet and practical, said: *It is only a clock. Whatever is wrong with it, it is only a clock.*

Another voice, older and less comforting, said nothing at all, because it knew better.

* * *

The Observatory crowned the highest hill in the city, white stone gone grey at the joints, its dome catching the late-morning light like a coin held up to heaven. Silas had passed beneath it ten thousand times without once being invited inside. Its master clock was famous—not merely a timepiece but an authority, the arbiter against which every church bell, every factory whistle, every railway schedule in the city set itself. When the master clock struck noon, forty lesser bells confirmed it, and the whole city agreed upon the hour as upon scripture.

Which was why, when the porter led her up the tower stair and she first heard the mechanism, her stomach tightened before her mind had caught the reason.

She had heard this clock all her life from the streets below. Its tick carried—her father used to say the Observatory's tick was the city's pulse, and he'd meant it as praise. But standing inside the tower now, close enough to touch the case, Silas heard something wrong in the rhythm. Not a fault, precisely. Faults stumbled. This was smooth. Smooth and slow, the beat drawn out like honey off a spoon, each second stretched to hold a little more than a second should.

"Master Montrose." A clerk met her at the landing, a narrow man with ink-stained cuffs and the particular pallor of someone who spent his life indoors counting other people's business. "You'll find the case open. The director wishes the work done quietly, and today."

"Quietly," Silas repeated. "May I ask what irregularity was observed?"

"You may observe it," the clerk said, with the ghost of a smile that wasn't one, "and repair it. That will be simpler for everyone."

He left her alone on the platform, and Silas stood a moment listening to the slowed heartbeat of the most important clock in the city, feeling the unease settle into her shoulders like damp.

She approached the case. It was a magnificent piece, her father had seen it once from the visitors' gallery and spoken of its train for a week—the great weight-driven movement, the deadbeat escapement, the gravity arm that corrected the pendulum against the stars themselves. The case doors hung open as promised, and inside, the brass and steel of the going train gleamed in the light from the tall slit window.

It took her less than four minutes to find what had been done.

The pendulum bob had been altered. Someone had added mass to it—two small lead discs, machined with real skill, seated flush against the bob's face and painted to match. A longer effective pendulum meant a slower swing; a slower swing meant every hour this clock announced was a little longer than an hour ought to be. Two minutes in the day, perhaps three. Small enough that no ordinary citizen would notice, great enough that over weeks the city's official time drifted away from the true time written in the sky.

And the workmanship—it was excellent. That was the worst of it. Whoever had done this had not botched the job; they had honored it, in the way of a surgeon making a wound that heals clean. This was not vandalism. This was policy.

Silas knelt there with her tools unrolled beside her and felt the strange vertigo of understanding arriving ahead of its reasons. A clock could be made to lie. She had known that since childhood—every schoolboy knew a stopped clock tells no time at all—but this was different. This was a lie built to run forever, a lie with a pendulum, a lie the entire city would synchronize its bells and trains and wages to. If the master clock said noon came later than noon, then noon came later than noon, and the stars themselves became the liars.

Why? Her mind turned the question like a gear turning under load. Contracts settled by the official hour. Trains arriving or departing by it. Men whose alibis depended on it. A few minutes' drift, applied patiently, could move money the way a millrace moves grain—quietly, continuously, in one direction.

Or it could do something else entirely. Something she hadn't thought of yet. That possibility sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

She began to work. There was no sense standing in the wreckage of a question; better to keep the hands busy while the mind circled. She removed the lead discs, weighed them in her palm—each stamped with a maker's mark she didn't recognize, which was itself remarkable, because Silas Montrose knew every clockmaker's mark within two hundred miles, having spent her life studying them the way other people studied saints—and set them aside in her cloth. Then she adjusted the rating nut, cleaned the pivot holes, oiled where oil was wanted and nowhere else. Honest work. It steadied her.

The pendulum, freed of its added weight, gathered its old tempo back within minutes. The tick quickened, firmed, resumed its proper stride—and the sound of it filled the tower again, that pulse she'd known from the cradle, right for the first time in however many weeks the discs had been in place.

She was closing the case when her fingers found the second door.

It was set low in the case's flank, behind the pendulum's swing, a panel no larger than a prayer book, fitted so finely into the joinery that she had taken it for part of the wood. Her father had taught her that trick: the best hiding places are not locked but invisible. A lock invites curiosity. Seamlessness forgives it.

She worked her thinnest blade along the seam and the panel came away into her hand.

Inside, in a recess lined with green baize, was a small clockwork movement—no larger than a carriage clock, exquisitely made, its own tiny pendulum beating in counterpoint to the great one above. And beside it, wound onto a brass drum turned slowly by that movement, a length of paper tape, dense with punched holes.

Silas held her breath without deciding to. She drew the tape out into the light. The holes ran in columns, regular as Morse, a record being kept in a language of punctures—dates, she guessed, times, readings. Something in this tower was measuring something else in this tower, secretly, continuously, and committing it to memory in the only form that could not burn.

The movement needed winding. That was the detail that turned her unease into something colder. A spring does not wind itself. Every few days, someone climbed these stairs, opened this hidden door, and gave the secret heart of the observatory its turn—someone with a key, a schedule, and a reason. The clock had been slowed deliberately. The recorder had been installed deliberately. And the summons to herself—*come alone, speak to no one*—had been issued by the same deliberate hand, or by another hand that wanted the slowing undone without anyone knowing it had ever been done.

Two parties, then. One who lied with the clock. One who wanted the lie recorded—or corrected—or both.

Silas replaced the panel exactly as she'd found it, aligned the seam until it vanished, and stood wiping her hands on her apron as if she'd touched nothing at all. Above her, through the tower's timbers, she could hear the great wheel turning, honest again, keeping the true hour for a city that did not know its time had been tampered with.

She packed her tools. At the landing, the clerk was waiting with a purse heavy enough to make a sound when he set it in her palm—a sound like a small bell, like a warning.

"The director thanks you for your discretion," he said.

"Tell the director the clock keeps honest time again," Silas said. "Whatever else he may wish to know about it, I couldn't say. I was only sent for a repair."

Something flickered across the clerk's face—relief, or its twin—and he showed her down.

The hill road unwound below her toward the city, smoke rising thin and numerous from ten thousand chimneys, and Silas walked it slowly, the purse weighing far more than coin should weigh. She had come up the hill a clockmaker. She was walking down it as a witness, though to what, she could not yet say.

That night, long after dark, a knock came at the shop door—three soft strikes, then silence, then three more—and when she opened it there was no one on the step but a child, seven years old at most, holding a brass key stamped with a star above an open eye.
