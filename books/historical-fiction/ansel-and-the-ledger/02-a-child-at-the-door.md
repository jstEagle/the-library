## 2. A Child at the Door

The knock came at half past eleven, which was itself an offense. In the clockmakers' quarter, half past eleven belonged to no one; the shops had shuttered hours ago, the lamps burned low, and even the watchmen had settled into the long dull stretch of their rounds where nothing happened and they preferred it so. Silas Montrose was still awake because she was always still awake at that hour—bent over a carriage clock with its escapement opened like a patient on a table, filing a pallet stone by lamplight, her father's loupe screwed into her eye.

She froze when the knock came. Three raps, soft, deliberate, spaced like a man counting his own courage.

For a long moment she did not breathe. In three years of living as Silas Montrose, journeyman clockmaker, she had learned that after dark, knocks meant soldiers or debt collectors or drunk men who wanted to argue about the price of a mainspring, and every one of those encounters was a small audition for the role she played. She set down the file. She pulled the cap lower over her cropped hair, squared her shoulders against the familiar ache of binding, and lit the second lamp so the shop would look occupied by a working man and not an ambush.

"Closed," she said through the door, pitching her voice down into its daytime register.

Another knock. Softer now. Almost apologetic.

She opened the door with the chain still fastened—and found no man at all. A boy stood on her step in the rain, seven years old perhaps, soaked through a coat too fine to be his own, sleeves rolled four times at the wrists. His shoes were good leather gone soft with water. He held a bundle wrapped in oilcloth against his chest the way children hold things they have been told, in whatever words adults use, are the most important things in the world.

Behind him, at the corner, a carriage lantern swung away into the dark. Hoofbeats faded toward the river.

"You're the clockmaker," the boy said. Not a question. Someone had coached him. Someone had stood him here and told him exactly what to say, and then gotten back into a warm carriage and left him in the rain.

"I'm closed," Silas said again, but the words came out wrong, too gentle, and she heard it happen—the crack in her own performance.

"He said you'd say that." The boy's chin trembled once and stilled, mastered. "He said to tell you: he's sorry about the hour."

Silas looked past him up and down the street. Empty. Rain needling the puddles. A dog turning over refuse two doors down, indifferent. Whoever had brought him was gone with a thoroughness that told her this had been planned down to the last detail except one: what the boy would do if she refused.

"What's your name?"

"Tam." Then, quickly, as if he'd already made a mistake: "I'm not supposed to give my name."

"No," Silas said. "I imagine you're not."

* * *

She let him in because the alternative was closing the door on a child in the rain, and there were limits even to survival—or rather, she discovered as the door shut behind them, there were limits, but they sat further out than she'd mapped them. That frightened her more than the knock had.

She gave him tea too sweet and bread from yesterday, and he ate standing up, watching the shop with wide eyes. It was a good shop to be watched in. Her father had built it to be watched in: clocks on every wall, none of them agreeing, pendulums swinging in slow opposition like a room full of metronomes arguing. On the workbench, disassembled movements lay in ranked order under glass. The boy turned slowly in the middle of it all, dripping on her floorboards, and for the first time since the door opened, he stopped looking afraid.

"My father says clocks lie," Tam said.

"Does he." Silas kept her voice flat, working. "Most men say clocks tell the truth better than people do."

"He says a clock only tells you what time it is. Not what time it was." The boy swallowed. "He says that's why they keep records."

Something cold walked up Silas's spine. *They.* Not *we*. And spoken in the present tense by a child who had been put out of a carriage like a parcel.

"Who is your father, Tam?"

The boy shook his head, and this time the shaking went on too long, and the trembling chin failed altogether, and he cried the way children cry when they've been holding it in for hours—silently, terribly, without any sound at all, tears just falling while his face stayed composed around them. He'd been taught that too, Silas realized. Taught not to make noise. She had spent three years learning to be silent in a body that wasn't hers; she knew the curriculum.

She crouched beside him, awkward, having no practice at comfort and knowing it showed. "You don't have to tell me anything tonight."

"He said you'd ask." Tam wiped his face with his sleeve, leaving a wet streak. "He said if you asked, I should give you this and nothing else."

The oilcloth bundle. He unwrapped it with great care on the workbench, pushing aside her tweezers, and inside was a key.

Brass, heavy for its size, hand-cut—not cast, cut, by someone who understood wards and wanted this one particular lock difficult to pick. Stamped into the bow was a mark Silas knew better than she wanted to: the armillary sphere and star, the seal of the Royal Observatory. The same mark rode on the work orders she carried in her coat pocket, the ones that had summoned her, twice now, up the tower stairs to service the master clock.

Under the key, folded twice, a note. She read it by lamplight while the boy watched her read it.

*Forget his face. Forget the hour he came. Forget that you ever opened this door. Do this and your shop stands. Fail, and it burns—with everything in it.*

No signature. But the handwriting was confident, unhurried, the hand of a man accustomed to being obeyed by ink. And beneath the threat, almost lost in the fold, a second line, smaller, pressed hard enough to tear:

*He has no one else. I am sorry.*

Silas set the note down. Her hands were steady—her hands were always steady; it was the first thing her father had trained into her, before he'd ever taught her a lathe—but her heart was going like a trainee escapement, quick and uneven.

"Tam." She waited until he looked at her. "The man who brought you. Did he say what happens tomorrow?"

"After tomorrow." The boy frowned, reciting. "He said, after tomorrow everything will be different, and you'll understand why you were kind."

Everything will be different. In 1848 everyone said some version of that sentence—printers, politicians, drunkards in the taverns, the pamphlets Okafor pressed downstairs and slipped under doors. Silas had treated it as weather talk. Now it arrived in her shop in a wet coat with a brass key, and it sounded less like prophecy than scheduling.

"And your parents?" she asked quietly. "Where are they tonight?"

Tam looked at the key.

"They're recorded," he said.

* * *

She could not sleep, so she worked, which amounted to the same posture. The boy curled on the cot in the back room under her spare blanket, asleep within minutes, in the boneless way of children whose exhaustion outruns their fear. Every few minutes Silas stopped filing and listened to him breathe.

Forget his face.

She crossed to the doorway and looked at him in the thin light. Round face, rain-damp hair drying in dark curls. A small scar over the left eyebrow, old and healed white. Hands that would be a craftsman's hands someday, if anyone let them become anything. She catalogued him the way she catalogued everything—automatically, helplessly, the habit of a woman who had spent her life memorizing details that could hang her. The scar. The gap between his front teeth. The way his right hand stayed curled even in sleep, as if still gripping something.

A brass key. An observatory seal.

She thought about the tower. About the master clock running four seconds slow per day—a fault she'd corrected, and been thanked for, and then noticed creeping back within the fortnight, as though someone *wanted* the city's official time drifting. About the second movement she'd glimpsed inside the case, a mechanism no clockwork of hers had built, wound from the outside through a channel that shouldn't exist, keeping its own count behind the public face. She had told herself she'd imagined it. Craftsmen who saw things inside other men's machines learned to stop seeing them, or stopped getting work.

Now a child slept in her back room carrying a key to that machine's house, along with a note promising fire, and somewhere across the city a man in a warm carriage believed she would obey a piece of paper.

Her father's voice, dead ten years, spoke in her memory with terrible mildness: *A clockmaker who winds what she doesn't understand is just the fastest hand in someone else's machine.* He'd said it about guild politics. He'd never dreamed how far it would travel.

She could take the boy to the parish house at dawn. Say she found him. Wash her hands. Forget his face—she was, God knew, practiced at forgetting faces; she forgot her own daily, in every mirror, in every ledger where Silas Montrose signed a name belonging to no such person. One more forgetting would cost her nothing new.

But the boy had said *recorded*, in the tone other children used for *buried*. And the note-writer had apologized. Powerful men did not apologize unless the thing done required it, and things requiring apology were precisely the things ledgers existed to hold.

Silas turned the brass key over in her palm. The observatory mark caught the lamplight. Tomorrow was Thursday; the tower accepted workmen on Thursdays. She had a legitimate work order in her coat, a reason to climb those stairs, a pair of hands trusted near the master clock.

And a key that would open something she was not invited to open.

In the back room, Tam sighed in his sleep and turned over. Silas stood a long while in the doorway, doing arithmetic she didn't want to do—invisible against involved, safe against true—and watching the boy's face until she had committed it past forgetting.

Then she blew out the lamps, all but one, and began laying out her tools for morning.
