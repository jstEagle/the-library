## 3. The Second Movement

The key was warm from Tam's fist.

I had meant to return it. That was the whole of my intention when I locked the shop that grey Thursday and walked up the hill toward the observatory with the boy's brass key in my pocket and a lie ready on my tongue—*found it in the street, thought the director would want it back.* A small lie. The kind a man like Silas Montrose could tell without his voice cracking.

But lies have weight, and keys have teeth, and by the time I reached the observatory gate I had already turned the thing over in my mind forty times. It was stamped with the observatory's mark—the star within the circle—and it was new. Not old brass gone green in some forgotten drawer, but bright, recently cut, recently used. Keys are made for doors, and doors are made for rooms, and no room in an observatory needed a key cut that same season unless someone wanted it kept shut.

I told myself I was only returning it.

The porter knew me now. Three visits in two months had bought me a nod, even a grudging half-smile. *The clockmaker's boy again,* his face said, and let me pass into the courtyard where the great dome sat against the sky like a skull wearing a hat. The master clock stood in its tower at the east end, and I climbed the stairs with my tool bag over my shoulder and my heart doing something far less regular than any escapement I had ever built.

*Only returning it.*

The tower smelled of oil and cold stone. Morning light came through the slit windows in long blades, and dust hung in them, turning slowly, as if the air itself had been geared down. The master clock filled the far wall—my father would have wept to see it, a movement of such ambition, weights and wheels tall as a man, governing not one dial but a dozen across the city, sending the true hour out along buried rods and wires so that every church bell, every factory whistle, every clerk's pocket watch took its authority from this one heart.

I put my ear to the case first. It is what my father taught me before he taught me anything else. *Listen before you look, Silas. A mechanism will tell you where it hurts.*

It told me something else.

Beneath the slow, deliberate tick of the master train—the beat I had regulated myself, the honest four-second swing of the pendulum—there was a second sound. Finer. Faster. A whisper of gearing hidden somewhere inside the case, running on its own power, keeping its own time.

I had heard it faintly on my first visit and told myself it was echo. On my second, that the tower's acoustics were playing tricks. But tricks do not repeat themselves in perfect phase, and echoes do not stop when you press your palm flat against the oak paneling and feel, through the wood, a vibration that has nothing to do with the pendulum swinging three feet away.

Someone was winding a second movement inside the master clock.

I set down my bag. I took out my tools because my hands wanted something honest to hold. And then, because I am my father's daughter however much I dress as his son, I began to look for the seam.

The case panels were pegged, not screwed—old construction, meant to be opened for maintenance. But one panel, low on the right side behind the weight channel, had been pegged twice. Two holes, two pins, where every other joint showed three. A workman's economy, or a workman's discretion. I worked the pins loose with a drift punch wrapped in cloth so they would not ring against the stone floor, and eased the panel away, and looked into the dark.

There was a door in the back of the case. Small, iron-strapped, no larger than an oven door. And set into it, gleaming dully in the light from the slit window, was a lock.

I did not need to take out the boy's key to know it would fit. I knew it the way you know a face in a crowd you were told to forget. My hand went into my pocket anyway. The brass was warm still, or I imagined it was, and it slid into the lock with the smooth certainty of a thing made for exactly this purpose, and turned.

Inside was not a room. It was a shelf—a shallow compartment lined with green baize gone grey with age—and on the shelf lay papers.

Not gears. Papers.

I should have closed it then. I have thought about that moment many times since, the way a man thinks about a road not taken, and I have never once believed I could have closed it. Curiosity is a mainspring. Once it is wound, it drives everything after it.

I lifted the top sheaf into the light.

A ledger. Bound in plain calf, unmarked on the spine, the pages ruled in a careful commercial hand. Dates going back nine years. And names—columns of them, each entry neat as a bill of sale:

*Vasquez, Miguel — cooper, Rue aux Loups. Removed 14 March. Payment to householder: 40 francs. Order ref: H.*

*Brandt, Elise — midwife. Removed 2 June. No payment recorded. Order ref: H.*

*Osei, Daniel & Osei, Marie — removed together, 19 September. Child unaccounted. Order ref: H.*

Child unaccounted.

My hands had gone very steady, which is how fear shows itself in me—not trembling but a terrible calm, a craftsman's calm, as if the body has decided that whatever comes next must be done precisely. I turned page after page. Payments to aldermen. Payments to the garrison commander. Sums entered against names I knew—shopkeepers, printers, a schoolmaster whose empty chair I had walked past for two years wondering why no one spoke of him. And beside nearly every entry, the same two letters, in the same disciplined hand:

*H.*

Havel.

The director who had shaken my hand on my first visit, whose rings had clicked against my knuckles, who had praised my father's reputation and paid my fee in advance without haggling—that man had been signing orders for disappearances from a desk somewhere below this tower while the whole city took its time from the clock above his head.

And the second movement—the whisper of gearing I had chased through the case—what was that? I looked deeper into the compartment. Behind the ledger, bolted to the inner frame of the master clock itself, was a smaller mechanism, elegant and vicious: a secondary train driven off the main pendulum but geared to run slow. Deliberately slow. Eleven seconds in the hour, if my eye was any judge. A drift so small no ordinary person would ever notice—but enough that, accumulated week upon week, month upon month, the clocks of the city would fall quietly out of truth with the sun and stars, and no one could say when it had happened, or prove it, or call it a lie.

He was not stealing time. He was editing it. Slowing the city's memory of noon itself, so that whatever record existed—whatever alibi, whatever testimony—could be argued into fog. I thought of Okafor next door, setting type by lamplight, swearing that yesterday's pamphlet contradicted the one from the week before. A city that cannot agree on the hour cannot agree on anything.

I heard the footsteps on the stair while I was still holding the ledger.

Heavy, unhurried, familiar. I had perhaps twenty seconds. Twenty seconds is enough to close a panel; it is not enough to un-know what you know. I slid the ledger back onto its baize shelf, pressed the little door shut, fitted the panel home with fingers that refused to hurry, and had my drift punch back in my bag when Director Havel came up the last steps into the tower.

"Master Montrose." He smiled the way a well-made lock closes—smoothly, completely. "I hoped it might be you. The hour hand on the west dial drags, they tell me."

"It does," I said. "I was just listening to the train." My voice held. I marveled at it from a distance, the way one marvels at a clockwork figure that bows and gestures while the spring inside screams.

"You have a good ear," Havel said. He crossed the tower slowly, hands clasped behind his back, and stood looking up at the great wheels with something like affection. "Your father had it too. A rare gift—to hear what a machine is saying." He turned his head, and the light from the slit window caught his eyes, and there was nothing affectionate in them at all. "Tell me. What did it say to you?"

"The third wheel wants oiling," I said. "And the escapement anchor has worn a hair proud. I can true it today."

"A hair," he repeated softly. "Such small things matter to your trade. A hair, a second, a name misspelled." He moved closer. Close enough that I could smell the coffee on his breath and see the fine stitching of his cuffs. "You found the panel, Master Montrose. Don't insult us both. The pins were re-seated a quarter-turn wrong. You noticed. Of course you noticed—you're the only man in this city who listens to clocks properly."

The tower was very quiet. Somewhere below, the porter's boots scraped the courtyard stones, impossibly far away.

"I don't know what you mean, sir," I said.

"No," Havel agreed. "You wouldn't. That's the beauty of hiring tradesmen—they see only the work in front of them." He drew something from his coat. A bank draft, folded once. He did not offer it; he simply held it, letting me read the figure written there. It was more money than my shop would earn in ten years. More than my father had earned in his whole careful, invisible life. "There is a movement inside that case that concerns the governance of this city. Its maintenance requires discretion. I am prepared to pay extremely well for a clockmaker who hears nothing but wheels."

"And if I refuse?"

"Refuse?" He seemed genuinely puzzled, a scholar confronted with an error in arithmetic. "Montrose, I'm offering you a fortune to do nothing. Men have done far worse for far less. Take the draft. Repair the anchor. True the escapement. Go home to your shop and your apprentice and your quiet life, and be rich, and be safe." He tucked the draft into my breast pocket himself, patting it flat, almost gently. "Or leave it here, walk out, and spend the rest of your life listening for footsteps on stairs. I find most men choose the draft. Most men understand that memory is a luxury."

*A child unaccounted.* Elise Brandt, who had delivered half the babies of the riverside. The schoolmaster's empty chair. A seven-year-old boy asleep in my back room with a bruise on his cheekbone and a key clutched in his fist like a talisman, and someone, somewhere, had written *child unaccounted* in a neat commercial hand and moved on to the next line.

I took the draft out of my pocket.

Havel's smile widened.

I held it out to him. "I repair clocks, sir," I said. "I don't repair ledgers. If your movement needs maintenance, hire a man who works in the dark. I hear you know several."

For a moment—only a moment—the mask slipped, and I saw beneath it something cold and patient and entirely without mercy, the face of a man who had signed *removed* beside dozens of names and slept soundly after. Then the smile returned, smoother than ever.

"Very well," he said. "Keep your hearing, Montrose. Ears can be stopped up later." He turned toward the stair, then paused, one hand on the rail. "Come Tuesday to regulate the west dial. Alone. And Montrose—" he did not look back— "the boy's key. Return it to me personally. Some doors should not be left open."

He descended. His footsteps faded. The pendulum swung on, four seconds out, four seconds back, faithful and deceived, and I stood alone in the tower with my heart hammering like a loose flywheel and the taste of iron in my mouth.

I had refused him. The word felt enormous, absurd—I had refused a fortune, refused safety, refused the comfortable dark—and I could not yet tell whether I had acted from courage or from sheer stubborn craft-pride, the clockmaker's horror of false work. Perhaps it did not matter. Refusal, once spoken, runs forward like a released train; it cannot be wound back.

Because I had lied to him too, and he did not know it. When I closed that compartment, I had not returned the ledger to its shelf entire. One sheaf of pages—three weeks of entries, six names, six *Order ref: H*s—was folded flat against my ribs, under my waistcoat, warming there now like a second heartbeat.

Below me, the city's bells began to strike eleven—eleven o'clock by the master clock, that is, which by the sun and the stars was eleven minutes past, and by next month would be a quarter hour adrift, and by spring would belong entirely to Director Havel.

Unless someone made the clocks tell the truth.

I picked up my tool bag and started down the stairs, past the porter, through the gate, downhill toward the shop where a boy waited who remembered a face he'd been ordered to forget—and where, come morning, a printer named Okafor would need to see what I carried against my ribs.

* * *

Tam was sitting on the step when I came around the corner, knees drawn up, watching the street with the wariness of a stray cat. He scrambled up when he saw me, searched my face for something, and found it.

"You went to the tower," he said.

"How do you know?"

"Because you smell like it." He said it simply, the way children say terrible things. "Oil and stone. That's where the man lives. The man with the rings." His hand went unconsciously to his own cheekbone, to the bruise gone yellow now. "He said I'd forget his face. I didn't."

I crouched down to his level, and through the wool of my waistcoat I felt the folded pages shift against my skin, and understood that whatever came next had already begun.

"Then you'd better come inside, Tam," I said. "There are things I need you to remember out loud."
