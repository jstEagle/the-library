## 4. What the City Remembers

The first smoke came before the first bell.

Silas noticed it from the shop window at dawn—a gray thread rising past the rooftops on the east side, where the grain warehouses stood. By the time she had lit the forge and set the kettle on, the thread had thickened into a column, and the column had begun to lean, and somewhere beyond the market a sound was building that she had never heard in this city: not a riot's scattered shouting but a single sustained roar, like a furnace door opened all at once across half a mile of street.

Tam stood at her elbow, small and warm through his borrowed shirt. He had slept three nights now in the cot behind the workbench, and he no longer flinched when she moved suddenly. He watched the smoke with the flat attention of a child who has already seen worse.

"Is it a fire?" he asked.

"It's more than a fire."

Down the lane, shutters were banging open. The lamplighter stood in the middle of the cobbles in his nightshirt, holding his pole like a pike, listening. A woman ran past with a bundle of bread under one arm and nothing else. Someone was hammering on doors two streets over—three blows, a pause, three blows again—and each time the hammering passed, another door opened and another man slipped out with whatever iron he owned wrapped in sacking.

Silas closed the shutters. It was instinct, the same instinct that kept her voice low and her collar high and her name signed as *S. Montrose* on every invoice: what is seen can be taken. But her hands paused on the latch, because closing shutters was also what a guilty house did, and she had spent three nights with a ledger's contents folded into her memory like a blade wrapped in cloth—the names, the sums, the word *removed* written in Director Havel's neat, patient hand beside eleven entries, and once, chillingly, *the child*.

She looked down at Tam. He was still watching the shuttered light.

"Eat your bread," she said. "All of it."

* * *

Okafor came at midmorning, and he did not knock so much as arrive, filling her doorway the way he filled every doorway, ink-stained to the second knuckle and breathing hard.

"You've heard." It wasn't a question. "They've taken the customs house. The garrison hasn't fired yet—they won't, I think, they're waiting to see which way the wind sits—but Silas, the *typesetters*. Half my men walked out at dawn. They're setting pamphlets for the committee instead of my orders." He laughed, a short unhappy sound. "My own apprentices. Setting type for a revolution before breakfast."

"Then you'll be paid in gratitude," Silas said, "which buys nothing."

"Gratitude buys everything, if you print it fast enough." He stepped inside without invitation—he had been doing that for twenty years—and stopped. His eyes went to Tam, then back to her, and she saw him decide, deliberately, not to ask. That was Okafor's whole character in a gesture: he knew what questions cost, and he rationed them.

"I need paper," he said instead. "You have packing stock. And I need you to look at something."

He drew a pamphlet from inside his coat—not a proof, a finished sheet, already folded, already creased by other hands. He laid it on her workbench among the gear blanks and the oilstone, and Silas read it standing, because her knees wanted otherwise.

*THE PEOPLE'S ACCOUNT OF THE THIRD OF MARCH*, the headline said. Below it, in bold, certain type: how the uprising had begun with the weavers' petition; how the petition had been answered with muskets; how the people rose as one body against tyranny. It was stirring. It was clean. Every cause had a single origin, every grievance a single author, and not one line of it matched what Silas had read in Havel's ledger—where the weavers' petition appeared not as a beginning but as an *expense*, entered weeks before any musket fired, paid to men whose names were recorded only as initials, with the notation *provoke and disperse*.

"This is dated tomorrow," she said quietly.

"Set last night," Okafor said. "Printed this morning. The fighting isn't finished, Silas. Men are dying in the streets right now for reasons that haven't been decided yet—and the decision is being made in lead type while they bleed." He tapped the sheet. "Whoever wins prints first. Whoever prints first owns the third of March forever. In ten years, this"—he held up the pamphlet—"will be what happened. Not the thing itself. This."

"And the ledger?" She hadn't meant to say it aloud. The words were out before she could weigh them, and she watched Okafor go very still, the way a man goes still when a clock he didn't know was ticking suddenly strikes.

"What ledger?"

"Nothing."

"Silas." He said her name the way he set type—one letter at a time, carefully. "I have known you since you were a boy too thin for your coat. I know there are things about you I don't ask after. But if you have something in this shop that men would burn a city to control, then this is not the morning to be alone with it."

She looked at the pamphlet. At Tam, who had gone quiet in the corner, watching them both with that flat, old attention. At the ledger's hiding place, which was also the boy's hiding place, which was also the only place in the world she could currently defend.

"Paper," she said finally. "Take the paper. Take all of it."

He took it. At the door he turned. "Whatever you're carrying—carry it to me before the fires do. Printers burn last, Silas. Remember that. We're useful to everyone until the very end."

*The Montrose boy is not a boy. I have suspected since the year of the flood, when I saw those hands at the pump—too fine for a laborer's son, and the way "he" counted change without looking, the way a woman counts, palm open. I say nothing. What does it matter? The city is burning its own face off, and every man in it is wearing a mask of some kind. Hers is simply better made than most. I am a printer; I deal in surfaces. I know a well-set page when I see one.*

*But the ledger worries me. Whatever she carries, she carries it like a man carrying water through a fireline—carefully, and knowing he will be burned anyway. If she brings it to me, I will set it. God forgive me, I will set every line of it, and let the type decide who hangs.*

* * *

The afternoon brought the sound of the mob past their street—a river of feet and voices that rose, crested, and passed without turning in, and Silas understood that they had been spared not by luck but by insignificance. A clockmaker's shop was worth nothing to a revolution. She should have been relieved. Instead she felt the particular chill of a person whom history has decided to walk around.

Tam did not eat his supper.

He sat on the cot with the brass key in his fist—the observatory key, stamped with the star-and-wheel mark, which he had carried against his chest for three days like a second heartbeat—and he watched the firelight move on the wall. Twice Silas caught his lips moving, shaping something without sound.

"Tam."

He looked up. In the firelight his face seemed older than seven, and younger, both at once.

"There was a man," he said.

Silas set down the pan. Her heart began a slow, heavy stroke, like a weight descending in a clock case. "What man?"

"The one who left me at your door. He wasn't the same one who—" Tam's brow furrowed, working at it the way he worked at a knot. "There were two men. One drove the cart. One sat with me and told me things. He said—" The boy's voice dropped, reciting, and Silas realized he was repeating words learned by rote, drilled into him until they'd worn grooves. *"Forget my face. Forget the house. Forget the cart. You remember nothing and nobody remembers you, and that is how you stay alive."*

"Did you see his face?"

"He thought I had my eyes shut. I peeked." Tam said it simply, without triumph. "He had a scar. Here." He touched his own jaw, left side, a thin diagonal line. "And he wore a ring. A big one. With a wheel on it. Like my key."

The star-and-wheel. The observatory's mark. Silas kept her hands very still on her knees, because a child reads hands the way sailors read sky.

"Did he say anything else?"

Tam nodded slowly. Then stopped. Then, in a smaller voice: "He said the director would keep me safe. He said the director always keeps what's his." His eyes came up, wide and dark. "Was that a bad thing to say?"

Every entry in the ledger rearranged itself behind Silas's eyes. *Removed.* Eleven times. And once, in a different ink, fresher: *the child — held pending.*

"No," she said. "It wasn't bad. It was brave." She crossed the room and sat beside him on the narrow cot, and after a moment he leaned into her side, and she let him, and the fire cracked, and outside the city roared and burned and began, even now, to write down what it would claim to remember.

"Tam," she said quietly. "The man with the scar. Tomorrow, I'm going to draw faces—all kinds of faces—and I want you to tell me which ones are wrong. Can you do that?"

He nodded against her shoulder.

"And Tam. No one is going to make you forget anything ever again. Not while I keep the shop."

Outside, far off toward the observatory hill, a church bell began to strike the hour—and stopped, mid-peal, one stroke short. Silas lifted her head. Somewhere in the dark, someone was slowing time itself, and she knew exactly whose hand was on the winding crank.

By morning, she decided, she would go back up the hill. She would need a face drawn first, and paper, and courage she did not yet own—but the clock would not wait, and neither, now, would the truth.
