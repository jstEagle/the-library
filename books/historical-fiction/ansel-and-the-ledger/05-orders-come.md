## 5. Orders Come

They came at dawn, when the light through the workshop windows was still the color of pewter and the glue pot had only just begun to smell of hide and heat.

Silas heard the boots first—too many of them, moving in step, which no honest men did at that hour—and then the knock, three blows with the flat of a hand against her door as if the door had done something wrong. She was upstairs in a heartbeat. Tam stirred on the cot behind the partition; she pressed one finger to her lips and he went still again, obedient in the way of children who had learned that stillness was survival.

"Open in the name of the Provisional Committee."

The words were new. The tone was old—she had heard it from tax assessors, from guild inspectors, from every man who had ever wanted into a shop where something valuable was kept.

She went down, buttoned her coat to the throat, bound her hair tighter beneath the cap, and opened the door on six men and a morning gone grey with woodsmoke. Their leader held a paper stamped twice over, the wax still tacky. Behind him she could see other doors opening down the street, other shops, other clockmakers standing pale in their thresholds while their wives pulled children back by the sleeves.

"Silas Montrose," the leader read, though his eyes said *the Montrose apprentice*, the way everyone's eyes did, the way they always had. "By order of the Committee for Public Order, all tools, records, and instruments of precision are subject to inspection and seizure."

"Instruments of precision." She kept her voice flat, bored, a man's boredom. "That's every tool I own."

"Then you'll have nothing to worry about." He smiled without warmth and stepped past her into the shop, and the others followed like water finding its level.

They were not gentle. They were not even thorough in any way that served justice—they swept lathe chucks and files into crates with the indifference of men clearing rubble, and Silas stood with her arms folded and watched eleven years of her father's craft being stacked like firewood. A boy of perhaps sixteen among them pried open the drawers of the bench where her father's finest escapements lay wrapped in oiled cloth, and something in her chest turned over like a mainspring snapping.

"The records," the leader said.

"Ledger's there." She nodded at the shelf above the bench—the shop ledger, orders and deliveries, twenty years of honest accounts. Let them read it line by line. There was nothing in it but time sold and time kept.

One of them flipped through it, licked his thumb, frowned. "You deliver clocks?"

"When they're paid for."

"What's outstanding?"

"One longcase," she said, before she had decided to say it. "Finished Friday. Waiting on the cart."

He looked up at her then, and she saw him do the arithmetic all inspectors do—whether this man was worth more compliant or squeezed. "Where is it?"

"Standing in the back room, waiting to be crated. You're welcome to look at it. It tells the hours, nothing else."

He didn't look. That was the mercy of men who believed the world was made only of what they understood: they never looked inside the cases.

* * *

The longcase clock stood seven feet tall in the back room, walnut darkened by decades, its bonnet crowned with a broken pediment Silas had restored herself. It was a fine piece of work and an honest one, and until two nights ago it had contained nothing but wheels, weights, and a pendulum that kept better time than most church bells.

Now it contained Tam.

She had built the false back years ago, a smuggler's trick learned from her father, who had once moved a whole movement past customs in a case lined with hymnals. The cavity ran the depth of the trunk, wide enough for a child of seven curled around the brass key he refused to surrender, breathing through a gap she had drilled beneath the sound board where the chime hammers rested. She had told him it would be like holding his breath underwater, and he had looked at her with those too-old eyes and said, "I can hold it a long time. I practiced."

*Practiced.* She had not asked what for. Some questions were stones she could not yet afford to turn.

The inspector's boots came down the hall toward the back room, and Silas positioned herself in the doorway with a delivery docket in hand, invented that morning in Okafor's back office, inked in the printer's own hand because a forged docket should carry genuine ink.

"Longcase, for delivery to the Rue aux Chats," she said. "Paid in full. You want it on your list?"

The man glanced at the docket, at the clock, at her. For one suspended second his gaze lingered on the case, and Silas felt the whole world narrow to the width of a walnut panel and the small, held breath behind it.

"Rue aux Chats is barricaded," he said.

"Then I'll wait for the barricade to come down. Clocks keep. That's rather the point of them."

Something almost like amusement crossed his face. He stamped the docket, moved on, and the boots receded down the corridor, and Silas stood in the empty back room listening to her own heart count seconds until Tam's small fist knocked twice from inside—the signal—and she opened the case and lifted him out, limp and shining with sweat.

"You breathed like I showed you?" she whispered.

He nodded. Then, muffled against her coat: "There was a spider."

"Well. Now you've survived worse than revolution."

He laughed—a small, cracked sound—and it undid her more than the search had.

* * *

Jem arrived at midday, whistling, and stopped whistling the moment he came through the door and saw the state of the shop. He was nineteen, broad-shouldered, slow to anger and slower to fear, and he had been her apprentice for four years without ever once asking why Master Montrose's son had such careful hands or such a horror of the communal bathhouse. Loyalty, Silas had learned, was mostly a matter of what people chose not to see.

"They hit Père Aubert's place first," Jem said, setting down his satchel. "Took his whole stock of jeweled bearings. Said precision instruments were instruments of the old regime. Old regime—" he spat toward the floor. "Aubert taught half the men doing the taking how to hold a file."

"I know."

"And they'll be back. They took names at every shop. Yours is on the list twice—I heard the clerk read it."

Twice. Once as Montrose, once, she suspected, in whatever file Havel kept in the drawer of his mind, filed under *the clockmaker who saw too much*. She began, without quite deciding to, sweeping shattered glass from the display case into a dustpan.

"Jem. The longcase goes out tonight. Rue aux Chats."

"Barricaded streets with a clock on a handcart? They'll stop us at the first corner."

"Not you. Me. Alone."

His face did the thing faces did when they decided to argue with her. "Master, with respect—you can't haul seven feet of walnut through a city that's burning. And if they stop you—"

"Then I'm stopped. But the delivery has to leave this shop tonight, and it can't be traced here afterward. If anyone asks, the clock was collected by a carter you never saw, paid in advance, and you haven't the least idea where it went. Say it dull enough and they'll believe you, because you believe it yourself by then. That's the whole art of testimony, Jem. Keep it boring."

He watched her a moment. "And you? Where will you be while your boring testimony holds?"

"On the Rue aux Chats. Delivering a clock."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only shape an answer comes in tonight."

He shook his head, but he was already pulling on his work apron, because Jem argued the way good apprentices did—with his hands already obeying. Together they crated the longcase in straw and planking, and Silas worked with a steadiness she did not feel, screwing down battens over the false panel that hid a sleeping child, thinking: *this is what my father meant about weight.* Everything true weighed something. Everything worth keeping had to be carried, and carrying meant putting something else down.

She just had not expected to put down a person.

* * *

They took Jem at dusk, on the corner outside the shop, where the gas lamps had gone unlit for a week.

Silas saw it from the window—two men detaching themselves from a doorway with the unhurried confidence of men who knew exactly whom they wanted. They spoke to Jem. Jem shook his head. One of them produced a paper, and Jem laughed, actually laughed, and then the second man took his arm, not gently, and the first one struck him across the mouth with a gloved hand, casual as swatting a fly, and then they walked him away down the street between them, and the street closed behind them like water.

Silas stood at the glass with her hand pressed flat against it and did not go out. She counted ten seconds. Twenty. Long enough to have shouted, to have run after them, to have said *take me instead, it's my name on your list twice*—and she had used those seconds to stand perfectly still, because Tam was asleep upstairs, and the crate with the false back sat crated in the front room, and a man who surrendered herself now surrendered everything she was carrying.

That was the arithmetic. She had measured it out herself, gear by gear, the way her father had taught her: what could be carried, what must be left. And the terrible thing, the thing she knew she would be turning over at 3 a.m. for the rest of her life, was that the mechanism worked. The weights balanced. She stayed free, the ledger stayed hidden, the child stayed safe—and Jem walked away between two men with blood on his lip because he had the bad fortune to answer her door at the wrong hour.

Okafor found her like that an hour later, still at the window, the shop dark around her.

"They released word through the printers' quarter," he said quietly. "Detainments for questioning. Names posted tomorrow. His will be on it."

"I know."

"You could trade something. You have things Havel wants."

"I know that too." Her voice came out lower than she intended. "That's what makes it filthy. It isn't even a hard choice, Okafor. That's what I keep coming back to. It's easy. Hand over the ledger, buy the boy back, go back to making clocks. Everyone keeps their hands." She turned from the window. "Except everyone whose name is written in that book. Except Jem, who'd learn exactly what he was traded for. Belonging's always been a transaction. I just never set the terms before."

Okafor was silent a moment. Outside, somewhere east, a building burned with the deep orange patience of a fire that intended to finish its work.

"There's a press run starting tonight," he said. "New pamphlets. The Committee's version of why the fighting started. My own compositors are setting it." He smiled, and there was no pleasure in it. "History's going to print very fast this week, Madame Montrose. Faster than truth can walk. Unless someone carries it."

She looked at the crated longcase standing in the shadows of her ruined shop, seven feet of walnut with a heartbeat inside it, and beyond it at the workbench where her father's tools had been, and at the empty hook where Jem's apron hung.

"Then truth learns to ride on a handcart," she said. "Wake me at midnight. The barricades come down easiest before dawn—they change the sentries, and new sentries don't know faces yet."

"And Jem?"

Silas picked up the docket, stamped that morning by a man who had believed a clock was only a clock.

"Jem is the next delivery," she said. "Whatever it costs."

Above them, faint through the smoke-hazed night, the observatory bell began to strike—and stopped, one stroke short of the hour, patient as a held breath, keeping the city's true time for whoever would finally come to set it right.
