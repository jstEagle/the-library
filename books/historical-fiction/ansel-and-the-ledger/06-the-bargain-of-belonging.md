## 6. The Bargain of Belonging

The chronometer lay open on my bench like a heart I had cut out of my own chest.

It was my father's finest work—the marine timekeeper he had spent eleven years perfecting, the one that kept true through the damp of three sea trials while every rival's mechanism wept rust. I had oiled it each month since his death, though it needed no oiling, the way one tends a grave. Now I disassembled it entirely, cleaned each wheel in benzine, and put it back together with hands that would not quite stop trembling.

Jem had been in the Prefecture cells for six days.

They had taken him on a Tuesday, in my apron, at my bench, holding my tools. He had not resisted. He had looked at me once over the shoulder of the man gripping him—not accusingly, which would have been easier to bear—and mouthed something I chose to read as *the back room*, because if it had been anything else I could not have gone on working. The back room held Tam, asleep among clock cases, and the ledger wrapped in oilcloth beneath the floorboards where my father had once hidden contraband brandy for a magistrate long dead.

I worked by candlelight because the shop's shutters stayed closed now, and because the city outside had begun to smell of smoke that never fully cleared. Barricades rose and were torn down and rose again two streets over. Gunpowder had replaced coffee as the morning scent of our quarter. And still I sat with jeweler's tweezers, setting a balance spring finer than an eyelash, because precision was the only prayer I knew.

Tam watched me from atop a crate of pendulum rods, legs swinging, a heel of bread forgotten in his fist.

"You're doing the thing," he said. "Where your face goes away."

"Am I."

"When Papa used to think about money." He said *Papa* without flinching now; he had adopted my father the way orphans adopt saints, without evidence and without apology. "His face went away too."

I set the last jewel into its chaton and pressed it home. "Then we'll both stop thinking about money when this is done."

"And Jem comes home?"

"And Jem comes home."

He nodded with terrible solemnity, seven years old and already fluent in the grammar of bargains, and went back to winding the small alarm clock I had given him to keep his hands busy. Its ticking filled the dark shop like a small stubborn pulse.

* * *

Director Havel received me in the observatory itself, not the Prefecture—a choice I understood at once as theater. He wanted the meeting conducted under the dome, among his instruments, on ground where he was master of everything measurable.

Two soldiers flanked the door. Havel stood at the great orrery, its brass planets frozen mid-orbit, turning a model of Mars with one finger.

"Monsieur Montrose." He did not turn around. "You brought it?"

"I brought what we agreed upon."

"The chronometer." He turned then, smiling the way frost smiles on glass. "Yes. Your father's work, isn't it? I remember when it won the Academy prize. I was younger then. We all believed instruments could be honest."

I set the case on his table and opened it. The escapement ticked—steady, indifferent, more honest than either of us. Havel bent over it for a long moment, and I saw something almost like grief cross his face before he shuttered it.

"It keeps true time," he said. "Rare quality. In a man or a machine."

"A man can lie about the hour. A chronometer cannot."

"No." He closed the lid gently, reverently even. "Which is why I keep mine inside a locked case and let lesser clocks tell people what they wish to hear." He lifted the chronometer and handed it to a soldier, who carried it from the room as if bearing relics. "Your apprentice will be released at the north gate within the hour. Beaten, I'm afraid—I couldn't prevent it entirely—but walking."

The relief hit me so hard I nearly swayed. I kept my spine iron. "Then we're finished."

"We're just beginning, Monsieur Montrose." He walked slowly along the orrery, trailing his fingers above the brass rings without touching them. "There is a second matter. A small one. A ledger, missing from my records hall. Kept, I believe, in the workshop of one S. Montrose, clockmaker, late of Rue des Horlogers."

My blood went to ice water. "I don't know what you mean."

"Don't you?" He stopped. Turned. And there it was—that smile again, but warmer now, which made it infinitely worse. "Let me tell you what else I know, since we are being honest, you and I. I know that Silas Montrose died of fever in Lyon in the winter of 1839. I know the man who buried him signed the parish register with a hand that shook. And I know that the apprentice who inherited the shop had the same burn scar on his left forearm that the daughter had—acquired, if my information is correct, at age nine, from a soldering iron she should not have been holding."

The room did not spin, though I felt it try. I gripped the edge of the table. Somewhere above us the great dome ticked with thermal expansion, the building breathing.

"Say it plainly," I said, and my voice came out lower than I intended, rougher. "Whatever you intend to do, say it plainly."

"What I intend," Havel said softly, "is nothing. That is the entire point. I intend that nothing happen. No arrest. No trial. No mob at your door—though mobs are cheap these days, Monsieur, cheaper than ever, and a rumor is cheaper than both. All I require is the ledger. You keep your shop. You keep your secret. You keep—" he gestured at me, at the man's coat, the man's name, eleven years of careful architecture "—*yourself*. Which I imagine is worth a great deal to you."

It was. God forgive me, it was worth everything. Eleven years of binding, of the voice pitched low, of learning to walk with my shoulders forward, of being addressed as *monsieur* and swallowing the strange grief of it every time—the grief of winning a prize written in someone else's name. My father's trade, my father's customers, my father's grave-marker pride: all of it stood on a foundation Havel could dissolve with one sentence spoken to the wrong ears.

And Jem. Jem, who had worn my apron into chains without knowing why anyone would want them.

"I want to see him first," I said. "Released. Walking. Then you get the ledger."

Havel spread his hands, magnanimous as a bishop. "Of course. I am not a monster, Monsieur Montrose. I am an archivist. There is a difference, though history may eventually confuse us." He smiled at his own joke. "Bring it tomorrow. Noon. And bring it alone—or rather," and here his voice dropped into something almost gentle, almost kind, which was the cruelest register he owned, "bring yourself. Whatever you are. Whoever you've decided to be."

* * *

They released Jem at dusk, as promised. I found him at the north gate leaning against the wall like a coat hung on a hook, lip split, one eye swollen nearly shut, walking on a foot that favored the left side badly. When he saw me he tried to straighten, tried to produce the grin he used when he'd botched a polish and hoped charm would cover it, and failed halfway.

"Begging your pardon, Master Montrose," he said. "I didn't tell them anything. I want that said plain. They asked about the back room and I told them I sweep it and it's full of shavings, which is even true, mostly."

"You told them nothing. I know it." I got my shoulder under his arm. He weighed nothing at all; they had starved him efficiently in six days. "Come inside. Tam's been keeping vigil."

"He has? It's been dull, then, has it? Poor lad." But his breath caught as we walked, and after ten steps he said, very quietly, so the sentries couldn't hear: "They knew things, sir. About the ledger. About—about you." His swollen eye found mine, and there was no accusation in it, only fear on my behalf. "I didn't understand half of it. I said they were liars. They laughed."

"I know what they know."

"Sir—" He stopped us both in the shadow of the gatehouse. "Whatever it costs you. Don't pay it for me. I'm one boy. I can take a beating. I've had worse from my own father."

"That is precisely why I'm paying it," I said, "because you shouldn't have to take beatings at all, and because the men who give them are counting on people like you to believe you're cheap."

He stared at me. Then, wincing, he grinned the whole way this time. "That's the most words you've ever wasted on me, Master Montrose."

"Walk."

* * *

That night I sat at the workbench until the candles guttered, the ledger open before me, and I did not touch it.

Tam slept in the loft above, breathing slow. Jem dozed in the chair by the stove, his bandaged foot propped on a stool, snoring with the magnificent unconsciousness of the young. The shop ticked around us—forty-odd clocks I had built or mended, all of them keeping slightly different times because I had never synchronized them, because some part of me had always liked the idea that a room could hold many truths at once.

Belonging, I thought. I had spent my whole life buying it in installments. Belonging to the guild: purchased with my father's name and my mother's silence. Belonging to the quarter: purchased with work so good no one dared look too closely at the worker. Belonging to my own reflection, even—that had been a transaction, negotiated daily in front of the glass with wax and string and a jaw set just so. And always the terms had been written by others. Always the price had been quoted in a currency I didn't control: *be invisible, be less, be a man, be silent, and you may remain.*

Havel had simply done what everyone had always done—named his fee. The ledger. My silence. Myself. And he'd been right about one thing, damn him: I would have paid it, a week ago. For Jem alone I would have paid it.

But Jem had come home. And Tam had climbed down from his loft in the night, padding barefoot across the boards, and stood beside my chair looking at the ledger with those eyes that remembered faces he'd been ordered to forget.

"Is that the book with the names?" he whispered.

"Yes."

"My mama and papa are in it?"

"I believe so."

He thought about that for a long moment, thumb in his mouth, too old for the gesture and past caring. Then: "If you give it away, nobody will know their names anymore. Except me. And I'm little." He said it without self-pity, the way children state arithmetic. "Somebody big ought to know."

He climbed back to bed. I sat until the first gray light came through the shutter cracks, and I took up a pen, and I began to copy.

Not to hide the ledger—to multiply it. If Havel wanted one book, he would learn what clockmakers know: that a movement, properly designed, does not depend on any single part. Break one wheel and the train still turns. I copied every page in a hand disguised as my father's, hid the original in the one place no searcher would look—in the weighted cylinder of the great regulator clock standing in Okafor's print shop, where it would swing above the press that had already begun printing lies, a truth beating inside the machinery of falsehood.

Tomorrow at noon I would carry the copy to Havel. Let him take it. Let him burn it, drown it, lock it in his vault with his frozen planets. The original would hang in the print shop, ticking, waiting.

And when the city finally rose—and it would rise; you could feel it gathering in the streets like pressure in a boiler—he would learn the difference between an archivist and a horologist.

An archivist locks time away. A clockmaker sets it loose.

At dawn I blew out the candles, woke Jem, and sent him to Okafor with a message written in cipher we had invented years ago for pricing disputes: *Deliver the regulator to the tower. Wind it fully.*

The bells, I decided, were going to ring the true hour whether the city was ready or not.
