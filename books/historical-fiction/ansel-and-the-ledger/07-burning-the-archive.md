## 7. Burning the Archive

The smoke came before the bells.

Silas saw it from the shop window at dawn—a black column rising over the university quarter, leaning east on the wind like a man drunk on his own weight. Okafor stood beside her, still in his nightshirt, ink to the second knuckle of both hands, and neither of them spoke for a long moment. Then he said, "That's the records hall," in the flat voice of a man naming a corpse, and went upstairs to dress.

"They'll burn the pamphlets too," Silas said. "Everything printed."

"Everything written." Okafor pulled on his coat. "Paper is paper when the fire wants it."

Tam was already awake—had been awake since the first distant crackle of musketry an hour before dawn—and stood at the foot of the stairs with his shoes held against his chest like a hymnal. He had learned in four months among clockmakers that when the adults went quiet and quick, you dressed fast and asked nothing. He looked at Silas now with a question he did not voice, and she hated that she could read him so easily, because the answer was no, you are staying here, behind a locked door, and she knew it was a lie she would not keep.

"The ledger," she said instead, turning away from the smoke.

"Already packed." Okafor tapped his satchel. "I made a second copy last week. You didn't know because you'd have told me not to waste the candle."

"I'd have told you it would get us hanged."

"It *will* get us hanged," Okafor agreed cheerfully, "if we don't get there first. The mob burns everything. Havel's men will burn selectively—the pages with his signature on them, if they can reach the hall before the fire does. Either way, by noon those names exist only if we carry them out." He shouldered the bag. "You built clocks your whole life, Montrose. You know what happens to a mechanism when one spring fails."

She did. She had spent the night not sleeping, listening to the city, and what she heard was a mainspring winding past its limit.

* * *

They went on foot, through streets already half-transformed. Barricades stood where cobblers' stalls had been two days ago; women handed up roof tiles to men on the barricade tops; a barber shaved a national guardsman in a doorway as calmly as if it were Sunday. Nobody looked twice at a printer, a tall apprentice clockmaker, and a boy of seven walking fast toward the smoke—which was, Silas thought, the whole strange mercy of a city in upheaval: for once, everyone was going somewhere they shouldn't.

The records hall occupied the old granary beside the observatory hill, three floors of shelving, forty years of the directorate's correspondence, tax rolls, court registers, and—on the second floor, in a cabinet Havel believed no one knew of—the originals of everything the hidden movement had transcribed into its secret twin. The observatory itself rose above the rooftops beyond it, its dome pale in the morning light, indifferent as a skull.

They smelled the fire before they turned the last corner. Not smoke now but fire itself, a low animal sound, and when the hall came into view Silas understood that they were late.

Flame stood in the ground-floor windows like congregation members singing. The great doors had been forced and fed with furniture; a crowd ringed the building at a distance that was half reverence, half fear, and among them moved men with sacks—Havel's men, she knew them by their civilian coats worn like costumes, by the way they worked the edges of the crowd rather than its center. They were not fighting the fire. They were waiting for the shelves to collapse far enough to reach the second-floor cabinet through the stairwell, and pull out what was theirs.

"We can't," Silas said. It came out as fact, not argument. The heat reached them across thirty yards of street, pressing on her face like an open oven door.

"No," Okafor said. "The stairwell's the only way up and it's a chimney." He was watching the men with the sacks, counting them under his breath. "But look where they're standing."

Silas followed his gaze. Behind the hall, separated from it by ten feet of alley, stood the bindery—a low stone annex where the records were rebound, joined to the main building by a delivery passage. The fire had not yet found it. And the bindery had its own small door, its own stair, and—she had repaired the clock above that door herself, two winters ago, and remembered the layout the way she remembered every mechanism she'd ever touched—a connecting gallery along the second floor, the one the clerks used to wheel trolleys of ledgers between shelf and press.

"The gallery runs over the reading room," she said slowly. "Above the cabinets."

"Above the cabinet," Okafor said. "If it hasn't gone through the floor yet."

"If it has, we die in there, and Tam—" She stopped. She had counted the wrong heads. She turned.

Tam was not beside her.

For one vertical, endless second the world narrowed to a single screaming point—and then she saw him, ahead, threading between two barricade watchers with the particular silence he had taught himself in four months of hiding from men who wanted faces forgotten, and he was running—not away from the fire but toward it, toward the alley behind the bindery, small and fast and absolutely certain.

"TAM!"

He did not stop. Of course he did not stop. She ran.

* * *

She caught him at the bindery door, got a fist in his collar, and swung him around, and the look on his face stopped her hand before the slap she had never once given him.

"You knew," he said. His voice cracked down the middle. "You and Okafor, whispering all week—copying, copying—I can count, Silas, I helped you fold the pages. That's his book in there. That's *him*." His eyes were wet and blazing. "The man who signed the order is the man who came to our house. I remember his face. He told me to forget my mother's face and I remember *his*. Every night. Every night since the door." His fists were shaking at his sides. "You're going in there to carry out the names. My mother's name is in there. I'm coming."

Behind them the fire roared through the wall, and the heat came off the stones in waves, and Silas had exactly the length of one breath to decide what kind of person she was.

Okafor arrived at a dead run, wheezing, took in the scene, and said only: "The gallery floor will hold a child easier than either of us. And someone has to watch the door."

It was not consent. It was arithmetic. Silas hated him for it, and loved him, and knew he was right, which was worse than either.

"You stay behind me," she told Tam. "You touch nothing that burns. If I say run, you run and you do not look back and you do not wait for me. Say it back."

"If you say run, I run." His chin trembled and set. "Don't say it unless you mean it."

God help me, she thought, he negotiates like a clockmaker. She pushed open the bindery door into the dark and the heat.

* * *

Inside, the world was orange and roaring. The delivery passage to the main hall glowed at its far end like the mouth of a forge; along the bindery's inner wall, shelves of unbound signatures smoldered where embers had come through a broken vent. The stair to the gallery was stone and cool, and they took it two at a time, Tam's hand locked in hers so hard her fingers went white.

The gallery was a narrow iron-floored walkway above the reading room, and below them the reading room was hell.

Shelves burned in rows like streets on fire, flame climbing the spines of forty years of record-keeping, and the heat rose against Silas's face and peeled the tears from her eyes before they could fall. Somewhere below, a beam groaned and shifted. Through the smoke she could see the far wall, and against it—the cabinet. Oak. Brass-bound. Still standing in a pocket of relative dark, because the fire had not yet eaten its way around the reading room's perimeter to reach it.

And beneath the cabinet, wedging something into the gap between cabinet and floor—two of Havel's men, coats black with soot, working with a crowbar and a sack. They had come up the burning stairwell wrapped in wet blankets, and they meant to take the originals out through the flames and let the fire do the rest.

"There," Tam breathed. "There—that's where—"

"Hold this." Silas shoved her coat-wrapped bundle of tools into his arms and was already moving along the gallery, low, toward the point above the cabinet where the iron walkway ended at a maintenance hatch she remembered from her repair visit—the clerk's hoist, a simple platform and winch, built to lower heavy folios to the reading-room floor.

A hoist works in both directions.

The beam nearest the cabinet was burning low and steady. She could not go down. But she could drop things.

She unbolted the hatch. Below her, one of the men glanced up—saw nothing, or thought he did, in the rolling smoke. She tied off the winch rope, checked the hook the way she'd check any escapement, and then, hands shaking, began unhooking the counterweight.

"What are you doing?" Okafor had reached her, satchel open, copying by memory what he couldn't reach by hand.

"Gravity," Silas said, "is impartial," and released the brake.

Two hundred pounds of cast iron sang down the shaft and struck the cabinet's brass-bound top dead center. The sound was apocalyptic—a bell the size of a coffin—and the cabinet lurched, tipped, and went over sideways onto the two men with a crash that competed with the fire itself. The sack flew. Papers burst from it like startled birds, scattering across the burning floor, catching light one after another.

The men staggered up screaming curses upward into the smoke. One fired a pistol blind; the ball shrieked off the gallery rail a hand's width from Tam's head, and Silas felt the near miss pass through her own chest a moment later.

"Now," she gasped. "Down the stairs. Now now now—"

They went. Behind them the gallery was beginning to glow at its joints, the iron expanding, the bolts starting to sing their small high note of surrender—a note Silas knew professionally and had never expected to hear beneath her own feet.

At the bindery door, Okafor shoved the satchel at her: inside, six pages copied in a running scrawl, taken from memory of the transcription plus whatever fragments he'd salvaged from the wrecked cabinet's spilled contents that had blown into the alley—she saw the corner of an original page, water-stained and scorched at one edge, Havel's signature still legible. Tam clutched a second armful, pressed to his chest, his face gray with soot and streaked where the tears had cut channels.

They stumbled out into the alley air and stood bent double, coughing, while behind them the records hall settled into itself with a sound like the whole city exhaling.

Then Tam straightened. He was looking at the papers in his arms—at the topmost page—and his whole body had gone rigid.

"This one," he whispered. "This is the order. This is my house." His finger, trembling, rested on a name that was not his parents'. A witness. A neighbor who had seen too much, entered in Havel's hand, and beside it, in the same ink: *removed.*

Silas read it. Read the date. Read the address.

The address was the clockmakers' quarter. The address was two streets from her shop.

"Jem," she said.

The word fell into the roar of the burning hall, and somewhere across the city, in a cellar or a cart or a cell, her apprentice was sitting in the exact place where that entry said people were removed to—and the fire was taking the proof, and Havel's men were regrouping at the corners of the square, and the smoke column over the records hall could be seen, Silas realized with a cold dropping certainty, from everywhere. Including from the windows of anyone hunting the last copy of the ledger now running through the streets in the arms of a printer, a woman pretending to be a man, and a seven-year-old boy who had just identified the handwriting of the man who destroyed his family.

"Move," Okafor said quietly. "All of you. Walk. Don't run until the corner."

They walked. Behind them the dome of the observatory caught the firelight and held it, pale and patient, keeping its own time.
