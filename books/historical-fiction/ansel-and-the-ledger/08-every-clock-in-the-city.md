## 8. Every Clock in the City

They came for the shop on a Tuesday, which Silas would remember afterward as the only mercy of it—Tuesdays were quiet days in the clockmakers' quarter, when the street ran thin of witnesses and thick of smoke.

She watched from Okafor's upstairs window, a hand pressed to Tam's shoulder, as four men in Havel's gray livery nailed the proclamation to her own door. The paper was large enough to read across the square. *Silas Montrose, clockmaker, deceased in the riots of the fourteenth. Premises seized by order of the Director of the Observatory.* Beneath the official hand, someone had already chalked a crude star—the mark the mobs left on houses they had emptied.

"They've killed me," she said. It came out almost amused, and that frightened her more than the soldiers had. "Officially. I'm dead, Tam."

Tam, seven years old and solemn as a pendulum, studied the door below. "Do I have to be dead too?"

"No." She crouched to his height. "You were never born, as far as any record says. That's what we're going to make them sorry for."

* * *

The tower had been hers once, in the way a borrowed coat is yours. Her father had kept the master clock of the Observatory of Saint-Aubin for thirty years, climbing its two hundred steps twice a week until his lungs gave out, and after him Silas had climbed them in his coat, his name, his life. Now she climbed them again with Tam's small hand in hers and the ledger wrapped in oilcloth against her chest like a second heartbeat, and this time no one had summoned her. This time she was a ghost entering her own house.

The city burned below in patches. The archive fires had spread to the customs house, then the granary district; each night the horizon glowed like a forge that would not cool. From the belfry gallery you could see the whole slow ruin—the boulevards choked with barricades, the printing-house where Okafor worked standing dark behind its boarded windows, the square where, if Okafor's plan held, a crowd would gather at noon tomorrow to hear a dead man read aloud.

"A dead man," she murmured, checking the great case for signs of entry. The lock showed fresh scratches. Her heart dropped before her eyes did—and then found what the scratches meant. Someone had been here. Someone had failed to get in. The lock was hers alone; she had cut the key herself two winters past, when she first suspected the clock was being watched.

Inside, the sabotage was worse than she'd left it.

She had known, since the night she found the second movement hidden in the case—a secret train of wheels geared to run the public dial minutes slow, so that every appointment, every alibi, every timestamped order in Havel's ledger could be made or unmade by whoever wound the hidden spring. She had corrected it once, quietly, and told herself correction was enough. But now the mechanism had been gutted deliberately: the escapement's pallets filed blunt, the minute wheel's teeth snapped like pulled teeth, the great drive weight's cable half-sawed through so that it hung by strands, trembling, ready to drop through three floors of gearing and take the whole movement into ruin.

"He wants it stopped," she said. "Not broken. Stopped. A dead clock can't testify."

Tam sat on the maintenance step where generations of Montroses had sat, swinging his feet, holding the lamp. "Can you fix it?"

"I can fix anything," she said, and heard her father's voice under her own, and for one moment let herself believe it.

* * *

Working by lamplight in the tower, she learned the shape of the plan the way you learn a tune: hands first, then harmony.

Okafor had come to her three nights ago, smelling of ink and fear, with the last copies of the ledger's pages sewn into the false binding of a prayer book. *Tomorrow noon,* he'd said. *The printing-house steps. They expect a mob speech—they always come for mob speeches, to count faces. So we give them a reading instead. Every page. Names, dates, payments. And every man who hears it will check his own watch, because a man checks his watch when a stranger names the hour.*

"And their watches are wrong," Silas had said slowly, understanding blooming like blood in water. "All of them. Because Havel slowed the city."

"Every church bell rings from your master clock. Every public dial. If the clocks say noon at noon—" Okafor had smiled the grim smile of a man setting type for his own arrest— "then when I stand up and say *at this hour, on this date,* the city itself will swear to it. You can't burn every bell in Paris, Silas. He knows that. It's why he killed the clock instead."

Time itself as witness. Not a pamphlet, which burns. Not a testimony, which a tribunal can dismiss. But noon—noon arriving everywhere at once, indifferent and undeniable, while a printer's voice laid the ledger against it like a plate against proof.

That was the bargain she had to build with brass and steel before dawn.

* * *

The escapement took her until midnight. She had spare pallets in her father's old kit—of course she did; he had taught her that the first law of repair is suspicion, and suspicion means spares—but fitting them blind, upside down, hanging from the great movement like a fly on a wheel, her arms burning, the lamp guttering in Tam's cupped hands—this was craft at its most prayer-like. Twice she wept without stopping work. Once she laughed.

The minute wheel she could not replace; she had to file new teeth, six of them, from a blank of hard brass she happened to be carrying for a client's repeater. Each tooth took forty minutes of patient cutting, testing, filing again. Tam learned to hold the wheel steady between two blocks of wood, and she let him, because his hands were small and sure and because there was no longer any sense pretending the boy would be safer ignorant.

"My papa fixed watches," Tam said, somewhere past the fourth tooth. His voice was flat the way children's voices go flat when they've decided not to cry about something. "Before Havel took him."

Silas set down her file. In the lamplight his face was all shadows and one bright eye, and she thought: *forget his face.* The note had said forget his face, and she never had, and neither, she understood suddenly, had he—not his parents' faces, not Havel's face. That was why Havel wanted a child with no record. A child who remembered was a loose gear in the machine.

"Tomorrow," she said carefully, "when the man reads the names, your mama and papa will be on those pages. Written down. Forever."

"Will Havel be there?"

"Yes."

Tam nodded once, the nod of a man much older than seven, and held the wheel steadier.

* * *

At four in the morning, the sawed weight cable chose its moment. She heard it before she understood it—a high singing whine, wire parting strand by strand above her head—and she shouted and shoved Tam off the platform and threw herself flat as three hundred pounds of iron let go. It missed her by an arm's length, punched through the floor boards, tore through the strike train below with a sound like a cathedral clearing its throat, and came to rest in the dark of the weights' shaft with a boom that rolled out over the sleeping quarter like a funeral bell.

Then silence. Then, distantly, dogs.

Tam's face appeared over hers, white in the lamplight. "Did they hear?"

"Everyone heard." She lay on her back among the brass dust, heart slamming, and began, absurdly, to laugh. Because beneath the wreck she could see it: the strike train had taken the blow, absorbed it, died for it—and the going train, the train that moved the hands, hung untouched above her, escapement fitted, minute wheel complete, waiting only for power.

"The bells are ruined," Tam said.

"The bells don't need to ring." She sat up, and the plan finished assembling itself in her mind with the clean click of a detent engaging. "The bells need to be *silent*. Don't you see? Tomorrow at noon, every church in the city fails to strike. And everyone in the square will look up at the observatory dial and see that it reads twelve exactly, and understand that the silence isn't neglect—it's *correction*. The city has been living on a lie about the time, and tomorrow the lie ends, loudly, by not making a sound."

It was madness. It was also the most beautiful thing she had ever built, and she had built it out of sabotage and wreckage and a sawn cable.

* * *

Dawn came up gray and smoke-sweet. Silas stood the great weight back on its new cable—she had spliced it herself, double-braided, tested with her whole weight and Tam's besides—and wound the barrel with the long crank, counting turns the way her father had taught her, feeling the mainspring take its charge like a lung filling.

Below, the city woke to its last wrong day. Bells rang eleven o'clock at twenty past. Watchmen called hours that had never been measured. Somewhere in the Observatory, Director Havel was winding his little hidden movement, easing the lie along another fraction, certain that the clockmaker whose shop bore his seal of seizure was ash in a common grave.

Let him wind it, she thought. Let him feed the lie its breakfast. By noon it would be starving in front of the whole city.

She wiped her hands on her coat—her father's coat, her coat—and looked down at the square through the dial aperture, where Okafor's boys were already stacking crates into a platform on the printing-house steps, where the crowd would come because crowds always come, where Havel's gray-coated men would line the edges because they always do, counting faces.

Counting faces, she thought, and put her hand on Tam's head. They should count better.

"Tam," she said. "When the hands touch twelve—whatever happens—look at the dial. Remember that it told the truth. Everything else, we'll write down after."

He nodded. Far below, a drum began to beat, gathering the square like a tide gathering against a seawall.

Silas set her shoulder against the winding crank for the last turn, and waited for noon to come find them.
