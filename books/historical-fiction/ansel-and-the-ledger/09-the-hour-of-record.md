## 9. The Hour of Record

I had not slept. No one had slept, I think, in that last hour before noon—the whole city holding its breath the way a mainspring holds its power, coiled, trembling, waiting for the escapement to let it go.

From the belfry window I could see everything: the printing-house steps where Okafor stood with the ledger wrapped in oilcloth against his chest, the square filling beyond all sense or safety, the rooftops black with watchers, the observatory rising behind it all like a head bowed over its own secrets. Below me, in the tower's throat, the master clock ticked with a voice I had restored note by note—every wheel true, every tooth filed to my father's standard, every second honest.

Havel had slowed it once so that the city would drift, minute by minute, out of agreement with itself. A city that cannot agree on the time cannot agree on anything else. That had been his genius, and his undoing: he taught me that time could be governed, and therefore that it could be taken back.

"Is it right?" Tam whispered beside me. He was pressed to the stone sill, only his eyes above it, and his hands were shaking.

"Listen," I said.

The church bells began—not one, but all of them, Saint Aubin first, then the others catching fire across the districts like lamps lit one from another, each striking toward the same instant because every clock in the city now kept the same truth. And beneath the bells, if you knew how to hear it, the master clock itself: steady, unslowed, unforgiving.

Twelve strokes. The crowd counted them aloud, thousands of voices, and on the twelfth Okafor unwrapped the ledger and lifted it high, and his voice went out over the square through a speaking-trumpet, cracked and enormous.

"Citizens! Before your clocks strike another hour, hear what was written while you slept!"

My stomach dropped through the floor of the tower.

He read the names first. That had been our decision, mine and his, made over three nights of copying pages by tallow light while the archive burned above us: names before numbers, faces before figures. *Ansel Brue. Marta Ilves. The Voss family, four souls. The child Tamasin, delivered to the clockmaker's door, whose parents were—*

Tam's hand found mine and gripped it hard enough to hurt.

Below, Havel's men were moving through the crowd. I could pick them out by their gray coats and the way they walked—shoulders squared, hands near their belts, men accustomed to being feared in small rooms now adrift in a multitude that had stopped being small. They were trying to reach the steps. The crowd would not part for them. It parted for no one; there was no room left to part into.

"They're going to take him," Tam said.

"Yes."

"Then we should be down there."

"No." I turned him by the shoulder to face me. In the bell-light his face looked older than seven, older than any child's face ought to look. "You wanted to come up here. You wanted to see the clock run true when they read it. So watch, and remember, and when it is your turn—" My voice caught on the word *turn*, because it was a turn of a wheel, a turn of a page, and I had built my whole life on turns I could control, and this one I could not.

"When it's my turn," he said steadily, "I name them."

"You name them."

He nodded once, the way I nodded when I seated a balance spring: precise, final, done.

Okafor read on. Payments to magistrates. Dates of arrests that had never been recorded anywhere else. Orders signed *H.* And then the disappearances, each one a line, each line a life folded flat into an entry—and the crowd below had gone silent in a way I had never heard a crowd go silent, ten thousand people listening the way one person listens, and the silence itself was louder than the bells had been.

A whistle shrieked from the steps. Gray coats pressing forward. Someone threw a stone—I saw it arc—and did not land, swallowed by the press of bodies, and Okafor did not stop reading. He held the ledger higher, turning so that all sides of the square could see the open pages, and shouted the next entry directly into the whistle's scream.

And then, at the edge of the square, a carriage. Black lacquer, matched grays, and I knew it the way you know a movement by its finishing—even at this distance, even upside down in my memory of every machine Havel had ever put into the world.

He had come to stop it himself.

* * *

Havel crossed the square on foot, his men clearing a wedge before him, and I watched through the tower glass as the crowd bent around him like water around a keel—not yielding, exactly. Measuring him. He reached the base of the printing-house steps and stood looking up at Okafor with an expression I will carry to my own grave: not rage. Arithmetic. He was calculating whether the crowd was still a thing that could be corrected, and I watched the calculation fail behind his eyes.

He climbed three steps anyway. He raised one hand, and somewhere a pistol cocked, and the whole square flinched like a single animal—

—and Tam stepped past me to the window.

Not down the stairs. Not into danger. To the window, the open belfry window, seventy feet above the square, and before I could seize his collar he filled his lungs and shouted with everything a small body has:

"I KNOW THEIR FACES!"

The sound dropped into the silence like a gear into its train. Ten thousand faces turned upward.

"My mother had a scar on her left hand, here—" he pressed his own small palm, "—from a loom. My father whistled through his teeth when he was happy. They were taken on the ninth of March by men in gray coats, and Director Havel signed the order, and I am alive because a clockmaker kept me, and I REMEMBER THEM!"

His voice broke on the last word and the breaking was the most terrible and perfect sound I have ever heard, because it was a child's voice and the whole city heard it break and did not look away.

Silence again. Then Okafor, from the steps, quiet as a man closing a ledger:

"The record stands confirmed by witness."

The crowd did not surge. That was the astonishing thing—that they did not fall on him, did not give him the violence he had always traded in. They simply closed. The gray coats found themselves walled inside a mass of people who had stopped being afraid, and fear had been the whole of Havel's currency, and I watched it devalue in real time, watch his men set down their pistols one by one not because anyone ordered it but because holding them suddenly meant nothing. Havel stood alone on the third step with his hand still half-raised, commanding a gesture no one obeyed, and the arithmetic finally finished behind his eyes and showed him the sum: nothing. Nothing carried forward. Nothing owed.

Someone began the names. Just one voice, low, from somewhere near the fountain—*Ansel Brue*—and another took up the next, and another, ten thousand voices passing the ledger's pages mouth to mouth until the square itself was reading, the city reciting its own stolen dead back into existence, and Havel descended the steps into that river of names and the crowd absorbed him without hatred, which was worse than any rope. They made him walk through the memory of everyone he had erased.

In the tower, the master clock struck the quarter hour, clean and true, and Tam slid down against the wall and put his face in his hands and shook.

I knelt and gathered him in. He was sobbing without sound, the way children cry when they have learned crying must be hidden, and I held his head against my shoulder and felt my own tears fall into his hair.

"You named them," I said. "They're written now in ten thousand memories. There is no power in this city large enough to erase that."

"They're gone," he managed. "Remembering doesn't bring them back."

"No," I said. "It does something harder. It keeps the world from pretending they were never here." I tipped his chin up. "Your father whistled through his teeth?"

"When he was happy." A wet, astonished almost-laugh. "How did you know I'd say it right? How did you know I'd be able to do it?"

"Because I've watched you wind a clock, Tam. You don't flinch from what's inside the case."

Far below, the names went on rolling through the square like a tide that would not ebb. Somewhere in it, Okafor was laughing—I could hear the shape of it through the trumpet. Somewhere in it, Jem, freed two days past, was surely standing on the printing-house steps with ink-stained fists raised. And somewhere in the crowd, a man in a fine coat who had owned the city's time for eleven years was learning what it weighed.

Tam wiped his face with both sleeves and looked up at me, red-eyed, resolute.

"What happens now?" he asked.

I looked out at the square—at the smoke still hanging over the burned archive, at the gray coats disarmed among their neighbors, at the ledger open in Okafor's hands with the sun full upon it—and I understood that we had won the hour but not the peace, that a city which remembers can also be made to forget again, that Havel had friends who had not been standing on those steps.

"Now," I said, taking his hand, "we go down and make sure it gets printed before nightfall. Every page. Every copy we can make." I pulled him toward the stair. "Because tomorrow, someone will try to say none of this happened—and we are going to be very, very difficult to contradict."
