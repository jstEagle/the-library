## 10. What Can Be Carried

The observatory had new windows.

That was the first thing Silas noticed from the foot of the hill, standing where the tram stopped and where she had once climbed these cobblestones with a toolbox in her hand and a lie on her lips. The old panes—cracked, some of them blown out during the riots, patched for years with oilcloth and hope—had been replaced with glass so clear it seemed the tower had been given back its eyes. Through them she could see the great dial, freshly gilded, its hands at rest until the ceremony began.

Eleven years. Long enough for a city to grow a new skin over its scars. Long enough for a boy of seven to become a young man with his father's jaw and his mother's patience. Long enough, she thought, for grief to stop being an emergency and become something you carried, the way you carried a tool you used often—the weight of it familiar, worn smooth by handling.

"You're staring," Tam said beside her. He was taller than her now, and he said it gently, the way he said everything, as if the world were a mechanism that deserved courtesy.

"I'm counting," she said. "Old habit. I count everything I almost lost."

He didn't laugh. He never laughed at that joke anymore; he had decided somewhere in his adolescence that it wasn't a joke, and he was right, and she loved him for it.

They walked up together through a crowd that parted without being asked. That was another thing eleven years had done: people knew her face now. Not *Silas Montrose, clockmaker*—or rather yes, exactly that, but with no asterisk attached, no footnote of scandal trailing after the name like a frayed thread. There had been a season, after the reading in the square, when every pamphlet and gazette had printed her story with relish: *the woman who kept her father's shop, the ledger hidden in the clock, the child who remembered.* For a year she had been a character instead of a person. Then the city's attention had moved on, as attention does, and what remained was quieter and better: a name spoken without surprise. A woman who repaired watches, who taught apprentices, who came to civic functions because she was invited and not because she was an exhibit.

At the tower door, an official in a gray coat checked a list, found her name—her true name, printed plainly—and bowed slightly. "Madame Montrose. They're waiting for you."

"Then they've improved their punctuality since I worked here," she said, and the official smiled uncertainly, not knowing whether the history between this building and this woman permitted jokes. She let him wonder.

Inside, the stairwell smelled of fresh plaster and old stone, the two smells layered like pages pressed together. She climbed with her hand on the rail, counting steps out of habit—one hundred and forty, she had never forgotten—and Tam matched her pace, carrying the case.

It was a plain case. She had insisted on that. Okafor had offered to have it made of walnut with brass corners, with an engraved plate; the archive committee had suggested velvet lining and a lock with three tumblers. Silas had refused all of it. The ledger had spent nine years wrapped in oilcloth inside a false panel of a grandfather clock, and before that it had spent one terrible night against her ribs while men with lanterns searched her shop, and it did not need ornament now. It needed only to be legible, and safe, and signed.

*Signed.* Her thumb found the edge of the case through her coat sleeve. The last page, the page she had written herself in the clock tower while Havel's men hammered on the street doors below—the attestation, dated the morning of the unified noon, witnessed by Okafor and by a bell-ringer named Ansel whose name had ended up in the book's title more by accident than design—bore her signature twice. Once as *Silas Montrose*. And beneath it, smaller, in ink she had mixed herself because she would not use Havel's stock even then: *Silas Sandoval Montrose, daughter of Edouard Sandoval, clockmaker.*

She had put her father's name first. She had put her mother's maiden name in the middle, resurrecting a woman the parish registers had half-erased. It was the only forgery she had ever committed that was also the truth.

* * *

The clock chamber had been scrubbed and whitewashed, and it was full of people: aldermen, archivists, the director's successor—a nervous, decent man named Pell who had personally come to her shop twice to apologize on behalf of an institution, as though institutions could apologize, as though apology were a gear ratio you could set and forget. Okafor was there, grayer now, thicker through the shoulders, his printing house grown into a press that employed forty hands. He caught her eye across the room and touched two fingers to his brow—the old printer's salute, ink to forehead—and she returned it.

The master clock stood restored at the chamber's center, its case opened for the occasion so the movement showed: wheels, escapement, pendulum, all of it cleaned and true. Silas looked at it the way a surgeon looks at a healed wound—with professional satisfaction and a private inventory of everything that had once gone wrong there. She could still see, if she let herself, the second movement nested inside the case, the secret train of gears Havel had installed to slow the public clock, bending the city's time a few seconds each day so that appointments slipped, documents expired, witnesses arrived late. A man who stole time in increments small enough that no one noticed. It had taken her three days of work to find it. It had taken the rest of her life to understand it.

Pell mounted the little platform and spoke about restoration, about transparency, about a new age of records open to any citizen. His voice was good and his intentions, she believed, were real. But Silas watched the crowd instead of listening, and she saw what Pell perhaps did not: that half the room had come for the spectacle and half had come for the ledger, and only a handful—for whom Okafor's pamphlets had been bread in a famine year—had come because they understood what a record was. What it cost. Who carried it.

Tam stepped forward when Pell called his name. "Tamasin Sandoval Montrose," Pell read from his card, and Silas felt her breath catch—not at her own name appended to his, but at the fact that he had chosen, at fourteen, to take it formally, signing the adoption papers with a steady hand while she wept into a rag in the kitchen like an unseemly fool.

He unfolded the dedication. It was short; they had agreed it should be. He had a good public voice, low and even, trained by three years of reading aloud at the printing house on evenings when the typesetters worked late.

"On this day," he read, "the Ledger of the Observatory is entered into the Civic Archive of this city, to be kept openly, to be copied freely, and to be surrendered to no authority, elected or otherwise. It records debts, payments, orders, and disappearances compiled under Director Aurel Havel between the years — and —. It was recovered and preserved at risk of life by Silas Montrose, clockmaker, who writes on its final page:" 

He paused. He did not need the paper for what came next. He had asked her, months ago, if he might memorize it, and she had said yes, and she knew why he wanted to: because a thing read from memory cannot be confiscated.

"*I was taught that time belongs to those who keep the clocks,*" Tam recited. "*But time belongs to everyone the clocks are honest for. Let this book be the clock that does not lie.*"

He folded the paper. The room was silent for a moment, and then it wasn't—it was applause and shuffling and Pell saying something ceremonial—but Silas heard none of it clearly, because Tam had turned from the platform and looked at her across the chamber, and neither of them needed sound.

* * *

Afterward, in the archive's long reading room, the ledger was placed in its case under glass, opened to her final page. An archivist—a young woman with ink-stained fingers, the kind of person Okafor called *the future wearing cardigans*—entered the record in the register while Silas watched over her shoulder.

"Name of depositor?" the archivist asked, pen poised, though she knew the answer; it was a formality, but formalities were the bones of an archive and Silas respected them.

"Silas Sandoval Montrose," she said. "Clockmaker. Daughter of Edouard."

"And relation to the document?"

Silas considered the question properly, which surprised the archivist, who had expected a quick answer. Relation. Debtor? Witness? Author of the last page? Survivor?

"Custodian," she said at last. "For a while. Now it keeps itself."

The archivist wrote it down. The nib scratched, a small honest sound, and Silas thought: this is how it survives. Not in marble, not in monuments—monuments get pulled down, she had seen it, she had helped pull one down—but in the scratch of a nib, in a copy made by a stranger who thinks it matters, in a boy who memorizes your words so they can't be burned out of you. Memory outlives power not because memory is strong but because it is *many*, and power, however cruel, is always only a few.

Okafor joined them at the case, hands clasped behind his back, reading his own handwriting from nine years ago with the expression of a man rereading a letter from a younger self. "You know what I keep thinking," he said. "Havel died comfortable. Pensioned off to his cousin's estate before the trials, dead of a heart complaint in 'fifty-three, buried with honors." He shook his head slowly. "The ledger didn't ruin him. It ruined the *lie*. And the lie was the thing with power."

"That's enough," Silas said.

"It's everything," Okafor said. "A man can die warm. A lie can't hide warm. That's the whole trade, Marguerite—" he caught himself, grinned, used the wrong name deliberately as he had done for years, their private joke about how many names a person could carry—"that's the whole trade. You don't print justice. You print the record. Justice has to walk to meet it on its own legs."

Tam appeared at her elbow with two cups of the thin ceremonial coffee and handed her one. Twenty minutes past four by the archive's wall clock—which, she noted with proprietary pleasure, ran three seconds fast, and she resolved then and there to offer the city her services gratis, because a building that held the truth ought at least to hold it *on time*.

"The apprentice question," Tam said.

"We don't have an apprentice question."

"We do. Jem's daughter wrote again. She wants to start in the spring."

Jem. Eleven years gone now, dead of the fever the winter after his release, before Silas had finished rebuilding the shop, before she could give him back anything for the month he'd spent in Havel's cells wearing her arrest warrant. She still kept Jem's first burnisher on the bench where he'd left it, handles worn to the shape of his grip. Some tools you didn't regrind. Some weights you carried.

"She's sixteen," Tam went on. "She sent drawings with the letter. Escapement designs. They're rough, but she sees the problem." He paused, and then added, in the careful voice he used when he already knew the answer and was giving her the dignity of arriving at it herself: "Her name is Constance Jemima Achebe. She signs her full name. All of it. Every letter."

Silas drank her coffee and looked up at the wall clock, ticking away its three dishonest seconds, and thought of a girl who refused to shorten herself even on an envelope.

"Tell her spring is late this year," Silas said. "Tell her she starts in March."

Outside the tall windows, the light was going gold over the rooftops, and the observatory clock above them gathered itself, as it did every quarter hour, to tell the whole listening city exactly, honestly, what time it was.
