## 1. The Boy Called Ansel

The coach left him at the foot of the hill an hour before dawn, and Paloma Fairweather climbed the last mile to Thornfield Observatory with his forged name folded against his chest like a second heartbeat.

*Ansel Voss, apprentice clockmaker, aged seventeen.* The papers were good work—he had paid a forger in Millbrook three months' wages for them, and the man had earned every coin. The ink had the right age to it. The seal was convincing. Only the name was false, and only he knew how false.

He said it over to himself as he walked, testing it the way one tests a floorboard before trusting it with weight. *Ansel. Ansel Voss.* His mouth had learned the shape of it weeks ago, in his rented room above the chandler's shop, saying it aloud until it stopped tasting of theft. It still tasted of theft.

Thornfield rose out of the morning fog the way cathedrals rise—suddenly, and all at once, and with the implication that you were smaller than you'd thought. The observatory dome caught the first grey light and held it, a dull silver eye staring down at the valley. Below it sprawled the house itself: three wings of honey-coloured stone, a clock tower rising from the central block like a raised finger. And in that tower, behind louvred shutters, hung the great master clock—the regulator that kept time not only for the Ashcombe household but, by telegraph wire and railway schedule, for half the county.

They had sent for him because the master clock was dying.

The summons had come by letter, unsigned except by a secretary's hand: *The regulator at Thornfield requires attention. Present your credentials at the gatehouse. You will lodge in the servants' wing. Discretion expected.*

Discretion expected. He had read that phrase a dozen times on the journey north, turning it over like a coin of uncertain mint. It might mean nothing. Great houses were jealous of their privacy; a broken clock in a clock tower was an embarrassment, and embarrassment was a thing the rich buried quietly. Or it might mean exactly what it said—that whatever happened inside those walls was to stay inside them, and a boy with false papers was in no position to argue.

At the gatehouse, a sergeant with a moustache like a wet brush took his papers, read them twice, and wrote his name in a ledger with the deliberation of a man carving a tombstone.

"Voss," the sergeant said. "Ansel Voss."

"Yes, sir."

"The housekeeper will take you up. Touch nothing but what you're sent to touch." He handed back the papers and looked at Paloma the way men look at luggage—assessing its worth, and whether it would be trouble.

"I'm here for the clock," Paloma said. "Only the clock."

"See that it stays that way."

A maid walked him up the gravel drive through gardens still sleeping under frost, past statuary wearing caps of white, and into the servants' entrance on the north side, where the air changed at once—from stone and silence to bread and tallow and the low constant murmur of a household waking. Here was a different country, he understood immediately, with its own borders and its own laws. He catalogued it the way his master had taught him to catalogue a mechanism: note every part, note how each part moves, note where the strain falls.

The housekeeper, Mrs. Redgrave, was a narrow woman with spectacles that made her eyes look twice their size and half again as cold. She interviewed him standing, in a passage too narrow for either of them to escape.

"You'll sleep in the attic room at the end of the east passage. Meals in the servants' hall, six o'clock and one o'clock, no exceptions. You work in the tower and nowhere else. Mr. Ashcombe does not wish to be troubled."

"No, madam."

"And Voss—" she paused, and the spectacles caught the lamplight so he couldn't see her eyes at all, "—the master clock has been unreliable since spring. Mr. Ashcombe is not a patient man about unreliability. Do you understand me?"

"I understand that I should mend it quickly."

Something moved behind the spectacles—not warmth, exactly, but the ghost of it. "Hmph. At least you're direct." She turned away. "Tibbs will show you the tower."

Tibbs was the under-butler, a soft-footed man of perhaps fifty who spoke mostly in sighs. On the stairs up to the clock room he offered, unprompted, a survey of the household's weather: "Mr. Ashcombe takes breakfast alone and is best avoided until noon. Mrs. Ashcombe takes hers in her room and is best avoided always. The magistrate dines Thursday. And the cook, Mrs. Halloran, rules the kitchen with a wooden spoon and a memory like the Book of Judges—cross her once and she'll remember it at your funeral."

"And who," Paloma asked, "is it wise to know?"

Tibbs considered this with the gravity of a bishop weighing doctrine. "Maren," he said finally. "Cook's assistant. If you want anything done below stairs—a parcel held, a message carried, a door unlocked—it goes through Maren. Not because she asks. Because people trust her, and she's never once given them reason not to." He sighed. "Which in this house is rarer than honest money."

* * *

The clock room smelled of oil and dust and old brass, and Paloma fell in love with it in spite of everything.

The great regulator stood nine feet tall in its case of black walnut, a monument to some dead craftsman's ambition. Its pendulum swung behind a glass door with the patience of something that had outlived everyone who cared about it. But when Paloma opened the case and put his ear to the movement, he heard what he had been sent to hear: a hitch in the strike train, a hesitation between the fourth and fifth strokes, like a speaker stumbling over a word.

He worked through the morning, sleeves rolled, hands black with oil, while the household went about its business somewhere far below. This was the only place in the world where he was not afraid. Inside a clock there was no Ansel Voss and no Paloma Fairweather; there was only the work, and the work did not care what name you gave it. He found the fault by noon—a worn pallet stone in the escapement, letting the escape wheel slip a fraction with each swing, stealing seconds the way a dishonest tradesman steals weights. He could true it, but not today. Today he would clean, and measure, and make ready.

And it was while he was measuring, flat on his back inside the case with a lamp balanced on his chest, that he noticed the gap.

Behind the strike train, where the plates met the backboard, there was a space that shouldn't have been there—a shadow where solid wood ought to be. He ran his fingers along it. A panel, loose, cut to fit flush. His heart began its own quick escapement, tick-tick-tick against his ribs.

He told himself he would leave it. He was there for the clock. Only the clock. Discretion expected.

But he was seventeen years old and curious, and curiosity is a mainspring that winds itself.

He pressed the panel. It gave, silently, hinged on the far side—and behind it lay a cavity perhaps eight inches deep, lined with felt gone grey with age. In the cavity sat a letter.

Sealed. Addressed to no one. The wax bore an impression he didn't recognize—a crest, a motto he couldn't read in the lamplight, pressed deep and deliberate.

Paloma lay very still inside the great clock, listening to the pendulum swing above him, counting the seconds it took to decide. Then he slid the letter back, closed the panel, and spent the rest of the afternoon truing the pallet stone with hands that were not quite steady.

He did not open it. Not that day. He told himself that was prudence. He knew, even then, that it was only delay.

* * *

The servants' hall at six o'clock was a long table lit by two lamps, and Paloma entered it the way he entered everything now—with a catalogue running silently behind his eyes. Exits: the service stair behind him, the scullery door to the yard, the coal chute in the corner that a thin boy might squeeze through if the day ever came. Allies: unknown. Threats: the footman named Dree, who watched newcomers the way cats watch birds.

He took the empty seat at the far end, near the girl Tibbs had named.

Maren was perhaps nineteen, broad-shouldered, with flour to her elbows and a smudge of it along her jaw like a scar of honour. She was arguing cheerfully with the under-cook about whether suet could be salvaged, and she won, and she did it without raising her voice, which told him more about her than any introduction could have.

She looked up when he sat down. "You're the clock boy."

"Ansel Voss."

"Maren Sorel." She pushed the bread basket toward him without being asked. "You've got oil in your hair."

"I've got oil everywhere. It's the occupational condition."

That earned him half a smile. "How's the old beast? The tower clock. Cook says it's been striking wrong since spring and Mr. Ashcombe blames the weather, and God, the government, and the previous clockmaker, in that order."

"It's the escapement. A worn stone. It'll be right in a day or two."

"Mm." She studied him with frank brown eyes, the kind of look that weighed and did not flatter. "You don't talk much for someone who just told me his whole trade in four words."

"I find most conversations are safer observed than joined."

Something flickered across her face at that—recognition, almost, though of what he couldn't say. "Careful with that philosophy here," she said, quieter. "People who observe too well get noticed for it." And then, louder, lighter, passing him the butter: "Eat. You're too thin for tower work."

Later, crossing the yard to the woodshed where he'd been given bench space, he found a boy of twelve or thirteen sitting on the mounting block in the dark, whittling a stick with intense concentration.

"You're the clock boy," the boy said, without looking up. "Ivo. Stable. Everyone says 'clock boy,' you'll never shake it."

"Better than some names."

Ivo glanced up at that, sharp-eyed, and grinned. "You want to see something?" He led Paloma around the stable's end and pointed up. Above them, the observatory dome was open to the sky, and the great telescope within caught starlight on its lens. "Clear nights, they let me sit and watch the astronomer work. He showed me Saturn once. Through the glass it's got rings. Rings, like a wedding band." He said it with the reverence other boys reserved for prizefights. "I'm going to look through a proper telescope someday. A big one. In a city."

"Saturn keeps better time than most clocks," Paloma said. "Its moons are like watch hands. You can set a chronometer by them."

Ivo turned and stared at him. "You're making that up."

"I promise you I'm not. Galileo timed them himself."

The boy's grin split his face. "Right. You're all right, clock boy." He held out the whittled stick—a rough figure, half-formed, maybe a horse, maybe a dog. "For your windowsill. Attic rooms need things on the sills or they eat you."

Paloma took it. It was nothing—a stick, a knife, ten minutes of a child's boredom—and it sat in his palm warm from the boy's hand, and he found he could not put it down.

* * *

That night, in the attic room with the whittled horse-dog on the sill and the moon coming through the eaves, Paloma lay awake and took inventory, as he did every night, of everything that could hang him.

The papers were good but not perfect. The sergeant at the gatehouse had written *Voss* in a ledger that any magistrate could read. Mrs. Redgrave's spectacles had missed nothing. And somewhere below him, behind a panel in a clock case, a sealed letter waited with a crest on its wax that he had already begun, helplessly, to try to identify.

He had come to Thornfield to be invisible. Three days' work, wages, a reference, and gone. That was the whole design.

But he thought of Maren pushing the bread basket without being asked. Of Ivo's grin under the stars. Of Tibbs sighing, *people trust her, and she's never once given them reason not to.* Threads, fine as hairsprings, already catching on him. He had spent two years learning that attachment was a liability—that every person who knew your face was a person who could describe it to the wrong man. And yet.

Yet the hall had been warm. Yet the boy had given him a gift.

The moon crossed the skylight. Down in the tower, the master clock struck eleven—one stroke late, still limping on its worn stone, faithful and faulty—and the sound came up through the bones of the house like a pulse.

Paloma Fairweather, called Ansel, closed his eyes and did not sleep.

Behind the strike train, the letter waited. And some part of him, the part that had already memorized the feel of the wax seal under his thumb, knew he would not be able to leave it there.
