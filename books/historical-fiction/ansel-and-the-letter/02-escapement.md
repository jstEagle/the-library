## 2. Escapement

The master clock of Thornfield Observatory stood eleven feet tall in its case of black walnut, and it hated being opened.

Paloma Fairweather had learned this the way he learned everything—by listening with his hands. The case door swung on hinges that had been oiled by someone who believed oil was a moral failing; they shrieked like a scalded cat when he pulled them wide. Inside, the movement rose in tiers, brass and steel stacked like a cathedral's scaffolding: going train on the left, striking train on the right, the great barrel of the drive weight between them, thick as a rain cask. The air inside smelled of graphite and old machine grease and something else beneath it—dust that had settled in the dark for years, undisturbed, holding whatever secrets dust holds.

He worked by lamplight, a storm lantern set on the floor at an angle so its glow climbed the movement instead of spilling out under the observatory door. Mr. Quill, the head groundskeeper, had made it plain enough at breakfast that the clock-minder was furniture. Furniture did not need to be seen working after hours. Furniture certainly did not need to be seen reading anything.

*Keep your head down,* his father had told him, pressing the forged papers into his hands two winters ago in a room that smelled of tallow and fear. *Ansel is a name nobody looks at twice. Be Ansel all the way through.*

So Paloma had been Ansel for twenty-two months now, and he had gotten good at it the way a swimmer gets good at holding his breath. He knew exactly how long he could exist in a room before someone noticed him, and he spent himself carefully, never past that mark.

But the clock did not care about any of that. The clock only cared about truth—the mechanical kind, which is the most unforgiving kind there is.

---

The fault was in the escapement, or near enough to it that he'd spent the morning convincing himself otherwise. A good clockmaker learns to distrust his first diagnosis the way a judge distrusts a nervous witness. He'd cleaned the pallets, dressed the escape wheel teeth with a fine file, re-oiled the pivot holes with a needle thinner than an eyelash. And still, every ninety seconds, the tick went slightly wrong—not audibly wrong, not to anyone but him, but wrong in the *feel* of it, a hesitation like a man about to say something and deciding against it.

Which meant the trouble was upstream. Which meant the strike train.

The striking mechanism was the ugly cousin of any great clock—all hammers and levers and counting wheels, built to make noise rather than keep time—and it was wedged into the right-hand tier of the case in a space clearly designed by someone who had never had to put his arm inside it. Paloma shed his coat, rolled his sleeves to the shoulder, and reached in up to the bicep, cheek pressed against the cold walnut, fingers walking blind across the levers.

That was when he felt the paper.

At first he took it for a wad of packing cloth, the sort of thing a careless repairman might have shoved into a gap forty years ago and forgotten. It was wedged flat behind the strike-release lever, jammed into the angle where the lever's tail swept on its arc—a space no wider than two fingers. Someone had wanted it *there*, in the one place in the whole movement where a stray object would foul the release just often enough to make a clock run imperfectly, and would make anyone who came looking dig deep enough to find what they'd hidden.

Or perhaps, thought Paloma, fingers gone very still, someone had hidden it in a hurry and chosen the first dark hole they saw, and the clock's imperfection was only an accident.

Either way, the paper had been keeping time with the clock for years. Every hour, the lever had brushed past it. Every hour, the letter had heard the house count.

He worked it loose with two fingers, easing it out along the lever's arc as gently as if it were a splinter near an eye. It came free with a dry whisper. His hand emerged from the case holding a packet the size of a prayer book: paper folded twice over and sealed with wax, the wax stamped with a crest he recognized without wanting to—a river bend beneath three stars, pressed deep and clean.

The magistrate's seal. Magistrate Corvane himself.

There was no address. No direction at all, neither name nor title, as though the letter had been written to no one—as though it were a message waiting for whoever was fated to open the clock.

Paloma sat back on his heels on the case floor, the packet in both hands, and for a long moment he did nothing at all. The lantern threw his shadow huge against the inside of the case door, a giant hunched over a small white thing.

*Put it back.*

The thought arrived fully formed, sensible, almost gentle. Put it back behind the lever, let the fouling stand, report the escapement repaired and collect your wage, and go on being Ansel until Ansel was old and gray and no one remembered there had ever been a boy called Paloma Fairweather with ink-stained fingers and a father who'd taught him that a man's name was the one thing he carried that couldn't be replaced.

His hands did not move.

It was not curiosity. Curiosity was a luxury, and he had trained himself out of luxuries the way one trains out of a taste for sugar. It was simpler than that, and worse: the seal was already cracked. Not broken—cracked, a hairline running from the river bend to the edge of the wax, as if someone had once begun to break it and stopped. As if the letter had already been half-read by some other frightened person in some other year, and then sealed shut again with the guilt still warm in their hands.

Someone had known. Someone had held this exact fear and put it back.

Paloma broke the seal the rest of the way.

---

The hand inside was small and precise, a clerk's hand, the letters upright and evenly spaced, written fast but written well. There was no salutation. It began mid-argument, as though the writer had been talking to himself for pages before the paper started:

*—and therefore the surveys must agree with one another before the Michaelmas sessions, or Corvane will have no ground to stand on when the claims come before him. Ashcombe says the river itself will do half our work; a flood season moves boundaries better than any pen. But the pen must still do the rest.*

Paloma read it twice before the words finished arranging themselves into meaning, and even then meaning arrived the way cold water arrives—slowly, and then all at once, and then everywhere.

The river settlement. Harrow Bend, the place downriver where the poor farms clung to the floodplain like barnacles, where Maren's people came from—he'd heard her say it once, *Harrow Bend*, with a particular flatness she gave to nothing else. The Ashcombes had been buying the valley's patience for years, and here it was in black and white: surveys drawn twice, once honest and once convenient, the honest ones to disappear. Deeds to be rendered doubtful by a magistrate whose seal was on this very page. Land taken with ink instead of force, because ink left no bruises and force left witnesses.

And then, lower on the second page, in the same neat clerk's hand, a paragraph that stopped Paloma's breath in his throat:

*One further matter. The observatory clock required attention again this spring, and the household has taken on a new clock-minder, a young man presenting papers under the name Ansel. You will recall the boy was present at the Harrow Bend survey of '91—he was apprenticed to the surveyor's party that season and saw more than a child ought. He will remember nothing useful, I am assured; boys forget. But Ashcombe is not a man who trusts forgetting. When the matter concludes, see that the boy is moved along. Quietly. There are ways.*

Paloma read it a third time. Then a fourth. The words did not change. That was the horror of writing—it stayed exactly what it was while the world rearranged itself around it.

*The boy called Ansel.* Present at the survey. He had been thirteen that summer, carrying stakes and chaining line for old Surveyor Pell, and yes—he had seen things. A boundary stone moved in the night. Pell drinking hard afterward and saying, *stones don't walk, lad, somebody walks them.* He had forgotten it, mostly, the way you forget weather. He had made a career of forgetting it.

They had not forgotten that he'd seen.

*See that the boy is moved along. Quietly. There are ways.*

The lantern flame leaned in a draft that hadn't existed a moment ago. Paloma became aware of his own body with terrible clarity: his heartbeat, too fast; his hands, steady—clockmaker's hands, traitorously steady; the cold of the case floor seeping through his trousers. Somewhere above him, through floors of stone and timber, the household went about its evening. A door closed. Someone laughed—light, brief, unburdened. The safe sounds of a house that did not know what he was holding.

Except it did know. The house had always known. The house had kept the letter the way a body keeps a splinter—walled in, aching, waiting.

He looked at the movement looming over him, the strike train with its gap now empty, the escapement that had hesitated every ninety seconds like a conscience trying to speak. All those months he had tended this machine believing it merely a machine, believing his work innocent, believing invisibility was safety. And all those months the clock had been ticking over a secret with his own name buried in it—*not his name, worse, the false one, the one that could be erased without anyone asking questions*—and the ticking had sounded, to him, like shelter.

A clock does not lie. That was the first law his father ever taught him, before gears, before files: *the wheel either turns true or it doesn't, and it doesn't care what you wish.* Paloma understood suddenly that the same law applied to him. He could wind himself back behind the lever like the letter, could let the household's hour hand pass over him forever—but the mechanism of this place had already caught him. He was in the train now. Something was going to strike.

He refolded the pages along their original creases, precisely, the way he'd do it for a customer's treasured document. He did not reseal the wax; there was no making it whole, and pretending otherwise felt like a lie the clock would catch. Instead he turned the packet over in his hands and considered the practical question, the craftsman's question, the question that had saved him a hundred times in a trade where panic ruins work:

*Where does a thing go so it cannot be found?*

Not in his room—rooms were searched. Not on his person—persons were searched faster. It needed to be somewhere no one would look because no one would think to look, somewhere belonging to the house's ordinary machinery, invisible the way he had been invisible.

His eyes found the great weight barrel. Beneath it, bolted to the case floor, sat the counterweight channel—a lead-lined trough where the cable weights descended at winding, greased, grimy, touched by no hand but his. He wrapped the letter in a scrap of chamois, greased the wrapping lightly, and pressed it into the trough's blind end, where it vanished into shadow and smell and the house's own grime.

Then he sat back, wiped his hands on a rag that did nothing for the feeling in them, and made himself think.

Three days. That was the arithmetic forming at the edges of his mind whether he wanted it or not. If the letter spoke truly, the eviction was close—the Michaelmas sessions, the final claims. And *see that the boy is moved along* would not wait for a convenient season. Men who wrote sentences like that did not mean by Christmas.

He had no allies. He had a cook's assistant who saved him the ends of the plum loaf, and a stable boy who showed him card tricks badly, and a head groundskeeper who thought he was furniture. He had a route out of this house he'd mapped in his head since his first week—every servant's habit, every unlocked gate, every hour the lanes went dark—and he had never once imagined needing it.

The clock above him gathered itself and struck eight, the hammer falling on the bell with a sound like a verdict. In the strike train, the release lever swept its arc through empty air now, clean and unimpeded, and the mechanism ran smoother than it had in years.

Paloma sat alone in the lamplight, listening to the silence after the chime, and understood that he had just become the most dangerous object in Thornfield Observatory.

From the corridor outside came footsteps—unhurried, heavy, stopping just beyond the case door. Then a knock, soft and deliberate, and Mr. Quill's voice, mild as milk:

"Clock-minder. Mr. Ashcombe would like a word. Tomorrow, after breakfast." A pause, the length of a held breath. "He asks how the repairs are coming along."
