## 4. Three Checkpoints

Fear, Paloma discovered on his second sleepless night at Thornfield, was a mechanism like any other. It had a mainspring and an escapement, a rhythm that could be studied, counted, and—if one were patient—regulated. He lay in the narrow cot above the workshop with the letter's words still burning behind his eyes, and instead of letting the fear swing wild, he made himself wind it down into something useful.

*Three days.* That was what the letter had promised him, though it hadn't meant to promise anything at all. Three days before the Ashcombes' man came looking for the boy called Ansel. Three days to move a piece of paper from the belly of a clock to the desk of a journalist named Corvane at the *Meridian Gazette*, forty miles away in the city.

He got up, lit the lamp, and turned a fresh page in his practice ledger—the one he used for sketching gear trains—and began, in the tiny cipher of a clockmaker's shorthand that no clerk would bother to read, to draw a map.

Not of roads. Of checkpoints.

* * *

The first was the gatehouse.

He had passed through it once, on the day of his arrival, and once was enough to understand its logic. The gatehouse stood at the mouth of the carriage drive like a stoppin in a watch case: nothing left the estate without passing its jeweled eye. A sergeant of guards kept a leather-bound log, and every departure—post, provisions, persons—was entered in a fair hand with time, name, destination, and purpose. Paloma had watched the man write while pretending to study the drive's gravel. The sergeant wrote slowly, deliberately, the way a man writes when the writing itself is the point. Every line in that book was a small act of ownership. *Nothing leaves here that we have not recorded leaving.*

Which meant the letter could not leave as a letter. Paper was searchable; paper had edges; paper could be felt through canvas with a suspicious thumb. It would have to travel inside something so ordinary, so dull, that inspection would slide off it like rain off waxed cloth.

The second checkpoint was the Tuesday mail coach.

It ran from the village crossroads to the city every week, and it was, by all accounts, honest in the way of things that are watched too closely to be otherwise. The driver took no bribes because the magistrate's men rode it twice a season unannounced. Luggage was inspected at the coaching yard—not thoroughly, but genuinely, hands actually opening satchels, actually turning over coats. Paloma had heard the chambermaid describe it with the weary authority of someone who had lost a jar of preserved plums to exactly such an inspection.

And the third checkpoint—

Paloma set down his pencil and pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes.

The third was Mr. Quillon, the magistrate's own clerk, who kept an office above the village post room and personally examined every parcel bound for a newspaper office, a solicitor, or any address he did not recognize. Maren had told him this in her flat, flour-dusted way, watching him too carefully as she said it. *"Quillon knows every courier between here and the city by face,"* she'd said. *"Knows their mothers' names, near enough. If you're thinking what I think you're thinking, think again."*

He wasn't thinking that. Not yet. But he drew Quillon on the page anyway—a small, precise square at the end of the route, the hardest jewel in the case.

* * *

In the morning he sought out Ivo.

The stable boy was mucking out the bay mare's stall, pitchfork moving in easy strokes, and he didn't look up when Paloma leaned against the stall door. They had learned each other's silences in the past weeks; this one meant *not here.*

"The pendulum bob," Paloma said quietly. "The cracked one from the regulator clock. You haven't sent it for scrap?"

Ivo's fork paused. "Still in the tack room bin. Why?"

"I need it."

That evening, in the workshop with the door barred and the lamp shaded, Paloma held the bob up to the light. It was brass, the size of a fist, split along one seam where someone years ago had dropped it. A clean crack, a hollow interior, a screw-cap at the top that had once held the rating nut. To anyone who knew clocks, it was junk awaiting the scrap cart. To anyone who didn't, it was scrap awaiting the same.

He unscrewed the cap, rolled the letter into a cylinder no thicker than his little finger, wrapped it in oilcloth against damp, and fed it into the hollow. Then he packed the space around it with tallow and sawdust until the letter could not shift or rattle, screwed the cap home, and filled the seam with soft solder, working the iron with the steadiness he'd learned on escapements a tenth this size. When he finished, the bob looked exactly like what it was supposed to be: a broken pendulum bob, dented, discolored, worthless.

He weighed it in his palm. Half a pound of brass with a man's whole future sealed inside.

"You'll go as freight," he told it. "Freight gets glanced at. Letters get read."

* * *

The rehearsals happened in the stable loft, after the lamps below went dark, in the smell of hay and horse liniment. Ivo sat cross-legged on an old saddle blanket, playing his part with more relish than Paloma thought wise.

"State your business," Ivo said, deepening his voice into a fair copy of the gate sergeant's growl.

"Apprentice clockwork, sent down with a damaged part for the brass founder in Millbrook. Mr. Harrow wants it recast." Paloma patted the tool satchel at his hip, where the bob now nestled under a rag among his files. "It's no use to anyone cracked."

"Open the bag."

Paloma opened the bag. This was the part they rehearsed most, because it was the part he could not control. Let them look. Let them lift the files, the pliers, the rag. The bob was only a bob. The trick was not in hiding it but in being, entirely and truthfully, a boy carrying junk to be melted down. Lies were heavy; he had decided to carry none across the checkpoints. Everything he said would be true except the single fact of what the bob contained.

"Again," Ivo said. "You flinched when you said Harrow's name. Do it again."

"I didn't flinch."

"You did. Like the name bit you." Ivo grinned in the dark. "Again."

They ran it eleven times. By the ninth, Paloma could say *Mr. Harrow wants it recast* with the bored irritation of a boy who resented being sent on errands, and Ivo pronounced him ready. Then Ivo grew quiet, turning his cap in his hands, and asked the question Paloma had been waiting for.

"And if they take you? At the gatehouse, or the coach yard, or—" He stopped. "If it goes wrong, Ansel. What happens to you?"

Paloma looked out the loft window at the observatory dome, black against the stars, the great clock ticking somewhere beneath it like a heart in a chest. "Then it goes wrong," he said. "But it goes wrong on my terms, not theirs. There's a difference."

"That's not an answer."

"No," Paloma agreed. "It isn't."

Ivo didn't press. Instead he said, "Tuesday, then. The coach leaves the crossroads at nine. I'll have the gray lame by eight—the farrier's been after me to walk her anyway. A lame horse is a reason for everyone to be looking at the stable and nobody looking at the road."

"You'd do that?" Paloma said. "If they trace it back—"

"They won't. And if they do, a stable boy walking a lame horse has committed no crime worth the trouble." Ivo shrugged, but his eyes were serious. "Maren's getting your delivery order drawn up. Harrow's own chit, copied true. She says the sergeant can't read past a seal."

Paloma felt something tighten in his throat that was not fear, or not only fear. These two people, who owed him nothing, were winding themselves into his mechanism, gear by gear, and if any tooth broke they would break with him.

"Ivo," he said. "Thank you."

"Don't thank me." Ivo lay back on the blanket with his arms behind his head. "Get the letter there. That's the thanks. Now go over it once more—the coach yard. What does the inspector ask?"

"He asks to see inside everything with a lid."

"So?"

"So he sees inside everything with a lid." Paloma tapped the satchel. "And finds scrap brass."

* * *

He spent the last hour before dawn in the clock housing itself, alone with the strike train, putting the final adjustments into the escapement he'd come here to mend. It seemed important, somehow, that the clock should run perfectly right up until whatever came next. That the thing they had built their power around should keep perfect time even as it devoured them.

His fingers moved through the familiar work, and his mind moved along the route one last time. Gatehouse: delivery order, bored apprentice, scrap brass. Coach: nine o'clock, city by dusk, the *Gazette*'s offices on Cordwainer Street before they closed. And Quillon—

Quillon he could not solve. He turned the problem over the way he'd turn a stubborn wheel, looking for the tooth that would catch, and found none. The clerk knew faces. The clerk read parcels. The clerk was the fixed point the whole train swung around.

*Then don't swing around it,* whispered some quiet voice in him, a voice that sounded less afraid than it had any right to. *Go through it.*

He shut that voice away—for now. There was a plan. There was a route. There was a bob full of truth riding in a satchel full of tools, and tomorrow the gray mare would go conveniently lame, and Maren's forged-but-true delivery order would sit warm in his pocket, and at nine o'clock on Tuesday morning the boy called Ansel would walk out of Thornfield for the last time.

Below him, faint through the housing, the master clock gathered itself and struck four.

Paloma counted the strokes, closed the case, and snuffed the lamp.

Somewhere in the dark of the house, he was almost certain, a door opened and closed—softly, deliberately—as though someone had been standing outside the workshop door, listening.
