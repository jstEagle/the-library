## 5. The Master's Dinner

The dinner was announced the way weather is announced—by servants moving through the house with a purpose that had nothing to do with them. By noon on Thursday, Thornfield had transformed. The long gallery was aired and beeswaxed, the silver counted twice by Mrs. Halloway with her spectacles pinched low on her nose, and a cart arrived from the village carrying hothouse flowers that would die in their vases before the soup was cleared. Paloma watched it all from the edges, as he had learned to do, his tool satchel over one shoulder like a shepherd's crook, marking which doors were opened and which were locked.

He knew now why the house was stirring. Mr. Ashcombe's guest of honor was the magistrate himself—Aldous Vane, whose seal Paloma had last seen pressed into wax on a letter hidden behind the strike train of the master clock. The letter rode against Paloma's ribs at this very moment, folded small inside a leather pouch he had taken to wearing beneath his shirt, because leaving it in his room felt like leaving a lit lamp in a hay barn.

"You'll be wanted in the gallery from seven o'clock," Maren told him that afternoon, finding him in the clock room with his sleeves rolled and his hands blackened with oil. She said it quietly, without looking at him directly, the way she said everything that mattered. "Mr. Ashcombe wants the clock seen to before the guests arrive. Wound, regulated, striking true. He says—" she paused, and something flickered across her face, "—he says the house must keep perfect time tonight."

"Does he," Paloma said.

"He does." Maren set down the tray of polishing cloths she'd been carrying and lowered her voice to almost nothing. "There'll be extra staff up from the village. Strangers. And the magistrate brought his own man—a clerk, they say, who goes where he goes." Her eyes came up then, quick and gray and steady. "I'd keep your head down lower than usual, Ansel."

"I always keep my head down."

"Mm," she said, in the tone of a woman who had raised two brothers and believed neither of them ever. Then she was gone, her footsteps fading down the service stair, and Paloma stood alone in the ticking dark of the clock room with his heart going faster than any escapement he'd ever mended.

* * *

By half past six he was dressed in the borrowed livery of a footman—the coat a size too broad in the shoulders, since the boy it belonged to was broader still—and stationed in the corner of the gallery beside the tall case clock that stood there as ornament, its face painted with roses by some decorator who had never once considered what the hands were for. Paloma's actual duty was invisible: to mind the timekeeping of the evening, to see that the dinner gong sounded at eight precisely, that courses moved on schedule, that no guest's carriage was kept waiting by a household whose clocks disagreed. It was, he had to admit, work he was good at. Time obeyed him. People, he was learning, did not.

The guests arrived in a rustle of silk and cigar smoke. He catalogued them from his corner the way he catalogued exits: the Ashcombes at the head, Mr. Ashcombe ruddy and expansive, his wife thin and bright-eyed and saying little; a colonel someone; two ladies whose names he missed; and then the magistrate, Aldous Vane, who entered the gallery like a man entering property he already owned.

Paloma had imagined him larger. Villains in the penny dreadfuls Ivo read aloud in the loft were large men, loud men, men you could pick out of a crowd by the weight of their shadows. Vane was none of these. He was slight, silver-haired, with a scholar's stoop and a smile that arrived a half-second after his eyes finished assessing whatever stood in front of him. When his gaze crossed Paloma's corner, it did not linger—it didn't need to—but Paloma felt it pass over him like a hand checking a lock.

Dinner was announced at eight exactly. The gong sounded under Paloma's mallet, clean and true, and the party flowed into the dining room, and Paloma took up his second station in the corridor outside, where the serving door stood open just enough to carry voices.

He told himself he would hear nothing useful. He told himself he was standing there because it was his post. Both statements were true, and neither was honest.

* * *

For an hour it was only the ordinary music of such evenings—the clink of glass, the rise and fall of talk about the season, the colonel's story about a tiger that grew longer with each telling. Paloma minded the clocks. In the corridor behind him, the hall clock struck the quarter hours with a voice he had tuned himself, and each strike fell into the dining room conversation like a coin into a well.

It was during the fish course that the voices changed.

"The river parcels," Vane said. Not loudly. The way a man says something he has said many times, in rooms like this, to audiences like this. "You've seen the surveyor's final report?"

"I've seen enough of reports," Mr. Ashcombe said, with a laugh that wanted to be easy and wasn't quite. "Tell me it's done, Aldous. Tell me it's finished and we can all stop pretending to be lawyers."

"It is done. The claims are perfected. Monday morning, the writs go out." A pause; the sound of wine being poured. "Three days. The settlement will be cleared by week's end."

Paloma's hand found the wall behind him. Three days. The number went into him like a splinter, small and deep, somewhere he couldn't reach to pull out.

"And the people?" This was Mrs. Ashcombe, her voice light, almost idle, the voice of a woman asking about the weather or the fish. "Where do they go?"

"They go," Vane said, "where people without papers go. Which is nowhere in particular. There is a kindness in it, if you look at it rightly—we are giving them the whole country to choose from."

Someone laughed. Paloma could not afterward have said who. The laugh seemed to come from the walls themselves, from the house, complicit in its own foundations.

"The deeds," Mr. Ashcombe said, quieter now. "The old deeds. They're accounted for?"

"Every scrap. Burned or buried, depending on which was safer." Paper turned against paper; the sound of Vane setting down his glass. "There was one complication. You know of it. The clerk at the registry kept copies—he thought himself clever, or safe, or both. He is no longer at the registry. But copies breed, Ashcombe. Copies are the rats of the paper world. You kill one and find its droppings in your pantry for years."

"And the witness? The one mentioned in—"

"There is a boy," Vane said.

The word *boy* landed in the corridor like a dropped plate. Paloma did not move. He had spent three years learning how not to move—to stand in a doorway until a room forgot the doorway had a person in it—and every hour of that learning went into the next ten seconds.

"A boy called Ansel," Vane continued, and the name went through Paloma like cold water through cloth. "That is what the registry clerk called him. An apprentice, apparently, who was present when certain documents passed through the office last spring. He may have seen nothing. He may have seen everything. Boys see everything and understand nothing—that has been my experience." A fork touched china, delicately. "But experience also teaches me that boys grow into men, and men remember, and remembered things have a way of arriving in newspapers."

"Then find him," Mr. Ashcombe said.

"We are finding him. My clerk has inquiries out through the trade guilds. A clockmaker's apprentice, young, new to the district, papers signed this winter." Another pause, longer, and Paloma heard in it the sound of a net being drawn slowly through water. "Papers can be checked, Ashcombe. Papers can always be checked. That is rather the lesson of the entire affair."

Mrs. Ashcombe said something then, very low, that Paloma could not catch—only its tone, which was not agreement and not protest but something wearier than either. And Mr. Ashcombe laughed again, too loudly, and called for more wine, and the conversation slid away toward hunting and the price of coal and a cousin's unfortunate marriage, as though a boy's name had not been spoken over the fish course like a sentence passed.

Paloma stood in the corridor and made himself breathe.

They knew. Not who—not yet—but that a witness existed, that he was young, that he was a clockmaker's apprentice, that his papers were new. Every hour Vane's clerk worked, the circle tightened. The forged name that had kept him alive for three years was now the very thing that would hang him: *Ansel* was a signature on false documents, and false documents, in Vane's world, were evidence. They would not silence him with a knife in an alley. They would do it properly—arrest the forger, try the forger, hang the forger—and the letter in its pouch against his ribs would burn unread in some magistrate's stove, and the river settlement would be cleared by week's end, and the whole country would choose from, exactly as the kind magistrate promised.

His hands were shaking. He looked at them with a strange detachment, the way he looked at a movement that wouldn't settle, and he thought: *a clock that shakes tells no one the time.*

So he stopped them. He planted his feet the way Ivo had taught him to plant them in the stable yard, shoulder-width, weight forward, ready. He breathed the way Maren kneaded bread—slow, deliberate, turning the thing over and over until it changed. And somewhere between one quarter-hour strike and the next, the terror in his chest stopped churning and began, instead, to burn—steady, hot, directional. Terror was a fire that consumed its owner. Resolve was the same fire put to work.

Three days. The writs went out Monday morning. Which meant the letter had to be out of Thornfield, past the gatehouse, onto the Tuesday coach, through the clerk's inspection, and into the hands of the journalist in the city—all within seventy-two hours, while a man with a scholar's stoop and a patient smile hunted a boy called Ansel through every guild ledger in the district.

The dinner ended near midnight. Carriages crunched away down the gravel; the guests' laughter faded into the cold; the house exhaled. Paloma wound the gallery clock, checked it against his watch, and found it—out of habit, out of craft—perfect.

In the kitchen passage, Maren was banking the fires. She saw his face and straightened up, flour to her elbows, and asked nothing, which was her way of asking everything.

"Monday," Paloma said quietly. "The evictions. Monday morning."

Maren closed her eyes for one breath. When she opened them, her brother's eviction was in them, and her brother's empty cottage, and every year since.

"Then we have until Sunday night," she said.

"Not we," Paloma began. "I—"

But she was already shaking her head, already reaching for her shawl, already moving toward the stairs where Ivo slept, and Paloma understood that the argument was over before it started, had perhaps been over since the day he'd first shown her the letter, had perhaps been over since the day her family lost the river.

Above them, faint through the floors, the master clock began to strike twelve. Paloma counted the strokes—one, two, three—all the way to twelve, and thought: *that is how many hours I will give myself to plan. After that, we move.*

The last stroke faded. Somewhere in the dark of the house, a door he could not see opened and closed softly, and he would learn only later whose footsteps those were, and where they had gone, and what they carried.
