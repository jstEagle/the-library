## 6. First Checkpoint: The Gatehouse

Ivo had been mucking out the same stall since before dawn, which was a lie of occupation so perfect it had become almost true. His hands smelled of straw and horse. The fork in his grip was real. Only his ears were dishonest, and they were turned toward the gravel court, where Paloma Fairweather—Ansel, to everyone but the three of them—was crossing from the workshop with a tool satchel over one shoulder and the particular way of walking that meant he was carrying something heavier than tools.

The pendulum bob. Ivo had helped him pour the melted brass around it himself, two nights ago, in the blue dark of the stable, watching the letter disappear inside a skin of scrap metal until it was nothing but a dented, ugly weight that any clockmaker in England might be hauling to be recast.

*Any clockmaker,* Ivo reminded himself, *if anyone asks.*

Nobody would ask. That was the plan.

The plan had a hole in it the size of Sergeant Brack, and Ivo could see him now at the gatehouse, a thick man in a gray coat, chewing something slowly, with the ledger open on his knee the way he kept it every morning—because Mr. Ashcombe paid him, generously, to know the name of every soul who passed out of Thornfield's gates, and because knowing things was the only pleasure Brack had left.

"Morning, sergeant," Ivo called, leading out the bay mare, Duchess, on her lead rope. She was not lame. She was, however, wearing three days of careful preparation: a hoof packed with a poultice that made her favor her near foreleg just enough to make a farrier weep, and just little enough that a man who did not love horses would call it a stone bruise.

Brack looked up. "That animal's limping."

"That's why I've got her, sir. Mr. Ashcombe wants her looked at by Cooper in the village before Tuesday's drive." Ivo let the rope go slack, let Duchess take an exaggerated, pitiful step. She was a good actress for a horse. "He's particular about his carriage team."

"He's particular about everything," Brack said, and went back to his ledger.

Now the second part. Ivo had rehearsed it against the stall wall until the words came smooth as oiled gears. He dropped the fork.

Not dropped—*threw*, with intent, so it rang off the cobbles like a struck bell, and he swore loud enough to wake the rooks. "You great clumsy beast—" He said it to Duchess, who flicked an ear, unbothered, while Ivo snatched up the fork and turned on the nearest thing he could plausibly blame, which happened to be Tom Oakes, the under-gardener, passing with a barrow of cut holly.

"You did that on purpose, standing there gawping—"

"I never touched your fork."

"You were *looking* at it!"

It was a stupid quarrel and both of them knew it, and that was what made it work. Brack hated stupidity. It drew him like a moth. He rose from his stool with a groan, hitching his coat, and came down the steps into the court, already talking. "Oi. Oi! If you two want to brawl, do it where Mr. Ashcombe can't hear you, or you'll both be walking the lane looking for new places—"

And behind him, past the gatehouse, past the ledger lying open and abandoned on its stool, a slim figure in a clockmaker's apron slipped through the wicket gate and onto the village road, satchel swinging, footsteps quick and even as a ticking escapement.

Ivo felt it go out of him like a breath—the sight of that door closing behind Ansel, the road taking him—and he nearly smiled, and caught it, and made his face stupid and angry instead, because Brack had arrived between them and was wedging his bulk into the quarrel like a cork into a bottle.

"—and if I hear another word about whose fault a *fork* is, I'll have you both scrubbing the cistern. Oakes, get your barrow out of my sight. Stable boy, get that animal to Cooper or get her back in her box, and log yourself out properly when you come back, because I'll be checking."

"Yes, sergeant," Ivo said, humble as dirt.

Brack turned back toward the gatehouse—and stopped.

The ledger lay on the stool. Beside it, where no ledger should be, sat a small square of paper, folded once, weighted down with a pebble. Brack frowned at it. Ivo watched him frown, and felt his stomach drop through the cobbles, because that paper was Maren's doing, Maren's handwriting, Maren's magnificent forgery—a delivery order in the hand of the house steward, authorizing one parcel of clockwork scrap to go out with the morning carrier—and it was not supposed to appear until Ansel reached the gate, and Ansel had already gone through, and Brack had not asked him a single question, and now here was this paper sitting in the empty gateway like a confession.

Unless—

Unless Maren had planned it exactly this way. Unless the order was not for Ansel's passage at all but for the return, for Ivo and the lame mare coming back through the gate with the story still warm. He couldn't know. Standing there with the fork in his fist and his heart going like a striking train, he genuinely could not know whether Maren had made a mistake or had thought three moves ahead of everyone, the way she thought ahead of everyone in the kitchen without ever appearing to move.

Brack picked up the paper. Read it. Read it again.

"Delivery order," he muttered. "Clockwork scrap." His eyes lifted, slow as a portcullis, and found Ivo across the court. "Boy. You come back through this gate with anything in your hands that isn't on this paper, I'll have the skin off you."

"On the paper, sergeant. Scrap brass, one parcel, the carrier takes it at the King's Arms at ten."

"The carrier." Brack sniffed, unconvinced, and Ivo understood with cold clarity that the man was not convinced—that some instinct in him, fed for years on Ashcombe silver, had caught a scent of something wrong in the morning air, and was worrying at it. "Who brought this order?"

"Mrs.—the cook's assistant left it, I expect. I don't read the household post, sergeant."

"No," Brack said. "You wouldn't." He tucked the paper into his coat, and Ivo saw that he did not throw it away, and understood that it would sit in the man's pocket like a splinter, working its way toward the surface. Days, maybe. Hours, if the magistrate's men came asking questions before the letter reached the city.

Ten o'clock. The carrier at the King's Arms. Ansel had four hours to put five miles behind him and hand a dented lump of brass to a man who drove mail for a living and never looked twice at anything heavier than a sack.

Ivo led Duchess back toward the stables, past the kitchen yard, and found Maren at the scullery door with her arms red to the elbow and her eyes on the road.

"He's through," Ivo said, low.

"I know. I watched." She did not smile. She was kneading something that didn't need kneading, her fists driving into the dough like it owed her money. "Brack kept the order."

"I saw."

"He'll match the hand against the steward's writing eventually. Or he'll forget it. We've bought him either way." She wiped her forehead with the back of her wrist, leaving a streak of flour, and for one moment she looked very young and very tired. "My brother used to say that about the Ashcombes. That you never knew if you'd bought them or been bought. Then the evictions came and he stopped saying clever things."

"Maren—"

"If you're going to apologize, don't." She said it flatly, without heat, turning back to her dough. "I'm not sorry. That's the trouble. I've been waiting eleven years for someone to carry that letter through a gate, and now it's gone, and all I can think is that it's gone *through my hands*, and if it stops at the King's Arms—if that boy is stopped on the road—then it stops with my writing on the paper that let it pass." She drove her fists in again. "So don't apologize. Go finish being lame with that horse. And Ivo—"

He waited.

"When he comes back," she said, not looking up, "or when word comes, whichever comes first—you come find me before you tell anyone else. Before you tell *him*. There's a thing I haven't told either of you yet, about who else in this house reads other people's letters, and if the coach is searched the way I think it might be searched, he's going to need to know it before tonight."

The dough slapped the board. Somewhere above them, faint through the morning air, the master clock began to strike the quarter hour, and Ivo thought of Ansel walking the village road with five miles and four hours and a whole life sealed inside a lump of scrap brass, and wondered—not for the first time that morning—what kind of person it made you, to help a friend run, and to pray he ran fast enough that you'd never have to find out what you were made of either.

He took up the lead rope. Duchess limped obligingly toward her stall.

Behind him, at the gatehouse, Sergeant Brack had taken out the delivery order again, and was holding it up against the morning light, as if the steward's signature might change its mind.

* * *

The King's Arms stood where the road bent toward the city, its sign creaking on old iron, and Ivo reached it at half past nine with Duchess shod, genuinely lame-acting, and a lie about the farrier's fees already worn smooth from rehearsal. The carrier's cart stood outside. The carrier himself was inside, drinking. And beside the cart, talking to the ostler with his hands in his pockets, stood a man Ivo had seen only twice before and never wished to see again: the magistrate's clerk, thin as a rule line, with ink stains to the knuckle and a face that forgot nothing.

He was not inspecting the mail. He was simply *there*, early, unhurried, waiting the way a spider waits—not for any fly in particular, but because it is Tuesday, and Tuesdays are reliable.

Ivo tied Duchess at the rail, bought a penny loaf he had no appetite for, and stood in the doorway of the taproom watching the road bend away toward the city, empty in both directions, and understood that the second checkpoint had begun without them, and that somewhere on it, alone, a boy called Ansel was walking straight toward the one man in the county who never forgot a face.
