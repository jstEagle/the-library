## 7. Second Checkpoint: The Mail Coach

The coach smelled of wet wool and varnish. Paloma took the backward-facing seat because it was the last one left, and only when the door swung shut and the steps folded up did he understand what the universe intended to do to him.

Mrs. Halloway—the magistrate's wife—settled into the seat across from him, arranged her gray skirts with two precise pats, and said, "You're the Ashcombes' clock boy."

Not a question. The word *boy* landed like a coin dropped in an empty room.

"Yes, ma'am." He kept his eyes on the buckle of his tool satchel, where the pendulum bob rode against his hip, heavier than lead, heavier than sin. Inside it, folded to the width of a wafer, the letter. The magistrate's own seal pressed into red wax not three feet from the magistrate's own wife.

"You're up early," she observed. "They work you terribly hard at Thornfield. My husband says the great clock has been failing for months. He says—" she smiled faintly, "—he says if the clock dies, the house dies with it. Isn't that a strange thing to say? As though time could be owned."

"It can be measured, ma'am. Measured things feel like they belong to someone."

She considered this with genuine pleasure, as though he'd brought her a gift. "How old are you?"

"Seventeen." The lie came smooth now, worn round as a river stone from handling. Ansel was seventeen. Ansel had been seventeen since the day Paloma Fairweather had bought those papers from a man in a tavern who never asked questions, because men who sell names don't care whose life they ruin.

"And where are you bound? To the city?"

"For parts, ma'am. Brass stock for the escapement." He patted the satchel. "Scrap, mostly."

"Ah." She turned to the window as the coach lurched out of the yard, Thornfield's towers sliding away behind wet elms. "I haven't been to the city in two years. Mr. Halloway prefers I stay near. He says the air disagrees with me." A small pause. "It isn't the air."

Paloma's hands went still on his knees.

Outside, the river country unrolled—hedgerows, then fences, then no fences at all, just the brown gleam of the lowlands where the settlement stood. Where it had stood. Smoke still rose from a few stubborn chimneys, thin threads of defiance against a gray sky. Eviction was set for three days hence; he'd heard it himself over the clink of dessert spoons, Mr. Ashcombe and her husband dividing land like men splitting a bill at supper.

Mrs. Halloway followed his gaze. "The river people," she said, conversational as one remarks on frost. "You'll have heard the talk. They're being moved on Thursday. It's all been arranged very fairly—compensation, new parcels inland. Though of course they'd rather stay and drown their children in flood years than accept a shilling from decent folk." She sighed. "My husband calls it the last untidy corner of the county. By Friday it will be tidy."

"Tidy," Paloma repeated. His voice came out level. Somewhere beneath his ribs, Maren's face surfaced—her brother, evicted, the way she'd said *the river takes everything twice*.

"Oh, don't look so grave, child. You're too young to carry other people's weather." She smoothed a glove. "Besides, it's settled law. Signed, sealed. There are papers." She laughed—a light, brittle sound. "There are always papers."

*There are papers.* In his satchel, against his thigh, there were papers that would burn her husband's world down. He felt them the way one feels a hot coal through cloth.

"The seal on those documents," he said carefully, "must be a fine piece of work."

"Mr. Voss does them." She said it the way one recommends a tailor. "The clerk. He has a beautiful hand. My husband says half the law is handwriting." She caught herself, or almost did—her smile flickered like a lamp in wind. "That was a joke, of course."

"Of course, ma'am."

* * *

The coach climbed the ridge road, and the rain began—not hard, just a fine persistent needling against the glass. Mrs. Halloway talked. That was the thing about her: she talked the way clocks ticked, steadily, filling silence because silence might hold something.

She told him about her daughter in the north, married to a man who never wrote. About the conservatory at the magistrate's residence where nothing grew but ferns, because she overwatered them out of something to do. About how she had once, before marriage, wanted to paint watercolors, and how Mr. Halloway said painting was for women with nothing better to occupy them, and how she'd laughed when he said it, because what else was there to do.

Paloma listened and nodded in the right places, and all the while some cold clear part of him catalogued her: *complicit. Lonely. Dangerous.* The three were not opposites. Loneliness made her talk; complicity made her talk *safely*—she would never say anything that truly endangered her husband, and so every confidence she offered was also a demonstration that nothing needed endangering. The eviction was weather. The forged claims were handwriting. The displaced families were an untidy corner.

And yet.

When the coach stopped at a crossroads inn to change horses, she did not get down. She watched the rain instead and said, quietly, without looking at him: "There was a woman at the settlement who used to sell eel pies by the bridge. Before your time at Thornfield, certainly. My husband bought one once and pronounced it excellent, and then two years later he signed the paper that put her off her ground." Her gloved fingers pleated the skirt fabric. "I asked him about her once. He said, *which woman?* He'd genuinely forgotten. I think—" she turned her pale eyes on Paloma, and for a moment something moved behind them, deep water under ice, "—I think forgetting is a kind of skill. Like your clockwork. Some people practice it until it runs without them."

"Do you practice, ma'am?" The question escaped him before judgment could catch it.

She didn't take offense. She looked almost grateful. "Every day," she said. "I'm very good at it."

* * *

The checkpoint came at the toll arch outside Marrowgate, where the road narrowed between stone pillars and a uniformed guard boarded with a ledger under his arm and boredom in his shoulders.

"Luggage inspection. Coach regulations. Everyone's parcel on the seat, please."

Paloma's heart performed one clean, terrible click, like an escapement releasing. He set the satchel on the seat beside Mrs. Halloway's hatbox and kept his breathing slow, counting seconds—he was an apprentice clockmaker; counting was what his body did under duress.

The guard worked down the coach. A fishmonger's crate, passed. A lawyer's document case, passed. A widow's carpet bag, opened, repacked, passed. He reached the front.

"You're the Ashcombe man?" the guard said, glancing at Paloma's collar pin, the little brass wheel the household servants wore.

"I keep their clocks," Paloma said.

"Then you'll have tools." The guard held out a hand. "Open it."

Inside the satchel: files, pliers, a coil of brass wire, oilcloth—and the pendulum bob, its hollow core plugged with a wax disc, the letter sealed within like a heart inside a chest. If the guard shook it, if he unscrewed anything, if he noticed that this bob weighed wrong—

"That's scrap," Mrs. Halloway said.

The guard paused.

"He told me himself," she continued, gesturing with one languid glove. "Brass stock for repairs. Honestly, sergeant, if you intend to confiscate the Ashcombes' broken clock parts, you'd best also arrest me for my hatpins. Half of England runs on scrap brass and nobody knows which half." She smiled at him with the full authority of twenty years of being obeyed in drawing rooms, and the guard—flustered, deferential, already retreating—muttered something about *formalities* and moved on to the next passenger.

The satchel came back untouched.

Paloma thanked her. His voice worked; he was almost proud of that.

"Don't thank me," Mrs. Halloway said, settling back as the coach rolled under the arch and onward. "I simply dislike being delayed. And I dislike that young man's tone." She gazed out at the rain. "Though I suppose we all serve someone's convenience, don't we? You keep their clocks. I keep my opinions. Everything runs smoother that way."

*Everything runs smoother that way.* Paloma looked at her—this pleasant, chattering, courteous woman who could name the clerk who forged the seals and the woman who sold eel pies, who knew exactly what her husband was and had chosen, daily, methodically, for years, to know it comfortably—and felt the last of his childhood assumptions die without ceremony. He had imagined corruption as masked men in dark rooms, violence, conspiracy whispered by candlelight. Instead it sat opposite him in a gray traveling dress, offering him a peppermint from a tin, complaining mildly about the damp, and discussing the destruction of forty families in the same breathless tone she used for the price of ribbon.

It was banal. It was polite. It passed inspection because everyone important agreed to wave it through.

He took the peppermint. It seemed important, somehow, to take it—to let her kindness be real, because it *was* real, and that was the whole horror of it. Real kindness, woven through real harm, thread by thread, like a mechanism designed by someone who genuinely loved the machine and never once wondered whom it crushed.

* * *

The city rose out of the rain in the late afternoon—chimneys, cranes, the gasworks' smudge against a darkening sky. The coach rattled along the high street toward the coaching inn, and Mrs. Halloway gathered her things, preparing to be met by carriage.

"You've been good company," she told him at the door, before the steps came down. "For a servant. Most of them just stare at their boots." Then, as he handed her down, she paused on the bottom step, rain spotting her veil, and said—lightly, so lightly it might have meant nothing:

"My husband expects word from Voss tonight. About a certain delivery being stopped at the post office tomorrow." Her eyes found Paloma's, mild as milk. "The magistrate doesn't trust the mails anymore. Not with Thursday coming. He says loose papers make loose ends." She smiled. "Do give the Ashcombes my regards. And mind the third checkpoint, child. Everyone forgets the third one."

She was gone into the waiting carriage before he could arrange his face.

Paloma stood in the rain outside the coaching inn with the satchel heavy against his hip, and understood, with terrible clarity, that he had not survived the second checkpoint at all. He had been *allowed* through—or worse, tested. She knew. She had known, perhaps, since the words *clock boy* left her mouth, and everything after—the confidences, the peppermint, the warning dressed as gossip—had been her own private arithmetic, her own quiet rebellion or her own meticulous trap, and he could not tell which, and he would never know which.

But the meaning was plain regardless: the post office would be watched tomorrow. Voss would be watching. The plan that had carried him this far died right there on the wet cobblestones, and dawn was coming, and somewhere behind him the Ashcombe household was already asking questions about a boy called Ansel.

He pulled his cap low and walked into the city, carrying the letter, the lie, and the name of the one man in the county who could stop him—straight toward the third checkpoint, where every courier was known by face.
