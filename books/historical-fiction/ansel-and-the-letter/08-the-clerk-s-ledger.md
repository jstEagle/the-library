## 8. The Clerk's Ledger

The post office on Candlewick Row smelled of sealing wax and wet wool, and Paloma Fairweather stood in the doorway of it for the better part of ten minutes, watching a man he had never seen before ruin everything.

The clerk was not the one Ivo had described. Ivo's intelligence—gathered from stable gossip, from grooms who carried parcels into town twice a week—had promised a Mr. Hobbins: nearsighted, fond of his dinner, content to wave through anything with an honest-looking address. Instead, behind the brass-grilled counter stood a spare woman in spectacles, or beside her rather—for she was not alone. The man at her elbow wore the magistrate's livery button on his coat, grey and unremarkable, and he greeted every courier by name.

"Morning, Tice. That's the third parcel to Harrowgate this week—you're making a living off me." A laugh, shared, easy. "Mrs. Quill! And how is your daughter? Still at the conservatory?" He did not weigh Mrs. Quill's package so much as bless it. "Mr. Dunmore—the usual?"

Every courier by face. Every face attached to a name, every name entered in a ledger that lay open on the counter like a confession waiting to be read.

Paloma pulled back from the doorway, his shoulder meeting the brick of the tobacconist next door, and let the rain that had followed him from Thornfield bead down the collar of his borrowed coat. In the satchel against his hip, wrapped in oilcloth and packed in cotton waste, the broken pendulum bob sat heavy as a skull. Inside it, folded small enough to survive the drilling, the magistrate's letter waited with the patience of all written things.

He had rehearsed this. He had rehearsed it on the coach, in the yard behind the Bell and Anchor where he'd changed coaches, walking the last mile into town so no one could connect him to a fare. *I am Ansel, apprentice clockmaker, sent down with a part for the silversmith in Fetter Lane.* The bob was genuine scrap—he'd broken it himself, honestly, three days ago—and the story had survived the coach guard's bored hands. It would not survive this man. This man would look at him, a boy of perhaps seventeen in a workman's coat, claiming to be Ansel of Thornfield Observatory, and something behind those spectacles would turn over like a page. Because *Ansel* was already a name in somebody's book. He had felt it since the dinner, since the words *the clock boy* had crossed the table between Mr. Ashcombe and the magistrate like a coin passed under the cloth.

A courier came out of the post office shaking rain from his hat, and Paloma turned away, pretending to study the tobacconist's window, pipes arranged like little drowned men. Through the glass's reflection he watched the grey-livered man step to the door, glance up and down the street—not suspiciously, only habitually, the way a shepherd counts sheep without thinking—and go back inside.

There was no way through. Not today. Perhaps not ever, now that they'd posted a man who knew faces instead of one who knew forms.

The despair arrived quietly, which was somehow worse than if it had come shouting. It settled into his chest with the weight of the bob itself. Three checkpoints mapped, two beaten, and the third was not a wall but a mirror—it would simply recognize him, and recognition was the one thing he could not survive. He had built Ansel out of forged papers and a lowered voice and eleven years of being nobody, and the whole structure rested on the assumption that no one would ever look closely. Someone was looking closely now. Someone had been looking closely for some time.

*Questioning at dawn.*

The thought arrived detached, the way bad news does, before he understood he already knew it. He had known since the gatehouse sergeant's eyes had lingered a half-second too long on Maren's forged delivery order; since the coach guard had weighed the bob in his palm and asked, almost kindly, *Ashcombe brass, is it?* They were tightening a net woven of ordinary courtesies, and every kindness shown him was another knot drawn snug.

He walked. It was that or stand in the rain until someone noticed a boy standing in the rain. He walked past the chandler's, past the corn exchange with its clock—*a poor thing, four-minute drift a week, I could fix it in an afternoon*, some traitorous professional corner of his mind observed—past the notice board outside the magistrate's offices where fresh bills were pasted over old ones. The top bill concerned the river settlement. NOTICE TO OCCUPANTS. FINAL DATE OF VACANCY. Three days hence, said the dinner. Two days hence, said the bill. The ink was still glossy.

Paloma read it twice. His own breath fogged the paper, and he stepped back as though the bill had touched him.

Then he saw the second bill beneath it, older, half-torn, and his stomach dropped through the cobbles. A reward notice. Not for a letter. For information regarding *persons employed at Thornfield Observatory within the last year*, inquiries to be directed discreetly to the magistrate's clerk. Discreetly. They were fishing, patiently, with the whole town as their hook and line.

He bought a meat pie he could not taste and ate it standing under the exchange eaves, and by the time he'd finished, one fact had assembled itself out of the wreckage with terrible clarity: there was no route left. Smuggling was finished. The letter could not travel because everywhere it might travel, *he* was already known, or about to be.

Which meant there was only one direction remaining, and it was the one he'd been walking away from for eleven years.

* * *

Maren was waiting in the scullery when he came back through the garden door after dark, her sleeves rolled, a knife paused over a pile of onions as though she had been holding that pose for hours. Maybe she had. She read his face in the space of one glance, set the knife down, and wiped her hands on her apron with the deliberateness of a surgeon.

"Well," she said.

"It's finished." He kept his voice below the sound of the range. "The clerk—they've replaced him, or added to him. A man who knows every courier by face. There's no sending it. There's no carrying it anywhere. And—" He made himself say the rest. "There's a notice board outside the magistrate's office. They're asking after Thornfield staff. Discreet inquiries. And Ivo heard the housekeeper telling the butler—I'm summoned. Dawn tomorrow. The magistrate's chambers. 'Regarding matters touching the observatory.'"

Maren stood very still. Steam rose between them. Outside, somewhere over the park, the great clock began striking the quarter hour, and even muffled through stone he could hear the hitch in the fourth stroke, the escapement still imperfect, his unfinished work, and it struck him absurdly that he might never finish it now.

"You could run," Maren said at last. "South road, tonight. Ivo would saddle something."

"And go where? On what name?" He laughed, and hated the sound. "They know the clock boy exists. If Ansel vanishes the same week the letter goes missing, they don't need to find me. They just need to describe me. And every honest paper in the country will print it for them."

"So you stay, and you hand it over at dawn, and you hope they believe you never opened it." Her voice was flat, testing. "You hope a magistrate who forged land claims believes a servant boy found nothing in his clock."

"I don't know what I hope."

"Yes, you do." She picked up the knife again, not to use it—to hold it, the way one holds a railing in a storm. "My brother Tom was put off the river land in '89. You know that. You know what they lost—the deeds burned in the 'accidental' fire at the records office, my father's signature declared a forgery by a man whose own signature isn't worth the wax under it." She set the knife down again, harder. "For four years I've carried that the way you carry that letter—in secret, in silence, certain that anything done openly would only get people hurt. And it got us nothing. It got them a bill pasted on a board and a date to be gone by."

Paloma said nothing. The onions wept on the board between them.

"They expect you at dawn," Maren said. "So go to dawn. Walk in the front way, the way they've invited you. And when they ask you what you found in their clock—" she looked up, and her eyes were dry and absolutely steady— "tell them the truth. All of it. Hand the letter to the magistrate himself, across his own desk, and tell him your name while you do it. Your real one. Let them have their questioning. Make it a testimony."

The room seemed to tilt. "They'll destroy me. The papers are forged, Maren. One word about Ansel and I'm not a witness, I'm a criminal. I lose the trade. I lose the name. I lose everything I built to stay alive."

"Yes," she said simply. "And if you run, you keep all of it, and it keeps you—hollow, and hiding, and watching every door for the rest of your life. I've watched people live like that. My father lived like that for two years before he died. It's not living, Paloma. It's serving out a sentence." She came around the table and took his hands, onion-smelling and warm and ungentle. "But if you stand in that room and speak your true name out loud—if the clerk hears it, if the journalist hears it, if *I* hear it—then it can't be taken from you afterward. It's spent. Spent things can't be stolen."

"The journalist." He grasped at it. "I never reached the journalist. The third checkpoint—"

"Then the third checkpoint comes to you. I wrote to him myself, weeks ago, when I first saw what you were carrying. Mr. Ellery Vance, the *Gazette*. He answered me Sunday." Something flickered across her face, the first crack in her steadiness—fear, allowed its single instant. "He'll be in the public gallery of the assize offices tomorrow morning. Whether you speak or whether you break, someone will be there writing it down. You won't be alone in that room. That much I could buy. The rest is yours."

Paloma stood in the warm dark of the scullery and felt the plan die inside him completely—the satchel, the cover stories, the rehearsed lies, Ivo's staged quarrels, the whole clever architecture of a boy trying to remain invisible while moving something true through a world built on lies. It had been a good plan. It had been the only kind of plan Ansel could make.

But he was so tired of being Ansel. Underneath the forged papers and the lowered voice there was a girl-shaped absence, a name worn smooth from disuse, and Maren was right: spent things cannot be stolen. If she was wrong, he would hang or be transported, and the letter would burn in a magistrate's stove, and that was the price. If she was right—

If she was right, then dawn was not an ending. It was a door, and for once he would walk through it facing forward.

Above them, faint through the floors, the master clock struck the hour—one, two, three, four, the fifth stroke catching, hesitating, and holding, as though the great machine itself had drawn breath to see what the boy would do.

"I'll need my good coat," Paloma said. "And someone to walk me to the door, so I don't turn back."

Maren smiled, and it was not a comforting smile. It was the smile of a woman loading a weapon. "Ivo's already polishing boots," she said. "We go at first light."
