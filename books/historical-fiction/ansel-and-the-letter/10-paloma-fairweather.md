## 10. Paloma Fairweather

The river town smelled of wet stone and woodsmoke and, in the mornings, of bread from the bakery two doors down from the shop. Paloma Fairweather had lived there six weeks when he finally stopped flinching at the sound of his own name.

It happened without ceremony. A customer—a fishwife with a broken mantel clock and a voice like gravel in a pail—called across the workbench, "Fairweather! You listening?" and he looked up before he remembered to be afraid, before he checked the door for magistrates or gate sergeants or anyone who might know that name had been invented only weeks ago by a clerk with honest ink and a sympathetic journalist's letter of introduction. He was simply Paloma Fairweather now, apprentice to old Tobias Wrenn, clockmaker of Marlow Bend, and the name fit like a coat cut to his shoulders.

"Sorry," he said. "The escapement. It wants a lighter hand than mine."

"Everything wants a lighter hand than yours," said Wrenn from his corner, not unkindly. The old man had white hair like frost on wire and eyes that missed nothing, which had terrified Paloma for the first fortnight until he understood that what Wrenn missed nothing *of* was crooked wheels and lazy polish, not boys with pasts. "That's why you're here. To grow the lighter hand."

Paloma bent back over the mantel clock. Through the shop's broad window he could see the river, low and brown-gold in the autumn light, and beyond it the first rooftops of the settlement—his settlement now, though the deeds had been restored to their proper owners and none of them were his. That was the strange arithmetic of it: he had given up everything, forged name and trade papers and the whole careful architecture of Ansel, and had somehow ended up owning more than he'd ever held. He owned a cot above the shop. He owned a Sunday dinner invitation from Widow Crane that never lapsed. He owned the habit of Maren's voice saying his true name, testing it, approving it.

He owned, most days, the feeling of being looked for rather than looked through.

* * *

Maren came on Thursdays. She arrived this one with a basket on her hip and flour still dusting her forearms, having walked the four miles down from Thornfield village because the coach fare was, she declared, robbery, and because walking let her stop at the milliner's where a young man named Aldous had begun saving ribbons for her.

"You're blushing," Paloma said, opening the door.

"I am winded," said Maren, "from the hill." She set the basket on the workbench among the gears and produced a loaf still warm enough to fog the air above it. "Rye. Widow Crane's recipe, but mine comes out better, which I've told her, and she pretended not to hear."

"How is she?"

"Fat as a bishop and twice as smug. She's giving testimony to anyone who'll sit still long enough—the trial made her famous, you know. There's a man coming from the city to write a pamphlet about it all. *The Ashcombe Forgeries*, he means to call it. She's instructed him to spell her name correctly."

Paloma laughed, and the laugh surprised him with how easy it was. In another life—in Ansel's life—he had learned to laugh quietly, sideways, a sound designed not to carry. Here the walls were thin and nobody minded.

"And Ivo?"

"Growing insufferable." Maren dug into her apron pocket and produced a letter, soft at the corners from handling. "Third one this month. He's got a position at a livery near the observatory—the new people kept him on, did you know? The Ashcombes' effects were sold off, the house let to some shipping family, but the stables needed a steady hand and everyone agreed Ivo was the steadiest thing left standing there."

Paloma broke the seal. Ivo's letters were dictated, mostly, to whoever stood nearest with a pen, and they read like weather reports delivered by a man shouting across a yard. *HORSE CALLED BISCUIT IS LAME AGAIN BUT IN A NEW LEG WHICH IS INTERESTING. GATE SERGEANT HOLLIS ASKS AFTER YOU. TOLD HIM YOU SEND REGARDS. HE SAYS TO SAY NONE BACK BUT HIS FACE SAID OTHERWISE.* At the bottom, in Ivo's own laborious hand, four words: *the clock runs again.*

Paloma read them twice. He had known, of course—the newspapers had reported it with a satisfaction usually reserved for executions. After the magistrate's disgrace, after the Ashcombes' flight ahead of the writs, the observatory had passed to the university, and the university had hired a clockmaker from the capital, a serious woman named Doctor Ehrl who wrote to Paloma sometimes, three letters so far, asking questions about the escapement modifications he'd made during his time there. *Your strike train correction is elegant,* she had written in the second. *Who taught you?*

*An old man in Marlow Bend,* he'd answered. It was even true. He just hadn't met the old man yet when he learned the lesson.

"They're saying," Maren said, watching him fold the letter away, "that she keeps it to the half-second. Better than the Ashcombes ever managed."

"The Ashcombes never listened to it," Paloma said. "They only ever listened to themselves."

Maren snorted. Then she went quiet, turning a gear between her fingers with a delicacy that would have scandalized Wrenn if he hadn't already retreated to the back room with the tact of long practice.

"Do you regret it?" she asked. Not looking at him. "Any of it. You lost your trade. Your name was dragged through every paper from here to the coast—they printed your age, your birthplace, everything. Some of those articles weren't kind about the forgery part."

"My forgery or theirs?"

"Either."

Paloma considered it honestly, because Maren deserved honesty and had paid for it in full: a brother evicted, years of silence kept in a hostile house, a fabricated delivery order pressed into a gate sergeant's hands while her own heart hammered. If anyone had earned the right to ask hard questions, it was her.

"I regret that it took me so long," he said at last. "I walked past that river town twice on my way north, when I was running the first time. I could have stopped. I could have found honest work and an honest name and none of it—" He gestured vaguely eastward, toward the city, the courthouse, the pamphlet with Widow Crane's correctly spelled name. "Instead I built Ansel. And Ansel was a good mechanism, Maren, truly—I oiled every joint of him. But a mechanism only does what it was built to do. It can't choose anything. I spent four years being very well-made and completely unable to move."

"And now?"

"Now I'm badly made and free." He grinned. "Wrenn says I'll improve with filing."

Maren laughed—her real laugh, the one that made the shop cat flee under the bench—and reached over and squeezed his wrist once, hard, the way she did instead of embracing anyone, which she claimed was a waste of good working hours.

Then she said, more carefully: "There's something else. The reason I walked today instead of riding." She wiped her palms on her apron, which Paloma had learned meant she was nervous. "The settlement's rebuilding the meeting hall. The one the Ashcombes burned out, back when they wanted the land empty for the survey. Widow Crane has taken charge of it, naturally, as though the hall were a regiment and she its general. And there's a tower on it. Small. Ugly. Built by the same mason who did the observatory gatehouse, which is why it leans."

"A tower needs a clock," Paloma said slowly.

"A tower needs a clock," Maren agreed. "And the committee—which is Widow Crane and two men too polite to disagree with her—has been arguing for a month about who to commission. The capital quotes are ruinous. But there's talk of someone local. Someone young, with recent experience of difficult mechanisms and worse owners. Someone whose references include, and I quote the widow directly, 'stopping a corrupt magistrate's entire future mid-chime.'"

"She wants me to build it."

"Widow Crane wants you to build it, the committee wants you to build it, and the settlement wants you to build it, though half of them are too shy to say so and the other half have already started calling it Fairweather's Tower, which you should know before you agree so you don't faint at the dedication." Maren folded her arms. "Well?"

Paloma looked out the window. The river ran gold-brown and unhurried toward the settlement, toward the burned hall and its leaning tower and the people who had, without ceremony or contract, decided somewhere in these six weeks that he belonged to them. Time, he thought. Everyone in his life had tried to own it—magistrates with their falsified ledgers, masters with their bells, the whole rigged machinery of who worked when and for whom. But time itself had never once turned against him. It had only waited, patient as a pendulum, for him to catch up to it.

"Yes," he said.

"You'll want terms," said Maren. "Wrenn will insist. He's already drafting them; he told me Thursday last."

"He—"

"Paloma. The man reads every letter you get upside-down across the bench. He's known for a month." She picked up her basket, then paused at the door, and her voice gentled into something almost shy. "Your brother would be proud of you. I never said it plainly before. I'm saying it now."

She was gone before he could answer, which was her way, and the shop was quiet except for the tick of forty clocks keeping forty honest counts, and Paloma stood in the middle of them with flour dust settling in the light and his brother's memory warm in his chest, and began, right there at the bench, to sketch a tower clock that would keep better time than any the Ashcombes had ever wound—

—and stopped, pen lifted, at the sound of footsteps on the stairs: quick ones, boyish ones, arriving far too soon for Thursday.

The door banged open, and there, hatless, dusty, grinning like the day he'd staged a quarrel with himself in a stable yard, stood Ivo.
