## 1. The Last Dispatch

The Republic was dying the way old men die in winter: slowly, and with everyone pretending otherwise.

Sebastian Underhill stood in the courtyard of the Chancellery at dawn, watching her breath rise and vanish, and counted the ways the building lied about itself. The banners of Vessar still hung from the colonnade—green and white, the orchard colors, though half of them had faded to a gray that matched the sky. The fountain at the courtyard's center had been drained for the season, its basin littered with leaves no one had ordered swept. Somewhere above, in the Minister's wing, a window stood broken and boarded, and no carpenter had been sent because there were no carpenters to send, or no money for them, or no will; the distinctions had stopped mattering months ago.

And yet the delegation assembled below her as if it were an embassy to a living power. Eleven coaches, forty-three souls, trunks lacquered and strapped, outriders in dress uniform with polished breastplates that would be filthy by noon. They were going to negotiate an armistice with Ostrava, and they were going to do it with ceremony, because ceremony was the last currency the Republic still minted in quantity.

"Underhill." The voice belonged to Sergeant Brakke, who managed the delegation's baggage and its couriers with the same expression he gave spoiled meat. "You're on the second coach. Papers in the document case, nothing loose. And for the love of whatever you pray to, don't let Corvane's people touch the case."

"No one touches the case," Sebastian said. Her voice came out low and flat, a voice she had built over three years the way a mason builds a wall—one stone at a time, until it didn't feel like disguise anymore, just architecture.

Brakke grunted and moved on. He had never once looked at her long enough to wonder.

That was the trick of it, she had learned. Not the binding, not the lowered voice, not even the name—though all of those mattered. It was being *boring*. A courier named Underhill, twenty-six years old, unremarkable face, ink-stained fingers, a stoop that suggested a man who spent his life bent over dispatch tables. Men looked through boring men. Ministers spoke secrets in front of them the way they'd speak in front of furniture. She had been furniture for three years now, and furniture survived wars that killed kings.

She touched the left side of her ribs, where the case rode beneath her coat. Inside it, wrapped in oilcloth against the cold, lay the codebook—the new one, compiled after the spring breaches, keyed to a rotation only four people alive could reconstruct. Forty pages of cipher tables and rotating keys, thin as a psalter and worth more than every coach in the courtyard. If Ostrava took it, they could read the Republic's military traffic for a year. If Corvane took it, he could sell it. Both possibilities were equally live, which told Sebastian everything about the state of her nation.

The plan—if anyone had asked her, which no one did—was that she would memorize it. Page by page, night by night, on the road. A book could burn, could be stolen, could be drowned in a river by a panicking officer trying to keep it from capture. A memory could be cut out of her skull only one way, and by then the codes would have changed anyway. So she carried the book against her ribs like a second heart, and each night she would open it by candle stub and feed another page into herself, and in the morning she would recite what she'd eaten, silently, while saddling horses or mending straps or doing whatever drudgery kept hands busy and eyes unwatched.

*Page one.* She began it now, standing in the frost, mouthing nothing, moving her lips not at all. *Column alpha, primary key derived from date of accession minus seven, transposed by the ordinal of—*

"Courier!" someone shouted, and the spell broke, and she went to work.

* * *

The delegation departed as the bells rang the third hour, eleven coaches grinding over cobbles slick with black ice, past crowds that had come out not to cheer but to look, the way people look at a funeral procession to see if the mourners are crying. An old woman near the Salt Gate spat as the Ministerial coach passed. A boy sold roasted chestnuts and did not look up at all. Someone had chalked on the gatehouse wall, in letters a hand's width high: **THE ORCHARD IS CUT DOWN**. The old slogan of the republicans, from before the war, when the metaphor had been hopeful. Now it read like a diagnosis.

Sebastian rode on the box of the second coach, beside the driver, a taciturn veteran named Pell who chewed tobacco and considered conversation a tax levied by the weak. That suited her. From the box she could watch the road, the guards, the ministers' windows, and pretend her stillness was vigilance rather than fear.

The capital fell away behind them in stages. First the walls, then the burning-grounds outside the eastern gate where the plague dead were stacked under quicklime—too many stacks, far too many—then the vineyards gone to ruin, their trellises standing bare like gallows in rows. The Republic had been beautiful once. She could remember it beautiful: her father alive, Tomas fourteen and insufferable, the orchard at home heavy enough in autumn that the branches needed propping. A country could rot from the inside and still look whole from a distance, the way a grafted tree looks fine until the stock fails beneath the scion.

Tomas. She put his name down the way you'd set down something fragile, then picked it up again immediately, because putting it down felt like losing it.

Three years dead, officially. Killed at Marrow Field with two thousand others when the Fourth Division broke and nobody admitted it had broken until the Ostravan cavalry was among the tents. Their mother had received the letter, the printed letter, the one with the eagle stamp and the phrase *died in service to the Republic*, and had died herself within the year—not of grief, the physician said, of her heart—but Sebastian knew arithmetic when she saw it. Grief had simply signed itself with another name.

Except. Except that in the last dispatch pouch she'd sorted before departure, buried between requisitions, there had been nothing. No letter. No word. She thought of him anyway, constantly, the way you worry a tooth. *If you're alive,* she thought, not for the first time, *then I am the worst sister in the world for not finding you, and if you're dead then I am the worst sister in the world for hoping, because hope this long is just pain wearing a costume.*

"You're chewing your lip," Pell said, without looking at her. "Do that in front of Corvane's clerks and they'll write it down."

Sebastian made her face still. "Yes, Sergeant."

Pell spat over the wheel. "Corvane," he said, tasting the name like the tobacco. "Man travels with his own guard, his own cipher clerk, and his own wine. For a peace mission." He shook his head slowly. "In my day we called that a siege train."

She said nothing. Saying nothing was her trade.

But she marked it, the way she marked everything now, in the ledger she kept behind her eyes: *Corvane brings his own people. Corvane does not trust the mission. Corvane intends to own whatever this mission produces.*

Minister Corvane rode in the fourth coach and had, since the courtyard, spoken to no one outside his circle. She had seen him only twice at close range—at briefings, at the signing of her orders—and both times she had noticed the same thing: he listened the way a moneylender listens, computing while appearing to wait. He was not old, but he was finished in some way, cured of doubt the way a body is cured of fever by burning everything the fever lived in. Men said he had lost sons in the war. Men also said he had profited from the grain contracts during the famine winter. Both things were true, probably. That was the Republic in miniature.

*Column alpha continues. Transposition by ordinals, then reduction mod twenty-nine. Secondary key rotates on the feast days of the orchard calendar—blossom, greenfall, harvest, bare bough.*

Bare bough. She looked at the fields going by, stubbled and gray, and fed herself another line of code instead of another memory.

* * *

They made the first night at a relay station whose name she never learned, a long low building with a stable attached and a landlord who charged the delegation triple and apologized with his shoulders. The ministers dined upstairs on salted pork and a wine that predated the war. The staff ate below, and it was below, in the steaming dark of the kitchen passage, that Sebastian did her real work: drying boots, sorting the dispatch satchels, copying route orders, becoming so useful and so unremarkable that the guards stopped noticing when she slipped away to the loft with a candle end and the oilcloth bundle.

Forty pages. She had memorized nine before the capital was out of sight. Tonight she meant to make it twelve.

The loft smelled of hay and horse and the tallow smoke of other travelers. She wedged herself into the corner farthest from the ladder, opened the case with the small brass key she wore on a cord around her neck under her shirt—a cord that also held, though she never let herself think about why, a dried apple-wood cutting no longer than her thumb—and worked by the smallest light she could tolerate.

The cipher was elegant. Whoever had built it had known they were building for a dying state; the whole scheme assumed compromise, assumed interception, assumed betrayal, and rotated fast enough to survive all three for perhaps a year. Perhaps. She copied the tables into her memory with the discipline her father had taught her for grafting: clean cuts, no wasted motion, match the layers so the living wood touches living wood. *Knowledge grafts the same way,* he used to say. *It takes only if you cut it fresh and bind it tight.*

Below her, through the floorboards, she heard the ministers' dinner breaking up—laughter too loud, a chair scraping, Corvane's voice rising once, level and cold, saying something she couldn't catch except the word *"leak."* Then footsteps, doors, quiet.

Leak. Already. The mission was six hours old.

She sat in the dark after the candle was pinched out, the codebook sealed again against her ribs, and let the dread arrive properly, the way you let cold water into a bath—gradually, all at once. Because here was the truth none of the lacquered trunks would carry: this mission did not matter. The armistice would fail or succeed based on armies and granaries and which foreign crowns decided the Republic was worth more intact than carved. Forty-three people traveling east through the snow were theater. Everyone in the delegation knew it, from the lowest groom to Minister Corvane himself, and everyone pretended otherwise, because pretense was the only flag still flying.

And yet she was here. Carrying a book worth a nation's secrets, wearing her brother's name like armor, lying with her whole body every hour of every day—here, in the freezing loft of a relay station in a country that had maybe two winters left in it. Why? Duty, she had told the Chancellery. Duty, she told herself on the bad nights.

But duty was a word for a thing, and the thing itself, if she was honest—and honesty, in the loft, alone, was cheap—was smaller and more stubborn. It was that the Republic was the last thing her mother had believed in, the last thing her father had pruned his trees under, the last country where Tomas had ever been alive. Serving it was the closest she could come to serving the dead. You did not abandon the house while the bodies were still warm in it.

She slept badly, and dreamed of orchards under snow, and woke before dawn with the twelfth page already repeating itself behind her eyes like a psalm.

* * *

On the third day they reached the river, and the river was frozen wrong—frozen in plates and shelves, black water showing through the seams, the kind of ice that took sledges down and drowned the teams. The ferry was chained up for winter. The bridge at Vell, thirty miles north, was the only crossing, which meant the whole delegation funneled north along a single road through territory that had changed hands twice in eighteen months and would change hands again before spring.

At the tollhouse where the road bent toward the hills, an officer of the Republic's Border Service flagged them down—not for papers, but for warmth, he claimed, stamping his feet and accepting mulled wine from the ministerial steward. He was young and friendly and his questions were soft and his eyes were quick, and Sebastian, perched on the coach box pretending to check a strap, watched him catalog the convoy the way a butcher catalogs a carcass.

He came to her last, because couriers were furniture.

"Papers, friend?"

She handed them over. Name, rank of convenience, authorization under the Chancellor's seal. The officer read them upside-down first, a habit she recognized and respected—he was checking whether she flinched at her own name spelled backward—then right side up.

"Underhill." He smiled. "Any relation to the Underhills of the Orchard Company?"

"The name's common," she said.

"Oh, certainly." He handed the papers back, still smiling. "Common as apples."

He waved the coach on, and she sat very straight on the box with her heart going hard against the document case, harder than any cipher table had ever made it, and understood two things at once. First, that the Border Service was keeping lists of names, which meant someone above the Border Service wanted lists of names, which meant the hunt had already begun inside the Republic for whoever would eventually betray it—as someone always eventually did.

And second, that somewhere ahead, past the frozen river and the contested hills, at the end of this road, there was a place called Vell with a bridge and an estate and an enemy waiting across a table—

—and that she was carrying, against her ribs, in oilcloth and memory and grief, everything anyone would ever need to destroy her.
