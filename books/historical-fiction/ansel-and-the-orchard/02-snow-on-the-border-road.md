## 2. Snow on the Border Road

By the third day out of the capital, the snow had learned the shape of the road.

It drifted in the wheel ruts first, then along the verges where the pines leaned in, and finally across the road itself in long pale tongues that the lead horses broke through with a sound like tearing linen. Sebastian rode at the rear of the convoy, as befitted a courier of no rank and less consequence, and she watched that sound repeat itself—*tear, tear, tear*—and thought it was very much like the sound the Republic was making as it came apart, only slower, and with more dignity.

The delegation numbered forty-one souls when it had left Vessarine: nine ministers of varying importance, their secretaries and clerks, an archivist whose name appeared on no manifest because couriers did not appear on manifests, and two dozen troopers of the Fourth Border Hussars under a captain named Ilić who had the look of a man who had already decided how he would die and found the decision restful. They traveled in six coaches and a baggage wagon, flying the Republic's blue-and-silver from every pole, though the silk had been dyed in a hurry and the silver bled gray at the seams when it rained.

Sebastian rode a dun mare called Pigeon, who had been assigned to her precisely because she was ugly, slow, and beneath anyone's envy. This suited her admirably. In four years of carrying dispatches for the Foreign Ministry she had learned the central paradox of her profession: the safest courier was the one nobody bothered to look at, and nobody bothered to look at women dressed as men unless they were pretty, or tall, or memorable in some way she had trained herself never to be. She slouched. She kept her voice low and her laugh lower. She bound herself each morning before dawn, alone behind the baggage wagon, in the cold dark, with the methodical patience of a woman dressing a wound.

The codebook rode against her ribs, sewn into the lining of a second waistcoat she never removed. It was not a large book—smaller than a psalter—but it contained the current cipher tables for all Republican diplomatic traffic, the recognition phrases for the next quarter, and the emergency instructions for what the delegation should do if the capital fell while they were abroad. That last section had been added in fresh ink, the pages still smelling faintly of the press room, and the fact of it sat in her stomach like a swallowed stone. Governments did not print instructions for their own funerals unless they had begun to hear the bells.

She had memorized the book twice over by now, once forward and once by table, so that even if the pages were taken she would carry them still. This was the discipline Master Orenn had drilled into his couriers: *the body is the last vault, and the only one that cannot be forced open without killing the clerk.* He had said it cheerfully, the way old men say terrible things.

Ahead of her, the ministers' argument rose and fell like weather.

They were arguing about precedence again—or rather, they were arguing about seating arrangements at the eventual conference table, which was the same thing wearing different clothes. Minister Corvane's voice carried even through coach walls, dry and precise as a surgeon's probe. He held the portfolio for the interior, which in the Republic's dying grammar meant he held the police, the censors, and the files on everyone else in the delegation. He argued the way ice forms: slowly, inevitably, and always in the direction of covering more surface.

Against him was old Vasseur of the Treasury, who had been negotiating treaties since before Corvane could write, and who fought every point not because he cared about it but because yielding anything to Corvane felt, to him, like the beginning of yielding everything. Between them fluttered young Minister Aune, whose specialty was agriculture and who had been included, Sebastian suspected, because someone in the capital believed a delegation that looked doomed ought at least to look complete.

"Ostrava will seat us alphabetically or not at all," Vasseur was saying, pitched for the whole column to hear, because Vasseur believed privacy was a form of weakness.

"Ostrava," Corvane replied, "will seat us however we make it expedient for them to seat us, and if you arrive having spent your strength on chairs—" something inaudible, then, "—then we have lost before the wine is poured."

Sebastian let the words wash past her and catalogued them instead, the way she catalogued everything: not for meaning but for weight. Corvane spoke of *expedient*. Vasseur spoke of *arrive*. Aune said nothing at all, which meant either that he agreed with whoever had spoken last or that he had already begun composing the letter in which he would claim he had opposed them both.

The guards were easier to read than the ministers, and she read them steadily, because in contested territory the guards were the true map of a convoy's loyalties.

Ilić commanded the hussars, and the hussars answered to him, and he answered—she believed—to the army, which these days meant to whatever general held the capital's gates. But four of his troopers had been attached to the mission only at the last moment, over his objection, on papers signed by the Interior Ministry. These four rode together, ate together, laughed at jokes no one else heard, and reported each evening not to Ilić but directly to Corvane's secretary, a soft-spoken man named Drell whose hands were always cold and who never took notes, which meant he remembered everything. Sebastian had made a small study of Drell. Drell studied everyone. It seemed only courteous to return the attention.

Then there was Brant, the sergeant who rode rear guard beside her most days—a heavy, red-faced man with a farmer's hands and a deserter's eyes, who had told her on the first evening, apropos of nothing, that his village was three days east of the border and that his mother kept bees. Sebastian had filed this away too. Men who told you about their mothers were telling you where their surrender lay.

"You're quiet today, Underhill," Brant said now, matching Pigeon's plodding gait to his own grey gelding.

"I'm always quiet, Sergeant."

"That's what I mean." He squinted against the wind. "Quiet ones are either thinking or hiding. Which is it?"

"Thinking," she said, and gave him the flat half-smile she kept for exactly such moments—the smile of a man with nothing behind his face—and asked after the bees. He talked about the bees for a mile and a half, and by the end of it she knew that the hive had died two winters ago and that he blamed the war's requisitions, and that this grievance sat closer to his heart than any flag. She nodded in the right places. Inside, where no one could see, she noted the cost of the exchange: another hour spent being a man named Sebastian Underhill, another layer laid over the woman underneath, until sometimes at night, unwinding the bindings, she caught herself surprised by what the mirror returned—as if the disguise had grown inward as well as outward.

That was the arithmetic nobody warned you about. Invisibility kept you alive, and invisibility erased you, and there was no exchange rate between the two currencies. You paid in yourself.

* * *

The border came without ceremony, which frightened her more than a gate would have.

There had been a customs post here once—she'd seen it on the survey maps, a squat stone house with a barrier arm and a flagpole—but what remained of it was a blackened shell with the roof fallen inward, its stones picked over by scavengers, its well choked with something she chose not to examine. The Republic's blue-and-white boundary marker stood tilted in the drifts like a headstone no one visited. Someone had painted over the Republican eagle with tar. The tar had cracked, and the eagle showed through anyway, one golden eye glaring up out of the darkness.

"Contested territory begins here," Captain Ilić announced, riding down the column, "and ends at the Vell crossroads, eleven miles on. From here to there we are guests of nobody. Ride close, ride armed, and if anyone hails us, no one answers but me."

The land itself changed character, or perhaps only seemed to. The farms they passed had that particular desolation of places fought over twice: fields unplowed going on two seasons, orchards cut for firewood down to ugly stumps, a church with its bell gone—melted for shot, probably, on one side or the other—and its door standing open to the snow. Crows worked the ditches. Once, far off across a white field, she saw figures moving, too distant to be soldiers or refugees with any confidence, and watched them until the road bent and took them away.

Brant crossed himself without appearing to notice he'd done it.

Near dusk they saw the checkpoint.

It occupied a bend in the road where a stone bridge crossed a frozen stream, and whoever held it had chosen well: approach visible for a quarter mile in every direction, the bridge narrow enough that six coaches must pass in single file, timber barricades dragged across both ends. There were perhaps thirty men there, wrapped in greatcoats of no uniform she recognized—mixed grays and browns, armbands of faded green—and above the barricade flew a plain white banner with nothing on it at all, which in the current war could mean neutral militia, bandits, Ostravan irregulars, or deserters from three different armies who had decided to become a government of their own.

"Hold," Ilić called, and the column halted with the discipline of men who understood that the alternative to holding was dying.

What followed took two hours in the falling dark, and Sebastian spent both of them learning the exact texture of fear.

A parley was struck. Ilić went forward under his own white kerchief and spoke a long time with a lean man in a wolf-fur cap who commanded the checkpoint and whose name, gathered in fragments from the wind, was Sorrel. Terms were shouted back: the convoy might pass, but it would be searched—not the ministers, whose persons were inviolable by custom even here, but the staff, the baggage, the troopers. Every man of the escort would be looked over. Weapons stacked. Papers examined.

Sebastian stood with the other clerks by the baggage wagon and felt the codebook against her ribs as though it had begun to burn through wool and skin.

They searched the coaches first. She watched Sorrel's men go through the ministers' trunks with a rough courtesy that stopped short of the ministers themselves, lifting out linens and ledgers, unrolling documents Vasseur protested over with magnificent outrage until Corvane, in a low voice, told him to let them. She watched Drell stand apart with his cold hands folded, watching the searchers in turn, and she saw him mark which items they lingered on—which meant that afterward, Drell would know exactly what Sorrel's people valued, which meant Drell was working even now, even here, turning humiliation into inventory.

Then it was the turn of the staff and escort.

They lined them up along the bridge rail in the lantern light: clerks and secretaries and troopers, twenty-odd men breathing steam into the cold, while Sorrel's men moved down the line with the bored thoroughness of professionals. Papers first. Then a patting-down that was rougher for the troopers, lighter for the clerks, but nowhere light enough.

Sebastian had prepared for this the way she prepared for everything: not with hope but with architecture. Her papers identified her as Sebastian Underhill, courier, fourth grade, born in the town of Marren—she could produce Marren's streets, its millrace, its two taverns, its annual flood, if pressed. Her coat pockets held nothing but a pipe she did not smoke, tobacco she did not use, a knife, and forty crowns in mixed coin, because a courier with no money was a courier smuggling something worth more than money. The bindings under her shirt were flat, layered, and had survived two searches before this one. The codebook was the only thing that could hang her, and the codebook was sewn where a searcher's hands would find only the shape of a chest.

But shapes could be noticed. Flatness could be noticed. And the searcher coming toward her now was a big man with careful eyes and hands like paddles, and he was not hurrying.

"Papers."

She handed them over. He read them longer than they deserved, looking between the page and her face, doing the ordinary arithmetic of suspicion. She let her shoulders curve, let her gaze go to a middle distance just over his left ear—the look of a man who has been searched many times and expects nothing better. Boredom, correctly performed, was the best armor she owned.

"Courier."

"Yes."

"For who?"

"The delegation. Foreign Ministry. Fourth grade." She recited it like a litany, because litanies are what they expect.

He grunted and put the papers away and began on her coat. Pipe. Tobacco pouch—he opened it and smelled it and frowned, because the tobacco was real but barely used; she had thought of that too, and rubbed a little into her cuffs each morning. Knife. Coin. Then his hands moved to her chest, quick and professional, pressing the ribs, the sternum—

And that was when the shouting started.

It came from the coaches—Vasseur's voice, cracking with fury, and then Corvane's underneath it, smooth and cold, and then Sorrel answering in a bark that carried across the whole bridge. One of the searchers had opened a strongbox belonging to Minister Aune and found inside it not agricultural reports but letters of credit drawn on a bank in a neutral country—enough, from the size of the box and the quality of the silence that followed, to buy a great deal of neutrality.

Every lantern turned toward the coaches. Every eye. Even her searcher's hands stopped, flat against her ribs, feeling—she would swear later—feeling the edge of the second waistcoat's seam through her shirt, one heartbeat, two—

"Sorrel!" Corvane's voice again, pitched now to carry, silky with contempt. "Your man has found something interesting. Perhaps you'd like to explain why your people think a minister of the Vessarine Republic travels with a banker's strongbox. Or perhaps"—and here the pause, the surgical pause—"we should discuss what your own men carry, since we've seen at least three Ostravan army rifles among your 'militia,' and my captain counted the serial stamps."

The checkpoint's attention swung wholesale to the coaches. Sorrel strode away, bellowing for the strongbox. The big searcher withdrew his hands, spat, muttered something about ministers, and jerked his chin at her: *move along.*

Sebastian moved along. Her legs performed walking. Somewhere behind her sternum her heart was going like a bird in a closed room, and the codebook lay against it warm as a living thing.

At the far end of the bridge, once the coaches had rattled across and the barricades were dragged aside and the convoy re-formed in the blue-black snowlight, Brant fell in beside her again and shook his head with a grin.

"Well, Underhill. You see that? Old Corvane bought us out of a searching with a scandal. Clever devil." He lowered his voice, pleased to be sharing conspiracy. "You know what I heard? That wasn't Aune's money at all. That box went into the coach at the last relay. Somebody *planted* it—gave the Ostravans a reason to dig through our baggage, so they'd find what Corvane wanted found and stop looking for what he didn't."

"Who'd want that?" Sebastian asked, in the tone of a man making conversation.

Brant shrugged, delighted. "Who indeed? Man who controls what gets searched controls what stays hidden, my father used to say." He laughed. "Bees, same principle. You move the swarm by controlling the entrance."

She smiled her flat smile and said the bees sounded wiser than the Foreign Ministry, and Brant laughed again, and the column rolled on through the dark toward Vell, and Sebastian rode with the codebook against her heart and a new understanding settling over her colder than the snow:

Corvane had used a planted strongbox to control a search. Which meant Corvane controlled searches. Which meant that somewhere ahead, at the estate where they would quarter, in the talks where she would sit transcribing minutes a few feet from his chair, the same hand that had arranged tonight's diversion would be arranging others—and the next time the net was cast, there would be no convenient scandal to pull it away.

Behind them the checkpoint's lanterns shrank to embers and went out. Ahead, somewhere past the frozen rows and the dark hills, Vell waited with its half-abandoned house and its orchard sleeping under the snow.

She did not yet know who would come asking for the archivist there. She only knew, riding into that darkness with her false name and her borrowed body and her stolen safety, that the price of being invisible was rising—and that sooner or later, someone would ask her to pay it in full.
