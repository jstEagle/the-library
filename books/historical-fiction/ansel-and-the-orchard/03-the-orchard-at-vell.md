## 3. The Orchard at Vell

The estate announced itself before they saw it: a smell of cold stone and old smoke rising off the hillside like breath from a sleeping animal. Sebastian rode at the rear of the column, where couriers were meant to ride—invisible among the baggage wagons—and watched the house of Vell assemble itself out of the grey afternoon piece by piece. First the chimneys, six of them, crooked as broken fingers. Then the slate roof furred with moss. Then the long façade itself, shuttered and pale, its windows reflecting nothing but cloud.

"Half the staff ran when the war came," said the local guide, a stooped man in a sheepskin coat who had met them at the crossroads. "The rest are old or slow. The master went to the capital to serve the Republic and never wrote again. The orchard's gone wild."

"The orchard," repeated Minister Corvane, riding forward with his collar up, "is irrelevant. Show me the cellars and the strong rooms. We will need somewhere secure for the documents."

Sebastian looked past him, past the whole fractious knot of the delegation—ministers in city wool, guards in mismatched uniforms, clerks already complaining about damp—to the slope below the house, where rows of bare trees stood in the falling snow like a congregation waiting for a sermon that had been cancelled years ago.

An orchard. Here, at the edge of everything.

She kept her face blank, the way she kept it blank through checkpoints and searches and the nightly ritual of undressing behind a screen while other men talked loudly about women they'd known. But something in her chest moved, quiet and enormous, like ice shifting on a river.

They quartered by lamplight. Corvane claimed the ground-floor study for himself and locked it twice. The senior ministers took the rooms facing the road, so that any arriving courier would pass their doors first. Sebastian was assigned a servant's room under the eaves, narrow as a coffin, which suited her exactly. She hung her coat on the single nail, set her boots beneath the window where she could reach them in the dark, and lay down with the codebook wrapped in oilcloth strapped against her ribs even in sleep, its edges printing themselves into her dreams. She had stopped noticing it the way one stops noticing one's own heartbeat. That, too, was a kind of armor.

The delegation would rest two days while outriders scouted the road ahead. Two days of ministers arguing over seating arrangements for negotiations that might never happen, of clerks copying letters no one would read, of everyone pretending, with increasing desperation, that this mission mattered—that a handful of exhausted men in a shuttered house could still stand between the Vessarine Republic and its ending.

Sebastian waited until the second morning, until the household had settled into its rhythms and the guards into their cards and drink, and then she pulled on her coat and went out through the kitchen garden, past a collapsed cold frame, down the frozen terraces to the orchard.

* * *

The snow lay unbroken between the rows. No one had walked here since the last snowfall; perhaps no one since the year before. She stood a moment at the head of the first row, listening to the particular silence of an orchard in winter—the held breath of it, the trees standing patient with their buds sealed tight as secrets—and then she stepped in, and the snow took her prints behind her like a debt being paid.

The trees were apple, mostly. Some pear. They had not been pruned in three years at least; watershoots climbed from the crowns in pale green whips, dead wood hung in the forks, and mistletoe had begun its slow theft in the upper branches of the oldest row. A neglected orchard did not die quickly. It declined the way the Republic was declining—not with a collapse but with a thousand small surrenders, each one survivable, none of them reversible.

She found what she needed in the tool shed beside the wall: pruning shears gone to rust but sound at the joint, a folding knife with a blade someone had honed soft, a coil of tarred twine. The shed smelled of creosote and mice and, faintly, impossibly, of apples—years of apples soaked into the timber, a ghost of sweetness that made her stop with her hand on the doorframe and breathe until her eyes stung.

Then she worked.

She worked the way she had worked every winter of her childhood, because her hands knew the way even when her heart insisted it had forgotten. Dead wood out first—she could read it by color, by the dullness of the bark, by the way it resisted the shears with a dry, apologetic snap instead of living green. Then the crossing branches, the ones that rubbed each other raw and let rot in through the wound. Then the watershoots, all that desperate vertical ambition, cut back to keep the tree's strength in its fruiting spurs. Snow slid from the branches onto her shoulders and down her collar and she did not feel it. Her breath came in white plumes. Somewhere above the orchard a crow argued with another crow about territory, and the argument went on and on without conclusion, which struck her as the most honest conversation she had witnessed in months.

By midmorning her fingers ached and her cheeks burned and four trees stood corrected against the snow, their crowns opened to light, and she realized she had been humming. An old tune, a harvest tune, the kind sung by people pressing cider with their sleeves rolled up. She shut her mouth so fast she nearly bit her tongue.

No one sang here. No one hummed. Courier Underhill was a thin, forgettable man with ink-stained fingers and a cough he exaggerated whenever anyone came near him, and thin forgettable men did not stand in abandoned orchards singing harvest songs with tears freezing in the lines around their eyes.

She wiped her face with the heel of her glove and kept working. Tenderness, she had learned, was not the opposite of armor. Done properly, tenderness *was* armor—it gave you a reason to be careful, a reason to be silent, a shape worth protecting. Every branch she pruned was a brick in the wall she had built around the girl she used to be.

* * *

Her brother Tomas had taught her to graft when she was nine years old and all elbows, in an orchard very much like this one and yet belonging entirely to another world: the family orchard at Merrow's Bend, forty trees on a south-facing slope behind a farmhouse with blue shutters, back when the shutters were painted every spring and the Republic was a thing you believed in the way you believed in weather—distant, occasionally inconvenient, fundamentally reliable.

It was the last real winter before the war. She remembered it that way now, though at the time it had simply been winter: Tomas home from his apprenticeship in the capital, nineteen and full of himself, his hands already a journeyman's hands, scarred and sure.

"Hold it like a bird," he said. He had her wrist in his grip, correcting the angle of the little knife. "Firm enough it can't fly, gentle enough you don't crush it. Everything worth doing is like that. Remember that and you'll be smarter than half the ministers in Vessarine."

The scion was a finger-length of applewood, cut from the Reine des Reinettes at the top of the slope—the tree their grandfather had planted, the one that ripened latest and kept longest, whose fruit their mother swore tasted better after the first frost. The rootstock waited in the ground at Tomas's knee, its top cut clean, ready to receive.

"Now watch. You split the stock—here, see, one clean stroke, don't saw at it, sawing is doubt made visible—" he grinned at her, because this was a joke between them, because Papa said it, because Papa had learned it from Grandfather, and suddenly she understood that she was not being taught a skill, she was being handed a inheritance disguised as a chore. "—and you cut the scion to a wedge. Two cuts. Both faces green. If the inside is brown you've cut deadwood and you start again, no mourning, no excuses."

"Two cuts," she said, "both faces green."

"Tongue of the scion into the tongue of the stock." He fitted the two pieces together with the tenderness other men spent on jewelry, and there it was: the seam almost invisible, green pressed to green, the cambium layers kissing along their length. "That's the whole secret, Seb. That layer. Cambium. It looks like nothing—a skin, a wet line—but it's the only part of the tree that's actually alive in the way that matters. Bark is just clothing. Wood is just furniture. The cambium is the tree's soul, and if you get soul touching soul, the wound heals over and the two trees become one tree. Different roots, different crown, one life. Understand?"

She nodded, not understanding at all, understanding completely.

"Wrap it. Twine, then wax, then patience. And then—" he tapped her forehead with one scarred knuckle, "—the hard part. You wait. You do nothing. You walk past it every day and you don't touch it, don't dig it up to check, don't fuss. Most people can't bear it. Most people kill the graft with loving it too much. The tree knows what to do. Your job is to protect the conditions and stay out of the way."

"And if it takes?"

He smiled, and for a moment the journeyman arrogance fell away and he was just her brother, windburnt, missing a tooth he'd lost to a stone in a bread roll, the person in the world she loved most absolutely and least wisely. "If it takes, in spring you'll see the bud swell. One bud. It'll look like nothing. Like the whole thing failed and died and rotted. And then one morning there's this—this small green fist, opening. Best sight there is. Better than anything they've got in the capital."

"What are we growing?"

"A new tree. Old fruit, new roots." He waxed the seam with his thumb, smoothing it flat. "Grandfather's apple on stock that can survive the wet low field. The old tree won't last forever, you know. Nothing does. But this—" he patted the earth around the graft, "—this goes on. You're not growing an apple, Seb. You're growing a continuity."

She had thought he was being dramatic. Nineteen-year-olds were dramatic. She had rolled her eyes and stolen his knife and run down the slope with him shouting after her, and the whole afternoon had dissolved into snowball warfare among the bare rows, and their mother had called them in to supper, and the candle had burned low, and none of it—none of it—had seemed like anything but an ordinary day in an ordinary life that would go on being ordinary forever.

Fourteen months later the war took the valley. Five years later it took everything else. And Tomas—

Tomas was dead. Everyone said so. The last dispatch from his regiment had listed him among the fallen at some river whose name she refused to learn by heart, and grief had eventually done what grief does: not healed but calloused, grown a hard smooth place over the wound so she could grip things again without screaming.

But her hands remembered grafting. Her hands remembered everything.

* * *

She worked until the light went thin and blue, and by then she had cleared seven trees and built a small brush fire of deadwood at the row's end, warming her hands over it, watching the sparks climb. From her coat pocket she took out the folding knife—the one she'd bought in the capital with her first courier's wage, chosen deliberately, a copy of Tomas's old one down to the worn horn handle—and a length of scionwood she had cut that afternoon from the healthiest tree, a pear, its buds dormant and tight.

She didn't know why she'd cut it. That was a lie; she knew exactly why. She cut it because the delegation would move on in two days and the orchard would return to its neglect, and because some stubborn rootstock in her own chest refused, absolutely refused, to let a living thing be wasted merely because the world around it had stopped making sense.

*Two cuts. Both faces green.*

She split the twig, seated the wedge, wrapped the join with twine from her pocket. There was nowhere to plant it—nowhere permanent, nowhere that would be hers in a week—but she did it anyway, the way she prayed, the way she memorized the codebook, the way she did everything now: not because it changed anything, but because refusing to do it would mean the war had taken more than it had.

A continuity, Tomas had said. Old fruit, new roots.

"You'd laugh at me," she told the fire quietly. "Courier Underhill, kneeling in the snow doing surgery on a stick." The sparks rose. "You'd laugh, and then you'd check my cambium alignment, and you'd say it was adequate, which was how you said well done."

The wind moved down the rows, and the bare branches stirred with a sound like distant applause, or like rain that hadn't decided to fall.

She was wrapping the grafted twig in a scrap of cloth, preparing—absurdly, hopelessly—to carry it with her, the way she carried everything else that mattered against her body where no search would find it, when she heard the crunch of boots on snow at the head of the row.

She was on her feet instantly, knife closed, shoulders rounded into the courier's slouch, the whole disguise snapping on like a mask. One of the delegation's outriders? A guard sent to fetch her? Her mind raced through explanations—checking the grounds, gathering kindling, a man could be excused a sentimental hour in an orchard if he laughed at himself first—

But the figure standing at the end of the row wore no uniform she recognized. Grey coat, travel-worn. A white cloth tied at the left arm, stark against the dark sleeve. A parley flag, small and folded, hanging motionless from his gloved hand.

Behind him, far off toward the road, she could hear voices—one of the gate guards shouting for an officer, the sudden clatter of the delegation waking to alarm.

The stranger stood quite still in the failing light, snow settling on his shoulders, and looked directly at her across seven rows of pruned and waiting trees.

"I'm looking for the delegation's archivist," he called. His voice carried cleanly in the cold, unhurried, as if he had all the time in the world and intended to use it. "I'm told he keeps unusual hours."

Sebastian's hand, hidden in her pocket, closed around the grafted twig until the twine bit her palm.
