## 4. A Stranger at the Gate

The horn sounded an hour past noon, three long notes that carried across the frozen parkland and into the house like a blade slipped between ribs.

Sebastian heard it from the archive, where she had spent the morning cataloguing the delegation's correspondence by candlelight because the estate's windows were too thin for the cold and too obvious to any watcher on the road. She set down her pen. Around her, the room held its breath—she could feel it in the way the junior clerks stopped shuffling papers, in the way Minister Corvane's aide paused halfway through a sentence in the corridor outside, his voice hanging unfinished in the air like smoke.

Then the house woke all at once. Boots on stairs. The rattle of a sabre being drawn somewhere above. A voice shouting for the guard captain, another calling for hot water as though a parley could be served like tea.

She should have kept cataloguing. That was the discipline of invisibility: when the world lurched, the invisible person did not lurch with it. But her hands had already closed the ledger, already smoothed the front of her coat, already betrayed her with their readiness—and she stood there in the candle-smelling dark of the archive and admitted, with a kind of weary honesty, that some part of her had been waiting for this horn since they crossed the border.

* * *

From the western gallery window she saw them come up the drive: four riders under a white flag stiff with frost, moving at a walk through the dead gardens as though the ground itself might be trapped. Ostravan grey, but muted, travel-worn—the colors of men who had ridden far and did not wish to be mistaken for anything but what they claimed to be.

At their head rode a man whose posture she noticed before his face. He sat his horse the way Tomas used to sit one, not perched upon it but joined to it, spine easy, reins held loose in fingers that trusted the animal more than they needed to. The other three rode like soldiers. He rode like someone remembering how.

The delegation assembled in the entrance hall to receive him, which meant the ministers arranged themselves by precedence while everyone else pressed against walls, and Sebastian took her place among the clerks with her eyes down and her ears open. Corvane stood at the front in his best coat, silver-grey, buttoned high, wearing the expression he reserved for moments he intended to profit from—a look Sebastian had come to think of as his hunting face. Beside him, old Minister Halvard coughed into a handkerchief, and young Vessik, whose family owned half the river trade, checked his reflection in a tarnished breastplate held by a servant.

The doors opened and the cold came in with the strangers.

He was younger than she expected. Not boyish—there was nothing soft in him—but young in the way a blade is young: new-edged, unbloodied, or at least careful about showing where it had been blooded before. Frost clung to the collar of his greatcoat. He removed his gloves finger by finger, unhurried, and gave the assembled ministers a bow that was precise without being deferential, the bow of a man acknowledging rank while privately reserving judgment on the people holding it.

"Captain Ansel Reyne," he said. His accent was northern Ostravan, clipped at the vowels. "Under flag of parley, at the direction of Marshal Ostrev. I'm instructed to request a preliminary conference regarding terms."

"Terms," Corvane repeated, tasting the word. "You ride four days through contested country to discuss *terms*? Your marshal sends one officer?"

"My marshal sends whom he trusts," Reyne said, and let the silence do its work.

Something shifted in the hall—a small recalibration among the ministers, the sound of men revising estimates upward. Sebastian watched Corvane's jaw tighten by a fraction and thought: *he doesn't like being outmaneuvered in the opening move.* She filed it away with everything else she filed away, in the crowded drawer behind her eyes where she kept the delegation's secrets alongside her own.

But then Captain Reyne did something no one expected, least of all her.

"While my credentials are verified," he said, "I have a separate matter. A private one." His gaze moved along the line of ministers, past them, past the guards, past the servants pressed against the wainscoting—and stopped, briefly, on the cluster of clerks. On her. Or near her; she couldn't be sure, and the uncertainty felt like a hand closing around her throat. "It concerns your archivist. I'm given to understand the delegation keeps one."

A pause, in which the whole room rearranged itself around the word *archivist.*

"Our records are sealed," Corvane said smoothly. Too smoothly, Sebastian thought. "Whatever your marshal imagines our archives contain—"

"Not your archives, Minister. Your *archivist.*" Reyne's tone didn't change, but something underneath it did—a patience stretched taut, like wire. "There are documents that must pass through such a person's hands. It's a courtesy of protocol. My government extends it; I assume yours does not forbid it."

Corvane's hunting face flickered. Sebastian saw him weighing it: refusing a protocol courtesy before talks began would be a concession of bad faith; granting it meant surrendering control of some channel he hadn't known existed. Either way, the Ostravan had drawn first.

"The archivist will receive you," Corvane said at last, each word laid down like a stone. "In the library. Under supervision."

"Of course," said Reyne, and bowed again.

And Sebastian understood, with the cold certainty of a woman who has spent years learning to read rooms, that whatever was coming had been aimed at her from the moment the horn sounded.

* * *

They gave him the library because it was the warmest room with two exits, and they gave him a guard at each exit because Corvane trusted no one, least of all courtesies. Sebastian was sent for twenty minutes later, time enough to compose herself—or so the messenger believed. She spent the twenty minutes standing in the archive with her back against the shelf of treaty precedents, breathing the way Tomas had taught her to breathe before recitations: in for four counts, hold, out for seven, until the heart agreed to be governed.

*You'll need a still heart in this life, little sister,* he'd told her, eleven years old and solemn as a magistrate, grafting knife in hand. *Everything else can be trained around it.*

Her heart was not still now. Her heart was a bird throwing itself against the inside of her ribs, and she made it walk down the corridor anyway, past the guards, into the library, and closed the door behind her with a click that sounded, in the quiet, like a lock turning.

Captain Reyne stood at the window with his back to her, watching the orchard—the frozen rows of it, black-limbed against the snow, the same view she had tended every dawn since their arrival. He didn't turn immediately. When he did, she saw that his face was carefully arranged into official blankness, and that beneath the arrangement he was studying her with an attention that made her glad of the binding across her chest and the ash rubbed into her jawline that morning, the small dishonesties that made a courier named Sebastian out of whatever she had been born.

"You're the archivist," he said.

"I keep the delegation's records." Her voice came out level. She had rehearsed it. "Courier and clerk, also, as needed. How may I serve?"

He crossed to the table where she'd instinctively placed herself, notebook open, pen ready—the armor of usefulness. Up close she could see the fatigue under his composure, the road worn into him: chapped lips, a healing split across one knuckle, eyes that had been open too many nights. He set a leather dispatch case on the table between them but did not open it.

"Before we begin," he said, "I'm required to confirm the identity of the recipient. Protocol." And then, quietly, almost gently, as though offering her a chance to run: "The documents are addressed by name."

"Then you have the wrong person," Sebastian said, "because I have no name your government knows."

"I think I do." He opened the case.

Inside lay letters. Three of them, tied with plain cord, creased from long carrying, soft at the edges from handling—as if someone had taken them out and read them and put them back, over and over, on roads and in camps, in whatever cold places such reading happened. He untied the cord with those careful fingers and laid the topmost letter on the table, facing her, and stepped back to give her room, the way one steps back from a patient or a mourner.

Sebastian looked down at the handwriting and the world went narrow and ringing, the library receding to a gray tunnel with a single point of light at its center.

She knew that hand.

She had grown up inside that hand. It was on the flyleaves of every book in her childhood room, on the labels of the grafting jars, on the mock land-grants he'd written giving her the north field and all its imaginary apples. A hand that slanted left, impatient, with a peculiar double-crossed *t* like a fence rail, and an *S* that always looked mid-flight, about to go somewhere and never quite arriving.

*Tomas's hand.* Written in ink that was not faded, not forty-years-old, not ghost-written by grief. Recent. Alive. Dated within the season.

"No," she said. She hadn't meant to speak aloud. The word came out cracked down the middle, and she heard her own voice break register, drop toward something lower and truer than Sebastian's voice was permitted to be, and she clamped down on it too late.

Reyne heard it. She watched him hear it—watched something move behind his eyes that was not triumph, exactly. More like sorrow taking notes.

"Sebastian," he said, using the courier's name deliberately, keeping her secret even as he shattered it, and she would remember that later, would hold onto it in the days to come like a plank in floodwater. "Read it. Please. I've carried these a long way."

Her hands were shaking so badly she could barely work the fold. Inside, the familiar slant ran down the page, and she read the opening line and the floor tilted:

*Little graft —*

Only one person alive called her that. Only one person alive knew why.

*I don't know if this reaches you, or in what shape, or whether you'll believe it. Ansel will tell you I'm alive. You'll want proof beyond his word, so here it is: the summer you were nine, I grafted Winter Russet onto bitter stock behind the smokehouse because Father said it wouldn't take, and it took, and we hid the fruit from him for three years and ate it in the loft and swore ourselves to secrecy on Mother's thimble. You cried when we cut the scions for practice and I told you a tree forgives a wound faster than it forgives neglect, and you wrote that down in your book like scripture.*

The letter blurred. She gripped the table's edge. Somewhere beyond the ringing in her ears, Captain Reyne was speaking—low, steady, giving her facts to hold onto the way one gives a drowning man rope—but the words arrived slowly, one at a time, through water:

*Your brother survived Kesselmoor. He has been in Ostravan protection since the spring after the battle. He is alive. He asks for you by the name only you would know. There are conditions, and none of them are mine to explain badly, so I won't try. Read the rest. Then decide.*

Decide. The word landed strangely, absurdly, amid the ruin of everything else—decide, as though she had any faculty left for decisions, as though she were not currently standing in a guarded library in enemy custody with her brother's living handwriting trembling under her thumb.

Because there it was, the thing beneath the joy, sharp as a grafting knife: *Ostravan protection.* Tomas lived because Ostrava allowed it. Whatever conditions waited in the rest of the letter, whatever price had purchased his breath, it had been paid in the currency she carried against her ribs in a codebook she had sworn to burn rather than surrender. Her brother's life and her nation's secrets, laid on the same scale by strangers, and her the only weight that could tip it either way.

She became aware, distantly, that she was crying—that tears had gotten past the ash and the discipline and were tracking down her face in full view of an Ostravan officer and two guards who could see everything through the door's glass panel if they cared to look. She turned her shoulder to the door. Old habit. Erasure as shelter.

"Captain Reyne." Her voice, when she found it, was low and scraped and entirely her own. "How long have you been carrying these?"

"Since autumn," he said. "Through two checkpoints and a week of weather I wouldn't wish on my horse." A pause. "Your brother said you'd know the handwriting better than anyone breathing. He said—" and here something in his composure frayed, just slightly, just at the edge, revealing the tired man underneath the officer— "he said if anyone in that house could tell true paper from forged, it was you. He said you'd been reading his hand since before you could read anyone else's."

"He would say that." She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, roughly, furious at the weakness and helpless before it. "He was insufferable about his own handwriting."

"What did you decide?" Reyne asked softly. "About believing it?"

Sebastian looked down at the letter again—at the double-crossed *t*, the fleeing *S*, the private scripture of a dead summer orchard—and felt the carefully constructed architecture of her anonymity, her safety, her entire disguised existence, crack straight through like ice under a boot.

"It's his," she said. "No one else alive could write this."

"Good." Reyne gathered the remaining letters, retied the cord, and held the bundle out to her—not slipping it to her slyly, not concealing it, simply offering it in plain sight, which was either profound trust or a trap of exquisite design. "These are yours to keep or burn. But the ministers will ask what passed between us, and I'll tell them it was archival protocol, and they'll believe me or they won't. So you should decide quickly, courier, which story you're going to live inside."

Beyond the frosted glass of the library door, boots were already coming down the corridor—Corvane's stride, unmistakable, heavy with purpose. Sebastian closed her hand around the letters, felt the cord bite her palm, and understood that whatever happened next, the woman who had walked into this room no longer existed.

The door handle turned.
