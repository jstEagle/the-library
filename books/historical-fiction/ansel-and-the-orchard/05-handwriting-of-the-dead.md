## 5. Handwriting of the Dead

The estate at Vell had a chapel once. Sebastian could tell by the shape of the smallest room off the east corridor—the arched window, the bracket where a saint's statue might have stood, the faint rectangle of unfaded plaster on the wall where some devotional picture had hung until the family that owned the place fled or died or simply stopped believing in anything worth hanging on a wall. Now the room held a broken chair, a stack of mildewed ledgers, and a candle stub in a saucer, and it held Sebastian Underhill, alone, with three letters she had no right to possess.

She had waited four hours for the house to settle.

It settled slowly, the way old houses do—creaking through their joints as the cold deepened, the guards outside changing watch with a scrape of boots and a murmured exchange she had memorized by now: *All quiet. All quiet.* Minister Corvane's lamp burned past midnight, as it always did; she could see its glow through the frost-ferned glass of his study window from her own narrow room, and she had learned to read his habits the way she'd once learned to read weather over the orchards at home. He slept badly and rose early and trusted no one, which made him dangerous in the particular way that careful men are dangerous. But even Corvane slept eventually. Even Corvane's lamp went out.

At half past one, it did.

Sebastian gave it another hour anyway. She sat on the edge of her cot with the letters sewn into the lining of her coat, and she did not take them out, because taking them out too soon was the kind of mistake that got couriers hanged, and she had spent six years being a person who did not make that kind of mistake. Instead she recited code tables to herself in the dark—*the Vessarine standard, third revision, column seven*—until the numbers blurred and became a kind of prayer, and then she listened to the house breathe, and when she finally heard nothing but wind moving through the frozen orchard outside, a sound like a thousand small bones shifting under snow, she took out the letters.

She had not opened them in the gatehouse. She had not opened them on the walk back through the corridors, though her hands had wanted to, had trembled with wanting to, so badly that she'd had to clench them behind her back like a soldier at attention just to keep them still. She had waited because waiting was discipline, and discipline was the only armor she owned.

Now, by the light of a stolen candle in a dead woman's chapel, she opened the first letter.

*Sebastian.*

Not *Courier*. Not the false name of the false man she had been for six years. Her name—the name only Tomas had ever used, shortened and softened, the name their mother had coined when Sebastian was small enough to answer to nothing else. Seeing it written there, in ink, in that hand, was like hearing a voice call to her across a frozen river. She put the page down. She pressed both palms flat against her thighs. She breathed.

Then she picked it up again and forced herself to do what she had come here to do: not to feel, but to verify.

Because that was the truth of what Ansel of Ostrava had handed her at the gate—not a gift, whatever it looked like, whatever it had done to her heart in the first unguarded moment before her training caught up with her. It was an instrument. It was either proof or bait, and the difference between those two things was the difference between her brother alive and her own corpse swinging from the delegation's makeshift gallows, and she would not—weary, hopeful, half-mad with six years of grief—she would *not* be taken in by a forgery, however beautiful, however cruel.

So she read like a clerk. She read like a spy. She read like a sister conducting an examination she dreaded failing more than she had ever dreaded anything.

*You'll remember,* the letter began, after her name, *that you were the worst liar in the household, and that I taught you everything I know about it, which is why you're better than me now.*

Her breath caught—but she made herself keep going, made herself look past the words to the hand beneath them.

The handwriting itself came first. Six years is long enough for a hand to change; war changes hands faster than almost anything. But this was Tomas's script the way a face is a face—the same forward lean, impatient, as if the words were always trying to outrun the pen. The same habit of crossing his *t*'s with a single slashing stroke that started low and climbed high, like a bird taking off. The same strange double loop he made in the letter *g*, which their schoolmaster had beaten out of every other child in the parish and never out of him. A forger could copy these things. A forger with time and samples could copy any of them.

But then—

*Do you still count fence posts when you're frightened? You used to do it standing at the end of the row, lips moving, and you thought nobody saw.*

Sebastian set the letter down again. The candle flame stood perfectly still in the dead air, and she wished it would flicker, wished something in the room would move besides her own chest rising and falling too fast.

Nobody had seen. That was the point of counting fence posts—that it was secret, that it was hers, a private superstition from the year their father died, when she had been nine and the world had needed counting to stay fixed. She had never told anyone. Not the delegation, not her superiors, not the handful of people in the capital who knew her true name. There was no document anywhere in the Republic that contained that detail. There was no way for Ostravan intelligence to know it unless—

Unless someone who had stood at the end of that row, watching a small girl move her lips, had told them.

*Second thing,* the letter continued. *Ask yourself about my left arm. And don't let anyone tell you the scar came from the thresher, because we both know that story was for the priest, and we both know what really happened, and if you're reading this then you're the only person alive who does.*

Sebastian pushed back her sleeve. In the candlelight, on her own forearm, the pale twin of that scar showed thin and silver—a burn, old, shaped like a crescent moon. She had been eleven. Tomas had been fourteen. They had been playing at alchemists in the ruined kiln behind the orchard with a pot of their mother's rendered tallow and a length of copper wire, and the wire had slipped, and the tallow had caught, and in the half-second before disaster Tomas had done the arithmetic of two children and one escape route and shoved her clear and taken the splash of burning fat across his own arm instead.

They had invented the thresher story together, sitting in the cold stream afterward, hissing with pain and laughing, because their mother would have killed them both if she'd known about the kiln. *We were never near the kiln,* Tomas had said, gripping her wrist hard enough to leave marks. *Swear it. We were at the thresher. We will be at the thresher until we die.* And they had kept that oath through inquests and neighbors' questions and years of family dinners, and it had become one of the load-bearing stones of their childhood, a secret shared by exactly two people in all the world.

One of whom, everyone had believed for six years, lay buried under a cairn at Kessel Field.

*Third,* the letter said. *If the man who brought you this is standing nearby, tell him from me that he owes me a bottle of plum brandy and has owed it since the autumn before last, and that I intend to collect.*

And then, lower on the page, cramped into the margin as if the writer had run short of paper and patience both:

*You'll want to know how I know grafting terms. Ask him about the whip-and-tongue. Ask him what I called the scions. He'll tell you. He tells the truth; it's his worst quality.*

Sebastian read the whole letter twice more, hunting for flaws the way she hunted for errors in coded dispatches—an inconsistency of phrasing, a date that didn't sit right, a word a brother wouldn't use. She found none. What she found instead, accumulating with each pass, was the unbearable texture of authenticity: the private jokes layered three deep, the references that pointed at nothing except shared history, the small cruelties and kindnesses of siblinghood preserved like insects in amber. *I hope you finally learned to lose at cards without cheating. I hope you're eating enough. I hope whoever you've become is kinder than either of us deserved to be.*

By the third reading she was crying, silently, the way she did everything—without witnesses, without sound, tears running down into the collar of her shirt while her face stayed composed and her hands stayed steady on the pages. This was the cost of invisibility, she thought, that even your grief had to be smuggled, carried in secret compartments, opened only in the dark. She cried for perhaps five minutes. Then she wiped her face on her sleeve, blew out nothing—there was nothing to blow out but her own weakness—and returned to work.

*

The second letter was longer, and stranger.

It was dated—she checked the watermark, the paper stock, the fading of the ink against known reference points in her memory of official correspondence—eighteen months ago. It was written in the same hand but with a different pressure, slower, more deliberate, a hand that had learned caution. And it was not addressed to her at all. It was addressed to *the officer commanding, Northern Parley Commission, Ostravan Third Army*, and it read less like a letter than like a deposition.

*I, Tomas Iversen, late lieutenant of the Seventh Vessarine Rifles, make the following statement of my own free will...*

She read it three times, and each time the floor of the world tilted a little further beneath her.

He had not died at Kessel Field. He had been captured—no. She stopped. She made herself read the exact words. *I did not surrender. I was pulled from the field wounded by civilians from the village of Hrast, who hid me through the winter. When I recovered, I chose not to return to Republican lines, for reasons I set out below.* And below, in careful, terrible prose, Tomas laid out what he had seen in the last months of his service: supply convoys diverted while front-line companies starved, orders falsified, a colonel selling ammunition allocations to smugglers, men sent to hold positions that command already knew were untenable because the positions' loss would justify a budget request. He named names. He named dates. He named, in one line that made Sebastian's blood go cold, *Minister Corvane's office, which processed the requisitions and took its percentage.*

He had defected, the statement said, because he could no longer serve a state that fed on its own soldiers. He had traded what he knew for protection. And the price of that protection—the condition stitched through every paragraph like wire through cloth—was silence. He lived under Ostravan guard. He would continue to live there exactly as long as Ostrava found him useful, and he would remain useful exactly as long as the war continued, and the moment the Republic collapsed in a way that made his testimony worthless—or, worse, made him a loose end—he would be worth more dead than alive to whichever faction inherited him.

*Your survival depends on our losing,* Sebastian whispered to the empty room, translating the bureaucratic language into its plain and monstrous meaning. *You live inside the enemy's victory. Every day the Republic stands, they have a reason to keep you breathing. The day it falls—*

The day it fell, he might simply disappear, another unrecorded body in a war full of them. Or he might be ransomed back to the Republic, where men like Corvane would try him for treason and use his execution to bury the testimony along with him.

Either way, the letters in her hands were not just proof of life. They were a clock. And somewhere beyond the frozen orchard, in the negotiation halls of Vell, that clock was ticking toward an armistice that would kill her brother as surely as a bullet—if the armistice succeeded.

She sat with that for a long time. The candle burned down another finger's width. Somewhere in the house a beam cracked with the cold, loud as a shot, and she did not flinch, because she had stopped flinching at sounds years ago, and it occurred to her only now, in this dead chapel with her dead-alive brother's confession in her lap, how much of herself she had amputated to survive this long, and how much more the coming weeks would demand.

*

The third letter was short. It was the newest—less than two months old—and it was the one that undid her completely, because it was not strategic, not legalistic, not a deposition or a proof or a plea. It was just a letter, from a brother to a sister, written by a man who did not know if it would ever reach her and had written it anyway.

*Little Seb. If you're reading this, then Ansel found you, which means you're wearing a uniform that isn't yours and answering to a name that isn't yours, and I'm sorry—I'm sorry for all of it, for leaving you alone in a country that makes women into ghosts to use them. I told him to find the courier, because I didn't know if my sister still existed anywhere but in my head. Forgive me. Come find me if you can. If you can't, plant something. Plant anything. That's how we'll know we're still talking.*

Beneath the signature, in the margin, a single line:

*Whip-and-tongue. You remember. You always cut the tongue too deep.*

She laughed. It came out of her like something breaking—a small, cracked, involuntary sound in the dark, and she clapped her hand over her mouth and sat shaking, laughing and weeping at once, because it was true, it was *true*, she had always cut the tongue too deep, Tomas had told her a hundred times in the orchard rows with the sun hot on their necks, *gentler, little Seb, the tongue carries the water, you're strangling it*—and no forger on earth, no intelligence service with a hundred captured documents, could have known that, could have known the specific way her knife had always bitten, the specific scold, the specific warmth underneath the scold.

He was alive. The knowledge arrived in her not as a thought but as a physical event, a warmth spreading through her chest like brandy, like spring, like the first thaw moving through frozen ground. *Tomas is alive.* For six years she had carried his death the way she carried the codebook, strapped against her ribs, a hard weight she had built her whole posture around. And now the weight was gone and she felt lighter and emptier and more terrified than she had ever been, because hope, she understood suddenly, was a far heavier thing to carry than grief. Grief asked nothing of you. Hope demanded everything.

And then, following close behind the hope, punctual as a debt collector, came the guilt.

Because the letters did not say only *your brother lives*. They said *your brother lives as a traitor*. They said *he bought his life with the Republic's secrets*. They said *his life is collateral, held by the enemy, payable only in defeat*. And they implied—no, they stated, in the careful language of the second letter—that the men she served, the ministers whose minutes she transcribed and whose codes she carried against her ribs, had starved and betrayed the very soldiers they'd sent to die, and that her brother's crime, the crime that would get him hanged if he ever set foot on Republican soil, was nothing more than telling the truth about it.

She was a courier of the Vessarine Republic. She had sworn—well. She had sworn nothing, officially, because official oaths required official identities, and she had none. But she had given six years of erasure, six years of binding and silence and counted fence posts in the dark, to the service of a state that had, according to her brother's testimony, been rotting from the head down the entire time. And now the state's survival and her brother's survival had been placed on opposite ends of a scale, and she was the only person alive holding both.

*What do I do?* she asked the empty chapel. The saint's empty bracket offered no opinion. The frost on the window thickened, feather by feather.

She thought of Ansel's face at the gate—the controlled courtesy, the eyes that had measured her and then, for one unguarded instant, seemed to pity her. *He tells the truth; it's his worst quality.* An enemy officer who told the truth. A brother who had chosen exile over lies. And around her, closing in like winter, a delegation of ministers who lied to each other, to their hosts, to themselves, and a minister named Corvane whose lamp burned late over papers she was not permitted to see.

She could report the contact. That was the dutiful act, the safe act, the act that would buy her another few months of invisibility—and it would kill Tomas, because Corvane would seize the letters, extract everything, and quietly arrange for a traitor's value to be zeroed. She could say nothing, carry the letters hidden, wait for the parley to conclude and slip away—which might save her and abandon him. Or she could do the thing the letters were actually asking, the thing that had no clean name: use what she carried, the codes, the access, the trust, to move her brother's proof of life out from under the noses of her own side, and damn the consequences.

*Plant something,* Tomas had written. *That's how we'll know we're still talking.*

Sebastian folded the letters along their original creases. She sewed them back into the coat lining with small, even stitches, working by touch more than sight, and as she sewed she made herself recite the code tables one more time—*column seven, the Vessarine standard*—because the tables were her duty and the stitches were her treason and she could hold both in the same pair of hands, at least for tonight.

Tomorrow, Minister Corvane would begin reviewing the delegation's personnel files. She had heard him say so to his aide through the study door, that afternoon, in the flat voice he used for decisions already made. *Start with the servants. Then the clerks. Then the couriers.*

The candle guttered and died. In the dark, Sebastian sat very still, listening to the wind move through the frozen orchard, and understood that her invisibility—the armor she had worn for six years—had just begun to fail.
