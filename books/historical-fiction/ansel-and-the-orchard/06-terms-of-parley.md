## 6. Terms of Parley

Ansel Veyra had learned long ago that the first hour of any negotiation belonged to the furniture.

The ministers of the Vessarine Republic arranged themselves around the long table in the great hall at Vell as though the chairs themselves were contested territory. Minister Corvane took the head, naturally—though technically he was not the head of delegation; that honor belonged to old Minister Halloway, whose gout had kept him abed since their arrival and whose absence Corvane wore like a borrowed coat, a size too large but tailored by circumstance to fit. To Corvane's right sat Undersecretary Maren, a narrow man who wrote everything down and said nothing worth writing. Then two others whose names Ansel had committed to memory along with their tells: Deputy Ilsevane, who touched her wedding ring whenever she lied, and young Pellham, who had been given the water and trade portfolios and clearly understood neither.

Ansel sat alone on the Ostravan side with an aide who was not an aide—a captain named Drescher with a soldier's patience and a clerk's handwriting—and let the ministers perform.

And they did perform. He watched it happen with something close to professional tenderness. The Republic was dying—he knew this the way a physician knows it of a patient whose color has gone wrong—and yet these five people sat upright and spoke of grain tariffs and river tolls as though the state they represented would exist to collect them past spring. It was not stupidity. That was the thing outsiders never understood about the Vessarine court, before its fall: it was never stupidity. It was theater sustained past the point of belief, because the alternative to performing confidence was admitting, aloud, that the play had no audience left.

"Ostrava's position on the northern tolls remains unchanged," Corvane was saying. He had a voice built for empty rooms—precise, resonant, needing no amplification. "We will not negotiate from a posture of concession."

*From a posture of concession.* Ansel turned the phrase over. There were perhaps eleven thousand Republic troops still under arms in the east, unpaid for three months, eating seed grain. There were bread riots in two provincial capitals that his own dispatches described in language his superiors had flagged as *possibly exaggerated* until they cross-checked it against refugee flows. And this man spoke of postures.

"We understand completely," Ansel said, and smiled the smile he had practiced in mirrors during his first year of service, the one that conveyed sympathy without agreement, agreement without commitment. "Which is why we've proposed the toll structure as a framework rather than a demand. Frameworks allow both parties to preserve dignity."

He watched Corvane receive the word *dignity* like a man catching something thrown at his face.

The morning proceeded. Ansel gave ground where ground cost nothing—the schedule of future meetings, the seating protocol, the question of whether minutes would be kept in both languages or translated afterward—and withheld everything that mattered. The armistice terms, the prisoner exchanges, the disposition of the river fortresses: these he mentioned only to defer them, citing the need to consult his government, which was true in the way that most useful lies are true. He *would* consult them. They would tell him what they always told him: prolong. Prolong, because every week the Republic's coalition frayed, every week another provincial governor quietly opened correspondence with Ostravan agents, every week made the eventual surrender cheaper and the eventual annexation smoother. His orders were not to make peace. His orders were to keep the peace talks alive long enough that they died of natural causes.

So he played for time, and he played it well, and if anyone in that room had asked him whether he was ashamed of it, he would have needed a moment to answer honestly.

What saved the morning from being merely tedious was the courier.

She entered twice in the first hour—once with dispatch cases, once with tea that no one drank—and both times she moved through the room the way weather moves through a house: present, felt, unremarked upon. She was slight for a man, or so the disguise suggested; her courier's gray uniform hung well enough but her hands were wrong, too fine-boned, and she'd done something with charcoal or ash to soften her jaw that fooled everyone except, apparently, anyone who actually looked.

Ansel looked.

It was not the hands alone. It was that she listened. Every other servant in that hall—every clerk, every aide, every functionary—had perfected the art of being in a room without inhabiting it. But when the courier crossed behind Corvane's chair with her case of papers, her eyes flicked—not to the minister, not to the guards, but to the documents themselves, and Ansel saw her read upside down, quickly, without moving her head, the way only someone does who has trained herself to steal meaning with her peripheral vision and then forget it immediately, because forgetting is survival.

Then she was gone, door closing softly, and Corvane was saying something about the sovereignty of navigable waters, and Ansel realized he had missed a full sentence for the first time in six years of negotiations.

He recovered. But he noted it, the way he noted all anomalies, and filed it under a heading he did not yet have a name for.

* * *

They broke at midday, and Ansel walked the grounds alone, ostensibly to stretch his legs, actually because Drescher reported that the estate's servants' wing was a sieve of rumors and he preferred not to be within earshot of anything he wasn't supposed to hear.

Vell had once belonged to some merchant family grown rich on river trade; now it belonged to winter and the Republic's requisition office, which amounted to the same thing. The formal gardens were dead hedges and brown lace. Beyond them stretched an orchard—apples, pears, something stone-fruit he didn't recognize—all of it frozen into a kind of silver sculpture, branches glazed with ice that caught the weak afternoon light.

He stood at the orchard's edge longer than he meant to.

There was a person in it.

The courier—of course it was the courier—stood among the trees with her bare hands wrapped around a branch, examining it with an attention that had nothing to do with duty. No one sent a courier to inventory an orchard in January. She was touching the graft unions—young wood knotted onto older stock, the scars of deliberate joining—and her expression, visible in profile, was one Ansel recognized from his own mirror in unguarded moments: the look of a person standing inside a memory and trying not to let it show through their face.

He withdrew before she saw him. It felt like theft, watching her. Or like being caught at something himself.

That evening, reviewing his notes by candlelight while Drescher transcribed, he found his pen stalling on the day's summary. *Ministers performed confidence. No substantive movement. Corvane consolidating personal authority over Halloway's mandate.* All true. All insufficient. Finally he wrote, in the margin, a line he would later strike out and then, finding himself unable to discard it, fold the page closed over:

*One honest person observed on premises. Not one of ours.*

Drescher glanced up. "Something, sir?"

"A detail," Ansel said. "Nothing."

But it was not nothing, and he knew it, because honesty in a diplomatic compound was not a detail—it was a fault line. Honest people cracked structures. He had seen it happen: the interpreter in Kolvary who couldn't bear to mistranslate a dying man's plea and brought two armies back from the brink by accident; the customs officer in his own garrison who had resigned rather than falsify manifests, and whose resignation letter had done more damage to morale than any enemy shell. Honesty was dangerous. Honesty was also, he was beginning to suspect after years of professionally manufactured conversations, the rarest thing he would ever encounter in his work, rarer than courage, rarer than luck.

He wanted, quite simply and quite inconveniently, to talk to her.

* * *

The second session convened at dusk, candles lit against the early dark, and it went worse than the first, which meant it went according to design.

Corvane had spent the interval doing what Corvane evidently did: tightening. Ansel could see it in small things—the way the minister's aides now flanked him rather than attending him, the way questions to junior members of the delegation were answered by Corvane personally regardless of portfolio, the way old Halloway's empty chair had been removed from the room entirely, so that no one would look at it and remember who was supposed to be leading this mission. A man building a wall out of other men's authority.

"The Republic," Corvane declared, in response to Ansel's carefully hedged proposal regarding prisoner repatriation, "does not recognize the category of *defector*. There are loyal soldiers and traitors, and traitors are not subjects for negotiation but for justice." He let the word sit in the candlelight. "I trust Ostrava is not sheltering traitors."

Ansel held his face still while something cold moved through his chest. It was nothing—almost certainly nothing. Corvane was fishing; ambitious men fished constantly; the word *traitor* was bait cast broadly across every negotiation since the invention of betrayal. But the timing snagged against something. Letters had reached the delegation recently—Ansel had seen the courier's face change when they were delivered, had catalogued it with the rest. If there were letters, there was a correspondent. If the correspondent was who Ansel believed—

He set the thought down carefully, the way one sets down something loaded.

"The crown of Ostrava," he said, "shelters persons entitled to its protection, under law and treaty. I am confident your own archives would confirm the distinction you're drawing doesn't exist in either nation's jurisprudence. Shall I have the citations prepared?"

A small silence. Ilsevane touched her ring. Pellham studied his notes as though they might contain an escape route. And Corvane smiled—thinly, the smile of a man noting a debt—and said, "By all means. We have time."

*We have time.* Ansel almost laughed. Neither of them had time; that was the entire architecture of this farce. The Republic had perhaps a season. Ostrava's patience had perhaps two. And somewhere between those two clocks, real people—soldiers, clerks, couriers—were going to be spent like currency by men who spoke of time as though they owned it.

The session collapsed, as scheduled, into procedural dispute over the minutes. It was then that the courier entered again, carrying the corrected transcript pages for signature.

Ansel watched her work. She moved down the table collecting signatures, and she was good—invisible again, the armor back on—but when she reached Corvane, something passed between them. Nothing overt. Corvane simply looked at her a half-second too long, the way a man looks at a lock he intends eventually to open, and she received the look without dropping her gaze, which was itself a mistake, because invisible people drop their gaze; that was the whole liturgy of invisibility. Meeting a powerful man's eyes evenly was the behavior of someone who considered herself, at bottom, his equal.

Corvane noticed. Ansel watched him notice. And the courier, gathering her pages, walked out of the room with her spine straight and her face composed and, Ansel would have sworn, the particular pallor of a person who has just heard a door close behind them that they cannot reopen.

* * *

Later—very late, past midnight, when the house creaked with cold and the watch changed—Ansel found himself in the corridor outside the delegation's offices with no official reason to be there. He told himself it was habit, the sleepless patrol instinct of a soldier billeted among strangers. He told himself a number of things.

Through the study door, low voices: Corvane and Maren.

"—personnel files beginning tomorrow. Servants first, then clerks, then couriers. I want to know who touches the correspondence and who reads what they touch."

"And if we find something?"

"Then we find something." A pause, and the sound of a decanter. "In a delegation this size, Captain, there is always something. One only needs to decide what it will be."

Ansel stood in the dark corridor and felt the shape of the next weeks assemble themselves: Corvane would manufacture a leak, or find one, or both; the finding would serve him either way; and the courier—with her listening eyes and her straight spine and whatever history lived in her hands as they touched tree bark—was already standing in the path of it.

He should have felt nothing. She was a functionary of a dying state, a stranger, possibly an enemy, certainly a risk. Every discipline he possessed, every scarred-over lesson of his career, said: *not your problem, not your war, not your life.*

But he stood in the freezing corridor a while longer anyway, listening to ice tick against the windows, thinking about a woman alone in a frozen orchard holding a grafted branch like a letter from home. He knew that gesture. He had made versions of it across ten years and four postings—touching the one unguarded thing in a guarded life, just to remember having a self beneath the uniform.

Tomorrow, Corvane would begin reviewing personnel files. Tomorrow, invisibility would start to fail her, wherever she was hiding in that house of watchers.

And tomorrow morning, Ansel decided, he would request the delegation's archivist by name. Officially. On the record. For consultation on the treaty archives.

Let Corvane chew on *that*.
