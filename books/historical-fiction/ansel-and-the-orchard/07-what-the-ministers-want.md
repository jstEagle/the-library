## 7. What the Ministers Want

The estate at Vell had been a house of accounts before it was a house of parley. Sebastian learned this on the third morning, when she was sent to fetch ledgers from the estate office and found the walls lined with shelves of them—forty years of harvest tallies, tenant rolls, tax assessments, the whole patient arithmetic of a family that had once believed in spring. Now the ledgers had been swept into crates and shoved beneath the windows to stop drafts, and men who had never counted anything but votes sat at the long table where the last Iversen of Vell had totted up his apple yields.

She had come for paper. She left with something else entirely.

Minister Corvane was already there.

He arrived early everywhere—that was the first thing she had noticed about him, back in the capital, before they ever crossed the border. While the other ministers slept off their farewell dinners and their private terrors, Corvane was awake, dressed, and reading. He read everything: dispatches, manifests, the delegation's own correspondence, though none of it was addressed to him. He read the way other men prayed, with total absorption and no expectation of mercy.

"Courier." He did not look up from the document spread before him. "You're up early."

"The horses need seeing to, Minister."

"The horses need seeing to." He turned a page. "Yes. Everything needs seeing to, these days. That is rather the point of us, isn't it?"

Sebastian stood at attention by the door and waited to be dismissed. Waiting to be dismissed was most of her life now; she had grown good at it, the way a stone grows good at being rained on. But Corvane did not dismiss her. He finished his page, squared the document against the table's edge with two precise taps of his fingers, and looked at her at last.

His eyes were pale gray, the gray of ice over deep water, and they performed a strange trick: they seemed to look through her and take inventory of her at the same time. She felt catalogued. Weighted. Filed.

"You've been with the chancellery how long?" he asked.

"Six years, Minister."

"And before that?"

"The Underhill orchards, south of Marrow Vale. My father's trade."

Something flickered across his face—not warmth, nothing so useful as warmth, but interest, the way a fisherman is interested when the line goes taut. "A fruit family," he said. "Honest work. Grafting, pruning, waiting. Do you know what I admire about agriculture, courier?"

"No, Minister."

"It cannot lie. A tree gives you exactly what you gave it, no more, and it does so on its own schedule regardless of your ambitions. There is no negotiation with an orchard." He rose, buttoning his coat with small economical movements. "I find I miss that. In this delegation, everything negotiates. Everything lies. Even the snow outside my window seems to be lying about how deep it is." He crossed toward the door, and paused beside her—close enough that she could smell the ink on his fingers and the faint camphor of the coat he wore against the cold. "You carry the codebook."

It was not a question, which meant he already knew the answer, which meant someone had told him, which meant he had asked.

"I do, Minister."

"Memorized?"

"Against regulations, Minister. The book itself is the instrument. Memorization invites—"

"—disaster if your memory is imperfect and treason if it is perfect. Yes, I know the doctrine. I helped write it." His mouth did something that was almost a smile and was in fact nothing of the kind. "I didn't ask whether you'd obeyed the regulation. I asked whether you had memorized it."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere below, a groom was cursing at a frozen pump in the flat vowels of the Vell countryside, and the sound came up through the floorboards like water rising.

"Yes, Minister," Sebastian said.

Corvane nodded slowly, as if she had confirmed something he had suspected about the world all along and found disappointing. "Good," he said. "That's the first honest answer anyone has given me in this house." And he left, his boots ringing on the stairs, taking the cold air with him.

* * *

She should have reported the exchange. That was the procedure: any ministerial inquiry touching the codes went into the log, countersigned by the senior courier, reviewed nightly. She took out the log that evening, after the lamps were lit and the ministers had withdrawn behind their doors to scheme in the particular silence each of them had perfected, and she sat with the pen above the line and did not write.

Because there was nothing to report, technically. He had asked questions. She had answered. No document had changed hands, no cipher had been exposed. And yet the exchange sat in her chest like a swallowed stone, and she understood—with the animal clarity that had kept her alive through six years of being nobody—that Corvane had not been making conversation. He had been measuring. He wanted to know whether the girl who carried the Republic's last functioning cipher was a servant or a weapon, and he had discovered, to whatever satisfaction lived inside him, that she was honest.

Honest people, Sebastian thought, blowing out her lamp, are the easiest people in the world to use.

She lay in the narrow cot in the courier's loft above the stables, wrapped in two blankets and her own careful anonymity, and let herself think the thoughts she permitted only in the dark, where even her own face could not witness them.

Tomas was alive. The letters were real—she had verified them twice more since the night Ansel brought them, checking the private proofs until her eyes ached: the joke about the magpie that stole their mother's thimble, the reference to the scar Tomas got falling from the north ladder at nine years old, the grafting terminology no textbook used, the little family dialect of cuts and cambium and scion-wood that their father had invented half as instruction and half as game. Alive, and held. That was the word Ansel had used, carefully, in the orchard dark: *held*. Protected the way one protects an asset. Kept the way one keeps a hostage, except that hostage implied someone wanted him back, and the Republic had declared him dead with a formality that had fooled everyone, apparently including the Republic.

His life hung on Ostrava's victory. Her duty demanded the Republic survive. Between those two facts stood Sebastian Underhill, carrying a codebook against her ribs like a second heart, and every day the space between the facts grew narrower and colder.

And now Corvane had begun asking questions.

* * *

The consolidation, when she recognized it for what it was, had the smoothness of something long rehearsed.

It began with the archives. On the fourth morning, a notice appeared on the kitchen door in Corvane's small, ruthless hand: *All delegation papers, personal and official, to be registered with the Minister of State's office by week's end. Unregistered documents will be considered compromised.* Personal and official. As if a man's letters home were state property. As if the delegation were already a government-in-waiting rather than a mission, and Corvane its head rather than one voice among five.

Minister Duvall protested—loudly, red-faced, in the corridor outside the map room, where everyone could hear and therefore everyone would remember that he had protested. Minister Ashe protested too, in her quieter way, with memoranda and procedural citations that Corvane received, read fully, and set aside unread, which was somehow worse. The old Chancellor's delegate, a dying man named Ferren who had come north mostly to die somewhere warmer than the capital, signed whatever was put in front of him. And that was that. By Friday, a clerk with ink-stained cuffs sat in the estate office registering papers, and Corvane knew what every member of the delegation wrote home, and to whom, and in what tone.

Then came the searches. Not announced—Corvane was too clever for announcements—but conducted in the softest possible way: rooms inspected while their occupants were at dinner, trunks weighed, seals checked against wax impressions kept in a leather wallet Corvane carried himself. Sebastian watched him do it once, from the stable yard, through the frost-furred window of the east wing. He moved through another man's belongings the way a surgeon moves through a body: without disgust, without reverence, with complete professional attention to what might be wrong.

And then, on the sixth day, the word *leak* entered the house.

She heard it first from the cook, who heard it from the under-clerk, who heard it from the guard captain: someone in the delegation, it was whispered, had passed information to the Ostravans. Nothing proven. A pattern, Corvane called it—the Ostravan negotiators had anticipated two positions at the table that only the inner circle had known. Two positions. Coincidence, said Duvall. Coincidence, agreed Ashe, with slightly too much speed. Corvane said nothing at all, which was his way of saying everything, and ordered the household staff questioned, the guards rotated, and the couriers' movements logged hour by hour.

Hour by hour. Sebastian read the new order pasted beside the kitchen door and felt the words go through her like a blade finding the gap between ribs. Logged hour by hour. Every trip to the well, every errand to the village, every walk—and there was no innocent explanation available to her for walks, because couriers did not take walks, couriers took routes, and hers were about to become a matter of record.

Ansel. The name rose in her unbidden, warm and dangerous. Their meetings in the orchard—two, now, brief and breathless among the frozen rows—had been cautious to the point of absurdity, ten minutes each, coded talk disguised as a courier stretching her legs. But caution was a currency, and Corvane had just devalued it. If he was hunting a leak, and if he hunted the way he read—totally, patiently, expecting no mercy—then sooner or later he would find the shape of her. The courier who walked alone. The courier who had memorized the codebook and admitted it. The courier whose brother was a traitor, if Corvane ever learned that Tomas Underhill lived, and Corvane learned everything eventually; that was becoming clear.

She stood in the kitchen doorway with the order in front of her and made herself look at the thing whole, the way her father had taught her to look at a tree before cutting: see the whole shape first, then the branch, then the cut.

The codebook against her ribs was the Republic's voice. With it, the delegation could still speak to the capital, still call for reinforcements that did not exist, still authenticate the armistice terms if terms were ever reached. Without it—or in the hands of a man like Corvane—it became something else. She had begun, in the last two nights, to understand what Corvane actually wanted, and it was not peace. Peace would send them all home to a Republic still barely breathing, still governed by the tired men who had bled it. Corvane did not want the Republic saved. He wanted it *ended properly*—ended in a way he controlled, its remnants gathered into his hands like scion-wood, ready to be grafted onto whatever rootstock survived the winter. She had seen him at the preliminary sessions, watching the Ostravan ministers with that inventory-taking gaze, and she had understood: he was not studying enemies. He was studying buyers.

If the armistice failed—if it was *made* to fail—the Republic would fall, and fall hard, and out of the collapse a strong man could build anything. And Tomas—

Tomas lived only if the Republic fell.

The thought arrived with its full weight for the first time, standing there in the steam and onion-smell of the kitchen, and Sebastian had to put her hand against the doorframe. Her duty and her brother's survival had been running parallel all this time, two lines she had refused to let meet. Now she saw they were not parallel. They converged. They converged on Corvane's ambition, of all things—on the ruin of everything she had sworn to serve—and the convergence was a knife-point, and she was standing on it.

Because here was the arithmetic no one else in the house could do: she carried the codes. She carried them in a book against her ribs, and the same contents lived in her memory, perfect, treasonous, indestructible. Those codes could keep the delegation's channel open—which meant keeping Corvane's channel open, feeding the machine of his ascent. Or they could fail. Codes failed in many ways: lost, burned, betrayed, mis-transcribed by a courier under pressure. A dead cipher meant the delegation went silent to the capital, meant Corvane's faction lost its lifeline, meant—perhaps—the talks collapsed faster, the Republic fell faster, Tomas lived sooner.

Or the opposite. Or silence meant the Ostravans grew suspicious, the parley died in distrust, and the war rolled over Vell and everyone in it, Tomas included, garrison prisoners being the first fruit shaken from any tree in a rout.

She did not know. That was the terrible truth underneath all the others: she did not know which way the knife pointed, and every day she stood in the house holding it, she was choosing anyway, by inertia, by cowardice, by the sheer human refusal to cut.

Her father had hated that in an orchardist—refusal to cut. *A limb you know is dead and leave hanging will take the tree down in the next storm,* he used to say, working the saw through blighted wood while she held the ladder. *Grief is for afterward, girl. First the cut.*

First the cut.

* * *

That evening she went to the orchard.

Not to meet Ansel—he had sent no signal, and the risk of an unscheduled meeting had doubled, tripled, since the leak-hunt began. She went because the trees were there, and because there were hours yet before the evening count of couriers, and because her hands needed work or they would shake.

The cold had deepened. Snow lay in the furrows between the rows like unspooled linen, and the trees stood black and patient, their graft-unions knuckled under the bark, each one a decision some dead hand had made decades ago and the tree had honored ever since. She walked the rows with her collar up and her breath streaming, and she stopped at the oldest block, where the Vell apples grew—*Vell Crimson*, the variety was called, though no market had sold it in twenty years—and she laid her bare palm against the trunk the way she had done as a child, testing for the faint warmth a living tree holds even in winter, the proof under the bark.

Alive. Held. Like Tomas.

"You're doing it again."

She spun. Ansel stood at the end of the row, half-shadow, in a plain cloak with no insignia, and her heart slammed once, hard, with fear before it remembered itself into relief. He should not be here. Neither of them should be here. The guards' rotation had changed; she had logged it herself that morning.

"You can't—" she started.

"I know." He came closer, boots silent in the snow, and lowered his voice to almost nothing. "Two minutes. I saw the new orders posted. Hour-by-hour logs. I came to say: stop walking here. For a while. Whatever we have to say to each other can wait. It has waited four years; it can wait until the thaw."

"It can't wait," she said, and heard her own voice come out flat and strange, stripped of the courier's blandness she wore like a uniform. "Corvane is hunting a leak. He'll find one whether there is one or not—he needs one, don't you see? A traitor in the delegation justifies anything. Emergency powers, sealed arrests, control of the codes. He isn't looking for the person who betrayed the Republic. He's looking for the person he can *make* into the betrayal, and then he won't need the armistice at all, because he'll have his collapse, and his crisis, and his clean hands."

Ansel was quiet a moment. Snow ticked down through the branches.

"You've thought about this a great deal," he said.

"I've had six years of watching men like him from the edges of rooms. It's the one advantage of being invisible—you see the whole shape." She hugged her arms against the cold, or against something colder. "He spoke to me. Three days ago. About the codebook. He knows I've memorized it—I told him, God help me, because he asked me a direct question and I have spent my whole life unable to lie to direct questions. And now I understand why he asked. He wasn't curious. He was pricing me."

"Pricing you."

"Everything gets priced in a collapsing country, Ansel. Loyalty, silence, a cipher. He wants to know what I'm worth and what I cost and which of those numbers is smaller." She looked up at him, and the moon came out from behind cloud just enough to show her his face—the careful officer's composure cracking along some older fault line, showing her the tired young man underneath, the one who carried dead men's handwriting across a border because someone had to. "And I realized today that I've been pricing myself too. Every day I carry those codes and say nothing, I'm selling something. I just haven't let myself read the bill."

"What's on it?"

"Tomas." The name came out steady, which surprised her; she had expected it to break. "His life depends on the Republic falling. My oath depends on it standing. And the codes in my chest sit right on the seam between those two facts, and Corvane knows it even if he doesn't know he knows it—he can smell a seam. He built his career on seams." She stepped back, putting the black bulk of a Vell Crimson between them, needing the distance to say the rest. "So here is what happens now. You tell Tomas—when you can, however you can—that his sister hasn't forgotten the promise. The cuttings. The vow. Tell him the promise is *moving*. And tell him it may cost more than either of us priced."

"Sebastian—"

"Go." The guard-change bell rang faintly from the house, silver through the frozen air. "Before the log catches up with both of us. Go."

He hesitated—one heartbeat, two—and in that hesitation something passed between them that had no name in either language, the recognition of two people standing on the same narrowing ridge with different fires burning behind them. Then he pressed his fist briefly to his chest, the Ostravan salute, absurd and solemn among the apple trees, and was gone down the row, and the snow closed over his tracks as if the ground itself were keeping her secrets better than she could.

She stayed a moment longer, palm against the bark.

*A limb you know is dead and leave hanging will take the tree down in the next storm.*

Tomorrow, Corvane would begin the staff interrogations. Tomorrow the log would start filling with her hours. Tomorrow the price of every choice would go up, the way prices do in a country learning to die.

But tonight, walking back through the frozen rows toward the lit windows of the house where her executioner-in-waiting was reading other people's letters by lamplight, Sebastian Underhill made the first cut. Quietly, in the dark of the courier's loft, she took the codebook from its binding against her ribs and opened it to the authentication tables, and began, line by line, with a memory trained on forty varieties of apple, to prepare for the possibility—no, the certainty—that soon she would need to make the Republic's voice fail.

And far off across the snow, toward the Ostravan lines, a single lantern moved and vanished, carrying her message to a brother who did not yet know that his sister had chosen which storm would bring the tree down.
