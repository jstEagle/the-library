## 8. The Promise in Peacetime

The orchard at Vell slept under a hand's depth of snow, and Sebastian walked its rows as she had not walked anywhere in years—without pretending to be someone else.

It was the strangest thing about the place. Here, among the frozen apple trees, she did not have to be Underhill, courier third class, a narrow-shouldered young man with ink-stained fingers and a stoop that made him forgettable. There was no one to perform for at four in the morning. The ministers still slept behind shuttered windows; the guards had their brazier and their dice; and the trees asked nothing of her except what her hands remembered.

She stopped before the oldest tree in the walled garden, a Ribston gone gnarled and half-hollow, its bark split into deep furrows like an old man's palm. Snow sat on every branch. She brushed it away from a low limb and found what she had half hoped to find: a sucker shoot rising from below the graft union, wild and vigorous, the rootstock asserting itself after decades of being told what to become.

Her breath fogged. Somewhere beyond the wall, a crow complained about the weather.

*You always start there,* Tomas had said, years ago, in another garden entirely. *The rootstock doesn't know it's supposed to be quiet.*

---

The Underhills' orchard had stood on the south slope above the Vessa river for three generations, forty acres of apple and quince and one stubborn line of sour cherries that no one had ever had the heart to rip out. In peacetime—the word itself now felt like something out of a storybook, a country she had visited once as a child—it had been a working orchard, but also a schoolroom. Their father had believed a child who could graft could think: could see two living things and imagine the join between them, could be patient across seasons, could accept that most of what you tried would fail and that failure was only information.

Sebastian had been nine when Tomas first put a knife in her hand.

"Not like that," he said, folding her fingers over the handle. He was fourteen, tall already, with their mother's dark hair falling into his eyes and the particular gravity he carried even then, as if he were listening to something the rest of them couldn't hear. "You're holding it like you're angry at it. The tree didn't do anything to you."

"It's winter," she said. "Everything's dead."

"Everything is *asleep.*" He crouched beside her in the cold shed, among the smell of oiled steel and dry wood shavings, and took a scion—a slender cutting, pencil-thin, cut from the Ribston the previous week—from the bundle wrapped in damp sacking. "There's a difference, and the whole world turns on the difference. Dead wood snaps. Asleep wood bends." He bent the cutting gently between his fists until it curved into a green-souled arc without breaking. "See? It's waiting. All winter it just waits, and then some fool with a knife gives it a new body to wake up in."

"A new body," she repeated, delighted by the phrase.

"That's all grafting is. You take the part that knows how to be fruit and you give it roots that know how to survive here. They don't have to be related. They don't even have to like each other." He grinned. "They just have to fit. Cambium to cambium. The growing layer touches the growing layer, and the tree decides they were always one thing."

She practiced cuts that whole February: tongue cuts, whip-and-tongue, cleft grafts on thicker stock, her first hundred failures stacked like kindling by the shed door. Her father watched from the window and said nothing, which was his way of saying *good*. By March she could match a scion to stock so cleanly that Tomas, inspecting her work with exaggerated solemnity, declared that the tree would never know it had been lied to.

"That's terrible," Sebastian said, horrified.

"It's the truest thing about grafting," Tomas said. "You're not hurting it. You're giving it a better story about itself."

---

The war came the way weather comes to a valley—not all at once, but as a series of small grey decisions made somewhere else, each one lowering the sky a little more.

Sebastian was seventeen the autumn the conscription notices reached their district. Tomas was twenty-two, exempt at first because of the orchard—food production, the magistrate's letter said, essential service—and then not exempt, because the word *essential* turned out to mean whatever men with stamps needed it to mean that month. She remembered the evening he told them. He had come in from checking the late apples with his collar up and his face carefully arranged, and he had said, "They want me in the spring," in the tone of a man reporting frost damage.

Their father had gone very still. Their mother had begun clearing dishes that did not need clearing.

It was later that night, in the loft above the press house where they dried the year's fruit, that Tomas woke her—or she found him already awake; she never learned which—and they climbed out onto the roof ridge where they used to sit as children, watching for the first wagons of harvest season coming up the valley road. The stars were hard and close. Below them the orchard lay silvered with early frost, every row legible, every tree where it had always been.

"I keep thinking about the grafts," Tomas said.

"You'll be back before bud-break. You'll see them take."

"Bas." Only he called her Bas. "I need you to listen to me without arguing for one minute. Can you do that?"

She had never managed it in her life. She said, "Yes."

"If I'm taken—if I'm killed, or worse, if I'm *lost*, which is a different thing—" His voice caught on the word and steadied. "If we're ever separated, I want you to promise me you'll find me. Not write. Not wait. Find. However long it takes, whatever you have to become to do it. Promise me you won't let anyone tell you I'm dead until you've seen it yourself. People say dead when they mean *stopped looking.*"

"Tomas—"

"Promise."

The strange thing, she thought afterward, was that she understood him instantly. Not the fear—she felt that too, a cold wire pulled through her chest—but the logic beneath it. Their whole childhood had been built on the grafting lesson: that identity was not fixed, that a living thing could be joined to a new life and continue, that the cambium layer—the thin green strip of *growing*—was what mattered, not the bark, not the shape, not the name. If Tomas was alive, then somewhere the growing layer touched hers, however different the ground they were planted in became. Distance was just bad rootstock. It could be worked around.

"I promise," she said. "But you have to promise the same."

"I promise," he said, and laughed, and the laugh cracked open something in both of them, and they sat on the cold roof ridge holding each other's sleeves like children, laughing and crying in the ugly honest way that siblings can, while below them the orchard kept its own counsel in the frost.

---

In the spring, before the train came, they sealed it.

He would not have let her come to the station otherwise—he had argued for weeks against any ceremony, any token, any weight she might have to carry—but Sebastian had inherited their mother's stubbornness along with her hands, and she presented him with it on the last morning like a verdict.

Two cuttings, wrapped in damp linen inside a tin that had once held tobacco. One from the old Ribston, the tree of their father's father. One from the Bessette pear their mother had brought as a bride from across the river, from soil that was now technically enemy soil, though pears had never learned about borders.

"You'll plant these," she said. "Wherever they send you. If it's a camp, bribe a gardener. If it's a farm, easier still. And I'll keep mine here, and I'll plant mine wherever I end up, and that's the promise made visible. Trees don't lie, Tomo. They can't. A grafted tree is a promise that got up and started growing."

He looked at the tin for a long time. Then he did the thing he used to do when they were small and she was frightened: he tapped his own chest twice, over the heart, and waited until she tapped hers.

"Cambium to cambium," he said.

"Cambium to cambium," she answered, and hated herself for the tears, and did not stop them.

At the station, among the steam and the shouting and the other families performing composure, he held her by the shoulders at arm's length and looked at her with sudden fierce attention, as though memorizing her—which should have frightened her more than it did, and did frighten her, and she ignored it.

"They'll try to make you smaller," he said. "Whatever happens, whoever's in charge—they will try to make you smaller, because small people are easy to file. Don't let them. Be exactly as large as you are, and hide it well."

The train took him east. The war took everything else on schedule.

---

Their father died in the second winter—not in battle; the war barely needed to touch him, it simply redirected all the doctors and the coal and the hope, and his heart, which had farmed through two hard decades, declined to farm through a third. Their mother lasted until the fourth year, long enough to see the Ostravan artillery reach the far ridge, not long enough to see the shelling stop. Sebastian buried her parents in the orchard's corner, under the sour cherries, because the churchyard was full and the priest had opinions about who deserved consecrated ground that Sebastian no longer had the luxury of sharing.

And the Republic, needing clerks more than daughters, offered her a bargain dressed as a courtesy: her brother's service record, her family's confiscated acreage, her own survival—all of it could be arranged, if she became someone the paperwork would accept. A man named Underhill, courier third class. Small. Easy to file.

She thought of Tomas on the roof ridge. *Be exactly as large as you are, and hide it well.*

She signed the papers with a hand that did not shake, and that frightened her too, and she ignored that as well.

---

Now, in the frozen orchard at Vell, Sebastian knelt in the snow with the sucker shoot in her gloved hands and her knife already open, and realized she had been walking toward this moment since the night on the roof.

The shoot came away clean at the base, pale and alive, its buds tight as secrets. Rootstock. Wild growth. The part of the tree that didn't know it was supposed to be quiet. She cut three more—one from the Ribston, two from an ancient Bessette pear against the south wall whose label plate had rusted to illegibility, though her hands knew it anyway—and wrapped them in linen from her own shirt hem, dampened with snowmelt, tucked into the tobacco tin she had carried through five years and two identities without ever once letting it out of her sight.

Cambium to cambium.

She had told herself the vow was a child's superstition, a rope thrown to a drowning girl, nothing that a grown woman running dispatches for a dying state could afford to honor. But kneeling there with the sap-smell rising faintly from the fresh cuts—even in winter, even now, that green ghost of a smell—she understood what Tomas had actually taught her, all those Februaries ago in the cold shed. It was never about apples. It was this: a promise, properly made, is a living thing. It can be cut, wrapped, carried through the killing season, planted in foreign ground. It does not require the permission of governments. Governments are weather. Promises are trees. The weather passes; the tree remains, if someone is stubborn enough to keep it watered.

The Republic would fall—she saw it now daily, in Corvane's hungry eyes, in the ministers' arithmetic of retreat, in the codebook warm against her ribs like a second heart. And when it fell, every oath she had sworn to it would dissolve with it, the way ice dissolves, leaving the water underneath unchanged. But the vow on the roof ridge had never belonged to the Republic. Neither had the one at the station. Those belonged to the orchard, to the loft, to the two children tapping their chests in the dark. Those were older than the war and would outlive it, and she was done pretending otherwise.

Somewhere east of here, past the lines, past the propaganda and the barbed arithmetic of nations, a man with their mother's dark hair had a tobacco tin with two cuttings in it—if he had kept it, if he had lived, if the letters in Ansel Voss's careful hand were what they seemed and not some intelligence officer's exquisite cruelty. She would verify them tonight, properly, alone, the way her father had taught her to test a graft: not by the bark but by the living layer underneath. The private jokes. The scar. The words only Tomas knew.

And if they were true—

A door banged somewhere beyond the wall. Voices, low and urgent, in the servants' passage—guards, moving faster than the hour warranted. The lantern-light swung across the snow and settled, briefly, at the orchard gate.

Sebastian closed her knife and stood among the sleeping rows, the tin against her ribs, listening to footsteps approach through the frozen garden—as if the morning itself had decided she had hidden long enough.
