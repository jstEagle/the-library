## 9. Second Meeting, First Truth

Ansel had told himself he would not go.

He had said it aloud, in fact, alone in the quarters the Vessarines had grudgingly allotted him—a narrow room in the east wing of the estate at Vell, its window glazed with frost, its stove smoking no matter how he coaxed it. *You will not go back to the orchard.* He had said it the way a man says a prayer he does not believe in, and then he had spent the rest of the day not believing it, watching from his window as the light went amber and then grey over the frozen rows of apple trees, waiting for a figure that did not appear.

She was not there. Of course she was not there. She was a courier of the Vessarine delegation; her days belonged to ledgers and ciphered minutes and the long corridors where Minister Corvane's shadow fell longer than anyone else's. She had no reason to walk among dead trees in the cold. And he—Ansel Veric, captain of Ostravan horse, bearer of the parley flag—had even less reason to seek her.

And yet at dusk, when the servants had gone in and the guards had changed their watch, when the snow began again in that fine, patient way it had all week, sifting down like ash from some distant burning—he found himself pulling on his coat, finding the side door with the broken latch that he'd noted the first day out of pure professional habit, and crossing the courtyard into the orchard with his collar up and his heart going like a boy's.

He hated himself for it. He noted that too, precisely, the way he noted everything: the self-contempt was real, and it changed nothing.

The orchard at Vell was older than either nation. That was what he liked about it. The trees had been planted by someone who spoke a language now forgotten by everyone who lived here, and they had gone on bearing fruit through the Republic's founding and the Kingdom's wars and every treaty signed and broken since. In winter they looked like a congregation of the drowned—black arms lifted, snow caught in the forks of their branches, the rows running away into the white until the world simply ended. Ansel walked down the central row with his boots crunching, and the silence closed behind him like water.

She was there after all.

Not in the middle of the rows, where he had half expected her—she was too careful for that—but off to the eastern edge, where the orchard met the collapsed wall of the old kitchen garden. She stood before one of the oldest trees, an enormous gnarled thing split nearly to the heartwood by some storm decades past, and she was doing something with her hands. Working at the bark. He stopped, uncertain whether to announce himself, and watched her for a moment: the courier's drab coat, the cropped hair pushed under a wool cap, the shoulders slightly too square, held that deliberate way he had begun to recognize—the posture of a person performing a body rather than inhabiting one.

Then she turned, and he understood that she had known he was there the whole time. Had probably heard him from the moment the side door banged shut. She had simply decided to let him come.

"Captain." Her voice was low, pitched for a man's register and mostly achieving it. Up close, in this light, the disguise fell apart entirely—not because of anything visible, but because of something in the way she looked at him. Men did not look at him like that. Men looked at him with calculation or fear or the flat courtesy of professionals. This was different. This was a look that had been waiting for him.

"Lieutenant Underhill," he answered, and let the pause stretch just long enough. "Or should I say—"

"Don't." It came fast, quiet, without heat. Her eyes flicked once toward the house, the dark windows, the smoke rising thin from the chimneys. "Not out here. Not anywhere."

"All right." He raised both hands briefly—an officer's gesture of truce, small and sincere. "Not anywhere."

They stood three paces apart among the frozen trees, and Ansel became aware that he had planned nothing. He was a man who planned everything—who negotiated for a living, who entered every room knowing which sentence he would open with and which he would withhold—and he found that every prepared opening had burned away somewhere between the side door and this row of black trees. What remained was only the truth, sitting in his chest like a stone he had carried so long he had mistaken it for part of his body.

"I brought you something," he said finally. "Though I suppose I already have."

"The letters." She said it flatly, guarding it. "I've read them."

"And?"

"And they're either from my brother or from the best forger on your side of the border."

"They're from your brother."

"You can't know that."

"No," Ansel agreed. "But I know him. I've sat across a table from him for two years. I've eaten at the same mess. I've heard him talk about grafting scions to a man who thought he was talking about cavalry rotations." He paused. "He talks about apples the way other men talk about God. Nobody fakes that. You could train a forger to copy a hand in a month. You couldn't train one to love a thing that much in ten years."

Something moved in her face—quick, quickly mastered, but he saw it. Grief and hope fighting for the same ground, each one accusing the other of treachery.

"He's alive," she said. Not a question. A woman setting a weight down to see if her legs still held.

"He's alive," Ansel said. "And he is thirty miles east of here, in a garrison town called Dzherin, under the protection of the Ostravan Crown, which is another way of saying he is a prisoner who is treated very well and may never leave."

* * *

They walked as they talked. It happened naturally, without agreement—two people whose professions taught them that standing still made you a target. Down the row, along the ruined wall, past the great split tree. The snow kept falling, thickening the hush, erasing their footprints behind them almost as fast as they made them, and Ansel thought there was a mercy in that. A conversation with no record. In two nations drowning in records—inventories, registers, ciphered dispatches, lists of names—here was a path that unmade itself as it went.

"Tell me," she said. "All of it. Don't arrange it first. I'll know if you arrange it."

So he didn't. He told her the way he would tell it to himself, late, in the honest hour when lies got tired: Tomas Underhill, lieutenant of Vessarine sappers, captured at Kolvary Bridge in the third year of the war. Reported dead in the prisoner rolls—clerical error, or convenience; Ansel had learned not to distinguish between the two. Held eighteen months at a camp where the mortality ran forty percent a winter. Then, in the spring of last year, a man from the Crown's intelligence service arrived at the camp with a list of names, and Tomas Underhill's name was on it, and the price of coming off the list was information.

"What kind of information?"

"Engineering assessments. River crossings, bridge load tolerances, fortification drainage—the things a sapper knows. Nothing tactical, at least not at first." Ansel kept his eyes on the path ahead. "At first."

"At first," she repeated.

"It escalated. They always do. By the end he was telling them things about Vessarine supply routes that got men killed—I'm fairly certain of it, though he doesn't know for certain and neither do I, and that uncertainty is its own punishment. He's been paying for it ever since. Not in cells. In comfort. That's the design." He glanced at her. "Your brother lives in a warm room in Dzherin with good food, a stipend, and a guard outside his door who calls himself his aide. He has not written a letter in two years that wasn't opened first. He is permitted to live exactly as long as he is useful, and he will be useful exactly as long as the war continues, and the war will continue—" Ansel heard his own voice flatten, "—as long as the Republic stands."

The words landed between them in the snow. She had stopped walking. So had he.

"That's what the letters mean," she said slowly. "That paragraph at the end of the second one. I thought I was reading it wrong. *If the tree comes down, the rootstock survives.*"

"Grafting terminology," Ansel said. "His code for it. The Republic is the top-growth—dead wood now, in their estimation, ready to fall. He's the rootstock. When the Republic falls, the arrangement ends, and he goes free. Not before. If the armistice succeeds, if the war winds down honorably, he becomes what he actually is: a defector. A traitor to a state that still exists to try him. They keep him alive as leverage while the war runs hot, and the moment it cools—" He didn't finish it. He didn't need to. He watched her arrive at the end of the sentence herself, watched her face do the terrible arithmetic.

"So my country must die," Sebastian said quietly, "for my brother to live."

"Yes."

"And you were sent to tell me this why? Out of mercy?" Her voice had sharpened; the courier was surfacing through the woman, or perhaps the woman through the courier. "Ostravan intelligence does not spend a captain's time on mercy. What am I to you? A channel? Are they using me to pass terms to Corvane? To test the delegation's security?"

It was a good question. It was the correct question, asked in the correct order, and Ansel respected her enormously for asking it, and he took a breath and gave the answer he had not planned because he had not dared plan it.

"I volunteered," he said. "When the letters came through channels, and someone noticed the addressee was a courier in the delegation—someone proposed exactly what you just described. A channel. A lever. I argued against it. I lost the argument, but I won the assignment, and I intend to misuse it." He turned to face her fully. "I intend to deliver these letters as a private matter between your brother and you, and to tell no one in Ostrava that the courier of the Vessarine delegation is a woman, which I also know, and which I have told no one, and which no one else knows. I am supposed to assess you, Lieutenant. My assessment, which I will write tonight, is that you are dull, punctual, and of no intelligence value whatsoever. It will be the most important report I've falsified in nine years of service."

She stared at him. The snow settled on both their shoulders. Somewhere far off, beyond the wall, a dog barked twice and quit.

"Why?" she said at last. Just that. No ornament.

Because I have not had an honest conversation in eleven years, he thought. Because I watched my own brother trade his soul in increments smaller than Tomas's and call it duty. Because you tend dead trees in the dark as if tending could save them, and I recognized it, because that is what I have been doing for my country since the war began. Because honesty is the one luxury left in either nation and I am tired of being poor.

"Because someone should be," he said. "That's all. Someone should be honest with you, once, about what was done to your family and why. Both sides have been lying to their people so long that lying is the only language left. I find I've forgotten the other one." He hesitated, and then, because it was true and because truth was the whole point: "This is the first honest conversation I've had since the war started. I don't expect it's different for you."

She laughed—one breath of it, startled out of her, immediately pressed flat. "No," she said. "It isn't different."

"My name is Ansel Veric. Not the rank, not the title. Ansel." He put out his hand, absurdly formal, gloved, in a frozen orchard in no-man's-land, and after a moment she took it, and her grip was strong and cold and did not perform masculinity at all, and he thought: *there she is.*

"Sebastian," she said. Then, quieter, with a small crooked smile that transformed her face entirely and was gone again in an instant: "My parents wanted a boy. They got me instead, and refused to revise. I kept the name. It seemed rude to waste it."

"Sebastian," he said, tasting it.

"You understand what I'm risking, telling you that."

"I understand better than you know. I've risked the same, tonight, several times over."

"Not the same." But she didn't press it, and he loved her a little for not pressing it, there in the dark among the trees, before love had any business existing.

They walked again, slower now, and she asked questions and he answered them, and the strange thing—the thing he would remember afterward, in the harder days coming—was how easy it was. How the words came without armor. She asked about Dzherin, and he described the garrison town honestly: the mud, the good bakery, Tomas's room with the view of the drill yard, the way Tomas had taken to growing herbs on the sill as if a windowsill garden could stand in for an orchard. She asked whether Tomas was well, and Ansel told the truth, which was: fed, healthy, hollowed. "Grief that can't be performed goes inward," he said. "He carries it like a soldier carries a bad wound—compensating, favoring the other side. You'll see it when you see him."

"When I see him." She said it like a vow.

"If the letters reach him. That's the condition, and I'll be plain about it, because plainness is the whole point of tonight: getting word to him means moving material through or around your delegation, and your delegation is currently hunting a leak. I've seen the pattern before—Corvane's clerks rechecking manifests, the archive access logs pulled yesterday. Whoever moves those letters will be walking through a closing door. I want you to know that before you decide anything. I want you to be able to say I told you plainly."

She was silent for a long stretch of row. Snow gathered in the creases of her sleeves. When she spoke, her voice had gone very level—the voice, he guessed, that she used for ciphered minutes and border checkpoints, the voice that had kept her alive.

"In my family," she said, "we made promises with trees. Cuttings, grafts—living contracts. If you plant a piece of someone's orchard, you owe the orchard. I promised my brother, years ago, that if war separated us I would find him. I sealed it with cuttings from our home trees." She looked up at the black branches overhead. "Governments interrupted. Governments are interruptions, Captain. The promise came first and the promise outlasts them. So yes. I've decided already. Tell me how dangerous, not whether."

Ansel felt something in his chest turn over—relief and dread in the same motion, inseparable, like the two faces of a coin spinning. He had come to give her truth and offer help, and he had told himself that was all, and standing here listening to her spend her life so casually on a promise, he knew he was lying to himself about the *all*. Somewhere in the last four days—perhaps at the gate, perhaps in the orchard, perhaps in the moment she had said *don't*, sharp and quick, defending not her secret but her brother's—this had stopped being an assignment misused and become something with roots of its own. Something he would not be able to prune out later, however hard he cut.

"There's a gardener's shed at the north corner of the wall," he said. "Broken lock, dry floor, nobody uses it. Tomorrow night, after the second bell, leave word there if you've moved the letters—or if you need me to move them for you. I can get material across the lines inside veterinary supplies; the horses' medicines are checked by our own men, lazily. After tomorrow it gets harder. Corvane is tightening daily."

"How do you know about Corvane?"

"Because I read men for a living, and he reads rooms. He isn't negotiating for the Republic. He's negotiating for whatever's left of it when it stops existing. Your ministers think they're buying peace. Corvane is pricing assets." He saw her absorb this without flinch—she had likely suspected it herself, had merely needed another professional to confirm it. "Which means the armistice will fail, Sebastian. Not because the terms are impossible—they're not; I could draft acceptable terms in a week—but because powerful men on both sides have bet on failure. When it fails, everything shifts. Borders, loyalties, the meaning of the word *traitor*." He paused. "Him most of all."

The wind moved down the rows, shaking a sift of snow loose from the branches, and for a moment the orchard seemed almost to breathe around them. She had stopped at the great split tree again, unconsciously, the way a person returns to a landmark, and her bare hand—it was bare, he noticed, gloveless despite the cold—rested against the split bark as if taking a pulse.

"This tree survived the split," she said. "Someone braced it. Bound the wound with tar and cloth. You can still see the scar tissue, sixty years on. It grows lopsided, half the crown dead, but it fruits. Every year it fruits." She took her hand away. "Tomas taught me that. Damage isn't the end of a tree. It's the beginning of its character."

Ansel looked at the tree, and then at her, and understood that she was not talking about the tree, and that she knew he understood, and that this—that mutual understanding arriving without negotiation, without terms, without a single clause—was the rarest thing he had encountered in a career built on drafting them.

"It's late," he said reluctantly. "Second bell soon. If we're missed—"

"Go first. I'll wait, then take the long way back through the kitchen garden. My absence is expected to be nowhere; yours is expected to be your room." A ghost of the crooked smile. "We're both professionals, Captain."

"Ansel," he corrected.

"Ansel." She tried the name carefully, the way one tests new graftwood for soundness. Then: "Thank you. For the letters. For the truth. For—" she gestured vaguely at the whole frozen dark, the whole impossible evening, "—this. I had forgotten what it was like. Talking without translating myself first."

"I know," he said. "That's why I came."

He walked back up the central row alone, and at the edge of the orchard he stopped and looked back once. She remained by the split tree, a slight figure in the falling snow, one hand raised—not a wave, he thought, more like a salute, or a blessing, or a gardener acknowledging a tree that had done its work. Then she turned and moved away down the shadowed row toward the kitchen garden, and the snow closed over the space where she had been.

In his room, by candlelight, Ansel wrote the promised report. *Courier U.: dull, punctual, of no intelligence value.* He read it over twice, signed it, sealed it, and set it aside for morning dispatch. Then he sat for a long time with his hands folded, looking at the frost on the window, thinking of a tree split to the heartwood that fruited anyway.

Tomorrow night, the shed. Tomorrow night, the letters begin their journey through a closing door. And somewhere in the house above him, a clerk was pulling access logs, and a minister was pricing the future, and the clock of the armistice ticked on regardless.

He blew out the candle and, in the dark, allowed himself—for the first time in nine years—to hope, and knew, even as he did it, that hope was the most dangerous cargo he had ever agreed to carry.
