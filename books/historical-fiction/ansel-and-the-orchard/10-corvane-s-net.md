## 10. Corvane's Net

The dispatch came in on a Tuesday, riding a sledge from the eastern relay with its courier half-frozen and its wax seal cracked by cold. Sebastian saw it first because she always saw the post first; that was her function in the delegation's small economy, to stand at the door of the chancery while the world outside went white and silent, and take what the road offered. The sledge driver handed her the leather satchel without looking at her face. Nobody looked at couriers' faces. That was the whole architecture of her survival: a person built out of drudgery, so ordinary that eyes slid off her like rain off oiled canvas.

She carried the dispatch inside, logged it in the ledger with her neat, unremarkable hand—*Received 14th of the Frost Month, one sealed packet, eastern relay, seal intact*—and set it on Minister Corvane's table, because routine dispatches of no classification went to Corvane, who had appointed himself the delegation's second pair of eyes and had done it so smoothly, so early, that no one remembered anymore that the First Minister had never actually asked him to.

Corvane was not there. His chair stood empty, pushed back at an angle that suggested he'd left in a hurry or wanted people to think he had.

Sebastian turned to go.

Then she stopped, because she was a professional, and professionals notice things, and what she noticed was this: the packet was not the only thing on Corvane's table. Beneath his blotter, carelessly half-exposed, lay a fan of papers she recognized—the transcription folios from the armistice preparatory sessions, the ones she herself had copied and filed three days ago. They should have been in the archive cabinet. They were here, under his hand, and along their margins ran annotations in a different ink: small, precise, predatory marks. Dates circled. Initials noted. A question mark beside the name of the junior clerk, Pellam, who had once, weeks ago, carried a message between Sebastian and the Ostravan liaison office through entirely legitimate channels—or what had seemed legitimate then, before every channel began to feel like a throat waiting to be cut.

She looked at those marginalia for perhaps four seconds. Four seconds was long enough. Four seconds was also, she would think later, exactly how long Corvane had intended them to be visible, if he had intended anything at all—and the uncertainty of that, the not-knowing whether she had stolen a glance at a careless man's work or walked open-eyed into a trap baited with paper, was a cold that settled into her bones and did not leave for the rest of the winter.

Footsteps in the corridor. She moved to the window, lifted the latch as though checking the draft, and by the time Corvane entered she was a courier fussing with a window, nothing more, a gray person in a gray room.

"Courier." He said her name—the false one, the male one, *Sebastian Underhill*—with a mildness that was worse than suspicion. Mild men were the dangerous ones. "You've brought the post."

"Eastern relay, sir. Seal intact."

"Good." He took off his gloves finger by finger, unhurried, a man who had never in his life needed to hurry because he arranged the world so that it came to him. He was younger than his reputation, Corvane, with a soft jaw and pale eyes that never seemed to blink so much as close and reopen elsewhere, already fixed on something you hadn't shown him yet. "Cold work, the door duty. You should ask for relief."

"I don't mind the cold, sir."

"No," he said, smiling faintly. "I imagine you don't mind much of anything. That's what I appreciate about you, Underhill. You're a man entirely without appetites. Rare quality. Most men want something, and wanting makes them legible." He weighed the dispatch packet in one hand. "You may go."

She went. Her heart beat the way a bird beats against glass—not fast, exactly, but trapped, striking the same place again and again behind her ribs where the codebook used to sit, before she had committed every cipher to memory and burned her way free of it. Now something else lived against her ribs. Three letters, folded flat inside her shirt, written in her dead brother's living hand.

* * *

That evening the estate felt smaller.

Vell had been a grand house once—the Vessarine Republic had sent its delegations only to places that flattered it, back when flattery was still worth spending—and its frozen orchard stretched down the south slope in long dark rows, two hundred trees asleep under snow, branches black and patient against the gray sky. Sebastian had come to love the orchard in the way one loves anything that asks nothing. In daylight she could walk the rows with pruning shears and be invisible even to herself.

But tonight the corridors pressed in. She counted the changes the way she had learned to count everything since Ansel had appeared at the gate with Tomas's letters: the guards doubled at the archive room, though no order for doubling had been posted. Pellam the clerk called twice into Corvane's office and emerging both times with a face scrubbed blank, the particular blankness of a man who has just been asked questions he didn't know were questions. The delegation's secretary, Madame Ostrev, locking her cipher drawer and then locking it again, testing the lock, testing it again.

Paranoia, Sebastian knew, was contagious in closed communities the way fever is contagious in ships. One case in the hold and soon everyone was measuring their own pulse.

She measured hers. Then she went to the orchard.

Snow came up over her boots. The light was failing, that bruised violet light of deep winter afternoons when the sun gives up by mid-afternoon and leaves the sky to negotiate its own surrender. She stopped at the third row, at the old Maren apple that had gone unpruned for years, and stood with her bare hands around the shears until her fingers burned, and let herself think the thoughts she was forbidden to think indoors.

*Tomas lives.* Eleven days now since Ansel had put those letters in her hands beneath the flag of parley, and still the fact refused to settle into her like warmth. It sat in her like a coal too hot to hold, too precious to drop.

And: *Corvane knows someone has made contact.*

Not that she had. Not specifically—that she could not know. But the net was going out all the same, and nets do not require their weavers to know precisely which fish they'll catch. A fisherman needs only to believe there are fish.

She thought about the marginalia. She thought about Pellam's blank face. She thought about the word Corvane had used—*legible*—and understood, with the clarity that comes only when it is already too late to benefit from it, that he had not been describing other men. He had been teaching her the lesson directly: *I read people, courier. I am reading you.*

The letters against her ribs felt suddenly loud, as though the paper itself might rustle audibly, as though ink had a smell that hounds could find.

She went back inside and burned one of them.

* * *

It was the least important one and she had known for days that it would be the one to burn, and knowing did not make it easier. It was the letter from last spring, dated the ninth of the Thaw Month—a date that meant nothing to anyone alive except the two of them, the day their mother had planted the cherry by the well, the day Tomas had fallen out of the grafting shed and split his brow and laughed about it with blood on his teeth. The letter was mostly about weather. It was mostly about cherries. It contained, between its lines about orchards and seasons, the confirmation of who he was and where he was held and that he was treated decently, and none of that intelligence was worth the risk of keeping paper.

She memorized it anyway. All of it. She had always had the family gift for text—their father had said she could carry a page in her head like water in cupped hands—and she knelt at the stove in the empty scullery past midnight and read the letter seven times through, folding each sentence away, and then she fed it to the fire a corner at a time and watched the handwriting curl and go brown and then bright and then nothing. Her brother's hand, burning. It felt like burning him a second time. It felt like setting him free a second time. She could not decide which, and suspected the truth was both, forever, inseparably, and that this was simply what love cost in the final winter of a dying republic: everything arrived doubled, grief and hope sharing one envelope.

Two letters remained. The older one—the one that explained, in language built entirely of grafting terminology, why he had done what he'd done, *the rootstock failed, the scion must live, you understand what I'm telling you, little sister, I know you understand*—and the newest, the one Ansel had carried across the lines himself, which said almost nothing at all except: *Trust the officer. He kept his word to me. Keep yours to me.*

She sewed them into the lining of her coat, flat against the small of her back, where a body's own heat made the paper indistinguishable from skin. Then she sat on her cot in the attic room she shared with the delegation's baggage master, listening to him snore, and did not sleep, and rehearsed.

Because that was the real work of that night—not the burning, not the sewing, but the rehearsal. If Corvane came for her—if the questions started, casual over breakfast, precise over dinner—what would she say? What did she know, and what had she seen, and what could she survive admitting? Every answer had to be prepared in advance, because lies improvised under pressure were legible, and Corvane read.

She built herself a history the way masons build a wall, stone by stone. Yes, she had carried messages between the delegation and the Ostravan liaison office during the parley arrangements—under authorization, she believed, from Madame Ostrev's office; she had the routing slip, she could produce it, the slip existed, she had checked that it existed, she had made sure it existed weeks ago because a courier who cannot document her routes is a courier who can be buried. No, she had not spoken privately with the Ostravan officer beyond formal exchange. No, she had not left the grounds. Yes, she had spent time in the orchard—everyone knew it, it was her one visible eccentricity, the courier who pruned trees—and an eccentricity was worth more than gold now, because eccentricity explained things. A strange habit was a locked door with a plausible key hanging beside it.

She lay in the dark and turned the stones over, testing each one for looseness, and found only one that wobbled badly, and it was the largest: *Ansel.* Not his letters, not his visits, but the eleven minutes she had spent with him in the orchard two nights ago, when he had told her the truth about Tomas in a voice pitched under the wind, and she had stood closer to him than protocol allowed and farther than innocence permitted, and any witness—even a distant one, even a shape at a lit window—would have seen two people standing like conspirators, because that is what they had become, and bodies tell that story no matter what the mouths are saying.

Had there been a witness? She didn't know. Not-knowing was the whole texture of her life now.

* * *

The interrogations, when they came, were not called interrogations.

They were called *routine verification of correspondence protocols*, and they were conducted by Corvane personally, in his office, one staff member at a time, with tea. That was the genius of it—Sebastian watched it unfold over four days with the horrified admiration of one craftsman observing another. No accusations. No guards. Just the First Minister's ambitious second, pouring tea for clerks and cooks and couriers, asking pleasant questions in his soft voice while his pale eyes did the actual work. Where were you on the night of the twelfth? Who else was awake? You handled the eastern dispatches—did you ever handle one whose seal was broken? Did you report it? Why not? And always, at the end, the same closing courtesy, delivered with a smile: *Thank you. We ask everyone these same questions. It means nothing.*

It meant everything. By the second day the household understood it meant everything, and by the third day the understanding had begun to change people. Trust, which had been ambient in the delegation the way damp is ambient in a cellar—a condition nobody noticed until it was gone—became suddenly a currency, counted, hoarded, spent carefully. People who had shared wine a month ago now answered questions about each other with careful neutrality, and careful neutrality was itself an answer. Pellam, questioned longest, came out gray-faced and silent and got drunk in the kitchen and told the cook, in a carrying whisper, that he had nothing to hide, nothing, *nothing*, which was exactly the wrong number of times to say it, and by morning the whole house had quietly agreed that Pellam had something to hide.

Sebastian drank her tea in Corvane's office on the fourth afternoon and performed the wall she had built.

"Orchard," Corvane said, near the end, refilling her cup uninvited. "You're often in the orchard."

"Yes, sir. Habit. My family kept apples."

"Family." He said the word like a man tasting a vintage. "Parents living?"

"Dead, sir."

"Brothers? Sisters?"

The question hung in the steam above the teacup. Somewhere in the walls of the old house a pipe ticked as it froze and thawed and froze. Sebastian looked at Corvane's pale eyes and understood that this question had probably been asked of everyone, that it was probably nothing, that it was certainly not nothing.

"A brother," she said, in Sebastian Underhill's flat, unremarkable voice. "Killed at Marrow Field. Before my time in service." She sipped her tea. Grief, she had learned, was the best armor a liar could wear, because true grief made every surrounding falsehood load-bearing. "I don't speak of it, sir."

"No," Corvane said softly. "I imagine you don't." He set down the pot. "Thank you, Underhill. These questions mean nothing."

She rose, bowed the correct degree, and walked out through corridors that had never felt longer, her face composed, her mind running ahead of her feet like a rider galloping a horse it no longer trusted. He had asked about brothers. Everyone was asked about brothers. But *she* had seen the marginalia on the transcription folios, and one of the circled dates—the twelfth of the Frost Month—was the night Ansel had first requested the archivist by name, the night the whole machinery of her double life had begun to turn, and Corvane circled dates the way a man circles prey.

At the end of the corridor, Madame Ostrev was waiting by the archive cabinet, ostensibly reorganizing files, actually watching her pass. Their eyes met for half a heartbeat. The old secretary's expression gave away nothing—but she touched her collarbone, briefly, the gesture meaningless to anyone else, and Sebastian's blood went cold and warm at once, because three days ago, in the chancery, she had watched Ostrev lock and relock her cipher drawer, and had assumed fear.

But what if it hadn't been fear?

What if the delegation's own secretary had seen the net going out, and was signaling—not to Corvane, but to whoever else was swimming outside it?

Sebastian did not stop. She climbed to the attic, sat on her cot in the dark with the baggage master snoring below her, and laid out the arithmetic of her position with terrible clarity. Two letters sewn into her coat. Ansel somewhere beyond the lines, reachable only through channels Corvane was methodically mapping. A brother alive on the far side of the war, whose life was mortgaged to a Republic's defeat. And now a secretary who might be ally, informer, or bait, and no way on earth to know which.

Trust had become currency, and she was nearly bankrupt, and somewhere downstairs a lamp was burning late in Corvane's office—she could see its glow through the frost-furred window—and she understood that whatever he was writing by that lamp tonight, tomorrow it would arrive in some form, and she would have to be ready, and being ready was no longer something a person could manage alone.

She thought of the newest letter. *Trust the officer.*

Outside, snow began again over the orchard, falling on the sleeping rows, filling the tracks of everyone who had walked there, erasing, erasing—patient as a government, gentle as a promise.
