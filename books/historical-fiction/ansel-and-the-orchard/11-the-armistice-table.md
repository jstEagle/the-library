## 11. The Armistice Table

The talks opened on the ninth of Deepwinter, in the long gallery of the estate at Vell, beneath a ceiling painted with clouds that had never once been true.

Sebastian arrived an hour before anyone else, as couriers did—invisible, necessary, early. The gallery had been chosen for its neutrality: no portraits, no banners, only those painted clouds and a pair of long tables set facing each other across a strip of worn parquet, with a third table between them for the recorders. Someone had hung green boughs from the Ostravan side and white-and-gold silk from the Vessarine, as if winter itself could be negotiated into taking sides. Frost had climbed the inside of the tall windows overnight and turned them into panes of grey lace, so that the daylight came through diffused and sourceless, like light remembered rather than seen.

She arranged her materials at the recorder's table: fresh ink, blotting sand, three nibs of different weights, and the transcription cipher—a slim folio of substitution tables keyed to the delegation's dispatch code, which she was to use to render the day's minutes into a form safe to travel by courier pouch to whatever remained of the capital by then. She had memorized most of it already. It was habit now, the way some men counted stairs or recited psalms. She read a page, closed it, wrote it out from memory, checked it, burned nothing but her own errors.

By eight o'clock the room began to fill, and she learned again what she had learned all winter: that power announced itself not by shouting but by how much space it required.

Minister Corvane came first among the Vessarines, though he entered last through the far door, timing it so that every head would turn. He wore dove-grey, almost the color of his eyes, cut close and severe, without the ribboned ostentation the other ministers favored. He took his seat at the center of the Vessarine table and did not unfold his papers. He simply folded his hands and looked down the length of the room at the empty Ostravan chairs as though they were already occupied and already losing.

The others came in clusters—Vessarine ministers in fur-trimmed coats talking over one another about grain tariffs and prisoner exchanges and the dignity of the Republic, each of them certain the war could still be won at a table if only the other side would admit it. Their aides stood behind them holding folios they had not written. Sebastian knew their faces now, knew which ones slept badly and which slept well, and she noted, as she always noted, that Corvane's aide slept very well indeed, and that Corvane himself seemed to require no sleep at all, or perhaps had made some private arrangement with the night.

At half past eight the Ostravans entered under escort: six officers in slate-blue, moving together, unhurried, and behind them Ansel.

He wore his uniform properly buttoned today, sword at his hip, and he did not look at her. That was the discipline of it—that was the whole art. His gaze crossed hers once, briefly, with the flat courtesy of a stranger cataloguing furniture, and moved on. Only someone watching for it—and she was always watching for it now, which frightened her more than any of Corvane's suspicions—would have seen the half-second pause, the way a man checks a lock he knows is sound.

*Good morning, courier,* said nothing at all.

*Good morning, officer,* said nothing back.

The sessions were called to order by a mutual exchange of credentials that took forty minutes and settled nothing, because credentials were never the question; the question was always whether either nation still existed in a form worth crediting. Then the Ostravan senior commissioner, a heavy-jawed man named Brandt whose voice sounded like gravel poured slowly, laid out terms so harsh that several Vessarine ministers actually laughed—the brittle laughter of men hearing a price quoted for something they believed priceless.

And so it went. All morning. Territorial concessions met with outrage. Outrage met with arithmetic. Arithmetic met with appeals to history, to honor, to the frozen dead of three campaigns. Sebastian transcribed it all in shorthand and thought: *we are rehearsing our own funerals and arguing over the order of the procession.*

Once, mid-morning, when Brandt had finished a long enumeration of occupied districts and the Vessarine response dissolved into overlapping protest, there was a silence—one of those silences that opens in negotiations like a crack in ice—and in it Sebastian looked up from her page and found Ansel watching her.

Not the flat courtesy this time. Something else, quickly banked, but real: the look of a man checking whether she was bearing up under the weight of the room. As if she were the one whose nation might not survive the winter. As if, of everyone in that gallery, she were the one he was concerned for.

She bent back to her minutes. Her pen did not waver. She had trained it not to waver years ago, in another life, signing another name.

But she thought: *the enemy across the table is not my enemy.* And she thought: *then who is?*

* * *

Ansel had sat through enough parleys to know that words at such tables are ballast, thrown out to keep the ship level while the real cargo is moved below decks. He had also sat through enough to know when a delegation has already decided to lose.

The Vessarines had decided. Not all of them—the old minister Vasquez still fought for every clause with the genuine fury of a believer—but the center of gravity had shifted, and Ansel could feel it the way one feels a floor beginning to tilt. They argued magnificently. They conceded nothing. And underneath the performance ran a current he recognized from his own army in its worst year: the energy of men no longer defending a country but positioning themselves within its ruins.

He watched Corvane especially.

It was instructive. Most ambitious men leaked ambition from every seam; you could read them like weather. Corvane gave off nothing. He objected to clauses he plainly intended to accept later, accepted points he plainly intended to break, and throughout it all maintained the demeanor of a man doing tedious but necessary bookkeeping for a state he loved. When Brandt demanded the demilitarization of the river provinces, Corvane's objection was measured, legalistic, almost reasonable—and Ansel saw two of the Vessarine ministers glance at him before speaking, the way soldiers glance at an officer before advancing. Not because he held formal command. Because they had already begun to obey.

*There,* Ansel thought. *That's the shape of it.*

His own commission was straightforward: prolong the talks through the thaw if possible, extract whatever concessions cost Ostrava nothing, and assess whether the Republic possessed any authority left worth treating with. He performed his part. He raised the question of prisoners with genuine persistence—he had seen the prison camps and had no appetite for the men freezing in them—and he watched Corvane concede the point with suspicious ease, giving away lives that cost him nothing to buy credibility he would spend later.

But it was the courier he kept returning to, in the gaps between clauses.

Sebastian Underhill—or whoever she truly was—sat at the recorder's table with ink on the side of her right hand where no amount of care quite prevented it, writing the day down as though the day deserved accuracy. He had spent three nights composing reasons why he should not think about her and had discarded each reason as insufficient. There was a loneliness in the way she worked that he recognized the way one recognizes one's own handwriting: total absorption, perfect competence, and beneath both, a silence where a life ought to be.

He knew what she carried against her ribs. Tomas had told him what his sister would be carrying, if she lived, if she'd kept to the service. *She'll have the codebook memorized,* Tomas had said, with the helpless pride of a brother describing a thing too bright to look at directly. *All of it. She does that. She'll know it better than the people who wrote it.*

And so Ansel, listening to Minister Vasquez denounce the occupation of the river provinces for the fourth time, found himself doing arithmetic of a different kind: what the Republic's enemies would pay for a mind like hers, and what her own masters would do to her if they ever suspected what she carried in her memory—and which side of that ledger her survival actually lay on.

The answer troubled him. He was an officer of Ostrava, and the answer troubled him anyway, because it did not divide along the lines the war drew.

In the afternoon session, a dispute over supply corridors collapsed into procedural wrangling, and during the shuffling of papers Ansel rose to stretch his legs—a permitted liberty, officers did it constantly—and passed behind the recorder's table. He did not stop. He did not speak. But as he passed, he said, in Ostravan, pitched for no one but her, the kind of sentence that appears in no treaty:

"The orchard trees survived the frost. The plum row especially."

Then he resumed his place, and the wrangling resumed above him, and Sebastian Underhill wrote down every word of it except those five, which she carried somewhere no cipher could reach.

* * *

They broke at dusk, and the gallery emptied by rank: ministers first, aides next, guards last. Sebastian remained to collate the day's minutes, because that was her function and her camouflage, and because the work was genuinely endless.

By lamplight she rendered the day into cipher. Terms proposed. Terms refused. Prisoner exchanges agreed in principle. The river provinces disputed. Supply corridors unresolved. Session to resume at nine.

It was meticulous work, and she was good at it, and that was precisely the problem—because being good at it meant she noticed things.

She noticed, first, small discrepancies in the official summary she'd been given to transcribe from. The delegation's chief secretary prepared a fair copy each evening, and the fair copies had begun, subtly, to differ from what had actually been said. Nothing that changed meaning outright. A softened refusal here. A concession recorded as "under discussion" when it had been flatly rejected aloud. A request from Brandt about prisoner rations that appeared nowhere at all—as if it had been spoken into a cloth.

Individually, each error was nothing. Together, they were a pattern, and patterns had authors.

She compared three days' worth, working past midnight, her lamp trimmed low, the frost on the windows thickening as the cold deepened outside. And the authorship resolved itself the way images resolve in a developing bath: gradually, then all at once.

Every alteration served the same end. Every change made the Republic's position appear firmer than it was—made the ministers appear united when they were fractious, made refusals appear strategic when they were merely stubborn, made failure appear to be patience. Whoever edited these minutes wanted the capital to believe the armistice was progressing toward acceptable terms. Wanted the government calm. Wanted it confident.

Wanted it, she understood with a cold that had nothing to do with the season, *asleep.*

Because a delegation that reported success would send no reinforcements. A ministry that believed peace was near would make no contingency plans. A capital that trusted its negotiators would be exactly as unprepared as a capital could be when those same negotiators returned not with a treaty but with a story: that the Republic had been betrayed at the table, sold out by incompetents and cowards, and that only one man had seen clearly enough to salvage anything from the ruin.

She sat back. The lamp flame stood perfectly still in the windless room.

Corvane.

Not sabotage of the talks—she had feared that, imagined poisoned clauses, deliberately botched translations. This was worse and quieter. Corvane was not sabotaging the peace. He was *curating* it—editing the record so that when the talks failed, and they would fail, the failure would arrive at the capital wrapped in surprise, and surprise was the mother of every coup ever staged. A nation that believes itself at peace cannot resist the man who tells it the war was stolen from it. And a man who controls the record—

Her hand had stopped moving. Ink pooled at the nib. She blotted it, mechanically, and realized she was shaking, not with fear exactly, but with the specific vertigo of a person discovering that the floor she has stood on all her life was a stage, and the scenery was canvas, and someone else had been writing her lines for months.

She thought of Tomas, alive because Ostrava protected him, silent because the Republic still breathed. She thought of the codebook against her ribs, memorized, useless and lethal. She thought of the letters hidden in the lining of her coat, and of Ansel's voice passing behind her in Ostravan—*the plum row survived*—a message in the only language the two of them had left: tenderness disguised as small talk, truth smuggled inside the mundane.

The enemy across the table, she thought, is not the true enemy. The true enemy sits on my own side of it, editing the minutes by lamplight in another room tonight, wearing dove-grey, folding his hands, waiting for his country to die so he can inherit the corpse.

She finished the cipher transcription. It was correct—it was scrupulously, damningly correct, every word as spoken, including the omissions she had restored. Let Corvane's fair copy go to the capital gilded. If her version ever surfaced beside it, the discrepancies themselves would be evidence.

Or it would be evidence against her. She understood that too. A courier who contradicted the chief secretary's record was not a witness; she was a traitor. The truth, in Corvane's architecture, was already treason.

She sanded the pages, squared them, tied the pouch, and sat alone in the great gallery with its painted clouds overhead—clouds that had never once been true, painted by some court artist who had surely known it, paid well to make the ceiling lie beautifully.

Somewhere below, a door closed softly. Footsteps in the corridor, unhurried, passing the recorder's room without pausing, continuing on.

She blew out the lamp and waited for her eyes to adjust, and in the dark she made herself a promise of a new kind: that whatever happened to the Republic—to Vessarine, to the flag, to the whole gilded fiction of it—she would keep one record honest. Even if she was the only one who ever read it. Even if keeping it killed her.

The frost on the windows was thicker now. Through it, faintly, she could see the orchard, the black rows of trees standing in the snow, patient as graves and more hopeful than any grave had a right to be.

Tomorrow the talks resumed at nine. And tomorrow, she would need to find a way to warn Ansel that the man across the table was playing a longer game than either nation knew—

—if she could find a way to tell him without becoming, in Corvane's net, the traitor he was already hunting.
