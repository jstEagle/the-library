## 12. Cuttings

The knife was meant for apples.

Sebastian had taken it from the estate kitchen three days after their arrival at Vell—a short paring blade with a bone handle worn smooth by generations of cooks—and no one had thought to miss it. A courier carried a hundred small tools of her trade: wax seals, cipher wheels the size of coins, inks that vanished or appeared depending on the heat applied. No one looked twice at a woman's knife. That was the whole architecture of her invisibility, and she had built her life upon it, stone by patient stone.

Now she knelt in the frozen rows of the orchard with that small blade against her wrist-bone for warmth, waiting for the light to come up over the eastern wall.

The cold was total. It had settled into Vell like a tenant who intended to stay past the lease, pressing down through the broken panes of the orangery, sealing the fountain in its basin, turning the gravel paths to iron. The estate had belonged to some merchant family before the war—Sebastian had found a child's arithmetic slate behind a cabinet in the library, half-finished sums still chalked on it, a lesson abandoned mid-division years ago—and now it housed two delegations pretending, in adjacent wings, that they were not enemies. The Republic's ministers slept behind one corridor of doors; Ostravan officers came and went under flags of parley through the gatehouse; and between them moved servants, clerks, guards, and couriers, all of them listening, none of them speaking plainly.

In the orchard, at least, there was no one to perform for.

The trees were old and had been loved once. Someone had pruned them properly for years—the cuts healed clean, collars raised like careful calluses—and then someone had stopped. Now the rows stood unmanaged, canes whipping loose, water sprouts crowding the centers, snow sitting in the crotches of the branches like unpaid debts. Sebastian walked the rows every morning when she could escape the archive room. It was the one habit from her old life that had survived the war intact. Tomas had taught it to her. Everything in this orchard, in a way, had been taught to her by Tomas.

She stopped at a Winter Pearmain near the south wall, one of the few trees here she recognized from her father's nursery catalog, and set the edge of the paring knife against a healthy year-old shoot.

*You always cut at an angle,* he had told her, eleven years old and insufferably certain, his hands steadying hers around the grafting knife. *Slant cut, because the tree doesn't owe you a flat surface. You meet the wood where it is.*

She made the cut. The shoot came away pale-green at the wound, astonishingly alive inside all that grey weather, and she held it to her lips without thinking, breathing warmth onto the exposed cambium as if she could apologize to it.

Three more shoots, each the length of her finger, each cut at the slant. She wrapped them in cloth torn from the hem of an undershirt and tucked them inside her coat, against the same ribs where the codebook rode. Her body was becoming a satchel of contraband. She supposed there were worse things to be smuggled than living wood.

Because this was the message. This was the only message she dared send.

* * *

She had worked out the grammar of it over four sleepless nights, lying in the narrow servant's cot with her back to the wall and her mind running the problem like water finding cracks.

Ansel would go back to Ostravan lines eventually—parley did not last forever, and neither did winter. When he went, he had promised nothing, but he had looked at her across the frozen fountain two nights ago and said, in the flat careful voice he used when he was being scrupulously honest, *If you ever need something carried, I am the only man in either camp whose loyalties are simple.* She had almost laughed. No one's loyalties were simple. His least of all—he served a kingdom that was sheltering her brother, and she served a republic that would hang him for it if it knew, and somewhere between those facts the two of them kept meeting in empty rooms and saying true things, which was more dangerous than anything either government had yet attempted.

But if Ansel would carry something, it could not be paper. Paper was searched. Paper was read. Paper, in Corvane's tightening net, was death.

Wood, though. Wood was innocent. A bundle of scions wrapped in cloth looked like what it was: a gardener's habit, a souvenir, a whim. And Tomas—

Tomas would understand.

Their family orchard had been planted by their grandfather as a kind of sentence written over decades. Every variety had been chosen for a reason, and every reason had a story attached, and Tomas and Sebastian had spent their childhood being marched down the rows while their father recited the liturgy: *the Gravenstein your grandmother brought as a cutting from her mother's yard; the quince that failed twice before it took; the Winter Pearmain we grafted the year you were born, Sebastian, so the tree and you would come ripe together.* Tomas had absorbed it all and gone further, grafting experiments of his own by fourteen, labeling his crosses with a private code of notches cut into the parent limbs—one notch for the mother stock, two for the pollen parent, a cross-hatch where the graft union sat. He used to say that any fool could read a ledger, but a real orchardist could read a tree.

So: she had cut her scions from the Winter Pearmain. And in the evenings since, in the archive room after hours, working by candlelight with the official minutes stacked innocently beside her, she had prepared them. One scion left plain—that was the greeting, *I live, I remember*. One scion notched once, low on its side, with the point of her knife: *mother stock*, which between them had always meant home, the orchard, the family that remained. One notched twice: *pollen parent*, which had meant the future, the thing you chose rather than inherited. And the third she had cut with the cross-hatch at the union point and then, after a long moment with the blade trembling slightly, a fourth mark—a single diagonal slash above the others that existed in no system Tomas had ever taught her.

It meant: *I know what you did.* Or perhaps it meant: *I am coming anyway.* She had not decided which, and she suspected the ambiguity was honest. Both were true. She wanted him to receive both truths in the same hand, because that was how she was carrying them.

She sealed the cut ends with wax melted from a stub of the archive candles—not sealing wax, nothing official, just ordinary candle wax, another layer of innocence—and bound the three marked scions together with thread pulled from the fringe of a runner in the east hall. The plain one she kept separate. She had not yet decided whether she could bear to give it away. Some part of her wanted one piece of the orchard to remain hers alone, unwritten, carrying no message to anyone. She was beginning to understand that a person could be erased so thoroughly that even her own gestures needed one private corner, or she would forget what her own handwriting looked like.

*You're doing it again,* she told herself, tying off the thread. *Talking to yourself in the second person. Tomas said that was the first sign of a spy.*

Tomas said a lot of things. Tomas was alive. Some mornings she woke with the knowledge sitting on her chest like a cat, warm and impossible, and had to lie still and let it become believable again. *He is alive. He defected. He traded—something—for his life, and the something may have been blood, and the kingdom holding him will only keep holding him while the war goes their way, and the armistice talks at which I take the minutes are the mechanism by which the war might stop.* Which meant, in the coldest accounting she could make of it, that her brother's safety was indexed against the failure of the very peace she was paid to record faithfully.

She had not let herself finish that thought. Not entirely. But it sat in her like the scions rode against her ribs: alive, green at the core, wrapped in cloth.

* * *

The trouble with Vell was that the house watched back.

Every corridor had its listener. Minister Corvane had begun his quiet campaign—Sebastian had seen the first fruits of it that week, a junior clerk escorted from the map room with his papers confiscated, two of the household staff dismissed without explanation and marched out through the snow toward whatever waited for suspected sympathizers—and the effect on the delegation was a new silence, a carefulness, people choosing corridors the way gamblers choose seats. Corvane himself moved through the halls with the unhurried satisfaction of a man checking traps he had set personally. He had smiled at Sebastian in the passage outside the archive two days ago and asked, pleasantly, whether she found the work at Vell congenial.

*I go where the Republic sends me,* she had answered, and watched him decide, visibly, that she was furniture. Furniture was safe. Furniture was also, she was beginning to realize, the thing that got burned first when the fire needed feeding.

So she went to the orchard at first light, when the guards changed shifts and the sentry on the garden wall stamped his feet and stared at the horizon rather than the rows below. She had mapped his habits the way she'd once mapped the capital's checkpoint rotations: he looked east at dawn because the sun came up over the ridge and he was young and homesick and probably watching for letters that never came. For twenty minutes, maybe twenty-five, the orchard was hers.

This morning she had a purpose beyond walking. She wanted to see whether the Pearmain had other shoots worth taking—whether the message could be duplicated, in case Ansel's route failed or the first bundle froze—and she wanted, though she did not name it to herself, simply to stand among trees for a while before she spent another day transcribing men deciding how best to betray each other.

She was reaching into the second row, testing the flex of a cane, when the gravel crunched.

Not the sentry. The sentry stayed on the wall. This was footsteps on the path, unhurried, deliberate, and a voice—Corvane's voice—low and conversational, addressing someone she couldn't see: "—no, leave the gardener's shed locked. If anything in this house has been misused, it will be in there."

Sebastian went still among the branches, one hand on the Pearmain's trunk, snow soaking through the knee of her trousers. Through the lattice of bare twigs she could see them: Corvane, gloved hands clasped behind his back, and beside him Sergeant Havel, one of the delegation's own guards, the one loyal—if her mapping was right—not to the Republic but to Corvane personally, which in the final arithmetic of a dying state amounted to the same thing and worse.

"And the orchard?" Havel asked.

"The orchard." Corvane considered it the way he considered everything, as property awaiting valuation. "No one tends it. If anyone is seen in it, note who and when. There are worse places to pass a message than a garden. Roots, cuttings, all sorts of innocent horticulture." He smiled at his own wit. "People think dirt makes things invisible. Dirt only hides what's already buried."

They turned down the row toward her.

There was nowhere to go. The wall was twelve feet of ice-slicked stone; the rows ran straight and open; the nearest cover was the collapsed cold frame thirty paces north, and Corvane walked like a man who noticed everything, because noticing was the entire engine of his advancement. Sebastian's mind performed its cold inventory—knife, scions, the wet prints her knees had left in the snow, the fact that a courier found kneeling in a forbidden orchard at dawn was not a crime but was certainly a question, and questions, these days, were how people disappeared.

Then a second set of footsteps came through the gatehouse arch, and a voice she knew said, easily, "Minister."

Ansel.

He came down the path with his breath steaming and his officer's coat buttoned to the throat, carrying nothing, armed with nothing visible, and Sebastian watched him do the calculation in a single flick of his eyes—the row, her position, the knee-deep snow, the angle of Corvane's approach—and begin talking before the arithmetic finished.

"I hoped to find you. Our quartermaster tells me your steward has been refusing our requisitions for lamp oil. I'm given to understand the oil is stored in the gardener's shed." He said it mildly, the complaint of one professional to another about logistics, boring as bread.

"Your requisitions," Corvane said, "are noted. The shed is locked pending inventory."

"Inventories are wise. Though I'd caution against opening it before the thaw—the oil's frozen solid regardless, and my men have candles enough." Ansel glanced down the row, past Corvane's shoulder, and for half a heartbeat his gaze touched the place where Sebastian stood among the branches. Nothing in his face moved. "Actually, Minister, since I've caught you—there's a matter for the afternoon session. Your Chancellor's proposed demilitarization schedule. My superiors want me to sound you out informally before it reaches the table. Do you have a quarter hour?"

Corvane's attention shifted, the way a predator's shifts when larger game crosses its ground. Informal sounding-out meant leverage; leverage meant advantage; advantage was Corvane's native food. "Walk with me," he said, already turning. "Havel, see the gate secured."

They went. The gravel crunched away toward the formal gardens, Corvane's voice dropping into the register of men discussing the division of a carcass. Havel lingered a moment, scanning the rows with the dull thoroughness of his trade, and Sebastian pressed herself against the trunk and became, with all the skill of ten years' practice, a shadow among shadows—until the sergeant shrugged and followed his master.

The orchard exhaled. So did she.

Ansel did not look back. That was the discipline of it, she thought later—no glance over the shoulder, no signal, nothing a watcher could read. He simply walked Corvane into the morning and kept him there, and it was nearly an hour before a shape detached itself from the yew walk at the orchard's northern corner and came toward her through the snow, and she knew his stride before she saw his face.

"You heard," she said, when he reached her.

"Most of it." He stopped a careful distance away, close enough to speak low, far enough that no window could make a story of it. "He's going to inventory the shed. And now he's watching the orchard."

"Because of me."

"Because of everyone. You especially, lately—you're the one who handles the minutes." He said it without accusation, which was somehow worse. "Sebastian. What were you cutting?"

She should have lied. She had a dozen lies racked and ready, courier's lies, furniture's lies. Instead she reached inside her coat and drew out the bundle—the plain scion separate in her pocket, the three marked ones tied in their cloth—and held it between them on her open palm, the wax-sealed cuts pale as bones in the grey light.

His eyes went from the scions to her face. He didn't touch them.

"Tomas," she said. "It's for Tomas. Grafting marks. He'll know what they say. No one else in the world could read them—it's a language our family invented, before the war, before any of this—" Her voice snagged and she hated it, and steadied. "You said your loyalties were simple. I'm asking you to carry a bundle of sticks across a border. That's all it is. Wood and wax. If you're searched, it's nothing. If you're not—"

"If I'm not," Ansel said quietly, "it's the most dangerous cargo in Ostrava."

The wind moved through the bare rows with a sound like paper turning. Somewhere on the wall the young sentry stamped his feet against the cold, watching his sunrise.

"He thinks I don't know what he did," Sebastian said. "Or he hopes so. I need him to know that I know, and that I'm coming anyway, and I can't write that. Writing gets people killed. This—" she turned the bundle slightly, and the cross-hatched mark caught the light, "—this just looks like gardening."

For a long moment Ansel studied the scion, and she realized he wasn't reading the marks. He was reading her. The way he'd assessed her across the negotiation table, she thought—the only honest person in the room—but closer now, and slower, as if honesty at this range required different instruments.

"My grandmother kept bees," he said at last. "In a village that isn't on maps anymore. She said you could tell everything about a family by what they planted, because planting is the only promise people make to a future they won't see." He took the bundle from her palm with both hands, careful as a man receiving a relic, and tucked it inside his coat against the same place she had carried it. Warmth passing from her body to the wood to his. "I'll get it to him. And Sebastian—" he hesitated, and for once the professional mask slipped enough that she could see the man beneath it, tired and young and as alone as she was, "—when the answer comes back, however he answers, you shouldn't be the one to receive it alone. Come to the orangery. After the evening session. Say you're checking the glass for breaks."

"That's not a secure meeting."

"No," he agreed. "It isn't. Neither is this one, and here we are." He stepped back, resuming his officer's posture piece by piece, armor going on. "Two days. If I can."

He was ten paces gone when she called, too low for any wall to catch it, "Why do you keep helping me?"

He paused. Snow gathered on his shoulders. When he answered he didn't turn around, and the words came back to her across the frozen rows like something carried on the wind from very far away:

"Because you're the first person in two years who wants something that isn't power."

Then he was through the yew walk and gone, and Sebastian stood alone among the trees with her empty palms and her hammering heart, and understood, with the slow inevitability of a graft taking hold, that something in her had crossed a line the codes and ciphers had never drawn.

Duty was a dead limb. She had known it for weeks, the way an orchardist knows—by the color under the bark, the give of the wood. And into the space where duty had been, something else was setting roots: dangerous, out of season, alive.

That night, taking the minutes of a session in which Minister Corvane proposed, with exquisite reasonableness, a delay of the armistice schedule "pending verification of good faith"—a delay that would serve no one but himself—Sebastian wrote the words down in her precise clerk's hand and felt the knife-mark on her soul the way the Pearmain must feel the cut: as loss, and as the necessary beginning of something new.

And in the margin of her private memorandum, in a notation no cipher clerk would flag because it looked like a horticultural aside, she wrote: *Cuttings taken. Carrier secured. Awaiting reply.*

Below it, after a moment, she added a single line that was not code at all:

*Tomas—I am coming. Whatever you did, I am coming.*
