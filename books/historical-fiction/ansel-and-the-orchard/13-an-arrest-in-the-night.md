## 13. An Arrest in the Night

Ansel had learned to sleep in war the way other men learned to pray—irregularly, and with a constant low awareness that he might be interrupted at any moment by something worse than silence. So when the dogs began in the courtyard of the Vell estate at half past two in the morning, he was already awake, lying fully dressed on top of his blankets, listening.

The sound was wrong. The garrison kept two hounds, both old, both lazy, both inclined to bark at owls and nothing else. These were not the garrison's hounds. These were animals brought on chains, animals with work in their voices, and they were barking at the servants' wing, where the Republic's junior clerks slept three to a room like spoons in a drawer.

He rose without lighting a lamp. In four days of parley at Vell he had made a careful map of the estate in his head—the way Corvane's men had quietly taken over the gatehouse, the way the delegation's own guards had been rotated until Ansel could no longer say whose man stood where, the way Minister Corvane had begun taking his meals separately from the rest of the delegation, as though he were already a head of state and merely waiting for the paperwork. Ansel had reported all of it to his own superiors in flat, unadorned dispatches: *the Republic does not send one voice to this table; it sends several, and they are not singing together.*

What he had not reported—and what sat in his chest now like a swallowed coal—was the courier.

Sebastian Underhill. A name too grand for the stooped, ink-stained drudge who carried dispatches between the delegations and never met anyone's eye. Ansel had catalogued him first as furniture: a thin young man with chapped hands and a habit of standing near doors, useful and invisible, the way good staff are supposed to be. Then Ansel had seen him in the orchard, alone, in the blue hour before dawn, laying his bare palm against the frozen bark of an apple tree with such naked tenderness that Ansel had felt, absurdly, like a trespasser in his own thoughts.

And then there was the matter of Tomas.

Ansel crossed to the window and eased back the shutter a finger's width. Snow was falling again, fine and dry, hissing against the glass. Below, lanterns moved through the courtyard in a pattern that had nothing casual about it—a cordon forming around the servants' stair, two men at the well, one posted at the kitchen door with his back deliberately to the wind so his hands stayed free.

Corvane's men. Not delegation guards. Not Ostravan observers. This was a private operation, mounted at night, without the knowledge of either army.

*Someone has leaked,* Ansel thought, and then corrected himself with the cold precision that had kept him alive through six years of intelligence work: *Someone is believed to have leaked. Those are different things, and the difference is usually a corpse.*

* * *

They brought the clerk out at dawn.

Ansel watched from the stable loft, where he had established a legitimate reason to be—the Ostravan escort's horses required attention, and he preferred to give it himself rather than trust them to foreign grooms—and saw them march the boy across the frozen yard with his arms folded up inside his own coat because his hands had been bound behind him and someone had decided, in the interest of appearances, that ropes should not show.

His name was Pellin. Ansel knew it the way he knew everything about the delegation, which was to say thoroughly and shamelessly: twenty-two years old, third son of a wool merchant from the river provinces, joined the diplomatic service because it promised him he would see the world before it saw him. He had a stutter that surfaced when he was frightened, and he copied minutes in a beautiful round hand, and he was, by every observation Ansel had made across eleven days of watching the Republic's people tear at each other with quiet knives, exactly what he appeared to be: a nobody. A boy who watered the ministers' inkwells and dreamed of home.

Which meant he was precisely the kind of man Corvane would arrest.

Not because he was guilty. Because he was safe. Because arresting a nobody sent a message that cost nothing, and because somewhere in the boy's rounds of copying and carrying there would be some thread—an overheard phrase repeated out of turn, a document shelved in the wrong box—that could be pulled until the whole cloth of the delegation's paranoia unraveled in whatever direction Corvane wished.

And Ansel knew, with a certainty that arrived in his stomach before his mind, in which direction Corvane wished it to unravel.

Because Ansel himself had been careless.

Twice, only twice—but in this trade *only twice* was already once too often. The first time had been in the orchard, the second meeting, when Sebastian had come to him among the frozen rows and they had spoken of Tomas for the better part of an hour with no more cover than the dark and the snow. The second time had been the cuttings. He had watched her wrap them—six slender scions cut from the estate's oldest apple tree, bound in damp sacking, labeled in a grafting shorthand no clerk would use—and he had agreed to carry them across the lines, and the agreement had passed between them in a look that lasted perhaps two seconds but contained, he suspected, enough meaning to hang them both if the wrong eyes had caught it.

Had anyone caught it? He turned the question over as he forked straw, keeping his hands moving, keeping his face set in the bored expression of a soldier minding horses. Down in the yard, Pellin stumbled on the iced-over trough and was hauled upright by the collar.

If Corvane had proof, there would be no arrest of a junior clerk. There would be an arrest of a courier—or, better, a quiet disappearance, a body in the well, a story about desertion. No. The boy was a lever, not a verdict. Corvane had suspicion and wanted shape. He would squeeze the clerk until the boy gave the suspicion a name.

And there was only one name it could become. The courier touched every document in the delegation. The courier was everywhere and nowhere, seen by everyone and known by no one. If you were building a traitor out of spare parts, you could not ask for better timber.

Ansel put down the fork. His hands were steady, which surprised him. Inside, something was moving fast and cold, running down corridors of consequence.

*Tonight,* he thought. Or tomorrow night at the latest. Whatever Corvane intended to do with the boy, he would do it quickly and off the books, because even a man as ambitious as Corvane understood that torture conducted under a flag of parley was a thing that could not survive daylight. Which meant Ansel had perhaps thirty hours to move a warning into Sebastian Underhill's hands without being seen delivering it—in a house where every corridor now had ears, in a court where the Ostravan envoy was watched as closely as any prisoner, to a person whose entire survival depended on never being noticed at all.

He looked at the falling snow, at the grey light coming up over the orchard, at the black shapes of the trees standing in their rows like a congregation waiting for a verdict.

Then he went to find a book.

* * *

The library at Vell had been the pride of whoever had built the estate, and its abandonment had been slow enough to leave dignity behind: shelves still stocked, dust sheets still draped over the reading chairs, a smell of cold paper and mouse. Both delegations used it, warily, because both delegations needed somewhere to spread maps, and because the alternative was working in rooms with walls that listened.

It also had a lending custom. Ansel had noticed it in the first week: the junior staff borrowed books and returned them, and the books circulated like small tame birds, passing from hand to hand with a freedom that no document enjoyed. Nobody searched a novel. Nobody logged a novel. A novel was beneath suspicion, which was the most valuable property anything could possess in a house full of spies.

At midmorning, while Corvane's clerks argued over the seating protocol for the afternoon session, Ansel took down a volume from the eastern shelf—a collection of Vessarine pastoral poetry, chosen because it was worn, because it was dull enough that no one would fight over it, and because its spine had already been cracked in three places by other readers. In the hour before the afternoon session he sat with it by the fire and worked with a pencil stub and infinite patience, writing in the margins in a hand that mimicked the idle annotations of a previous owner: small marks, faint, deniable.

In the margin beside a poem about winter pruning, he wrote:

*Your friend the clerk has been taken. They will make him sing tonight. He knows your face, your rounds, your hours. He will name the courier whether or not he means to. Do not go to the records room after dark. Do not take the east stair. Burn what cannot be memorized. Trust the orchard and nothing else.*

He read it back four times, adjusting words. *Your friend*—was that too much? It told her how much he knew, and knowing how much he knew told her he had been watching her, and being watched was either comfort or threat depending entirely on the heart of the watcher. But softening it would cost clarity, and clarity was the whole point. He left it.

*Trust the orchard.* That was the message under the message: *I will be there. I will get you out.* He did not write that part. Some things must travel unwritten or not at all.

The difficulty remained delivery. He considered the obvious routes and rejected each in turn: handing it to her directly was impossible in daylight—she had not come within ten paces of him since the second meeting, and her avoidance was itself a discipline he admired; leaving it in her quarters risked discovery by whichever of Corvane's men had surely begun searching the staff rooms; giving it to a servant introduced a variable he could not control.

But the book did not need delivering. The book needed only to enter circulation.

At the end of the afternoon session, when the ministers retired to argue in smaller rooms and the clerks gathered the papers, Ansel left the volume on the library table amid the abandoned maps—open, face-down, the way a reader leaves a book he intends to return to. Then he walked out, took his evening meal in view of a dozen witnesses, and waited.

The book moved within the hour. He saw it go—one of the delegation's junior attachés picked it up on her way through, glanced at the title, shrugged, and tucked it under her arm toward the staff wing, toward the little economy of borrowed things that operated below the notice of ministers. From her it would pass, as all the books passed, hand to hand, shelf to shelf, until it reached the hands it was addressed to. Sebastian Underhill handled the staff's correspondence and ran their small errands; she was the circulation of that world. The book would find her the way water finds a drain.

Whether she would understand—whether she would believe—he could not know. He could only sit through the interminable dinner, answering the Ostravan envoy's questions about supply lines with half his mind while the other half counted exits and watched the door for a thin, stoop-shouldered courier who did not appear.

She did not appear at supper. She did not appear at the changing of the watch. Ansel told himself this meant nothing; couriers ate apart, worked late, lived in the cracks of the schedule. He told himself a great many things, and none of them stopped the coal in his chest from burning.

* * *

They began on Pellin shortly after midnight, and they were not subtle about it.

Ansel's quarters faced the interior court, and the sounds carried through the frozen air with terrible fidelity: boots, a door, the dogs again, and then—after a silence long enough to hope—voices. Not shouting. That was the worst of it. Corvane's men did their work quietly, in the manner of men who have done it before and expect to do it again. What came through the wall was mostly rhythm: a question, a pause, a sound, a question again. Once, a single high note of the boy's voice, breaking upward, the stutter gone to pieces.

Ansel lay in the dark and made himself listen, because he owed the boy that much at least—not to flinch away from the cost of the machine he served. Every intelligence service had its cellar. His own was no cleaner; he knew what happened in the rooms beneath the Ostravan garrison at Kessel, and he had signed the requisitions that kept those rooms supplied. War did not have innocent sides. It had only sides, and people, and the distance between the two.

But knowing the machinery was universal did not warm the night. Somewhere below him a wool merchant's son was learning what his government thought of nobodies, and Ansel could do nothing—less than nothing, because intervention would burn not only himself but the one person left in this frozen estate whom he had any wish to save.

He thought of Tomas instead. Tomas, who had sat across from Ansel in a whitewashed room in Ostrava two years ago, gaunt and guarded, trading what he knew for what his sister needed, and who had said—Ansel had never forgotten it—*You think I sold my country. I didn't. My country was an orchard and a girl who could graft a cutting better than I could, and I sold everything except that. Find her someday. Tell her the trees remember.*

He had not told her yet. He had given her letters instead, letters that said in Tomas's oblique, loving cipher what Tomas could not say openly while he lived under Ostravan protection. And Ansel had watched her read them, and had understood that he was watching a woman decide, letter by letter, night by night, to betray everything she had ever served for the sake of one promise made among apple trees.

He had admired her then. Lying awake to the muffled rhythm of the interrogation, he discovered the admiration had grown roots of its own, and that these roots were not professional. When had that happened? In the orchard, with the snow falling between them and her breath making small clouds in the dark? Earlier, watching her carry dispatches through hostile rooms with her chin tucked and her eyes down, doing invisibility the way swordsmen do forms—perfectly, daily, at cost? He did not know. It hardly mattered. What mattered was that in approximately thirty hours, if Corvane's machine worked as machines do, a name would surface from that cellar, and the name would be hers, and everything she had carried—codes, letters, cuttings, hope—would be weighed in a room with no windows.

Unless she was gone before the name surfaced.

He rose and dressed. Through the shutter crack, the orchard was a field of pale rows under a sky going slowly from black to iron. Snow had stopped. The moon was a fingernail clipping behind cloud. Perfect weather for a message that could not be written down.

But no—too soon. Her window was dark; the household still stirred; a figure crossing the yard now would be remembered by every sentry Corvane had salted through the grounds. Patience. The book would reach her by morning. She would read the margins. And then—

A floorboard shifted somewhere above him. Footsteps, light, unhurried, moving along the gallery past his door. Ansel went still. The steps paused outside his room—two seconds, three—and moved on. Someone was walking the corridors at three in the morning, checking doors, counting sleepers. Corvane's net, drawn tighter.

And then, faintly, from the direction of the servants' wing: a window opening. The groan of old wood, quickly stifled. A long silence. The window closing.

Ansel smiled in the dark, and the smile hurt his face.

Somewhere in that wing, a woman who had spent months perfecting her own erasure was awake, and reading, and—God willing—understanding. By dawn she would have burned what could be burned and memorized what could not. By tomorrow night she would know that the cellar had a name ready, and that the name was hers.

She could no longer stay invisible. That was the truth of it, and he wondered if she had grasped it yet herself, or if she still believed there was some corner of the schedule deep enough to hide in. There wasn't. The moment Pellin spoke her description—*thin, young, stoops, carries the dispatches*—she would become the most visible person in the Vessarine Republic, hunted by her own side, disowned by her superiors, worth more dead than alive to the man who caught her.

There was one road out of Vell, and it ran through the orchard, and it ended beyond the lines in country that called her enemy.

Ansel checked that his dispatch case held what it needed to hold—blank passes, a route map, the token that would bring his own patrols to a halt instead of opening fire—and set it by the door.

Then he sat down to wait for morning, listening to the snow begin again, softly, like something trying to cover everything before anyone could see what had been done.
