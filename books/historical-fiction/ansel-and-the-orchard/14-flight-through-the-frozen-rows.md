## 14. Flight Through the Frozen Rows

The knock came at the hour when even the guards dozed against their pikes, three soft raps on the door of her quarters that were not the watchman's rhythm. Sebastian had been awake anyway, dressed, the codebook already strapped beneath her coat against her ribs where it had lived for eleven weeks. She had known this night would come the way one knows a storm is coming—not by seeing it, but by tasting it in the air.

She did not open the door. She waited for the second signal, and when it came—two raps, then silence, then one—she blew out her candle and slipped into the corridor.

The estate at Vell was dark in the way of large houses losing their war: shutters nailed, wings closed off, candles rationed to the rooms where ministers still pretended to govern. Sebastian moved through that darkness the way she had learned to move through everything, quietly and without claiming space, a shadow wearing a courier's drab coat. In the servants' stair she passed the clerk's door. It stood open. The room beyond had been stripped—mattress gone, papers gone, even the nail from which he'd hung his spare shirt unscrewed and carried off, as though Corvane's men wanted no evidence that a person had ever existed there.

*They took him two nights ago,* she reminded herself. *They broke him last night.* And tonight they would come for the courier whose name a terrified man had given them between sobs.

Her breath fogged in the stairwell. She counted her assets as Ansel had taught her to count them, back in the orchard, in another life eleven days long: the letters sewn into her left sleeve, Tomas's handwriting folded against her wrist like a second pulse. The codebook against her right side. A knife she barely knew how to use. And somewhere below her, past the frozen rows of apple trees, an Ostravan officer waiting under a moonless sky with horses and a plan.

Everything else—the delegation, the Republic, the ministers who had stopped pretending the armistice mattered—could burn.

She reached the kitchen door and stopped.

Lantern light moved in the yard beyond the glass. Not one lantern. Three, spread in a line, moving toward the orchard gate. She pressed herself flat against the cold stone beside the door frame and watched through the frost-furred pane as the search party fanned out—she recognized the loping walk of Captain Idris, Corvane's creature, and behind him two men she'd served coffee to, laughed with, lied to daily for months. One of them, young Pellam, held a lantern up to the first row of trees, and the light slid across the black branches like a hand searching a body.

They were not searching the house. They were cutting off the orchard.

*He told them,* she thought, and the thought arrived without heat, without surprise. *Of course he told them. Everyone tells them.*

But Ansel was out there. Ansel, who had warned her through the planted book, who had said *the third row from the wall, before the moon sets or not at all*, and if the Ostravan line of lanterns swept the orchard before she crossed it, then all of this—every risk, every lie, every small treachery stacked on small treachery—would end in a cellar in Vell with a lamp in her face and Corvane's patient voice asking questions she could not survive answering.

Sebastian put her hand to the codebook beneath her coat.

It was warm from her body. Eleven weeks it had ridden there, memorized twice over by now—every cipher table, every rotation key, every dead-drop protocol burned into her mind so thoroughly that she sometimes dreamed in its patterns. It was the most dangerous object in the Republic, and she had carried it like a heartbeat, so constant she had forgotten it was a choice.

It was a choice. She understood that now, standing in the freezing kitchen with lanterns blooming in the orchard. Everything about her life as Sebastian Underhill, courier, had been a series of choices disguised as necessities, and this was the last one, and it would not be disguised at all.

She unstrapped the book.

For a moment she only held it, feeling its weight, the leather gone soft with the oil of her skin. Somewhere inside those pages was the cipher that could buy Tomas's safety—and the cipher that Corvane wanted to sell to whoever paid highest, and the proof of every secret the dying Republic still possessed. If she carried it out, it became a bargaining chip. If she abandoned it, she became nothing but a woman running through snow with her brother's letters, a traitor with no leverage except love, which had never once been accepted as legal tender.

Ansel would tell her to take it. Any sensible officer would tell her to take it.

She opened the stove.

The coals beneath the ash still held a dull red life, banked hours ago by some cook who no longer believed in tomorrow's breakfast. Sebastian fed the codebook to them page by page, tearing the leaves free and laying each one flat so it caught properly, watching the cipher tables curl and blacken, the ink flaring green-gold before it died. She worked quickly but not frantically. There was a strange ceremony in it, she found. Eleven weeks of dread ending in smoke.

*Let Corvane hunt for his treasure,* she thought. *Let him tear the estate apart looking for what a frightened girl fed to a stove.*

When the last page crumbled she raked ash over the coals until the glow vanished, and she felt lighter and emptier than she had ever felt in her life—as though she had cut away not a burden but an organ, and would spend the rest of the night discovering whether she could live without it.

The lanterns had reached the third row of trees.

Sebastian pulled her hood up and opened the kitchen door onto the cold.

* * *

The snow came to mid-calf and had frozen into a crust that broke under each step with a sound like snapping kindling. She had meant to move along the wall, low and quick, but the crust made stealth a fiction, so she simply ran—hunched, gasping, weaving between the trees she knew now as well as she had ever known the orchard at home. The apple trees stood bare and black, their frozen fruit still clinging here and there, little brown lanterns of rot that glowed faintly when the searchers' lights swung near.

A shout went up behind her. Not her name—they didn't have her name yet, only *the courier*, which was somehow worse, being hunted as a function rather than a person. Lanterns swung. Someone crashed through the rows to her left, cutting the angle toward the wall.

Then a horse screamed.

It burst out of the darkness beyond the orchard gate—a gray, blowing steam, hooves striking sparks of ice—and for one absurd moment Sebastian thought it riderless, a runaway, before she saw the figure bent low along its neck, cloak flying, and recognized the set of the shoulders even before the voice came.

"Down!"

Ansel hauled her up sideways across the saddle with an arm like an iron bar, and the world became speed and cold and the smell of horse sweat. Behind them the lanterns scattered, voices shouting orders that tangled and contradicted each other; someone fired a pistol, and the ball went wide with a sound like a whip cracking the sky. Ansel wheeled the gray through the gap in the far wall—a gap Sebastian hadn't known existed, though of course he had known, he had walked this ground at night the way she walked her own memory—and then they were through, and the orchard, the estate, the whole lit stage of Vell fell away behind a screen of frozen trees.

"Idris will ride to the crossroads," Ansel said against her ear, not slowing. "We go north through the fields. Can you hold on?"

"I've held on to worse."

Something that might have been a laugh shook through him. He shifted her weight, settled her more securely, and drove the horse north into the open dark.

* * *

They rode for two hours through country that did not care which nation claimed it. Frozen streams. Abandoned hayricks sagging under snow. A village with every window dark, and once, distantly, the barking of dogs that did not stop until they were well past. Sebastian lay against Ansel's back and let the rhythm of the horse shake loose everything the night had wound tight in her, and gradually she became aware that she was crying—not sobbing, just leaking, the tears freezing at her jaw, and that Ansel knew, and said nothing, and that this too was a kind of language between them.

Near dawn they stopped in a hollow where a stream ran black under shelf ice, and Ansel watered the horse while Sebastian stamped feeling back into her feet.

"The book," he said quietly. It was not a question. He had watched her come out of the kitchen empty-handed; he had known.

"I burned it."

His face, half-lit by the graying sky, did something complicated. Relief, she thought, and underneath it, regret, and underneath both, respect. "Corvane will assume we have it. Every soldier between here and the border will be hunting a courier carrying the Republic's codes." He glanced at her sleeve, where the small hard shape of the letters rode against her wrist. "You gave up the thing that could have bought your life to keep the thing that can't buy anything at all."

"They're not for buying," she said. "They're for delivering."

"I know." He said it simply, without argument, and she understood that he had perhaps been waiting since the first day at the gate to hear her say exactly that—to find out whether the woman who tended frozen trees in the dark was someone who kept promises or merely someone who made them. "My government will ask you for the codes anyway. They'll assume you kept copies. You should decide now what you'll tell them."

"And what should I tell them?"

"The truth," Ansel said. "That you burned them. It's the one story no spymaster will believe, which makes it the safest one you own." He handed her the water skin. "Drink. The border is six miles. The pickets change at dawn and for twenty minutes no one is quite sure who has authority to shoot us."

She drank. The water tasted of iron and pine. Above the eastern hills the sky was turning the color of a bruise healing, and somewhere on the other side of those hills was Ostrava—the enemy, the kingdom that sheltered her brother, the nation she was about to enter as a deserter, a thief of nothing, a traitor to everything except a promise.

"Do you know what they'll call me?" she asked. "Back there. When Corvane writes his reports. He'll make me monstrous. He'll need a monster to explain why the codes vanished, why the clerk died, why the armistice failed. He'll build me out of paper and hang me in effigy, and people will believe it because believing it is easier than believing the truth."

"What is the truth?"

Sebastian looked north, toward the hills, toward Tomas.

"That I loved my country," she said, "and my country loved itself instead."

Ansel was quiet for a moment. Then he took her hand—the first time he had touched her except out of necessity, hauling her onto a horse, steadying her through doors—and pressed the letters' small shape through her sleeve, over her pulse, as though taking an oath upon it.

"Then let's go be monsters," he said.

They mounted together, and rode up out of the hollow into the last dark, toward the border, toward whatever waited on the other side of the picket lines—and toward the garrison cell where, alive and changed and ashamed, a man with their father's hands and their mother's stubborn mouth sat waiting to learn whether promises outlive governments after all.
