## 15. Enemy Territory

The border was a river frozen to the color of old pewter, and they crossed it before dawn, when even the sentries' fires had burned down to sullen orange eyes in the dark.

Sebastian rode wrapped in Ansel's spare greatcoat, its wool smelling of woodsmoke and horse and something else she could not yet name—Ostrava itself, perhaps, or simply a life that was not hers. Behind her the Vessarine Republic lay under snow and silence, and she did not look back. She had learned on the orchard road that looking back was a luxury she could no longer afford. Every glance behind was a thread, and threads could be pulled.

They were nine now, their party pared down from the extraction: Ansel, two troopers whose names she still stumbled over—Vasik and Hrelle—a surly muleteer who spoke to his animals more tenderly than to any person, and Sebastian herself, riding as *Aldous Verre*, courier, dead man of the Vessarine delegation, traitor twice over by any accounting that mattered.

The crossing itself was anticlimactic. A frozen ford, a line of stakes with faded streamers marking the boundary, an Ostravan picket that materialized out of the willow scrub with rifles unslung and then, at Ansel's word, shouldered again. One of them looked at Sebastian too long. She kept her chin down, her shoulders square, her voice a low rasp when asked for her name. The rasp had served her well for three years. It would have to serve her a while longer.

Then the river was behind them, and she was in enemy territory, and the strange thing—the thing she would turn over in her mind for weeks afterward—was how little it differed from home.

* * *

The first village sat three miles past the border, huddled in a fold of hills like something trying not to be seen.

It was called Brzava, though no one told her so; she read it later off a milestone whose letters had been chiseled and re-chiseled, Ostravan script carved over older Vessarine characters that still ghosted beneath like scars under new skin. Even the villages here had been fought over by language.

The war had passed through Brzava the way a flood passes through a valley—not all at once, but leaving its mark in high-water lines. She saw it everywhere once she knew to look: the fresh timber patching a barn roof where a shell had come through; the church with one bell instead of two; the fields lying fallow not from rotation but from absence, because the hands that would have worked them were gone. Conscription notices were pasted to the granary door in neat official rows, three layers deep—the oldest sun-bleached and curling, the newest crisp enough to have gone up that week. Men eighteen to forty-five. Men sixteen and up, in the latest printing, the ink barely dry.

Ansel reined in at the well at the village center, and the villagers came out the way villagers came out anywhere: slowly, warily, drawn by curiosity they didn't quite trust. An old woman brought bread without being asked. A child stared at Sebastian's boots until his mother pulled him back by the collar. A young wife stood in a doorway holding a baby and watching the road with an expression Sebastian recognized down to the marrow, because she had worn it herself, or seen it worn, in a hundred Vessarine villages: the look of a woman counting riders, searching each face for a name.

*Please don't be him. Please let it be him.*

That look needed no translation. It was the same in every tongue, on both sides of every river ever used as a border.

"Conscripted last spring," Ansel said quietly, following her gaze toward the notices. "Half this village. The other half the spring before."

"And yours?" Sebastian asked. "Vessarine levies?"

"Worse, near the capital. They took boys of fifteen by the end." He said it flatly, without performance, and that flatness was its own kind of confession. Three years of listening to him across parley tables and orchard fences had taught her that Ansel wore his country's sins the way he wore his uniform: correctly, without complaint, and never comfortably. "You'll want to see it plainly," he added. "I'd rather you did now than later, when you might feel obliged to pretend otherwise."

*You'll want to see it plainly.* Not: don't judge us. Not: it isn't what it looks like. He offered her the wound rather than the bandage, and she found, absurdly, that this made her trust him more than any oath could have.

She dismounted at the well and let the old woman press bread into her hands—dark rye, dense as a brick, tasting of caraway and wood ash—and ate it standing in the road of the country she had been raised to hate. The bread tasted like bread. That was the whole of it. That was the unbearable thing.

* * *

They lodged that night in the tithe barn, which the army had requisitioned and half-returned, hay still stacked along one wall, a stove vented through a hole chopped in the roof planking. Sebastian sat against the hay with her hood up and watched the firelight and listened.

This was the part she hadn't expected—the listening. In the Republic, in the delegation, she had spent three years making herself small enough to pass unseen, and unseen people hear everything. Old habit made her ears work even now, even here, where eavesdropping served no mission and protected nothing. She heard Vasik complaining about his boots. She heard the muleteer singing something low and tuneless to the mule. And through the barn's thin wall, from the farmhouse adjoining, she heard a family at supper.

A grandmother's voice, cracked but commanding, telling some story with long pauses for effect. A man laughing—the kind of laugh that starts reluctant and then can't stop. Children squabbling about something trivial, a spoon or a seat, escalating until a woman's voice cut through, tired and amused at once: *Enough. Both of you, enough.* Then quiet, then the clink of dishes, then the grandmother resuming her story as if she'd never stopped.

Sebastian closed her eyes.

She could have been anywhere. She could have been eight years old at the long table in the house above the orchard, Tomas kicking her under the table, their mother's voice cutting through the same way, *Enough, both of you,* the words worn smooth from use like river stones. Grief, she thought, had taught her that loss was particular—that her losses were hers, shaped like no one else's. But sitting in an enemy barn listening to an enemy family quarrel over a spoon, she understood something that undid her more quietly than any revelation of the winter: the shape of ordinary life was the same everywhere. It was only the borders that differed, and the borders were lines men had drawn, and lines could be redrawn, but the sound of a family at supper was eternal and belonged to no flag.

She had lost a country. Sitting there, she began to suspect she had never actually had one—or not one that lived in ministries and codes and the Republic's marble halls. What she had loved, all along, was smaller and truer: an orchard, a brother's laugh, a mother's voice saying *enough*. Those things did not require the Vessarine Republic to exist. They had only required her to notice them, and she had noticed too late.

*Belonging*, she thought, is not granted at the border post. It is built—at wells, in barns, over bread broken with strangers. And if that were true, then it could be built here. The thought frightened her almost more than Corvane's net had.

* * *

In the morning they passed through a market town called Ostrohul, and there Sebastian saw the propaganda.

It covered the walls the way ivy covers a ruin—posters, proclamations, a great painted mural on the side of the customs house depicting a heroic Ostravan soldier shielding a weeping peasant woman from a fanged, black-clad figure labeled, in block letters, THE VESSARINE HYDRA. The soldier's jaw was impossibly square. The woman's tears were enormous and glistening. The Hydra's many necks rose from a map of the Republic, and beneath it ran a slogan in bold type: *One People, One Crown, One Peace.*

Sebastian studied it the way she'd been trained to study everything, cataloguing, assessing—and then laughed. She couldn't help it. It came out of her like a sneeze, short and startled, and Vasik gave her a sharp look.

"The same poster," she rasped. "Backwards. In Vell, there's a mural on the Chancellery wall. Our soldier, shielding our woman, from the Ostravan wolf. Same composition. Same square jaw. I used to walk past it every day." She shook her head. "Someone should introduce the painters. They clearly share a teacher."

"It isn't funny," Vasik said stiffly.

"No," she agreed. "It isn't. That's why I laughed."

Because it wasn't funny—it was terrible, the symmetry of it, two nations painting identical lies on opposite walls while the same grief walked both sides of the river wearing different-colored coats. In the delegation she had believed, or had tried to believe, that the Republic's cause was just and Ostrava's aggression naked. Now, walking these streets, she saw that Ostrava's people had been fed the mirror image of her own people's diet, and had swallowed it the same way, hungrily, because hunger makes anything nourishing. Somewhere in Vell a woman stood at a window counting riders, searching faces. Here in Ostrohul another woman did the same. Neither of them cared about the Hydra or the Wolf. They wanted their men home. Everything else was furniture arranged by men who would never stand at those windows.

At the town's edge, past the last posters, they overtook a column of conscripts marching west—new men, untrained, their greatcoats too big, their breath rising in a single ragged cloud. Boys, most of them. They sang as they marched, some marching song with a stomping rhythm, and their voices cracked on the high notes, and they laughed when they cracked, and shoved each other, glad of any excuse to feel young.

Sebastian watched them until the road curved and took them away.

"Tomas marched like that," she said, before she could stop herself.

Ansel rode close enough that only she could hear. "I know. He told me. He said he sang the whole first day and couldn't remember a note of it after."

She looked at him. There it was again—the thing she kept discovering about this man, that he carried her brother's small histories carefully, like a man carrying water across ice, attentive to every step. He understood what he was carrying. He had understood since the gate at Vell.

"They're someone's sons," she said. "All of them. On both sides. And everyone keeps telling me whose side God is on."

"God," Ansel said, "is on the side of whoever ends it soonest. Everything else is commentary." He paused, then, dry as the frozen road: "That's my grandfather's proverb. He was a fence-mender. He claimed wars happened because men forgot how to mend things properly."

* * *

They made a cold camp that night in a spruce plantation—trees in perfect rows, planted by some forester's arithmetic decades ago, now grown tall and dark and silent. Sebastian sat apart from the troopers, ostensibly keeping watch, actually holding the oilskin packet that held Tomas's letters, reading them again by the shielded light of a shuttered lantern.

*Remember the Medici plum,* one letter said, in that unmistakable hand, the letters leaning right like windblown wheat. *Remember how we argued about whether it liked the south wall. I was right. Write it down somewhere. When this is over I want it witnessed that I was right about the plum.*

He was right about the plum. She remembered the argument, the summer heat, the two of them sprawled under the tree itself eating the evidence. She had forgotten, until this moment, that being right about the plum mattered to him—had mattered enough to carry across a war, across a defection, across years of silence. That was what survival did to a person, she was learning; it shrank the world down to things that could be defended. A plum tree. A memory. A sister.

And his survival depended on the fall of everything she had served. The letters said it plainly enough between the lines: *protection*, *arrangements*, *what I owe them and what they hold*. Tomas lived inside Ostrava's victory like a seed inside a hard frost, waiting for a spring that required his homeland's death. There was no version of this where she got both. She had chosen on the frozen rows of the orchard at Vell, running with the codes abandoned behind her, and she would keep choosing it every morning, because choosing once was not how treason worked. Treason was a craft, like grafting. You returned to it daily. You tended the cut.

But sitting among the ordered spruce, she found the guilt had changed texture. It was no longer a blade. It was more like a stone in her boot—present, grinding, hers. She had not betrayed the Republic. She had betrayed a fiction, and the fiction had been betraying her all along, spending boys like the ones on the road today, spending them on murals and ministers' ambitions, spending them the way Corvane would spend the whole nation if it bought him a chair in whatever government grew from the collapse. Let the historians argue about treason. She had kept faith with the only country she had ever truly held citizenship in: a dead mother's kitchen, a brother's argument about plums, a promise made under a grafted branch.

Those things did not stop at rivers. Neither did she.

* * *

On the fourth day they reached the garrison town, and it was there, in the shadow of walls flying the black-and-gold, that belonging finally showed her its true face—not as an idea but as a gesture, small and human and entirely unsanctioned.

They stopped at a cookhouse near the barracks gate, and while Ansel reported to the duty officer, Sebastian waited in the queue with the soldiers, anonymous in her hood, holding her tin cup. The woman ladling soup was broad and gray-haired and moved with the economy of someone who had fed armies through worse winters than this. When Sebastian reached her, the woman glanced up, glanced again—women always saw, sooner or later; they saw through three years of binding and rasping voice and careful posture, because they knew what they themselves would have hidden and how—and then, without pausing, without changing expression, the woman ladled the soup and, with the same motion, laid a heel of dark bread on top of the pile in Sebastian's cup.

"For the road," the woman said, in the toneless voice of a woman saying nothing at all. Her eyes flicked to the queue, to the sergeant at the end of it, back to Sebastian. Nothing else. No question, no offer, no risk taken that couldn't be denied.

Sebastian carried the cup to a bench by the wall and sat with it steaming in her hands, and found her eyes stinging, and was furious at herself for it.

One loaf of bread. One look exchanged between women across the lines of a war, one moment of *I see you, and I won't say so, and neither will you*—and it did more for her than all the parleys and protocols of the winter. This was the thing the nations could not give and could not take: the small clandestine kindness of people who recognized each other beneath the uniforms history had put on them. The Vessarine Republic had never done this for her. It had given her a codebook and a death sentence and a brother's empty grave. This stranger in an enemy cookhouse had given her more of a home in ten seconds than her homeland had managed in twenty-three years.

Belonging, she thought, finishing the soup to the last drop, was not something a border granted. It was something people built—out of bread, out of silences, out of the decision to see each other. And she could build it here. The knowledge settled into her like a stone into a riverbed, definite, unmoving.

Ansel came out of the barracks gate walking fast, his face composed into the particular blankness she had learned to read as bad news worn in public. He mounted, gathered his reins, and only when they were through the town gate, alone on the open road, did he speak.

"We've reached the garrison where your brother is held," he said. "Protective custody, as promised. But there's a complication." His jaw worked once. "The commandant has received orders from Vell—Corvane's orders, routed through channels we didn't know existed. Someone has told them a courier fled west. And they've requested that any persons matching the description be detained on arrival."

The road stretched ahead of them, straight and white and utterly exposed.

"So," Ansel said quietly, "before you meet your brother—you're going to have to decide who you are when they ask you."
