## 16. Corvane Ascendant

Minister Corvane woke before dawn because he had trained himself to, decades ago, in the years when waking before dawn meant outpacing rivals who slept. He lay for a moment beneath the heavy quilts of the Vell estate's best bedchamber—best by his own reassignment of it, three days after their arrival, on the grounds that a minister's health was a matter of state—and listened to the house.

It was a good sound, if you knew how to hear it. The creak of floorboards under sentries changing watch. The scrape of the kitchen fires being lit below. The muffled coughing of the junior clerks billeted in the east wing, who had been given the coldest rooms and understood, without anyone saying so, that this was the natural order. A delegation was an instrument, and an instrument made noise when it was properly tuned.

The Republic was dying. Everyone at Vell knew it; only the dispatches still pretended otherwise. Corvane had read the last three from the capital with something close to pleasure—the careful, hemorrhaging euphemisms, *repositioning*, *consolidation of forces*, *temporary suspension of supply*—because he had stopped grieving for the Republic years ago, around the time he understood that grief was a luxury purchased by men who expected to survive on sentiment. The Republic had been a fine thing once. So had his first wife's dowry. Both were spent.

What remained was salvage, and salvage went to whoever arrived at the wreck first.

He dressed himself, refusing the valet—a small performance he repeated each morning, *I am a man of the people reduced to hard circumstance*, though no one watched anymore, which made it less a performance than a habit, and habits were the only honest things left about him. In the mirror his face looked as it always did: long, dry, correct. A face like a ledger. Men trusted ledgers.

Downstairs, in the study that had belonged to some dead orchardist or other—he had not troubled to learn the name—his secretary Pellam waited with the morning's intercepts arranged in three piles, because Corvane had taught him that too: everything in threes, the readable, the probable, and the poisoned.

"Anything from the capital?" Corvane asked, though he could see from the thinness of the first pile that there was not. The capital's silence had its own eloquence. Cities did not go quiet unless they were deciding what to become next.

"No, Minister. But there is this." Pellam held out a single sheet with the delicacy of a man offering something that might bite. "The night clerk's report. On the courier."

Corvane took it and read standing, because sitting would come later, when the news deserved it.

* * *

The clerk—Vossel, a gray little man with ink-stained cuffs and a widow in the provinces—had died badly, and Corvane had permitted it, and now Corvane intended to spend him.

This was not cruelty. Corvane wished it were, sometimes, because cruelty at least implied appetite, and what he felt instead was a kind of arithmetic. Vossel had broken under questioning—not even real questioning, only the preliminary sort conducted by Brant and two of the guards loyal to Corvane rather than to the delegation—and had named *the courier*. A courier. There were eleven couriers attached to the delegation. The information was therefore worth almost nothing and cost them their one lead, since Vossel had then done the sensible thing and hanged himself with his own belt from the beam in the tack room, leaving a note that named no one and apologized to everyone.

An incompetent death. Even his suicide was poorly executed. And yet—

And yet a dead man was a document, like any other, and documents could be annotated.

By nine o'clock the delegation had been assembled in the great hall of the estate, all forty-one souls, ministers and secretaries and clerks and couriers and the servants who had stayed on at Vell out of habit or nowhere else to go. Corvane stood before them on the shallow dais where the estate's last owner had presumably once announced harvest festivals. Behind him, two guards flanked the tack-room door, through which—arranged overnight by Corvane's instruction—certain items were now visible to anyone who looked: a foreign-made pistol, Ostravan issue, oiled and recent; a satchel of papers bearing the Ostravan field-post stamp; and Vossel's body, covered with a sheet but not concealed, because concealment suggested shame and Corvane required suggestion, not shame.

"My colleagues," Corvane said, and let the word hang until the hall quieted. He never raised his voice. Raising your voice told the room you feared it could not hear you. "I will be brief, because brevity is a courtesy and I intend to keep extending courtesies as long as this delegation deserves them."

He let them look at the tack-room door. They could not help themselves. Forty-one heads turned like a field of grain in wind.

"Last night we lost Clerk Vossel. Some of you knew him." No one had known him. That was the tragedy of bureaucracies: they assembled strangers and called them colleagues right up until they called them casualties. "What you do not know—and what I am now compelled to tell you—is how he came to die. Vossel was approached. Over a period of weeks. By an agent of Ostrava operating inside these walls."

He paused. He had learned pauses the way other men learned languages, and he spoke pause fluently.

"The evidence," he continued, gesturing without looking at the tack-room door, letting the room look for him, "was found among his effects. Correspondence in cipher. Foreign currency sewn into his coat lining—I will spare you the coat. And a list." Here he produced it, unfolded it slowly, folded it again without reading it. "A list of names. Members of this delegation whose loyalties, according to Ostrava's accounting, might be purchased."

The hall made a sound like a held breath breaking. Somewhere a chair scraped.

"The list is incomplete," Corvane lied smoothly, because the list did not exist, or existed only insofar as he had written it that morning in his own hand and burned it, which was a form of existence. "But I will tell you what it tells me. It tells me that Ostrava believes this delegation can be bought. And it tells me that someone believed Vossel could be bought cheaply. That is the insult in all of this, colleagues—not treason, treason has a certain grandeur—but the *cheapness* of it. They priced one of ours the way one prices a sack of turnips."

Let them feel the indignation. Indignation was useful; it was loyalty's cheapest distillation. And beneath the indignation, fear, which was more durable. A frightened delegation obeyed. An obedient delegation could be moved.

"Effective immediately," Corvane said, "all correspondence passes through my office. All codes are surrendered and reissued under seal—all codes, without exception; I will hear no exceptions, exceptions are how traitors dress. Movement beyond the estate requires countersignature. And the talks—" he allowed himself the ghost of regret, beautifully calibrated, "—the talks are suspended pending internal review. We cannot negotiate with Ostrava while Ostrava's hand is already inside our glove."

A minister near the front—Old Havelle, half-deaf and wholly self-important—protested something about protocol, about the armistice timetable, about the Republic's honor. Corvane regarded him with the patience one extends to weather.

"The Republic's honor," Corvane said, "is currently represented by forty-one people freezing in a dead man's orchard. I am trying to make sure some of us are alive when it needs representing again. You may thank me later, Havelle, or you may write to the capital. Ah—" a flicker of genuine, unperformed satisfaction, quickly sheathed, "—but of course the capital does not answer."

Silence. He had them. He dismissed them with a nod, gracious as an emperor.

* * *

In the afternoon he walked the orchard alone, as he did every day now, because the orchard was where the delegation's soul lived and he liked to know where souls were kept.

The trees stood in their frozen rows like a congregation that had heard the sermon too many times. Snow sat in the crooks of the branches. Someone had been pruning—one of the garden tools missing from the shed, returned duller—and Corvane noted it the way he noted everything: filed, cross-referenced, assigned no meaning yet. Meaning could wait. Meaning, like money, compounded.

The courier. Vossel's dying gift had been two words, and Corvane turned them over as he walked. Eleven couriers. But Vossel had been questioned about the leak in the minutes—the coded transcription of the parley sessions—and whoever transcribed the minutes was one of perhaps four people with access, and of those four, one was a woman.

That fact Corvane had carried privately for weeks, the way a man carries a splinter he has decided not yet to dig out. Sebastian Underhill. Competent, punctual, invisible—which was itself suspicious, in Corvane's experience, because true mediocrity resented invisibility and complained of it, while performed invisibility cultivated it. She kept to herself. She tended trees, of all things, in her off hours, like a person preserving something. Like a person keeping a door open to somewhere else.

Or like a person who simply missed home. He acknowledged the alternative. He simply did not believe in it. Sentimentality was a cover story the world offered to the credulous.

He did not move against her yet. That was the discipline. Accusation was a bullet and you fired it once; suspicion was a net and you drew it slowly. He would reassign her duties closer to his office, where proximity would do his work for him. He would have her quarters searched while she transcribed the afternoon session. He would watch her hands during the searches—guilty hands always wanted to be helpful.

And if she proved clean? Then he would find another name, or invent one, because the hunt itself was the instrument. A delegation hunting a traitor could not simultaneously question its minister's accumulating powers. Fear was a solvent; it dissolved everything except authority.

At the far end of the orchard he stopped at the wall, where the ground rose toward the border road, and looked north toward Ostrava. Somewhere over those hills was the enemy's army, and beyond it the enemy's capital, warm and lit and full of men exactly like himself—men doing arithmetic. He felt, briefly, not hatred but recognition, which was worse, or better, depending on which side of the table you ended up on.

Because that was the truth Corvane carried beneath all the others, the ledger beneath the ledgers: the war was ending, the Republic was ending, and endings were markets. Every institution about to die put its assets up for sale whether it admitted it or not—codes, routes, names, secrets, the loyalty of forty-one frozen functionaries. The only question was who acted as broker. The capital had been the broker once. The capital was silent now. Silence, in Corvane's experience, was the market clearing.

He had already opened accounts. Not with Ostrava—that was crude, that was Vossel's error, sewing coin into a coat. No. He had opened conversations with three parties: an Ostravan spymaster who preferred subtlety to victory; a consortium of neutral shipping interests who would need port rights in whatever republic, kingdom, or rubble emerged; and—his favorite, because it cost nothing yet—a faction within the Republic's own garrison command who understood, as he did, that nations were stories and that the storytellers survived every revision.

Whichever buyer prevailed, Corvane would hold the inventory. Codes, minutes, names, the delegation itself, wrapped and ribboned. He was not betraying the Republic. One could only betray something alive. What he was doing was closer to probate.

* * *

Evening brought Pellam with the day's final report, and with it, the day's first genuine surprise.

"The courier," Pellam said. "Underhill. The guards completed the quarter search you ordered."

"And?"

"Nothing. Books, clothes, a sewing kit. Writing implements, all accounted for. Paper stock matches the office register to the sheet." Pellam hesitated, which he rarely did. "There is one thing. The garden shed. The tool count is off by one grafting knife. It was found this morning in the snow near the north wall. Cleaned, but recently used—there were fresh cuts on two of the apple stocks. Cuttings taken, we think. We haven't located any cuttings."

Corvane stood at the window, watching the last light fail over the frozen rows.

Cuttings. Taken in winter, cleaned, hidden, and gone—who knew where. To whom? A gardener's sentiment? Or a message? Grafting was a language; he knew that much from the agricultural reports he skimmed for intelligence value. Certain cuts, certain pairings, certain scions—orchardists could say things to one another across a valley with a pruned branch. It was a folk cipher, unbreakable precisely because it was not designed to be secret, only to be *understood*.

Someone in his delegation was writing letters in tree branches, and the recipient was almost certainly not in the delegation.

"Who found the knife?"

"A guard. Kessler. Loyal to you."

"And where was Underhill when it was found?"

Pellam checked his notes. "Transcribing the afternoon session. In the hall. In view of six witnesses."

Alibis were like alibis everywhere: solid until you noticed they had been built in advance. Corvane touched the cold window glass. Somewhere out in the dark, between the orchard rows and the north wall, a set of footprints was filling with snow—or had already filled, which was better planning, which was the work of someone careful, someone patient, someone who had learned to be invisible so thoroughly that even her sentiments left almost no trace.

Almost none. A missing knife. Missing cuttings. Two words from a hanged clerk. Corvane had built a career on less.

"Double the watch on the orchard," he said. "Quietly. And move Underhill's quarters to the wing nearest my office. Tell her it is for her safety—after tonight's announcements, she will believe it, and belief makes better bars than iron."

"Yes, Minister." Pellam gathered his papers, then paused at the door. "And the talks, sir? Ostrava has sent word. They request a resumption date. Their envoy—" a slight distaste, as if the name itself were foreign matter, "—Officer Ansel, requests it personally."

Corvane smiled. It reached his eyes, because he permitted it to; a smile that didn't reach the eyes wasted everyone's time.

"Tell Officer Ansel the delegation welcomes the resumption." He turned back to the window, to the black rows of trees holding their white burden, to the whole frozen estate that no longer belonged to anyone and therefore, briefly, belonged to him. "Tell him we will meet in three days. And tell him—" he chose the words with a collector's care, "—tell him that Vell has suffered a lamentable loss, that we have caught the hand that caused it, and that we look forward to discussing terms."

Three days. Enough time to finish drawing the net. Enough time for the courier—if it was the courier, and the arithmetic said it was—to grow comfortable, or desperate, or both. Desperation made people move; movement made people visible; visible people could be spent.

Behind him, the door closed. Corvane stood alone in the darkening study of a dead orchardist, in a dying republic, at the center of a web he had woven from other people's fears, and felt the only thing he had ever allowed himself to feel without apology:

Anticipation.
