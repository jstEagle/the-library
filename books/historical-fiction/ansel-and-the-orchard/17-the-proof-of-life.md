## 17. The Proof of Life

The garrison at Krevna had been a monastery once, and it still carried the habit of silence. Ansel felt it in the corridors—stone that remembered prayer, walls that had learned to keep secrets for men in wool robes and had simply transferred the skill to men in gray uniforms. He walked Sebastian through its passages at the hour between dusk and lamplighting, when the sentries changed and no one counted who passed.

She walked beside him without touching him. That was its own discipline, he thought—the way she kept half a pace behind his shoulder, the way her eyes moved along the walls cataloguing exits, guard posts, the number of steps to each turning. She had been doing it since they crossed the border, three days of it now, this constant quiet inventory of a country she did not yet belong to. He wondered if she knew she did it. He wondered if she would ever stop.

"You could tell me," she said at last. Her voice was low; the corridor swallowed it anyway. "You've been walking me through this maze for an hour. You could simply say where we're going."

"I wanted you to see it first." He turned down the stair that smelled of old incense under the new smell of lamp oil and gun grease. "The place. Before you saw him in it."

"Does it matter?"

"It matters to you. Everything about how a thing is framed matters to you—you read a letter's margins before you read its words." He glanced back at her. In the gloom her face was all shadow and one pale line of cheekbone, but he could feel her attention on him like a hand at his collar. "I didn't want your first sight of your brother to be bars."

They came out into the cloister.

It had been the monastery's garden court, and the Ostravan army had done what armies do: they had put a roof of corrugated iron over the arcade, stacked crates where the fountain had been, strung wire across the colonnade. But the center of the court was still open to the sky, and snow still fell into it, unhurried, indifferent, filling the square of darkness above them with slow white motion. A single lamp burned by the far door. Beneath it, wrapped in a soldier's greatcoat too large for him, a man sat on an overturned crate with a book open on his knees.

He was thinner than the boy in the letters. That was the first thing Ansel registered—not as surprise but as confirmation, the way a lock confirms its key. The letters had described a man hollowed and remade; here was the hollowing made flesh. Tomas Iversen had been broad-shouldered once, according to the photographs Ansel had studied until he could have drawn them from memory. Now the coat hung off him like weather. His hair had gone early grey at the temples. He read with his head bent forward, protectively, as though the book were a coal he was sheltering from wind.

Sebastian stopped so abruptly that Ansel heard the crunch of her boots cease as a kind of sound.

"Tomas," she said.

The word barely left her. It wasn't a call. It was more like a test—a word set down on the air between them to see whether the world would accept it or reject it. Ansel had watched her verify handwriting for six hours by lamplight, tracing the private shorthand of their childhood, the grafting terminology, the joke about the pear tree that had never once borne fruit. He had thought then that he understood what verification meant to her: proof before hope, always proof before hope. But watching her now, he understood he had only understood the paper version.

The man looked up.

For a moment nothing happened. Snow fell into the court. The lamp guttered and steadied. And Ansel stood very still beside her, hands loose at his sides, feeling like a man holding a door open for something enormous to pass through—or be refused.

Tomas closed the book. Slowly. His thumb stayed inside it, marking the page, as if some part of him believed this might still be interrupted, revoked, revealed as a clerical error.

"Seb," he said.

Then he was moving, and she was moving, and they met in the falling snow in the middle of the ruined cloister with the gracelessness of people who have rehearsed a moment too many times for it to survive contact. She struck him hard enough to stagger him back a step. Her fists were in his coat, gripping the wool, shaking him—Ansel saw that she was shaking him, actually shaking him, as though the truth needed rattling loose from the man wearing it—and Tomas was holding her arms and saying her name over and over, *Seb, Seb, little seb,* the childhood name, and laughing, and the laugh broke in the middle and became something else entirely.

Ansel looked away. Not from modesty. From the older instinct of a man who has spent years in rooms where grief is currency: the knowledge that some transactions must not appear witnessed.

When he looked back, they were standing forehead to forehead, her hands flat against his chest now, his hands cupping her face, and they were speaking too quietly for him to hear, and the snow was settling on both their heads and neither of them moved to brush it away.

* * *

Later there was a room—the garrison commander's office, vacated with Ostravan efficiency and a look of curiosity the commander had the sense not to voice—and a table, and tea gone bitter in a tin pot, and the three of them arranged around these humble objects like survivors of a shipwreck taking inventory.

Sebastian had not let go of her brother's wrist. Ansel noticed it because Tomas noticed it; every few minutes his eyes dropped to her hand there, and something moved across his face that Ansel had learned to read in interrogation rooms and parley tents alike, in enemies and allies and the unfortunate men caught between: guilt, arriving on schedule.

"You got my letters," Tomas said.

"Three of them. Over two years." Sebastian's voice was steady now, but Ansel could hear the effort of the steadiness, the way one hears the effort in a bridge bearing weight. "Through a man I'd never met, delivered under a flag of parley, asking me to betray my country. Yes. I got them."

"And you came."

"You doubted it?"

"I hoped it. There's a difference." Tomas looked down at the table. "Hoping is free. Believing costs."

The fire ticked in the grate. Outside, somewhere in the garrison, a bugle called the change of watch, thin and cold in the winter dark.

"Tell me how," Sebastian said. "All of it. Not the version in the letters. The letters were careful. I want the careless version."

Tomas laughed again, briefly, without much humor in it. "The careless version is longer."

"We have the night," Ansel said. "And I have orders to sit in this room until morning regardless. You may as well perform."

Tomas glanced at him—there it was again, that flicker of assessment, of a man measuring how much the third person in the room knew—and then began.

He told it plainly, which was itself a kind of confession. How his company had been overrun at the Sarnel crossings in the second year of the war, and he had surrendered rather than die for a bridgehead his own command had already written off. How the Republic's newspapers had listed him dead because no other word suited a captured officer whose family name had political weight. How the Ostravans, instead of shipping him to the prison camps in the east, had put him in a room with a courteous major who asked questions—first about supply routes, then about the delegation's personnel, then about Minister Corvane himself.

"He answered," Sebastian said. It wasn't quite a question. Her hand had tightened on his wrist.

"He answered." Tomas said it the way a man confesses to a priest, flatly, watching the middle distance. "Not at first. They starved it out of me slowly—not food, they fed us—but time, and isolation, and the arithmetic of survival. Every question had a price and every answer had a payment, and I told myself the things I gave them were small. A depot here. A rotation schedule there. Names of officers already dead." He shook his head. "That's the lie they let you keep, Seb. That the answers are small. They're small going out and enormous arriving. Somewhere on the other side of the line, everything I sold was assembled into something large enough to kill people. I know that. I did the assembly myself, in my head, at night, and I kept doing it anyway, because the alternative was—" He stopped. Started again. "Because I wanted to live. That's the whole careless version. I wanted to live, and living had a price, and I paid it in installments."

Sebastian was silent for a long moment. Ansel watched her the way he had watched her in the orchard at Vell, in the nights of verifying letters: not intruding, but refusing to look away either, because looking away was a courtesy she had never once asked for and probably despised.

"The protection," she said finally. "Ostrava protects you. Houses you, feeds you, keeps your existence secret. Why? Men who trade intelligence are spent like currency, Tomas. Spent men get buried in camps or shot in ditches. What makes you worth a warm room and a guard detail?"

"Because I'm not finished being useful." Tomas met her eyes, and did not flinch from whatever he found there. "Because the war isn't over just because your capital fell. Corvane is still alive. Half the Vessarine treasury is unaccounted for, and I'm one of perhaps four people who can identify the signatures on the transfer documents. They keep me the way a bank keeps a key." His mouth twisted. "Protective custody. Those were the actual words. I've come to understand that 'protective' modifies the custodians, not the custody."

"So you're a prisoner."

"I'm an asset with a bed." He said it without self-pity, which somehow made it worse. "There's a difference, and the difference is that prisoners get released."

The fire ticked. Sebastian's hand was still on his wrist. Ansel realized she had not moved it since the story began—as though letting go might permit her brother to finish dissolving into the compromised stranger sitting across from her.

* * *

Past midnight, Tomas slept—truly slept, suddenly and completely, mid-sentence almost, the sleep of a body that had been rationing rest for years and had been given permission at last. Sebastian pulled the greatcoat up over his shoulders herself, tucking it with the unconscious tenderness of someone performing an act learned in another life, and then she stood looking down at him for a long time.

Ansel waited by the door. He had gotten good at waiting. It was, he reflected, most of what his profession consisted of.

"He's not what you carried across a border for," he said quietly, when she came to stand beside him.

"No," she agreed. "He's smaller than the idea of him. Ideas don't sell supply routes." She rubbed her eyes with the heel of her hand. "Do you know what I did with those letters, Ansel? I burned one and memorized the others while a man named Corvane was building a gallows for whoever leaked to you. I abandoned the state codes of the Republic in a frozen orchard—I committed treason with my whole chest—for this." She nodded toward the sleeping man. "For a tired collaborator in a borrowed coat."

"Yes."

"You're supposed to argue with me."

"I'm supposed to be honest with you. It's rarer, and it's the only thing I've ever had to offer anyone." He leaned against the doorframe. "You didn't cross a border for the man he is. You crossed for the promise. Promises aren't made to what people become. They're made to what they were when you loved them. If promises had to pass inspection first, no one would ever keep one."

She looked at him for a long moment. In the low light her exhaustion showed plainly—the hollows under her eyes, the chapped lips, the hair escaping its practical knot—and Ansel thought, not for the first time, that she had never once seemed beautiful to him in the ways that beauty is usually deployed, and had never once seemed anything else.

"When he wakes," she said, "he'll ask what happens next. And I'll have to tell him I've spent our last secret buying his passage out of this garrison—to where, exactly, I don't yet know, because Ostrava doesn't release assets either." Her jaw set. "So tonight I'm going to sit in that chair and watch my brother breathe and decide whether forgiveness is a feeling or a decision. Because I can't do it as a feeling. The feeling isn't here yet. Maybe it comes later. Maybe it doesn't."

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then I'll forgive him anyway, and wait for the feeling to catch up." She said it like a woman signing a document. "Tomas taught me grafting when I was nine. Do you know the first rule?"

"Cut clean, bind tight."

"Cut clean, bind tight, and don't check under the binding for six weeks." Almost, she smiled. "You bind the wound and you leave it alone and trust the living wood to find its way together. That's what I'm doing. Binding it. Leaving it alone. Six weeks."

From the cot, in his sleep, Tomas murmured something—half a word, maybe a name—and turned toward the wall.

And it was then, as Sebastian crossed the room to take her chair beside him, that the knock came at the door: three measured raps, official weight behind them. Ansel opened it to find a junior signals officer, cap in hand, snow melting on his shoulders, holding out a dispatch case with a seal Ansel recognized and had hoped never to see again this winter.

"Captain," the young man said. "Dispatch from Vell. Priority. It concerns the prisoner's status."

Behind him, Ansel heard Sebastian go very still.
