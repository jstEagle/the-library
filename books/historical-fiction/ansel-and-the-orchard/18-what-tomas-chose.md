## 18. What Tomas Chose

The garrison at Krevna had been a monastery once, before the war took its bells and gave it barracks. Sebastian could see the ghost of its older purpose in the cloister walk where she waited, in the arched windows that still held scraps of colored glass—saints with their faces blown out, leaving only their halos and the blue of their robes. Snow fell past those windows in long, unhurried lines. Somewhere below, a soldier was singing badly to keep himself warm.

She had not slept. The letters were in her coat again, against her ribs, though there was no longer any reason to hide them from Ansel. Habit, she supposed. Or perhaps superstition: as long as they stayed pressed against her body, unread by anyone else, her brother remained a secret the world could not take back.

Ansel found her in the third hour of waiting. He came down the cloister walk without hurrying, snow caught in the dark wool of his collar, and stopped a few paces off—the distance of a man who had learned that people under strain needed room.

"They've brought him up," he said. "He asked for you first. Before the commandant's questions."

"Before his own defense?"

"There won't be much of one." Ansel's voice was careful, flattened in the way he flattened everything that mattered most. "Protective custody is still custody, Sebastian. What he did—he did it for them, but they don't fully trust him either. A man who betrayed one country can be bought by another. That's how they think here. It's how everyone thinks now."

She looked at him. "And you? How do you think?"

"I think he was twenty-two years old and they put a rifle in his hands and told him the alternative was his sister's name on a list." Ansel held her gaze without flinching, which was one of the things she had come to rely on about him: he never softened a thing for her comfort. "I think what he did was terrible and survivable, and I don't know which of those words should come first. But I'm not the one who has to decide. That's why I walked you through the gate myself instead of letting the guards do it."

The room they gave them was small—a former cell, probably, or a novice's chamber—with a stove, two chairs, a table scarred by decades of knives. A barred window looked out on the ruined orchard behind the garrison, six bare apple trees standing in the snow like men waiting for a verdict.

Tomas was already there when the guard opened the door.

Sebastian had prepared herself for many versions of him. The boy of eleven she remembered; the young officer of her nightmares, dead at some frozen river crossing; even a stranger, gray and hollowed, wearing her brother like a borrowed coat. What she had not prepared for was how ordinary he would look. He sat by the window with his hands flat on his knees, a thin man in an Ostravan uniform with the insignia stripped away, and when he rose and turned, he was simply Tomas—her Tomas—with a new scar over one eyebrow and ten years carved into his face.

"Seb," he said.

It was the old name. Nobody had called her that since she became Sebastian Underhill, courier, a man in every ledger and dispatch of the Vessarine Republic. Hearing it was like hearing a bell from a house she had believed burned down.

"You're shorter than I remember," she said, because if she said anything else she would weep in front of the guard, and the Underhills did not weep in front of guards.

"You're taller. And you've got Mother's chin. She'll be furious when she hears you grew into it." His voice cracked on *mother*, and he turned his face toward the window as if checking the weather, which was how the men in her family cried.

The guard withdrew. The door shut. And then there was nothing between them but the small cold room and ten years of silence, and Sebastian crossed it in four strides and held onto her brother hard enough to hurt them both.

* * *

They talked first of small things, because the small things were the only safe ground. He told her about the winter he'd spent in the mountains, eating bark soup and dreaming of plum dumplings. She told him about grafting cuttings in the dark of a baggage wagon, wrapped against her chest like contraband—which, she admitted, they were. He laughed at that, a real laugh, and for a quarter of an hour they were just two orchard children playing at grown-up lives.

Then Tomas set down his cup, and she watched the laughter drain out of his face the way water drains from a field, and she knew the safe ground was gone.

"I have to tell you what I am," he said. "Before you decide anything about me. Before you—" He stopped. Started again. Their father had always said Tomas thought in circles and landed late; it seemed the war hadn't changed that. "You carried my letters through a war zone. You gave up your country for them. You deserve to know exactly what you gave it up for."

"Tomas—"

"No. Let me finish, because I've practiced this speech for three years and every version of it ends with me being brave, and I'm tired of lying even to myself." His hands had gone flat on his knees again, pressing down as if to keep himself anchored to the chair. "The night I was captured—I wasn't captured. You understand? I read the situation reports. I knew the Ninth Company was going to be encircled at the Vessarine line, and I knew our family name was already on a list Corvane's people kept—you remember the lists, the loyalty audits after Father refused the grain levy. So when the Ostravan patrol found me, I didn't resist. I gave them the Ninth Company's position. I gave them the supply routes. And in exchange, they took my name off the execution rolls and put it on the protection rolls, and my family was spared reprisal, and forty-one men of the Ninth Company died in the snow because of what I traded."

The stove ticked. Outside, the snow went on falling past the broken saints.

"Forty-one," Sebastian repeated.

"Forty-one. I know the number because I made myself learn it. I say it to myself every morning so I don't get to forget it. Some nights I say their names, the ones I ever knew. Halvic. Bren. The Odry twins." His voice didn't waver; that was the worst part. This was a wound so old it had calloused into something like stone. "The letters Ansel brought you—that was part of the arrangement too. Proof of life, held in reserve. They wanted leverage over someone inside your delegation, and I wanted—" He finally looked at her, and his eyes were wet and steady at once. "I wanted my sister to know I was alive. Is that a sin or a mercy? I've never worked it out."

"It's both," Sebastian said quietly. "Everything worth anything is both."

"Don't absolve me yet." There was something almost fierce in it. "There's more, and it's worse. The intelligence I gave them—it didn't stop with the Ninth Company. For three years I've translated documents for their military archive. Intercepted dispatches. Your codes, Seb. Old ones, mostly superseded, but some of them—" He hesitated. "Some of them are the ones you're carrying right now. I helped revise the cipher tables in '39, before I defected. I know the book against your ribs better than you do. If Corvane's delegation falls—and it will fall—the Ostravans will want me to sit across a table from their cryptographers and empty out everything left in my head. And I will be asked to do it willingly, gratefully, as the price of keeping this protection. Do you understand what I'm telling you? They haven't freed me. They've just found a more comfortable shape for the cage."

The room felt smaller. Sebastian thought of the codebook memorized against her heart, every page of it, and understood suddenly that she had been carrying her brother's confession all along without knowing it—that the weight between her ribs had never been paper at all.

"What are you asking me?" she said.

Tomas stood. He went to the window and looked out at the six apple trees, and when he spoke again his voice had dropped into something very quiet, very level—the voice he used for the true things.

"I'm asking you to help me disappear."

* * *

"Not escape," he said, turning back to her. "Escape is what prisoners do, and prisoners get hunted. Disappearance is different. It requires paperwork, patience, money, and a story airtight enough to survive boredom. A dead man's name from a village nobody can find on a map. A trade. A face that belongs to no one important." He almost smiled. "I've had three years to design it. I know exactly what I need. What I don't have is anyone outside these walls willing to build it with me."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I translate their archives until the war ends, and then they either pension me off as a useful collaborator or shoot me as an inconvenient one. Depends on whose faction wins the peace." He shrugged, a small terrible shrug. "You wouldn't be wrong to refuse. That's what I need you to hear. Whatever you choose, you choose clean."

Sebastian rose and walked to the window beside him. Below, the ruined orchard lay white and silent, and she found herself counting the trees the way she counted everything now—in grafts, in seasons, in what might be coaxed back to life from what had been cut.

"You want to know what I thought," she said, "when Ansel put those letters in my hands? I thought: he survived. Everything else—the Republic, the delegation, Corvane, all of it—it was scaffolding, and the real building was just this. My brother is alive. Get to him. Everything I did after that, I did with open eyes. So don't stand there warning me about the price, Tomas. I paid it at the border road, in the snow, before I ever heard the number forty-one."

"But now you've heard it."

"Now I've heard it." She turned to face him. "And here is what I know about you, little brother. You were nine years old when you climbed the north apple tree to prove you weren't afraid of heights, and you froze up there for two hours, and Father had to fetch the ladder, and you cried the whole way down—from shame, not fear. You have never once been able to live inside a lie. You confessed to breaking the grafting knife when I was the one who broke it. You confessed to losing the tithe money. You are the worst liar in the history of the Underhill family, and you have spent ten years being the best one in the Ostravan army, and it has been killing you by inches. I can see it. You're stone on the outside and hollow on the inside, and the hollow is getting bigger."

Tomas's jaw worked. He said nothing.

"So no," Sebastian said. "I'm not going to absolve you. Absolution is what priests hand out, and priests weren't in the snow with the Ninth Company, and neither was I, thank God, and neither were you in the end—not really, not the way you describe it. You were a frightened boy in a machine that ate boys, and you bought the only currency you had, and you have been paying interest ever since. I can't forgive that on behalf of forty-one men. Nobody can. Not even you."

"Then what—"

"What I can do," she said, and her voice did not shake, though she felt the shaking underneath it like a current under ice, "is choose you anyway. Not because it's deserved. Because it's decided. Forgiveness isn't a feeling, Tomas—it's a graft. You cut two living things and bind them together and you tend the join every day whether it takes or not, and most of the time it doesn't take, but sometimes—sometimes you get a tree that couldn't have existed any other way. That's what I'm offering. Not a feeling. A decision, renewed daily, in all weather. Take it or leave it, but understand what it is."

For a long moment her brother looked at her, and the stone on his face developed cracks, and through the cracks came not tears exactly but something older—relief, maybe, or the exhaustion of a man setting down a weight he'd mistaken for his skeleton.

"A graft," he repeated. "You've been carrying actual cuttings across a war zone, and you give me horticulture as a theology."

"We're Underhills. It's the only scripture we have."

He laughed then—or sobbed, or both, the sounds indistinguishable—and pulled her in, and they stood a long time in the small cold room, two children of a drowned republic holding each other upright.

* * *

They worked through the afternoon, because grief and planning use the same hours and the practical mind is a mercy to the broken one. By the time the light failed past the barred window, the shape of it stood clear on the table between them:

A dead man named Ferenc Odry—one of the twins, lost at the same engagement where Tomas traded the Ninth Company—had no surviving kin in Ostrava's records and a birth village burned off the map in the first autumn of the war. Papers existed in the garrison archive confirming Odry's survival of a later skirmish; a clerk's error, uncorrected for years. One signature, properly bribed, and Tomas Underhill ceased to exist while Ferenc Odry walked out of a records office with a discharge and a travel pass.

"The bribe is the fragile joint," Ansel said when he joined them at dusk, pulling up the third chair uninvited and unremarked, as he had apparently decided he now belonged in this family's business. "The records clerk is a man named Duszek. He owes me. But owing and doing are different animals, and if the protection detail notices their asset has evaporated, questions travel upward fast."

"Then the asset shouldn't evaporate," Sebastian said slowly. Something was turning over in her mind, heavy and cold and inevitable. "Not entirely. A disappeared man leaves a shadow, or the hunters get curious. What if the archive translations continue—handwriting matched, pages delivered—but the man producing them is a fiction maintained on purpose?" She looked at Tomas. "You keep translating. From wherever you end up. Through intermediaries. They keep their useful ghost, and you keep your freedom, and neither side ever looks too closely at a bargain that's working."

"That's years of servitude," Ansel said.

"It's years of *alive*," Sebastian answered. "We're not shopping for perfect. We're shopping for possible."

Tomas was watching her with an expression she recognized from childhood—from the day she'd shown him her first successful graft, a scion of winter pear married onto stubborn crabapple root. Wonder, and the beginnings of belief.

"When did you become the dangerous one in the family?" he asked.

"About the time they made me carry secrets for a living." She gathered the papers into her coat, next to the letters, next to the memorized codes she still carried nowhere but in her skull. "We move at first light. Duszek opens his office early, and Ansel says he's honest before his second drink and purchasable after it."

"And after that?" Tomas asked. "After the papers, the pass, the road—where does a dead man go?"

Sebastian looked out the window one last time. The six apple trees stood black against the snowfield, patient as promises, and somewhere south of them, in another country that no longer existed, there had once been an orchard where two children learned that nothing living survives by staying whole—you survive by being cut, and joined, and tended.

"To the border villages," she said. "Where the maps run out and nobody asks a quiet man with a spade where he came from. We'll plant in spring."

Tomas nodded slowly. Then he frowned, glancing toward the door, where boots had begun to ring on the cloister stones—too many boots, and moving with a purpose that had nothing to do with evening rounds.

Ansel was already on his feet, hand inside his coat, listening.

"That," he said quietly, "is Duszek. And he was supposed to come alone."
