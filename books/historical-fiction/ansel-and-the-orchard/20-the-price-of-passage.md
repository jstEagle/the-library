## 20. The Price of Passage

The garrison at Kolvary had been a monastery once, and it had not entirely surrendered the habit. The corridors were too quiet. The windows were too tall, admitting long gray blades of light that fell across the stone floors like the bars of some confessional built for a whole army. Sebastian walked those corridors on the third morning after Tomas's reunion with her, and she felt the building listening.

Ansel walked beside her, his stride shortened to match hers without seeming to notice he was doing it. He wore his dress uniform for the first time since she'd known him—the dark Ostravan blue, the silver collar-tabs of a man whose rank was spoken rather than written—and she understood that this was not vanity. It was currency. Today they would spend everything they had, and Ansel meant to arrive looking like a man who could afford the transaction.

"You can still refuse," he said quietly, as they climbed the stair to the intelligence wing. "You owe them nothing. Not yet."

"I owe them Tomas's life," she said. "That's the same thing, from where I'm standing."

"It isn't. That's what I need you to understand before that door opens." He stopped on the landing. Below them, a clerk crossed the hall carrying a ledger against his chest like an infant. "Spymasters are not buyers, Sebastian. They are appraisers. They will weigh what you offer, and then they will weigh *you*, and whichever is worth less is what they'll take. If you walk in there as a woman trading a secret to save her brother, you will be priced as a woman trading a secret. If you walk in there as a courier of the Vessarine Foreign Ministry—*the* courier, the one who carried every code and cipher the Republic trusted in its last year—then you are something rarer. You are inventory walking in on its own legs."

"Then I'll be inventory," she said. "Inventory doesn't flinch."

Something moved through his face—not quite a smile, but the place where a smile would live in a man who had learned to keep his own small. He touched two fingers briefly to her elbow, the only touch either of them permitted themselves inside these walls, and opened the door.

The room beyond was warm in a way that felt administered rather than earned. A stove burned behind a brass screen. Three men sat at a table too large for them, and the arrangement told her everything before anyone spoke: the one in the center was old, dry as winter paper, with eyes the color of dishwater and a stillness that suggested he had once been very fast; the man to his left was fat and pleasant and taking notes; the one to the right was young enough to be ambitious and had not yet learned to hide it. Papers lay before them in neat stacks. None of them were open. They did not need to be. Sebastian recognized the format of Vessarine dispatches from across the room, and understood that they had already read everything Corvane's intercepted traffic could teach them about her.

"Courier Underhill," the old man said. His voice was soft, almost kind, which was worse. "Or shall we use another name? We have several."

"Sebastian will do," she said. She did not sit until invited, and when invited, sat the way Tomas had taught her to sit at the negotiation table of their father's orchard dealings: back straight, hands visible, gaze level. "You know why I've come."

"We know why you *say* you've come." The old man folded his hands. "You want safe passage for the traitor Tomas Underhill out of territories we hold, under papers we issue, past checkpoints we control. That is a great deal of our goodwill to spend on a woman who arrived here three days ago wearing our enemy's colors in her heart."

"My brother is not a traitor to anything but a government that sold him," Sebastian said. "But I'm not here to argue the word. Words are cheap and I have none to spare. I'm here because I have something you want more than him."

The pleasant fat man smiled. The ambitious young one leaned forward. The old man did not move at all, and she knew then that she had already guessed right, because a man who does not move when bait is offered has already decided how much the bait weighs.

"Say it," the old man said.

"Corvane keeps accounts outside the Republic's ledgers." She let the name sit in the warm air like a coin dropped into a well. "Minister Corvane, first voice of the delegation, architect of the armistice everyone in this room knows is a fiction. He has been selling the Republic twice over—once to your kingdom in the terms he pretends to negotiate, and once to whoever pays best for the pieces after. Gold moved through Vell in the autumn. Not ministry gold. His gold. Routed through a grain merchant called Halvet Sarn, whose warehouse receipts are fiction and whose signature appears on transfers that never touch grain at all."

She watched the fat man's pen stop moving. Watched the young one glance at his superior. The old man continued to look at her with his dishwater eyes, and she had the sudden vertiginous sense that behind them was not boredom but arithmetic.

"How do you know this?" he asked.

"Because someone had to carry the correspondence," Sebastian said. "And no one guards a courier's eyes, because no one believes a courier is anyone at all. That invisibility was their design. It became my education. Corvane dictated three letters to Sarn while I waited in the room with his sealed dispatch case at my feet. I memorized the dates, the amounts, the routing through the border house at Tessel. I carried the confirmation replies myself, in my own hands, and watched his face while he read them. He thinks the knowledge died with the delegation's innocence. It didn't. It lives behind my ribs, along with everything else I ever memorized."

"And why," the old man said, with terrible gentleness, "would you spend this now? A clever woman holds such knowledge in reserve. Insurance. Leverage for her own skin."

"Because insurance is for people who expect to survive by hiding, and I am finished hiding." She heard her own voice go quiet and was glad of it; quiet made men lean in. "Corvane ruined my country before your armies reached its walls. He is ruining it still, from inside your armistice, and every day he stands at that table he spends lives—mine among them, my brother's among them—as though they were his to spend. I kept his secret while I needed my invisibility. My invisibility is gone. He has named me a traitor to a state that no longer exists to judge me, and so I am free to be honest for the first time in a year." She unfolded her hands, laid them flat on the table. "You want Corvane's ruin. Here is how you get it. Not by arresting him—an arrest makes him a martyr to whatever faction buys his story next. Ruin him the way he ruins others. Follow the money. Publish it. Let the men he's cheating see the ledger. He has partners in Vell, in your occupied towns, in the free ports. Partners who believe they are the only ones being paid. Show each of them the others' names, and you won't need to fire a shot at Minister Corvane. His own associates will do it for you, and thank you for the privilege."

Silence held the room for the length of ten heartbeats. The stove ticked. Somewhere below, boots rang on stone in the measured cadence of a changing guard.

Then the old man said, to the room rather than to her: "The Tessel routing. Recite it."

So she did. Dates, sums in crowns, the false weights entered on the warehouse receipts, the initials used in place of names, the day the payments doubled and why—that week the northern magazines fell, and Corvane had raised his price. She gave it all in a flat courier's cadence, unembellished, the way she had carried codes for a dying republic: accurately, completely, without opinion. When she finished, the fat man had filled four pages and the young one had gone pale in a way that suggested ambition had just met its subject matter.

The old man looked at her for a long moment. "You understand what you've done. This ends Corvane. It also ends the fiction that the Vessarine delegation speaks for anyone but itself. Our ministers will use this to break the talks entirely. Your republic dies faster."

"My republic died in the autumn," Sebastian said, "in a warehouse that never contained any grain. I'm just burying it properly."

* * *

They gave her tea afterward, in a smaller room, which she understood to mean she had passed some examination whose questions she never saw. Ansel stood at the window with his hands clasped behind him, and only when the door had shut on the last adjutant did he turn.

"They'll give you the passage papers within the week," he said. "For Tomas. Under a merchant's name—I heard them settle on Halvet Sarn's, which I suspect was someone's idea of a joke."

"Their humor needs work."

"Sebastian." He came toward her, and the formal distance between them collapsed by half, which from Ansel was a confession. "Do you know what you actually did in that room? Not the accounts. Anyone might have carried the accounts. You walked into the nest of the people who decide whether your brother breathes, and you offered them the last valuable thing you owned, freely, before they could take it. Do you understand how rare that is? In my trade, everything is taken. Nothing is given. Men build entire careers on withholding less than what you handed over before lunch."

"I didn't hand it over," she said. "I spent it. There's a difference. Handed over means it's gone and I mourn it. Spent means it bought something." She looked down at her hands, at the faint green stain still ghosted under the nails from the cuttings she had potted two nights ago in the garrison's cold frame—Tomas watching her do it with an expression she was still learning to read, shame and wonder fighting for possession of his face. "It bought him. Whatever else happens, whatever the war does next, that ledger bought my brother's road out."

"And what did it cost?"

She considered lying to him and found she no longer wished to practice that skill in his presence. "It cost me the last thing I owned that Corvane couldn't touch. Now he has nothing of mine left to fear except my testimony, and I've just given that away too—to men who will use it better than I ever could, and for purposes I don't fully control." She laughed, short and without humor. "I spent my whole year learning to hold secrets. Turns out the point of holding them is choosing the moment to let them fly."

Outside the window, snow had begun again, fine and dry, sifting down through the gray light over the garrison yard. Somewhere in that weather, in a cell warmed by a single stove, Tomas was waiting to learn whether his sister's silence had been worth more than his confession. And somewhere far south, in the dying body of the Republic, Minister Corvane was at this hour dictating letters, signing receipts, believing himself untouchable—believing it still, this very afternoon, while three men in a former monastery read four pages of notes and began, quietly, with the patience of men who had ended greater men than Corvane, to sharpen the knife.

Ansel followed her gaze to the window. "There will be consequences," he said. "Not for you. For everyone else. When the money stops moving, men like Corvane don't go quietly. They burn what they can't keep."

"Then we should be elsewhere when it burns," Sebastian said.

He turned to look at her fully then, and she saw the question form in him before he spoke it—the question that had been growing between them since the orchard, since the frozen rows, since the night flight south with her hand in his and the Republic's codes abandoned like a shed skin on the snow behind them. Where. With whom. What they were building in the space where two nations had failed to exist.

"There's a village," he said carefully, "two days east. On the Ostravan side of the line, but barely. There's land there. Old orchard ground, fallow twenty years. The soil remembers apples even when no one asks it to."

Sebastian looked at him. Outside, the snow went on falling, patient and indifferent, covering the roads, the borders, the graves, the ledgers—covering everything equally, the way snow does, the way winter reminds the world that all claims are temporary.

"When the papers come," she said, "we leave. All three of us. And we plant in spring."

* * *

The papers came on the sixth day, earlier than promised, which meant someone important had intervened, which meant the account of Halvet Sarn had already begun to work in the dark like water working through a wall.

Ansel brought them to her at dusk, to the small room she shared with a widow's allotment of furniture and her potted cuttings sleeping under glass on the sill. He set the packet on the table—three documents, wax-sealed, bearing names that belonged to no one—and stood a moment without speaking, and she saw that his hands were not quite steady.

"What is it?" she asked. "The passage is good?"

"The passage is good." He drew a folded slip of paper from inside his coat instead, and laid it beside the seals. "This came up the wire an hour ago. From Vell."

Her chest tightened. She took the paper. It was a summary, clipped and official, of events she had set in motion six days earlier in a warm room above a cold yard: the grain merchant Halvet Sarn arrested attempting to cross the border with forty thousand crowns and no grain; his ledgers seized; his ledgers *read*. And beneath that, in the flat language of military reporting, the sentence that made the room tilt gently around her:

*Minister Corvane departed the delegation seat at Vell on the night of the 14th, destination unknown, accompanied by two aides and an armed escort. The delegation's remaining ministers have petitioned Ostrava for protection.*

"He ran," Sebastian said. She read it again, as though the words might rearrange themselves into something she could hold. "Before the money could finish burying him. He ran, and the rest of them—" She looked up at Ansel, and heard her own voice come out strange, thin with something that was not grief and not triumph but some bastard alloy of both. "The delegation is begging you for shelter. The men who hunted me. Who put a clerk to death hunting me. They're begging."

Ansel sat down across from her, slowly, the way a man sits when his knees have opinions. "Corvane will surface somewhere," he said. "Men like him always surface, in some port or other capital, with new names and other people's gold. But he'll surface ruined, Sebastian. Hunted by his own partners, stripped by his own thefts, worth nothing but his talent for survival. That's what you bought with your last secret. Not his death. Something better. His irrelevance."

She looked at the cuttings on the sill—the twelve small pots, bare whips of dormant wood, each labeled in her own hand with a variety name from a childhood orchard that no longer existed in any way that maps recorded. Snowlight lay blue and faint upon them.

"Tomas will want to know," she said. "About Corvane. About all of it."

"Yes. And there's something else he should hear from you first, before the officers tell it their way." Ansel hesitated, and she realized with a small shock that after everything—after the parley and the orchard and the flight through the frozen rows—this man could still be afraid. "When they processed Sarn's ledgers, they found correspondence. Letters between Sarn and a second buyer. Someone inside Ostrava who had been selling Corvane information about *our* troop movements for a year. The spymasters think that's why Corvane ran—he knew we'd find the other end of the thread." He met her eyes. "The trail points toward someone at this garrison, Sebastian. Someone with access. And the interrogators start asking their questions tomorrow."

She sat very still, the sealed passages forgotten in her lap, the snow falling blue and silent outside, and understood that the price of passage, like every price, had only begun to come due.
