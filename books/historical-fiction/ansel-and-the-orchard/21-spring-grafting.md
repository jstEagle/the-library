## 21. Spring Grafting

The village had no name on any map that mattered anymore. It sat in the fold of a valley where the border had been drawn and redrawn so many times that the stones marking it had been moved like furniture—once by treaty, once by army, once by a farmer who simply wanted his well on the other side of whatever line the ministers were arguing about that decade. The farmer's grandson still lived there, in a house of grey timber with a roof patched in three different generations of shingles, and he asked them nothing when they came up the road in the first week of thaw: not their names, not their nation, only whether they could pay for the loft and whether the tall one could mend a fence.

Ansel said he could. Tomas said nothing at all. And Sebastian stood in the mud of the lane with a satchel against her ribs—the same place she had once carried a codebook worth more than the village—and felt something loosen in her chest that had been clenched since the day she had left the capital pretending to be someone she was not.

The thaw had come late that year, and all at once, the way thaws do in the high valleys. Snowmelt roared down from the ridgelines and filled the ditches until they ran brown and urgent, and the air smelled of wet earth and rotting leaf litter and, faintly, sweetly, of buds splitting open along every bare branch. In Vell the spring had always announced itself with church bells and processions; here it arrived without ceremony, without permission, indifferent to whose flag flew over which customs house. The world simply decided to begin again.

They had been in the village eleven days before Sebastian took the cuttings out of the cold cellar.

She had kept them the way she had once kept secrets: wrapped in damp cloth, close to her body, checked obsessively. Twelve scions cut from the orchard at Vell on her last morning there—she had gone out alone in the grey light while Ansel waited by the gate with the horses, and she had chosen the branches the way her brother had taught her to choose them, young wood, pencil-thick, taken from limbs that bore the truest fruit. She had wrapped each one separately, labeled them by variety in a shorthand only two living people could read, and carried them across a border, through three checkpoints, past soldiers of two armies who had searched her bags for documents and never thought to look twice at sticks of dormant wood.

Tomas was in the yard when she brought them out into the light. He had taken to sitting in the sun these last weeks, hands flat on his knees, watching the road as though expecting it to bring something. It never did. He was thinner than she remembered him being, even accounting for the years, and there was a watchfulness in him now that had not been there when he was seventeen and teaching his little sister to graft—that quickness of eye that catalogued every stranger, every hoofbeat, every knock. He had paid for his survival with exactly that vigilance, she knew. He wore it the way other men wore scars.

He saw the bundle in her arms and went very still.

"You kept them," he said. His voice had changed too—lower, careful, the voice of a man who had spent years measuring every sentence for what it might cost him. But underneath it, for one moment, she heard the boy from the orchard at home, the one who had pressed a grafting knife into her palm and told her that a tree was just a promise you planted.

"I told you I would."

"I told *you*," he said. "In the letters. That you should burn them. That carrying them—" He stopped himself, jaw working. The old habit. Measuring. "It was dangerous. What you did."

"It was," she agreed. "Most things worth doing are." She knelt in the mud beside him and unwrapped the cloth, laying the twelve scions out in a row on an old board, and watched her brother look at them the way a man looks at water after a long thirst.

* * *

The farmer's grandson—his name was Davor, though half the village called him by his father's name and the other half by a nickname involving a mule—had an abandoned corner of his land where an old orchard had stood before a fire took the trees some thirty years back. The stumps were still there, blackened and soft with rot, arranged in the long straight rows of someone's forgotten ambition. He had offered the ground to Ansel the second week, shrugging, saying that dead trees made poor shade anyway and if strangers wanted to dig, digging was free labor.

Sebastian spent a morning walking those rows before she chose the rootstock. There wasn't any, not properly—the fire had seen to that—but crab apples had seeded themselves wild along the field edge, gnarled and stubborn things that survived on neglect, and it was among these that she found what she needed: four seedlings, two or three years old, thick enough at the base to take a scion.

"Those?" Ansel crouched beside her, sleeves rolled, forearms already streaked with mud from the fence he had promised to mend and had mostly mended. "They're hardly more than weeds."

"They're survivors." She laid her hand flat along one of the little trunks, feeling the life in it, the slow pressure of sap rising. "Everything good I know about grafting, my brother taught me, and everything he knew he learned from our father, and my father learned it working trees exactly this neglected. A fancy rootstock gives you a fancy tree, if you have fancy soil and fancy weather and someone to fuss over it. These—" she tapped the seedling "—these give you a tree that doesn't ask permission. It takes what the ground offers and makes fruit anyway."

"Like certain couriers I could name."

"Like certain officers," she returned, "who learned to play for time so well he forgot how to stop." But she said it gently, and he heard the gentleness, because that was the language they had built between them over that long winter—not the languages of either country but the private one of people who had watched each other be brave and afraid and honest, and had counted it.

Davor lent them a spade with a cracked handle and a knife that had probably been used for everything except grafting, and Ansel sharpened it on a whetstone until the edge came back, patient as tide. Sebastian showed him the cuts. Whip-and-tongue, the way her father had done it, the way Tomas had taught her with a stolen branch and a stolen hour behind the smokehouse when she was nine and her hands were too small to hold both knife and wood.

"You match the cambium," she said, fitting the tongue of the scion into the matching tongue of the stock, the green layers sliding against each other like hands clasping. "Not the bark. The bark is just the outside—the part the world sees. The cambium is the living part, the layer that grows. If the living layers touch, if they're held tight together and kept from drying out, the wound heals and the two become one tree. You can't tell where one ends and the other begins, after a few years. Not without cutting it open."

"And if they don't match?"

"Then it dies," she said simply. "Or it lives for a season and dies in the winter. Grafting doesn't forgive almost."

Ansel was quiet for a moment, looking at the joined wood in her hands. "My grandmother did this," he said at last. "I'd forgotten. Back home—or where home was supposed to be—she had pears, and every spring she'd stand at the table with her knife and her wax and this same expression you have right now, and I thought she was praying."

"She was," Sebastian said. "It's the same thing."

* * *

They planted on a morning of thin gold light, the kind that comes when the mist hasn't fully burned off the valley. Tomas dug the holes. He insisted on it, waving off Ansel's offer of the spade, and worked in silence with the economy of a man who had done hard labor under supervision and never unlearned the habit of making his body efficient. Four holes, spaced as the old stumps suggested the rows had once been spaced, in ground that Davor swore grew decent potatoes and therefore decent anything.

Sebastian set each scion onto its stock and bound the union with strips of cloth she had torn from a shirt that had already given enough to bandages and disguise that it owed neither Republic nor Kingdom anything further. Over the binding she painted warmed wax, sealing the wound against air and doubt. Twelve scions, four trees, three varieties—the early apple that ripened yellow as candlelight, the keeper that lasted in the cellar till Lent, and the third, the one that mattered most, the one their father had grafted himself from a cutting brought back from his own father's orchard in a country that had also stopped existing before Sebastian was born.

"That one," Tomas said, kneeling beside her as she finished the last binding. His voice had gone rough. "You chose that one."

"Of course I did."

"It won't fruit for five years. Maybe six. If it takes at all."

"Yes."

"We might not—" He stopped again. The measuring voice. But then he did something he had not done since she had found him in that garrison room, pale and ashamed behind a desk that belonged to men who owned him: he reached out and put his hand over hers, over the wax and the cloth and the small green secret of the graft, and left it there. "We might not see it."

"Then we'll have planted it," Sebastian said. "That's the whole point of orchards, Tomi. Nobody plants one for themselves. You plant them for whoever comes after, and you trust them to plant for whoever comes after them. Papa knew he'd be dust before the keeper apples got good. He planted anyway."

"He'd have liked this ground," Tomas said, looking around—at the valley, the meltwater running silver in the ditches, the mountains holding their last rags of snow like old men keeping their collars up against April. "High enough for frost, low enough for water. Stubborn soil. Nobody important would ever fight over it." He laughed then, short and cracked. "Which is precisely why everyone will leave us alone."

* * *

Ansel worked alongside them all morning, and Sebastian watched him when she thought he wouldn't notice, and caught him watching her the same way, and understood that this too was a kind of grafting—two people pressing their living layers together and holding them tight and waiting to see if the wound healed into one thing. They had met across a table with a war between them. They had lied to their superiors, betrayed their nations, run through frozen orchard rows with dogs behind them. None of that was love. Love was this: the ordinary morning, the shared work, the sharpened knife passed back and forth handle-first, the silence that didn't need filling.

At midday Davor's wife brought them bread and hard cheese and a jug of something fermented that made Tomas cough and laugh at the same time, the first real laugh Sebastian had heard out of him since the reunion—the laugh of a boy sneaking cider behind the smokehouse. They ate sitting on the old blackened stumps, which was either sacrilege or consecration, and nobody in the village looked twice at the three strangers eating lunch in a dead man's orchard, because in a border village nobody is a stranger for longer than it takes to trade news of the roads.

"The passes opened Tuesday," Davor reported, settling onto a stump with the ease of a man invited or inviting himself, whichever suited. "Caravan traffic starting up. Merchants coming through say the fighting's over around Vell—properly over, not just quiet. They say the Republic's done. Signed away, parcel by parcel. Corvane's faction handed over the river provinces without a shot." He said it the way he might report rain or the price of salt, and that was the thing about borders drawn in pencil: the people who lived inside them had long ago stopped expecting the paper to protect them.

Sebastian felt Tomas go rigid beside her, and put her hand on his arm. Not grief, exactly. Something more complicated—a nation dying is like a parent dying whom you loved and resented in equal measure, whose death frees you and orphans you in the same breath. She had chosen exile; exile did not stop being a wound just because the blade was your own hand.

"And the delegation?" she asked, keeping her voice level. "The ones who were stationed at Vell?"

"Gone, most of them. Scattered or bought. Your Minister—" Davor squinted, dredging memory. "Corvane. They say he's in the capital of the new protectorate now, dining with the men who signed the surrender. They say he sold the accounts, the reserves, everything, and they say the Ostravans let him keep a title because it was cheaper than hunting him." He shrugged. "Merchants talk. Half of it's lies. But the roads are open, and that part I saw myself."

So it had ended the way she had known it would end, standing at the armistice table transcribing minutes in code while powerful men arranged the corpse of their country like tailors arranging a suit. Corvane ascendant, fat on ruin. The Republic a word in old dispatches. Somewhere in a records office, no doubt, a warrant with her name on it was quietly expiring, one more loose thread in an unraveling garment—*the courier*, the clerk had said, under duress, and by now the courier was a ghost story, a rumor, a woman who had vanished into the snow with a dead man's letters.

Dead man. She looked at Tomas, chewing bread in the sun with wax on his fingers, alive, complicit, forgiven—being-forgiven, rather, which was a slower and more daily discipline—and she found that the anger had finally burned down to embers she could live beside. He had traded intelligence for his life. She had traded nations for his safety. Between them lay a ledger neither would ever balance, and they had decided, both of them, somewhere in the hard weeks of late winter, to stop trying. Forgiveness, she was learning, was not a feeling that arrived. It was a tree you planted and tended and did not expect to shade you for years.

"Corvane will fall eventually," Ansel said quietly, when Davor had wandered off toward his fences. "Men like that always do. They mistake the collapse for a ladder." He turned the grafting knife over in his hands, cleaning the sap from the blade. "When he falls, there will be questions. Accounts. Someone will find the hidden ledgers, and they'll trace them back, and they'll wonder who emptied them."

"I know," Sebastian said. She had spent Corvane's last secret months ago, in a cold room in Ostrava, buying her brother's passage—and she did not regret it, would never regret it—but spending a weapon does not mean forgetting its shape. "Let them wonder. Let them chase it. By the time anyone follows the money to this valley, we'll be old and the trees will be taller than the house."

"Taller than two houses," Ansel said. "I'm planting more next week. Davor says I can have the whole east slope if I keep the mule off it."

"You've adopted the mule."

"The mule adopted me. There's a difference, and it matters legally."

Tomas snorted. And for a while none of them spoke at all, because the afternoon was warm and the work was waiting and some silences are fuller than speech.

* * *

They finished at dusk. Four little trees in a row, knee-high, absurdly slight against the vastness of the valley—twigs really, bundles of hope tied with rag and sealed with wax. Any wind could break them. Any late frost could kill them. A wandering goat could undo in ten minutes everything the winter had cost.

But the unions were clean. Sebastian had checked each one twice, thumbing the joins, feeling the cambium kiss cambium beneath the bark, the living layers held fast. They would take, or they wouldn't; that was the covenant of grafting, struck honestly, no guarantees given. All she could promise them was what her father had promised his trees and his children: good ground, honest work, and someone who would stay to see it through.

She stood back and wiped her hands on her skirt, and found that Ansel had come to stand beside her, and Tomas on her other side, and that the three of them made a small strange family shaped like a parenthesis around four sticks in the mud.

"No flag," Tomas observed. His eyes were wet, but his voice was steady, and he didn't wipe his face, because there was no one here he needed to hide from. "No garrison. No minister within fifty miles. Just us and the mule."

"Just us and the mule," Ansel confirmed gravely.

Sebastian looked down the row—at the early apple that would fruit yellow as candlelight, at the keeper that would outlast every winter they gave it, at her grandfather's tree reborn in foreign soil that no map claimed and no army wanted—and she thought about all the names she had worn. Daughter of the Underhills. Courier. Sebastian, the boy's name that had armored her for years. Traitor, on at least two warrants. Widow of a Republic that had died owing her wages. She had been all of these, and none of them had ever quite fit, the way a scion never fits its stock by anything except the living layer where growth happens.

Here, she supposed, she was just the woman who grafted the trees. It seemed, finally, enough.

The evening star came out over the ridge, the first one, and down in the village someone lit a lamp in a window, and the meltwater sang in the ditches the way it would sing all night, going wherever water goes when nobody commands it.

Tomas touched the smallest graft once, lightly, the way you touch a sleeping child. "Five years," he said. "Six at the outside."

"Then we'd better get started on the fence," Sebastian said, and picked up the tools, and walked home.
