## 22. Ansel and the Orchard

The traveler came up the road at the hour when the light went long and gold across the valley, when the frost had burned off the grass and the air smelled of wet earth and apple blossom. Sebastian saw him first from the top of her ladder—a speck on the white road, a pack on his shoulders, walking with the deliberate pace of a man who had somewhere to be but was in no hurry to arrive. She watched him for a while before she went back to her work. In the years since the war, she had learned that most people who came up this road were only lost, and lost people needed nothing from her but directions.

She finished the cut she was making—a water sprout growing too eagerly from the scaffold limb of a Gravenstein, one of Tomas's favorites—and wiped her knife on her apron before she climbed down.

Ansel was already in the yard, which did not surprise her. He had a way of appearing wherever she was, as if some part of him were always oriented toward her like a compass needle, patient and unerring. He stood by the well with his sleeves rolled and his gray-streaked hair tied back, talking to the traveler in the easy Ostravan of the borderlands, and when he caught her eye over the stranger's shoulder he gave her the small look—the private one, the one that still, after all these years, made her feel like a girl hiding something sweet in her cheek.

"Sebastian," he said. "A guest."

"A guest," she agreed, and set down her ladder.

The man took off his cap. He was perhaps forty, weathered, with the ink-stained fingers of someone who wrote for a living—not a soldier's hands, not a farmer's. His accent, when he spoke, was Vessarine. The old Vessarine, the capital's clipped vowels, softened by years and distance but unmistakable, the way a scar is unmistakable no matter how it fades.

"Forgive me," he said. "I'm traveling. Writing an account of the war, or trying to. I heard there was an orchard here planted by a woman who came out of the Republic in the last winter, and I—" He stopped, and she saw him gather himself, the way men did around her sometimes, sensing something they could not name. "I've been to Vell," he said. "To the estate. It's a garrison school now. They tore out most of the orchard for a drill yard, but a few trees survived. Someone has been pruning them. Badly." He smiled, apologetic. "I wondered if you knew anything about it. About those days. People say you don't speak of them."

"I speak of them," Sebastian said. "Just not the way historians want."

Ansel made a low sound that might have been a laugh. He knew better than anyone what she meant.

* * *

She fed the traveler at the long table under the pear tree because it was nearly noon and because refusing food to a hungry man was against everything Tomas had ever taught her. He ate bread and hard cheese and the first radishes of the season, and he talked, because writers always talked, and she listened with her hands wrapped around a cup of chicory coffee while Ansel split kindling nearby with the unhurried rhythm of a man who had done ten thousand chores beside her and would do ten thousand more.

The traveler told her about the Republic. Or what people now called the Republic—the way one spoke of a drowned friend, in the past tense, with the careful tenderness owed to the dead. He told her about the capital under Corvane's faction, the last desperate months, the auctions of ministries, the ledgers sold to Ostravan bankers even as the flags came down. He told her about the trials afterward, how Corvane had been taken not by his own countrymen but by creditors, hauled before a tribunal in a borrowed coat because his own had been repossessed. He told it all in the flat, dutiful tone of a man reciting facts he had repeated so often they had worn smooth as river stones.

"You were in Vell during the talks," he said. "Some accounts mention a courier."

"Some accounts are wrong," Sebastian said.

He looked at her then, really looked, and she saw the moment the arithmetic finished behind his eyes. Not certainty—he would never have certainty; she had spent a lifetime making sure of that—but the writer's instinct, the nose for the buried thing. He glanced at Ansel splitting wood. At the orchard climbing the hill behind the house, rows and rows of young trees, most of them grown from cuttings she had carried through fire and border and betrayal in the pocket of a dead woman's coat. At the second chair at the table, worn smooth at the arms, where Tomas sat every morning with his coffee and said almost nothing, as he had said almost nothing for twenty-two years.

"I won't print your names," the traveler said quietly.

"There's nothing to print," Sebastian said. "A courier died in the last winter of the war. That's all any account needs to say."

* * *

Afterward she walked him to the gate, because that was the custom of the house, and because the orchard path passed close enough that he would see the trees, and she wanted him to see them. Not for pride. For witness.

They walked between the rows in silence for a while. Spring was late this year and the blossom was just breaking, white and faintly pink along the branches, the bees working the early flowers with a sound like distant surf. Some of these trees she had planted herself, from seed, patient as stone. Others were grafts—Gravensteins and Winter Bannocks and the little tart apples nobody grew anymore except her, the ones Tomas had called *the stubborn ones*, the variety their mother had tended in the orchard behind the house in the capital, the house that no longer existed, on a street that had been renamed twice since.

"The estate at Vell," the traveler said, as they neared the gate. "You must have known it. Before."

"I knew its orchard," Sebastian said. "It was frozen solid when we came. Half the trees were dying. Nobody had pruned in three winters." She paused at the gate and rested her hand on the post. "I used to go out at night and work on them. Everyone thought I was mad, or a spy. Both, eventually."

"And the trees here—are they from there? From Vell?"

"Some." She looked back up the hill, at the rows catching the light. "Cuttings I carried out. You can carry a nation in a coat pocket, if the nation is small enough and you love it properly."

The traveler was quiet for a moment. Then he asked it—the question she had known was coming since the hour he appeared on the road, the question that came every few years in one form or another, from journalists, from pilgrims, from drunk veterans, from children born after the fall who studied the Republic in schoolbooks printed in Ostrava:

"Do you mourn it?" he said. "The Republic. Do you mourn what was lost?"

Sebastian considered him. She considered the question. She had had years to consider it, and the strange thing—the thing that had taken her the longest to learn—was that the honest answer had never changed, though everything else about her life had.

"I mourn what it might have been," she said.

The traveler blinked. "Not what it was?"

"No." She said it without bitterness, which surprised even her sometimes; the bitterness had drained out of her the way winter drained out of the soil, slowly and then suddenly. "What it was deserves no mourning. What it was was Corvane selling ministries out the back door while boys froze on the walls. What it was was ministers who negotiated for leverage over each other instead of peace. What it was was a state so frightened of its own people that it hunted a courier through an orchard at night rather than ask why she was crying." She turned back to the rows of blossom. "But what it might have been—that's worth grieving. A republic is only a promise. Ours was broken before it was kept, and the tragedy isn't that the promise failed. It's that it could have held. There was a version of those talks at Vell where both sides were tired enough, and cold enough, and honest enough, and it held. I transcribed the minutes. I know exactly which sentence it turned on. One sentence."

"What sentence?"

She smiled, small and tired. "If I told you, you'd put it in your book, and then everyone would spend another fifty years arguing about whether one man's ambition outweighed one sentence. And it doesn't matter. The sentence is dead. The men are dead. What matters—" she touched the gatepost again, the grain of it smooth under her palm, "—is what you plant after."

The traveler followed her gaze up the hill. "And that's your answer? Planting?"

"That's my answer." She opened the gate for him. "I mourned for years. Properly, in the dark, the way you're supposed to. And then one spring I noticed I'd stopped pruning the way grief prunes—cutting everything back, punishing the tree for growing—and started pruning the way hope does. Making room. Letting light in. A tree doesn't care what flag flew over the field where it was planted. It cares whether someone comes out with a knife in March and knows what they're doing."

The traveler laughed despite himself, and she liked him for it. At the gate he shook her hand—her bare, work-roughened hand, calloused in the shape of a lifetime of knives—and he said, "May I write one line? Not names. Just that somewhere past the border there is an orchard, and the woman who tends it does not mourn the Republic, only what it might have been, and prunes anyway."

Sebastian looked at him for a long moment. Somewhere behind her, up the hill, Ansel's axe had gone quiet, and she knew without turning that he was listening, that he had been listening the whole time, that after twenty-two years he still listened to everything she said as if it were dispatches in code.

"Prunes anyway," she agreed. "That will do."

* * *

He went down the road and became a speck again, and then nothing, and Sebastian stood at the gate until Ansel came to stand beside her, his shoulder warm against hers.

"He'll write it," Ansel said. "Whatever you tell him, he'll make it prettier than it was."

"Good. It should be prettier than it was. We earned that much."

"Tomas is mending the press in the barn. He wants to bottle cider tonight, if the light holds."

"Then we'd better prune fast." But she didn't move yet. The wind moved through the orchard, lifting the blossom, carrying the bees' surf-sound up the hill, and Sebastian let it move through her too—all of it, the whole of it: the capital going down in smoke behind a departing convoy, the codebook warm against her ribs, the checkpoint lantern sweeping her face, the letters in a dead man's handwriting, the orchard frozen silver at midnight, Ansel's breath in the dark saying her brother's name, the flight through the frozen rows, the border crossing where the world turned inside out, Tomas's ruined face in the garrison light, the fall of Vell arriving as a rumor and staying as a fact, the price of passage paid in secrets, and then—after, after, after—the slow years, the ordinary years, the cuttings taking root in ground that belonged to no flag and never would.

"Do you regret it?" Ansel asked. He asked it rarely now, maybe once a year, always at the same hour of the day, as if the question needed the right light to survive being spoken. "Any of it."

Sebastian thought about the honest answer. She had always given him the honest answer; it was the whole foundation of what they had built, the one law of their house, harder than any constitution the Republic had ever failed to keep.

"I regret that I had to choose," she said. "Duty made me, and love unmade me, and neither one was wrong. That's what I'd tell the traveler, if he could print it. I don't regret choosing my brother. I don't regret choosing you. I regret a world where choosing was necessary." She turned her face up toward him, into the light. "But Ansel—I got to choose. So many people in that story never got to choose anything. The clerk didn't. The guards didn't. Even Corvane, in the end—ambition chose him long before he chose it. I stood in an orchard with a knife in my hand and a dead brother alive on the other side of a border, and I chose. Whatever else happened, whatever it cost—I was free for exactly one moment, and I spent it correctly."

Ansel took her hand, the knife-callused one, and pressed it to his mouth the way he had done on the worst night of the flight through the frozen rows, when they had both believed they would die among the trees and he had kissed her knuckles anyway, absurdly, formally, like a man sealing a treaty.

"Then it wasn't wasted," he said. "The Republic. If it bought one free choice."

"It bought a hundred thousand choices," Sebastian said. "Every cutting we ever planted was a choice. Every morning Tomas gets up and says almost nothing and stays—that's a choice. This orchard is a country made entirely of choices. It's the only one I'm a citizen of now, and I'll tell you what I told the historian: I mourn it not at all."

From the barn, Tomas's voice called them in—not words, just her name, the way he always said it, *Sebastian*, the full name, never the boy's name she had worn through the war, because the first gift he had ever given her after the reunion was the return of her own name, spoken aloud, unhidden. The light was going gold. The bees were settling. Somewhere down the valley a church bell rang the hour in a language that had never been either nation's.

Sebastian picked up her knife and her ladder and walked up the hill into the rows, and pruned anyway.
