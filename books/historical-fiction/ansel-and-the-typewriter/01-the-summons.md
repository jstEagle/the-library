## 1. The Summons

The telegram arrived on a Tuesday, which Paloma Pemberton would later think was fitting, because Tuesdays were the days her master reserved for complaining.

"Read it aloud," said Herr Brandt, not looking up from the escapement he was dissecting on the bench between them. He had a jeweler's loupe screwed into one eye and the expression of a man who had found a personal insult inside a watch. "No — wait. First tell me whether it is long. If it is long, it's bad news, and I'll want to sit."

"It's short," Paloma said.

"Then it's worse. Short telegrams are expensive telegrams, and nobody pays by the word for good news." He set down his tweezers at last and held out a hand, palm up, like a surgeon demanding a scalpel. "Give it here."

She gave it to him and watched his eyebrows perform their small tragedy as he read. The workshop around them went on with its own quiet weather: the regulator clock in the corner ticking its patient eight-to-the-second, the smell of oil and cedar shavings, the gray October light doing its best through windows that hadn't been washed since the Kaiser was crowned. Somewhere upstairs, Frau Brandt was beating a rug with the rhythm of a woman settling an old argument.

"Hm," said Herr Brandt.

Paloma had worked for him for four years, since she was sixteen, and she had learned to read his *hm*s the way other people read weather. This one was the *hm* of a man handed a problem he did not want and could not refuse.

"The Sternwarte," he said. "The observatory. On the Schneeberg."

"I know where it is. Everyone knows where it is. It's the reason the valley has a road nobody uses."

"They use it in summer." He tapped the telegram against his palm. "Their master clock is failing. Running slow, then stopping outright. They want it seen to before the snows close the pass." He looked up at her over the top of his spectacles, and there it was — the thing she had been dreading since she'd heard the word *telegram*, arriving exactly on schedule. "They've asked for you."

"For me," Paloma repeated.

"You."

"Not for you. The finest clockmaker between here and the capital, a man who has repaired the cathedral chronometer twice, who once made a pocket watch so accurate that a railway inspector accused it of cheating—"

"He apologized afterward."

"—*that* man received a telegram asking instead for his apprentice."

Herr Brandt had the decency to look mildly stung. "I am sixty-eight years old," he said. "My knees have opinions about mountain roads. And my hands—" He held them out: broad, spotted, steady as ever, but she saw the faint tremor he pretended wasn't there, the one that had started last spring and gotten worse by autumn. "My hands are fine. But a master clock is not a mantel piece, girl. It's a week's work at altitude, in the cold, lying on your back inside a case the size of a coffin. They want someone young. Someone whose eyes still believe what they're told."

"And they asked for me by name?"

He passed her the telegram. She read it properly this time.

**STERNWARTE SCHNEEBERG STOP MASTER CLOCK FAILING STOP REQUIRE CLOCKMAKER IMMEDIATELY BEFORE PASS CLOSES STOP SEND PEMBERTON STOP FALK**

*Send Pemberton.* Not *send your best man*. Not even *send someone*. Her name, spelled out at a shilling a letter, chosen by a director she had never met.

"Well," she said, because the alternative was saying something truer. "That's flattering."

"That's strange," said Herr Brandt flatly. "I've never done work for them. You've certainly never done work for them. Observatories buy from instrument houses in the city, big firms with catalogs and brass plaques. They do not send telegrams to a two-room workshop above a chandler's in a town most maps forget." He took off his loupe and rubbed the eye beneath it. "Unless someone told them to."

"Who would tell them to?"

"How should I know? I'm an old man whose knees object to stairs." But his voice had gone careful, the way it did when he was thinking hard and didn't want her to see it. "Your mother, perhaps. Before she died, she knew half the scientific men in this country. She typed letters for more professors than the university has chairs."

The mention of her mother landed in Paloma's chest the way it always did — not a blow anymore, after two years, but a weight set down gently on a shelf that hadn't finished learning to hold it. Her mother, Adelheid Pemberton, typist, correspondent, keeper of other people's secrets in immaculate carbon copies, dead of the influenza the winter Paloma turned eighteen. Dead before Paloma had thought to ask her a tenth of the questions she now carried around like loose screws in a pocket.

"She was a typist," Paloma said. "Not a clockmaker."

"She was a woman who made herself useful to important people, which is rarer and more powerful than being a clockmaker." Herr Brandt picked his tweezers back up and bent over the escapement, conversation visibly concluded. "Well? Will you go?"

It was not really a question. That was the thing about duty, Paloma had learned: it rarely announced itself. It arrived dressed as an ordinary Tuesday, as a telegram, as a master saying *well? will you go?* when both of them already knew that a promise made to a customer was iron, and that she had been trained since girlhood to keep iron promises.

"I'll go," she said. "Someone has to keep you humble, and if I'm gone a week you'll have to be humble all by yourself."

"God forbid," said Herr Brandt, and the corners of his mouth twitched, and that was the whole ceremony. Duty as habit. You wound it every morning like a clock, and it carried you whether or not you'd chosen the hour.

* * *

She packed that evening, which did not take long, because Paloma owned few things and organized them like a tool chest. Two shirts, wool stockings, the heavy coat her mother had bought her the winter before she died — too large then, fitting now, which was its own quiet grief. Her father's old leather tool case, which she had inherited along with the trade: loupes, files, pin cutters, the box of brass and steel stock, the tiny screwdrivers ranked like soldiers. At the bottom of the case, wrapped in oilcloth because it always had been, lay her mother's things — not tools, but the small archive Adelheid had kept: a fountain pen worn to her grip, a packet of letters tied with string, a photograph of a house by a lake that Paloma had never been able to identify and had never quite thrown away.

She hesitated over the oilcloth bundle, the way she always did, and packed it, the way she always did. Some weights you carried because putting them down felt like losing the person twice.

Frau Brandt came down while Paloma was warming milk in the workshop kitchen, ostensibly to inspect the stove and actually to press a parcel of sausage and dark bread into her bag with the stealth of a woman who had been feeding apprentices for forty years and considered refusal a personal illness.

"The Schneeberg road," Frau Brandt said, folding the cloth down with unnecessary firmness. "You take the coach to Gründl and walk from the tollhouse. It's four hours if the weather holds. If the weather doesn't hold, you stay at the inn in Gründl and go up in the morning, and you don't let anyone shame you into otherwise. Mountain weather kills the proud first."

"Yes, Frau Brandt."

"And you write to us from the observatory. A postcard is sufficient. One line. *Arrived safe, clock is dreadful, love Paloma.* Even that."

"Yes, Frau Brandt."

"And if—" The old woman stopped, hands flat on the table, and looked at Paloma with an expression that had nothing of the stove about it. "If anything seems wrong up there. If the people seem wrong. You come home. Clocks can wait; they've waited centuries already. Do you understand me?"

"Perfectly," said Paloma, who understood nothing yet, and smiled, and was believed.

* * *

The coach to Gründl took most of a day, climbing out of the river country into country that had opinions of its own. Paloma shared a bench with a cheese merchant who talked for three hours about the price of alpine pasture leases and required no answers, only occasional nods, which suited her; she spent the time watching the peaks assemble themselves ahead, white-capped already though it was barely mid-October, and feeling the particular alertness that came over her near unfamiliar clocks — or unfamiliar problems dressed as clocks.

At Gründl she slept at the inn as instructed, under the disapproving portrait of some local worthy, and rose before light to walk the mountain road. The tollhouse keeper gave her a boiled egg, directions, and a prophecy.

"You're going up to the Sternwarte?" He squinted at her tool case. "For the clock? Hah. Whole valley knows that clock's been sick since summer. Old Falk up there, he's proud of it — says it's the most accurate clock in the province. Says the astronomers set their stars by it." He spat expertly into the frost. "Stars don't care, far as I can see. But there you are."

"Has anyone been sent for before?" Paloma asked. "A clockmaker, I mean."

The keeper scratched his jaw. "Not that I've heard. Mind, I don't hear everything. But the road's been empty since the tourists left." He nodded up-valley, toward a thread of track climbing through pines until the fog ate it. "Bridge over the gorge is the only way across. If the snows come early, that bridge shuts and everyone up there stays shut in with it till thaw. Happened in '09. Fellow got stuck up at the Sternwarte seven months. Came down in spring talking to himself and married a girl in Gründl within the month, so it can't be all bad."

"Encouraging," said Paloma, and shouldered her case.

The road climbed through pine and then above pine, through grass gone bronze, through the first thin skirmish lines of snow lying in the shadows like troops waiting for orders. By noon she could see the observatory itself: a pale stone bulk on the shoulder of the mountain, dome gleaming dully under cloud, a flagstaff bare of any flag, smoke rising from its chimneys in straight, disciplined columns. It looked less like a building than like a person standing very still, listening.

She had been walking four hours and had not met a single soul on the road, and somewhere around the third hour she had noticed that she was counting things without meaning to: the number of farmsteads passed (none), the number of carts (one, going down, driver who touched his cap and did not stop), the number of times the wind changed direction (four). Counting was what her hands did when her mind wouldn't settle. Her mother had been the same with words, she supposed — filling silence with neat rows of type.

Why her name? The question had ridden with her since the workshop, patient as a tick. Herr Brandt was famous in the way clockmakers are famous, which is to say among a hundred people, half of them rivals. Paloma was twenty years old, four years apprenticed, competent past her years by every honest measure and modest enough to know it proved nothing. An observatory director needing a clock fixed before winter does not consult the heavens for names. Somebody had given him hers. Somebody who knew it.

Her mother had known the director — that much Herr Brandt guessed. But her mother had been dead two years, and dead women do not dictate telegrams.

Unless, said a small voice at the base of her skull, in the tone it used for unwelcome arithmetic — unless something she arranged before she died has only now come due. Unless there's a letter somewhere, or an instruction, or a promise—

The thought broke off, because she crested the last rise and the observatory stood before her at fifty paces, close enough now to show detail: the shuttered dormer windows, the iron lamp bracket by the door, and the door itself opening while she was still a dozen steps away, as though someone had been watching the road for her.

A tall man came out onto the step, gray-bearded, erect, wearing the particular gravity of a director whose instruments are misbehaving. Behind him, half in shadow in the doorway, stood someone else — a young man, narrow-shouldered, holding his hat in both hands, who looked at Paloma with an expression she couldn't read at that distance and would spend a season trying to.

"Herr Pemberton?" the director called.

"Pemberton," Paloma agreed, coming up the path. "Paloma Pemberton. My master sends his regards and his knees' apologies."

Something crossed the director's face at the name — quick, unreadable, gone. Relief, or recognition, or the look of a man checking an item off a list drawn up by someone else. "Herr Falk," he said, extending a hand cold as the morning. "You are welcome, Fräulein Pemberton. More than you know. Come in out of the wind." He stepped aside, and behind him in the dim hall the young man shifted, and the door began to swing shut behind them all with a sound like a held breath being let out slowly.

Inside, somewhere deep in the stone heart of the building, a clock was not ticking. Paloma noticed it the way you notice a missing heartbeat — not as silence, but as absence, a room leaning slightly wrong. She had been summoned to mend a machine. She did not yet know that the machine was only the smallest of the things in this house that had stopped.
