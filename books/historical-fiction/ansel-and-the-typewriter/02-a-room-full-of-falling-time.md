## 2. A Room Full of Falling Time

The observatory announced itself before Paloma could see it. First came the smell — cold iron and pine resin and something faintly electrical, like the air after lightning — and then the sound: a low, rhythmic thrumming that rose through the soles of her boots as the carriage crunched to a stop on the gravel apron.

She had heard that sound once before, in Herr Brandt's workshop in the valley town, when a customer brought in a broken telegraph relay and asked if it could be made to sing again. It was the sound of machinery doing honest work, and it settled something in her chest that had been rattling since the telegram arrived three days ago.

*Master clock failing. Send Pemberton. Before winter.*

Not *send Brandt*. Not *send anyone*. *Send Pemberton* — as though she were a thing one could order up like coal or lamp oil. She was twenty-two years old and had been a journeyman clockmaker for exactly eleven months, and somewhere at the top of this mountain, someone with money and a telescope believed she was the answer to their prayers.

"Either they've confused me with my master," she said aloud to the driver, who was busy unloading her tool case as though it contained an anvil rather than forty pounds of brass and steel and hope, "or they're desperate."

"Mountain folk are always desperate," the driver said cheerfully. "It's the altitude." He heaved the case onto the steps, accepted his coin, and was gone down the road before Paloma could decide whether that had been a joke.

The building loomed above her — a long, low stone structure crowned by a dome the color of a storm cloud, its shuttered slit aimed at the sky like a patient eye waiting for something worth looking at. Smoke curled from two chimneys. A man in shirtsleeves stood at the door as though he'd been watching the road for some time, which, given the state of the road, probably meant he had nothing better to do than watch for disasters arriving by mule.

"You are the clockmaker." It was not a question. He was perhaps fifty, with a face like a folded map — all creases and no clear country — and eyes that performed a rapid inventory of her person and found several items missing. "I expected someone older."

"I expected a working clock," Paloma said, and stuck out her hand. "Paloma Pemberton. Journeyman of the Worshipful Guild, valley chapter, population currently me."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth that might, under better conditions, have become a smile. "Herr Falk. Director of this establishment, such as it is." He shook her hand with the careful grip of a man accustomed to breaking things he touched too firmly. "Come inside. You'll want to see it before you say anything clever."

"I already said something clever."

"Yes, and I'm choosing to ignore it. This way."

* * *

The clock room lay at the heart of the observatory, and everything else in the building arranged itself around it the way planets arrange themselves around a stubborn sun. Corridors radiated outward; doorways faced toward it; even the staircase seemed to lean in its direction, as though the whole architecture were leaning close to listen.

Herr Falk opened the door with a small ceremonial hesitation — the pause of a priest about to reveal an altar — and Paloma stepped past him into falling time.

She felt it before she saw it. Every clockmaker knows the hum of a room where time is being kept properly: the sub-audible agreement of pendulums, the tick-tock consensus of a dozen instruments all swearing to the same truth. This room had no such consensus. The master clock — a magnificent Regulator movement, walnut-cased, weight-driven, easily the finest machine she had ever been asked to touch — stood silent against the far wall, and around it hung a scatter of lesser clocks, each keeping its own private opinion of the hour.

The wall chronometer read 3:52. The ship's-style deck watch in its glass case read 4:03. The little alarm clock on the technician's bench, still gamely ringing at intervals nobody had bothered to silence, insisted it was perpetually five o'clock and getting later.

And the master clock, frozen mid-swing, held its hands at four-seventeen.

"Not stopped recently," Paloma murmured, already moving toward it, her fingers twitching with the particular hunger she felt in front of a broken mechanism. She crouched to look through the case glass at the pendulum, hanging dead-still in its wooden cradle of air. "When did it stop?"

"Eleven days ago."

"At four-seventeen in the afternoon?"

"At four-seventeen in the morning," Falk said. "We only discovered it at dawn. The night assistant noticed the sidereal times weren't agreeing with the transit observations." He paused. "He notices most things. It's his single virtue and his principal occupation."

"And since then?"

"Since then we have kept time by comparison — chronometers against stars against each other. Adequately. Astronomers are adaptable people; it's how we survive our funding." His voice was dry as old varnish. "But the master clock is the spine of this place. Without it, every observation we take is a rumor. The institute in the capital will not fund rumors. And winter—" He gestured vaguely at the window, at the mountains, at the entire concept of winter, which up here appeared to be less a season than a permanent condition with occasional interruptions.

Paloma barely heard him. Her case was open, her loupe was in her eye, and the world had contracted to the size of a clock movement.

Whoever built this Regulator had loved it. That was immediately, unmistakably clear. The plates were hand-frosted; the wheels had been crossed out to save weight without sacrificing strength; the escapement — she pressed her face nearly against the glass — the escapement was a deadbeat anchor of unusual refinement, its pallets jeweled, its impulse faces polished to a mirror finish. This was not a clock bought from a catalogue. This was a clock somebody had *made*, here or very near here, with the patience of a saint and the vanity of an artist.

And somebody had hurt it.

Or let it die. She wouldn't know which until she got the back panel off, and she couldn't get the back panel off until she stopped gawking, and she couldn't stop gawking because eleven months of journeyman work in a valley workshop had prepared her for cuckoo clocks, carriage clocks, and the occasional church tower, not for this.

"Take your time," Falk said, and there was, unexpectedly, kindness in it. "The room will wait. Rooms full of clocks always do — that's rather their nature."

"It stopped clean," she said. "No winding error, no power loss — the weights are still high. Something interfered with the escapement directly, or the train jammed." She straightened up, suddenly aware of how intently she had been ignoring the director of the entire observatory. "I'll need oil, benzine, my own tools, and I'll need everyone to stay out of this room until I understand what I'm looking at. Including you. Especially you."

"Especially me?"

"Directors touch things," Paloma said. "I can tell. There's a fingerprint in the dust on the lower case, and it isn't mine yet."

This time the smile definitely happened, briefly, like a fish surfacing. "You'll do," he said. "Dinner is at seven. Marta cooks better than the mountain deserves, and she takes insults to her food as declarations of war. Ivo — the night assistant I mentioned — will want to interrogate you about your qualifications. He means well the way a hailstorm means rain."

"And the rest of the staff?"

Falk's pause was small, but Paloma had spent eleven months learning to read the pauses of people who didn't want to answer questions, because her master answered almost none of them directly.

"The staff is smaller than it was in spring," he said. "Budgets. Wars elsewhere make donors nervous about telescopes." He moved to the door. "Rest first. The clock has waited eleven days; it can wait another hour while you eat something and remember you're a person."

But Paloma did not go to eat, or rest, or remember anything of the sort. She went to find the kitchen, because a clockmaker who doesn't learn where the food comes from never learns anything else about a house either.

* * *

Marta turned out to be a broad, gray-streaked woman with forearms like a blacksmith and an expression suggesting she had already catalogued every fault in the building and forgiven none of them. She fed Paloma bread and cheese and stewed plums without being asked, watched her eat with frank assessment, and answered questions in the manner of a woman paying taxes — accurately, minimally, and with visible resentment.

"How many people live here?"

"In winter? Fewer." Marta wiped a counter that did not need wiping. "The director. Ivo. Me. The assistant who tends the telescope drives — Ansel. Two others left when the funding thinned. There was a third, but he went down the mountain in October and hasn't come back, and good riddance, he ate like a woodcutter and worked like a poet."

"Ansel," Paloma repeated. "That's not a local name."

"No."

"Where's he from?"

"Far enough away that you'd need a map and a war to find it," Marta said, and then looked briefly horrified at herself, as though the second half of that sentence had escaped without permission. She attacked the counter with renewed violence. "He winds the drives and keeps to himself and pays for his bread in work, same as anyone. Ask him yourself if you're curious. Curiosity is free. Everything else on this mountain costs double."

Paloma decided she liked Marta enormously and told her so, which earned her a second helping of plums and a look of deep suspicion, both of which felt earned.

* * *

The director's study sat behind the clock room, connected to it — she noted with professional interest — by a short corridor, so that whoever ran the observatory could step from his ledgers to the heartbeat of the building in twenty paces.

She hadn't meant to snoop. She had come to ask Falk a technical question about the clock's history — who had built it, whether original drawings existed — and found the study empty, the desk lamp burning, and the door standing open like an invitation nobody had remembered to rescind.

On the desk, among the inevitable drift of correspondence and star charts, sat a typewriter.

It was an ugly, heroic old thing, its black enamel chipped to bare metal along the edges, its typebars fanned open like the ribs of some fossilized animal. A sheet of paper protruded from the platen, half-typed. Paloma would have walked past it — she had no business with other people's letters — except that her eye, trained by years of reading machines the way other people read faces, caught the detail that stopped her.

The ribbon was warm.

Not metaphorically. She knew ribbons the way she knew mainsprings, and she knew what a freshly used ribbon smelled like — ink and solvent and warmth off the type slugs — and this one carried all three, faintly but unmistakably, like a guest who has just left the room. She glanced at the paper. It was a letter, begun in a neat, upright hand:

*Messrs. Haller & Sons, Instrument Makers —*

Business, then. An order form, probably. Perfectly innocent. She stepped back from the desk and told herself, firmly, that warm ribbons proved nothing except that somebody in this house wrote letters at odd hours, which described every astronomer who ever lived.

And yet.

The house was wrong. She had been feeling it all evening the way you feel a clock running slightly fast — nothing you can point to, just a persistent sense that the mechanism and the dial don't quite agree. A director who watched the road for hours before she arrived. A cook who spoke of wars in the plural and flinched at her own words. A staff pared down to skeletons, a bridge that closed early, a summons sent down the mountain for a journeywoman instead of her master — and now a letter half-written at seven in the evening by someone who had apparently left the room so quickly the lamp was still burning.

Everyone here was keeping time. They were simply not telling each other what hour it was.

"Miss Pemberton."

She turned. Falk stood in the doorway, and behind his shoulder — half in shadow, deliberately or not — stood a young man she hadn't seen before. Tall, dark-haired, with the careful stillness of someone who had learned that sudden movements cost more than they bought. He wasn't looking at her. He was looking at the typewriter, and his expression, before he smoothed it, was the expression of a man looking at a grave.

"My question," Paloma said, steadying her voice, "was about the clock's builder. Original drawings. Service records. Anything."

"There are drawings," Falk said slowly. "In the archive. I'll have them brought to your bench tomorrow."

"I'd rather fetch them myself."

A silence, brief and load-bearing. "Tomorrow," Falk repeated, in a tone that closed doors.

The young man in the doorway shifted his weight, and for a moment his eyes came up and met hers — dark, quick, and carrying something she couldn't name, something between recognition and apology, as though he had been expecting her for a long time and dreading it in equal measure.

Then he murmured something in accented German about the telescope drives needing winding, and was gone down the corridor, and Paloma stood alone in the study with the warm ribbon cooling on its spool and the thought settling over her like snowfall, soft and certain and cold:

Whatever was broken on this mountain, it wasn't only the clock.
