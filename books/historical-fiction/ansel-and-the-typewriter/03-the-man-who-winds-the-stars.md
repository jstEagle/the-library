## 3. The Man Who Winds the Stars

There was a right way and a wrong way to wind a telescope drive, and Ansel Vogel had learned the wrong way first.

His father had taught him — or rather, his father had taught him badly, with great confidence, on a homemade orrery built from bicycle parts and brass curtain rings — and it had taken Herr Falk's patient predecessor, old Professor Lindt, an entire summer to unteach him. *You do not force the spring,* Lindt had said, in the slow, careful German he used for foreigners and children and anyone who wound things too eagerly. *You persuade it. A spring is not a servant, Vogel. It is a colleague.*

So every morning at six, before the sun cleared the eastern ridge and set the whole dome glowing like the inside of a pearl, Ansel climbed the iron stair to the drive chamber and persuaded the spring.

He had been at the observatory eleven days. Long enough to learn that Marta left the coffee pot on the low burner because Falk liked it scalded nearly bitter; long enough to know that Ivo Brandt said *good morning* to him in a tone that suggested good mornings were being issued against Ivo's better judgment; long enough to have memorized the exact number of steps from his narrow room under the east eaves to the clock room — sixty-two, if you skipped the loose one, which everyone did, including, apparently, whoever had built the staircase.

Long enough, also, to hear that a clockmaker was coming up the mountain.

He heard it the way one hears most things at the observatory: sideways, through doors, while carrying something. He had been hauling a crate of lantern oil across the yard when Ivo's voice came through the open window of the director's study, crisp with the particular satisfaction of a man delivering bad news about someone else.

"—the master clock is worse than you let yourself believe, Herr Falk. And Pemberton can't come. So they're sending the apprentice."

A pause. Then Falk's voice, lower, unreadable: "The daughter?"

"The daughter." Ivo's tone made *daughter* sound like a diagnosis.

Ansel stood in the yard with forty pounds of lantern oil in his arms and did not move for what felt like a full minute. Somewhere behind him a raven argued with the wind. The word went through him the way cold water goes through a coat — not all at once, but everywhere at once.

*Pemberton.*

He had known, of course, in the abstract way one knows anything written in a letter nine years old. The promise had come signed *E. Pemberton*. Letters do their work at a distance; they are made of ink, not faces. He had never imagined the daughter as a person with hands and a voice and a surname that would arrive ahead of her like a scout.

He set down the crate, straightened his back, and went to wind the stars.

* * *

The drive chamber smelled of oil and cold iron and, faintly, of the pine boards beneath everything, the way the whole observatory smelled of pine beneath everything — the mountain's quiet insistence on being noticed even indoors. The great driving weight hung in its shaft like a held breath. Ansel fitted the crank, checked the pawl, and began to turn.

*You persuade it.*

The spring took up its tension degree by degree, and the gears above him — invisible, sealed behind the telescope pier — began their slow daily consumption of stored time. That was the honest way to think of it: winding was not giving the telescope power. It was giving it *yesterday*, carefully rationed, so that tonight it could follow a star across the sky without trembling. Everything at the observatory ran on the master clock below — the sidereal drives, the chronograph, the shutters, the very schedule of the astronomers' sleep. When the master clock kept time, the mountain kept time. When it didn't—

Well. When it didn't, they sent for a clockmaker, and a clockmaker came, and the world rearranged itself around the arrival of one small woman with a tool case.

He heard her before he saw her. Not footsteps — though there were those, quick and light on the gravel path — but a kind of *audibility*, a presence that arrived slightly before itself. Then she came through the door of the drive chamber without knocking, because clockmakers, he would learn, regard doors as suggestions, and stood looking around the room with the frank inventory-taking gaze of someone assessing how much work a building is secretly doing wrong.

She was small. That was his first observation and it embarrassed him immediately, so he discarded it. She wore a man's canvas jacket two sizes too large with the sleeves rolled in careful cuffs, and she carried a leather case that she set down with the tenderness other people reserved for sleeping children. Her hair was dark and pinned back with what appeared, on closer inspection, to be an actual mechanical pencil. Her eyes moved over the drive mechanism the way a doctor's hands move over a patient — impersonal, thorough, already apologizing in advance for what they were going to find.

"You're the apprentice," she said, without turning around. "Ivo told me there'd be one. He described you as 'the foreigner,' which I found both economical and useless."

Ansel's hand stopped on the crank. "Ansel Vogel," he said. His voice came out steady, which surprised him; inside, something was performing acrobatics. "Assistant observer. I wind the drives. And the clocks, when—" He stopped himself.

"When they let you," she finished. She turned and looked at him directly, and he had the sudden vertiginous feeling of being read like a dial. "Paloma Pemberton."

"I know," he said.

Too fast. Far too fast, and too simply, with none of the polite bafflement a stranger ought to produce at being introduced. He watched the small flicker of it land in her face — not suspicion yet, just the faintest snag, like thread catching on a rough edge — and then she let it go, because she had a clock to look at and mysteries are a luxury for people whose escapements aren't failing.

"The master clock," she said. "Show me."

"It stopped four days ago at seventeen minutes past four," Ansel said. "It hasn't moved since."

"Stopped clocks rarely do," said Paloma Pemberton, and picked up her case, and he followed her down the stairs with his heart going like a runaway train.

* * *

That evening he could not sleep, which was not unusual; what was unusual was the shape of the thing keeping him awake. Usually it was the war-shaped absence at the center of every newspaper he no longer dared to ask Marta to save for him. Tonight it was a letter.

Nine years ago, when Ansel was fourteen and his father's watch shop still had a sign with gold letters and a future, a letter had come to their house in Vellmar. His father had brought it inside still wearing his apron, turning it over twice before opening it, because letters from abroad were rare in their street and usually meant either money or trouble, and his father had long ago stopped expecting money.

It was typed. That alone made it remarkable — nobody they knew owned such a machine; letters came handwritten or not at all. The paper was thin and the typing had that particular character of a machine working hard, each letter pressed deep enough to be read from the back, except where the ribbon had frayed and certain words arrived ghosted, half-present, like people seen through frosted glass.

His father had read it standing by the window. Ansel remembered that exactly: the window, the grey afternoon, his father's thumb moving along the lines. And he remembered his father sitting down afterward on the good chair — the chair reserved for Sundays and funerals — and reading it aloud, slowly, because his mother was alive then and wanted to hear it twice.

*Dear Herr Vogel,*

*You will forgive me for writing in your language instead of mine, and forgive the machine, which has opinions of its own. I met your cousin Josef at the trade congress in Basel, and he spoke of your difficulties with the same honesty he brings to everything, which I suspect has cost him customers and gained him friends. I am writing about the matter he mentioned — the trouble your family may find itself in, if the laws continue the way they are trending. I will not pretend to understand politics. I understand mechanisms, and it seems to me that a country, like a clock, gives fair warning before it fails. You can hear it in the strike train. Something goes uneven.*

*I cannot move mountains. But I keep a house on one, and houses on mountains are far from the places where laws trend. If it comes to the worst — if your family needs a door that opens — my door opens. There is work on a mountain for hands like yours, always: things need winding, mending, watching. Write to me first, so I can prepare the valley's heart. Valleys are like clocks too. They must be warmed gradually or they crack.*

*Consider this letter a key. I hope it rusts unused.*

*Yours in good faith and good time,*
*E. Pemberton*

His father had kept it in the cash drawer, beneath the tray where the coins lived, which was where he kept everything he believed in more than money. Over the years Ansel had watched him take it out and read it on certain evenings — after the new restrictions were posted, after the neighbor's shop was sold out from under him, after the night men came for the Kessler family two streets over and their windows stood dark ever after. Each time he read it, his father's face did something complicated: relief and shame fighting to a draw.

"We won't use it," his father said, more than once, folding it away again. "We won't be a burden to a stranger's kindness. We'll manage here."

Then his mother died, gently, of the ordinary kind of sorrow that kills no one dramatically and some people quietly. Then the sign came off the shop — not torn down; *unscrewed*, which was somehow worse, unscrewing implying paperwork, procedure, permanence. Then his father sat Ansel down at the kitchen table with the letter between them like a fourth person, and said, "It's time to write to the mountain."

And so Ansel had come — first the letter, then the long train journey, then the climb — carrying his father's half of the promise: the reply his father never had the courage to finish, finished now in Ansel's own hand, folded in the lining of his coat where it lay against his ribs like a second heartbeat. *Safe passage and shelter.* He had not needed to claim it yet. The observatory had hired him honestly enough; his references were genuine; his German was good. He had told himself he would wait, and watch, and find the right moment — and meanwhile the war clouds gathered over the newspapers like weather no one could admit to forecasting.

But now the clockmaker had come, and the clockmaker was a *Pemberton*, and the daughter of E. Pemberton was walking the corridors of the only house in the world where his family had ever been promised a door.

Hope, he thought, lying in his narrow bed listening to the wind worry the eaves, is a terrifying mechanism. Terror at least behaves predictably — it makes you run or freeze, and either way you know where you stand. Hope does neither. Hope makes you stay. Hope stands next to a stranger in a drive chamber and says *I know* three seconds too quickly and spends the rest of the day wondering whether she noticed.

She had noticed. He was almost sure she'd noticed.

* * *

The following morning he found her in the clock room at half past five, already at work, sleeves rolled, spectacles pushed up into her hair, surrounded by the dismantled innards of the master clock laid out on white cloth like a surgical theater. The clock towered over her, its pendulum hanging dead and reproachful, its face showing 4:17 to an empty room for the fifth day running.

"Good morning," Ansel said, and then, because silence invited questions and questions were dangerous, added quickly: "Coffee is on the low burner. Marta makes it strong enough to dissolve a spoon. This is considered a virtue here."

"Everything strong is considered a virtue on mountains," Paloma said, not looking up. "Pass me that loupe, would you — the one by the oiler. No, my left. Your other left."

He handed it over. Their fingers did not touch, which he noted with relief, and then noted that he had noted it, which was worse.

For a while there were only the sounds of work: the tick of a part set down, the breath of the oiler, her occasional muttering in the private language of clockmakers, which seemed to consist mostly of endearments addressed to screws and imprecations addressed to whoever had last serviced the movement. ("Whoever you were," she informed the dead clock, "you oiled this pallet like you were basting a goose.")

Ansel took up his cloth and began polishing the clock case, which was technically his job and conveniently located him six feet from her.

"You've worked here long?" she asked, conversationally, dangerously.

"Eleven days."

"And before?"

"Vellmar. My father repaired watches. Thirty-one years on the same street." He kept his eyes on the polish. "He taught me the wrong way to wind a spring, and a Swiss professor here taught me the right one. Between them, I'm passable."

"Hm," said Paloma, in the tone of a woman filing information rather than discarding it. She lifted the escapement into the lamplight and turned it slowly, and Ansel saw her expression change — saw the professional pleasure drain out of it and leave something cooler underneath.

"What is it?" he asked.

"Wear," she said slowly. "Wear where wear shouldn't be." She was quiet a moment, and then she looked at him — that direct, dial-reading look — and asked a question that had nothing whatever to do with escapements, delivered in the lightest possible voice:

"Tell me, Herr Vogel. When you came up this mountain — what exactly were you hoping to find?"

And Ansel Vogel, who had rehearsed a hundred answers to a hundred questions on the long climb, discovered that his entire stock of prepared sentences had deserted him at once, and that the letter against his ribs had gone suddenly, loudly warm, and that outside the window the snow was beginning — early, weeks early, fat and silent and wrong — to fall.
