## 4. The Bridge Goes Quiet

The storm came down the valley like a debt collector.

Paloma heard it first as a change in the sound of the observatory — a low, pressing hush against the windows that made the panes sing faintly at their edges — and then, an hour later, as a silence so complete she could hear the pendulum of the dismantled master clock ticking on her workbench, which she had never once been able to hear before. The mountain had swallowed every other noise whole.

She was three days into the escapement and had learned two things: the clock's failure was stranger than Herr Falk had let on, and nobody at the observatory answered a direct question if they could possibly answer a different one.

"Is it always this quiet after snow?" she asked Marta, who had brought her coffee that morning along with a slice of plum cake dense enough to serve as ballast.

Marta set down the tray and considered the window, where the world had been reduced to white on white with a seam of grey between them. "In summer," she said, "you can hear the river."

"And in winter?"

"In winter," said Marta, "you can hear yourself thinking." She gathered up the tray. "Some people find that worse."

She left before Paloma could ask what she meant by that, which by now Paloma recognized as the local dialect.

* * *

It was Ivo who came to fetch her at midday, breathless, his scarf wound around his face twice so that only his eyes showed — young eyes, bright with the particular excitement of someone about to deliver bad news to a captive audience.

"You'd better come to the hall," he said through the wool. "The director's called everyone. It's the bridge."

"The bridge?" Paloma set down her loupe. "What about it?"

"Gone," said Ivo, with something dangerously close to relish, and led the way.

The hall was the largest room in the observatory proper, a high-ceilinged space dominated by a long table scarred with decades of ink rings and knife marks, and above it hung a framed photograph of some long-dead astronomer looking profoundly disappointed. The staff had assembled: Marta at the edge of things with her arms folded; two junior observers whose names Paloma hadn't yet fixed, standing shoulder to shoulder like fence posts; Herr Falk at the head of the table with a telegram flimsy crushed in one fist as though he intended to eat it.

And Ansel Vogel stood near the window, apart from the others in the way of a man who has learned exactly where the edges of rooms are.

He glanced at Paloma when she entered — just a glance, quickly away, but there was something in it that snagged on her attention and would not come loose. He looked, she thought, like a man listening for a second shoe.

Herr Falk waited until the door closed behind her. He was not a tall man, but he had the gift of standing as though height were a matter of conviction, and he used it now.

"I will be brief," he said, "because there is nothing to elaborate on. At approximately four o'clock this morning, a slide came down from the western shoulder. The road is buried. The bridge over the gorge — the lower span — took debris and has shifted on its abutment. The engineer's assessment from last year was that the lower span could not survive another winter of heavy use regardless."

He paused. Someone's chair creaked. Outside, the wind found a corner of the building and worried at it patiently, like a dog with an old bone.

"The telegraph line went with the slide. It will not be repaired until spring. The pass road is already closed above the tree line — you all knew that when you signed your winter contracts. What this means—" Falk's voice flattened out, deliberately, the way a man flattens dough he does not wish to look at too closely, "—is that no one comes up and no one goes down until the melt. Six months, perhaps seven. We are provisioned for eight. We will manage. We always have."

Nobody spoke. In the photograph overhead, the disappointed astronomer seemed to grow more disappointed still.

Paloma felt it then — not the news itself, which she might have taken with professional calm, having known since childhood that mountains close like books — but something underneath it. A pressure. She looked around the room and saw it reflected back at her in small ways: Marta's jaw set tight; the two junior observers exchanging a glance that lasted half a second too long; Ivo standing very straight, very pleased, very frightened.

And Falk. Falk was watching Ansel Vogel.

Not glancing — watching, openly, without shame or art, the way a man watches a crack in a dam. And when he spoke again, his words were addressed to the room entire, but his eyes did not move from the assistant astronomer by the window.

"It means," said Falk, "that whatever we are to one another, we shall be it for a long time. I expect everyone to conduct themselves accordingly."

Ansel held his gaze. He did not drop his eyes and he did not stiffen, and somehow that was more unsettling than either would have been. He simply stood there, hands loosely at his sides, a man being weighed on scales whose workings everyone could see.

"Yes, Director," said Ivo, before anyone else could, and the words fell into the room like a coin into a well.

"That will be all," said Falk. "Miss Pemberton — a word, if you please."

* * *

Falk's study smelled of pipe smoke and lamp oil and, beneath both, of paper — reams of it, stacked in drifts on every surface, observation logs and funding correspondence and almanacs, the sediment of thirty years. The battered typewriter sat on its desk under a leather cloth, and Paloma noticed — because she noticed everything mechanical, it was the one sense she trusted — that the cloth lay slightly askew, as though recently disturbed.

"You wanted a word," she said.

Falk closed the door. For a moment he looked older than he had in the hall, the conviction drained out of his posture and leaving behind a tired man in a cold house.

"How goes the clock?"

"Badly," said Paloma honestly. "Or rather — interestingly, which is worse. The wear patterns don't match any natural failure I know. I'll need more time."

"Time," Falk repeated. He turned to the window. The glass was furred white at the corners; through the clear center, the valley below had vanished entirely. Where the bridge had been there was now simply weather. "You understand our situation, Miss Pemberton. Without the master clock, the timing observations are worthless. Without the timing observations, we cannot justify our budget to the institute. Without the budget—" he gestured vaguely at the paper drifts, the whole fragile edifice of the place, "—well. Mountains are patient. Institutions less so."

"I understand."

"Do you?" He turned back to her, and there was something searching in it that she liked considerably less than the tiredness. "I asked for you by name, you know. Not your master. You."

"My master's hip," said Paloma. "The climb. He said so himself."

"He did say so himself," agreed Falk. "Yes." He studied her a moment longer, and whatever he was looking for, he apparently failed to find it, because he sighed and became merely a director again. "Six months is a long time, Miss Pemberton. Houses change in six months. People do too. Keep your work honest and your ears open, and we shall get along."

"Sir," said Paloma, "is there something I ought to be listening for?"

The pause was short, but it existed.

"No," said Falk. "Nothing specific. Winter makes men strange. That's all." He opened the door for her, and the warmth of his manner returned like a coat shrugged on. "Plum cake at four, if Marta hasn't eaten it all herself. Go on."

She went. Behind her, she heard him cross to the desk, and heard the soft clatter of the typewriter's keys begin — a few letters, then stopping, then a few more. Writing, perhaps. Or unwriting.

* * *

The clock room had become hers by right of occupation, and she retreated to it gladly, because the alternative was to keep replaying the image of Falk watching Ansel Vogel across the long table, and because work was the only prayer she had ever reliably managed.

The master clock lay open before her like a patient on a table. Its great brass wheels caught the lamplight. The escapement — the little heart of the thing, tick and tock, release and catch — sat in her padded jaws, and even now, three days in, it kept offering her its puzzle: metal worn where metal shouldn't wear, clean where it should be worn, as though time itself had passed through the mechanism crookedly.

She was deep enough in thought that she didn't hear the door at first. Then the cold came in ahead of the visitor, and she looked up to find Ansel Vogel standing in the entrance with a covered pail in both hands, wearing the expression of a man delivering a ransom.

"Marta sent soup," he said.

"Did she send it for the clockmaker," said Paloma, "or for whoever happened to be nearest?"

"For you specifically." He set it down on the bench beside her tools with exaggerated care, as though it might detonate. "She said — and I quote — 'the girl works too hard and eats like a bird, tell her if she starves the clock won't care.'"

"She sounds like my mother."

Something crossed his face then, quick as a shutter closing, and Paloma felt the snag again — the second shoe, hovering. But he only said, "Then she sounds like a wise woman," and moved to go.

"Mr. Vogel."

He stopped. He did not turn around immediately, and when he did, his face had been arranged into polite blankness, which on a man his age looked almost obscene, like frost on a June rose.

"In the hall today," said Paloma. "When the Director gave the news. Everyone looked worried. You looked—"

"Worried," said Ansel.

"Relieved," said Paloma.

The silence between them filled up with the sound of the wind and, faintly, the ticking of the stripped-down pendulum on its test stand — the only honest voice in the building.

"I was relieved," Ansel said at last, carefully, "because I had feared the slide would take the tower. The east tower. Where I sleep." He offered a small shrug. "A man may be relieved his bed survived."

"A man may," Paloma agreed. "Though most men, hearing they're shut in for six months, look like they've been handed a sentence. You looked like a man whose train finally arrived."

For just a moment — a moment she would think about later, much later, in circumstances neither of them could yet imagine — his careful blankness cracked, and underneath it she saw something raw and hopeful and terrified, all three tangled together like wire in a knot.

"Miss Pemberton," he said quietly, "may I ask you a strange question?"

"You may ask. Whether I answer depends on the question."

His mouth twitched — the ghost of humor, quickly leashed. "Your mother. Is she living?"

The question landed strangely, off-key, like a note played in the wrong octave. "No," said Paloma. "She died four years ago. Why do you ask?"

And here was the thing she would remember: he looked at the master clock, open and broken between them, for a long three seconds, as though considering whether to confess to something — and then Falk's footsteps sounded in the corridor outside, unhurried and deliberate, and whatever lived in Ansel Vogel's face packed itself away with remarkable speed.

"Because you have her hands," he said. "That's all. Clockmakers' hands run in families, I'm told. Good evening, Miss Pemberton. Enjoy the soup."

He was gone before Falk reached the door.

* * *

Falk looked in, confirmed she was working, said something about the institute and patience that she absorbed without hearing, and left. The soup was excellent — venison, root vegetables, a crust of bread balanced on top like a hat — and Paloma ate it methodically while the pendulum ticked and the storm leaned on the walls.

Six months, she thought. Seven months, maybe.

She tried the idea on from different angles, the way she'd try a part against a plate, checking the fit. Six months of Marta's cooking and Falk's watchfulness and Ivo's bright frightened eyes and the young observers' fence-post silences. Six months of a house keeping secrets — and she was increasingly certain it kept several, because she had spent three days among these people and had not once seen anyone finish a sentence they meant.

Six months of Ansel Vogel, and his careful face, and his questions about her mother.

She wiped the bowl and set it aside and bent back over the escapement, and it was somewhere in the next hour, with her loupe down and her mind gone quiet and narrow as a file's edge, that the thought arrived — uninvited, absurd, arriving the way true thoughts do, sideways and fully formed:

He asked about Mother. Not about me. About *her*.

She sat back. The lamp hissed. Somewhere below, muffled by meters of new snow, the bridge that no longer existed creaked its absence into the dark, and the whole valley settled deeper into its white silence — and Paloma Pemberton, apprentice clockmaker, alone in a room full of stopped time on a mountain that had just closed its only door, understood with sudden certainty that she had not been summoned to fix a clock at all.

Her tool case sat against the wall. Her mother's effects were packed in the bottom of it, beneath the files and the oilstones — packed, she realized now, by whom? By her mother, years ago? By arrangement? Had the woman planned even this, even from beyond the reach of any clock?

Paloma knelt and drew the case out and began, with fingers that were not quite steady, to unpack it.
