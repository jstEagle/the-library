## 5. What Mother Typed

The storm that closed the bridge also, in its way, opened something else.

Paloma had been in the observatory four days when the snow came down like a curtain being drawn across the world, and on the fifth morning she woke to a silence so complete she could hear her own pulse ticking in her ears — a small, faithful escapement of blood and breath, keeping time whether anyone asked it to or not.

She lay in the narrow bed in the attic room they'd given her and listened to it. Then she got up, because lying still was for people who hadn't promised a clock they'd have it running by Sunday.

Her tool case sat where it always sat, under the window, where the light was honest. It had belonged to her mother before it belonged to her — a leather trunk of a thing, brass-cornered, with a tray of compartments fitted for files and pin-vice and the little jeweler's screwdrivers that her mother had called, without any explanation at all, *the choir*. Paloma had never asked why. Some of her mother's language you simply inherited, like the case itself, like the hands.

She knelt and lifted out the top tray to get at the oil bottles beneath, and that was when she saw it: an envelope, flat and cream-colored, wedged into the compartment where her mother used to keep spare mainsprings. It had no business being there. She knew every object in this case the way a priest knows his altar. There had never been an envelope.

Her name was on it. Not *Paloma Pemberton* — just *Paloma*, in her mother's careful hand, the letters upright and unhurried, as if they'd been set there by someone who believed that anything worth writing was worth writing properly.

Paloma did not open it immediately. She held it instead, feeling its weight — almost nothing, and yet not nothing; paper has weight the way promises do — and she felt the strange premonitory cold that comes when you understand, before understanding, that your life is about to be rearranged.

Then she broke the seal.

Inside was a single sheet, typed. The type was uneven, slightly faded toward the right margin where the ribbon had been tired, but the words were clean and certain:

---

*Observatory of the High Col,
the 9th of June, 1908*

*To Herr Emil Vogel, watchmaker, of Vranov —*

*Dear Sir,*

*You will forgive a stranger the liberty of this letter. I am not in the habit of making promises by machine, but I have thought long about how to make this one so that it cannot be mistaken for anything less than what it is.*

*You sheltered my husband for eleven days in the winter of '02, when he came through your valley with a broken axle and a fever, and neither knowing who he was nor asking, you fed him from your own table and mended what was broken and sent him home. You told him then: "What is given to a stranger is kept by the mountain." I have never forgotten it.*

*I make you now the same promise, in the same spirit, and I make it formally so that it may outlive me: should you or yours ever have need of shelter or safe passage — whatever the season, whatever the reason, whatever the politics of the hour — come to the observatory of the High Col, and you will be received. My daughter will keep this promise after me, if it comes to that, for it is hers to keep as much as mine.*

*This letter is witnessed and true. I have signed it and sealed it, and I have left a copy where it will be found by the one who must honor it.*

*With respect and gratitude,*

*Miriam Pemberton*

*Beneath the signature, in ink:* *Received and understood. E. Vogel, 14 June 1908.*

---

Paloma read it twice. Then a third time, standing in the grey morning light with her mother's tool case open at her feet like a mouth that had finally said the thing it had been holding in its cheek for years.

*My daughter will keep this promise after me.*

She sat down slowly on the edge of the bed, because her knees had opinions about the matter.

Five years ago. Her mother had died in the autumn of '09 — a heart that simply decided, between one tick and the next, that it was finished keeping time — and in all the years since, in all the grief and all the sorting of effects, in all the nights Paloma had spent wearing her mother's apron and learning her mother's trade in her mother's workshop until she could no longer tell which of them was the apprentice and which the master — in all of that, there had been no hint of this. No letter. No mention of a Herr Vogel. No mountain debt.

And now here she was. On a mountain. Summoned — she had assumed by chance, by availability, by the accident of her master's bad knee — to repair a clock at an observatory where a man named Vogel wound the telescope drives.

The ground did not shift all at once. That was the terrible part. It shifted the way a clock's hands move — invisibly, continuously, so that you only notice the change when you look up and find yourself somewhere else entirely.

* * *

She carried the letter down to the clock room, because it seemed wrong to leave it alone in the attic, and because — she admitted this only grudgingly to herself — she wanted to see Ansel Vogel's face while she asked him questions she didn't yet know how to form.

He was there, of course. He seemed to live in that room the way she did, drawn to the machinery of time the way some men were drawn to fire. He was winding the sidereal drive when she came in, the great brass gears turning overhead with their slow patient patience, and he turned when the door opened, and something crossed his face — quick, there and gone, like a bird past a window — when he saw what was in her hand.

"You've read it," he said. Not a question. His accent put the weight in unexpected places, made even plain words sound like they'd traveled a long way to get here.

"When were you going to tell me?" Paloma asked.

"I was waiting," he said. He set down the winding key. "For the right moment. And then the bridge closed, and there stopped being a right moment, and I began to think perhaps there never would be one."

"That isn't an answer."

"No." He folded his hands, and she noticed they trembled slightly, and that he was pressing them together to hide it, and that he knew she noticed. "It is difficult, Fräulein Pemberton, to say aloud: *I came here because of you.* It sounds — " he searched — "it sounds like a claim."

"It is a claim." Her voice surprised her with its sharpness. She looked down at the letter, at her mother's name signed at the bottom of it, five years old and binding as iron. "You let me walk into this house thinking I'd been summoned to fix a clock. You watched me work for four days and said nothing."

"What would you have had me say?" Something flared in him now, quiet but real. "*Excuse me, miss, my father carries half of a promise your dead mother made to my family, and I have crossed two borders and spent my last coin to arrive before winter, and now I find the daughter herself sent for, which is either providence or a trap — * when? On the road? In front of Herr Falk?"

"Anywhere," Paloma said. "Somewhere. You don't get to decide what I can bear to know. That is exactly what my mother did — " and she stopped, because the sentence had gone somewhere she hadn't aimed it.

Ansel was watching her with an expression she couldn't read. "Your mother," he said carefully, "was the kindest person I never met."

"She never mentioned you. Never mentioned your father. Not once." Paloma heard her own voice doing something brittle. "I found her letters after she died. Hundreds of them. Bills, orders, notes to suppliers, a whole correspondence about a disputed invoice for balance wheels that went on for two years — she kept *that*. But not this. Not you."

"Perhaps," Ansel said gently, "she kept it where it would be found by the one who needed it."

"I'm not the one who needed it. Your father was. And then he died too, I take it, since it's you standing here."

"In March. Two years ago." Ansel's voice went quieter. "He kept the letter in the shop safe, wrapped in oilcloth, the way you keep things you intend to hand to someone else someday. When the trouble started — the conscriptions, the talk — he took it out and showed me, and he said, *Ansel, if it comes to war, go north, go high, and find the mountain that owes us.* And I laughed at him. I said, Papa, it is a letter from a kind woman, not a deed to land." He shook his head slowly. "Then it became war, or nearly became it, and I stopped laughing."

"Nearly?"

"The last courier said the declarations were coming. Any day." He met her eyes. "When it comes, Fräulein — when it is official — I will be an enemy national sleeping under this roof. Everything your mother promised will become ten times harder to keep and a hundred times more dangerous. Which is why I should have told you on the first day, and why telling you now feels like handing you a stone when you expected bread."

Paloma looked down at the letter again. At the line she'd already read six times and would read six hundred more: *my daughter will keep this promise after me.*

Her mother. Who had taught her that a mechanism was only ever as good as its weakest spring. Who had drilled into her, over twenty years of benches and lamplight, that you never, *never*, left a fault in a train of wheels and hoped nobody would notice — because faults compound, and what begins as a whisper in the escapement ends as a stopped clock. Who had apparently written a promissory note in peacetime, sealed it in a toolbox, and let it lie there compounding interest for five years without saying a word.

"You knew her," Paloma said suddenly. "Your father knew her. How?"

"They corresponded. After the winter of '02. Watchmakers' matters — he supplied her with parts she couldn't source locally, she repaired a repeater watch for my mother that no one in our country could fix." A small, sad smile. "They wrote to each other for years. I remember the envelopes. I remember the stamps. I was a boy; I thought she was a business contact. I suppose in a way she was. Business of the other kind. The kind you can't invoice."

Outside the clock room's tall windows, the snow had begun again — fat, silent flakes falling straight down, the air so windless that each one seemed to descend on purpose, individually, like the sky delivering mail.

Paloma felt the full strangeness of it settle over her: that she had been summoned — *summoned*, by telegram, by a director she'd never met, over the head of her own master — to this exact place, where this exact letter had been waiting in her own tool case, placed there by a dead woman's hand at some moment Paloma could not now reconstruct and could not stop trying to.

"Herr Falk," she said. "Did he know? When he telegraphed for a clockmaker — did he know who he was asking for?"

Ansel's silence lasted three seconds too long.

"Ansel."

"He knows about the letter," Ansel said at last. "I told him when I arrived. I had to — I wanted his protection in advance, in case — " He gestured vaguely at himself, at the foreignness of him, at everything. "Whether he engineered your summons, I don't know. But he read your mother's signature, and he looked at me for a long moment, and he said: *then we shall need a Pemberton.*"

The clock room hummed around them — the drive turning, the pendulum of the master clock hanging dead at 4:17, the whole building leaning on its machinery the way a sick man leans on a cane.

Paloma folded the letter along its original creases, precisely, the way her mother would have, and put it inside her coat, against her chest, where she could feel it like a second heartbeat.

"My master thinks I'm up here fixing an escapement," she said. "He thinks I'll be home by Advent."

"And are you?"

She didn't answer. Through the window, far below, she could just see the bridge — or where the bridge would be, under eight feet of new snow, its iron spine buried like a fossil of the world that had existed last week. Cut off. Sealed in. Committed.

There was a noise in the corridor — footsteps, brisk, and then a knock that did not wait for permission before Herr Falk opened the door. He looked from Paloma to Ansel and back, and his face wore the expression of a man who has just heard news he was already dreading.

"Fräulein Pemberton," he said. "A word. The telegraph line is down — properly down, poles snapped in the pass — but Marta has just told me something about the last courier that you both need to hear."

Behind him, faint through the stone walls, came the sound of Marta's kitchen bell — rung, Paloma would learn later, at the wrong hour, in the wrong way, the way you ring a bell to call people together when the news is too heavy to carry door to door.

The war, it seemed, had arrived before the snow finished falling.
