## 6. Enemy Aliens

The courier came up the mountain on a Tuesday, which Ansel would remember later because Tuesdays were the days he cleaned the lenses of the small refractor in the east dome, and he was halfway through the second lens when Marta's voice carried up the stairwell — not shouting, because Marta never shouted, but pitched in that particular key she used when the bread had burned or the director had forgotten to eat again.

He set down the chamois cloth. He did not finish the lens. He would think about that, afterward: how he had left a job half done for the first time since arriving at the observatory in September, and how the unfinished lens had sat there all winter, gathering a fine grey film of dust, like a small blind eye.

The courier was a boy of perhaps sixteen, snow-crusted to the knees, standing in the entrance hall dripping onto Herr Falk's best rug. The telegraph line had gone down three weeks ago — a tree somewhere below the treeline, or ice on the wires, no one knew — and so the world now arrived the old way, on foot and out of breath. Ansel stood at the edge of the hall with his hands still smelling of lens polish and watched Falk break the seal on the envelope with a thumb that trembled, just slightly, just once.

Ansel read the news upside down. He was good at reading upside down; it is an occupational hazard of being a foreigner, of always standing slightly outside the circle of other people's attention, close enough to see the page but never handed it directly.

*War declared,* said the newspaper's headline, in letters tall enough to be legible from across a room. And beneath it, in smaller type, the name of his country, printed the way one prints the name of a storm or a plague.

The hall went very quiet. Even the boy stopped dripping.

Ansel felt the words land in him the way a stone lands in still water — first the hard impact, then the rings spreading outward, touching everything. His father's face. His mother's hands. The village where he had been born, which was now on the wrong side of a line someone in a distant capital had drawn with a pen. The word *enemy*, which until that moment had been an abstraction, something from history lessons and newspaper editorials about other people, settled over him like a coat he hadn't asked to wear and could not take off.

"Vogel." Falk's voice was quiet. He had folded the paper already, quickly, as though putting away something dangerous. "A word."

* * *

They spoke in Falk's study, behind the closed door, while the staff drifted through the corridor outside pretending to have errands. Ansel stood before the desk. On its corner sat the typewriter — his mother's letter lived in the drawer beneath it, he was nearly sure, though he had never found the courage to look — and its keys glinted dully in the lamplight like a row of small metal teeth.

"You understand what this means," Falk said. It was not quite a question.

"I am now a citizen of a country your country is fighting," Ansel said. His voice came out steadier than he felt. "Yes, Director."

Falk looked at him for a long moment. He was not an unkind man — Ansel had learned this slowly, the way one learns the habits of a difficult instrument — but he was a man who had spent thirty years protecting an observatory perched on a mountain like a lantern on a pole, and protection was the only muscle he had ever been required to exercise.

"The valley will know by tomorrow," Falk said. "The courier will go down. There will be talk. There may be worse than talk." He pressed his fingertips together. "Here is what will happen in this house. Nothing. No one speaks of the war. No one speaks of your — situation. You are the assistant who winds the telescope drives, and you will continue to be the assistant who winds the telescope drives, and if I hear so much as a whisper of politics in my mess hall, I will put whoever whispered it out into the snow to reconsider their conversational habits. Do you understand?"

"Yes, Director."

"It is not kindness," Falk said, and there was something raw under the words, something almost like apology. "Or rather — it is kindness wearing the uniform of self-interest. This observatory runs on funding from men in cities who read newspapers. If it becomes known that we sheltered an enemy national through the winter, there are people who will call it treason, and people who will call it stupidity, and either way the money stops, and either way the mountain goes dark. So. We say nothing, and we hope the winter says nothing either. The snow is good at keeping secrets."

"Yes, Director," Ansel said again, because it seemed to be the only sentence the language offered him.

Falk dismissed him with a nod, and then, as Ansel reached the door, added — quietly, without looking up from the paper he had unfolded again — "Your family. Are they far from the border?"

"Four hundred kilometers," Ansel said. "Perhaps five."

Falk nodded once, slowly, the way he nodded at figures in a ledger that balanced. "Then they are farther from it than you are from the bridge," he said, "and the bridge is under six meters of snow. Go wind your stars, Vogel."

* * *

That night Ansel lay awake in his narrow bed in the assistants' quarters and listened to the observatory breathe.

It breathed differently now. That was the thing nobody warned you about war — not the guns, not the headlines, but the way it changed the acoustics of ordinary rooms. The floorboard outside his door creaked at two in the morning, and he lay rigid, counting footsteps, wondering whether the creak meant anything. The kitchen sounds that drifted up through the floor — the clatter of pans that had always sounded like comfort — now sounded like a household going on without him, a warm life visible through glass.

He thought about his father's letter, which he carried against his chest in an oilcloth packet, worn soft as cloth from handling. Half a promise, his father called it. Your half, Papa had written, in his cramped schoolmaster's hand, is to be worth sheltering. Their half is written down, typed on the machine in the study, signed by a woman named Pemberton. Find her daughter, if you must find anyone.

He had come to the mountain to find Paloma Pemberton, and he had found her — or she had arrived, summoned by a broken clock, which was either providence or the most elaborate coincidence ever engineered by a nervous universe — and he had said nothing, day after day after day, because how did one begin such a conversation? *Good morning, Miss Pemberton, your dead mother promised my family your roof.* There was no polite grammar for it. Every opening he drafted in his head collapsed under its own weight.

And now the war had arrived, and every draft had grown heavier.

Because here was the arithmetic that kept him staring at the ceiling: before Tuesday, he had been a foreigner, which was inconvenient. After Tuesday, he was an enemy, which was dangerous. And Paloma Pemberton — whose mother had made a promise in peacetime, in ink, on paper — was now being asked to honor it in wartime, for a man she did not know, whose country her country was shooting at. If he told her, he might be handing her a choice that would cost her everything. If he didn't tell her, he was living under her roof on a lie of omission, and lies of omission had a way, in his experience, of ripening into betrayal.

Somewhere below, a door opened and closed softly. Footsteps on the stairs. They paused outside his door — he heard the pause, the particular silence of a person deciding whether to knock — and then moved on.

He slept eventually, badly, and dreamed of borders: long lines of them, drawn across snowfields, across dinner tables, across the space between two beds in a dark room.

* * *

The valley learned the news the way valleys learn everything — fast, and slightly wrong.

By Thursday, according to Ivo, who went down to the village for supplies and came back glowing with the importance of having information, the story in the valley was that the observatory was harboring a spy. Not an assistant. A spy. Someone had remembered that Vogel was a foreign name; someone else had improved upon the memory. By Friday the version Ansel heard secondhand involved wireless equipment hidden in the telescope dome, which at least had the virtue of flattering the telescope dome.

Ivo delivered this intelligence in the mess hall at supper, loudly, to no one in particular, in the tone of a man reporting the weather.

"They're saying in the valley—" and here Ivo lowered his voice in a way that guaranteed everyone listened— "that we've got ourselves a spy up here. Wireless sets. Codes. All of it."

"That's absurd," said the young astronomer Weiss, around a mouthful of dumpling.

"Is it?" said Ivo, and looked directly at Ansel, and smiled the smile of a man who has discovered that fear can be sharpened into a tool and is pleased with the edge. "I'm only saying what they're saying."

Herr Falk set down his fork. It was a small sound, the fork against the plate, but it cut the table in half.

"We do not discuss the war at this table," Falk said, in the voice he used for announcing comets. "We do not discuss nations. We do not discuss newspapers. We discuss stellar parallax, we discuss the weather, and we discuss whether Marta has made strudel, which she has, and which is getting cold. Is that understood by everyone at this table?"

Everyone understood. Ivo smiled again, smaller, and ate his strudel, and Ansel understood that nothing had been resolved, only postponed — that Ivo's silence would now have the quality of held breath, and held breath always comes out somewhere.

After supper, Ansel washed the dishes. It was not his job — it was no one's job formally, and therefore everyone's informally — but he had found early on that washing dishes was the fastest way to make a household stop regarding you as furniture. Marta cooked; he washed; between them they ran a small neutral country at the sink.

Marta was a broad, slow-moving woman of about fifty who spoke perhaps forty words a day and made each one count, like a miser spending coins. Tonight she said none of them. She cooked in silence, and her silence had a different texture than usual — not disapproval, but something tauter, watchful. When Ansel finished the last pot and dried his hands, she was standing by the pantry door looking at him with an expression he couldn't read.

"Good night, Frau Marta," he said.

She nodded. Then, as he turned to go, she said — in her own language, not his, though he understood it well enough, everyone in the mountains spoke all the border tongues the way stones speak water — "Eat more at supper. You've gone thin."

"I eat plenty," he said.

"Mm," said Marta, which in Marta's currency was a paragraph.

* * *

The next morning there was a loaf of bread outside his door.

Not a slice. A whole small loaf, dark and dense, the mountain kind that keeps for a week, wrapped in a clean cloth. It sat on the floorboards beside the hinge, positioned precisely so that no one walking past would see it unless they were the person opening the door.

Ansel stood in the doorway in his stocking feet and looked at it for a long time.

There was no note. Of course there was no note. A note would have been evidence. But the cloth was a kitchen cloth — he had dried pots with a dozen identical ones — and the loaf was Marta's baking, unmistakable, the crust scored with her four diagonal slashes the way no other baker in the province did it.

He picked it up. It was still warm.

He stood in the cold corridor holding a warm loaf of bread, and to his considerable embarrassment his eyes stung, and he had to stand there for another full minute pretending to examine a water stain on the wall before he trusted his face.

Because here is what the bread meant, and why it undid him: Marta had broken the rule. Falk's rule — *nothing changes, no one speaks* — and Marta had spoken, fluently, in the only language that could not be reported to anyone. She had walked past his door in the dark, on her way to light the stove at five, and left him a loaf, and said nothing, and would say nothing, forever. It was the most eloquent silence Ansel had ever received.

He ate a piece standing up, right there in the corridor, like a thief, like a boy, like a man who had forgotten since Tuesday what warmth tasted like when it was given instead of merely endured.

* * *

The days took on a strange doubled quality after that. Outwardly, nothing changed, and the nothing was performed with tremendous effort by everyone. Falk discussed parallax. Weiss discussed the weather with the fervor of a man reciting scripture. Ivo was scrupulously, aggressively polite, greeting Ansel each morning with a *Guten Morgen, Vogel* enunciated so perfectly it sounded like an accusation.

Inwardly, everything changed, and the everything was conducted in whispers and glances and loaves of bread.

Ansel threw himself into work. The telescope drives needed winding daily — great weights descending through the pier of the great dome, keeping the instruments turning in time with the sky — and he wound them with a devotion that was only partly professional. There was a comfort in it that he could not have explained to anyone: the mechanism asked nothing of him except strength and regularity. Turn the crank. Feel the resistance. Trust the gearing. In a world that had redrawn itself overnight around lines and loyalties, the drive clock remained exactly what it had been yesterday, indifferent to treaties, faithful to the stars.

Some evenings, Paloma Pemberton worked late in the clock room, and he passed her door on his way to the dome, and once — only once — she was coming out as he came along the corridor, and they stood facing each other in the lamplight, both slightly startled, like two animals meeting at a stream.

"How is the escapement?" he asked, because it was the only conversation they had ever had, and therefore the safest.

"Improving," she said. "Slowly. The original maker had ideas about friction that I can only describe as theological." She paused. Her eyes went briefly, involuntarily, to his face — searching it, he thought, for something — and whatever she saw or didn't see, she nodded and stepped past him toward the stairs.

"Miss Pemberton," he said, before he could stop himself.

She turned.

He had prepared, in that half-second, seventeen sentences. *My father knew your mother. There is a letter. Before the war, there was a promise.* All of them true. All of them suddenly radioactive, in a house where even the weather had become political.

"The bread in this valley," he said finally, "is very good."

Something flickered across her face — disappointment? relief? he genuinely could not tell — and she said, "Marta bakes it herself. Good night, Herr Vogel," and was gone down the stairs, and Ansel stood alone in the corridor feeling like a man who had spent his entire fortune on a stamp and then forgotten to write the letter.

* * *

On Sunday night, the loaf appeared again. And on Wednesday. Always at dawn, always beside the hinge, always in a clean cloth, and always — this detail he noticed only the third time, and it frightened him more than anything the valley's gossip could invent — always accompanied, some mornings, by a second, smaller item. Once, a twist of salt. Once, a few dried apples in a paper cone. Things that were not sustenance so much as care, and care, in a house under orders of silence, was contraband.

He began leaving things in return. He could not cook — his one attempt at bread in Marta's kitchen had produced an object Marta had examined silently for a full ten seconds before saying, "For the birds," which was generous, since the birds had refused it — but he could mend things. He fixed the wobbly leg of the kitchen stool. He sharpened every knife in the drawer while Marta pretended not to notice. He re-hung the pantry door that had sagged since autumn. Neither of them ever mentioned any of it. Their friendship, if that was the word, existed entirely in objects and omissions, like a code neither had agreed on and both understood perfectly.

But at night the fear grew anyway, patiently, the way frost grows on a window — beautiful, silent, and from the edges inward.

He lay awake doing sums he could not stop doing. How long until spring? Four months, perhaps five. How long until the bridge opened and the world climbed the mountain with its papers and its questions? The same four months. What would the world ask? What would the answers cost? Would Falk protect him, or fold him, the way he'd folded the newspaper — quickly, and put away somewhere safe?

And underneath all the sums, the one he never let himself finish: *when do I tell her?*

Because the letter in the oilcloth packet against his chest had grown heavier every week, the way unspoken things do, until it weighed more than the telescope. His father's half of the promise. Her mother's signature. A debt of shelter owed by a dead woman to a living stranger, now payable only in the currency of enormous risk.

And on a bright, bitter morning in the middle of December, winding the drive weights in the cold of the dome, breath steaming, fingers numb on the crank, Ansel looked down through the open clock-room door and saw Paloma Pemberton bent over the master clock's open case, a jeweler's loupe screwed into her eye, tweezers in hand, entirely absorbed — and he realized with a lurch that went through him like a dropped weight hitting the bottom of its channel:

She had taken the movement apart. She was holding a small brass pin up to the lamp, turning it, frowning at it.

And the pin, though he could not possibly have known it yet, was not brass at all.
