## 7. 4:17

The clock room at night was a different country.

By day it belonged to the astronomers — their boots on the iron stairs, their chalk squeaking on the slate board, their voices full of declinations and right ascensions and the cheerful arrogance of men who believed the universe kept appointments. But after midnight the observatory went quiet in layers: first the offices, then the dormitory corridor, then the great dome overhead where the telescope stood closed like a sleeping animal. Last of all came the clock room itself, which had never truly been quiet, because a stopped clock is not silent — it is a held breath — but by two o'clock in the morning even that seemed to soften, as though the room were letting Paloma work without commentary.

She had spread her tools across the long bench like a surgeon laying out instruments, and she was not ashamed to admit that this was the happiest she had been since the telegram arrived. There is a particular peace in a problem that does not lie to you. A clock that has stopped will tell you exactly what happened to it, if you know how to listen. People, she was learning, were another matter entirely.

The master clock stood against the north wall, taller than a man, a walnut case built by a firm in Geneva whose nameplate alone was worth more than everything Paloma owned. Inside, behind a glass door she'd cleaned herself that first afternoon, hung the great pendulum, motionless now for days, and above it the movement: brass wheels the size of dinner plates, steel arbors polished to mirrors, the whole mechanism gleaming with the fat, well-fed confidence of an instrument that had been maintained by someone who loved it. Someone before her had been good at this work. She felt that in her fingertips the way you feel a handshake.

And someone, or something, had killed it at 4:17.

She had been circling that number for two days the way a dog circles a bone. Clocks stop for ordinary reasons all the time. Oil thickens. Dust cakes. A weight runs down because some fool forgot to wind it. The observatory's own log told a mundane story: the clock had been found stopped three mornings ago by the night assistant, Ivo, who had written in his neat hand, *Master clock stopped, cause unknown, restarted at 6:02.* Restarted — and then stopped again within the hour, and left alone until Herr Falk sent for help down the mountain.

Left alone. That was the detail that had been worrying at her like a loose thread. A man of science finds his master clock dead twice in one morning and simply... walks away? Doesn't stand over it watching? Doesn't send for the director immediately? She had met Ivo only briefly, a young man with a jaw like a clenched fist and eyes that moved too quickly, and she had no reason to distrust him except that he had failed to be curious, and in her experience curiosity was the one thing astronomers never ran short of.

But that was a question for daylight and other people's consciences. Tonight was for the escapement.

She lit the second lamp, then a third, arranging them so the light fell into the heart of the movement without casting her own shadow across the works. Her master, old Herr Brandt, had taught her that trick her very first week: *The light belongs to the clock, girl, not to you. You are only visiting.* She had thought it was about humility. Later she understood it was also about seeing clearly. Your shadow will hide exactly what you most need to look at.

The escapement sat near the top of the train, that small, patient genius of a mechanism that lets a clock fall forward one tooth at a time instead of all at once — the part that converts falling weight into measured time. It was here that clocks confessed their sins. Wear showed here first. Abuse showed here always.

Paloma took off her spectacles, breathed on them, polished them on her sleeve, put them back on. Then she leaned in.

* * *

For the first hour she found nothing. That was, in its way, informative. The pallets were sound. The escape wheel's teeth were even, unworn, oiled with a light hand. The suspension spring was whole. Whatever had stopped this clock had not done it through honest decay, because there was almost no decay to be had; the movement was in better condition than half the town clocks she serviced back home, and better than her master's own regulator, which she would deny saying if ever asked.

So she worked downward, wheel by wheel, the way her mother had taught her to read a difficult letter — not hunting for the sentence that explained everything, but reading every line as though any of them might matter.

Her mother. Paloma pushed the thought away, and it came back, and she pushed it again. Since finding nothing yet in her mother's effects beyond spare clothes and a tin of peppermints, since arriving at this cold-eyed house where everyone watched everyone else over the tops of their soup bowls, her mother had been sitting in the back of Paloma's mind like a visitor who refused to announce her business. There was something wrong here. Not the clock — the clock was a puzzle, and puzzles were friendly. The house was wrong. The way Falk had looked at her when she arrived, as though checking her against a description. The way conversation stopped when she entered rooms, mid-word, like a cut thread.

*You're imagining things,* she told herself, and lifted out the count wheel to check the locking pins beneath it.

That was when she saw the pin.

Not the count wheel's pin — that one was fine, steel, correct. But below it, deep in the works where the going train met the strike train, there was a small pin that ought to have been there and wasn't. Or rather: there was a pin where a different pin ought to have been. Paloma stared at it for a long moment, her breath fogging faintly in the cold room, and felt the particular electric shiver that clockmakers get, the one that is half delight and half dread, because it means the clock has just told you a secret.

The pin was the wrong color.

Every pin in this movement was steel — bright where polished, gunmetal gray where aged, brown where a careless hand had let moisture touch it. This pin was pale. Dull. Soft-looking, if a thing can look soft, the way butter looks soft, the way a lie looks soft when you finally see it sitting among true statements.

She did not touch it. First rule of detection, whether in clocks or anything else: look long before you touch, touch gently before you move, and write everything down before you forget what looking taught you. She fetched her notebook and sketched the position, the angle, the neighboring components, noting the time — 3:41 in the morning — and then, and only then, she took up her smallest loupe and leaned in until her eye nearly brushed the brass.

Soft lead. She was nearly certain of it. And lead, in a clock train, was a confession all by itself, because lead is the worst possible metal for the job. It deforms under load. It wears fast. It shears. A steel pin would have carried that stress for fifty years; a lead pin would fail — could be made to fail — precisely when its murderer wanted it to.

*Timed,* she wrote in her notebook, and then crossed it out, because she hadn't proven timing yet, and a clockmaker who writes conclusions before evidence deserves every broken spring she gets.

* * *

Proving it took her until dawn, and it was the finest morning's work of her life, though she could tell no one, and that began, slowly, to curdle the joy of it.

Here was the shape of it, assembled piece by piece like a mechanism itself. The original pin had been worn — honestly worn, years of honest service, the kind of wear that announces itself gradually to anyone maintaining the clock. Someone had known that pin was near the end of its life. Perhaps everyone knew; perhaps it had been noted in maintenance records; she would check the ledger in the morning. And instead of replacing it with steel during the last service — or instead of leaving it to fail naturally, which would have happened eventually anyway — someone had replaced it with lead.

Lead would hold. Lead would carry the train for days, weeks, holding the clock running, keeping perfect time, innocent as anything. And then, at the moment the accumulated load and vibration found the soft metal's weakest grain, it would deform a fraction, slip a fraction, and the escapement would miss its lock, and the train would run past its catch, and the whole beautiful machine would fall forward into silence — at 4:17 in the morning, when the night assistant was supposedly asleep and the dome was dark and no one but the mountains saw.

She tested the theory with her fingers and then with fine pliers, applying pressure along the same axis the train applied it. The pin deformed. Not dramatically — a whisper of give, a few hundredths of a millimeter — but enough. Enough, repeated over hours, over the patient drumming of the escape wheel, to walk the mechanism out of truth.

Someone had wanted the clock to stop. Not broken — stopped. At a chosen hour. By a method invisible to casual inspection, deniable as wear, attributable to age. It was elegant. It was patient. It was, Paloma admitted with unwilling professional admiration, the best-made lie she had ever seen constructed out of hardware.

And it raised the question that made her set down her tools and sit very still on the cold bench while the sky outside the east window went from black to iron to the color of dishwater.

Who stops an observatory's clock?

An enemy, obviously — that was the answer the valley would leap to, the answer that needed no thinking at all. The foreigner. The man with the careful accent who wound the telescope drives with hands like a surgeon's. She had seen him twice now, once at a distance on the stairs and once across the mess table, and he had the kind of face that people would find trustworthy or suspicious depending entirely on what they had already decided, and she suspected they had decided. She'd heard the pauses when he entered rooms. Pauses travel faster than gossip in a small house.

But Paloma had spent four days inside this clock, and she knew its body better than anyone in this building, possibly better than anyone alive. And sabotage by a stranger — by a newcomer with no access, no tools, no knowledge of this particular movement's worn pin, of this particular grain of weakness — required opportunities she could not account for. Whereas the pin itself said otherwise. The replacement had been done *well*. Done by someone who understood escapements, who understood load paths, who understood that the crime should look like senility rather than murder. That was not the work of a passerby. That was the work of a craftsman. Of someone with keys to this room and time at this bench.

Of someone, in fact, rather like herself.

The thought arrived fully formed, unwelcome as a bill, and Paloma sat with it while the first gray light touched the frost on the window. She thought about Ivo and his failure to be curious. She thought about Herr Falk announcing the closed bridge and watching Ansel Vogel as he said it, the way a chess player watches the square he intends to attack. She thought about the typewriter in the study, its ribbon still warm on her first afternoon — warm, she had checked herself, pressing one fingertip to the metal type slugs like a fool, like a woman in a melodrama, and finding them holding the ghost of heat. Someone had typed something recently and urgently, and in this house, urgent typing and urgent secrets appeared to be the same occupation.

She could go to Falk now. The sun wasn't even properly up. She could knock on his door with her notebook full of sketches and say *the clock was murdered, and the murderer lives in this house*, and watch what happened next. It was what duty prescribed. It was probably what her mother would have —

Her mother again. Always her mother lately, standing in doorways she had no business standing in.

Paloma looked down at her notebook, at the sketch of the lead pin, and realized her hand was shaking slightly, and could not honestly say whether it was from the cold, the excitement, or fear.

Because here was the trouble with accusing anyone: the moment she spoke, she would no longer be the clockmaker. She would be a witness. Witnesses get managed. Witnesses get doubted, redirected, thanked politely and sent home over the bridge — if the bridge were open, which it was not, which meant she would not even have the dignity of being sent home, merely of being contained. And whoever had installed that pin had been clever enough to build a lie that would survive casual inspection. Would it survive her? Yes. But would *she* survive being the only person in the valley insisting the accident was a murder? She thought of the mess table, of Marta's fixed silence, of Ivo's quick eyes, of Falk's courteous, bottomless caution, and she thought: *not necessarily.*

There was also this, and she made herself look at it squarely: she did not yet know *why*. A stopped clock at 4:17 was a fact, but facts without motives are just noise with good posture. What happened at 4:17? What observation, what meeting, what darkness was worth killing the observatory's time for? Until she knew what the darkness was *for*, she couldn't know whom it served. Accusing the wrong person wouldn't just fail — it would hand the real saboteur exactly what they wanted, which appeared to be suspicion itself, suspicion aimed anywhere but backward at its source.

So she said nothing. She closed the glass door of the case, snuffed two of the lamps, and packed her tools with hands that steadied as they worked, because tools always steady the hands that use them; it was one of the reasons she loved them.

Then, because she was who she was, she did the repair.

The proper steel stock was in her case — she carried pins and wire in six gauges the way other women carried hairpins. She cut the new pin, turned it, fitted it, stoned the shoulders smooth, and seated it home with three taps of a brass-faced hammer so gentle each one sounded more like a suggestion than a blow. She cleaned the old oil from the pivots, oiled them with nine — she counted, because counting kept her calm — nine drops of the good Swiss oil she hoarded like a miser. She wound the barrel, eased the pendulum into motion with one finger, and stood back.

Tick.

The sound filled the room the way water fills a dry boat. Tick. Tick. The escapement catching, releasing, catching — that small patient heartbeat she had coaxed back into a hundred dying machines, except that this morning it sounded less like triumph than like testimony. The clock began, solemnly, to count away the minutes toward 4:17 of some future day, keeping time now for a house full of secrets, including hers.

* * *

She was washing the oil from her hands in the basin when the door opened behind her without a knock.

She turned, drying her fingers, wearing what she hoped was the blank pleasantness of a tradeswoman finishing a job. It was Ansel Vogel. He stood in the doorway with a can of lamp oil in one hand, clearly come on some errand, and he stopped when he saw her the way a man stops when he has walked into a room and forgotten why.

"The clock," he said. His English was precise, each word set down like a coin on a counter. "It is running."

"It is," Paloma said. "I finished the escapement this morning."

Something crossed his face — relief, she thought, or something wearing relief's coat — and then, underneath it, something else she could not name. He looked past her at the great pendulum swinging in its case, and his expression was so complicated, so layered with things he was not saying, that she felt suddenly certain he knew far more about this clock's death than any assistant astronomer reasonably should.

"You were up all night?" he asked.

"Clockmakers keep odd hours. Time doesn't wait for us to be rested."

"No," he said quietly. He was still looking at the pendulum. "It does not wait for anyone."

He wished her good morning — formally, a little stiffly, like a man reciting lines in a language he'd learned from books — and went on down the corridor, and Paloma stood in the clock room listening to the tick and to his footsteps fading down the iron stairs, and thought about how a man who had nothing to do with a stopped clock might reasonably smile at its repair, and how he hadn't smiled at all.

He had looked, she thought, like a man watching a trap close around himself, and pretending it was weather.

Outside, the sun came up hard and white on the snowfield, and somewhere below in the kitchen Marta was lighting the stove, and in the east wing Ivo was writing in a logbook, and in the director's study the typewriter sat waiting under its dust sheet like a witness nobody had sworn in.

4:17, Paloma thought. Somebody in this house had wanted the world dark at 4:17. And she was beginning to suspect that whoever it was had gotten exactly the darkness they ordered — and that she had just switched on a lamp in the middle of it.

She buttoned her tool case shut, and went down to breakfast, and said nothing.
