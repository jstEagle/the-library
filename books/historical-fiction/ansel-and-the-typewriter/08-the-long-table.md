## 8. The Long Table

The mess at the observatory was a long room built for a longer staff than it now held — twelve chairs at a table meant for twenty, and the six of us spread out along it like beads on a string that had lost its knot. The walls were stone the same gray as the mountain, hung with star charts gone yellow at the corners and a photograph of some director's predecessor standing beside a telescope so old it looked like it had been carved from a tree by patient monks.

I had been given the seat at Falk's right hand, which I understood to be an honor and also understood to be a wall: between me and Ansel Vogel sat the entire length of the director's attention.

"Fraulein Pemberton." Herr Falk unfolded his napkin as though it had insulted him. "You find our kitchen adequate?"

"It smells like a miracle," I said honestly. Marta had been cooking since noon, and the air was thick with roast pork and onions and something with caraway in it that made my stomach announce itself before I could stop it.

A flicker of something crossed Falk's face — pleasure, quickly filed away behind the mustache. "Marta cooks for fewer mouths than she was hired for," he said. "She compensates."

From the far end of the table, Marta herself made no sign that she had heard this. She set down a tureen of potatoes with the particular care of a woman who has decided that her opinions will be expressed exclusively through gravy, and withdrew to the doorway, where she stood with her arms folded like a sentry who had been posted there by fate itself.

The pork went around. The wine went around. And then Herr Falk, who I was beginning to understand did nothing without purpose, turned his brittle courtesy upon the man at the opposite end of the table.

"Herr Vogel. You have been with us — what is it now? Six weeks?"

"Seven, Herr Director." Ansel's voice was quiet and carried anyway, the way voices do in rooms built for listening to very distant things. He had the kind of accent you couldn't place immediately — vowels rounded a half-beat too long, consonants softened at their edges, as though he'd learned German from someone who'd learned it from someone else across a border nobody had bothered to mark on the map. "Seven weeks on Thursday."

"Seven weeks." Falk sawed at his pork. "And how do you find our mountain?"

"Tall," said Ansel.

There was a pause in which I nearly choked on my wine. At the far end of the table, Marta made a sound that might have been a cough or might have been the first laugh she had permitted herself since the bridge closed.

Falk's mustache twitched. "Indeed."

"The air is thinner than I expected. The stars are thicker. Both take getting used to." Ansel cut his meat with neat, economical movements, eyes on his plate. "I am getting used to them."

"And your work? Ivo tells me you keep good hours at the telescope."

At the name, the young astronomer two seats down from Ansel looked up from his plate with the expression of a man who has been waiting all evening for the conversation to arrive at him. Ivo was perhaps twenty-three, with the kind of earnest, wind-chapped face that mountains produce in abundance and cities not at all, and he had spent dinner so far eating in the quick, guilty way of someone who believes meals are time stolen from more important things.

"He keeps better hours than the clock does," Ivo said.

It was said lightly. It was meant lightly, or mostly lightly. But the words dropped into the middle of the table like a coin into still water, and I felt every person at that table flinch away from the ripple — everyone except Falk, who watched Ansel with steady, tired eyes, and Marta, who had gone very still in the doorway.

Because that was the thing about a stopped master clock in an observatory: it wasn't merely inconvenient. It was a wound. Every observation logged since the clock failed bore a correction, an asterisk, a small admission of doubt. Time was the one thing an observatory could not buy, borrow, or apologize for. Whoever let the clock die — or helped it — had let the whole mountain go blind in one eye.

"I'm sure Herr Vogel keeps whatever hours are asked of him," I said.

I hadn't planned to speak. I want to be clear about that. I had planned, insofar as I had planned anything beyond *eat the pork, survive the evening, get back to the escapement*, to be furniture. Furniture is safe. Furniture doesn't get asked questions. But something about the way Ivo said *keeps hours* — as though hours were a currency and we were all auditing Ansel's account — put my back up.

Ivo turned to me with the bright, unguarded confidence of youth confronting an unfamiliar adult. "Do you think so, Fraulein Clockmaker? Or does the profession make you generous to anyone who winds things?"

"The profession makes me precise," I said. "Which is different." I set down my fork, because I had learned from my master that a technical point delivered while holding a fork sounds like gossip, and delivered while setting the fork down sounds like scripture. "If you're asking whether Mr. Vogel's attendance at the telescope has any bearing on the clock's failure — it doesn't. A clock doesn't stop because a man looks through a telescope near it. That's like blaming the postman for the weather."

A few chairs down, one of the older assistants — Herr Brandl, who had spoken perhaps ten words in my presence, nine of them about soup — made a noise that was almost certainly approval.

"The clock didn't simply *stop*, though," Ivo said. He had leaned forward now, elbows on the table, and I saw that under the needling there was something harder and less playful. "Did it, Fraulein Pemberton? Escapements wear. Everyone knows that. But ours stopped at four-seventeen in the morning, dead, on a night when nothing else in the building failed. No storm. No power failure to the winding weights. Just—" he snapped his fingers, "—darkness."

"That is indeed what I intend to determine," I said carefully.

"And until you determine it," Ivo said, "every hour we log is suspect. Every transit, every plate. Two years of measurements might have to be thrown out if the error ran long. Do you know what that means for the observatory?" He wasn't looking at me anymore; he was looking down the length of the table at Ansel, and the lightness had gone out of his voice entirely. "It means Berlin stops sending money. It means we all go home. So yes — I ask who keeps what hours. Somebody should."

The silence afterward had texture to it. I could feel Falk not speaking the way you can feel weather coming through a window. Marta, in the doorway, had become a painting of a cook.

Ansel, to his credit, did not look up. He continued cutting his meat into smaller and smaller pieces, as though somewhere at the bottom of the plate there was an answer and he intended to excavate for it. But his hands were careful in a way that hands aren't when they're relaxed, and I thought: *he heard it too.*

"You're right about one thing, Herr Ivo," I said, because apparently the furniture had opinions tonight and would not sit down. "Every hour logged since the fourth is suspect. Which is precisely why I'll tell you what I told Herr Falk — the fault is mechanical, and mechanical faults leave fingerprints. Wear patterns. Tool marks. A clock is the most honest witness in any building; it cannot lie about what was done to it, only about when. When I've finished reading it, it will say exactly what happened and roughly who. Until then—" I picked up my fork again, "—the potatoes are getting cold, and Marta will haunt us all."

That got a breath of a laugh out of Herr Brandl, and even Falk's shoulders came down maybe half a centimeter. Conversation lurched back into motion — soup opinions, the price of coal, whether the snowline would hold till Christmas. Ordinary talk, reassembled hastily, like a dropped stack of plates.

But I had made an error and I knew it even as the laughter faded. In defending Ansel Vogel, I had done two things I could not undo. First, I had publicly promised the table a culprit — *it will say exactly what happened and roughly who* — which meant that when I found what I suspected I might find in that escapement, I would have an audience waiting for my verdict, and audiences are terrible things to owe. And second, I had drawn attention. Not to myself. To the space between myself and the man at the end of the table, where a clockmaker's loyalty had no business sitting.

*Why her,* I could practically hear Ivo thinking. *Why does the clockmaker leap for the foreigner?*

I kept my eyes on my plate and ate potatoes and told myself the feeling crawling up my neck was the warmth of the room.

* * *

Dinner broke up the way observatory dinners must — by the sky. Somewhere past nine, the clouds that had sat on the valley all day tore open like a letter, and Herr Brandl rose mid-sentence, checked the window, and said "clearing" with the reverence other men reserve for the names of saints. Chairs scraped. Falk excused himself with a nod to me that contained, folded inside it, three separate messages I would need hours to decode. Ivo left last and fastest, taking the stairs two at a time toward the dome.

Which left the long table, the ruins of the pork, and Marta.

She came out of the doorway to clear plates, moving with the efficiency of someone who considers tidiness a moral category. When she reached my place she paused, tureen in hand, and looked at me — really looked, for the first time all week.

"You defended him," she said. Her voice was low and rough, like flour sifting.

"I defended the clock," I said. "He happened to be standing next to it."

"Mm." She gathered my plate. For a moment she stood there, and I had the distinct impression of a woman weighing something on internal scales, deciding what a kitchen servant may safely say to a visiting tradeswoman in a house full of listening walls. What she finally said was: "In this valley, Fraulein, when the snow comes, everything gets quieter. And quiet things get louder." She nodded once, as if that were a complete sentence, which grammatically it was not but spiritually it entirely was, and carried the plates out.

I sat alone at the long table for a while, in the smell of cooling roast and lamp oil, and listened to the mountain. Wind at the shutters. The tick-tick-tick of the pendulum regulator in the corner — the little slave clock, faithful, keeping the master's stopped time like a widow keeping a dead husband's coat brushed.

*Everything gets quieter. And quiet things get louder.*

I thought about the lead pin waiting in my tool case. I thought about soft metal and hard tools and the specific minute of 4:17, and about how a saboteur — a real one — would have needed access, knowledge, and a reason, and how the staff of this observatory were the only people on earth with all three. Ivo with his quick fingers and quicker suspicions. Falk with his locked study and his warm typewriter ribbon. Even Marta, who moved through every room in this building at hours no one audited.

And Ansel, whose accent Ivo had needled, and whom I had leapt to defend for reasons I had not yet admitted to examining.

I blew out the lamps and climbed toward the clock room, and it was only halfway up the stairs, passing the dark mouth of the corridor that led to the east wing and the telescope floors, that I realized I was not alone in the building's silence. Someone was ahead of me in the dark — footsteps, unhurried, climbing the same stairs — and when I reached the landing and the moonlight came through the stairwell window, it fell on Ansel Vogel, standing at the door of the clock room as though he had been waiting for it to be opened.

Or as though he had something to say to the person who would open it.

"Fraulein Pemberton," he said quietly. "Before you work tonight — there is something I think you should know. About why you were sent for."
