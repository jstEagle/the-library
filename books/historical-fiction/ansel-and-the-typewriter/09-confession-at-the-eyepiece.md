## 9. Confession at the Eyepiece

The great refractor had a smell Ansel had come to think of as his own: cold iron and old lacquer and the faint mineral breath of the mountain itself, which seeped in through every seam of the dome no matter how carefully Herr Falk ordered them sealed. He wound the drive clock at dusk, as he did every dusk, turning the crank until the weight rose humming into its housing — a smaller cousin of the master clock downstairs, though nobody at this observatory spoke of the big one anymore except in the tense, lowered voices people used for the dead.

*Four seventeen,* they said. *Stopped at four seventeen.* As if a time could die.

Ansel cranked the last quarter-turn and let his hand rest on the warm brass. Below him, through three floors of timber flooring, he could hear the small percussion of Paloma Pemberton still working — tap, tap, pause, tap — like rain on a tin roof. She had been down there since morning. She ate her lunch standing up over the movement, Marta said, bread in one fist and a loupe screwed into one eye, looking for all the world like a woman arguing with God about tolerances.

He should not have been listening for her footsteps. He knew that. Knowing it had done nothing whatsoever to stop him.

He climbed down from the observing platform, checked that the eyepiece cover was seated properly against tomorrow's frost, and found her at the top of the stairs.

She was carrying a lamp and a coil of wire and an expression that suggested the wire was for someone's hands. "Your telescope," she said. "Does it keep time?"

"Better than I do."

"Then it's ahead of everyone in this building." She started past him toward the corridor, and something in Ansel — some animal instinct that had been pacing a cage in his chest for eleven days — finally put its shoulder to the bars.

"Fräulein Pemberton."

She stopped. She didn't turn all the way around, just enough to show him her profile, lamplit, tired, wary. The wariness was new. It hadn't been there the first two days, when she'd asked him to hold things and he'd held them and she'd said *good, you don't breathe on the parts, that's rarer than talent.* Somewhere between then and now she had learned to be careful of him, and Ansel understood exactly when: when the last courier came up the frozen road with newspapers under his coat, and the word *war* went through the observatory like current through a wire, touching everything, warming nothing.

"Your name," he said. "Pemberton. Your mother — she was English? Before she married?"

The pause stretched long enough for the dome above them to tick as it cooled.

"Why do you ask?"

Because I have carried your mother's name across two borders and a mountain range folded inside my coat like a second heart. Because I have rehearsed this conversation so many times that in my dreams it has developed scenery. Because I am, at this moment, more frightened than I have been since the courier came, and that is saying a great deal.

"I ask," he said, "because I would like to show you something. Not here." He nodded toward the corridor, where light from the mess hall leaked under the door and voices murmured — Falk's low rumble, young Ivo's sharper tenor, rising whenever Ansel's name happened to pass near it. "Somewhere without ears. The dome is empty tonight. The sky is clear. If you have ever wanted to see Saturn's rings—" He heard himself and winced. "That is not — I am not bribing you with astronomy. I only mean there is nowhere else in this building where a man can speak and be certain the walls are only walls."

Paloma looked at him for a long moment. The lamp threw her shadow huge against the stairwell wall, shoulders squared like a boxer's, and Ansel thought: she is deciding whether I am dangerous, and the honest answer is going to disappoint us both.

"The rings," she said at last. "You're offering me Saturn."

"And the truth. They come as a set."

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth — not quite a smile, but the place where one might be built if materials became available. "I'll want both," she said. "In writing, eventually. I'm a clockmaker. I trust what I can take apart."

* * *

The dome breathed cold. They stood in the blue dark beneath the shuttered slit, where the first stars were already pricking through, and Ansel worked the slow-motion controls to bring the tube around while Paloma stood with her arms crossed and her lamp unlit, because she said starlight ought to be allowed to do its job.

"There," he said, and stepped back from the eyepiece. "Look."

She bent to it. He watched her face rather than the sky — a liberty he permitted himself precisely once, and memorized. Her frown, which seemed to be her resting condition, dissolved by degrees into something younger.

"It has gaps in it," she said. "The ring. Like a wheel with missing teeth."

"Cassini's division. A gap between the rings themselves. Nobody knows why."

"Hm." She straightened up, and he saw the professional pride reassemble itself around her like armor being buckled on. "Well. Somebody will." Then, briskly, before wonder could get any further foothold: "All right, Herr Vogel. The truth. You've bought yourself five minutes of my undivided attention, which is more than the master clock gets, so spend it well."

So he spent it.

"You are right," he began, "that I asked your name before I ever arrived here. You are right — I can see you thinking it — that a junior assistant post at a minor observatory is a strange destination for a man trained in spectroscopy at Göttingen. Ivo is right about my accent. I am right to be afraid. All of these things are true, and I am going to say them in the wrong order, because I have rehearsed them in the right order for so long that the right order has worn smooth and won't hold a sentence anymore."

He took the letter from its oilcloth sleeve inside his coat. He had carried it there since Vienna, since before Vienna, since his father pressed it into his hands on a station platform with snow coming down and said, *if anything happens to me, this is your road north.* His father was alive; the letter had simply traveled with him anyway, the way a compass travels even when you know the direction.

"My father kept an inn. In a valley you have never heard of, because until this year I lived my whole life in it." He unfolded the paper. It crackled in the cold, loud as a shot, and both of them flinched. "Eleven years ago, a woman stayed with us. An Englishwoman traveling alone, which even then made people stare. Her health was bad — her heart, we learned later. She collapsed at our table on the third night, and my mother sat up with her for two days while the passes were closed, and fed her broth, and read to her, and never once asked her for anything."

Paloma had gone very still. Stillness, Ansel was learning, was her native language.

"When she was well enough to travel, my father refused her money. She insisted. They argued — my father could out-argue a bishop — and finally she agreed to pay for her lodging on one condition. That the debt be considered paid instead by a promise. Shelter. Safe passage. For her family, if ever her family needed it, and for ours, if ever ours did." He held out the letter. "She wrote it that night, on the director's typewriter downstairs. My father couldn't read English. She translated it aloud to him line by line, and then she signed it, and then she laughed and said she had just invented the most lopsided contract in history, because what could an innkeeper's family ever need from a clockmaker's widow in another country?"

The words hung in the cold air. Below them, faintly, tap-tap-tap: no, that was memory. The clock room was silent tonight, its patient stopped.

"Your mother," Ansel said quietly, "was Marianne Pemberton."

Paloma did not take the letter. Her arms stayed crossed, but her knuckles had gone white where her fingers gripped her sleeves, and her eyes were fixed on the page — on the typed lines, the faded ribbon-impressions, the signature she must have known the shape of before she could write her own name.

"She died," Ansel said. "Three years ago. I'm sorry. My father lit a candle for her at our church, and the priest asked who the foreign lady was, and my father said, *the reason our family will survive whatever comes next.* We thought he was being dramatic. My father was very good at dramatic." His voice caught; he pushed through it. "And then this year came, and the papers began to talk the way papers talk before wars, and my father put me on a train and gave me this and said, *go north, find her daughter, and ask nothing — knock, show her this, and if she opens the door, you are the luckiest Vogel in three generations, and if she doesn't, come home and we will manage.* And then the bridge closed behind me before I could even—"

"Before you could even tell me." Paloma's voice came out flat and precise, each word machined to tolerance. "You let me walk into this house thinking I was summoned for a broken escapement. You watched me work for days. You handed me wrenches. And the entire time, you knew why I was really here."

"I tried to—"

"You knew." She turned to face him fully now, and the starlight showed him her anger building like pressure in a boiler, gauge needle climbing. "Do you know what I thought? I thought my master sent me because I was competent. Do you understand how much that meant to me? Three years of apprenticeship, three years of being *the girl who's good with her hands*, and finally a summons that said: *send Pemberton. Only Pemberton.* I climbed that road in the snow feeling like I had finally earned something. And it was arranged. Wasn't it. Someone wrote to my master — or paid him, or persuaded him — and chose me, the daughter, specifically, deliberately, the way you'd choose a key for a lock."

"Fräulein—"

"Paloma." She snatched the letter out of his hand at last — he almost sighed with relief that she'd taken it at all — and held it up between them. "You don't get to use my Christian name while you're explaining how you maneuvered me onto a mountain. My mother promised your family shelter. Fine. I believe you. I believe her signature better than I believe most living people. But she is *dead*, Herr Vogel. She has been dead three years, and you let a dead woman do your asking for you, because you knew I'd have to answer for her. You didn't come to me. You came to a promise and waited for me to walk into it."

The unfairness of it — and worse, the fairness of it — hit Ansel simultaneously, which was, he was discovering, how most true things arrived.

"I never touched your clock," he said. "Whatever you suspect—"

"I don't suspect you of the clock."

"But the others do, and you have said nothing, and I have watched you say nothing, day after day, while Ivo sharpened his little knives." He stepped closer, and the cold between them seemed to thin. "I am not asking you to love me, or even to like me. I am asking you to understand that everything I did, I did because I was afraid that if I told you too early, you would send me away before you had read the letter. That you would look at me and see a stranger asking for a dead woman's favor, and the door would close, and my father would be wrong, and everything he believed about kindness would be wrong with him."

"So instead you lied to me by omission for eleven days."

"Yes," Ansel said simply. "It is not the first cowardice of my life and I fear it will not be the last. But I am telling you now, in a dome, under Saturn, which is the most honest architecture I know. I never touched the clock. I came for shelter. And the shelter your mother promised—" his voice dropped, steadied itself against the cold, "—she promised it for times exactly like this one. War times. Door-closing times. This is not me exploiting a loophole, Fräulein Pemberton. This is the promise doing precisely what it was built for."

Silence. The dome ticked as it cooled. Far below, muffled by floors, the mess hall murmured on, and somewhere in that murmur was Ivo's voice, and somewhere beyond Ivo was a ledger none of them yet knew existed, filling page by patient page.

Paloma looked down at the letter in her hands. Her thumb moved along the edge of the paper, back and forth, a clockmaker's unconscious gesture — measuring, testing, checking for flaws.

"She never told me," she said at last, and her anger had cracked along one seam, and through the crack came something that hurt to hear. "Eleven years. Letters, visits — I visited her in this valley, I helped her pack this very tool case — and she never once mentioned an innkeeper's family, or a promise, or a debt she thought we owed. Why would she keep it secret? What did she think I'd—"

She stopped. Ansel watched her arrive somewhere he couldn't follow, some interior door opening onto a corridor he had no map for, and her expression shifted from fury to something rawer and less finished.

"What did she think I would say," Paloma whispered, mostly to herself. "That's it, isn't it. She didn't tell me because she knew me. She knew I'd have called it naive."

"Was it?" Ansel asked gently. "Naive?"

"Don't." She folded the letter with sharp, exact creases and held it out to him. When he reached for it, she didn't let go. For a moment they stood holding opposite ends of her mother's handwriting in the freezing dome, and her eyes came up to his, and they were bright and furious and frightened all at once.

"Here is what happens now," she said. "Tomorrow I go back to that clock, and I find out what actually stopped it, because I am beginning to have suspicions that have nothing to do with you and everything to do with soft metal where hard metal belongs. And when I know, you and I are going to have a very different conversation — one where you answer questions instead of giving speeches. And Herr Vogel—" her grip tightened on the letter, "—the next time you want to tell me something true, try starting with it. I mend clocks for a living. I can always tell when a mechanism has been tampered with."

She released the letter, took up her unlit lamp, and was gone down the stairs, her boots ringing on the iron treads like a striking mechanism counting out hours.

Ansel stood alone under the stars a long while, the letter safe inside his coat again, over the heart, where it had always lived. Above him Saturn wheeled on, ringed and gapped and indifferent, a wheel with missing teeth that turned anyway.

He did not see, three floors below, Paloma stop outside the silent clock room with her hand flat against the door — nor the moment she pushed it open, struck her lamp, and bent over the escapement with her loupe screwed in and her jaw set, hunting through the works for the small, soft thing that should not have been there.
