## 10. The Weight of a Signature

Paloma worked best when her hands were busy and her mind was empty. This was a fact she had learned young, bent over her mother's workbench with a jeweler's file while the radio played dance music from Vienna, and it was a fact she was now discovering did not hold. Her hands were busy. Her mind would not shut up.

She sat at the long bench in the clock room with the master clock's escapement spread before her on green baize like a small silver city after an earthquake — the anchor lifted free, the pallets inspected, the escape wheel cradled in her palm turning its slow, toothy revolutions. Somewhere below her, Ansel Vogel was winding the telescope drives. She could feel it through the floorboards: the faint, patient rhythm of the crank, the hum of the great gears taking up their load. He wound the stars every evening at this hour. She had learned his schedule without meaning to, the way one learns the schedule of weather.

*You knew*, she told her mother, who was not present, who could not be present, and who therefore enjoyed an unfair advantage in every argument they had ever had. *You knew he might come. You typed that letter and you packed it away with my things and you never said one word.*

Her mother, being dead, declined to answer. Being dead, she also declined to be wrong.

Paloma picked up the escape wheel and held it to the lamp. The teeth were sound. The pivot ran true. Whatever had stopped this clock at 4:17 — and she now knew, with the sick certainty of a woman who has found a hair in her soup, that it had been stopped deliberately — whatever had done it, it lived in that little brass pin she'd replaced two nights ago. Soft metal. Lead, or something dressed up as lead. A pin that wears fast and dies quiet, so that a clock fails not with a crash but with a sigh, hours or days after the hand that set it has gone.

Someone at this observatory wanted the clock dark.

Or — and here the argument turned, as arguments do, toward the person standing nearest — someone wanted the observatory dark, and Ansel Vogel had simply arrived too conveniently to be innocent of anything.

*Report him*, said Duty, in a voice that sounded unpleasantly like Herr Falk clearing his throat. *He is a national of a country we are now at war with. He came here under false pretenses. He admits he sought you out. The bridge is closed, the telegraph is down, and you are the only person on this mountain who can send for the authorities come spring.*

*He came here because of your letter*, said Memory, in her mother's voice, which was worse. *Because I promised his family shelter, and I signed my name, and my name was the truest thing about me. Would you have me un-sign it?*

*I never signed anything,* Paloma said aloud, and then pressed her lips together, because Marta had just come through the door with a tray of bread and stewed plums, and cooks hear everything.

"The clockmaker talks to herself," Marta observed, setting down the tray with the particular gentleness she reserved for Paloma and no one else. "Good. Talking to oneself is the only honest conversation on this mountain."

"Marta—"

"Eat," said Marta, and left.

Paloma ate, because refusing Marta's food was a war no one won. The plums were good. The bread was still warm. And underneath the plate, folded once, she found a second heel of bread wrapped in cloth — the kind of portion a person leaves at a locked door when she cannot be seen leaving it. Paloma looked at the extra bread for a long moment. Marta's back was already retreating down the corridor, spine straight as a pendulum rod, carrying her silences ahead of her like a lantern.

So it wasn't only Paloma, then. The mountain had decided something, quietly, in kitchens and corridors, without holding a vote.

She put the bread aside — she would find a way to get it to him; there was always a way past a locked door if one understood locks, and clocks, and the general stupidity of padlocks — and went back to the escapement.

* * *

The trouble with repairing something broken by human hands, rather than by time, was that repair required her to think like the saboteur, and thinking like the saboteur meant sitting inside a head she didn't want to sit inside. She held the lead pin in her fingertips. It was crudely cut — no, not crudely, she corrected herself. Efficiently. Cut by someone who knew exactly how much wear a pin needed before it failed, which meant someone who understood escapements at least a little, or had watched one fail before, or —

Or had asked someone. The thought arrived cold and unwelcome: *had asked someone*. Had stood in this very room, perhaps, chatting amiably about gear trains, learning what he needed to learn from the one person whose expertise he intended to borrow and betray.

No. That was the fear talking, and fear was a poor horologist; it kept terrible time and lost minutes everywhere. She made herself look at facts.

Fact: the pin was soft metal. Fact: it had been installed cleanly, seated properly, by hands that respected machinery even while misusing it. Fact: whoever did it had chosen 4:17 — she was almost sure of the timing now, working backward from the wear pattern, the way water tells you where the stone was — and 4:17 meant nothing to her yet, but it would mean nothing to no one until she let it mean something. Fact: Ansel Vogel had been at the observatory eleven days when the clock stopped, and she herself had been there nine, and the pin had been old when she found it. Old wear. Weeks of wear, maybe more.

Which meant the pin had gone in before either of them arrived.

She set the pin down very carefully, as though it were sleeping, and sat back, and felt the whole shape of the thing tilt beneath her. Ansel had been on the mountain less than a fortnight. The sabotage predated him. Unless he had climbed the road twice — in winter, on foot, unseen, absurd — the foreigner had walked into a trap already built for him.

*Duty says report him anyway*, whispered the Falk-voice. *Let the authorities sort it out. It is not your job to investigate. It is your job to fix clocks.*

"It is absolutely my job to fix clocks," Paloma agreed with the empty room, "and fixing this clock means understanding why it broke, and why it broke is a question with a person-shaped answer."

Her mother would have liked that sentence. That was the problem with mothers: they got into your sentences and stayed there like tenants who paid no rent.

* * *

She worked until the lamp burned low, and then she did the thing she had been avoiding all day, which was to open her tool case — not the tray, but the case itself, the deep bottom drawer where her mother's effects lived in oiled paper like relics. She hadn't repacked them since Innsbruck. She hadn't dared.

The letter was where it always was, beneath a spare loupe and a photograph of a woman squinting into sun outside a workshop that no longer existed. Paloma unfolded it and read it again, though she had memorized it against her will sometime around three in the morning.

It was typed. Of course it was typed; her mother had loved that machine beyond reason, had hammered out invoices and birthday cards and half-finished novels on it, claiming that her handwriting lied but the typewriter told the truth. The letter said:

> To the Vogel family —
> What I promised you in person I put down in type, because type does not fade the way memory does. If ever the world turns ugly and your family needs a roof, come to the observatory at [the name smudged by water, but Paloma knew it], and say my name, and you will be sheltered. My daughter will know my meaning.
> Signed,
> E. Pemberton

And then the signature in ink, careful as engraving, each letter a small act of stubbornness.

*My daughter will know my meaning.* Paloma read that line four times. Her mother had died believing it, apparently. Her mother had died believing that a typed page and a signature could reach across years and borders and wars and land in her daughter's hands at exactly the right moment, like a watch handed down that keeps perfect time forever.

"Well, it doesn't," Paloma informed the letter. "Nothing keeps perfect time forever. Everything needs winding. Everything drifts. Even promises." Especially promises, she thought, promises most of all, because promises are made by people, and people drift, and people die, and people declare war on each other while the promise sits in a drawer being beautiful and useless.

But the letter didn't argue back. It just lay there, smelling faintly of oil and lavender, being certain.

Here was the collision, laid out on the bench between the plums and the lead pin:

A dead woman, in peacetime, with a typewriter and a generous heart and — Paloma had to admit it — a certain recklessness she had always mistaken for courage, had promised shelter to a family she liked. That family now belonged, on paper, to an enemy nation. One member of that family had come to collect. He had done it by arrangement, by maneuver, by letting a summons go out that would bring a Pemberton to the mountain — and yes, she had checked, the telegram had requested *Pemberton* specifically, and she had flattered herself for days about her reputation before the truth landed on her like a dropped weight.

Duty said: report him. Say the word *foreigner* the way Ivo said it, with the safety catch off. Let the law do what laws do.

Memory said: *my daughter will know my meaning*. Memory said: your mother fed strangers at our own table when food was short. Memory said: you learned your trade at her elbow, and every gear you ever cut was cut with tools she bought, and you cannot amputate the hand that taught you without losing the hand itself.

And underneath both voices, quieter than either, a third voice that was entirely her own, and it said: *he wound the stars tonight, same as every night, and he did it knowing what the world now calls him, and he did it anyway, because the telescope doesn't care where a man was born and neither does a clock, and someone on this mountain broke that clock to make him look guilty, and if you report him you'll be doing the saboteur's work for him.*

That third voice was the one she trusted. It was also the one that would get her into trouble.

* * *

Near midnight she finished. The new pin was hardened steel, cut and polished by her own hand, seated with a touch she'd spent ten years earning. The escapement went back together under her fingers the way a sentence goes back together when you finally find the right word: click, click, click. She raised the weights. She set the pendulum breathing.

Tick. Tick. Tick.

The master clock took up its old authority, filling the room with its heartbeat, and Paloma stood in front of it feeling — not triumphant. Watched. Because the clock now ran true, and the moment it ran true, everyone upstairs would know the clockmaker had fixed it, and the question would come, and the question was: *what was wrong with it?*

And she would have to decide, in front of Falk and Ivo and Marta and Ansel, whether to say the word *lead*.

Say it, and the hunt begins — and the first hound loosed will be pointed at Ansel, because suspicion is lazy and always takes the nearest path. Stay silent, and she carries the knowledge alone, like a weight in a case, and if the saboteur strikes again the silence becomes hers.

"Thank you, Mother," she muttered. "This is a lovely inheritance. Truly. The tools were better."

She cleaned her files. She banked the lamp. At the door she stopped, and looked back at the clock, ticking its steady, blameless tick, and at the lead pin lying in the saucer where she'd left it — evidence, or accusation, or just a small gray lie somebody had screwed into a beautiful machine.

She covered the saucer with her handkerchief and put it in her pocket.

Unspoken. For now. Not hidden — she told herself it was not hiding; it was *pending*, the way a pendulum hangs at the top of its arc for one impossible instant before choosing a direction.

Somewhere above her, through three floors of stone and timber, the great telescope turned its slow circle, and a man she was supposed to turn in was keeping time with the stars.
