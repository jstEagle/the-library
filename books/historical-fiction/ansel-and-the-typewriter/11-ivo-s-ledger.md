## 11. Ivo's Ledger

The ledger was a gift from Ivo's father — a proper one, leather-bound with a marbled fore-edge, the kind of book meant for recording investments or births. *For the boy who counts everything*, his father had written on the flyleaf when Ivo turned sixteen, and it had been meant as a joke then, an affectionate tease about a son who counted the stairs on the way to bed (fourteen) and the seconds between lightning and thunder (always too many) and the coins in the church plate (never enough). Six years later the joke had curdled into something else entirely, and Ivo sat at his desk in the astronomers' quarters with the ledger open before him and a fresh nib and the peculiar feeling that he was about to do either the most virtuous thing of his life or the most shameful, and that there was no way to know which until afterward.

He wrote the date at the top of the page. He ruled a column. And then, in his small, precise hand — a hand his professors in Zürich had praised, a hand built for tables of stellar parallax — he began:

*Oct. 14. V. at telescope drive, 6:10–8:40 p.m. Wound both drives. Checked nothing else. Spoke to no one.*

He sat back and looked at what he'd written and felt, briefly, ridiculous. There it was: a man had wound two clockwork drives for nearly three hours, which was his job, which he did every night, which was — Ivo had to admit, grinding his teeth a little — done *well*. Ansel Vogel wound those drives the way a mother rocks a cradle. The telescope never stuttered, never overshot, never woke the whole mountain with that awful stripped-gear shriek that old Herr Brandl's carelessness had produced twice last winter. And here Ivo was, keeping accounts of it like a customs officer at a border where nothing was being smuggled.

But that was just it. That was exactly it. Nothing was being smuggled *visibly*.

Ivo got up, lit the little spirit lamp under his tea — gone cold again; he kept doing that, another thing to count, forty-one cups gone cold since September — and stood at his window looking down at the valley floor far below, where the clouds were stacking themselves against the peaks like dishes in a cupboard nobody would ever empty. The bridge was somewhere under all that white now. He tried to picture it: the iron span, the toll-house, the road winding down toward the railway and the telegraph office and the world where things made sense. He couldn't picture it anymore. That frightened him more than anything, and Ivo was a man who had made a careful study of his own fears the way other men studied stamp collections.

Because here is what Ivo knew, what he carried around inside him like a stone in his boot: war was coming. Everyone knew it. You could read it in the newspapers that arrived three days late and stale as yesterday's bread; you could hear it in the way Herr Falk read them, his lips moving slightly over the troop figures; you could feel it in Marta's kitchen, where she had stopped singing. And when war came, every foreigner on this mountain would stop being a person and start being a category, and categories did not wind telescope drives gently or leave their boots outside doors or say *guten Morgen* with that soft southern vowel that made even the word good morning sound like an apology.

And there was one such category sleeping thirty feet from Ivo's own room.

*V.,* he wrote, because writing *Ansel* felt like a betrayal of the exercise and writing *the Vogel man* felt like something out of a cheap thriller. *V.* was neutral. V. was scientific. V. was what you called a variable star whose light you could not yet explain.

He dipped the pen again.

*Oct. 15. Breakfast: V. seated at end of table as usual. Spoke twice — once re: bread, once to thank Marta. Marta replied in low voice, duration approx. 4 seconds. Subject of exchange unknown.*

There. Four seconds. He had actually counted. He had sat there with his spoon halfway to his mouth counting the seconds of a conversation between a cook and an assistant astronomer about, in all probability, whether there was enough butter, and he had recorded it, and the record now existed in ink in a book with a marbled fore-edge, and some small honest clerk deep inside Ivo's chest put down its pen and looked up at him with an expression of profound disappointment.

"Subject of exchange unknown," Ivo said aloud to the empty room. "Yes, well. Perhaps if you'd simply asked him."

But asking was how you got lied to. Asking was how you ended up believing people. Ivo had believed people before — believed the recruiting sergeant who'd told him his flat feet wouldn't matter, believed his cousin Leopold who swore the mining shares were sound, believed, God help him, a girl named Resi who had promised to write and had written exactly once, a postcard of the lake at Brienz with no message on the back at all. Believing people was a door you opened, and behind it was always the same room.

Whereas a ledger — a ledger never lied to you. A ledger only held what you put in it. It was the most honest conversation available to a man.

* * *

The trouble with keeping a ledger, Ivo discovered over the following week, was that it required watching, and watching required proximity, and proximity was dangerous in ways he hadn't anticipated.

He took to arriving at the telescope dome early, before Vogel, so that he could observe the man's arrival rather than simply share the room with him. He noted the exact time (7:02, 6:58, 7:11 — the man was punctual within a ten-minute window that Ivo found himself oddly charmed by and resented for finding charming). He noted that Vogel checked the oil reservoirs first, always, before touching the winding cranks, though the manual prescribed the reverse order, and that the drives therefore ran smoother than the manual promised they could. He noted that Vogel hummed while he worked — low, tuneless, more like a kettle thinking about boiling than like music — and stopped humming whenever anyone else entered the dome, as though he'd been caught at something.

Caught at humming. Ivo wrote that down too, and hated himself, and wrote it anyway.

It was on Thursday that the matter of the notes arose. Ivo had come down to the kitchen corridor for his eleven o'clock bread and cheese — he counted these too, the walk was ninety-one steps — and came around the corner in time to see Marta pressing something small into Vogel's hand. A folded paper. Their heads were close together. Vogel said something Ivo didn't catch; Marta laughed — *laughed* — and they separated, Vogel pocketing the paper without looking at it, Marta returning to her sink with her back very straight.

Ivo stood in the corridor with his heart going like a escapement with a cracked pallet stone. His first thought was: *codes*. His second thought, arriving half a second later and considerably less welcome, was: *it is almost certainly a shopping list, you absolute fool.* But the second thought arrived too late; the first had already been written down. That evening the ledger received its longest entry yet:

*Oct. 19. M. passed folded paper to V., kitchen corridor, ~11:05. Neither observed by others. Paper not examined (impossible). Prior instances of private exchange between M. and V.: minimum 3, possibly more. M. has also been observed leaving extra bread at V.'s door on nights following deliveries. Pattern consistent with provisioning. Question: provisioning for what?*

He read the entry back three times, hunting for the flaw in it, the place where fear might have crept in wearing arithmetic's clothing. He couldn't find it. Every fact in it was true. That was the maddening thing, the thing he would lie awake turning over: every single fact was true, and arranged end to end they pointed somewhere, and he could not make them point nowhere no matter how fairly he weighed them. Bread left at a door. Papers passed in corridors. A foreigner in a house full of instruments worth more than the valley below, on a mountain whose summit — Ivo had heard Falk say it at dinner, saying it lightly, saying it with his eyes on Vogel — was one of only four stations in Europe fitted to measure certain things that armies would very much like to know.

*Provisioning for what?* The question sat on the page like a spider in a web of perfectly accurate threads.

* * *

On Sunday it snowed all day, the fat silent flakes that mean business, and the observatory drew inward like a fist closing. Ivo used the confinement to take inventory of his evidence, laying the ledger open beside his observation logbooks, cross-referencing dates. This was the work he was actually good at — this was the work he'd been born to, if he was honest, though the thought gave him no pleasure tonight. Tables agreed with each other. Dates lined up. And at the center of the alignment, like a dark star inferred from the wobble of everything around it, sat one fact that had been bothering him for a fortnight and that he had not yet committed to paper because committing it would make it real.

The clock. The master clock, stopped at 4:17 on the very night before the Pemberton woman arrived. Stopped, according to everyone, through simple decay — worn parts, tired metal, a mountain's worth of cold in the works. Except Ivo had been in the clock room. Ivo, unable to sleep, had gone down at three in the morning two days after the failure, ostensibly to check the barometers, actually because sleep had become a country he needed visas for, and he had seen the new escapement pin.

He was not a clockmaker. But he was an observer, and observers notice things, and the pin had been wrong. Too bright. Too clean-cut at its ends. New metal among old, like a fresh coin in a purse of worn ones. Someone had put that pin there recently — and the clock had failed *before* Miss Pemberton ever climbed the mountain. Which meant someone in this house had stopped the master clock deliberately, in the dark, in the minute between one hour and the next.

Ivo sat with that knowledge for a long time while the snow ticked against the window. Then he opened the ledger and wrote, slowly, pressing hard enough to emboss the next three pages:

*Nov. 2. Master clock stopped at 4:17 a.m., Oct. 13. Escapement pin replaced prior to Pemberton's arrival — by person unknown, within this house. V. arrived Oct. 9. Window of opportunity: 4 days. Motive (possible): force summoning of outside expert; create crisis requiring scrutiny of all residents incl. V.? Alternative reading: V. sabotaged clock to disguise... [entry unfinished]*

He stared at the unfinished sentence. His pen hovered. Because here was the crack running through the middle of everything he'd built, the fault line he kept stepping over and refusing to look into: if Vogel had wanted to sabotage the clock, why had he spent every evening since coaxing its little sisters, the telescope drives, into smoother running than they'd ever known? If he were the sort of man who broke clocks, why was Ivo's ledger quietly filling with evidence that he was the sort of man who *mended* them?

Ivo closed the book. He pressed his palm flat on its cover, on the marbled edge, on his father's joke — *for the boy who counts everything* — and understood suddenly, with terrible clarity, that his father had never meant it as praise, and that the ledger was not protecting anything. It was a machine, same as the great clock downstairs, same as the drives overhead, and a machine does only what its maker builds into it. He had built this one out of fear. Every gear in it was fear. Fear of the war, yes, but underneath that, older and less speakable: fear of being the fool again. The one who believes Resi will write. The one who buys the mining shares. The one who opens the door.

A ledger full of fear, dressed up in dates and columns, calling itself virtue.

"No," Ivo said to the room, to the snow, to the small honest clerk in his chest. "No. This is duty. Someone has to watch. That's all this is. Someone has to."

The clerk in his chest said nothing. Clerks, Ivo reflected, knew better than to argue with their superiors; they simply waited, and kept their own records.

He blew out the lamp. In the dark, the facts went on arranging themselves without his permission, patient as stars, and at their center the dark star grew heavier by the day — *V., V., V.* — pulling at everything, explaining everything, and Ivo fell asleep composing the entry he would write tomorrow, and the one after that, already rehearsing the day he would carry the ledger to Falk's study and lay it on the desk and say, in a steady voice, *Herr Director, I believe there is something you should see.*

Down the corridor, faint through two closed doors, a kettle began to hum.

* * *

Three nights later, the master clock died a second death — a bad one, a loud one, a sound like a bone going — and Ivo was awake to hear it, and his first feeling, before dread, before guilt, before any of it, was a flash of pure vindication so hot and shameful that he sat up in bed clutching it like stolen money.

*You see,* said the dark star. *You see?*

By morning the whole observatory was ringing with it, Falk's voice was raised in the clock room for anyone to hear, and every eye in the house had swung — Ivo watched it happen at breakfast, watched it happen with his spoon halfway to his mouth, counting, always counting — toward the end of the table, where the man who wound the stars sat very still over his bread, and said nothing at all.
