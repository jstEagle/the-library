## 12. The Broken Escapement

The clock lasted nine days.

Nine days of running true, of striking the hours with that clean, unhurried voice I had coaxed back into it, of the astronomers downstairs setting their observations by it as though nothing had ever been wrong. Nine days in which I allowed myself the small, dangerous pleasure of believing a thing was fixed because I had fixed it.

Then, on the morning of the tenth day, I came down the spiral stair to the clock room and stopped on the third step from the bottom, because something was missing.

Sound. That was what was missing. A clock room is never silent — not really. Even when the mechanism hangs between beats there is the pendulum's long breath at either end of its swing, the faint tick of thermal expansion in the case, the whisper of the weights against their cables. I had spent enough nights in that room to know its silence the way you know a sleeping child's breathing: by its texture. And this silence had no texture at all. It was the silence of an empty room.

The master clock stood dead. The pendulum hung straight and still, and the minute hand pointed to 11:52 with the dumb obedience of a hand that would point there until the mountain crumbled.

I did not go to it at once. I stood on the stair with my tool case in my hand and made myself look, the way Papa taught me to make myself look before I touched anything. *Diagnose with your eyes first, Paloma. Your hands will lie to you; your eyes only need practice.*

The weights were down. All three of them, settled at the bottoms of their runs like men who had fainted in their chairs. The great wheel had stopped mid-tooth. And there — I saw it even from the stair, and my stomach went cold and stayed cold — there was a brightness inside the movement that had no business being there. A scatter of small metal fragments across the lower plate, glinting in the lamplight like spilled seeds.

I set down the case and climbed the last steps slowly, as if the floor might resent my weight.

* * *

By noon I knew, and knowing was worse than not knowing.

It was not decay. It was not wear, or cold, or any of the hundred honest ways a clock dies in a mountain winter. The escapement — my escapement, the one I had rebuilt with my own hands nine days ago — had been broken deliberately, and broken *worse*. Whoever had done it had learned from my repair. They had seen what I replaced, understood why, and gone one step further. The new pallet arbor had been forced out of alignment — not snapped, which any fool could do, but bent just past tolerance, so that the escape wheel had been driven against the pallets until the teeth began to shed themselves one by one, like a man pulling out his own hair. The fragments on the plate were teeth. Fifteen of them, sheared clean.

And there was more. When I lifted the movement free and turned it to the light, I found the lead pin.

My pin — the soft, wrong pin I had found on my first night here, the one timed to stop the clock at 4:17 — should have been in my evidence box, wrapped in cloth, waiting for me to decide what it meant. It was not in my box. It was in the clock. Or rather, its hole was in the clock, freshly drilled, exactly where it had been before, and beside the movement lay a curl of bright new metal shavings and a drill bit still dusted with them.

Someone had taken my discovery and put it back. Erased my repair, restored the sabotage, and then built a larger ruin on top of it, the way a liar builds a second lie over the first when the first begins to show through.

I sat down on the floor with the sheared teeth cupped in my palm and let that settle over me. This was not vandalism. Vandalism wants destruction; this wanted *narrative*. This wanted the observatory dark, and the foreign assistant standing in the shadow of the dark, and every honest gear in this house turning toward suspicion. Someone in this observatory — someone who could walk into the clock room unremarked, who could watch my work closely enough to reverse it — was spending enormous effort to make Ansel Vogel look guilty.

And I was the only person on the mountain who could read the handwriting.

* * *

Herr Falk convened us all in the mess hall at four o'clock, which told me everything about how bad he thought it was. Falk was a man who governed by schedule the way other men governed by decree; unscheduled meetings were his equivalent of ringing alarm bells.

He stood at the head of the long table with his hands flat on the wood, and he did not sit, and he did not begin until Marta had retreated to the doorway with her dish towel wrung into a rope in her hands and Ivo had stopped shifting in his chair.

"The master clock has failed again," he said. "Permanently, as far as Fräulein Pemberton can determine." He glanced at me, and I nodded, and hated how much the nod cost me. "I want to be plain with you all about what that means. The observatory does not exist to house telescopes. Telescopes are glass and steel; they exist in dozens of places. What we have that they do not is time. Our funding, our charter, our reason for existing on this mountain depends upon our ability to time our observations against a standard the world will accept. Without the master clock we cannot do that. Without the master clock we are four men, a cook, and a girl playing with very expensive toys."

Nobody laughed. Nobody ever laughed at Falk's jokes, which was perhaps why he made so few of them.

"The telegraph remains down. The bridge remains closed. We cannot summon help until spring, and by spring," his voice flattened, "the consortium will have concluded that this observatory is finished. Unless the clock runs, we are done. Do you understand? Everything we are ends with this season."

Silence. Outside the tall windows the snow was coming down in that fat, windless way that means the sky has given up pretending it might stop.

Falk let the silence do its work. Then he turned — I watched him turn, watched the deliberateness of it, watched the moment he decided — and looked directly at Ansel.

"Fräulein Pemberton repaired this clock nine days ago. It ran perfectly. Then it failed again, worse than before, in a manner she describes as—" he consulted a scrap of paper he did not need, "—deliberate. In those nine days, Herr Vogel, who has been in this room?"

Ansel sat very still. I had come to read him a little in the weeks since the bridge closed, and stillness, I had learned, was his armor. He wore fear the way other men wore anger: quietly, buttoned up, hoping no one would notice the shape of it.

"I have been in the clock room, Herr Direktor. To wind the telescope drives. The drives are regulated against the master clock."

"And who else?"

"Marta brings tea on Thursdays. Fräulein Pemberton works there most days. Ivo—" Ansel paused, and I saw the pause cost him something too. "Ivo passes through often."

Ivo's chair scraped. "That's a lie—"

"Is it?" Falk said mildly, and the mildness was worse than shouting. "You pass through the north corridor frequently, Herr Ivo. Your own log shows irregular hours. You were near the clock room the night of the first failure — you said so yourself, when you reported hearing 'a scraping sound.'"

I had not known that. I turned to look at Ivo, and Ivo looked back at me with an expression I could not at first name — and then I could, and wished I hadn't. It was triumph held carefully behind fear, the face of a man watching a fire he started spread toward the house he meant to protect.

"The scraping sound," Ivo said. "Yes. I reported it. Because I heard it. Because I am the only one in this house who reports what he hears." His chin came up. "Everyone else tiptoes around the obvious. A clock is sabotaged. Twice. Who benefits from a darkened observatory? Who waits for spring — for *authorities*, for a border to open, for a war to swallow whatever happens here?" He spread his hands. "Ask him where he was at 4:17 this morning. Ask anyone where *they* were. But ask him first."

"I was asleep," Ansel said quietly. "As I was asleep at 4:17 the first time."

"Convenient, 4:17," Ivo said. "Twice."

"That will be enough, Ivo." Falk's voice cracked across the table like a whip, and Ivo subsided, though his ears had gone red and his hands had not stopped moving in his lap. Falk pressed two fingers to the bridge of his nose. When he spoke again his voice was tired in a way I had not heard before — the tiredness of a man carrying something heavier than a directorship.

"Herr Vogel. I am not accusing you. I want that understood by everyone in this room. But I am telling you that if this clock fails again — if anything else goes wrong on this mountain while we are sealed in here together — I will have no choice but to confine you until the bridge opens and the authorities can make a proper inquiry. Not because I believe you did this. Because I can no longer afford *not* to believe it. Do you understand the difference?"

"No," Ansel said.

It was so quiet, so simply said, that for a moment nobody moved.

"You understand the difference perfectly well, Herr Direktor," Ansel went on, and his voice stayed level but something underneath it had begun to shake, the way ice shakes before it goes. "You are saying that truth no longer matters here, only arithmetic. One suspect is cheaper than none. I understand. My country understands this arithmetic very well at present." He pushed back his chair, stood, bowed — actually *bowed*, a stiff, correct, unbearable little bow — and walked out of the mess hall with his spine straight and his hands trembling where I alone could see them.

Marta left by the kitchen door without meeting anyone's eyes. Ivo sat looking pleased with himself and frightened of his own pleasure. And Falk stood at the head of the table with his palms still flat on the wood, staring at the place where Ansel had been, and I thought: *he doesn't believe it. He is doing arithmetic and hating himself for it.* Which was, I would learn, exactly what I was doing too.

* * *

I did not sleep that night.

I sat in my narrow room with the sheared escapement teeth laid out on a handkerchief on my desk — fifteen of them, bright and accusing — and I conducted a trial in which I was prosecutor, defense, judge, and, increasingly, the accused.

Here is what I knew. The sabotage required knowledge of horology — not genius, but real knowledge, the kind that comes from study or apprenticeship. It required access to the clock room at hours when no one would question the visitor. It required a drill, and patience, and the specific cruelty of reversing another craftsman's work. Ansel wound the telescope drives daily and understood regulation, yes — but bending an arbor past tolerance without snapping it, drilling a replacement seat for a pin whose purpose he could not possibly have known I'd discovered — no. It didn't fit him. It fit someone who had watched me work.

And it fit someone left-handed.

I had not told a soul about the wear patterns on the original pin — the slight ovaling of the hole, the drag marks angled the wrong way, the signature of a grip that approached from the left. I had told myself I was keeping it back because the evidence wasn't ready. Because a good clockmaker confirms before she accuses. Both true. And also, underneath, quieter: because I had watched Ivo take his tea, and Ivo held his spoon in his left hand, and I did not yet want to know what that observation meant.

There it was. Say it plainly, Paloma. You know. You have known since the afternoon you turned that movement to the light, and you sat in this mess hall tonight and said *nothing*, because saying it would put you at the center of a storm you are not sure you can survive. Saying it would make you the witness against a colleague, in a house already bleeding trust, with months of winter still to get through. Saying it would mean being certain — and certainty is a heavy tool, and once you pick it up you cannot put it down until you have used it.

So instead I had nodded when Falk asked me to confirm the clock's death, and I had kept my mouth shut about lead pins and left-handed grips and drill shavings, and I had let Ansel Vogel walk out of that room wearing suspicion like a coat cut precisely to his measure.

I blew out the lamp and lay in the dark listening to the building. Without the master clock the observatory had lost its heartbeat; I could feel the absence of it the way you feel a missing tooth with your tongue. Somewhere below, Ansel was awake too — I was nearly sure of it. Two people lying in the dark in the same frozen house, each keeping a silence that was slowly crushing them both.

And that was when I heard footsteps in the corridor outside my door. Light ones. Pausing.

A soft knock — no, not a knock. A scratching, quick and nervous, like someone testing whether the door was locked. Then silence again, retreating down the corridor toward the stairs.

I lay still for a long moment, heart hammering. Then I got up, lit the lamp, and opened my door.

On the mat outside, still warm, sat a folded note. And beneath the note, arranged with a cook's unconscious precision, three slices of bread and a knob of butter — the same quiet ration Marta had been leaving at Ansel's door since the war news came. Only now she was leaving it at mine.

The note was written in pencil, in a hurried hand, in German, and then, as if the writer had thought better of it, repeated beneath in careful, schoolroom English:

*I saw who was in the clock room the first night. It was not him. Meet me tomorrow, after the bread comes up. Come alone. Please.*

No signature. None needed. I stood in the doorway holding the paper, watching Marta's candlelight wobble away down the stairwell, and felt the whole rotten architecture of my silence begin, at last, to crack.
