## 13. Snowbound

**Ansel**

The storm came up the valley like something with intent.

Ansel had been carrying coal to the clock room when the light changed — not darkened, exactly, but *whitened*, the windows going the color of unpolished silver. Ten minutes later you couldn't see the flagpole from the porch, and twenty minutes after that you couldn't see the porch from the door.

"Nobody goes anywhere," Herr Falk announced to the corridor at large, in the tone of a man issuing a decree to the weather itself. "The observatory is closed until further notice. Fräulein Pemberton — your clock will keep."

"It would," said Paloma Pemberton, "if someone hadn't—" She stopped herself. Ansel, standing by the coal scuttle, watched her swallow the sentence whole.

He had learned her silences in the weeks since the bridge went quiet. There was the silence she kept for Falk, who paid her and therefore must be endured. The silence she kept for Ivo, which was colder and more deliberate, like a drawer being closed on something sharp. And there was the silence she kept for Ansel himself, which was the strangest one, because it was full of things she clearly wanted to say and had decided, for reasons he could only guess at, not to.

The guess was not difficult. He had, after all, maneuvered her. He knew it. She knew it. Somewhere between the confession at the eyepiece and the second failure of the escapement, they had built a small architecture of unfinished arguments between them, and now the storm was going to lock them in a room together with it.

"I'll need the escapement parts from the east cabinet," she was saying to Marta. "And the lamp oil. If I'm going to be trapped in here all night I may as well work."

"The clock room has a stove," Marta said. "And blankets. In winter we kept watch there sometimes, before the telephone." She said it mildly, but her eyes went to Ansel, then back to Paloma, and something passed across her face that Ansel chose, charitably, not to interpret.

It didn't matter what anyone intended. The mountain intended what it intended. By the time the lamps were lit, the wind had begun its long note against the tower stones, and the door of the clock room had frozen in its frame so thoroughly that even Falk, hauling at it red-faced, pronounced it immovable until morning.

"There's a back stair through the gear loft," Paloma said, "but the hatch ices shut. My father used to say—"

She stopped again. Ansel noticed how often she did that now: began a sentence about her family and then folded it away, as if the words were tools she wasn't sure she had the right to use.

"My father," she finished instead, "used to say a clock room is the warmest cold place in the world. All that brass. It holds the heat like a grudge."

So it was settled, in the way things on mountains are settled: by weather, and by nobody having a better idea. Marta brought bread and a pot of stew down the spiral stair, set them on the workbench, looked from one of them to the other with an expression of magnificent neutrality, and said, "I will knock at six. If the wind allows."

Then it was the two of them, the stopped clock, and the storm.

* * *

**Paloma**

The master clock stood silent in the middle of the room like a grandfather everyone was politely ignoring.

Paloma worked. That was the arrangement she'd made with herself: she would work, and he would sit by the stove reading — he read everything, that man; half her master's library had passed under his eyes in six weeks — and they would not talk about the letter, or the promise, or the fact that she had known why she was sent and let him believe otherwise.

But the escapement defeated her twice, and the third time her hand shook from cold, and she swore at it — a word her mother would have boxed her ears for — and heard him laugh.

"Not a sound I expected from a clockmaker."

"You expected church bells?"

"I expected someone very precise. You are precise. You're just also angry." He turned a page. "Angry people make good clockmakers, I think. It takes a certain violence of attention."

"That is not a real philosophy."

"No. It's a Vogel philosophy. We have several. Most of them were invented by my father to avoid paying debts." He said it lightly, but Paloma had spent enough years around mechanisms to hear the click of something engaging underneath.

She put down her pliers. "Tell me about him. Your father. Not the man in the letter. The man who owed money."

Ansel closed his book on his finger. For a while the wind filled the room, that long note rising and falling like breath.

"He borrowed," he said finally, "from everyone, and repaid almost no one, and yet every person he ever borrowed from came to our house for dinner. Do you understand? He owed the butcher nine months of payments, and the butcher ate at our table every Sunday. He owed my uncle the price of a farm, and my uncle wept at his funeral. I used to think it was because he was charming. He was charming. But that wasn't it." Ansel looked at the stopped clock. "It was that he treated the debt as if it were a relationship. Not a number. A thing between two people, that could be carried together. People forgave him because he never pretended the debt didn't exist. He'd pour you wine and say, 'I still owe you everything, brother, isn't it wonderful that we're still talking about it.'"

"And did they ever collect?"

"One man did. A hard man. Came on a Tuesday and took our piano." Ansel smiled at some private corner of the memory. "My father helped carry it to the cart. Helped! Waving goodbye to it like a daughter getting married. And then he wrote to your mother."

Paloma's chest tightened. "To mine."

"To yours. Because your mother had written to him first — years before, when we still had the piano. Did you know that? She had stayed at a guesthouse near our town, before you were born perhaps, and my father fixed the innkeeper's clocks for nothing, and she sat in the kitchen listening to him and drinking terrible coffee. When she left she wrote: *If ever you or yours need shelter, come to the observatory where I work, and it will be given.* Just that. No conditions. My father framed the letter. Framed it! Hung it over where the piano used to stand, as if the letter were the piano's replacement."

Paloma stared at her hands. "That's not how she told it. Or — she never told it at all. I found the reply among her things. Her half of it. I've been carrying it around for months without knowing what it was."

"Neither of us asked for it," Ansel said quietly. "The promise. It was made over our heads, like weather."

"Yes." And then, because the storm had taken everything else from her — the road, the bridge, the careful distance — she said the true thing. "I was angry at you for using it. For coming here and letting me be summoned without knowing why. But I've been angrier at her. She made a promise that would cost other people to keep. She died and left it to me like a debt I never signed for."

The words came out harder than she meant. Ansel didn't flinch. He got up, took the stew pot off the stove, and ladled two bowls with the unhurried competence of a man who had cooked for his family since childhood.

"Your mother," he said, handing her one, "fixed my father's broken clock for free, that week at the inn. He told me that too. He said she opened it up and found it wasn't broken at all — it was wound wrong. Someone had been forcing it, trying to make it run faster than it was built to run. And instead of telling the innkeeper he was a fool, she cleaned it, oiled it, wound it gently, and told him it had simply needed care." He sat down across from her. "Maybe the promise cost. Maybe debts do. But she didn't sign it thinking about the cost. She signed it thinking about who she wanted to be when the bill came due."

* * *

**Ansel**

She fell asleep in the chair at nearly two in the morning, mid-sentence, a jeweler's loupe still pushed up on her forehead like a second eye.

Ansel banked the stove, fetched one of Marta's blankets, and hesitated over the ancient problem of how to cover a sleeping stranger without waking her or presuming anything. He solved it the way his father would have: badly, but with warmth. He dropped the blanket over her shoulders like a tarpaulin over a boat, and she woke instantly, of course, because she was Paloma.

"What time is it?"

"After two. The clock won't tell us and the stars won't either. The sky is a white wall."

"So we're outside time entirely." She pulled the blanket around herself with as much dignity as the situation allowed. "You should sleep. Take my chair, it's nearer the stove."

"I'm fine."

"You are not fine. You've been winding telescope drives all day and pretending you weren't watching me work, which is more tiring than the work." She said it without accusation — almost fondly — and Ansel felt something in his chest turn over like a mechanism finding its beat.

"I watch you work," he admitted, "because it's the only hour of the day I forget what's coming. When the bridge opens. When they come for me. You bend over that clock and the world is just — teeth and springs and a problem that can actually be solved. I would watch anyone do it. But it happens to be you."

Paloma was quiet for a long moment. The wind screamed once, far above, and settled.

"When I was twelve," she said, "my mother caught me taking apart the kitchen regulator. Every screw on the floor, guts everywhere. Any sane parent would have been furious. She sat down on the floor next to me and asked me what I thought was wrong with it. Not why I'd done it. What I thought was *wrong with it*. As if I were a colleague." She laughed, short and wet. "That's who typed that letter. That woman. So maybe I can't be angry at her for believing people could be good. It would be like being angry at her for teaching me the lathe."

"Is that forgiveness?"

"It's a start. Don't push it, Herr Vogel."

They arranged the chairs by the stove, facing each other, and somewhere in the grey hours they both slept, sitting up, the blanket shared between them in a manner neither would later be able to explain precisely, and neither would want to.

* * *

**Paloma**

Marta knocked at six, exactly as promised, and the door came free with a crack like a rifle shot, admitting a blast of snow-smelling air and one disapproving cook.

Paloma woke with a stiff neck, Ansel's book open on the floor between their chairs, and the mortifying awareness that Marta's expression contained an entire novel.

"The storm's easing," Marta said. "Herr Falk wants everyone in the mess at eight. There was a courier before the pass closed completely yesterday — the news came with him." Her voice was carefully flat. "It's official now. War. Declared on both sides. It will be in the papers when the line restores, but Herr Falk wished you to hear it properly first."

The room seemed to tilt. Paloma looked at Ansel. He had gone very still, the way a clock goes still — not resting, but *stopped*, every part of him waiting on a force that hadn't arrived yet.

"My mother is in Vienna," he said, to no one in particular. "My sister is in Trieste. And I am here, behind a wall of snow, in a country that is now at war with mine."

No one answered, because there was no answer. Marta pressed a heel of bread into his hand — Paloma saw her do it, saw the swift, defiant gentleness of it — and left.

Paloma stood, gathered her tools, and found she couldn't leave. Not yet.

"Ansel." He looked up. "Last night you asked if that was forgiveness. It wasn't. But this is something: whatever happens when this mountain opens — whoever comes up that road, whatever they decide about you — I'm going to find out who really stopped that clock. Because right now the only evidence against you is a lie, and I don't keep company with lies. Even convenient ones."

Something moved behind his eyes — hope, handled carefully, like a part too fragile to grip hard.

"And after?" he asked. "When you find out?"

Paloma slung her tool case over her shoulder and walked toward the door, toward the mess hall, toward whatever Falk's face would look like at eight o'clock.

"Then we'll know what we owe each other," she said. "And isn't it wonderful that we're still talking about it."

Down the corridor, she could already hear Ivo's voice, bright and brittle, asking Falk whether the courier had brought any instructions about *certain persons resident on the premises* — and she understood, with a clockmaker's certainty, that the mechanism she truly needed to open was not the one in the tower.
