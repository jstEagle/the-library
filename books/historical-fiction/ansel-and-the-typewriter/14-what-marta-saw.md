## 14. What Marta Saw

Marta Brunner had cooked for the observatory for nineteen years, and in that time she had learned one iron law of mountains: everything up here eventually comes down to the kitchen. The stars came down as gossip. The weather came down as complaints. The director's moods came down as requests for stronger coffee. And secrets — secrets came down as leftovers nobody finished.

So it was that Marta knew about the war before Herr Falk had finished announcing it, because Ansel Vogel had left half a plate of dumplings untouched, and a man only abandoned dumplings like those if his whole world had tilted.

She knew other things too. She knew which of the young astronomers chewed their pencils and which swallowed them. She knew that Ivo Steiner took his coffee with four sugars and pretended to take none. She knew that Paloma Pemberton skipped supper on work nights and could be bought entirely with a slice of plum cake left outside the clock room at ten o'clock.

And she knew — though she had told no living soul — what she had seen on the first night, the night the master clock stopped.

* * *

It had been an ordinary evening, or as ordinary as evenings got at the top of the world. Marta had been scrubbing the big pots, sleeves rolled, forearms pink from water hot enough to scald sin out of brass, when she remembered she'd left the butter crock unturned in the cold pantry. The pantry sat along the east corridor, past the stair that led down to the clock room, and so it was that Marta, carrying nothing but her own curiosity and a damp cloth, happened to be standing in exactly the wrong — or exactly the right — stretch of hallway at a quarter past four in the morning.

The lamp at the stair landing was turned low. She heard footsteps first: quick, light, trying very hard to be slow. Then Ivo Steiner came around the corner of the stairwell with a candle in one hand and something small cupped in the other, and he was walking like a man who has rehearsed innocence in front of a mirror and found the performance wanting.

He didn't see her. She was standing in the shadow of the pantry door, and Ivo Steiner had never once seen Marta when there was food involved; cooks, in his cosmology, were furniture that produced meals.

He stopped at the clock room door. He looked over his shoulder — once down the corridor toward the dormitories, once back up toward the director's study, where a light still burned — and then he let himself into the clock room with a key he was not supposed to have, because Marta kept the key board clean every morning and she knew precisely how many keys hung on it.

The door clicked shut behind him.

Marta stood in the dark with her damp cloth going slowly cold in her hand and thought, in order: *That boy is up to something. That boy will get caught. And when that boy gets caught, whoever is standing nearest will be blamed, and who is standing nearest?*

The foreigner. The quiet one who wound the telescope drives at all hours and said *good morning* like it was a gift he'd wrapped himself.

She should have knocked on Falk's door right then. Nineteen years of kitchen wisdom said you put trouble in front of the director while it was still warm, before it set. But there was another kind of wisdom too, older than kitchens, older than the observatory itself, and it lived in valleys like this one: *a servant who sees things is a servant until she says them, and a witness after.* Witnesses got asked questions. Questions got asked by men from the valley, and men from the valley did not leave quickly once they'd arrived, and where they lingered, positions evaporated.

Marta was fifty-eight years old, widowed, and possessed of exactly one marketable skill, which was making edible miracles at four thousand meters on a coal stove. Her position was not a thing she could afford to spend.

So she went back to her pots. She turned the butter crock. And in the morning, when the whole mountain woke to find its master clock dead at 4:17 and Herr Falk's face gray as porridge, Marta said nothing, and ladled breakfast, and hated herself efficiently and thoroughly between servings of oatmeal.

* * *

That had been weeks ago now. Weeks of watching the suspicion settle over the house like flour dust — everywhere, invisible, getting into everything. Weeks of watching Falk watch Ansel. Weeks of watching Ivo watch everyone, with his little notebook that he thought nobody noticed, as if a man could carry a ledger around an observatory the size of a walnut and expect the cook not to see it. Marta saw everything. It was practically her job description.

What she had not expected — what had crept up on her the way spring would eventually creep up on the valley, slowly and then all at once — was how much she minded.

It started, as these things do, with bread.

The night the courier brought word of the declaration, Marta had baked late, working off feelings the way she always did, through dough. War. Actual war, over there in the world below, and Ansel Vogel's homeland on the wrong side of it, and Ansel himself locked up here above the clouds like a letter nobody could deliver. When the loaves came out she cut the heel end — the best part, the part with the crackled crust — and set it aside without letting herself think about why, and left it outside his door with a knob of butter, the way her mother used to leave bread for travelers, because in her mother's village you fed the stranger first and asked questions after, and if the questions turned ugly at least the stranger hadn't starved on your doorstep.

Ansel never mentioned it. But the plate came back washed, and once with a little paper folded under it on which he had written, in careful, schoolroom-perfect script, *Thank you for the kindness. It is noticed.* And Marta had read that sentence six times and then put it in the tin where she kept recipes worth keeping, because it seemed to belong there.

A man who thanked you for bread in writing was not a saboteur. Marta had known saboteurs — well, she had known petty vandals, and one poacher, and a bishop whose sermons sabotaged her Sundays — and they did not write thank-you notes. Saboteurs, in her long experience of human nature as observed over soup, were almost always people who wanted something for themselves. Ansel Vogel wanted nothing. He wound the telescope drives, he ate quietly, he thanked her for the kindness, and he watched Paloma Pemberton across the mess table with an expression Marta recognized instantly because she'd worn it herself at twenty-two, looking at a certain stonemason's apprentice who had died at thirty-one and taken most of her verbs with him.

It was longing. Plain as salt. And longing, whatever else you could say about it, kept a man's hands busy elsewhere.

Which meant somebody else's hands had stopped that clock.

* * *

The trouble with keeping silence, Marta discovered, was that silence is not a neutral substance. It weighs. It accumulates interest. Every day she said nothing, the silence grew heavier, and every day the noose around Ansel Vogel's neck — figurative, she reminded herself, *figurative*, though the valley had a long memory for what started figurative — drew tighter.

Then came the night the clock failed the second time, and Falk stood up at the head of the table with his knuckles white on the wood and accused Ansel Vogel to his face, in front of everyone, and Ansel just sat there taking it like a man who had expected worse and was relieved to be disappointed slowly. And Paloma Pemberton, the clockmaker girl, said nothing either. Marta caught her eye across the table — one flash of it — and saw something there that frightened her more than Ivo's notebook ever had: the girl *knew* something. You could tell. There was a look that people got when they were carrying a fact they hadn't decided whether to give birth to, and Paloma Pemberton had it, and she was sitting on it, and the whole table was pretending not to smell anything burning.

After dinner Marta cornered the girl the only way a cook can corner anyone: with food.

"You've eaten nothing but bread and stubbornness for three days," she said, pressing a bowl of stew into Paloma's hands in the corridor outside the mess. "Eat. Standing up if you must. My kitchen, my rules."

"Thank you, Frau Brunner." Paloma took the bowl but didn't eat. She looked like a girl who had been arguing with a ghost and losing.

"My mother," Marta said, apropos of nothing, because that was how she preferred to say everything, "used to tell me there are two kinds of silence. There's the silence you keep to protect yourself. And there's the silence you keep that protects the wrong person." She ladled extra dumpling into the girl's bowl without asking. "The first kind is survival. The second kind is a debt. And debts up here collect fast, because there's nowhere to run from your own kitchen."

Paloma looked up sharply. "Is that a general observation, Frau Brunner?"

"It's a stew," Marta said. "Eat it while it's hot."

But the girl wasn't stupid — nobody who could rebuild an escapement was stupid — and Marta saw the words land somewhere behind her eyes and begin doing whatever words do in clockmakers' heads, which was presumably turning gears.

* * *

It was the snowstorm that decided her.

The whiteout came down on the mountain two nights later like God shaking out a feather mattress, and somewhere in the middle of it Ansel and Paloma got themselves sealed into the clock room together — trapped, the story went, by the drift against the east door, stuck until morning. Marta packed them a basket: bread, cheese, a jar of pickled plums, two blankets, and — because she was fifty-eight and not dead — a small bottle of the plum brandy she officially did not keep under the flour bin. She carried it down the east corridor herself, past the pantry, past the stair landing where the low lamp burned, and she paused at the clock room door and listened.

Inside, faintly, two voices. Not arguing. Worse than arguing. *Talking.* The low, unhurried kind of talking that goes on all night and changes people.

Marta stood there in the drafty corridor with the basket handle biting her fingers, and she understood suddenly, with terrible clarity, that the silence she had been keeping was not protecting her position anymore. It had stopped being survival somewhere around the second failure of the clock and the accusation at dinner, and it had become the other kind — the kind that protected the wrong person. Because while she kept quiet, Ivo Steiner walked these corridors with his little ledger growing fat, and Herr Falk grew grayer, and Ansel Vogel grew quieter, and quiet men in valleys like this one had a way of staying quiet permanently if nobody spoke up.

Her mother had fed strangers first and asked questions after. Marta had spent her whole life assuming that was softness. Standing in the dark outside the clock room, listening to two voices finding each other through a wall of snow, it occurred to her for the first time that it might have been courage instead — the ordinary kind, the kind that looks like a loaf of bread.

She left the basket. She went back up to her kitchen. And she made a decision the way she made all important decisions: with her hands, at night, over dough that needed kneading anyway.

In the morning, the storm had blown itself hoarse but not out, and the mountain lay buried to its windowsills, and Marta Brunner climbed the stairs to the director's study with flour still on her apron and her heart going like a kneading machine, and knocked on Herr Falk's door.

He looked terrible. He looked like a man who had been sleeping in his chair and losing. "Frau Brunner," he said, "if this is about the coffee ration—"

"It's about the night your clock stopped," Marta said. "The first time. Four-seventeen in the morning. I was in the east corridor getting the butter crock, and I saw who went into the clock room with a key that wasn't theirs." She folded her arms, partly for courage, partly because her arms liked having opinions. "And it wasn't the foreigner."

Falk went very still. Outside the window, the wind dragged its sleeve across the glass. "Who," he said quietly, "did you see?"

"Ivo Steiner," said Marta. "Candle in one hand. Something small in the other. Let himself in and shut the door behind him like a man who'd practiced it." She met the director's eyes and did not flinch, though it cost her. "I've held my tongue since, Herr Falk, and I'm not proud of it. I told myself I was protecting my position. But there's two kinds of silence, and I've lately worked out which kind mine was. So now you know what I saw. What I don't know—" and here she leaned forward, and her voice dropped to the register she saved for bread that refused to rise, "—is why the girl hasn't said it first. Because Paloma Pemberton knows something too. I've cooked for enough families to know when someone's chewing on a truth they can't swallow. Whatever she's carrying, it's heavy, and it's about that clock. And I think, sir, that if you want the whole of it, you'll have to ask her gently, because some truths are like soufflés. They fall if you come at them loud."

Falk stared at her for a long moment. Then he passed a hand over his face, and beneath it, briefly, Marta glimpsed something that looked almost like relief — the relief of a man who had been carrying a suspicion alone and was handed, at last, a piece of evidence instead of a feeling.

"Thank you, Frau Brunner," he said.

"Don't thank me yet," Marta said grimly, turning for the door. "Thank me when that boy of yours doesn't hang his ledger on my foreigner. Because I'll tell you something else for free, Director: Ivo Steiner's been keeping a book. Movements. Hours. Little notes. He thinks nobody's seen it." She paused at the threshold, and the old kitchen wisdom rose in her like steam. "Men who keep books like that don't stop at writing. Sooner or later, they *present*. And when a frightened man presents his fear to authority dressed up as duty—" she shook her head, "—that's when mountains get people buried in them."

She went down to her kitchen and began the day's baking, hands moving, heart settling, the truth finally out of her and rising in the air of the house like the smell of fresh bread — impossible now to put back.

Upstairs, she knew, Herr Falk was already reaching for his coat.

And somewhere below, in a tower room, Ivo Steiner was closing the cover of a little black notebook.
