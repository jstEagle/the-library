## 15. The Ledger Goes to Falk

Ivo Reinhart had never in his life done anything that required a clean shirt after supper. He was, by his own honest accounting, a man of small habits and smaller ambitions: he observed the stars, he recorded what he saw, he wound what needed winding. The universe rewarded this sort of diligence with patterns. Stars kept their appointments. Comets returned when they promised. It was people who disappointed him — people, with their accents and their secrets and their hands that were never quite where their words said they should be.

So on the evening of the twelfth of December, Ivo dressed carefully. He put on the clean shirt, the one Marta had starched for Sundays, and he bound the ledger in brown paper as though it were a parcel of evidence, which in his heart it was. Then he stood outside the director's study for eleven minutes by the corridor clock — an observatory man measures everything — rehearsing.

*Herr Director. I have something you must see.*

No. Too theatrical.

*Herr Director. Forgive me, but there are matters concerning Herr Vogel—*

Too apologetic.

He settled at last on simply knocking, because courage, like an escapement, worked best when you stopped winding it and let it go.

"Enter." Falk's voice, always, sounded as though he were reading it aloud from a document slightly more official than the situation warranted.

The study smelled of pipe smoke and cold tea. Falk sat behind his desk with his spectacles pushed up into his thinning hair, and beside him — Ivo's stomach performed a slow, professional somersault — sat the typewriter. The old battered machine, its ribbon exposed like the gizzard of some mechanical bird, its keys worn shiny at E, T, N, and A. It seemed to watch him. Machines did not watch, of course. But Ivo had spent enough nights alone with telescopes to know that anything could acquire a gaze if you stared back long enough.

"Herr Reinhart." Falk removed his spectacles entirely, which was bad. Spectacles off meant the conversation was about to become human, and human conversations at this observatory had been going poorly all winter. "You wanted to see me. At nine in the evening. In your Sunday shirt."

"Yes, Herr Director."

Falk waited. Falk was very good at waiting; it was most of his job. Somewhere below them the master clock ticked on its new pin — Paloma Pemberton's repair, holding, for now — and the sound came up through the floorboards like a patient pulse.

Ivo set the brown-paper parcel on the desk between them.

"What is that?"

"A record, sir. Of movements." He heard how it sounded and pressed on before shame could catch him. "Of Herr Vogel's movements. Since the bridge closed. Hours at the telescope. Conversations. Notes passed with Marta. The night the master clock was found stopped — I have written down where everyone was, or claimed to be. I have been keeping it since November."

The silence that followed had texture to it, like felt. Falk looked at the parcel the way a man looks at a snake someone has just laid on his desk.

"You have been *keeping* it."

"Yes, sir."

"For how long?"

"Six weeks, sir. Nearly seven."

Falk leaned back in his chair until it creaked — a sound Ivo knew well, the sound of the director's conscience changing position. "And why," Falk said slowly, "does a junior astronomer with no authority over personnel, over security, over anything larger than a notebook of star positions, believe it his duty to spy upon a colleague?"

And here it was, the question Ivo had spent six weeks not asking himself, arriving now at the worst possible moment, wearing Falk's tired voice. He had prepared answers. He had prepared three of them, written out in his head in fair copy. None survived contact with the director's face.

"Because nobody else was doing it," Ivo said, and heard immediately that it was both entirely true and entirely damning.

Falk took off his reading glasses altogether and rubbed the bridge of his nose. "Sit down, Reinhart."

Ivo sat.

"I am going to ask you some questions," Falk said, "and I want you to understand that I am not your enemy. Do you understand? Whatever you think is happening on this mountain — whatever you have convinced yourself — I am not your enemy."

"Yes, Herr Director."

"When did you first suspect Herr Vogel?"

Ivo's hands found each other in his lap and held on. "When the clock was found stopped. At 4:17. Everyone else went to bed worried about the observations. He — Vogel — he asked questions. Good ones. Precise ones. As though he already knew the answer and wanted to hear us say it wrong."

"He is an assistant astronomer," Falk said flatly. "Asking precise questions is the entire job description. By that standard I should arrest myself, and also the barometer."

"It isn't only that." The words were coming faster now, the way water comes faster near the lip of a weir. "It's the letters, sir. He writes letters and gives them to Marta to hold. It's the way he watches the road. Every day, twice a day, from the north window — I have logged it, it's in there, forty-one instances — watching the bridge. A man who means no harm does not count the days until the bridge opens like a prisoner counting scratches on a wall."

"A man whose family is on the other side of that bridge," said Falk quietly, "counts exactly like that. I would. Wouldn't you?"

Ivo had not considered this. It landed somewhere beneath his ribs and stayed there, uncomfortable, like a swallowed stone. "His country is at war with ours," he said, falling back on the solid ground. "That is not suspicion. That is a fact. He is an enemy national stranded inside a national institution, with access to instruments, to correspondence, to—" he gestured helplessly at the typewriter, "—to communications. When the authorities come in spring, they will ask why we did nothing. They will ask *you*, sir. And I did not want this observatory to be caught without a record. Without proof that someone was watching."

"Watching," repeated Falk. "You keep using that word as though it were clean."

The typewriter sat between them, saying nothing, which was unlike it. Ivo had come to think of the machine as the truest resident of the study — older than Falk's tenure, stained with tea rings, capable of producing promises that outlived their maker. He did not know about any promises it had made. He only knew that it made him uneasy, and that everything making him uneasy lately wore Vogel's face.

"Open it," Falk said at last.

Ivo untied the string with fingers that had gone clumsy. The ledger fell open to a page near the middle — he had not chosen it, but he flinched anyway when he saw the entry:

*Dec. 3 — V. at telescope 6 hrs 40 min (longer than assigned). Spoke w/ M. in kitchen passage, 4 min. She gave him something wrapped in cloth. Bread? Also: P.P. entered clock room 11 p.m., did not emerge till 3 a.m. Alone together? No witness.*

He watched Falk read it. He watched the director's jaw tighten — at the entry, or at the handwriting, or at the man who had produced it, Ivo could not tell.

"'Alone together? No witness,'" Falk read aloud, in a voice like a scalpel. "Reinhart. You have written down that two adults spent an evening repairing a clock, and you have appended a question mark."

"The hours are irregular, sir."

"The *hours are winter*, boy! What do you imagine people do in December at four thousand meters?" Falk pushed the ledger away from him as though it smelled. And then, because he was Falk — fair, exhausted, cornered by a season — he picked it up again and read further, page after page, while Ivo sat in the chair across from him feeling each entry leave his own body and turn into something he no longer recognized. Six weeks of vigilance, spread out under lamplight, looked different than it had in the keeping. In the keeping it had been armor. On the desk it looked like — he searched for the word and found it and hated it — it looked like gossip with a ruler applied.

"There is good work in here," Falk said finally, and Ivo's heart lifted stupidly before the rest of the sentence arrived. "Times. Dates. Durations. You are a meticulous observer, Reinhart, it is why I hired you." He closed the ledger. "Now tell me what conclusion your meticulous observation supports. Not what you fear. Fear is not data. What do the numbers say?"

And there it was again, the stone under his ribs. Because the numbers said nothing. The numbers said a man worked long hours, missed his family, ate bread given by a kind cook, and spent evenings with the clockmaker while she fixed the clock. Six weeks of observation and the sum total of his evidence was a foreign accent and a coincidence of geography.

But underneath the numbers lived the thing the numbers couldn't weigh: the war, declared by courier in November and since sealed up in the valley like air in a jar. The word *enemy* spoken once by Falk himself and then forbidden. The way terror behaves when it is not allowed out to walk — it digs. Ivo had dug. Every hour in the ledger was a shovelful.

"They say nothing," Ivo admitted, barely above a whisper. "The numbers say nothing. But sir — if I am wrong, then we have lost nothing. And if I am right, and we have done nothing—"

"Then in spring men with badges climb that road and ask why an enemy national spent five months with the run of a national observatory, and the answer will be 'because one junior astronomer wrote it in a diary.'" Falk stood. He walked to the window, where snow was coming down with the soft indifference of weather that has no opinion about human affairs. His reflection looked older than the man casting it. "Do you know what happens to this observatory, Reinhart, if scandal comes over that bridge with the thaw? The funding committee meets in April. There are three institutions competing for the same grant. A whisper of espionage — a *whisper* — and we are finished. Forty years of star catalogues, finished, so that you could feel safe in your bed."

"So you'll do nothing." It escaped before he could stop it, hot and young and disrespectful, and Ivo flinched at his own voice.

Falk turned around. To his surprise, there was no anger on the director's face. There was something worse. There was recognition.

"No," Falk said. "I will not do nothing. That is the trouble with men like you and me, Reinhart — we are constitutionally incapable of doing nothing, and so the world makes us its instruments." He came back to the desk and stood over the ledger with his hands flat on either side of it, a man framing a decision the way one frames a difficult print. "Here is what will happen. Your ledger stays with me. You will not add another page. You will not speak of it to anyone — not to the cook, not to the Pemberton girl, not to God in chapel. If you do, I will know, and your career at any observatory in this country ends before it has properly begun. Am I understood?"

"Yes, sir." Relief and dread arrived together, twins.

"And Herr Vogel." Falk exhaled through his nose, long and slow, smoke without a pipe. "Until the bridge opens and the civil authorities can make their own judgment — a judgment made by law rather than by laundry lists — Herr Vogel will be confined to the east tower."

Ivo's mouth opened. Closed. He had imagined this moment for weeks — had rehearsed it, actually, standing at his washbasin: *Vogel taken away, the corridor lighter, the valley safer.* Now that it had happened he felt as though he'd broken something valuable and been thanked for it.

"The tower, sir? It's — the stairs are hardly used, and the heating—"

"Is poor. Yes. So is the heating in the barracks at the front, I'm told, and nobody chose to be there." Falk straightened the ledger's corners with one finger, a fussy gesture that betrayed him completely. "It is confinement, Reinhart, not punishment. He will have his books, his coat, meals brought up. He will have a lock on the door and my word that no harm comes to him while he is behind it. And in spring, when the road opens, the authorities will read whatever statements exist and decide. That is called procedure. It is the difference between a civilization and a mob, and I would thank you to remember which side of that line your notebook nearly carried us across."

"Yes, Herr Director."

"Go to bed."

Ivo rose, tucked in his chair, got as far as the door. His hand was on the handle when Falk spoke again, quieter.

"Why the east tower, do you suppose, out of everywhere on this mountain?"

"Because it's secure, sir?"

"Because," Falk said, "it is the room farthest from the clock. I thought about that for a long time tonight, Reinhart. A man who loved this observatory would want to be near its heart, even locked up. Especially locked up." He put his spectacles back on, and the human being disappeared behind the official. "Good night."

* * *

Ivo did not go to bed.

He told himself he would. He climbed the stairs, passed the kitchen where Marta was banking the stove — she looked up at him with those flat gray eyes that missed nothing and forgave less, and he nodded and hurried past like a burglar in his own house — and he reached his room and sat on the bed with his boots still on.

Below him, faintly, he heard voices. Falk's, measured. Then another voice, lower, accented, rising once in something that might have been protest and settling again into something worse: acceptance. Ansel Vogel did not argue. That was the thing about him that had frightened Ivo from the beginning, though he had never managed to write it in the ledger because it wasn't observable. Guilty men argued. Innocent men argued too. But Vogel simply listened, the way a man listens when he has been listening his whole life to doors closing in other countries, and has learned that the sound is easier to bear standing still.

Then footsteps on iron stairs, receding upward, toward the east tower. A door. A lock — the old lock, stiff, taking two tries.

Silence, except for the clock. The clock went on ticking through all of it, indifferent as snowfall, keeping its repaired time with Paloma Pemberton's pin in its heart, and Ivo sat on his bed in the dark and discovered that victory felt exactly like the flu: hot, ashamed, and certain to get worse before morning.

He thought about the entry he hadn't written. The one from tonight. *Dec. 12 — V. confined, east tower. Witness: self.* He thought about how the page would look in April, under lamps, in front of men with badges. He thought about Marta leaving bread at a locked door, and about the Pemberton girl's face at dinner whenever the conversation bent toward Vogel — not softness, exactly. Recognition. As though she were reading a letter she already knew by heart.

He lay back and stared at the ceiling, where the frost had grown ferns in the corners, and he told himself the sentence he would need for the next hundred nights: *If I am wrong, we have lost nothing.*

Somewhere below, in the clock room, a light was burning that had no business burning at midnight. Ivo heard, or thought he heard, the small bright music of tiny tools against tiny metal, and he did not sleep at all.
