## 16. Escape Is Not the Answer

The keys were in the third drawer of my tool case, beneath a felt roll of pinions and a spare mainspring, and they had been there for three days before I admitted to myself what I was going to do with them.

Falk had given them to me on my second night at the observatory — "every lock on this mountain, Miss Pemberton, in case the clock demands attention at an hour when no one wishes to climb stairs for you" — with the careless generosity of a man who trusted his staff completely and had never once been wrong to. There were eleven keys on the ring. The smallest opened the east tower.

I sat at my workbench in the clock room with the key in my palm and did the arithmetic, because arithmetic was what I did when I could not bear to do anything else. The guards — there was one guard, young Lukas, who mostly guarded his own boredom — changed watch at midnight. Between midnight and two, the stairwell to the tower was unobserved; I had checked, not because I intended anything, but because a clockmaker checks everything, and then lies to herself about why. The tower door's lock was an old Bramah, temperamental but honest. My mother's tools could persuade it in under a minute. From the tower, the roofline of the north wing ran to the ice-house, and from the ice-house the drifts came within a body's length of the tree line, and from the trees a man with a head start and a grudge against gravity could be over the pass before anyone thought to look.

Ansel Vogel could be gone by Thursday. By Friday, no one would be able to prove he had ever stood trial at all.

I put the key back in the drawer. Then I took it out again. Then I put it back and closed the drawer with more force than the drawer deserved, so that the whole case rang like a struck bell.

"You'll wake the dead, doing that," said Marta, from the doorway, with a tray of bread and cold sausage she had ostensibly brought for me and had actually brought for everyone, since Marta's trays had a way of passing by certain doors on their way past mine.

"I'm working."

"At midnight." She set the tray down and looked at me the way she looked at a soufflé that had begun to think about collapsing. "In this house we are all working at midnight lately. It's very industrious, being frightened."

"Marta—"

"I say nothing," she said, which was Marta's way of saying a great deal. "Only that the bread is fresh, and that some people eat better than others in this observatory, and that if anyone asked me how such a thing happened, I would have no idea whatsoever." She gathered her empty cups, paused at the door, and added, without turning around: "The tower gets very cold at night. Whatever one might be doing up there — or not doing — one would want to be quick about it. And quiet. Lukas sleeps like a stone but dreams loudly."

And she was gone, leaving me holding a heel of bread and the distinct impression that I had just been conscripted into something.

* * *

The trouble with being raised by a clockmaker — or in my case, by two clockmakers, one of whom turned out after her death to have been rather more than a clockmaker — is that every moral question arrives already disassembled on your bench, its parts laid out in order, waiting for you to find the flaw. And I had found the flaw. I had found it the moment I imagined Ansel's face when I opened the tower door, and imagined him saying *no*.

Because that was the thing, wasn't it. He would say no. Or worse, he would say yes — yes, get me out, and thank you, and God keep you — and then he would be a fugitive, and I would be an accomplice, and the letter in my mother's careful hand would stop being a promise and become evidence. *Shelter and safe passage*, she had typed, in peacetime, to a family who had fed her when she was stranded with a broken axle in a town whose name meant nothing to either of us. Shelter and safe passage — not escape. Not conspiracy. A promise made in daylight cannot survive being kept by moonlight. The moment Ansel ran, every kindness anyone had shown him — Marta's bread, Falk's hesitation, even my silence about the lead pin — would curdle into proof. Flight was a confession you wrote in someone else's hand.

And underneath all the arithmetic, quieter and less forgivable: I wanted to open that door because opening doors is what I am *for*. Give me a seized mechanism and I am happy for a week. But this was not a seized mechanism. This was a man, and men do not forgive being solved the way locks do.

I went up anyway. Not with the keys. With the bread.

* * *

The east tower smelled of cold iron and pigeon, and the moon through the arrow-slit laid a stripe across the floor like a ruler measuring how long the night intended to be. Ansel sat on the cot with his back to the wall, and when my shadow fell across the stripe he was on his feet before I had finished climbing the last stair — quickly, quietly, the way a man moves who has spent months being careful where he puts his hands.

"It's me," I said, unnecessarily. "Paloma."

"Yes." He didn't come closer. He had learned, in the days since the ledger and the lock, exactly how much distance a prisoner is permitted to close, and he observed the regulation scrupulously, which broke my heart slightly more than pleading would have. "You shouldn't be here. If someone saw—"

"No one saw. Marta timed it." I held up the bread. "I've brought contraband. Caraway. Try not to make a scene."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth — not quite a smile, but the ground where one might build. He took the bread and sat again, and I sat on the step below the door, because sitting on the cot was not something I was prepared to explain to myself, let alone to Ivo's ledger.

"They feed me," he said, nodding at the bread. "Adequately."

"Marta says adequately means starving."

"Marta is correct." He tore the loaf and handed half back to me, which was absurd, since I had brought it, but I took it, because refusing would have been its own kind of cruelty. We ate in the dark like two children hiding from a governess. Somewhere below us, the master clock ticked through its repaired escapement, steady as a pulse, and I realized I could hear it and he could hear it and we were both listening to the same heart.

"I need to tell you what I can do," I said finally. "And then I need you to tell me why I shouldn't."

He went very still. That was the thing about Ansel — stillness came to him naturally, the way it comes to men who have practiced disappearing inside rooms. "You have keys," he said. Not a question. Of course not a question; he'd watched me carry that tool case for weeks.

"I have keys, I know the roofline, and between midnight and two no one is watching the stairs." I heard my own voice go brisk and technical, the voice I used for quoting estimates, as though this were a job of work I was bidding on. "By Friday you could be over the pass. By Sunday, invisible."

"And you would be—" He stopped. Set down the bread. "Miss Pemberton. Paloma. What happens to you, on Friday?"

"Nothing happens to me. No one watches the clockmaker's daughter. I'm furniture."

"You are the person who let him out," he said gently. "That is not furniture. That is a name on a list, and I promise you" — and here his voice did something complicated, something that had crossed a border or two itself — "I know what lists look like when they are finished."

"The alternative is spring. The bridge opens, the officials come, you surrender, and you stand in front of strangers who have already decided—"

"—who have already decided nothing," he said. "Or who may yet decide nothing. I don't know. You don't know. That is the entire horror of it." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and the moon-stripe cut across his hands. "When my father received your mother's letter, he read it aloud at the table. Twice. He said — this family does not know us. They owe us nothing. And still they wrote it down, in ink, where anyone could read it. He said that was the bravest kind of promise. The kind made where it can be seen."

"He typed it," I said. "On the director's machine. She always typed first and signed after. She said typing was thinking and signing was meaning."

"Then she meant it." He looked at me, and there was no plea in it, which was somehow worse. "If I run, her letter becomes a crime scene. Every word of it turns to evidence against the woman who wrote it. And against the daughter carrying it. I will not do that to her. I will not do it to you."

"So you'd rather sit here until spring and take your chances with men who call you *the foreigner* to your face?"

"I would rather," he said, "not make your mother a liar."

The clock ticked below us. Forty-one minutes past midnight. I thought about my mother — the real one, the one from before the letter, who burned toast and hummed off-key and once spent an entire Sunday teaching six-year-old me to true a wheel by feel alone — and I understood suddenly that she had known exactly what she was doing. She hadn't promised shelter because she was naïve. She had promised it because promises are load-bearing. Take them away and the whole structure comes down, and everyone inside it with it.

"All right," I said. "Then we don't run."

"We?"

"*We.* Because if you're standing trial in the spring, you're not standing there alone with a ledger full of Ivo's suspicions and a sabotaged clock hanging around your neck. I'm going to prove you didn't touch it. I'm going to prove who did. And I'm going to do it with gears, which is the only language this mountain respects." I got up, brushed the crumbs from my skirt, and immediately sat back down, because standing made me feel like I was leaving and I wasn't finished. "But I need something from you first."

"Anything."

"Don't say anything. People in towers always say anything, and then the anything turns out to be impossible." I took a breath. "The lead pin. In the escapement. I told you I found it. Soft metal, cut clean, installed to fail at 4:17. Whoever did it used an observatory tool — a graver, worn left-handed. Ivo is left-handed. His log claims he was asleep."

Ansel had gone from stillness to something beyond stillness — the stillness of a man watching a fuse. "You've known this," he said carefully, "for how long?"

"Nine days."

"Nine days." He laughed, once, without any humor in it at all. "Nine days I have sat in this room while they looked at me, while Falk looked at me, while you—" He stopped himself, visibly, the way a man stops himself on ice. "Why? Why would you carry that alone?"

"Because I wasn't sure. Because suspicion isn't proof, and if I named Ivo and I was wrong, I'd have done to him exactly what they're doing to you. Because—" And here was the truth, the ugly little worm at the center of the apple, and I made myself say it. "Because part of me hoped if I stayed quiet long enough, the problem would solve itself. Run away. Get lost. Be somebody else's clock to fix."

"That is the most honest thing anyone in this observatory has said to me," Ansel said, "including me."

"So I'm asking you to wait nine more days. Maybe ten. Let me finish the proof. The wear pattern on the graver, the log, the minute itself — 4:17, when the sky was clear and every telescope in this building should have been recording, and only Ivo's wasn't. When I have all of it, I put it in front of Falk with the clock running, and let him watch it strike a true hour while he reads."

"And if Falk doesn't listen? If the valley has already decided?" He gestured at the walls, the slit of window, the whole vertical fact of his imprisonment. "Paloma. Men have been locked in towers for less than a ledger and released for less than a demonstration."

"Then we'll know that too," I said. "And knowing is worth more than guessing. Even when it's terrible. Especially when it's terrible. My mother used to say—" I stopped. Swallowed. Started again, because apparently inheritance works like that, arriving in your mouth at the worst possible moments. "She used to say a clockmaker's first duty is to tell the truth about what time it is. Not the time you wish it were."

The moon had moved its stripe the width of two fingers across the floor. Down the mountain, wind worried at the ice-house eaves. Ansel looked at me for a long moment, and whatever he was weighing, he weighed it the way I weighed a balance wheel — patiently, and with both hands.

"Ten days," he said. "And if you fail?"

"I won't fail."

"That is not an answer."

"It's the only kind I have." I stood, and this time I stayed standing. "I'll bring bread tomorrow. Marta sends her regards, and by Marta's regards I mean half her kitchen. Don't make a scene."

"Paloma." He was on his feet too now, at the exact limit of what the room allowed him. "Your mother's letter. The promise. If it ends with me in front of a tribunal — if it ends badly — do you regret that she wrote it?"

I thought of the typed page in my tool case, the ribbon-warm machine upstairs, the woman I was only beginning to actually meet four years after burying her.

"No," I said. "But ask me again in the spring."

I climbed down the stairs with the bread crumbs still on my sleeve and the keys still in their drawer, untouched, and it was the hardest repair I had ever declined to make. Below me, right on the hour, the master clock struck twice — a clean, unhurried pair of notes rolling through the dark observatory like a word spoken clearly in a noisy room.

Two o'clock. Time enough, if I hurried, to catch Falk before he slept — or at least to leave the first page of my findings under his door, in handwriting steadier than I felt.

The proof wouldn't build itself. And neither, I was learning, would the truth.
