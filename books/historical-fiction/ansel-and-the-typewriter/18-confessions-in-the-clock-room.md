## 18. Confessions in the Clock Room

Ivo Brandt had always believed that a man's conscience was like a telescope: you pointed it outward, at the world, at the stars, at other people's errors — and it never once occurred to him to swing the thing around and look into the wrong end.

Now, standing in the clock room with every soul in the observatory staring at him, he had a very clear view of the inside of the lens. It was smudged.

Paloma Pemberton held up the lead pin between finger and thumb, turning it slowly so that the lamplight caught along its cut face. She had been talking for some minutes — about files, about grip, about the particular wear pattern left on brass by a left-handed hand — and her voice had the pleasant, unhurried tone of someone explaining arithmetic to a child who has just gotten the sum badly wrong in public.

"—and of course," she was saying, "the pin was installed at four-seventeen in the morning, which is precisely when your observation log says you were asleep, Herr Ivo." She looked at him then, and there was no cruelty in her face, which somehow made it worse. "You wrote *slept soundly* in your own hand. I've read it three times. It's very tidy."

"I have tidy handwriting," Ivo said, before his brain could stop his mouth. It was possibly the least useful sentence ever spoken aloud in the history of astronomy.

Falk stepped forward. The director had not spoken since Paloma had laid out her evidence on the workbench like a surgeon laying out instruments, but now his voice came out low and gravelled, as though it had been dragged over rocks on its way up from wherever directors keep their worst words. "Ivo. Is this true?"

And here was the moment — Ivo saw it coming the way one sees a wave coming, from far off, growing all the while — where he could lie. He was, after all, rather good at small lies by now. He had told himself a great many: that he was protecting the observatory, that he was being vigilant, that a ledger full of another man's hours was patriotism rather than spite. He had polished those lies nightly the way one polishes an eyepiece, until they seemed almost to show something true.

But the clock room was warm from the stove, and Marta stood by the door with flour still on her wrists, and Ansel Vogel sat on the bench where he'd been brought up from the east tower, thin and patient and looking at Ivo not with hatred but with — and this was the unbearable part — with *recognition*, the way one astronomer looks at another through a bad night.

"I stopped the clock," Ivo said.

His own voice sounded strange to him, as if it belonged to someone else, someone smaller.

* * *

He talked because once he had started he could not find the handle to shut himself off.

"It was me," he said again, stupidly, and then, because the silence demanded more, "The first night. Before Miss Pemberton arrived. I went down at four o'clock — I'd checked the duty roster, no one walks past the clock room at that hour, no one ever does — and I took the escapement apart and I replaced the pallet pin with lead. I filed it myself. In my room. With the door locked." He heard how it sounded, said plainly: a boy locking his door to commit a crime against a clock. "I used the observatory file. The small one. From the third drawer."

"You knew we'd summon outside help," Falk said.

"Yes." His voice cracked on it. "That was the entire — yes. A broken master clock means a telegram, and a telegram means a clockmaker comes up the mountain, and a clockmaker means—" He gestured helplessly at Paloma, at the whole arrangement of events that had delivered them all into this room. "I thought if the mountain sent us someone, the valley would send someone too. Officials. Someone who would look at Herr Vogel properly and ask the questions nobody here would ask. Everyone kept saying *wait for the spring, wait for the inquiry*, and I couldn't—"

"Couldn't what?" Falk asked quietly.

"*Wait!*" It came out of him louder than he meant, bouncing off the pendulum case, and Marta flinched, and Ivo hated himself a little more. "Don't you understand? War. There is a *war*, and there is a man sleeping down the corridor from me whose country is shooting at mine, and everyone in this building keeps winding clocks and cooking bread and behaving as though the world hasn't turned upside down, and I am supposed to simply — to simply *observe*?" He laughed, high and awful. "I'm an observer. That's the whole joke, isn't it. I observe things. I observed him for weeks. Every hour at the telescope, every note with Marta—"

"The notes were shopping lists," Marta said flatly, from the doorway. "Salt. Thread. Once, a recipe. For strudel."

"They could have been anything!"

"They were strudel," Marta said, with the finality of a woman who has buried three husbands' worth of nonsense in her time, and Ivo felt the ground tilt under him.

* * *

Because here was the truth of it, the truth he had kept in the dark drawer of himself alongside the file and the ledger: Ivo Brandt was afraid, and fear had gotten into him the way damp gets into stone, invisibly and everywhere, and he had dressed the fear up each morning in the clean uniform of virtue and called it duty. He had written *enemy national* in his neat hand forty times and never once written *I am frightened*. He had measured Ansel Vogel's hours as though measurement were protection, as though a ledger were a wall, as though if he only recorded enough of the foreigner's ordinary days the recording itself would hold back the war.

It hadn't. The war had come anyway, over the pass, on the last courier's sledge, folded into a newspaper six days old. And Ivo had discovered that a ledger cannot stop artillery, and that frightened men who cannot fight anything real will fight whatever is nearest — and what had been nearest was a quiet man with careful hands who wound the telescope drives every evening and thanked Marta for the bread.

"I thought you'd confess," Ansel said.

Everyone turned. He had barely moved; he sat with his wrists loose in his lap, a prisoner entirely by consent, and his voice was mild as evening.

"At dinner," Ansel went on. "When you asked where I learned German. You watched me the way my father watches a horse he thinks is lame. I thought — either this man will report me, or this man will crack, and either way the waiting would end." He shrugged slightly. "I preferred the waiting. But I understood it. Fear makes people do arithmetic badly. My father always says that. He was an accountant before the debts."

"Your father," Ivo said, and couldn't finish it, because the sentence he wanted was *your father is a good man* and he had no right to it.

"He is," Ansel agreed anyway. "Which is why I know what fear costs. We paid it for years."

* * *

Falk had gone to the window. Snow stood deep and blue in the last light, and beyond it the world did its enormous indifferent business of wintering. When the director finally turned around, Ivo saw that he had aged a decade in twenty minutes, or perhaps had merely stopped pretending otherwise.

"Do you understand what you've done?" Falk asked. "Not the clock. The clock is brass and steel; Miss Pemberton will mend it by Thursday. I mean the rest. You spent six weeks building a case against an innocent man. You cost this observatory its autumn observations. You cost Vogel his liberty — such as it was. And you did it all while writing *slept soundly* in your logbook, which I must say takes a certain nerve."

"Yes, sir."

"Don't *sir* me. Sir is a word for men you respect." Falk rubbed his eyes. "Here is what will happen. When the bridge opens, the officials will come, and they will hear about Vogel, and they may as well hear about you in the same breath. I am not going to hide a sabotage trial inside an alien-enemy hearing like a bad stitch under good cloth. This house has told enough lies for one winter."

"And until then?" Marta asked.

"Until then, Herr Brandt will take Vogel's duties in the tower. All of them. Including the drives, including the coal, including the four o'clock winding." Falk's mouth did something that was almost, distantly, a smile, though it hurt to look at. "You wanted to watch him so closely. Now you'll do his work and learn exactly how innocent it all was. And Ivo — you'll write *awake* in your log, every night, honestly, even when it's dull. Especially when it's dull."

Ivo nodded. His throat wouldn't manage anything larger. He looked at Ansel, who was watching him with that terrible gentleness, and heard himself say, "I'll wind them properly. I know how you do it now. I counted." And then he wished the floor would open, because *I counted* was perhaps the most damning thing he'd said all evening, and he had said quite a lot of damning things.

Ansel, however, almost smiled. "Then you know the third drive sticks. Give it a quarter turn extra and a tap."

"A tap?"

"With the heel of your hand. Firmly. Like waking a slow child." Ansel paused. "It works on most things. Even, I think, on astronomers."

Marta snorted. Paloma Pemberton, gathering her pins and files into their roll of leather, made a sound that might have been a laugh swallowed whole. Only Falk remained grim, and rightly so, because — as he said, pulling his coat on — pity was easy at sundown, but the officials would arrive with the bridge, and they would bring forms, and forms have no humor in them at all.

"One more thing," Falk said at the door. "Vogel, you're free to walk the observatory again. Confined to the grounds, nothing more. I'll not keep a man locked in a tower who surrendered voluntarily while a saboteur winds clocks beside me." He looked around the room once, at all of them — the clockmaker, the cook, the broken young astronomer, the freed prisoner — and shook his head slowly. "A house is like a clock, apparently. Everything depends on the smallest parts behaving."

He left. Marta followed, announcing supper as though the evening were an ordinary one, which was her particular genius. Paloma lingered, rolling the lead pin between her fingers, and gave Ivo a long considering look.

"For what it's worth," she said, "your filing was very clean. Left-handed or not."

"That's not worth anything," Ivo said.

"No," she agreed pleasantly. "But it's true, and you should get used to those."

She left too, and Ansel rose stiffly, rubbing his wrists, and paused beside Ivo at the door. For a moment neither spoke. Then Ansel said, quietly, "My father forgives slowly and remembers everything. I forgive quickly and forget most things. Between the two of us, we manage." He tapped the third drive's housing as he passed — firmly, with the heel of his hand — and went out into the blue evening toward the tower he no longer had to sleep in.

Ivo stayed behind alone with the clocks. They ticked around him, dozens of small honest hearts, none of them aware of what had nearly been done to their mistress. He found the logbook and uncapped his pen, and after a long moment wrote, in his tidy hand:

*4:17 p.m. Awake.*

Then, because it was true and he was trying to get used to those, he added beneath it:

*Ashamed. Beginning repairs.*

Out the window, the first star was up, and somewhere below, faintly, he could hear the master clock — mended, running, forgiven nothing and everything — striking five.
