## 21. What the Valley Decided

The inquiry lasted four days, which everyone agreed was remarkably swift for a matter involving treason, sabotage, a master clock, and one very thorough ledger.

It was held in the observatory's library — the only room in the building with enough chairs and not enough telescopes — and it was conducted by a magistrate named Herr Brandt, who had climbed the mountain road on the first day of spring with snowmelt soaking his good boots and an expression suggesting he'd rather have been anywhere else in the empire. He was a round, patient man who smelled of pipe tobacco and ink, and within an hour of arriving he had done two things that changed the shape of everything: he asked to see the escapement of the master clock, and he asked Paloma to explain, in her own words, how a pin comes to be made of lead.

"The rest of you," he said mildly, settling into Falk's chair as though chairs were meant to be settled into, "may listen or not, as you please."

Paloma stood at the head of the long table with her tools laid out on a cloth like a surgeon's instruments, and she told them.

She told them about soft metal and hard metal, about how a lead pin wears smooth in days where a steel one would last a lifetime, about the direction of tool marks and what they say about the hand that made them. She held up the ruined pin between finger and thumb, small as a grass seed, and turned it slowly in the light from the tall windows.

"Whoever cut this used our bench shear," she said. "The blade has a nick here — see, it leaves this little step on every piece it cuts. I found the same step on scrap brass in the workroom bin. And the grip wear on the shear handle is left-sided. The handle is polished on the left of center and dull on the right." She paused. "There are eleven people wintering at this observatory. Ten of them are right-handed."

The silence that followed had a texture to it. Paloma did not look at Ivo. Looking at him would have been cruelty, and she had decided, somewhere in the long white nights, that there was already enough of that going around.

Herr Brandt turned the pin over twice, put it down with the delicacy of a man handling someone else's grief, and said, "And the hour? Why 4:17?"

"Because at 4:17 the night assistant's log says he was asleep," Paloma said. "I checked the handwriting against his observation records. The entry was added later — the ink is different, the pen pressure heavier. Someone wanted a clock stopped at a minute they could account for." She folded her cloth over her tools, corner to corner, the way her mother had taught her. "A clock doesn't lie about when things happened. That's rather the whole point of clocks."

* * *

They cleared Ansel on the third day.

It took less than an hour, in the end. Herr Brandt read the statement Paloma had typed in March on her mother's battered typewriter — read it twice, running one thick finger under the lines — then read Ansel's own papers, then asked him perhaps twenty questions, most of them about his father's trade and the route he'd taken over the mountains, questions that sounded like small talk and were nothing of the kind. Ansel answered all of them in careful, accented German, standing straight as a pendulum rod, and when Brandt asked him why he had come to the observatory at all, knowing what his homeland was becoming, Ansel looked across the table at Paloma and said:

"My father received a letter once. It promised shelter. I came to find out if promises were still kept here."

Brandt wrote something down. Then he closed his notebook and said, "The letter is not legally binding. No court in the empire would recognize it." And then, before anyone could react: "But courts recognize what actually happened, and what actually happened is that this young man surrendered himself to officials the moment the road opened, though God knows the tower window is large enough for a slim fellow to climb through, and the snowpack below would have broken his fall. What actually happened is that he stayed." He gathered his papers. "I find no grounds for any charge. You're free to go wherever you like, young man."

*Wherever you like.* Four words, and the whole valley seemed to lean in to hear which way they'd tip.

Ivo's ending came quieter than anyone expected, which was somehow worse. On the fourth day Brandt met with Falk behind closed doors for forty minutes, and afterward Falk came down the corridor to the workroom where Paloma was oiling the strike train, and he sat heavily on her stool, uninvited, and rubbed his face with both hands.

"Ivo is leaving," he said. "This week. I've written to his old professor in Zürich — there's a position, computing lunar tables, respectable work, no telescope time required." He dropped his hands and looked at her. "No charges. His family need never know more than that the mountain didn't suit him."

"You're being kind," Paloma said carefully, because she wasn't sure yet whether she approved.

"I'm being practical." Falk sighed. "If I report him, there's an inquiry, and inquiries have a way of asking why the director didn't notice his own assistant keeping a ledger for three months. And besides—" he paused, choosing the words like a man crossing thin ice— "he's twenty-two years old and terrified. Fear makes fools of clever people. I've seen it do worse things than stop a clock." He stood, steadied himself on the doorframe. "Marta is making a cake tonight. She informed me of this in the tone she uses when it isn't optional. I suggest attendance."

He got as far as the door before turning back.

"Herr Pemberton. Your mother—" He stopped. Started again. "I knew her, you know. Slightly. Before you were born. She came up here with a case of tools and fixed something no one else could fix, and she argued with me about star charts until two in the morning, and I thought at the time: there is a person who will never once do the easy wrong thing." His voice went rough at the edges. "I see now where you learned it. Or half of you did. The other half, I suspect, is entirely your own."

Then he left, and Paloma sat alone in the workroom with the strike train gleaming under her hands, and let herself cry for exactly as long as it took the third wheel to finish one full revolution, because a clockmaker knows better than anyone that some motions must be permitted their full arc.

* * *

Falk's offers came at dinner, delivered with the ceremonial gravity of a man announcing a comet.

"I have," he said, tapping his spoon against his glass, "two pieces of business. First: the observatory requires a new assistant astronomer, as the previous occupant of that post has discovered a vocation for arithmetic elsewhere. Second: the observatory requires a clockmaker of permanent standing, as I am tired of writing telegrams that begin *the master clock has again failed* and end with my superiors asking whether we might simply buy an alarm clock like ordinary people."

He looked at Ansel first.

"Vogel. Your hearing is concluded, your name is cleared, and your country—" he chose the next word with visible care— "your country's situation remains what it is. I cannot promise the road stays open. I can promise a salary, a bed, and work worth doing. If you want it, it's yours."

Ansel set down his fork. For a moment he was silent, and Paloma watched his hands — those careful, telescope-winding hands — turn slightly upward on the table, open, as though weighing something invisible.

"When I left home," he said slowly, "my father told me that a man who accepts shelter should earn it, or it curdles into debt. I would not take charity, Herr Falk. But work—" and here something loosened in his shoulders, something that had been clenched since the day the bridge closed— "work I can give you until my arms fail."

"Good," said Falk briskly, before the moment could become emotional and therefore unbearable. "You start Monday. Marta, second business."

Marta, who had been pretending great interest in the bread basket, snorted.

"And Pemberton." Falk turned to Paloma, and his voice did something it almost never did, which was soften without breaking. "Permanent post. Your own workshop — the east room, with the south light. Salary negotiable, hours terrible, view unmatched. I'm told you argue about star charts too, which I consider a qualification."

Paloma had known the offer was coming. She had rehearsed, in the privacy of the clock room, several gracious and professional responses.

What came out instead was: "Does the east room have room for a typewriter?"

Falk blinked. "I— presumably. Why?"

"Because I intend to keep one there," Paloma said, "and use it regularly, and I don't intend to explain myself to anyone who asks."

Down the table, Marta hid a smile in the teapot. Ansel caught Paloma's eye, and neither of them smiled exactly, but something passed between them that was warmer than smiling, and Falk, observing it with the expression of a man watching weather he had personally ordered, said, "Done. Both of you. Now eat your cake before Marta takes it back."

* * *

The valley, as Falk had predicted, made room the way valleys make room for rivers: slowly, grudgingly, and with a great deal of grumbling about the banks.

The grocer's wife, who had refused to sell Ansel butter all winter on the grounds that foreign money might be cursed, appeared at the observatory two weeks after the inquiry with a basket of eggs and a speech about how she had always said the boy seemed honest, delivered loudly enough for the whole kitchen to hear. Old Brenner, who drove the supply cart, offered Ansel a lift to the village chapel on Sunday and pretended it was on his way. The schoolteacher sent up a note asking whether Herr Vogel might visit the class someday and tell the children about the stars in *other* countries, since apparently stars were one subject on which foreigners could be trusted.

"It's not forgiveness," Ansel said one evening, watching the lights come on in the valley below. "It's forgetting. But I think forgetting may be how forgiveness begins, in places like this."

"I think," Paloma said, "it's how mountains work generally. Something enormous happens, and then everyone agrees to walk around it politely forever."

They were standing on the observatory steps. Below them the bridge — repaired, inspected, declared sound — caught the last of the sun, its new planking pale as bone against the dark water. The road beyond it wound down and away, toward stations and borders and the whole loud machinery of the world, and Paloma found herself looking at it the way one looks at a door one has decided, for now, not to open.

* * *

On the morning Ansel might have left — the morning after the inquiry, when his papers were in his pocket and the road lay open and nobody, not Brandt, not Falk, not the entire empire, had any claim on his footsteps — he came to the clock room instead of the bridge.

Palome knew the exact moment he arrived, because the master clock was ticking beside her and she felt, absurdly, that it ticked differently when he was near, the way a room feels different when someone enters it even silently. She was adjusting the rate nut, tongue between her teeth, and she said, without looking up, "You're supposed to be halfway down the mountain by now."

"I know."

"The officials leave at nine. After that you'll have missed your escort and you'll be walking to the border alone, in mud season, with papers that say you're free to go anywhere you like."

"I know that too." He came closer, and she heard him stop just inside the doorway, where the smell of oil and brass lived. "I stood on the bridge this morning. Early. Before anyone was awake. I walked to the middle of it and I stood there."

Paloma set down her screwdriver and turned around.

"And?"

"And I realized I was standing on a bridge that leads away from the only place where anyone ever kept a promise to me." He said it plainly, the way he said most true things, as though facts were simply facts regardless of their weight. "So I came back. And I am going to walk to Herr Falk's office and ask him formally for that contract, and then I am going to spend the rest of my life winding the telescope drives and arguing with you about gear ratios, if you will permit it."

"Arguing with me about gear ratios," Paloma repeated. "That's the offer."

"It's a generous package. There will also be meals. Marta insists."

Paloma looked at him for a long moment — this man her mother had promised shelter to before either of them was born, this stranger the mountain had sent her, this person who had chosen, on a bridge in the early light, to walk back uphill. Her mother had bet on who people could be. Standing in the tick and hum of the repaired clock, Paloma collected her winnings.

"Well," she said. "Someone has to keep the clock company. It gets lonely at 4:17."

* * *

They went down to the bridge together that evening, when the light went gold and long across the water.

It was a thing people did now, apparently — walking to the bridge and back, just to feel it solid underfoot, just to prove the world was connected again. Half the observatory had made the pilgrimage that week. Tonight it was empty except for the two of them and the river talking to itself underneath.

Paloma put her hand on the new railing, still rough and pale, and looked down the road. Somewhere past the bend it became a highway, then a railway, then the whole humming anxious continent. Somewhere along it was the world where promises needed lawyers and strangers needed ledgers kept against them.

"Do you regret it?" she asked. "Staying. When you could have gone clean away from all of it — the suspicion, the inquiry, the whole year."

Ansel considered this with the seriousness he gave everything, gazing down the road as if he could see all the way to its far end, past borders and years, to whatever waited there.

"Astronomers have a saying," he said finally. "*We do not mourn the light that does not reach us. We build better mirrors.*" He glanced at her, and in the gold evening light his eyes crinkled in a way she had learned, over the long winter, meant he was about to be either very wise or very foolish, and had not yet decided which. "Also, I calculated once that if I had left in March, I would have missed Marta's plum cake, which is widely regarded as a tragedy."

"That's the wisdom, is it? The cake?"

"The cake and the mirror," Ansel said. "In roughly equal measure."

Paloma laughed — really laughed, the kind the winter had rationed — and the sound went out over the water and came back off the far bank, the valley returning it like something it had decided, after all, to keep.

They stood there while the sun went down, two people on a bridge built for leaving, going nowhere at all. Behind them, up the mountain, faint as a heartbeat, the observatory clock began to strike eight — a true hour, struck truly, on time.

Paloma counted the strokes. All eight, unhurried, unbroken.

"Come on," she said, taking his arm as the last note faded. "Marta hates it when the cake goes cold. And tomorrow we start learning each other properly — you can tell me everything my mother wrote to your father, and I'll tell you everything she never told me. We have time now."

Ansel turned with her, back up the hill, back toward the lit windows of the observatory.

"We have time now," he agreed. "All of it, if we wind it daily."

And above them, in its tall stone room, the master clock ticked on into the dark — steady, faithful, repaired — keeping the kind of time that is not given but built, minute by minute, by the people who choose to stay.
