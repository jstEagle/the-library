## 22. Keeping Time

The master clock struck seven, and Paloma counted the strokes the way other people said their prayers.

One, two, three — each note falling clean and true down the stairwell of the clock room, out through the corridor, into the bones of the observatory itself. Seven strokes, neither hurried nor dragging, the interval between them as even as breathing. A year ago she had stood in this same room with oil on her fingers and dread in her chest, listening to silence where those strokes should have been.

Now she listened to them every morning, and every morning they sounded to her like a heartbeat she was somehow responsible for keeping.

"You talk to it," Ansel had said once, catching her murmuring an apology to the escapement after a cold snap made the brass contract and run her two seconds slow.

"I apologize to it," she'd corrected. "It's a professional relationship."

"Ah." He had nodded gravely, winding the telescope drive beside her. "In my country we only argue with telescopes."

She smiled at the memory now, alone in the clock room with the morning light coming gray and then gold through the east window. The snow on the peaks had that particular brightness it got in late April — the brightness that meant meltwater, meant mud, meant the valley waking up again below them like something long asleep turning over in its bed.

She crossed to her workbench, which was no longer just her workbench. That was the thing about a year. It didn't pass so much as it accumulated — a second stool dragged over from the mess, a second set of files worn to fit a second hand, a coffee cup with a chip in the rim that nobody would ever admit to owning but which both of them used without comment. The bench had become a shared territory the way the bridge had become a shared road: not by decree, but by daily use.

On the shelf above the bench sat her mother's letter, in its frame of plain pine that Falk himself had planed one evening when he thought nobody was watching. *Shelter and safe passage,* typed on the old machine, signed in her mother's careful hand. Paloma had read it so many times she could recite it, could see not just the words but the rhythm of them, the small hesitation at the third line where the ribbon must have been wearing thin, the way the final sentence ran slightly uphill across the page as though the woman typing it had been leaning toward whatever came next.

For a long time Paloma had believed the letter was a wound — evidence of a mother who kept secrets, who promised things to strangers, who sent her daughter up a mountain under false pretenses with a tool case full of someone else's hope. She had carried that grievance through the winter like a stone in her boot.

But grief, she had learned, is a kind of clockwork too. It runs down if you let it, or you can wind it daily until it becomes something else entirely. Memory, maybe. Or inheritance.

She took down the letter now, not because she needed to reread it but because it was Tuesday, and Tuesdays were for dusting the shelf, and some rituals you keep even when no one is checking.

* * *

The typewriter lived in the clock room now. This had been Marta's suggestion, delivered with the particular firmness of a woman who had spent a winter watching everyone else decide things.

"That machine sat in Herr Falk's study collecting dust and secrets," she'd said, setting down a tray of coffee with a click that ended all debate. "Now it sits where the truth got told. Let it earn its keep."

So Paloma had carried it down the corridor herself, feeling like a priest relocating a relic, and set it on the small desk beneath the window — the desk that had belonged to no one and now, unmistakably, belonged to the machine. The typebars still stuck occasionally on the *e*, and the ribbon was the same one her mother had used, faded from black to a soft bruised purple, and Paloma had not replaced it. Not yet. Some consumables are not consumed so much as consecrated.

Every morning, before she touched the clock, she pressed the keys. All of them, top row to bottom, just to feel the mechanism move. It was a strange sort of maintenance — there was nothing in the manual about exercising a typewriter — but she had learned that machines remember neglect in their joints, and people do too. A machine left idle seizes. A promise left idle does something worse: it forgets what it was for.

The observatory had changed in ways that were mostly small and therefore enormous. Ivo's departure had left a gap in the young astronomers' table that nobody filled gracefully; his replacement, a solemn girl named Berta who blushed when spoken to and wrote observations in a hand like copperplate, had taken three months to laugh at anything and then, one night in February, laughed so hard at Ansel's impression of a Swiss train conductor that she snorted coffee into the star atlas. The atlas still bore the stain. Falk had declared it "a historical document" rather than replace the page.

Falk himself had softened the way old timber softens — imperceptibly, then all at once. He had stopped calling Ansel "Vogel" in that clipped official tone sometime around midwinter, and started calling him "Ansel" the way you call a man whose chair you've stopped moving away from your own. And in March, when the funding committee's letter arrived demanding justification for the observatory's continued existence, Falk had stood at the long table and read aloud Ansel's timing measurements — three years of meticulous transit records — and said, "This is why we exist," and looked directly at Ansel while he said it, which was its own apology, and everyone understood it as such.

The inquiry had come and gone in four days of spring, quick as weather. The officials climbed the road with their ledgers and their questions, and Ansel walked out to meet them with the statement Paloma had typed, and Paloma had watched from the clock tower with her heart going like a flywheel. Then it was over. The words *cleared* and *no further action* and *regrettable circumstances* did their bureaucratic work, and Ivo's name appeared nowhere at all except in Marta's kitchen, where it appeared often, usually followed by a German word that Paloma had decided not to translate.

* * *

The letter had come up with the last mail of the week, forwarded twice and stamped in three languages, addressed in a hand Paloma didn't know.

She'd opened it at breakfast, and by the time she reached the third line Marta had quietly refilled her coffee and Ansel had stopped pretending to read the almanac.

*Dear Sir or Madam,* the letter began, in careful English with the grammar slightly bent, *I write to you because a friend of my family knew a woman named Ada Oyelaran, who once helped strangers cross a border in another year of trouble, and he says her daughter keeps the mountain observatory now. My name is Tomas Reiner. I am seventeen. My father is gone and my mother says I must leave before the summer, and we have no money and few friends, and I can repair bicycles and I learn fast. Is there any place on your mountain for a boy who learns fast?*

Paloma had read it twice. Then she'd folded it along its original creases, which felt important somehow, and put it in her pocket, and eaten her bread, and said nothing, and felt everyone at the table saying everything.

Now it lay on the desk beside the typewriter, and the morning light moved across it, and Paloma sat down on the stool and rolled a fresh sheet into the platen with the care of a surgeon and the stomach of a first-year apprentice.

Because here was the thing she had discovered, the thing her mother had known and never gotten to explain: a promise is not a debt. She had spent a whole winter believing it was — a weight, an obligation, a chain forged by a dead woman's pen. But sitting here, with the clock ticking true above her and the letter waiting, she understood that a promise is more like a movement in a watch. It doesn't weigh anything. It *does* something. It converts intention into motion, minute by minute, whether anyone watches or not.

Her mother had bet on who people could be. Not who they were — who they *could be*. It wasn't naivety. It was engineering. You built a shelter into the design before the storm, because afterward it's too late to add walls.

The keys went down under her fingers, the *e* sticking as always, requiring that little extra pressure she'd learned to give without thinking.

*Dear Tomas Reiner,* she typed.

She stopped. Deleted nothing — there was nothing to delete, only the vast white space of everything unsaid. What did you say to a stranger on a mountain of paper? Her mother would have known. Or maybe her mother hadn't known either; maybe she'd simply sat down and begun, the way you wind a clock without calculating how much spring is left.

*You don't know me,* she typed, *and I don't know you, but I know the shape of your letter, because I have received letters like it and answered one like it myself, in a way, though mine was typed by someone else.*

Outside the window, Ansel crossed the yard toward the dome, carrying his morning coffee and Berta's star atlas with the coffee stain, pausing as he always did at the corner of the building to look up at the clock tower. He couldn't see her through the window — or he could, it hardly mattered — but he raised the atlas to her anyway, a little salute, and she raised the screwdriver she happened to be holding, and this exchange had repeated itself so many mornings that it had worn smooth as an old coin.

*I cannot promise you the mountain will welcome you,* she typed. *Mountains welcome no one. They tolerate us, and occasionally they try to kill us, and we stay anyway because of what can be seen from up here that cannot be seen anywhere else. But there is a roof, and work, and a cook whose bread has been known to change people's minds, and there is a rule in this house which I did not make but intend to keep.*

She paused, hands hovering. The clock struck the quarter hour, four bright notes.

*The rule is this: whoever climbs to us in good faith will find the door open and the table laid, regardless of their nation, their name, or the season. My mother wrote those words to another family, years ago, on the machine I am typing on now. I have decided they are not hers alone. They belong to the house now, and I keep the house.*

It was not elegant. It was not her mother's prose, which had run gently uphill toward hope. But it was true, and true prose holds its time better than elegant prose — she had learned that from a year of statements and inquiries and one very long typed confession that had cleared a man's name.

*Come in the summer, when the bridge is open,* she finished. *Ask at the valley post for the observatory road. Tell them the clockmaker sent for you. Bring warm clothes and your bicycle tools. We are short of everything up here except time, and we have learned to spend it well.*

She signed it — *Paloma Pemberton, Clockmaker* — and sat back, and read it through, and found that her eyes had gone hot in the way they sometimes did at odd moments, striking twelve or dusting a shelf, when the past and present ticked briefly into phase.

A shadow fell across the doorway. Ansel stood there with his empty cup, hair full of wind, looking at the sheet in the typewriter with an expression she recognized because she'd seen it in the mirror all winter: hope and terror braided together.

"A new one," he said softly.

"A new one." She turned the platen and drew the page free. "His name is Tomas. He's seventeen and repairs bicycles."

"Bicycles." Ansel considered this with great seriousness. "We have no bicycles."

"We will." She laid the letter on the desk, squared its edges. "He learns fast."

Ansel came and stood behind her shoulder and read it through, and when he reached the part about the door and the table, his breath went out of him slowly, the way air leaves a room when a window opens in spring. He put his hand flat on the desk, beside the machine, not quite touching it — a courtesy he always paid her mother's typewriter, as though it might startle.

"She would have liked him," Paloma said.

"Your mother?" He smiled. "She would have liked all of this. She would have pretended it was inevitable." He looked around the clock room — the running clock, the shared bench, the stained atlas propped against the wall, the letter in its pine frame. "You know what I think? Belonging isn't given. I searched half a continent for somewhere I was given it. It doesn't exist. You build it. Like a clock. Part by part, and you keep winding it."

"And it strikes true," Paloma said, "if you build it true."

Above them, right on the hour, the master clock gathered itself and struck eight — eight clean strokes rolling down through the mountain like a bell dropped into still water. Neither of them spoke until the last echo faded.

Then Paloma rolled a fresh sheet into the platen, and began a second copy of the letter, because paper traveled badly over passes, and promises — she had learned this too — should always exist in duplicate.
