## 1. The Hour That Stopped

The train gave up on Astrid's Harbor a mile before the town did. Zephyrine Whitlock stepped down onto a platform of wet gravel with her tool case in one hand and her hat pinned against a wind that seemed to come from every direction at once, and she understood immediately that she had arrived somewhere that did not want her.

Not hostile, exactly. Not yet. Just — attentive.

A boy in an oilskin coat stood at the platform's edge, holding a lantern he didn't need in the grey morning light, and he watched her the way gulls watch a fish cart: patiently, with total confidence that something would eventually spill. An old woman on the bench by the ticket office looked up from her knitting, counted Zephyrine's cases — two, one heavy — and looked back down as though filing the information somewhere permanent.

"Whitlock?" the boy said. "The clock one?"

"The clock one," Zephyrine agreed.

He nodded once, satisfied, and led her down the hill toward the town without another word, which suited her fine. She had spent four days on trains and had used none of them for conversation. Talking on trains felt like spending money you hadn't earned yet.

Astrid's Harbor assembled itself below them in pieces: first the masts, a forest of them rising out of fog like winter trees; then the roofs, slate and rust-red, stacked up the hillside in no particular hurry; then the smell — tar, brine, coal smoke, and under it all something sweeter, pitch and sawdust from the boatyards. A harbor town. She'd repaired clocks in a dozen of them and they were all the same town wearing different coats.

Except this one was watching her.

She noticed it properly on the high street. A man sweeping his step stopped sweeping to look. Two women outside a bakery paused mid-sentence, and their sentence did not resume until Zephyrine had passed. The boy with the lantern walked faster, as if proximity to her were itself a kind of gossip.

*They know why I'm here,* she thought. *Of course they do. The clock has been dead for four years. They've all been counting.*

That was the thing about a stopped clock in a harbor town: it wasn't furniture, it was a wound. Every ship's departure timed by it, every wage paid by it, every tide reckoned against it — and then one morning in August 1914 it had simply stopped, at 11:04, and nothing in Astrid's Harbor had been quite on time since. Four years of ships leaving when someone guessed they should. Four years of the whole town living slightly out of true, like a picture hung by a drunk.

And now the guild had sent for her.

* * *

The Clockmakers' Guild hall sat where the high street met the quay, a stone building with a brass door handle worn to brightness by a century of hands. Inside smelled of oil and cold metal and, faintly, of tea gone bitter in a pot left too long.

Corvus Hale was waiting for her in a room lined with glass-fronted cabinets of clock movements, hundreds of them, pendulums hanging still like hanged men. He was a tall man built like a doorframe, fifty or so, with a beard trimmed so precisely it looked drawn on with a rule. He shook her hand the way a customs officer examines luggage.

"You're younger than the guild expected."

"I'm told that often." She set her tool case on his desk. "I'm also better than the guild expected, but we can discover that together."

Something flickered behind his eyes — not quite amusement, not quite offense. "You came recommended by the Guildhall in the capital. They say you're the best hands they've seen in twenty years."

"They say that too."

"And your family? The Whitlocks have been in the trade a long time."

"My grandmother was. My father wasn't. I am." She opened the case. "The master clock. When can I see it?"

Hale studied her a moment longer than the question required, as if deciding how much of her to let into the room. Then he took a ring of keys from his waistcoat.

"The observatory is on the headland. Half an hour's walk, longer in weather. I'll take you up myself." He paused at the door. "There are things you should understand before we arrive, Miss Whitlock."

"There usually are."

"The clock stopped at 11:04 on the fourth of August, 1914. The hour war was declared. It has not run since." He said it flatly, the way a man reports a death he has repeated too many times. "Three clockmakers have looked at it. Two declared it sound and could not explain the stoppage. The third refused to touch it at all and left the town within the week."

"Superstition?"

"He called it something else. But yes. Superstition." Hale's jaw tightened. "The guild does not hold with superstition. We hold with gears and escapements and gravity. You will find a mechanical fault, or you will find wear, or you will find sabotage, and you will repair it, and the clock will run. That is the entire scope of your engagement."

"Is there a part of the scope I'm not supposed to ask about?"

His stride hitched — barely, but she caught it, the way she could feel a worn tooth in a gear train through three layers of wood. He resumed walking before answering.

"The war is not popular here in the way wars are usually unpopular," he said. "Half this harbor trades with nations we are now obliged to consider enemies. Men who apprenticed together in peacetime are supposed to shoot each other now. People are frightened, and frightened people look for reasons. So: repair the clock. Ask nothing about why it stopped. Ask nothing about what was kept here before the war. Do your work, take your fee, go home."

"Home being where, exactly?" Zephyrine asked, mostly to see what he'd do.

It landed harder than she intended. Something moved through Hale's face like weather — grief, maybe, or its exhausted cousin — and was gone.

"The capital," he said. "Wherever clockmakers go when the work is done."

* * *

The walk up the headland took forty minutes because the wind was against them and because Hale walked like a man attending a funeral he'd organized himself. The fog thickened as they climbed, until the harbor below dissolved entirely and the world contracted to the path, the gorse, and the sound of surf hitting rocks they couldn't see.

Then the observatory stepped out of the fog all at once, close enough to touch: a round stone tower crowned with a green copper dome, and beside it the clock tower itself, square and plain, its face blank white, its hands frozen.

Zephyrine stopped walking.

She had read the specifications, of course. She knew the movement was a Whitlock pattern — her grandmother's own design, licensed to coastal observatories thirty years ago, the escapement modified for salt air. She knew the dimensions of the drum, the weight of the great driving weights, the count of the bells. Knowing a thing and standing beneath it were different countries.

The hands stood at eleven minutes past four. Of course they did. Everyone had told her so. But seeing them — two black lines pointing at a moment four years dead, a moment that had happened to other people, in cities whose names she knew only from newspapers — made her chest do something complicated.

11:04. The minute the world ended, if you believed the newspapers. And here it still was, preserved in brass and steel, waiting for her.

"It's my grandmother's design," she said.

"I know," said Hale. "That is why you were sent for."

* * *

Inside, the clock tower rose through three floors of timber scaffolding, and the master clock itself occupied the second: a case of black walnut taller than a man, its glass door clouded with salt haze, its pendulum hanging motionless in the gloom like a diver suspended underwater.

Zephyrine unlocked the case with her own key — Hale surrendered the tower keys without comment — and put her ear to the works.

Silence. But not the silence of a machine at rest. Machines at rest have a looseness to them, a settled quality, weights down, springs slack. This silence was *held*. Taut. As if the clock had stopped mid-breath and never exhaled.

She checked the weights. Fully wound. Both barrels loaded, power sitting in the mechanism like water behind a dam, going nowhere.

"That's wrong," she murmured.

"What is?"

"A clock runs down. That's what clocks do. This one didn't run down. It was stopped — deliberately, cleanly, at full wind. Someone arrested the escapement by hand." She straightened up. "Your third clockmaker was right to be nervous. This isn't a failure, Mr. Hale. It's a decision."

For a long moment Hale said nothing. The fog pressed against the windows. Somewhere below them, muffled by stone, the harbor went on making its sounds — gulls, water, a bell buoy clanking like a patient ghost.

"Can you start it again?" he asked finally.

"Yes."

"Will you?"

"That depends," Zephyrine said, "on what I find inside."

She turned back to the movement and began, methodically, to clean it. Forty years of salt and dust came away on her cloths in grey ribbons. She worked in silence, and Hale watched her work in silence, and the afternoon drained away through the fogged windows without either of them noticing.

It was nearly dark when she found it.

Behind the escapement — wedged into a gap no wider than two fingers, in a space she only reached because her hands were smaller than any of the three clockmakers who had preceded her — her fingertips found paper.

She drew it out carefully. An envelope, sealed with wax, unopened. The wax had cracked with age but held. On the front, in a fine, slanted hand she recognized with a jolt that went through her like a current — because she had seen that handwriting a hundred times, on instruction sheets, on workshop labels, on the flyleaf of the book that had taught her the trade —

— the words: *For the Whitlock who comes after.*

"Miss Whitlock." Hale's voice came from very far away, across the room, very quiet. "What have you got there?"

Zephyrine looked down at the letter in her palm. Her grandmother's handwriting. Her grandmother's seal. Addressed, impossibly, personally, to her.

Outside, the fog swallowed the last of the light, and the frozen clock ticked nothing at all.
