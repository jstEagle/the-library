## 3. The Shipwright's Bench

The boatyard had been dead for four years, but Odile Vasseur still swept it every morning.

She told herself it was habit — the same way she told herself the yard was only closed for the season, the same way she kept Anselm's tools oiled in their drawer as though he might come back from whatever war had swallowed him and complain about rust. Four years of telling herself things. She was good at it now. Practice makes a kind of craft, her mother used to say, and Odile had made an art of not knowing.

The morning fog sat heavy on the harbor, and through it came footsteps on the shingle. Not the gulls. Not old Perrin checking his lobster pots. Someone walking with purpose, which in Astrid's Harbor in autumn meant either trouble or a stranger, and lately the two had grown hard to tell apart.

Odile leaned her broom against the slipway shed and watched the figure come up the lane. A woman, young, coat too fine for the weather and boots already ruined by salt air. She stopped at the yard gate and stood there reading the sign — *Vasseur & Krieg, Boatwrights* — the way people read gravestones.

"You'll want the other yard," Odile called. "We're not taking work."

The woman turned. Her face had that particular stillness Odile had learned to distrust in the last four years: the stillness of someone who has rehearsed what to say and is waiting for the moment to use it.

"I'm looking for the Krieg bench," the woman said. "I was told — I found something that mentioned it."

*Found something.* Odile picked up her broom again, mostly to have something between them. "Found what? Where?"

"A letter." The woman hesitated, and Odile saw her decide how much truth she could afford. "In the observatory clock. I'm the clockmaker they sent for. Whitlock. Zephyrine Whitlock."

The name went through Odile like cold water through a seam.

Whitlock. She set the broom down very carefully, because her hands had begun to shake and she did not intend to let this girl see it. Four years she'd kept the name locked away, and here it walked up her lane on legs, wearing a fine coat, asking after the Krieg bench.

"Your grandmother," Odile said, "was Marguerite Whitlock."

Something flickered across the girl's face — surprise, yes, but under it a quick bright grief, like light off water. "You knew her?"

"I knew her." Odile pushed open the gate. It groaned; everything here groaned; the sea got into all the iron eventually. "Come inside before the fog takes you. And mind where you step. The yard remembers its feet."

* * *

Inside the long shed, the smell hit the girl the way it always hit people — pine tar and oakum and linseed, four years of nothing rotting gently into something else. Odile lit the lamp over the workbench at the far end, the one bench that was never swept into disorder, and watched Zephyrine Whitlock stop three steps away as if the bench were fenced round.

Tools hung above it in their painted outlines. A mallet worn smooth at the handle. A plane whose blade Odile sharpened monthly, though no wood had touched it since 1914. On the wall, nailed up high where damp couldn't reach, a child's drawing of a boat with far too many sails, done in pencil gone brown with age.

"Anselm Krieg built hulls like nobody on this coast," Odile said. "Better than anybody. He'd take a length of timber and listen to it first. Knock along the grain and *listen*, like the wood was telling him what it wanted to be. Half the boats in this harbor have his lines in them and half the owners don't know it, because after August 1914 they decided they didn't want to know."

Zephyrine touched the edge of the bench, lightly, the way you touch a rail at a graveside. "He vanished."

"He didn't vanish. Vanishing is what happens to strangers." Odile heard the sharpness come into her voice and let it stay; some things deserved sharpness. "He lived here eleven years. Married a local girl, Celeste, God rest her, who died of the fever in '11. Had a daughter. He taught Sunday carpentry to boys whose fathers now won't say his name out loud. Then one night in September 1914, after the mob broke his windows for the second time, he was gone. Took nothing. Left the yard to me and the rest to the dark."

"Left what to the dark?"

"That," Odile said, "is the question, isn't it. That is the exact question." She pulled the stool out from under the bench. "Sit down, clockmaker. You didn't walk through fog to admire my sweeping."

* * *

The letter — or the shape of it; Zephyrine wouldn't show her the thing itself, and Odile didn't blame her, not yet — had spoken of *the bench*, of *the keeper of the yard*, of a promise *kept in oak*. Odile listened with her hands flat on the wood, feeling for the lie in it the way Anselm used to feel for a flaw beneath a plank's surface. There was no lie. That was the strange part. Whoever wrote that letter had known this bench, this yard, this harbor, and had trusted it with something heavy.

"There was a compartment," Odile said slowly. "Under the till. Anselm made it himself — he loved hiding places, said a good hiding place was just honesty wearing a coat. We cleared the yard out after he left. The guild sent men to inventory everything." She said *the guild* the way you'd say a bad weather forecast. "They took papers. Ledgers, letters, his naturalization petition, everything with his name on it. But the bench—" She laughed, short and bitter. "The bench they left. Because what could a bench hold?"

She knelt, joints protesting, and ran her palm along the underside of the till until she found the lip of it, hidden under forty years of wax and grime. "Help me lift. It's heavier than sin."

Together they raised the till free of its runners, and there in the shallow channel beneath lay dust, and a folded square of oilcloth, tied with tarred twine.

Neither of them moved for a moment. The lamp flame stood perfectly still, as if it too was holding its breath.

"You open it," Odile said. "I've carried it long enough. Whatever it is, it should be carried by someone new."

Zephyrine cut the twine with a small knife she wore at her belt — a clockmaker's knife, thin as a promise — and unfolded the oilcloth. Inside was paper. Two sheets, actually, and a small brass key gone green with age, and a photograph Odile recognized so suddenly and so completely that she had to sit down on the floor of her own dead yard.

It was Anselm at the bench, laughing at whoever held the camera, his sleeves rolled, a half-built dinghy behind him. And beside him, hand on his shoulder, young and straight-backed and unsmiling in the way of women who smile only when they mean it:

Marguerite Whitlock.

"My grandmother," Zephyrine whispered.

"They were friends," Odile said. "More than friends, maybe — no, don't look at me like that, I don't know, and neither does anyone, because everyone who knew has spent four years not saying. What I know is this: when the town turned on him, Marguerite Whitlock was the one person who didn't. She came down here every week with bread and books and her grand ideas. People called her mad behind her back and worse in front of it. When he disappeared, she stopped coming to the harbor at all. Moved inland within the year. Died alone, I heard, up in some gray town full of people who never knew what she'd done."

"Done?" Zephyrine looked up, and Odile saw the girl understand she was standing inside a story that had been going on long before she arrived. "What had she *done*?"

Odile nodded at the papers in the oilcloth. "Read it."

* * *

Zephyrine read. Odile watched her read — watched color leave her face and come back wrong, watched her hands go careful around the pages like they were live coals. The lamp burned. Somewhere outside a herring boat knocked against its mooring, patient as a heartbeat.

"It's a plan," Zephyrine said at last, and her voice had changed, thinned down to wire. "For a child. For his daughter. Safe passage — papers, money, a route out of the harbor if — when — they come for her. My grandmother wrote it. She signed it. She wrote *I will carry this promise or be ruined carrying it.*" She looked up. "There's a date. It's dated the week he vanished. And there's a line here — *if the clock stops, the clock will know where.*"

"The clock," Odile repeated.

"The observatory clock. The one that stopped the day war was declared. The one they hired me to fix." Zephyrine stood, and the photograph slipped from her fingers, and Odile caught it before it touched the floor — caught it and held it a moment too long, thumb over Anselm's laughing face, because grief braided itself strange in her chest: sorrow for the man, warmth for the woman, and underneath both, sudden and fierce, a feeling she hadn't permitted herself in four years.

Hope. Somebody was finally asking the questions.

"Lene," Odile said quietly.

Zephyrine turned. "What did you say?"

"His daughter's name was Lene. Magdalene, but he called her Lene. She'd be — fourteen now. Fifteen." Odile got to her feet, knees cracking, and felt four years of careful not-knowing begin to crack along the grain like bad timber. "After he vanished, the neighbors said the girl went to an aunt in the south. Nobody ever checked. Nobody ever wanted to check. The whole town agreed not to look, and the guild helped them agree, and I—" She stopped. Started again. "I told myself she was safe somewhere warm. Telling yourself things is a craft, clockmaker. I've practiced."

Zephyrine was staring at the letter again, at the line about the clock, and Odile saw the thought arrive in her face before she spoke it — saw the whole shape of it land, terrible and bright.

"She hid her in plain sight," Zephyrine breathed. "In the observatory. In the bell loft. All this time—"

Outside, the fog was lifting off the harbor, and from across the water came the sound Odile knew better than her own breathing: the observatory bell, striking nine, each note falling on the town like a stone into still water.

Four years of silence in that yard, and suddenly every hour it struck sounded like a question.

"We have to go back," Zephyrine said, already gathering the papers. "Now. Before anyone sees us together."

But Odile was looking past her, out the open door of the shed, at the lane below — where a man in the gray coat of the Shipwrights' Guild stood among the gulls, watching the boatyard with the patience of a man being paid to watch.

He tipped his hat to her, unhurried. Then he turned and walked toward the observatory hill, and Odile understood that whatever promise Marguerite Whitlock had made in oak and ink, the guild had been keeping count of it all along.
