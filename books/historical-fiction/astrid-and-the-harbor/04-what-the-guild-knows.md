## 4. What the Guild Knows

He sent for me on the third morning, and I knew before the boy finished speaking that he'd found out about the letter.

There is a particular quality to being summoned in a town that doesn't trust you. The message arrives politely — *the guildmaster requests your presence at his convenience* — but convenience, you understand, means now. I put down my oiler, wiped my hands on the rag that had already turned grey with four days of clock grime, and looked at myself in the small mirror above the workshop basin. A woman of twenty-six, ink-stained cuffs, hair escaping its pins, eyes that hadn't slept properly since the letter came out from behind the escapement and rearranged everything I thought I knew about my family.

*The Whitlock who comes after.*

I had not opened it. I want that recorded somewhere, in whatever ledger this town keeps of outsiders' sins: I had not opened it. It sat in my coat's inside pocket like a coal, warm where my body warmed it, and every hour I spent winding the great train wheels or truing the escapement, I felt it there, patient as a debt.

I followed the boy down through streets that smelled of tar and cold brine, past shuttered windows where faces appeared and vanished like fish rising and sinking. Astrid's Harbor watched me go. By now I was used to it — or I told myself I was.

* * *

The guildhall stood at the head of the quay, granite-faced, its clock tower a smaller cousin of the observatory's. Corvus Hale received me in a room that seemed designed to make visitors feel they'd arrived late for something important. He sat behind a desk of black oak, and behind him, mounted on the wall, hung an old barometer and a framed charter bearing wax seals gone the colour of dried blood.

"Miss Whitlock." He did not rise. "You've been in the tower five days."

"Four and a half. The going-train needed stripping before—"

"I'm not interested in the train." His voice was quiet, which somehow made it worse. Quiet men are the ones who've learned that volume costs them something. "I'm interested in what you took out of it."

My hand moved toward my pocket before I could stop it. He saw. Of course he saw.

"The clock stopped at eleven minutes past eleven," Hale said, "on the third of August, 1914. Do you know why clocks stop, Miss Whitlock? Not broken ones — I mean good clocks, master clocks, clocks that were maintained by men who loved them."

"They're allowed to stop," I said. "Sometimes. Deliberately."

"Allowed." He leaned back. "That clock has been wound every seven days since before your grandmother was born. Except once. One week, in August 1914, when the man whose job it was chose not to wind it. And then it started again, and ran, and no one asked why it had stopped, because asking questions in this harbor got people's boats burned." He let that sit between us. "Until you came. An outsider. A Whitlock, of all names, with guild papers and clean hands, and within a week you've pulled a sealed letter out of the one place in that tower everyone agreed never to look."

"You knew it was there."

It wasn't quite a question, and he didn't treat it as one. He rose, crossed to the window, and looked out at the harbor, where fishing boats lay at their moorings like dogs pretending to sleep.

"My predecessor knew. And his predecessor. It's been passed along the way such things are passed — not written down, never spoken aloud, just... understood. The compartment stays shut. The clock keeps time. Nobody remembers anything inconvenient." He turned back to face me. "And then the guild writes to your firm in London because the escapement's failing, and London sends *you*, and here we are."

"Why her?" I asked. "Why a Whitlock?"

Hale studied me for a long moment, and something shifted behind his eyes — not softening exactly, more like ice shifting over deep water.

"That," he said, "is precisely the question you must not ask."

* * *

But he answered it anyway. I think he'd been waiting four days for someone to ask it out loud, and I think it frightened him how much he wanted to.

We stood either side of the cold fireplace while he told me, in the flat voice of a man reciting coordinates, a story he had clearly rehearsed for years without ever expecting to say.

"Before the war, this harbor built ships. Good ones. There was a shipwright here — German-born, naturalized, thirty years in Astrid's Harbor, married to a local girl who died young, raising a daughter who'd never seen Germany. Anselm Krieg. He built half the fleet you saw this morning. When the talk of war started, in that last summer, some of us — a few men, a few women, people who remembered him as a neighbor rather than a nationality — made arrangements. Papers. Money set aside. A route, if it came to it. Safe passage out, for him and the girl."

"And?"

"He refused it." For the first time, Hale's voice cracked open a degree. "Can you imagine? We offered him a boat, a captain we trusted, papers under another name. He said no. Said he'd done nothing wrong and wouldn't sneak out of the country he'd chosen like a thief. Said—" Hale stopped, jaw working. "Said his daughter would need this harbor someday, and he wanted to be able to look at anyone in it. So he stayed. They came for him in October of '14. Interred him as an enemy alien, shipped him inland somewhere, and he died — we don't know where, we don't know when. And the girl disappeared."

"Disappeared."

"Went to ground. Someone hid her. Someone has been hiding her for four years, feeding her, keeping her off every list, and doing it so quietly that even I—" He caught himself. Even I don't know who, he had been about to say, and swallowed it like glass.

"The letter," I said slowly. "In the clock. What does it have to do with any of this?"

"The promise had keepers. People sworn to act if the girl was ever found, or ever needed help. Names were written down — one copy, sealed, hidden where only the right person would find it. Your firm was engaged to repair that clock, Miss Whitlock, by a request that came through channels I did not choose and cannot explain. And the letter addressed itself to *the Whitlock who comes after*." His eyes fixed on my coat pocket. "So either your family's name is in this, or your family's name put it there. Either way, it's yours now, and God forgive whoever decided you should carry it."

* * *

I walked back along the quay with the fog coming in off the water, and the town felt different around me — not hostile now, but haunted. Every shuttered window might hide someone who knew. Every boat at anchor had been built, possibly, by the hands of a dead man who'd refused rescue out of love for a harbor that had let them take him.

My grandmother. The thought kept circling back. My grandmother, who died when I was nine, who smelled of lavender and machine oil, who taught me to read a gear train before I could read scripture. What had she signed? What had she promised, and to whom, and at what price?

At the foot of the observatory hill I stopped. Behind me the harbor muttered and smoked; ahead, the tower rose into the fog with its blind white face, keeping its stopped-and-started time over all of it.

Hale had given me a warning dressed as a history lesson. *Repair the clock and ask nothing.* But he had also, deliberately or not, handed me a map. A girl hidden in the harbor. Keepers sworn to act. A promise still breathing somewhere under four years of silence.

And Lene — the name had surfaced in my mind unbidden, from the letter's edge, from Odile Vasseur's half-remembered grief. *Lene Krieg.* Four years old when her father vanished. Eight years old now, if she lived. If she was anywhere.

I climbed the hill as the fog thickened, and in the tower's shadow I noticed it again — the thing I'd taken for weathering on the first day. Chalk marks, low on the stone by the postern door. Small. Child-sized. A little ladder of them, climbing the wall toward the bell loft, renewed again and again, the way a child renews a thing she cannot bear to let fade.

I stood very still, and the letter burned against my ribs, and somewhere above me, faint through the stone, I heard a sound I had mistaken all week for gulls.

Someone was singing.
