## 5. The Girl in the Bell Loft

Lene had a system for the bells.

Not the great ones — those belonged to the clock, and the clock belonged to God, or to the guild, or to whoever had keys now. But the small bells, the handbells and the cracked chapel bell and the little brass one with the split lip that made a sound like a swallowed word. Those she kept on a shelf behind the water tank, arranged by voice. She knew them all by ear. In four years she had learned every sound this building could make, and which of them meant danger.

The new footsteps were neither.

They came at dusk, when the light through the louvres went the color of weak tea. Lene was on her pallet in the loft with a stub of pencil and the back of an old tide-table, copying out the alphabet because Papa had said — Papa had said so many things, and she kept them like coins in a purse she was afraid to spend. *A girl who can read is never entirely lost,* he'd said, and then he had gone out into the last warm week of 1914 and never come back, and reading had turned out to be true: she was lost, but never *entirely*.

The footsteps stopped below her. Then started again. Slow. Careful. Not the guildmaster's tread — Hale walked like weather coming, heavy and announced. Not old Marta from the fish market, who sometimes left bread on the observatory's back step without ever admitting it. These steps paused at things. Touched things.

Someone was learning the building. The way *she* had learned it.

Lene put down the pencil and did not breathe.

She heard the creak of the stair to the clock room, the particular groan of the fourth step that she had learned to skip, and then — silence, long enough that she thought they'd gone — and then a small scrape, high up, near the escapement. A drawer opening. The tick of the master clock filled everything else; it had run again these past days for the first time since the war began, and Lene had wept the first night it did, though she couldn't have said why, only that the building had felt like it was holding its breath for four years and had finally, finally let it out.

Then the footsteps came up the narrow stair toward the loft. Her stair.

There was nowhere to go. There never had been, really. The loft was a wooden pocket above the bells, reached by a ladder and hidden by a false wall of pigeonholes, and if someone found it, they found it. She pressed herself flat against the water tank and listened to her own heart doing something much too fast.

A chalk mark caught the lamplight from below.

That was the thing about the marks. Papa had taught them to her the way other fathers taught prayers — little white arrows and circles drawn low along the baseboards, inside the coal store, up the ladder frame. *If you are frightened, follow the white lines. They will always lead you somewhere I have been.* She had thought they were a game. She had followed them for four years, and they had led her everywhere he had been, and nowhere he was.

But the stranger below had stopped at the first mark. She heard it — the pause, the small intake of breath, the sound of a finger tracing chalk.

And then the stranger said, quietly, to no one, in an accent that wasn't harbor-accent: *"Follow the white lines."*

Not a question. A reading.

Lene's whole body went cold and then hot. Nobody knew about the marks. Nobody living. Unless —

Unless the letter. The letter Odile Vasseur had whispered about across the fish barrels two weeks ago, thinking no one was near — *the Whitlock woman found a letter in the clock, Anselm's name in it* — and Lene had stood very still among the barrels with herring ice melting around her boots and thought: *Papa.*

Her father's name, alive in someone's mouth after four years of being a disease.

The footsteps climbed. The ladder shook under weight. Light bloomed through the gap in the pigeonhole wall, and a shadow moved across the floor of her hiding place, long and thin as a sundial's arm.

"I know you're there," the stranger said. Not unkindly. Tiredly. "I've been following your chalk since the coal store. You're better at this than I am."

Lene said nothing. Her hand found the little brass bell with the split lip, the one whose voice was a swallowed word, because Papa had also said *if anyone comes who means you harm, make noise, noise brings people*, but she had never believed it. Noise brought people, and people were the thing she hid from.

"Anselm Krieg built the cases for the regulator clocks downstairs," the stranger went on. "I read his maker's mark yesterday. He cut his initials inside the case where no customer would ever see them. I don't think that's vanity. I think that's a message." A pause. "I'm Zephyrine Whitlock."

Whitlock.

The name went through Lene like a wire. It was in the marks — not the chalk ones, the other kind, the ones stitched into the hem of the blanket she slept under, *W* in red cross-stitch, done by hands she didn't remember. It was in the way old Odile sometimes looked at her, quick and grieving, and turned away. It was a name from the grown-ups' silences.

"My grandmother," the voice said, "was Magda Whitlock. Before she married."

Lene's breath came out of her all at once, loud in the dark, and there was no taking it back.

The light swung. A face appeared in the gap between pigeonholes — young, sharp-boned, smudged with machine oil, eyes the gray of the harbor in November. The eyes went wide.

"Oh," said Zephyrine Whitlock. Softly, as if to the clock itself. "Oh, *there* you are."

* * *

Later — after Lene had decided, in the space of ten heartbeats, between the brass bell in one hand and the wire of hope pulling tighter in her chest, that she would rather be found than be a ghost another year — they sat together on the loft floor with the lamp between them and the master clock ticking below like some enormous patient animal.

"You've been here four years?" Zephyrine asked.

"Four years, two months, eleven days." Lene could hear how strange that sounded, how precise, and didn't care. "Since Papa went to the yard and didn't come home. Old Marta brings bread Tuesdays. Odile leaves oil for the lamp. The clockmaker—" she stopped.

"What clockmaker?"

"The one before you." Lene picked at a splinter in the floorboard. "Herr Brandt. He kept the clock before the war. He's the one who fixed the false wall, after. He used to bring me books. He taught me the names of the gears. He said—" she hesitated, then gave it away, because keeping it had grown too heavy. "He said my father saved his life once, in a storm, off the Mole. Pulled him out of the water when nobody else would go. And Herr Brandt said a debt like that doesn't drown. It just waits."

Zephyrine was quiet a long moment. Below them the clock ticked, and ticked, and Lene realized the woman was listening to it the way Lene listened to bells — like a language.

"He died last spring," Zephyrine said finally. "Brandt. That's partly why they sent for me. The guild wanted the clock running and no questions." Her mouth twisted. "Everyone wants no questions. I'm beginning to think questions are the only thing worth having."

"They'll hurt him," Lene said. "If they find out about me. Herr Brandt. Even dead."

"They already tried to hurt his memory once." Zephyrine touched the false wall gently, the way you'd touch a wound. "The guildmaster told me to repair the clock and ask nothing. So naturally I asked everything."

Lene almost smiled. It was a strange feeling, smiling at a stranger in a lamp-lit loft, and she examined it like a new gear, turning it over to see how it worked.

"Do you know what happened to my papa?"

"No." Zephyrine said it plainly, no softness, no lie. "Odile says he vanished in the first weeks of the war. The letter I found doesn't say more — it's sealed, and I haven't opened it, and I want you to understand that I *wanted* to, every day, but it isn't mine yet. Or maybe it is, and that's worse." She looked at Lene directly. "It's addressed to 'the Whitlock who comes after.' My grandmother wrote it. Or someone did, in her hand. And your father's name is in it. And a promise."

"A promise," Lene repeated. The word felt like the big bells felt from inside — a vibration in the ribs, too large to hold.

"Safe passage," Zephyrine said. "For someone. I don't know who. I know my family's name is tied to it, and yours, and that the guild would rather both our families were forgotten." She paused. "You. You're the someone, aren't you? Anselm's daughter."

Lene looked at her hands. They were small hands, work-worn early, smelling always faintly of lamp oil and pigeons. "I don't know what I am," she said. "I'm the girl in the bell loft. I'm chalk marks and Tuesday bread. I'm a name nobody can say out loud." She looked up, and found she was angry, and the anger was clean and bright and hers. "My own name. Do you even know it?"

"Tell me."

"Helene. Helene Krieg." Saying it aloud in front of another person for the first time in four years made her dizzy. "Krieg means war. Did you know that? Mama chose to laugh about it. *We gave her the worst word and dared the world to use it against her.*"

"Helene." Zephyrine said it carefully, respectfully, like setting a delicate mechanism into its jewel bearing. "Well, Helene Krieg. Here's what I know. My grandmother left this harbor under a cloud nobody will describe to me. She never spoke of Astrid's Harbor again, not once, and she died with a sealed letter in her effects and instructions that it go to 'the Whitlock who comes after' if the clock ever stopped." She laughed, short and disbelieving. "The clock stopped the minute the war began. Someone designed that. Someone *meant* it."

"Herr Brandt said the clock keeps more than time," Lene offered. "He said everything in this building is a promise somebody made and then had to keep."

Zephyrine stared at her. "How old are you?"

"Twelve. Thirteen in March."

"And you talk like a shipwright's daughter."

"I talk like a book hoarder's daughter," Lene said. "There are forty-one books up here. Herr Brandt's taste ran to sermons, but Papa's ran to adventure."

For a moment they were just two people in a loft, laughing in a small startled way, and the clock ticked, and outside the fog came up the harbor streets swallowing lamps one by one. Fragile, Lene thought. This is fragile. Like the thinnest gear in the train, the one that breaks first if anything jars.

Then boots on the stairs. Heavy. Announced. Weather coming.

Both of them froze. Lene's hand flew to the brass bell; Zephyrine's went to the lamp, snuffing it in one motion, and in the sudden dark Lene heard the woman whisper, fast and fierce:

"If it's Hale, you stay hidden and you say nothing, do you hear me? Whatever happens. Whatever he says. The promise has waited four years — it can wait one more hour—"

The boots stopped on the landing below. A fist struck wood, twice, unhurried.

"Miss Whitlock." Corvus Hale's voice, muffled by the door, pleasant as a knife being sheathed. "I see lamplight under your door. We need to discuss what the harbormaster's men pulled from the water this evening."

A pause, long enough for Lene's heart to stop and start.

"It seems," said the guildmaster, "someone has been asking after a boat."
