## 6. Armistice, Postponed

The news came up the hill on a Tuesday, riding in the mouth of every fishwife and dockhand until it reached the observatory as a shout.

Zephyrine heard it first from the boy who delivered her coal. He stood in the doorway, breath steaming, eyes too bright. "Talks is off," he said. "Foch won't sign. They're saying weeks more, maybe months." Then he was gone, boots ringing down the stair, already shouting it to the next street, the next ear, the next small fire he could light.

She stood at the window of the clock room with a rag still in her hand and watched the harbor take the news like a hull takes water. It started at the quays — a knot of men around the telegraph office, then another around the customs house — and spread inward along the crooked streets the way oil spreads along a current. She could not hear the words from this height, but she knew their shape. Every port town in every war had that shape. She had read about them in newspapers printed three hundred miles away, safe behind the flat distance of print.

History was something that happened to other people. She had believed that when she stepped off the ferry six weeks ago, and she had believed it, mostly, even after the letter behind the escapement, even after Odile Vasseur's kitchen and the name Anselm Krieg spoken like a prayer half-remembered. But belief was a luxury of altitude, and the town below was climbing toward her.

By evening there were lanterns moving through the lower streets in patterns that were not patrols and not processions. Someone had painted a word across the shuttered front of Krieg's old boatyard — she couldn't read it at this distance, but she knew its size, the way you know a wound's shape through a bandage. And someone else had thrown a stone through the window of the German bakery on Cordwainer Lane, which had been flying a French flag since August of '14 and flying it crooked, which was apparently no longer enough.

Zephyrine locked the observatory door and did not sleep. In the bell loft above her, she knew, Lene was not sleeping either. She could hear the girl moving — the soft drag of a pallet, the creak of the beam she used for a shelf, once, terribly, the sound of humming cut short mid-note. A lullaby in German, strangled before its second bar, as though the song itself had become contraband.

* * *

Hale came at dawn.

She heard him on the stairs — that heavy, deliberate tread, each step placed like a signature — and had the door open before he knocked. He looked as though he hadn't slept either. His collar was buttoned wrong by one, and for the first time since she'd known him, Corvus Hale looked less like a guildmaster than like a man carrying something heavier than his office.

"You've heard," he said.

"The whole harbor has heard."

"Then you'll understand I have no time to be gentle." He stepped past her into the clock room without invitation and stood before the master clock, hands clasped behind his back, studying its face as if it might testify against him. "There was a deputation at the guild hall last night. Ship owners. The harbor trust. Two aldermen. They know the yard belonged to Krieg. They know the yard has had visitors." He turned. "They named you, Miss Whitlock."

The word landed cold. "Named me how?"

"An Englishwoman asking questions about a German. Asking about papers. About passages." His voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "In November of 1918, in a port where four hundred families have sons under the earth because of German shells, that is not a question. That is an accusation waiting for a mob to finish writing it."

"Then let me answer it—"

"You will not answer anything." He crossed to her, close enough now that she could see the exhaustion sitting in the hollows under his eyes. "You will repair the clock. You will wind it. You will keep to this tower until the armistice comes or the sea freezes over. And the child—" he paused, and she saw him choose the word with care, laying it down like a card he hated holding, "—the child goes to the authorities. Today. Quietly, before the tide turns the crowd back up the hill. There are forms. There is an internment process. There is a matron in the parish who is not cruel. It can be done gently, if we do it now."

"Lene is fourteen years old. She has lived in your bell loft for four years on your guild's charity, and now you'd hand her to strangers because a crowd got loud?"

"I'd hand her to strangers because the alternative is handing her to a crowd!" For one instant the level voice cracked and she saw the man beneath the office — a man who had spent a war signing papers that sent boys to ships that did not all come home, and who could not bear to add one more name to any list. He mastered himself. When he spoke again it was quiet. "You think I don't remember what her father built for this harbor? I signed his apprenticeship papers myself, Miss Whitlock. I drank at his wedding table. But memory is not a defense. It never has been. It's just the thing we sacrifice first."

"She isn't yours to surrender."

"No." He straightened his collar, found the misplaced button, fixed it with two precise movements. "Neither is she yours to protect. And yet here we both stand." At the door he stopped, back to her. "The letter you carry — the sealed one. Burn it. Whatever your grandmother's generation promised, promises made in peacetime are paid for in wartime, and the exchange rate is ruinous. I am telling you this as a kindness. It is the only one I have left to give."

Then the tread on the stairs, descending, each step placed like a verdict.

* * *

She went to Odile that afternoon, taking the long way along the cliff path so as not to pass through the lower town. The boatyard's gate hung open — someone had torn the chain loose — and the painted word on the shutters resolved at last into letters: *BOCHE*. Below it, in smaller, shakier chalk: *WE KNOW WHO REMEMBERS*.

Odile was in the workshop, sweeping up glass. The window over the bench — Anselm's bench, the one Zephyrine had first seen her at, worn smooth by thirty years of hands — was gone. November air moved freely through the room. Odile swept with the terrible calm of a woman who had done this before, in another decade, and would do it again.

"They came at midnight," she said, without turning. "Four of them. Boys, mostly. One of them cried while he threw the stone, if you can believe it. Cried and threw, cried and threw." She set the broom aside. "Sit down, Zephie. You look like a woman who has been given orders."

"Hale wants Lene surrendered today."

"And what do you want?"

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one that matters." Odile sat across from her, folding her hands on Anselm's bench, and her eyes were fierce and old and unbearably kind. "I told Hale no, this morning, if you're wondering. Told him if his authorities come to my gate they'll find a mad old woman and a yard full of rotting hulls and nothing else. I've hidden things from officials before. I'm better at it than he is." A ghost of a smile. "But listen to me now, because I'll only say this once. If the letter is taken from you, everything ends. Your grandmother's promise, Anselm's daughter, all of it — it lives in that wax seal and nowhere else in this world. Not in my head. Not in Hale's ledgers. Where is it now?"

"In my room. Behind the clock case. Wrapped in—"

"Stop." Odile raised a hand. "Don't tell me where. Tell me whether it's safe."

And Zephyrine, who had wrapped it herself in oilcloth and tucked it into the cavity behind the clock's weight chutes, who had checked it twice, who had told no one — Zephyrine opened her mouth to say *yes* — and felt the bottom fall out of the morning.

Because she remembered the boy with the coal. Remembered that he had stood in the doorway of the clock room Tuesday morning while she stood at the window, back turned, watching the harbor boil. Remembered the delivery yesterday, when she'd been down the tower stairs arguing with the coal merchant about payment and anyone — anyone — could have climbed twelve steps to a room with an unlocked door.

"I have to go," she said.

She ran. Up the cliff path with her breath tearing, past the chapel, through the observatory yard, up the iron stair two risers at a time, flinging open the clock room door—

The oilcloth was there. The cavity was there. The space behind the weight chutes was empty.

On the workbench, centered, deliberate, laid square to the bench's edge the way a craftsman squares a joint, sat a single sheet of paper. No seal. No signature. Just five words in a blunt, careful hand:

*THE GUILD KNOWS WHAT YOU KEEP.*

Below it, a time: *MIDNIGHT. THE OLD CUSTOMS HOUSE.*

Zephyrine stood in the cold room with the paper shaking in her fingers, listening — beneath the wind, beneath the far-off murmur of the harbor — to the faint, muffled movement of a child in the loft above her head, breathing quietly, waiting for a knock that might be rescue or might be the end of everything.

Midnight. Six hours.

Somewhere between the coal scuttle and the customs house, her grandmother's promise was walking through a hostile town in a stranger's pocket — and Zephyrine Whitlock had until midnight to get it back.
