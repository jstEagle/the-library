## 7. The Weight of a Promise

The letter was gone.

I stood in my room at the Salt & Anchor with the drawer pulled out and my hands empty, and I understood with a terrible clarity that the world had finally noticed me — not Zephyrine Whitlock, clockmaker's granddaughter, but the woman holding a piece of paper worth something to someone willing to steal it. The window latch was untouched. The door had been locked from inside; I'd kept the key on its ribbon around my neck since Odile's kitchen. Which meant it hadn't been stolen so much as taken, quietly, by someone with a copy of the key and no fear of me.

Hale? The guildmaster had looked at me across his desk two days ago like a man watching a fuse burn down. *Repair the clock. Ask nothing.* But Hale struck me as too proud for burglary. He'd have demanded it to my face.

That left the guild itself — or whoever moved through the guild like water through a hull.

I sat down on the edge of the bed and made myself breathe. Panic is just a clock running too fast; you correct it the same way. Find the escapement. Find the beat. One thing at a time.

The one thing, absurdly, was this: I had never read the letter. Four days in Astrid's Harbor and I'd carried a sealed envelope addressed to *the Whitlock who comes after* without once breaking the wax. I told myself it was respect. It wasn't. It was cowardice dressed up in respect's coat. As long as it stayed sealed, I could still believe my family's story was small — a clockmaker, a shop, a quiet line of people history had politely declined to visit.

Now someone else had decided the letter mattered enough to take. So it mattered. And if it mattered, then whatever was inside it already belonged to me more than to any thief.

Except the thief had the paper and I had nothing.

No. Not nothing. I had a grandmother who had taught me to cut gears when I was nine, whose hands smelled of oil and beeswax, who had left Astrid's Harbor before I was born and never once said why. I had her voice in my head the way you have a tuning fork in your chest. And I had this: when she gave me the letter, three weeks before she died, she had held it a moment longer than necessary and said, *"You'll know when."*

I hadn't known until now. Now the knowing was useless.

* * *

I went back to the observatory because it was the only place in the harbor where thinking was possible. The fog had lifted into a hard grey afternoon, wind coming off the water with teeth in it. In the clock room the works ticked on — my work, mostly sound now, the great escapement releasing its seconds like a heart that had remembered its rhythm. Below me the town went about its business, unaware that above it a girl named Lene was breathing in a bell loft, alive because of a promise I was only beginning to understand I'd inherited.

I sat at the workbench where Anselm Krieg's tools still hung in their rolls, and I did what I always do when a mechanism defeats me: I took it apart.

Not the clock. The problem.

Who knew about the letter? Hale. Odile. Lene. Me. And whoever had written it — my grandmother, Marguerite Whitlock, dead these six years. Four living suspects, one dead author.

Odile would sooner cut off her own hand than take anything from me; she'd fed me stew and called me *petite horlogère* and wept over a photograph of a vanished friend. Lene was twelve and terrified and hiding. That left Hale — and the thought snagged on something. Because when Hale had warned me, he hadn't asked what was in the letter. A man planning theft asks. He plans around contents. Hale had only wanted it sealed, wanted it buried, wanted it *gone* — which is the posture of a man protecting something, not stealing it.

Which meant either Hale was a better actor than any man I'd ever met, or there was a fifth party. Someone who watched the guild. Someone the guild feared.

I was turning this over when I saw it.

The workbench drawer — Anselm's old drawer, the one Odile had said he kept locked — stood open an inch. I didn't remember leaving it open. Inside, on top of the chisels, lay an envelope.

My handwriting was on the front. My address at the Salt & Anchor, in my own ink. But it was not my envelope. The seal was new wax, unbroken, pressed with a signet I didn't recognize: a ship's wheel over crossed calipers.

Someone had copied my letter. Word for word, seal for seal, they had reproduced it — and returned the original to me, and kept... what?

With fingers that did not feel like mine, I broke the fresh wax.

Inside was a single sheet, in a hand I did not know, cramped and urgent:

*Mademoiselle Whitlock — Your grandmother's letter has been read. What it promises can still be done, but not by the guild, and not openly. Come alone, tonight, after the nine o'clock bell. The yard gate. Tell no one, not even the Vasseur woman. Burn this.*

No name.

I should have burned it. Instead I sat very still in the ticking dark and understood that I was being handled — pulled by strings I couldn't see toward a meeting I shouldn't attend. Every sensible nerve said *trap.*

But underneath the fear, another thing stirred, patient and enormous, the thing the letter had cracked open in me four days ago: *my family's story is not small.* Whatever waited at that boatyard gate tonight, I would walk into it carrying my grandmother's name. And I intended to know, before I walked, exactly what that name had promised.

Because there was one more place the letter might exist besides my stolen envelope.

Grandmothers keep copies. Clockmakers' daughters keep *everything.*

* * *

It took me until dusk to find it, and it was where Marguerite Whitlock would have put it all along: sewn into the lining of the tool roll I'd carried from London, the one she'd pressed into my hands at the end, saying only, *You'll need your own tools where you're going.* I'd thought it sentiment. It was freight.

The packet inside was thin. Two items.

The first was a photograph: a young woman standing on the steps of the observatory, laughing, her arm through the arm of a broad-shouldered man with a woodworker's careful hands. On the back, in pencil: *M. & A., Easter 1913.*

Anselm Krieg. My grandmother had stood on those steps with Anselm Krieg and laughed.

The second item was a letter, sealed with the wheel-and-calipers signet — the same signet as the note in the drawer. Old wax, brittle, addressed in my grandmother's own hand: *To the Whitlock who comes after. Open when the clock stops.*

The clock had stopped. I opened it.

And my grandmother spoke.

* * *

*If you are reading this, then the clock has stopped again, and you have come to Astrid's Harbor, and you are angry with me for the things you have already learned. Good. Anger will carry you further than grief.*

*Let me tell it plainly, because I was never given the courtesy of plainness myself.*

*I made the promise. Not my father, not the guild — me. Marguerite Whitlock, aged twenty-four, apprentice to the master clockworks, in love — yes, in love, say the word, it costs nothing now — with Anselm Krieg, shipwright, the best pair of hands this harbor ever produced. When the war came, the guild turned on him in a single afternoon, the way weather turns. They gave him a week to leave. They forgot he had a daughter.*

*I did not forget.*

*The promise was mine: safe passage for Lene Krieg, at any cost, whenever she needed it. I swore it on the clock itself — do you understand what that means, child? We keep time; we do not break oaths inside it. Anselm refused to run while his daughter stayed hidden, and the authorities were closing, and so we made the plan together: he would go out ahead, draw the eyes, vanish north, and send for her when the sea lanes cleared. He never sent. I have spent thirty years not knowing whether that means he is dead or whether he simply could not risk a letter. I hope, wherever you are reading this, that you learn which.*

*Here is what it cost. The guild discovered my part in hiding Lene — not all of it, but enough. Corvus Hale's father offered me a choice: exile or arrest, silence or the dock. I chose exile. I chose it gladly, and I would choose it again, because the alternative was a little girl in a courtroom answering for her father's blood. I lost the harbor. I lost the workshop. I lost the right to ever see the clock I loved. I built a life elsewhere and I put a wall around this chapter of it, and behind the wall I kept only two things: the oath, and the hope.*

*Now hear the part that matters. The promise does not expire. Promises made on the clock are wound like the clock — they run until someone stops them, and I have not stopped this one, and neither will death. If Lene lives, she is owed passage, protection, a name that is hers by right of love if not by law. If she is grown, find her. If she is small —*

*— if she is small, child, then God forgive us all, and get her out.*

*I know what they will tell you: that times are different now, that the harbor is afraid, that fear makes otherwise good people do arithmetic with other people's lives. Fear has always done its arithmetic. Love does not. That is the whole of what I have to teach you. You may inherit my tools, my eyes, my stubbornness — but the only inheritance I ever cared to leave you is this: you are the promise now. Not its witness. Its keeper. There is a difference, and the difference is everything.*

*Wind the clock. Keep the girl. Forgive me, or don't, but finish what I started.*

*Your grandmother,
Marguerite Whitlock,
who paid in full and would pay again*

* * *

I read it three times. The third time my tears fell on the paper and I blotted them hastily with my sleeve, furious at myself for wetting her words.

So. She had loved him. She had hidden his daughter. She had been exiled for it and had carried the secret across the sea and thirty years of silence rather than burden me with it too soon — or perhaps rather than trust me with it too late. Either way, she had chosen me for this. *You'll know when.* She'd known I wouldn't know until the clock stopped, until the harbor forced the question, until I stood in her old town with a stolen letter and a hunted child and no way back to being the person I'd been on the train.

I am the promise now.

The words settled into me not like a weight but like a mainspring — tension, yes, but tension is what makes a thing move.

Outside, the nine o'clock bell began to ring. Nine strokes, each one falling through the fog like stones into deep water.

The boatyard gate. Alone. Tell no one.

I folded my grandmother's letter into my bodice, next to my heart where promises belong, took up my coat, and blew out the lamp. Somewhere below, in a bell loft, a girl was waiting for a rescue arranged forty years before her birth — and somewhere between here and the harbor gates, whoever had copied my letter was waiting to learn whether I would come.

I picked up my bag of tools.

I went.
