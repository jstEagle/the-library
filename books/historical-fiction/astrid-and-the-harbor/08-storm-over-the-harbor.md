## 8. Storm Over the Harbor

The storm came in from the west an hour after dark, and Zephyrine felt it before she heard it — a pressure change in her ears, a tightening in the old timbers of the observatory, the way Lene went still in the bell loft and said, without turning around, *It's starting.*

Below them, in the town, torches were moving.

Zephyrine watched from the gallery window as they gathered at the foot of Observatory Hill — not a mob yet, not quite, but a crowd with a direction to it, forty or fifty men carrying lanterns that guttered sideways in the rising wind. She could hear fragments of their noise even through the glass. Not words. Just the sound people make when they have agreed on something terrible and are working up the courage to begin.

"They know," Lene said. She was holding the chalk she used for her marks on the loft wall, turning it over and over in her fingers. "Odile said if they ever came up the hill, we'd have one night. Maybe less."

"Then we don't wait for the night." Zephyrine turned from the window. Her hands were steady, which surprised her; everything else in her chest was shaking. "We go now, ahead of them. The gale will cover us."

"The gale will drown us."

"It might." She took Lene's face in her hands — the girl was sixteen, thin as a mast spar, with her father's grey eyes and four years of hidden living written into the way she flinched. "But I made a promise, Lene. My grandmother made it before I was born, and it has been sitting in a clock for ten years waiting for someone to keep it. We are not going to let it die in this building tonight."

Lene looked at her for a long moment. Then she nodded once, the way sailors nod — no comfort in it, only agreement — and began gathering the small bundle that was everything she owned.

* * *

Odile Vasseur had spent thirty years reading weather off the water, and what she read now she did not like.

The wind was backing south by west and strengthening past anything honest for November. The harbor bar would be a graveyard of standing waves. The channel markers would be invisible. And somewhere behind her, coming down the hill road through the rain, there were men with lanterns and a warrant with Lene's name on it — or close enough to her name, the name they'd decided to hate.

She got the *Petrel* ready anyway.

The old boatyard's last seaworthy hull sat in the flooded slip like something sleeping. Odile worked fast, hands remembering what her mind had tried to forget: fresh water cask, tarpaulin, the spare tiller, the oilskin bundle of bread and salt pork she'd packed three days ago when she first understood how the town was turning. She worked and she talked to Anselm Krieg the way she always did when the work was hard, under her breath, in the old mixed tongue of the yard.

*You built her to survive worse than this,* she told him. *Prove it.*

Boots on gravel. She spun, knife out of her apron — and stopped.

Corvus Hale stood at the edge of the slip, hatless, rain running off his jaw, his guild coat dark with water. Behind him, faintly, the crowd noise from the hill. He looked older than he had a week ago. He looked like a man who had been awake for a very long time.

"I'm not here to stop you," he said.

"No," Odile said. "You're here to watch. So you can tell them you saw nothing, and sleep at night."

"I'm here because the harbor pilot's station is unmanned in this weather, and the tide gate at the east mole is chained." He pulled something from inside his coat: a key, heavy, green with salt. "And because if the authorities ask me where three women went in a gale, I intend to say I was checking the tide gauge and saw nothing at all. Which will be true, because I'll be looking at the tide gauge."

Odile didn't lower the knife right away. Thirty years of watching this town turn its face away from things had taught her what promises from comfortable men were worth.

"Why?" she said.

Hale looked out at the black water. For a moment he seemed about to say something large — about the guild, about 1914, about the promise his own signature sat beneath. What he actually said was quieter.

"Because my father signed the same paper your friend signed. And because I've spent four years telling myself the war required silence, and I am tired of not being able to tell the difference anymore." He held out the key. "The tide gate opens onto the outer channel. Take it, get clear of the mole before the lightship sees you, and don't come back until the world has lost its mind more gently."

Odile took the key. Their eyes met, and something passed between them that neither would ever put into words — the recognition, maybe, of two people who had both kept a thing alive by never speaking of it.

"Go home, Corvus Hale," she said. "Check your tide gauge."

He nodded, turned, and walked back up into the rain. And Odile noticed — though she would keep it to herself for years — that he walked slowly, and that twice he stopped, and that the second time he stopped, he stood directly in the lane between the hill road and the boatyard, a single broad figure in the dark, for a long time, facing uphill.

Buying them minutes. Maybe only minutes. But minutes.

* * *

They came down the slip in the teeth of it — Zephyrine first, then Lene with the bundle strapped tight, then Odile hauling the painter. The *Petrel* bucked against her lines like a living thing that wanted the storm more than they did. Rain came flat and horizontal, stinging. Somewhere above the harbor the observatory clock struck nine, muffled almost to nothing, but Zephyrine heard it and thought: *nine hours.*

"In," Odile shouted. "Both of you forward. Low."

The cabin was cold and smelled of tar and bilgewater. Zephyrine got Lene down onto the thwart and held her there while the deck heaved and Odile fought the boat out of the slip into open black water. Through the hatch Zephyrine caught one glimpse backward: the harbor lights smeared by rain, the hill with its torches descending, and — impossibly, briefly — a line of figures standing across the road at the tide gate, arms linked, letting the boat pass and nothing else pass.

Hale's men. Or Hale himself. She couldn't tell. The image closed over like water.

Then the mole was behind them and the sea took the boat entirely, and there was no more harbor, no more town, no more anything except wind and the enormous moving darkness and Odile's voice counting the seconds between lightning and thunder, steering by a storm she couldn't see.

"How far?" Zephyrine shouted into the cabin.

"Twelve miles to the headland!" Odile roared back through the hatch. "Six hours, if she holds! If the bar doesn't take us!"

Lene's hand found Zephyrine's in the dark. The girl was soaked and white-lipped and terrified, and she was also — Zephyrine saw it clearly, in the flash of the next lightning — watching the compass light with the focused calm of someone who had grown up around boats built by her father's hands.

"She'll hold," Lene said. "My father built her."

* * *

The bar nearly took them at half past eleven.

Zephyrine felt it coming before she understood it — a change in the boat's motion, the swell shortening, stacking, the *Petrel* climbing walls of water that broke white on either side. Odile's voice came down the hatch, stripped of everything but command: "Hold her! Hold her, hold her—" and then a wave hit the hull like a train, and the world became noise and cold and the taste of salt blood in Zephyrine's mouth where her lip had split against the thwart.

When the wave passed, the boat was still there. Bailing was suddenly the whole of existence. Zephyrine bailed with a bucket while Lene braced her and Odile screamed the heading, and the sea threw itself at them again and again, and again and again the boat rose to meet it, Anselm Krieg's lines proving themselves plank by plank against everything the sky could send.

Once, in a gap between waves, Lene lifted her face to Zephyrine's and shouted over the wind:

"My father said — if anyone ever kept the promise — tell them —"

"What?"

"He said — *tell them the clock was never broken.*"

Zephyrine stared at her. Lightning filled the cabin, and in its light Lene's face was fierce and wet and certain.

"He said the Whitlocks would understand!"

Zephyrine didn't understand. Not yet. But she held the girl tighter, and bailed, and the boat drove on into the dark toward a headland neither of them could see, while behind them Astrid's Harbor burned its little torches against the rain and searched a hilltop observatory that was already empty.

Except that it wasn't empty. That was the thing none of them knew yet — not Zephyrine, not Odile at the tiller, not the crowd swarming the clock tower with their lanterns and their fury.

Because when they reached the headland at dawn, wrecked and half-drowned and alive, and looked back at the harbor one last time, the master clock was striking.

Striking, and striking, and striking — far too many times, wild and uncounted, ringing out over the water like something trying to be heard across the whole width of the sea.

Someone was still up there. Someone had wound the clock.

And Zephyrine, gripping the rail of her father's boat with Lene's hand locked in hers, understood with a cold drop through her stomach exactly who was missing from the deck.

Odile was gone.
