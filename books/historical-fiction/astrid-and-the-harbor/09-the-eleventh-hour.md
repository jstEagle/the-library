## 9. The Eleventh Hour

They came back the way no one escapes: through the front door, in daylight, soaked to the bone and seen by everyone.

Zephyrine had planned it differently. She had planned a fish cellar, a coal chute, some dark anonymous hole where she and Lene could breathe until the harbor forgot their faces. But Lene's lips were gray, and her small hands would not close, and the observatory was the only building on the headland with a fire, and Zephyrine was done — done — letting promises die of politeness.

So she walked up the hill holding the girl against her side like something rescued from the sea itself, which was not far from true, and she did not look at the windows of the houses along the lane, though she felt every one of them looking at her.

The bell loft was exactly as they'd left it two nights ago. The chalk marks still climbed the beam — little ships, little waves, a lighthouse drawn by a child who had learned the harbor only from above it. Zephyrine built up the fire in the old stove, stripped off Lene's wet coat, wrapped her in the clockmaker's blanket that still smelled of machine oil and cedar, and sat with her back against the wall listening to the girl's breath come slow and even.

Below them, through the floor, the master clock ticked.

Ticked, steady as a pulse, for the first time in four years.

Zephyrine had repaired it. That was the bitter joke of it — she had come here to fix a stopped clock, and she had fixed it, and now it counted down the hours toward an armistice everyone said might never come, while Odile Vasseur lay somewhere in the water or on some rock or —

*Don't,* she told herself. *Not yet.*

She had left word at the harbormaster's. She had paid a boy to walk the tideline. She had done everything except believe, because believing was a door you couldn't close again.

Lene stirred. "Is Miss Vasseur coming?"

"She'll come," Zephyrine said. "She's stubborn as ballast."

"What's ballast?"

"Something heavy that keeps a boat from tipping over." She smoothed the wet hair from the girl's forehead. "Someone who keeps everyone else upright."

Lene considered this with the terrible seriousness of nine-year-olds, then said, "Like Papa."

Zephyrine's throat closed. "Yes," she managed. "Exactly like your papa."

The clock struck four below them, and the sound came up through the floorboards and into their bones, and they waited inside it.

* * *

Hale came at dusk.

She heard him on the stairs — that deliberate guildmaster's tread, unhurried, the tread of a man who had decided something and would not be argued out of it — and she moved between him and the ladder to the loft before he reached the landing.

He stood in the doorway of the clock room with his hat in his hands, which was somehow worse than if he'd brought soldiers. Behind her the escapement whispered its small faithful counting. Hale looked old tonight, older than he had in the guild hall, older than he had shouting about surrender and duty. He looked like a man who had been carrying something heavy uphill for a long time.

"The whole town saw you come up the hill," he said. "Half of them are drinking courage in the Anchor right now, deciding whether to fetch the constable themselves."

"I know."

"They'll come tomorrow. Maybe tonight, once they've found enough of each other." His eyes went past her shoulder, up toward the loft, and something in his face moved — grief, or its cousin. "Odile Vasseur hasn't been found."

"I know that too."

"A fishing crew pulled a dinghy off the rocks this morning. Swamped, mast gone." He paused. "No body."

"No body," Zephyrine repeated. She held the words carefully, the way you hold a live coal. No body was not nothing. No body was almost everything.

"Zephyrine." He said her name the way her grandmother must have once said it to someone — full weight, full warning. "Four years ago I stood in this room and watched them take Anselm Krieg away in a cart, and I said nothing. He had built half the boats in that harbor. He had taught me to read tide tables when I was younger than that child upstairs. And I said nothing, because the guild said silence was safety, and safety is the one thing men like me worship." His voice roughened. "I am forty-nine years old and I have just learned that I am a coward with good posture. I don't intend to die one."

"Hale—"

"Listen." He set his hat down on the workbench, precisely, the way he did everything. "There is a provision in the guild charter. Old one. From the plague years, when the guild took charge of orphaned children so the parish wouldn't ship them inland. A ward of the guild is under the guild's protection — its roof, its books, its seal. Not the constable's. Not the parish's." He drew a folded paper from his coat. "It requires three signatures of standing members. I have two. I am the third."

Zephyrine stared at the paper without touching it. "You'd put your name to it. With the harbor watching."

"With the harbor watching," he agreed. "And tomorrow, when they come up this hill, I will stand on my own steps and tell them the girl is the ward of the Astrid Harbor Shipwrights' Guild, and that any hand raised against her is raised against four hundred years of charter law, and I will do it in my loudest guildmaster voice until they either believe me or drown me in the harbor alongside everyone else I've failed." He held out the paper. "Take it up to her. It needs her to understand what it means. And it needs—" he hesitated, the first crack in all that deliberateness, "—it needs to be done before eleven o'clock."

"Why eleven?"

He looked at the clock face, at the hands crawling toward the evening, and for a moment he seemed to be reading something written there.

"Because if the armistice comes," he said quietly, "the world changes tonight. And I would rather we changed with it than let them decide we hadn't."

* * *

Later, with Lene asleep again and the paper signed — the girl had printed her name in careful pencil, *Magdalena Krieg*, the first time Zephyrine had ever seen it written anywhere but in a dead man's letter — Zephyrine climbed to the lamp room alone.

The harbor lay below, black and glittering, every window in the town lit. They were waiting up. All of them, waiting for news from across the sea, the way people wait outside a sickroom. Somewhere out there ministers were signing things, or failing to sign things, and the whole world was holding its breath between one hour and the next.

The wind had dropped. The sea made a long soft sound against the headland rocks, patient, enormous.

And then, far out past the breakwater, where the channel light swung its slow red arc — movement. A boat. Small, low in the water, running dark.

Zephyrine leaned over the parapet, heart slamming. The boat came on, clumsy, wallowing, worked by a single figure hauling on the oars with the rhythm of someone who had learned boats before she learned anything else. As it crossed the channel light, the red sweep caught a face turned up toward the observatory — a broad, weathered, impossible face — and Zephyrine laughed out loud, one cracked sob of a laugh, at Odile Vasseur rowing home out of the sea like something out of the oldest story the harbor had.

*No body.* Of course there was no body. You didn't sink a woman who weighed anchor for a living.

Zephyrine took the stairs three at a time.

* * *

Odile sat by the stove wrapped in blankets, gray-lipped and steaming, refusing tea until she'd finished telling it: swamped off the point, clinging to the dinghy's keel through the night, washed onto the shingle at Gray Cove near dawn, walked six miles along the coast road with a borrowed oilskin and a borrowed hat and a story about being drunk, which, she said, the coast road fishermen had believed instantly and with warm approval.

"You wound the clock," Zephyrine said. "Before the storm. That's why it was striking when we looked back."

"Somebody had to." Odile shrugged, wincing at her shoulders. "A promise isn't kept halfway up a hill. Your grandmother knew that. I'm only following her instructions." She looked at Zephyrine over the rim of the cup, and her eyes were bright and fierce and tired. "It's nearly time. Go up. Wind it the rest of the way yourself. It should be a Whitlock's hands on it when it strikes."

* * *

At ten minutes to eleven, Zephyrine stood in the clock room with the winding crank in both hands.

Through the open window came the sound of the harbor — hundreds of voices now, spilling into the streets, a low oceanic murmur of waiting. Someone down there began to sing. Others took it up, ragged, uncertain, a hymn half-remembered.

Lene stood beside her, holding the guild papers pressed flat against her chest with both arms, wearing the clockmaker's coat over the blanket, looking smaller and more certain than anyone Zephyrine had ever known.

"Will it be loud?" Lene asked.

"It's meant to be heard across the whole harbor," Zephyrine said. "Across the whole sea, if it has its way."

"Good," said Lene Krieg, ward of the guild, daughter of a shipwright. "Papa always said clocks should speak up."

The minute hand touched the hour.

For one heartbeat, nothing. The whole world balanced on the escapement's click.

Then the great hammer fell, and the first stroke rolled out over Astrid's Harbor — over the rooftops and the masts and the mob that had become a crowd, over the breakwater and the channel light and the open sea beyond it — and the crowd's murmur broke apart into cheering, into weeping, into church bells answering from the town below, wild and overlapping, because the wireless had come through, because it was over, it was over, it was over.

Eleven strokes. Zephyrine counted every one with her hand on the case, feeling the promise travel up through her palm and into her chest. Beside her Lene was crying without noise, smiling without knowing it.

When the last stroke faded, when the harbor's bells were still ringing and would ring all night, footsteps sounded on the stairs — Hale, capless, breathless from climbing, holding a telegram in one hand.

He stopped in the doorway. He looked at the clock, at the girl, at Zephyrine, and for once the guildmaster's deliberateness abandoned him entirely.

"It's official," he said. "Signed at the fifth hour, Paris time. The war is over." He swallowed. "And Zephyrine — there's more. The telegram office says a second wire came this morning, addressed to the observatory. To *you*. From the coast station at Gray Cove."

He held it out.

"It's marked *urgent*. It concerns a boatyard, and a name that isn't Vasseur."

Zephyrine took the paper with cold fingers, as the bells rang on below.
