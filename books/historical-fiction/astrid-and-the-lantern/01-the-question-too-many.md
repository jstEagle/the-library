## 1. The Question Too Many

The binding went on before dawn, the way it always did.

Bram stood in the narrow washroom of her lodgings on Fischergasse with a candle stub guttering on the sill and wrapped the linen strips around herself in silence, counting turns the way she counted teeth on a wheel—seven across the ribs, four above that, tucked flat, pinned tight. The trick was not to hold your breath. The trick was to breathe shallow, all day, every day, until shallow became the shape of you.

Five years of practice had made it mechanical. That was the mercy and the horror of it: she could bind her chest half-asleep, pin her hair under her cap without a mirror, drop her voice into its lower register before her feet touched the floor. The body learned obedience faster than the heart did.

Outside, the fog was doing what the fog always did in October, rolling up off the harbor in gray sheets and swallowing the masts along the quay. Somewhere out on the water a ship's bell rang twice and was eaten whole. Bram listened to it the way other people listened to weather—with a professional ear. Two bells, slightly flat, probably a Russian coaster with a worn clapper shoe. She filed it away without meaning to. Everything about time got filed away; it was the only inheritance her father had left her, this compulsive accounting of hours.

She buttoned the waistcoat over the binding, shrugged into the jacket with the padded shoulders, and looked once at the face in the cracked mirror—a young man's face, sharp-jawed, sleepless, unremarkable—and then blew out the candle.

*Good morning, boy,* the world would say today. And Bram Montrose would nod and keep his hands busy, because busy hands were invisible hands, and invisibility was the only country she held citizenship in.

---

Hessler & Sons occupied the ground floor of a crooked brick building on Uhrmacherstrasse, and despite the name there was exactly one Hessler left, and he was neither young nor anyone's son anymore. Old Hessler ran the workshop like a liturgy: benches scrubbed at seven, lathes oiled at seven-ten, the great plate-glass window wiped so passersby could watch the apprentices bent over their work like monks at prayer. Respectability was the shop's second product. It sold better than the clocks.

"Montrose." Hessler's voice came from the back room before Bram had her coat fully off. "The Kessler order. Is it moving?"

"It's moving, Master."

"Is it *finished?*"

"By Thursday."

A grunt, which from Hessler was applause. Bram hung up her coat, tied on the leather apron, and took her seat at the third bench by the window—the good bench, the one with the north light, which she'd earned two years ago by re-cutting a broken escape wheel so precisely that a Swiss importer had asked who made it and Hessler had lied and said he had. Bram hadn't minded. Being unnamed was the point. A clockmaker named in an invoice was a person; a clockmaker folded into *Hessler & Sons* was furniture, and furniture didn't get questioned.

She set the Kessler carriage clock's movement on the bench and picked up her tweezers.

For three hours the world contracted to the size of a brass plate. This was the part no one understood, the part she couldn't have explained to anyone without ending everything: that inside a movement there was no such thing as a girl or a boy or a liar. There were only parts that did their work or didn't. A pallet either released the wheel cleanly or it dragged. A spring either returned or it failed. Brass didn't care what name you gave it. Brass only wanted to be true.

Her hands knew what to do the way tide knows the moon. She adjusted the banking pins by a hair, tested the escapement's tick against her own pulse, and lost herself so completely that when the shop bell over the door rang at eleven she surfaced like a diver coming up too fast, heart slamming, tweezers frozen mid-air.

"Shop!" Hessler called from the back, meaning *you*, meaning always *you*, because the other two apprentices—Kessel and fat little Brandt—were allowed to pretend deafness when official visitors came.

Bram set down the tweezers, squared the movement on its stand, and went out front.

The woman standing inside the door was shaking rain off a black umbrella, though it wasn't raining so much as the sky was dissolving. She wore a plain gray coat, government-plain, and carried a leather document case with the city seal embossed in dull gold. Census. Of course. The notices had been posted for a fortnight: *Imperial Census of Persons and Dwellings, all residents must declare place of birth, occupation, and household.* Bram had read the notice four times and told herself five times that it meant nothing. Workshops weren't dwellings. Apprentices lived at their lodgings. Someone else would answer the questions, someone whose answers were simple.

"I'm looking for the proprietor," the woman said. Her accent was coastal but educated, vowels softened by schoolrooms. "Master Hessler?"

"He's in the back. I can take a message."

"You're an apprentice?"

"Bram Montrose. Journeyman-track." The lie about rank came automatically; *journeyman-track* sounded better than *third-year apprentice* and cost nothing.

Something flickered across the woman's face—not suspicion, worse than suspicion. Interest. She opened the document case and produced a ledger bound in blue cloth, stamped with the double-headed eagle, and a pencil sharpened to a needle's point. "Then perhaps you can save us both time. Astrid Lindqvist, census bureau, district six. I'm verifying commercial premises as well as residences. I'll need the names, ages, and places of birth of everyone employed here."

"Places of birth," Bram repeated.

"For every person. It's a new requirement." Lindqvist said it evenly, without apology, the way people say things they've been ordered to say and have decided not to hate themselves for. "The empire wants to know where its hands come from."

*Its hands.* Bram almost laughed. If the empire only knew whose hand was holding its pencil tray steady right now.

"I'll fetch the Master," she said. "He handles the records."

"The Master is hard of hearing and hates paperwork—I've dealt with him at the chandlery next door." Lindqvist flipped the ledger open to a fresh page and wrote *HESSLER & SONS, UHRMACHERSTRASSE 9* in a hand so upright and disciplined it looked typeset. "You're clearly the one who knows where things are. Start with yourself. Name?"

"Bram Montrose."

"Age?"

"Twenty-three."

"Occupation?"

"Clockmaker. Apprentice grade."

"And—" the pencil paused, hovered, came down "—place of birth?"

There it was.

The question sat on the counter between them like a bomb with the fuse already cut. Bram heard the shop sounds continue around it—Kessel's lathe whining through the wall, the case clock in the corner ticking its patient eight-day tick, fog pressing against the glass—and felt, absurdly, that all of it should have stopped. That the whole mechanism of the morning should have jammed at so clean a fault line.

Because there was no answer. There had never been an answer, not since the border treaties redrew the map two years ago and turned the fishing village of her childhood into a piece of another empire, an enemy province, a name that on any official form now flagged a man—or a boy—for registration, questioning, expulsion. *Born in Vasterhavn, Ostmark Province,* written in a clerk's hand, was a door slamming. But she couldn't say she'd been born in this city either, because the guild registry required proof of provenance for apprenticeship, and proofs could be checked, and checked papers were how boys like Bram Montrose ended up in the customs house explaining themselves under a lamp.

"Montrose?" Lindqvist said. Not impatient. Just precise.

"North coast," Bram said. "Small town. You won't know it."

"I was born on the north coast myself." A small smile, professional, offered and withdrawn. "Which small town?"

"Fishing town." Bram kept her eyes on the ledger, on the blank space after *Place of birth:* —a rectangle of nothing, waiting. "My family moved around. Father was a cooper. We followed the herring."

"We?" The pencil didn't move.

"My father and mother. Both dead." True enough to say smoothly; grief made an excellent shield because nobody pressed a grieving man. "Look, if it's not on the approved list of districts it doesn't matter, does it? We're all subjects of the crown now. That's what the posters say."

Something changed in the census taker's face. It wasn't softening, exactly. It was more like focus—a lens being racked from middle distance down to close work. Bram had spent five years learning to read faces the way she read dials, because her survival depended on catching the exact moment attention became scrutiny, and she saw it happen now, in real time, in a stranger's gray eyes.

"Mr. Montrose." Lindqvist closed the ledger, gently, deliberately, the way one closes a watch case. "Do you know what happens to incomplete entries?"

"They get marked incomplete."

"They get *revisited*. There's a difference." She tucked the pencil behind her ear—an odd, human gesture that contradicted the rest of her—and looked past Bram's shoulder at the wall of clocks, forty faces all telling the same time in forty different voices. "I'll leave your employer's section blank pending his statement. Yours stays open as well. District six does a verification round within the month; I do those rounds myself. You can give me a proper answer then. Or—" and here her voice dropped half a register, conversational, almost kind, which was infinitely more frightening, "—you can tell me now why a clockmaker's apprentice breathes from the top of his chest."

The workshop noise went distant, as if someone had packed wool in Bram's ears. She was aware of the binding against her ribs, of the shallow tidal pull of air she'd practiced for five years, aware suddenly and totally of her own body as evidence. No one had ever noticed. No one was supposed to be able to notice. She had built this disguise the way she built movements—every part fitted, every tolerance checked—and here was a woman with a government pencil who had walked in off the street and put her eye to the flaw in thirty seconds flat.

"I don't know what you mean," Bram said.

"No," Lindqvist agreed. "I expect you don't." She snapped the document case shut. "Thursday or Friday, then. Verification round. Have a proper answer ready, Mr. Montrose. For the file."

She let herself out. The bell over the door rang its little tin note, and the fog took her, umbrella first.

Bram stood at the counter for a long moment with her hands flat on the wood, feeling her own pulse bang against the linen strips—seventy-two, seventy-six, climbing. Behind her, forty clocks ticked in agreement, indifferent, accurate, keeping perfect time for a world that had just developed a crack straight through the center of hers.

"Who was that?" Hessler demanded from the back room.

"Census," Bram called back, and was proud—genuinely proud, later, remembering it—of how steady her voice came out. "Government business. Nothing for us."

Nothing for us. She went back to the third bench and picked up the tweezers and looked at the Kessler movement without seeing it, because all she could see was a rectangle of blank space in a blue ledger, and a pencil hovering over it like a blade deciding where to fall.

Verification round, Thursday or Friday. Within the month, she'd said, and then Thursday or Friday, which meant sooner, which meant the bureau had already flagged something, or the woman herself had decided to come back on her own authority—which was worse, because a bureau could be dodged, but a person who had *seen*—

Bram pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes until green sparks swam.

Keep the hands busy. That was the rule. Busy hands were invisible hands. She bent back over the movement, seated a jewel, adjusted a click spring, and worked with more precision than she'd ever used, because precision was prayer and control was survival and if she stopped working even for one minute she would have to sit in the ticking dark and admit what she already knew:

Somewhere in the gray fog of the harbor district there was a black ledger with her name half-written in it, and a woman who breathed like a normal human being and noticed everything, coming back.

Three days. She had three days to invent a birthplace out of thin air, forge whatever paper backed it up, and pray the census taker's interest was nothing more than bureaucratic thoroughness.

Outside, the fog thickened until the window showed nothing but white. Inside, the case clock struck eleven—all eleven notes of it, clear and unhurried—and Bram Montrose, who could mend any broken thing brought across her bench, sat very still among all that accuracy and waited for the one thing she couldn't repair to arrive on schedule.
