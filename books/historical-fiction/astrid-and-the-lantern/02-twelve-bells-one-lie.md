## 2. Twelve Bells, One Lie

The summons came on a Tuesday, folded twice and delivered by a boy who smelled of herring and had no interest in waiting for a reply.

*The Observatory. Master clock requires attention. Come prepared to stay.*

Master Hessler read it at the bench, holding it close to the lamp because his eyes had begun their long surrender, and his eyebrows went up in a way that meant opportunity. "They've asked for you," he said. "Not me. You."

"They asked for Hessler & Sons."

"They asked for *you*, boy. By name." He tapped the paper. "Do you know what that means?"

It means someone is watching, Bram thought. It means someone wrote my name down somewhere and remembered it. But she said, "That they heard I fixed the Krüger regulator."

"That took you four days."

"The Krüger regulator took four days because Herr Krüger kept talking about his gallstones."

Hessler laughed — a dry sound, like a mainspring snapping in an empty room — and waved her off before she could be late for an appointment she hadn't asked for.

She walked up through the old town with her tool roll under her arm and her collar high, past the fish market where the ice was going grey, past the customs house flying its flag at half-mast for some dignitary or other whose death the papers were still arguing about. The fog was doing what fog did in this city: erasing the rooftops first, then the masts, then everything above knee height, until the whole port seemed to exist underwater. She liked the fog. The fog was the closest thing this city had to a conscience. It looked away.

The observatory sat on the hill like a stone argument against the weather — squat, dome-crowned, indifferent. Its tower carried the master clock: a regulator built forty years ago by a man whose name was still engraved inside the case in careful Gothic script, the way men used to sign things they expected to outlive them. Every clock in the city answered to it, eventually. The harbor bells. The railway. The patrol sergeants' watches, when the patrols bothered to wind them. It was, though almost nobody thought of it this way, the thing the city agreed on. The one thing.

The caretaker let her in without a word, which she appreciated. He was seventy if he was a day, moved like a man wading through honey, and had the gift — rare in this world — of not needing to fill silence with himself.

"She's been losing time," he said, on the stairs. "Two minutes in a week. Maybe three."

"Who's she?"

He gave her a look over his shoulder, mildly offended. "You'll see."

Bram climbed into the clock's works just after nine and understood, within the first minute, what the old man meant.

The master clock was not a machine. That was the error everyone made, looking at it from outside — thinking of gears and weights and escapements, of *mechanism*, as if it were a loom or a printing press, something that did work. But once you were inside it, once you'd swung yourself up onto the maintenance cradle and put your hands on the frame, you learned the truth, which was that the machine was dead and something else was alive in there. Something patient. Something that had been keeping time since before Bram was born and would go on keeping it after every person who currently drew breath in this city was gone. You didn't repair it. You negotiated with it. You listened to where it hurt.

She found the hurt by ten.

A wear-spot on the third-wheel pivot — hairline, invisible unless you knew to run your thumbnail along the steel and feel the catch, the tiny stutter like a swallowed word. And below it, the real trouble: the suspension spring of the pendulum had fatigued, gone slightly soft, and the whole great breathing body of the clock was swinging a fraction too slowly, accumulating its two minutes a week the way a tired man accumulates debts. Someone had compensated for it years ago by nudging the rating nut — a lazy fix, a fix that treated the symptom — and the clock had been quietly resenting it ever since.

"You need a new spring," she murmured, into the dark smelling of oil and dust and cold iron. "And an apology."

She said things like that sometimes, alone inside machines. She'd stopped being embarrassed about it years ago. There was no one to hear, and besides — and this was a thought she kept in the deepest drawer of herself, only taking it out at night, only in the dark — inside the works there was no Bram and no Birgitta. There were no names at all. There was no chest to bind, no voice to pitch low, no story to keep straight. Inside a clock there was only the work, and the work didn't care what you were. A gear turned or it didn't. A hand pointed true or it lied. Of all the mercies the world had ever shown her, that was the one she trusted most: that steel could not be scandalized.

She worked through the morning. She made drawings of the spring in her notebook — measurements to the tenth of a millimeter, because this clock deserved nothing less — and by noon she had disassembled the escapement and laid its parts out on flannel in the order of their removal, which was also the order of their return, which was a kind of sentence grammar only clockmakers spoke.

That was when she heard the door below, and boots on the stairs, and a voice saying something to the caretaker in the flat official cadence that meant paperwork.

She knew the voice. She'd been trying not to think about the voice for six days.

* * *

The census taker had come to Hessler & Sons the previous Wednesday, and Bram still hadn't decided whether the visit had gone well.

On paper it had. Astrid Lindqvist — a tall woman, mid-twenties, with ink-stained fingers and the unhurried manner of someone paid by the form and not the hour — had sat at the shop's front desk with her ledger open and worked through the household roster with brisk competence. Names, ages, trades, birthplaces. Master Hessler answered for the firm. Kessel answered for himself at length, managing to make even his birthplace sound like an achievement. Then it was Bram's turn, and Bram had given the answers she always gave, the answers she'd been giving for four years now, worn smooth as river stones:

Name: Bram Montrose. Age: twenty-two. Trade: apprentice clockmaker. Birthplace—

Birthplace.

The question had come and Bram had felt it land, the way she'd known it would someday, the way you know a worn gear will fail eventually but never exactly which Tuesday. Because *born in Rostock* was a lie that unraveled if anyone pulled the thread — there'd be records, church books, a parish priest with a memory. And the truth — *born in Flensburg, daughter of Anna Montrose, née—* — the truth wasn't a thread at all. It was a rope, and it went around her neck.

So she'd done what she'd trained herself to do, all those years ago in another city, practicing in a rented room with a mirror: she'd hesitated. Just enough. A boy from nowhere, ashamed of nowhere, embarrassed in front of a pretty clerk.

"Somewhere south," she'd said. "We moved around. My mother kept lodgings."

Astrid Lindqvist had looked at her then. Not at the ledger. At her. Two seconds, maybe three — an eternity in census work — with an expression Bram still couldn't file anywhere. Not suspicion. Not pity. Something more like recognition, which made no sense, because there was nothing to recognize. She'd written something in the ledger. She'd moved on to Kessel's window tax.

Now those same boots were climbing the observatory stairs, and Bram, wedged in the clock's frame with her sleeves rolled and her hands black with oil, had nowhere whatsoever to go.

"Bram Montrose?" The voice came from below the cradle, pitched to carry upward. "Census verification. I'm told the apprentice clockmaker is up there."

"He's up there," the caretaker confirmed, with the serenity of a man who would not be moving quickly for anyone.

"Then I'll wait."

"I wouldn't climb up there if I were you, miss. Ladders don't like skirts."

There was a pause. "I wasn't proposing to climb."

Bram closed her eyes briefly, forehead resting against cold brass. Then she called down, pitching her voice low and easy, the voice she wore like a coat: "Give me five minutes. I've got the escapement apart. If I leave it, the whole train runs down and we lose the hour strike and the harbor office will have my head."

"Take your time," said Astrid Lindqvist, in a tone that suggested she had all the time in the world and intended to spend it here.

* * *

It took eleven minutes, and Bram used every one of them, and hated how aware she was of the woman below the entire time — the small sounds, the scratch of a pen, the caretaker shuffling off somewhere, the particular quality of a silence that contains someone watching your ladder.

She descended with the tool roll slung across her back and her hands wiped as clean as they got, which was not very, and found Astrid standing by the clock case with her ledger closed, hat slightly damp from the fog, looking up at the great dial with an expression of unfeigned interest.

"It's beautiful," she said, without turning around.

"It's tired."

"Tired?"

"Worn spring. Fatigued steel. Somebody papered over it twenty years ago instead of fixing it properly." Bram set down the tool roll. "You're here about forms. The shop's roster was filed last week."

"It was." Astrid turned. Up close her eyes were a pale grey-green, the color of the sea in February, and they did not do Bram the courtesy of pretending to be casual. "Standard practice. Discrepancies get a follow-up visit. Verifications. Signatures."

"What discrepancy?"

"There isn't one. Not officially." Astrid opened the ledger anyway, more from habit than need, and ran her finger down a column. "Apprentice, age twenty-two, born—" she paused, delicately, the way a surgeon pauses over the exact spot, "—somewhere south. We moved around. My mother kept lodgings."

The words in Astrid's mouth sounded exactly as thin as they had in Bram's own. Worse, somehow.

"That's what I said."

"It is." Astrid closed the ledger. "I've verified perhaps four hundred people in eight months. Do you know how most of them answer the birthplace question? Instantly. Their father's village. Their mother's street. People carry the answer around like a coin in the pocket; they give it without thinking. It's the easiest question on the form." She tilted her head. "Yours took three seconds. And in those three seconds, Fräulein—"

"*Don't.*"

The word came out before Bram could shape it — came out in her own voice, the real one, the one she kept locked away, higher and younger than the apprentice's growl, and the horror of hearing it in this room, in this city, in the open air of her one safe life, hit her like cold water. She actually stepped back. Her heel caught the tool roll and she steadied herself against the clock case, palm flat on wood older than both of them, and for one absurd second she wanted nothing so much as to climb back inside the works and bolt the door.

Astrid had not moved. Had not reached for the ledger, or a whistle, or the door. Her face had done something complicated and settled into something quiet.

"Birgitta," she said, softly. Not a question. Not an accusation. Almost — impossibly — an introduction.

"How." Bram's mouth had gone dry. She made herself stand straight, made her hands stay loose at her sides, because running was already impossible and cowering would only confirm it. "How do you have that name."

"Because I grew up on Strandgasse, four doors from your mother's lodgings. Because Emil Lindqvist was my brother, and Emil Lindqvist spent the summer of 1906 following a girl named Birgitta Montrose around the regatta grounds like a lost dog, and I spent that summer listening to him talk about her." Astrid's voice stayed level, almost gentle, and somehow that was worse than shouting. "You think I forgot a face? You've cut your hair and dropped your voice and bound yourself down to a shape that would fool a doctor. Fine. It fooled the guild. It fooled me for three seconds on Wednesday, until you hesitated, and then I watched your hands." She nodded at Bram's fingers. "You hold a pen like a girl taught calligraphy. Boys don't learn that grip. Not here."

The clock ticked above them, enormous, indifferent, keeping its honest time while Bram's whole constructed life stood in the balance of a woman with ink on her fingers.

"Say it plainly," Bram managed. "What happens now. Am I arrested? Reported? What's the procedure — do you take me to the bureau, or does a constable come for me here?"

Something flickered across Astrid's face — surprise, then something that might have been anger, though at whom was unclear.

"Nothing happens now."

"That isn't how it works."

"No," Astrid agreed. "It isn't. Which is why I'm telling you plainly, since you asked." She tucked the ledger under her arm. "Your entry in the register says what you told me. Apprentice clockmaker, born somewhere south. It will continue to say that. I verify the record; the record is consistent; there is no discrepancy. I have no idea why you'd imagine otherwise."

Bram stared at her. "Why."

"Why?" For the first time Astrid looked away — up, at the dial, at the slow patient sweep of the minute hand. When she spoke again her voice had changed, flattened, the official calm stretched thin over something that hurt. "Because four years ago my brother stood in our mother's kitchen and swore that if anything ever happened to any of us, there was one person in this world who'd move heaven to help, and it was a girl who fixed watches and kept promises nobody asked her to keep. Because he talked about you the way sailors talk about fair winds. And because when I saw your name on my route list — *Montrose, B., clockwork apprentice* — I told myself it was a coincidence, and then I met you, and it wasn't."

The silence rang. Somewhere below, the caretaker coughed, distant as a gull.

"I don't know what Emil told you," Bram said carefully.

"I imagine he told you the truth, which is more than either of us seems able to manage professionally." Astrid adjusted her hat brim, and when she looked back her face had reassembled itself into something official, almost, but not quite. "I'll write my verification report this afternoon. The record will show the census of Hessler & Sons is complete and accurate. You'll never see me again in any capacity that matters."

She turned for the stairs.

"Astrid." Bram heard herself use the name — the Christian name, uninvited, reckless — and saw Astrid stop on the top step without turning around. "Why are you telling me this? Truly. A woman in your position could ruin me with a single line in a margin. People have been ruined for less, and been glad of the reason."

For a moment Astrid didn't answer, and the fog pressed against the high windows, and the clock ticked, ticked, ticked.

"Because being seen is terrible," she said finally, still facing the stairs. "I know. I've been invisible myself, in ways the forms don't ask about. And because—" a breath, "—because Emil sails home next month. On the *Vesta*, from Riga. And I find I want him to come home to a city where his friend is still allowed to exist."

Her boots went down the stairs, measured and unhurried, and the door closed behind her with the small civil click of a latch that keeps no secrets and tells none either.

Bram stood a long time in the clock room with her heart going far too fast for a person whose trade was steadiness.

Emil. Coming home. Next month.

She looked up at the dial, at the hands moving with the honesty she'd spent her morning restoring, and felt — beneath the relief, beneath the strange raw gratitude of having been seen and not destroyed — the first cold touch of something else entirely. A premonition, ticking to itself, keeping its own time.

The *Vesta* was due in three weeks.

And the harbor office had told Master Hessler that the master clock must be perfect by then — certified, synchronized, the city's single true time — because, the letter had said, *of matters pending concerning the shipping registry.*

Matters pending. Enemy nationals. The words had meant nothing on Tuesday morning.

Bram picked up her tools, climbed back into the works, and began to fit the new spring with hands that, for the first time in four years, were not entirely steady.
