## 3. What Was Promised in Peacetime

The summer of 1906 smelled of tar and sugar.

Bram remembered it that way first, always—the smell, before anything else. Tar from the shipwrights' sheds along the quay, and sugar from the regatta stalls where vendors sold candied almonds and spun honey over ice. Five years later she could stand at her bench with a loupe screwed into her eye and a hairspring trembling under her tweezers, and one whiff of either smell would pull her straight back to the water, to the noise, to the afternoon she made the worst promise of her life and was glad of it.

She had been seventeen that summer, and still called Birgitta by everyone who mattered, which was no one.

* * *

Her mother had sent her to the harbor with a parcel—a christening gift for the daughter of a woman from the old congregation—and Birgitta carried it the long way along the waterfront because the long way took an hour and the short way took ten minutes, and every hour out of the house was an hour in which nobody told her to sit straighter, speak softer, be more like her dead father's daughter ought to be. She wore a blue dress she hated and her hair pinned up in a style that gave her a headache by noon, and she walked the quay with the parcel balanced on one hip like a woman who had done it all her life instead of a girl pretending at it.

The regatta had swallowed the harbor whole. Bunting strung between mastheads, flags of four nations snapping above the customs house, rowing shells cutting white seams across the gray-green water. A brass band murdered a waltz near the fish market. Boys dove off pilings for coins thrown by laughing officers, and gulls wheeled screaming through it all as if the whole city had gone mad and they approved.

Birgitta stopped at the rail where the crowd thinned, and that was where she saw him: a young man standing on the roof of a moored coal barge, entirely alone, conducting the band with a boat hook.

He was terrible at it. He was magnificent at it. He swept the hook in great arcs, cueing entrances the band could not hear and did not follow, mouthing the oboe part with total commitment, his coat tails flying. He was perhaps twenty, sandy-haired, with the sunburned face of someone who worked outdoors and the impractical boots of someone who wished he didn't. When the waltz collapsed into its final chord he bowed deeply to the empty water, noticed Birgitta watching him, and bowed again—lower—to her.

"Madam," he called up. "You are my entire audience. I hope you found the interpretation faithful."

"It was the finest thing I've heard all summer," Birgitta said, before her caution could catch up with her mouth.

"Then you have had a grim summer," he said, "because we were dreadful." He hopped down from the barge roof to the deck, then up a ladder to the quay with an ease that suggested he'd been climbing on and off boats since he could walk. Up close he had ink stains on his fingers and a ledger tucked under one arm, and when he offered his hand he said, "Emil Lindqvist. Clerk, third class, Harbor Master's office. Also, today only, maestro."

"Birgitta," she said, and shook his hand, and felt something loosen in her chest that she would spend the next five years trying to name.

* * *

That was how it began—not with drama but with an afternoon. He walked her partway home because the parcel was heavy, though it wasn't, and they argued about whether the band had been playing in three-four time or some lawless private time of Emil's own invention, and by the time they reached her street they were friends in the way that happens sometimes in youth: instantly, completely, without either party having agreed to it.

Emil Lindqvist turned out to be everywhere in her life after that. He appeared at the chandlery when her mother sent her for rope. He materialized outside the church on Sundays with two ices from the Italian cart and a look of innocence so transparent it was practically a confession. He got her onto the water twice that summer—once in a borrowed dinghy he nearly capsized through incompetence, once in the Harbor Master's own gig, which he had absolutely no right to borrow and which they returned, miraculously, undamaged and dry, both of them breathless with laughter and guilt.

He never asked her the question everyone else circled. Other people asked why a girl her age wasn't walking out with anyone; why she read books bound in plain paper; why she looked at clocks in shop windows the way other girls looked at jewelry. Emil asked none of it. Instead he asked what she thought about things—the new electric lamps going up along the boulevard, whether the harbor pilots were right to distrust steam tugs, whether God kept time or merely listened to it. He listened to her answers as if they were answers. Once, watching a regatta heat from the seawall, he'd said, apropos of nothing, "You know you're wasted in that dress," and she had gone cold all over, and he'd simply continued—"the blue would do better against the water"—and let it pass, and she understood that he had seen something and chosen, deliberately, to file it away unopened. That was the kindness of him. That was the whole of it. He saw people as they might prefer not to be seen, and then he built them a door out.

In August he showed her the pocket watch.

It had been his grandfather's—a heavy silver case, chased with anchors, and inside it a movement of beautiful old-fashioned construction, a cylinder escapement, the kind of work nobody did anymore. It had stopped years ago and stayed stopped; the balance staff was snapped clean through, a hairline break invisible until you looked.

"Grandfather wound it every morning of his life," Emil said. They were sitting on the breakwater, legs dangling, feet a yard above the lapping water, sharing a paper cone of fried smelt. "Died in March, and the watch died the same week. My mother says it's grief. I say it's a broken part." He turned it over in his fingers. "Everything is a broken part, if you know where to look."

"You should have it repaired."

"Can't afford it. And there's no one I'd trust." He glanced sideways at her, sly. "You're always staring into clock shops. Tell me honestly—do you understand them? Truly?"

And here was the moment she could have lied. Seventeen years old, sitting on a breakwater in the last golden August of the old world, and she could have said *a little*, could have said *I find them pretty*, could have done any of the hundred small self-betrayals that were expected of her daily and that she performed without thinking, the way one performs breathing.

Instead she heard herself say: "I could fix it."

Emil didn't laugh. That was the miracle. He turned the watch over and handed it to her with both hands, formal as a knighting, and said, "Then I'm in your debt, watchmaker."

"I'm not a watchmaker. I've never touched one except through glass."

"You'll learn. You clearly already know everything else." He grinned. "Take it. Fix it whenever you can. No hurry. It's waited eleven years—it can wait eleven more."

* * *

She did not fix it that summer. She took it apart secretly at night on a towel spread across her bedroom floor, memorizing every wheel and bridge, drawing the train in a notebook she hid inside her Bible, and she learned exactly how badly broken it was and exactly what part she'd need and where such a part might be bought, and she saved her allowance toward it coin by coin. But the balance staff defeated her—she couldn't cut one, couldn't buy one without explaining herself—and so the watch sat in her drawer, open-hearted and stopped, all autumn, all winter.

By spring everything had changed, including her name.

* * *

She had left in April. That part came later, in memory, like a wall built across a road: the night departure, the ferry, the false papers bought from a man her mother wept over having to know, the binding cloth she learned to wear, the barbershop mirror, the first time a stranger said *young man* and she answered to it and felt—God forgive her—free. By June of 1907 she was Bram Montrose, apprentice at Hessler & Sons, sleeping in a rented room above a chandler's shop, living a life so small and so carefully constructed that a strong wind could have knocked it over and a strong will never would.

She meant to write to Emil. She composed letters in her head for months. *Dear Emil, I have gone away, I am well, don't look for me.* Every version failed. There was no honest letter that didn't undo her, and no lie she could bear to send him, of all people. So she sent nothing, and told herself that silence was a kind of mercy, and hated herself for the cowardice dressed up as mercy, and got up each morning and went to the workshop and became, hour by hour, better and better at being no one.

She kept the broken watch. It moved with her twice, wrapped in a stocking, buried beneath shirts. Some nights she took it out and held it to her ear as if a stopped watch were a seashell—as if, pressed close enough, it might hold the sound of a summer that had belonged to a person who no longer existed.

* * *

They met again by accident, two years on, in the way that port cities arrange such things.

It was evening, October 1909, fog coming off the water in slow white banks. Bram was carrying a repaired regulator back to a shipping office on the quay, boxed on her shoulder, collars up, hat low. She heard him before she saw him—his voice carrying out of the Harbor Master's doorway, arguing cheerfully about tide tables—and her whole body went rigid, the box suddenly weighing forty pounds.

He came out onto the step lighting a pipe, saw her, squinted through the fog.

"Bram Montrose," she said, before he could speak. Her voice, pitched low, held steady by sheer craft. "New apprentice at Hesslers. You don't know me."

A pause. The fog moved. And then Emil Lindqvist, older, a little thinner around the jaw, looked at this strange young man on his quay and smiled the smile she remembered from the roof of a coal barge, and said, "No. But you've got the same look about you as someone I knew. Someone who owed me a watch."

"I don't know anything about a watch."

"No," he said easily. "Of course not." He shook Bram's hand, warm and firm and lingering half a second past ordinary. "Pleased to meet you, Montrose. This city needs keeping. I'm glad someone's doing it."

He went inside. Bram stood on the quay with her heart slamming and understood, with total clarity, that he knew—that he had known from the first syllable, maybe from the first sight of her walk, that he would carry it silently forever, and that he had just given her the only gift available: the fiction, maintained between them, that she was free.

* * *

After that, they were friends again—or something shaped like friendship, fitted around the shape of her secret. They drank together at the Anchor & Chain, where the harbor men drank and nobody looked closely at anybody. He introduced her as his clockmaker, and the phrase filled her with a pride so fierce it frightened her. They argued about politics, about the growing German presence in the harbor offices, about whether the new patrol schedules posted on the customs house were prudence or provocation. The times were tightening; even in 1910 you could feel the screws going in. Emil felt it most—he was half Swedish on his mother's side, and the registries being drafted in the ministry offices upstairs from his own desk were starting to use words like *national origin* and *repatriation*, words that had teeth.

One night in late spring—they had walked her home the long way along the quay, as they always did, and neither wanted the evening to end—they ended up on the breakwater where they'd first shared fried smelt, except now there was schnapps involved, a bottle Emil produced from his coat with the shameless grin of a man celebrating nothing in particular.

"To the city," he toasted. "May it stay free, wet, and badly governed."

"To badly governed," Bram said, and drank, and the schnapps was harsh and cheap and perfect.

They talked until the bottle was low and the fog was thick and the lights along the quay had gone soft and haloed. And it must have been the drink, or the fog, or the particular tenderness that comes over two people who have kept each other's secrets for years—but Emil went quiet for a while, turning his glass, and then said, without looking at her:

"If it ever comes to it—if they put my name on one of those lists—you'd help me go. Wouldn't you?"

Bram's stomach dropped through the stones of the breakwater into the black water below.

"That won't happen," she said.

"No. Probably not." He shrugged, too casually. "But say it did. Say some clerk upstairs writes my name wrong, or right, and one morning I've got a week to be somewhere very far from here. Would you?"

"Emil—"

"You're the only person in this city," he said, and now he did look at her, and his eyes were perfectly sober, whatever the rest of him was, "who knows how things work. Locks, doors, papers, time. You told me once—everything is a broken part, if you know where to look." He fumbled in his waistcoat and brought out the silver pocket watch, the one she had returned to him repaired two Christmases past, running true ever since. "I carry it every day. Grandfather's watch. Fixed by a friend who doesn't exist." He set it on the stone between them. "So swear it on this. If I ever need leaving, you'll move the hands of the city to get me out. That's all. Just words. Words cost nothing tonight."

"Words cost everything tomorrow," Bram said.

"Tomorrow I'll be a clerk again and you'll be a clockmaker and we'll both pretend we never said it. Come on." He nudged the watch toward her with one finger. "Swear it, watchmaker. Humor a drunk man who reads tide tables for a living."

And she looked at him—her first friend, the keeper of her oldest secret, the boy who had conducted a band that wasn't listening and bowed to an audience of one—and every warning she owned stood in a row inside her and said nothing loudly enough.

She put her hand flat on the warm silver case.

"If you ever need leaving," she said, "I'll move the hands of the city. On my craft, on my hands, on everything I am. I swear it."

Emil grinned, picked up the watch, kissed its case like a relic, and poured the last of the schnapps over the breakwater stones for the sea, which accepted the tribute without comment. Then he laughed, and she laughed, and they walked home through the fog talking nonsense, and the whole thing folded itself away into the pleasant drawer where drunk promises go to sleep.

* * *

Five years later, Bram Montrose stood at her bench in the workshop of Hessler & Sons with a hairspring trembling under her tweezers, and the memory arrived on schedule, the way memories do when they have been waiting.

She had thought of the promise perhaps twice a year since—fondly, absently, the way one thinks of a childhood oath sworn on a graveyard gate. It had seemed safe. Promises made in peacetime, she had believed, dissolve in their sleep when the weather changes. Everyone knew that. Everyone counted on it.

But now there was a war on the horizon with a timetable attached, and registries with columns for national origin, and a census taker with clear gray eyes who had looked at Bram across a counter and said, *You were born Birgitta,* and somewhere upstairs in the ministry a clerk was ruling lines on a list, and names were falling into those lines like coins into a tray.

Bram set down the tweezers very carefully, because her hand had begun to shake, and looked at the small brass clock on the shelf above her bench—the first movement she had ever completed alone, her masterpiece of apprenticeship, ticking away with idiotic faithfulness.

Somewhere in the harbor, Emil was reading tide tables. Somewhere in her locked drawer lay a letter from her mother she hadn't opened. And somewhere in the future—weeks away, maybe days—there was a night tide, and a northbound barge, and a promise standing at the door of her careful little life with its hand raised to knock.

She had sworn it on the watch. On her craft. On her hands.

The hands, Bram thought, were the problem. Everything she was now lived in her hands, and everything she had promised would demand them.

Above the bench, the little clock ticked on, marking time toward an appointment she had made five years ago and hoped, until this moment, she would never keep.
