## 4. The List

The registry arrived on a Tuesday, in a gray canvas satchel with a wax seal the color of dried blood.

Astrid Lindqvist saw it before her supervisor did. She was standing at the filing cabinets when the courier came through the bureau's double doors, shaking rain off his coat, and she watched the clerk at the front desk sign for it with the same bored efficiency he'd sign for a delivery of ink cartridges. That was the thing about lists like this one. They traveled first class only in novels. In life they came by courier, damp and ordinary, and were set down beside the tea things.

"Registry supplement," the clerk said, reading the label aloud to no one. "Enemy nationals. Classification B."

Classification B meant deportation. Classification A meant internment, which everyone understood was simply deportation with a longer wait. Astrid had learned the classifications in her second week at the bureau, along with the stamp colors: blue for verification pending, red for action required, black for closed files that would never be opened again because the people inside them would no longer exist within the city's paperwork.

She went back to her cabinet. Drawer fourteen, surnames H through K. She was verifying addresses on households that had answered the census honestly, which was most of them, because most people had nothing to hide or no imagination about what might count as hiding.

The morning proceeded. The stove ticked. Somewhere above, gulls argued over the harbor's leavings. At half past ten, Supervisor Brandt emerged from his office with the registry under his arm and called for two clerks to help him cross-reference.

"Lindqvist," he said. "You have the steadiest hand in this room. You'll do the fair copy."

The fair copy. The clean version, entered into the permanent record, each name rendered in careful official script so that no patrol officer, no harbor official, no railway conductor could mistake who was to be put on a ship and sent away.

"Yes, sir," Astrid said.

Her voice did not shake. She noted this with a strange, detached pride, the way she'd once noted that her hands didn't shake when she'd sewn the button onto her father's funeral coat.

* * *

They worked at the long table by the window, where the light was best. Brandt read the names; a junior clerk named Ossler checked them against the master census; Astrid wrote.

The list was not long. Forty-one names. The city had spent decades swallowing foreigners—dockworkers, machinists, merchants, sailors between ships—and now it had begun, gently, bureaucratically, to spit some of them back out. The criterion was nationality of birth, not residence, not marriage, not the thirty years a man might have spent unloading coal on these same quays. Birth was the crime. One could not appeal one's birth.

"Holm, Anders. Born Rostock. Machinist."

Astrid wrote it.

"Iversen, Peder. Born Flensburg. Dockhand."

She wrote it.

The fog outside the window had thickened toward noon, pressing against the glass like something that wanted in, or wanted them out. The gas lamps along the quay were already lit, yellow smudges in the white.

Brandt turned the page.

"Lindqvist, Emil. Born—" He paused, squinting. "Born here, isn't he? No. No, born across the strait, before the family moved. Mother was your aunt, wasn't he related to you? Second cousin?"

"Cousin," Astrid said. Her pen did not stop moving. It finished the stroke of the *y* in her own surname—the previous entry, Iversen—and lifted cleanly away from the paper. "My mother's sister's son."

"Mm." Brandt looked at the entry again, then at her, and Astrid watched him perform the small arithmetic that officials perform: relation, embarrassment, liability. "Well. It happens in the best families. War makes strange bedfellows out of blood, eh?"

"It does, sir."

"You'll write it all the same."

"I'll write it all the same."

And she did. *Lindqvist, Emil.* The letters came out even and upright, each one a small act of obedience. She dated the line. She entered the deportation window—three weeks, beginning the first of the month—in the column provided. She initialed the margin as procedure demanded, *A.L.*, two letters that suddenly felt like a signature on a confession.

Ossler brought tea at one o'clock. Astrid drank hers without tasting it.

* * *

The bureau emptied slowly, the way offices do—coats retrieved, lamps trimmed, the stove banked. By six Astrid was alone with drawer fourteen and the fair copy, which lay drying on the blotter under a paperweight shaped like an anchor.

Here is what she had sworn. Not dramatically, not with a hand on anything holy; there had been no ceremony, just a printed oath signed in triplicate when she took the post three years ago. But she had read it, and she had meant it, the way she meant most things: that she would record truthfully, that she would disclose no confidential matter, that the census served the city and the city served its people, and that the two were the same thing. She had believed they were the same thing. She had believed it the way you believe a floor.

Emil had taught her to swim in this harbor, she thought, looking at the fog. He was nine and she was seven and he had held her up by the chin and told her the water would hold her too if she stopped fighting it. He'd been wrong about the water but right about the principle. Their mothers had been sisters who married badly in different countries and consoled each other in letters for twenty years, until Aunt Marta's letters stopped coming and the visits became Astrid's alone. Emil had carried her trunk up four flights when she took this post, joking that his cousin the government woman would come to arrest him someday, ha ha, and she had laughed, because in those days it was funny.

Three weeks.

She thought of Bram Montrose then—not for the first time since the workshop, not without irritation. There was something in the young clockmaker's face when asked a plain question, a stillness like a held breath, that made Astrid want to ask plainer ones. And there was the other thing, the thing she had turned over on the walk home from the observatory two nights ago like a stone with a warm underside: *Birgitta*. A girl's name spoken like a password, given to a stranger for no reason except that strangers sometimes need to give someone something true.

What did Bram Montrose know about promises? What had Emil said, that summer at the regatta, laughing over his broken watch? Astrid hadn't been there. She only knew that Emil spoke of the clockmaker's apprentice the way other men spoke of old comrades—with a warmth that outlasted the acquaintance, and lately, since the registry rumors started, with a kind of faith. *If it comes to it,* Emil had told their aunt on his last visit, *I know someone who can move the hands of this city.*

Foolishness. Sentiment. The kind of promise drunk men make and sober cities laugh at.

But Emil believed it. And belief, Astrid knew from her work, was the most reliable data there was. People lied on forms every day. Almost nobody lied to themselves about whom they trusted.

The stove had gone cold. The anchor paperweight sat on the fair copy like a small, official god.

Astrid took out her notebook—the plain one she used for field verifications, the one that lived in her coat pocket and therefore belonged to no file—and copied the entry. Name. Date and place of birth. Last registered address, the lodging house by the customs shed. Deportation date. Ship if assigned, none yet. She wrote it in her ordinary handwriting, not the official script, as though the words might pass unnoticed if they weren't dressed for the occasion.

It took ninety seconds.

Ninety seconds against three years of oath. She understood, with the clarity that comes only after the fact, that she had decided this sometime earlier—perhaps in the observatory, perhaps years ago at the water's edge with Emil's hand under her chin—and that the copying was merely the moment the decision admitted itself.

She did not feel like a traitor. That frightened her more than feeling like one would have. She felt like a woman finishing her work.

* * *

She returned the fair copy to Brandt's office, squared on his desk, initials aligned. He thanked her without looking up. Outside, the fog had eaten the streetlamps down to halos, and her boots sounded very loud on the cobbles, as though the city were listening for exactly one pair of feet.

At the corner by the chandlery she stopped. The observatory hill rose black against the blacker sky, and at its top the tower's dial glowed faintly, lit for the benefit of the harbor: eleven minutes past eight, and honest about it. Somewhere inside that building was a young man—or a young woman, or whatever precise word applied—with steady hands and a secret, who had promised Emil Lindqvist the impossible.

Astrid took out the notebook and held it a moment, the pages damp at the edges from the fog.

Then she climbed the hill.

The side door, the one tradesmen used, was unlocked; she let herself into the stairwell that smelled of oil and cold brass, and followed the ticking upward, layer upon layer of it, until she reached the works floor and saw lamplight moving behind the great clock's skeleton, and a slim figure in shirtsleeves elbow-deep in the escapement, absorbed, unguarded, unaware.

"Bram Montrose," Astrid said, and the figure went absolutely still, the way a mechanism goes still when a finger touches the balance wheel. "We need to talk about my cousin."
