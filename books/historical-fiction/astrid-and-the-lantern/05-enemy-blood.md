## 5. Enemy Blood

The voice came out of the dark like a dropped wrench, and for one full second Bram did not move at all.

Then she pulled her arm out of the escapement—slowly, deliberately, the way you'd pull a hand from a trap if the trap were watching—and turned with the lamp in her other hand so that the light fell where she wanted it: on her own face, steady, mildly annoyed, the face of a journeyman interrupted at his work and not much caring for it.

"You're not supposed to be up here," she said. "Civilians aren't allowed past the second landing."

"I'm not a civilian," Astrid Lindqvist said. "I'm a census official verifying a registration. And I'm your cousin's sister, which outranks both."

She came forward into the lamplight. She'd climbed the hill fast; there was color high in her cheeks and her breath hadn't settled yet, and she held her notebook against her chest like a shield or a warrant. Up close, in this light, she was younger than her voice had sounded on the stairs. Or older. It depended, Bram thought, on which of them was doing the looking.

"There is no cousin," Bram said. "You said that before. You keep saying it as though repetition will build it into fact."

"Emil Lindqvist." Astrid said the name flatly, without drama, the way a clerk reads an entry. "Born here, in this city, twenty-six years ago. Apprentice cooper, then harbor foreman's assistant. Registered in the fourth district in 1906. You drank with him at the regatta five summers ago. He told me about it himself—the broken watch, the promise. He tells it like a story about someone else now." Her jaw tightened. "He's been dismissed from the harbor. His name went onto a list three days ago."

Bram set the lamp down on the workbench. She did it carefully, because her hands wanted to do it carelessly, and careless hands were how men got found out.

"A list," she said. "What list?"

"You know what list. The whole city knows what list by now; they just don't say it out loud yet. Enemy nationals. Deportation within three weeks of certification. Emil is on it because his father kept his Swedish papers and never naturalized, because my father said it would be sentimental to do otherwise, and now sentiment is a legal category and my brother is standing in it." Astrid's voice stayed level, but Bram could hear the effort holding it there, like a weight hung on a fine spring. "I'm telling you because he won't ask you himself. His pride won't let him. So I'm asking for him, and I want an answer tonight, because I've already lied once today in a government building and I find I don't have the stomach to do it twice."

The clock filled the room around them. That was the thing people who didn't work on machines never understood: a great clock wasn't furniture, it was weather. Its breathing was in the floor, in the walls, in the bones of your inner ear. Bram had spent eleven days inside its skeleton and had come to feel that she lived in its chest.

She made herself stand away from it. Made herself look at Astrid Lindqvist directly, which cost more than she let show.

"Madam Lindqvist," she said. "I think you believe you know something about me. I think you've built a story out of a name and a brother's drunken summer and whatever it was you thought you saw when you came to Hessler's shop asking after births. But I'll tell you what I know. I know I was born in this city. I know I'm apprenticed to the finest clockworks on the Baltic coast. I know that man you're describing is a stranger to me, and that even if he weren't—" she heard her own voice go cold and clean, the voice of prudence, of sense, of every sensible thing she had ever told herself at four in the morning among ticking clocks, "—even if he weren't, I'm an apprentice with no money, no influence, and no power over trains or tides or lists. There is nothing I could do. So whatever answer you came here to collect, you've collected it. I'm sorry for your brother. Truly. But sorrow isn't a plan."

It was a good speech. She'd have believed it herself, maybe, if it hadn't come out of her own mouth.

Astrid didn't move. The lamplight moved instead, the flame guttering slightly in the draft off the stairwell, and their shadows slid along the gear teeth of the great wheel behind Bram like figures on a screen.

"Say his name again," Astrid said quietly.

"What?"

"Say *Emil* again. You said it just now—I'm sorry *for your brother*—and you flinched on the word *brother*. Not on *Emil*. On the word for family." She took a step closer. "You know what I noticed at the workshop? When I asked where you were born, you didn't lie the way liars lie. Liars reach for something smooth. You reached for nothing. You stood there with a file in your hand and let the silence sit, because any name you gave me would be a thread someone could pull, and you have spent years making sure there are no threads on you anywhere." Her eyes were very bright. "That's not a guilty man's silence, Montrose. That's a careful woman's."

The word went through Bram like a pin through a watch crystal—a small, precise, total destruction.

"Get out," she said.

"No."

"This is guild property. If I call down—"

"Call down. Have me removed. And then explain to whoever comes why a census taker walked up four flights unchallenged to find you alone at night, working late on the most important mechanism in the city, sweating." Astrid lifted her chin. "I don't need you to admit anything, Bram. Birgitta. Whoever you are tonight. I need you to listen for five minutes, and then you can throw me out and we never have to speak again, and I will carry my knowledge home and sit on it until it rots. Five minutes. You're a mechanic. You understand tolerances. This is me, asking for a tolerance of five minutes."

Bram's heart was going too fast, and she hated it, hated that her body would not obey the same discipline as her hands. She looked at the clock rather than at the woman. The minute hand crawled. Somewhere below them the city was lighting its lamps and closing its shutters and saying goodnight in two languages, none of it aware that the time it lived by was being rebuilt ten feet above its head by a person who did not legally exist.

"Five minutes," Bram said. "And then you go, and you don't come back."

* * *

Astrid opened the notebook.

"I copied this by hand this afternoon," she said. "There are forty-one names on the first schedule. Emil is number thirty-seven. They're processed by district, and the fourth district comes up in"—she checked a page she didn't need to check, Bram realized; she knew it cold—"eighteen days. After that he goes to the collection point at the east barracks, then a transport north, and after the transport nobody in this city will tell me where he is, because the same law that puts him on the list also makes it rude to ask."

"Deported isn't dead," Bram said.

"No. Deported is deported. It's a train and a border and a war on the other side of it, and he has an aunt in neutral territory, and if he gets there he lives." Astrid turned the page around so Bram could see the copied line, the ink still sharp. "But he doesn't get there by standing in a customs shed waiting for the watch to collect him. Somebody moves him. Somebody who knows the city's rhythms better than the patrols do."

"And you think that's me."

"I think you spent eleven days learning every gear in that tower. I think you can tell the time by the fog. I think my brother swears you promised—" Astrid stopped herself. Started again, quieter. "I think he believes it. He believes it the way he used to believe the tide would come in. And I have watched him wait for you these three days, watching the door of his shed, and it is the only thing keeping him from doing something stupid and fatal, and I resent that, Montrose, I resent it with my whole body, because I don't know you. I know a name on a ledger that shouldn't exist and a pair of hands and a silence where a birthplace should be. And now I'm asking you to be worth his faith. Do you understand how insulting that is to me? I am a servant of the state. I take oaths seriously. I have never falsified so much as a date. And I am standing in a clock tower at night asking a stranger to break laws I enforce, because my brother loved you once and love apparently keeps no records."

The room ticked. Bram became aware, distantly, absurdly, that the third wheel was running a hair heavy on its left pivot and she would need to dress it tomorrow, and she clung to that thought like a plank.

"If I were the person you think I am," Bram said slowly, "what exactly do you imagine I could do? Hide him in a case under my bed? He's a grown man with a face. The harbor gates are watched. The trains are watched. The roads out are watched. In eighteen days they'll be watched harder. You're asking a clockmaker to stop being a clockmaker. That's all you're asking. Everything else is decoration."

"I'm asking you to remember a promise."

"Promises are made by people," Bram said, and heard her own voice crack on it, and covered the crack with cold. "People end. Whatever Emil had at that regatta, he drank it, he lost it, it drowned with the summer. What's left is a man named Bram Montrose, and Bram Montrose has one asset in this world, madam, and it is that no one—not the guild, not the bureau, not the watch—has ever had a single reason to look twice at him. You are asking me to spend the only thing I own to buy a debt I don't owe."

"You owe it to yourself," Astrid said. "Whatever you buried to become him—you think it stays buried while my brother drowns next door? You think guilt respects your disguises?" She closed the notebook. Her hands, Bram noticed, were shaking now that the speech was done; she'd held them steady through all of it, and they shook only afterward, honestly, when it no longer mattered. "I'm not threatening you. I want that understood. I could have gone to my supervisor. Instead I climbed a hill. That should tell you what I am."

"It tells me you're desperate," Bram said. "Desperate people make poor allies and worse witnesses."

Something went out of Astrid's face then. Not hope—Bram doubted she'd arrived with any—but the assumption of common ground, the belief that beneath the lies they were both, at bottom, honest women. She put the notebook away. Straightened her coat. When she spoke again her voice was the public voice, the bureau voice, and it was worse than anger.

"Eighteen days," she said. "If you change your mind, leave a note in the drop box at the corner of the Kanalgasse, marked with a drawn circle. Nothing written—just the circle. I'll understand." She paused at the top of the stairwell, silhouetted against the gray window. "My brother says you were the best liar he ever met, because you lied with your whole life. I used to think that was a compliment. Tonight I watched it done professionally, and I want you to know it's the coldest thing I've ever seen. Goodnight, Montrose."

Her footsteps went down and down, fading under the ticking, until the stairwell gave them back to silence.

* * *

Bram worked for another hour. That was the discipline: when the blood went bad, you worked, because the work didn't care what you were and neither, for a little while, did you.

She dressed the left pivot of the third wheel. She adjusted the pallet stones a quarter-turn and listened to the escapement change its voice, that soft patient tick-tock like a heartbeat with the fever gone. She cleaned the lens of the regulator dial with a chamois until the numerals floated. Somewhere in there her hands steadied. Her hands always steadied. It was the rest of her that had begun, lately, to run fast and lose time.

Because here was the arithmetic she could not escape, no matter how many wheels she dressed: Astrid Lindqvist had climbed the hill and offered blackmail and gotten refusal, and had then—this was the part that would not resolve—given her a way to say yes anyway. A circle drawn on a scrap of paper. No confession, no signature, no thread to pull. A desperate amateur would have threatened. Astrid had engineered her an exit ramp and left the door open behind her, and that was either kindness or competence, and Bram genuinely could not tell which frightened her more.

And underneath it, ticking faster than the great clock itself, the other arithmetic. Emil at the regatta, sunburned, laughing, holding up the ruined pocket watch with its cracked crystal and swearing on it—*you get me out, Bri, whenever I need leaving, you move the whole city to do it, I swear it on this corpse of a watch.* And her own young voice, wine-warm, answering: *sworn and witnessed.* And the watch going into her pocket afterward because Emil said a dead watch belonged with someone who could raise it, and it sat in her drawer to this day with the crack across its face like a scar she'd chosen.

She had told Astrid promises were made by people, and people end. True. All true.

But she had also told herself, for five years, that the person who made that promise had ended, and it was only now, standing in the machine she'd rebuilt with her own careful invisible hands, that Bram admitted the truth: Birgitta Montrose had not ended. She had simply been wound down and set aside, running slow in a drawer, like everything else in this city that the new time had no place for.

And the new time was coming. Eighteen days, and then the master clock certified, and the patrols marching to her pendulum, and a coal-barge tide or a train or a road—all of it governed, soon, by a mechanism so accurate that no one would ever slip through the city again. She had thought she was building her masterpiece. She was building the wall.

Bram put down her tools. She looked at her reflection in the polished brass of the motion-work—thin, sharp-faced, a young man with tired eyes—and the reflection looked back with an expression she recognized from mirrors long ago, from a girl's bedroom in another life, the night before she cut her hair: the expression of someone about to lose an argument with herself.

The lamp burned low. Below, the city slept between bells.

At the corner of the Kanalgasse, Bram knew, there was a cast-iron drop box bolted to a wall, and somewhere in the city a customs shed held a sleeping man who still believed in a promise made by someone who no longer existed—and Bram stood in the ticking dark a long time, not moving, while the minute hand above her crawled toward the hour, and toward the eighteenth day, and toward whatever she was going to do about it.
