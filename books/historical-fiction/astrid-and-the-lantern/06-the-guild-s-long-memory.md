## 6. The Guild's Long Memory

The summons came on a Tuesday, delivered by a boy with ink-stained cuffs who didn't wait for a reply.

*Master Hessler requests the apprentice Montrose at the close of business.*

Bram read the note twice, then folded it into quarters and slid it beneath the leather apron hanging by her bench, where it lay against her ribs like a second heartbeat. *Requests.* Hessler did not request. Hessler summoned, or he sent Kessel to sneer, or he said nothing at all and let the silence do the work of a reprimand. In four years at Hessler & Sons, Bram had been called into the back office exactly twice: once when she'd milled a pivot three-tenths too thin and ruined a carriage clock worth more than her yearly wages, and once when old Hessler's wife had died and he'd needed someone to sit in the shop while he wept in private, because grief was the one thing he couldn't allow the guild to see.

Neither occasion had ended well for anyone.

She worked through the afternoon the way she'd taught herself to work through every dangerous hour: hands moving, mind elsewhere, the body running on rails it had laid years ago. Filing a pallet stone to tolerance. Truing an escape wheel under the loupe. The small brass world of the bench, where everything obeyed laws she could trust — where nothing asked where she was born or what name had been written in the parish register before she'd scratched it out of existence.

Around her, the workshop ticked in its dozens of voices. Wall regulators, carriage clocks, a ship's chronometer awaiting collection, all of them keeping their separate times like separate lives. Bram had always found that comforting. A room full of clocks was a room full of alibis.

Kessel watched her from across the aisle. She felt it without looking up, the way you feel weather change. He had a talent for watching that he mistook for a talent for craft; his work was adequate, his ambition was not, and he'd spent four years waiting for Bram Montrose to make a mistake large enough to climb over. Lately — since the observatory commission, since her name had begun appearing in guild bulletins next to phrases like *exceptional restoration aptitude* — the waiting had curdled into something with edges.

"You've been summoned," he said, not looking up from his own bench, filing with the flat, graceless stroke that would have earned Bram a cuffing in her first year.

"News travels fast."

"The boy who brought it told everyone." Kessel blew filings off his plate. "Close of business. That's the office, isn't it? He doesn't take apprentices to the office unless—" He let it hang, the way he liked to let things hang, a hook baited and left dangling. "Unless something's changing."

"Then I'll find out at close of business."

"Journeyman's papers," Kessel said, and now he did look up, and his eyes were doing arithmetic. "That's what they gave Weissmann. Office, close of business, papers. And Weissmann wasn't half the hand you are, which must gall him, so I suppose it follows—"

"Kessel."

"Mm?"

"Your escape wheel is cutting loose on the fourth tooth. It has been for two days. If you spent half the time on your own bench that you spend on mine, you'd have heard it."

He flushed. It was a small victory and she paid for it immediately, because he went quiet in the wrong way — the way of a man who has decided to stop talking and start counting. She could feel him counting for the rest of the afternoon. Counting the times she declined tea when the shop girl came around. Counting how she never rolled her sleeves above the elbow even in August, how she kept her collar buttoned, how she left for the privy alone and returned alone and never lingered. Small evasions. She'd built them so carefully, one per day, no more, each one load-bearing — and now she could feel them being weighed, one by one, on Kessel's cheap little mental scale.

At close of business, she hung her apron, took the note from behind it, and walked the corridor to the office with her spine doing the work her stomach refused to do.

* * *

Hessler's office smelled of pipe smoke and machine oil and, faintly, of the pickled herring he ate from a tin every day at noon and pretended nobody knew about. He sat behind a desk too big for the room, and on the desk, between a bust of some dead guildmaster and a dish of screws sorted by diameter, lay a folder stamped with the seal of the Clockmakers' Guild of the Free City.

"Sit down, Montrose."

She sat. The chair was lower than his, deliberately; she'd noticed that years ago and had never once let it show.

"Do you know what this is?" He tapped the folder without opening it.

"Papers of some kind, Master."

"It is your examination record." He opened it now, unhurried, a man who enjoyed the theater of documents. "Four years. Theory marks in the top decile. Practical assessments — I'll read this aloud, because I want you to hear how it sounds." He cleared his throat with ceremony. "'The apprentice Montrose demonstrates a command of escapement theory unusual in one so young. His finishing is patient to the point of obsession. His instinct for the relationship between mass and impulse is, frankly, better than mine was at twice his age.'" He closed the folder. "I wrote that myself. Last spring. I don't write praise often. It depreciates."

"I know, Master."

"So." He leaned back until the chair creaked. "Here is what happens now. The observatory commission reaches its certification in six weeks. The council will inspect the master clock, and if it performs — and it will perform, because you will not permit it otherwise — the guild will certify it as the official timekeeper of the free city. Curfew bells. Train departures. Patrol synchronization. Every clock in every harbor office and police station set to that one movement." He said it plainly, without pride, the way a man describes a bridge or a tax. "And the maker of record — the journeyman whose hand certified the work — will be named in the register. Permanently. Guild records outlive us all, Montrose. They are the longest memory in this city. Longer than churches. Longer than families."

Bram's mouth had gone dry somewhere around *patrol synchronization*. "Yes, Master."

"Yes," Hessler agreed. "So here is my offer, and I will make it once, so attend. Complete the master clock flawlessly. Not well — flawlessly. There is a difference, and the difference is your career. Do that, keep yourself beyond reproach in the interim — no debts, no disputes, no irregularities of any kind — and at certification I will sign your journeyman's papers myself. Full standing. Your own bench. The right to take commissions under the guild's seal." He picked up his pipe, tamped it, did not light it. "You understand what that means. No more answering to 'boy.' No more apprentice wages. A name in the register that no one can take away."

A name in the register.

Bram sat very still and let the sentence turn over inside her, the way she'd turn a mechanism to see where it caught. Because that was the thing about Hessler's offer — it was real. That was the unbearable part. Four years of binding her chest before dawn, of answering to a name she'd invented in a border town at seventeen, of measuring every friendship against the risk of discovery — four years, and here was the prize, held out on a silver salver: legitimacy. Papers. A name in the guild's long memory, written in ink that outlived bodies.

It was also a cage with the door painted on it.

*Beyond reproach.* She heard the phrase again and felt its true weight. Beyond reproach meant no questions. It meant that between now and certification, no one — not a census taker, not a rival, not a clerk with a registry — could look at Bram Montrose too closely and expect the looking to survive scrutiny. It meant the trap and the prize were the same object, and Hessler, honest craftsman that he was, had no idea he'd just offered her both.

"There is one other matter," Hessler said, and Bram's pulse ticked up a beat. "Kessel."

"Master?"

"He has approached me. Twice now. With concerns." Hessler's voice flattened into the tone he used for defective movements. "About your conduct. Your — evasions, he calls them. Your refusal of company. Your habit of leaving the workshop by the rear stair rather than the front door. Small things. He presents them as concern for the guild's reputation during an important commission."

Bram kept her face the way she kept her clocks: regulated. "I take the rear stair because the front stair is where he stands."

Something moved at the corner of Hessler's mouth. Not quite a smile. Recognition, maybe — a man who had spent fifty years among apprentices knowing precisely what apprentices were. "I told him that concerns without evidence are noise, and noise is a defect in the workshop. I told him if he brought me a third complaint without substance, I would consider the source defective instead." He lit the pipe at last, and the match flared between them. "But understand me, Montrose. I can protect you from noise. I cannot protect you from evidence. Whatever it is you're carrying — and every person carries something, so don't mistake this for curiosity — whatever it is, you will bury it deeper than you think necessary, and then bury it again. Six weeks. Beyond reproach. After certification, the register makes you untouchable." Smoke curled toward the ceiling. "Before it, you are the most exposed man in this guild."

*Man.* The word landed the way it always did, a coin dropped into a slot that didn't fit but had learned to accept it. Bram stood when he dismissed her, bowed the correct depth, and walked out along the corridor past the long case clocks, each of them striking the quarter hour in staggered succession, so that the hallway rang for a full minute with time being kept imperfectly, humanly, by machines that only ever wanted to be right.

* * *

She didn't go straight home. She walked, which was itself a small evasion — the route home was direct, and direct routes were for people with nothing to hide. Instead she took the harbor road, where the fog was coming in off the water in slow grey banks, and the cranes stood against the last light like gallows, and the gulls made their arguments overhead.

Six weeks.

She tried to hold the two halves of it together in her mind and they wouldn't join. On one side: the papers, the register, the name that no one could take away — except that every name in the register was a lie of omission, hers most of all, a name with no birth behind it, no mother listed, no parish, no history, a name she had assembled from a dead uncle and a changed vowel and sheer nerve. If the guild's memory was the longest memory in the city, then hers was the shortest: four years deep, and below that, nothing. A shaft with a false floor.

On the other side: Astrid Lindqvist, standing in the observatory's dome light with the census ledger under her arm, saying *Birgitta* in a voice pitched for no one else to hear. Saying it gently, which was worse. Bram had survived exposure by force before — a border guard's suspicion, a landlady's prying — and force she knew how to answer. But mercy undid her. Mercy meant there was now one person alive outside herself who could end her with a signature, and had chosen not to, and might choose differently tomorrow, and Bram owed her — what? Gratitude? Silence? The truth?

And beneath both halves, deeper than either, the third thing she refused to look at directly: Emil. *If you ever need leaving, I'll move the hands of the city to do it.* Five years old, that promise, sworn on a broken watch over regatta beer, by a person who no longer existed — Birgitta had made it, and Birgitta was supposed to be dead, and promises were supposed to die with their makers. But promises didn't work like mechanisms. There was no mainspring you could remove, no pallet you could file down until the debt ran out. It stayed wound. She could feel it staying wound.

Beyond reproach, Hessler had said. Six weeks of being the most careful creature in the free city, while a census taker held her secret and a rival counted her evasions and a promise ticked in the dark like something buried alive.

She stopped at the rail where the harbor road bent toward the fish market, and looked back the way she'd come. The observatory hill rose black against the darkening east, and at its crown, faint as a held breath, the master clock's temporary dial caught the first lamp-light — her work, her masterpiece, soon to be the single heartbeat every patrol and prison and train platform in the city would march to.

Someone was standing near the base of the hill, under the plane trees by the customs wall. Still. Watching upward, the way she herself had been watching.

Even at that distance, even in fog, she knew the set of those shoulders. Kessel had no business anywhere near the observatory after hours. Unless he was following the one person in the city with a reason to be there.

Unless he was following her.

Bram turned up her collar and walked home the long way, and behind her the city began, street by street, to strike seven — a dozen church towers disagreeing with one another by seconds, none of them yet knowing that soon they would all be forced to agree, and that the agreement would be her name, and that her name was a door with nothing solid behind it.

Three streets from her lodgings, she heard footsteps fall in behind her, unhurried, matched to hers.

They were still there when she reached her door.
