## 7. Emil at the Door

Bram's hand went to her pocket for the little knife she carried for cutting gut cord, and then the fog thinned around the shape behind her and she knew the walk before she knew the face.

Emil Lindqvist had always walked like a man arriving somewhere pleasant. Even now, even in a coat too thin for October and shoes gone soft at the seams, he had that unhurried sailor's gait, as if any street were a deck and any weather merely weather. Five years had put gray in his sideburns and hollows under his eyes, but the shoulders were the same, and the grin — God help her, he was grinning.

"Montrose," he said. "You walk like a man being followed."

"You were following me."

"I was walking behind you. There's a difference." He held out his hand.

Bram did not take it. She stood with her key half-turned in the lock of her door and felt the cold come up through the soles of her boots, and understood that everything she had built in this city — the room above the watchmaker's shop, the name signed in the guild ledger, the careful flatness of her voice, the five years of small daily acts of becoming Bram Montrose — all of it was standing on this doorstep in a bad coat, waiting to be let in.

"Emil." She said it quietly, testing the weight of it. "How did you find me?"

"Astrid told me you were here. Years ago. She writes it down when people tell her things; it's how she's made." He said his sister's name easily, without knowing what it meant to Bram now, what it had come to mean since the observatory, since the census form with its question about birthplaces that no honest answer could survive. "Then I asked at Hessler & Sons. The boy who sweeps there is chatty."

The boy who sweeps there. She filed the sentence away with all the other sentences that could one day be used against her, in the drawer where she kept them.

"Inside," she said. "Not out here."

* * *

Her lodgings were one room and a corner: bed, stove, washstand, and along two walls the clocks. Eleven of them now, bought broken at auction or taken in trade from customers who didn't want them, each one running because Bram could not bear a stopped clock in the same room where she slept. They ticked in their different rhythms, eleven hearts none of which agreed with any other, and she had long ago stopped hearing them the way you stop hearing your own pulse.

She heard them tonight.

Emil stood in the doorway dripping fog and looked around the room with an expression she couldn't read — something between admiration and grief. "You have a trade," he said. "A real one. Look at these. Did you make them run?"

"I made them tell the truth. That's most of the work."

He laughed, the same laugh, and hung his coat on the back of her only chair, uninvited, familiar, as though the intervening years were a weekend. That ease was the thing about Emil. Astrid had it too, Bram realized — the terrible comfort some people took in simply occupying space near you, as if proximity were permission. Astrid occupied space differently, though: like an auditor. Emil occupied space like a brother.

"Sit," Bram said, because her legs wanted to sit anyway.

"I'll stand. I've been sitting for three days on a coal lighter from Danzig." He warmed his hands over her stove without asking. "I got your letter. Last year. About the regatta, the anniversary. I never wrote back. I'm sorry for that."

"It doesn't matter."

"It does to me." He turned. The stove light caught his face and she saw properly, then, how much the years had cost him: not age exactly but erosion, the way water works at a piling. "It matters because I'm going to ask you for something, Birgitta, and I need to know we're square before I ask it."

The name landed like a dropped tool on a workshop floor.

"Don't—" she began.

"There's no one else in this room," Emil said, "and there are eleven clocks ticking, and I have been carrying your real name across four borders like contraband for five years because I thought someday it would buy you something. So yes. Birgitta. Sit down. Listen."

She sat. It was either that or stand up and open the door, and some part of her — the part that had spent the evening hearing footsteps behind her and knowing whose they were before she turned — had already sat down, hours ago, when the census taker first wrote *where were you born* in her neat clerk's hand and waited for an answer.

"Talk," Bram said.

"They dismissed me Tuesday." He said it flatly, the way men report weather. "Eleven years on those quays. Foreman twice. The harbor master read it off a list. Not my name specifically — a list. Enemy nationals, registry of, effective immediately, pending deportation." He smiled, and it was the worst thing she'd seen all year. "They gave me a paper with a date on it. Three weeks. I asked what happens if I'm still in the city after the date, and the clerk looked at me the way you look at a clock that's stopped. Like the answer was self-evident and slightly boring."

Bram said nothing. One of the clocks, a French carriage piece with a faulty escapement she'd been meaning to fix for a month, ticked fractionally faster than the rest, and in the silence it sounded almost like speech.

"So I came to you," Emil said. "Because five years ago — do you remember? Midsummer, the regatta, you'd had that awful cherry brandy—"

"I remember."

"—and my pocket watch had died that afternoon, salt water, off the Petrel, and you opened it up on the harbor steps and couldn't fix it there but you wouldn't give it back to me. You kept it. And I said *when am I getting that back,* and you said—" His voice caught, just once, a hitch like a gear catching a tooth. "You said, *when you need leaving, I'll move the hands of this whole city to do it.* Do you remember saying that?"

Bram remembered everything. That was the curse of her kind of mind: she remembered the exact slant of the sun on the water, the taste of the cherry brandy, the weight of the dead watch in her palm, and the fact that she had sworn it laughing, drunk, at twenty-one, in a world where such promises were currency that never came due. She remembered thinking: *this is what having a friend is.* She had not had many.

"I was drunk," she said.

"You don't get to take it back for being drunk. Drunk words are just sober ones without the fear in them." Emil crouched in front of her chair, close enough that she could smell coal smoke and salt on him. "I'm not asking you to move the city, Birgitta. I'm asking if the promise stands. Yes or no. That's all. Because I've got three weeks and nowhere in this city that's mine anymore, and every plan I make starts and ends with a promise you made me on a set of harbor steps in peacetime."

And here was the moment. Here was the fork in her life, laid out plain, the two rails diverging, and she saw both of them whole in a single instant the way she could see a whole mechanism from a drawing.

One rail: *yes.* Yes, and then the forging of papers, the bribes, the barge, the lies stacked on lies until the tower of them reached her own throat — and beneath it all the certainty that discovery meant not deportation for her but something worse, because a woman who had lived five years as a man inside a guild, inside a *registry*, was a fraud in the eyes of every court in the Baltic, and Emil's escape would drag her name into the light like a body out of deep water. She saw it: the cell, the inquiry, Astrid's face across a table, the clocks sold off by strangers.

The other rail: *no.*

"No," Bram said.

The word came out steady. She had trained her voice for five years; it did what she told it.

Emil didn't move for a long moment. Then he straightened up slowly, the way old men do, although he was thirty-four. "No?"

"The promise doesn't stand. Things change. War changes them. I have a position here, Emil — I'm six months from journeyman's papers. What you're asking isn't a favor, it's treason with extra steps, and I'd be a fool—"

"A fool." He nodded, taking it in, and she watched him decide to be gentle about it, which was worse than anger. "All right. All right. You've built something. I understand building something." He retrieved his coat from the chair. "For what it's worth, I believe you. That's the hell of it. You're a person who keeps things true — the clocks prove that. If you say the promise is dead, then it's dead, and I won't hold a corpse against you."

"Emil—"

"I'll stay at the customs shed by the coal yard. The night watchman owes me from '09." He shrugged the coat on. "Don't worry. I won't come to the workshop. I know what you've got at stake." At the door he paused, hand on the latch, and did not turn around. "Keep the watch, by the way. Sell it, if you like. It was never really mine after that day — you'd earned it more than me, the way you looked at it. Like it was a person."

The door closed behind him. His footsteps went down the stairs, unhurried, matched to nothing now, and faded into the street noise.

Bram sat in her chair and listened to eleven clocks keep time without her.

* * *

She did not sleep.

This was not new — sleep and Bram had negotiated a difficult treaty years ago — but tonight the clocks refused to fade into the wallpaper the way they usually did. Every one of them was suddenly articulate. The carriage clock with its quick tick said *coward, coward, coward* in French. The tall case in the corner, which she had rebuilt movement-and-all and loved best of all of them, struck the quarter hour with a sound like a verdict. The alarm clock on the sill, a cheap tin thing she kept only because its bell was loud enough to wake the dead, seemed to be counting down something.

Shame, she discovered, keeps excellent time. It arrives on schedule. It does not drift.

She got up at some black hour and lit the lamp and took Emil's pocket watch out of the drawer where it lived under her spare eyeglasses and a bundle of guild correspondence. Salt water had gotten into it five years ago and she had never finished the repair — had never *wanted* to, some stubborn superstitious corner of her insisting that the watch should stay dead until the promise was either paid or honestly buried. She held it now under the lamp: the corroded balance staff, the mainspring seized into a rusted knot, the case back engraved *E.L.* in a sailor's clumsy script.

*I'll move the hands of this whole city.*

What a thing to say. What a stupid, magnificent, drunken thing — the kind of sentence only someone who believes the world is still soft says aloud. And she had said it in peacetime, and peacetime was a country that no longer existed, and its debts — did its debts travel? Was a promise like a note of exchange, good in any jurisdiction? Or did it expire with the government that issued it?

She set the watch down and pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes until she saw gears in the dark.

Because that was the truth she was circling, the truth with teeth: she hadn't said no to protect herself from the risk. Or — no. She had. That was the lie she'd tell herself, and it was even partly true. But underneath it, colder: she had said no because saying yes would mean letting Emil see her. Not the secret — he'd known the secret for years and carried it gently, like contraband, he'd said so himself. He would see *her*. The person underneath the apprentice, underneath the bound chest and the lowered voice and the name signed in a man's hand. The girl from the regatta who swore impossible things and meant them. Bram Montrose could refuse Emil Lindqvist and remain intact. Birgitta — Birgitta owed him, and Bram had killed Birgitta precisely so that no one would ever collect.

Except Astrid had seen. Astrid had written *Birgitta* on a scrap of paper in the observatory gallery with the master clock ticking overhead, and hadn't betrayed her, and the sky hadn't fallen, and Bram had walked home from that feeling like a woman who has touched a live wire and lived — changed at the cellular level, unable to stop hearing the hum.

Two people alive in the world knew her true name. By morning it would be three, if she counted the mother she hadn't spoken to since she left home — and she did count her, at 3 a.m., lying in the dark, doing arithmetic she despised.

The tin alarm clock rang. Six o'clock. Time to bind her chest, lower her voice, walk to Hessler & Sons through streets already filling with men going to work, and stand at the bench beside Kessel and be the best apprentice the guild had seen in a decade.

Bram dressed in the dark. At the door she paused, went back, and put Emil's dead pocket watch in her coat pocket — not the drawer. Her pocket. Where she'd feel its weight against her hip all day, a small stopped heart.

On the stairs she passed her landlady coming up with bread. "Late night, Herr Montrose?" the woman asked pleasantly.

"Work," Bram said. "There's always work."

And there was — but all day, at the bench, under Master Hessler's approving eye, with Kessel watching sidelong between tasks, Bram's hands moved with perfect precision on work she no longer believed in, while in her coat pocket a dead man's watch lay against her hip, and in a customs shed by the coal yard a living man slept badly believing in her anyway, and somewhere across town a census clerk was writing down names in a book that decided who belonged.

Three weeks, Emil had said. The clerk had given him a date.

Dates, Bram thought, filing a pivot to within a hair's width, dates are just numbers until someone makes them true. Someone had to move the hands.

She hated herself for thinking it. She hated, more, that she was already beginning to design it.
