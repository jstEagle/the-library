## 8. Curfew Hours

The city after nine o'clock was a different animal—same bones, new habits.

Astrid had walked the census routes by daylight for three years and thought she knew every street in the harbor district: the fishmongers' row with its silver-smelling gutters, the chandler's yard stacked with rope like coiled hair, the crooked lane behind the customs house where the buildings leaned toward each other as if sharing secrets. By day these streets belonged to commerce, to noise, to the honest ugliness of work. By night, under the new curfew ordinance, they belonged to boots and whistles and the particular silence of people pretending not to be home.

She should not have been out. Her own office had drafted the regulation; she had stamped the circulars herself, forty copies, her thumb aching from the press. *All persons found abroad between the hours of nine and five without written permit will be detained for questioning.* The ink had been barely dry before she'd learned to read it twice—once as a clerk, once as a sister.

The lantern was her excuse. Bureau property, brass, with the double-headed eagle stenciled on the glass. Any watchman who stopped her would see an official on official business, verifying addresses, and the watchmen were mostly old men who wanted only to finish their rounds and go home to wives who complained about the cold. She carried a folder of blank forms as ballast. It was almost enough to convince even her.

Almost.

The fog came off the water in slow gray sheets, and the gas lamps turned it to a dirty gold that never quite reached the ground. Astrid moved through it listening—not for patrols but for the absence of them, the gaps in the whistle-pattern she'd memorized over four nights of walking. The eastern beat passed the customs shed at ten minutes past the hour and again at twenty-five to. That left a pocket of thirty-five minutes in which the shed belonged to nobody, or to whoever was desperate enough to sleep among confiscated cargo.

Emil had been gone from his lodgings for six days. She knew because she'd gone there first, before the bureau could, and found the landlady wringing her hands and saying he'd left owing two weeks' rent, and wasn't it a shame, such a polite young man, though of course with the war talk and all, one couldn't be too careful, could one? Astrid had agreed gravely and noted nothing, and had spent the nights since walking a circuit of every place a man might hide when the city had decided what he was before asking who he was.

The registry said *Lindqvist, Emil — laborer — enemy national — deportation ordered.* Three weeks, the file said, though files lied with the ease of anything official. Her supervisor had already begun talking about quotas, about how the northern district was behind schedule, about how sentimentality at the clerical level was a form of sabotage. He'd said it while looking directly at her, because everyone knew Lindqvist was a harbor name, and everyone knew what the harbor names were now.

She found the shed door ajar.

Not broken—ajar, wedged with a folded square of cardboard, the kind of careful entry that meant someone inside was trying not to be caught rather than trying to get away. Astrid stood in the fog and listened to her own heart argue with itself. Go home. Report it. Let the machinery do what machinery does; you are a cog, cogs don't weep.

She lifted the wedge and went in.

* * *

Inside smelled of tar, coal dust, and old rope—the customs house stored its seizures here, bales and crates marked in chalk with lot numbers, everything waiting for an auction that never quite came. A sliver of moonlight came through a gap in the roof planks and fell across a shape against the far wall that might have been sacks.

Might have been, until it coughed.

"Don't," the shape said. "Please don't."

"Emil." She set the lantern down where its light would find his face and not blind him. "It's me."

He took a long moment to become her brother. Six days had done what six days do to a man hiding: hollowed his cheeks, sharpened the line of his jaw, taught his eyes to flinch at light. He was twenty-six and looked forty, wrapped in a borrowed coat that smelled of fish oil, sitting up slowly against the wall as if sudden movement might cost him something he couldn't spare.

"Astrid." His voice cracked around her name. "God. How did you—"

"You snore," she said. "You've always snored. Even through the floorboards at home, Mother used to complain." She sat down on an upturned crate across from him, skirts pooling in the dust, and let the absurdity of the domestic detail hang between them. It worked better than sympathy. His shoulders came down half an inch.

"I didn't think you'd come."

"I nearly didn't." True. She'd stood outside the shed for ten full minutes doing arithmetic with her life. "I'm going to feed you first, and then I'm going to shout at you, and then we're going to decide what happens next. In that order. Agreed?"

He almost smiled. "You always were the one who made lists."

"One of us had to." She opened the satchel she'd packed in the dark of her kitchen: bread, hard cheese, a jar of pickled herring she'd bought from a stall keeper who asked no questions because she paid no attention to answers, a flask of coffee gone lukewarm. She watched Emil eat the way she imagined people watched drowning men surface—with a terror that looked, from outside, exactly like patience.

He ate fast, then slowed himself deliberately, the way starving men learn to, and she had to look away from that, at the chalked lot numbers on the crates, because watching her brother ration his own hunger was more than the night required.

* * *

"Now," she said, when the bread was gone. "Explain yourself. Not the registry version. Yours."

"There isn't much of one." He wiped his hands on his trousers. "They dismissed the foreign crews first. Then the registered ones. I got the letter on Tuesday—*services no longer required, effective immediately, report to the registration office within seventy-two hours.* I reported. They gave me a card and a date." He produced it from inside his coat: a gray rectangle, official type, his name misspelled. "Deportation transport leaves on the twenty-third. North, they say. Processing, they say."

"And you're going to sit in a shed for three weeks eating stolen cheese until they collect you."

"What's the alternative? Run?" He laughed, and it was the worst sound she'd heard all year, because it had no bottom to it. "Run where? Every road north has a checkpoint. Every train has a manifest. Every port—" He gestured at the walls around them, the whole fog-bound city beyond them. "Every port has a customs house, Astrid. You know what's in those houses better than I do."

"So you'll wait here to be collected like a crate. Like something seized."

"It's what I am now. That's the whole point of the card." He held it up again, and she wanted to knock it out of his hand. "They didn't ask me a single question when I registered. Did you know that? Four years loading timber, one drunk fight at the regatta when I was nineteen, and not one question. Just the name. The name did everything. I stood there thinking, this is what it feels like. Being reduced. You stand very still and a stranger writes your whole life into a box, and then you're a box too."

Astrid knew precisely how it felt. She wrote boxes for a living.

"You could appeal," she said, and heard how thin it sounded even as it left her mouth.

"On what grounds? My mother was born on the wrong side of the water?" He shook his head. "There's a clerk in some office deciding my life with a stamp, and I will never see his face. That's the part that—" He stopped. Pressed his knuckles against his mouth for a moment. "That's the part I can't get past. Not the leaving. The not-being-seen."

*Oh, Emil.* She thought of the workshop, of the boy-apprentice with the burn-scarred forearms who answered to *boy* and kept his eyes down and bound something flat beneath his shirt—she'd known since the second census visit, had carried the knowledge for weeks like a coal in her chest—and she thought: *you both understand being unseen. That's why he promised you.*

"Why her?" she said. It came out harder than she intended. "Montrose. Why do you trust her?"

Emil went still in a way that told her everything before he said a word.

"She hasn't answered my letters," he said carefully.

"No. Hasn't she." Astrid folded her hands so they wouldn't do anything. "And yet you sit in a freezing shed six days from a transport, and somehow I don't believe you're waiting for the authorities. You're waiting for her."

"I'm not asking her for anything." But his eyes went to the roof gap, to the fog beyond it, to somewhere else entirely. "Five years ago, at the regatta—we'd been drinking that rotten cherry brandy Kessler's cousin sold, and I'd just lost my father's watch over the side of the boat, dropped it in the harbor like an idiot, and I was carrying on about how it couldn't be fixed, how Father would—" He stopped. "Bram fished it out. Dove in, fully clothed, in front of everyone. Came up holding it like it was a live coal and said, *give me time and I'll give you back time.* Something like that. Drunk nonsense. And then—" He shrugged, embarrassed by the memory and unable to leave it alone. "Then later, sober, by the fire, Bram said: if it ever comes to it, if you ever need to be gone from this city, I'll move the hands for you. Whatever it takes. I'll make the city lie for one night."

"That's a mad thing to promise."

"That's a mad person's promise," Emil agreed, with a fondness that made Astrid's chest hurt. "But Bram doesn't say things Bram doesn't mean. You spend an afternoon watching her work on a movement and you understand that. Every gear does exactly what it was made to do. Nothing extra. Nothing false." He looked at her finally, directly. "So no, I'm not asking. But yes. I'm waiting. Because I believe her. The way I believed her when everyone said the watch was ruined and she put it back together in eleven days."

Astrid stood up. Walked three steps into the dark end of the shed and back, because sitting still had become impossible.

"You believe in someone you've seen maybe a dozen times in five years," she said. "Someone who won't answer your letters. Someone whose whole life is a locked room I happened to glimpse through a keyhole, and I'm telling you, Emil, whatever is in that room is fragile and frightened and worth more to her than your promise, and you're betting your deportation date on it anyway."

"Yes."

"Just—yes?"

"Yes." He said it simply, without defiance, which was worse. "When I was small and our father lost his berth, and everyone said we'd lose the house too, you were the one who said *we'll manage,* and we managed. I believed you because you were right. This is the same thing, Astrid. Some people are load-bearing. You know when you've met one. I've met one."

"And if she can't do it? If moving the hands costs her everything—the guild, the papers, the name she's built—what then? You'll still be on the transport on the twenty-third, and she'll be ruined beside you, and I'll have helped."

"Then it will have been worth it to try." He said it without drama, and she realized with a lurch that he meant it, that this was not bravado but the plain arithmetic of a man who had already counted his losses. "Astrid. Listen to me. You came here tonight with bread in a bag and a bureau lantern in your hand, walking curfew you helped write, looking for a brother the state has decided doesn't count. Do you think I don't see what you've already spent? You inherited this the moment you copied that list. Don't resent me for handing you the rest."

"I don't—" She stopped. Started again. "You don't know what you're asking me to carry."

"I know exactly." Gently. Infuriatingly gently. "I watched you carry Father's debts at seventeen."

The silence that followed had weight and texture. Somewhere outside, a patrol whistle sounded, distant, moving away, and they both froze and then exhaled together, and the shared flinch did what argument couldn't: it made them family again, two children of the same drowned watch, hiding in a room full of other people's confiscated things.

Astrid sat back down.

"Here is what I know," she said, when she trusted her voice. "Her file is clean. Too clean—she appeared in the registers five years ago out of nowhere, no birth record I can find, which means someone buried the trail well, which means the masquerade isn't a costume, it's architecture. And she has refused you. I've seen her refuse you. I stood in the observatory and watched her choose the gears over the promise, and I believed her refusal, and then I came here anyway, which makes me either a fool or an accomplice, and I haven't decided which."

"Bram refuses things out loud and keeps promises quietly," Emil said. "It's the reverse order most people use."

"You defend her well."

"I defend her accurately."

Astrid looked at her brother—hollow-faced, fish-smelling, certain—and felt the resentment arrive fully formed, the way he'd predicted: not at him, not exactly, but at the shape of the thing itself. Faith, handed to her like a debt she hadn't signed for. Trust in a near-stranger, transferred to her account because she was the one standing in the room with bread. Somewhere across the fogged city a woman who bound her chest and spoke in a borrowed voice was lying awake refusing a promise, and Astrid was expected to simply *believe* that the refusal was procedural, temporary, a gear mid-turn.

She didn't believe it. That was the trouble. Belief required evidence, and evidence was her trade, and every scrap she'd gathered pointed one direction: the letters unanswered, the anger at the observatory, the way Montrose's hands had shaken—just slightly, just visibly, to a trained eye—when Emil's name entered the room.

People didn't shake over promises they intended to break.

"I'll come back tomorrow night," Astrid said, standing, gathering the empty jars, businesslike because feeling was leaking everywhere and procedure was the only cloth that held water. "Same hour. Eat what I bring. Don't move from the shed—if you relocate, I can't find you, and neither can anyone friendly. And Emil—"

He looked up.

"If she comes through, I'll help. God help me, I'll help. But if she leaves you on that transport, I want your word you'll walk onto it alive rather than do anything stupid at the dock. Promise me the stupid thing off the table."

He promised. He crossed his heart like a child, and she almost smiled, and then the smile died because through the roof gap she saw it: the observatory hill, black against the slightly-less-black sky, and at its crown the faint glow of a work lamp burning in the tower window at half past midnight.

Someone was working on the master clock. Tonight. Now. After certification announcements, after guild inspections were concluded, after any legitimate reason to touch it.

Bram Montrose was awake in the tower, alone, with the machine that now governed every patrol, every checkpoint, every transport departure in the city—and Astrid understood, with the cold click of a mechanism seating into place, that she had just watched the promise begin to move.

She picked up the lantern. "Stay here," she told Emil. "Don't open that door for anyone."

"Where are you going?"

"To catch a liar in the act," Astrid said, "and decide whether to thank her."
