## 9. The Clock That Keeps the Law

The proclamation went up on the customs house door on a Tuesday, pasted there by a city clerk with damp hands and a government paste that smelled of fish glue. Bram read it the way she read everything now—sideways, over her shoulder, with her cap pulled low—though there was nothing in it that required hiding. It was addressed to everyone.

*By order of the Senate of the Free City: as of the first of November, the master clock of the Royal Observatory shall constitute the official time of the city and its dependencies.*

Official time. She turned the phrase over like a coin she suspected was shaved.

*All curfews shall be reckoned thereby. All train departures, all patrol rotations, all telegraphic signals to the garrison at the river forts shall be synchronized to the observatory standard. Citizens are advised that discrepancies between private timepieces and the official standard are not grounds for appeal in matters of ordinance enforcement.*

Citizens are advised. As if it were advice. As if a clock were weather, something that happened to you rather than something a person built with her hands.

She stood in the gray drizzle with her collar up and read it twice more, and by the third reading she had stopped seeing words and started seeing gears—the great going-train of the observatory clock, each wheel polished until it blushed, each pivot running true to a tolerance she had set herself, alone, at three in the morning, with her sleeves rolled and her breath fogging the case glass. She knew that machine down to its escapement's heartbeat. She knew it would lose no more than two seconds in a week, because she had made it lose no more than two seconds in a week, because losing time was the one sin Hessler & Sons did not forgive and she had never once committed it.

And now every patrolman's whistle in the city would blow on her word. Every gate would close when she said close. Every barge would cast off—or fail to cast off—on a schedule drawn out of the machine she had rebuilt with such love that Master Hessler had wept, quietly, into his beard, and pretended he hadn't.

She walked back to the workshop through streets gone strange to her, as if the fog itself had been reorganized overnight into something administrative.

* * *

"Have you seen it?" Kessel asked, before the door had shut behind her. He was at his bench, but he wasn't working; he was watching the door, which he'd been doing for weeks now, in the way of a man who has decided patience is a tool and means to wear it smooth with use. "The proclamation."

"I saw it."

"They're calling it the most accurate public clock in the Baltic." He said *public* with a small curl of contempt, as if accuracy were common property now, devalued. "Hessler told the guild council it was your work. Yours." He picked up a graver and set it down again without using it. "Funny. A year ago nobody knew your name."

"A year ago I was finishing my apprenticeship."

"Were you." He smiled without warmth. "Well. Enjoy it while they're printing it in the papers. Accuracy doesn't stay private either." And he bent to his work as if a bell had rung, which, in this shop, it nearly always had.

Bram hung her coat, sat at her bench, took up the pallet stones she was lapping flat, and worked for four hours without looking up once. This was the discipline that had saved her life since she was seventeen: put your hands where your head cannot follow. The stones whispered against the iron plate. The shop ticked around her in forty small voices. Somewhere across town, under the observatory dome, the big clock ticked in a voice only she could hear, because she had tuned it, and it was keeping perfect time, and perfect time had just been conscripted.

* * *

Master Hessler came down from the front office at midday carrying the newspaper the way other men carried infants.

"Read this," he said, though he read it aloud himself, standing over her bench, his voice climbing with pride. "*The Observatory standard, restored to service after years of neglect by the firm of Hessler & Sons, is declared by the Naval Chronometric Board to be accurate within three seconds per week.* Three seconds, boy. In a week. Do you know what the Berlin observatories claim? Four. Four seconds, and they have electricity and a staff of six." He laid the paper down on her bench, flattening it with both palms, an old man smoothing a tablecloth for guests. "You understand what happens now."

"The patrols synchronize to it," Bram said. Her voice came out level. She had learned level the way other people learned prayers.

"Everything synchronizes to it. That is what *official* means. When I was young the city ran on eleven church bells and a harbor gun, and half the clocks disagreed with the other half, and somehow goods moved and men got paid." He shook his head, but fondly, the way you shake your head at a child who cannot remember a famine. "Now one clock. One truth. And yours." He tapped the paper twice, as if sealing it. "There will be a ceremony. First of November. The burgomaster, the guild council, the naval people. They'll want the maker present. You'll stand up straight and let them look at you."

"Yes, Master."

"And Montrose." He paused at the stair, and something in the pause made her look up. He was older than the shop; he had repaired instruments through two blockades and a cholera summer, and he had eyes that had watched many hands work and knew what hands could hide. "Whatever else you are carrying around in that head of yours—and you carry too much, all you young ones do—leave it outside the tower. From November on, that clock isn't ours anymore. It's the city's law. You don't get to be tired near it. You don't get to be anything near it."

"No, Master," Bram said, and meant it in a way he could not have guessed.

* * *

That evening she climbed to the observatory anyway. There were still adjustments to make—there were always adjustments, a hair of drop to correct in the pendulum's arc, a whisper of friction in the third wheel—but the truth was simpler: she wanted to sit inside the machine while she still could, before it stopped being hers.

The clock room smelled of oil and cold stone. Through the slit windows the harbor lay below in its usual smoke, masts leaning like tired men, cranes hooked against the sky. She opened the case, checked the beat with a watch against her ear, found it clean, and then did the thing she never did during working hours: she sat down on the floor inside the framework, among the weight lines and the great slow pendulum, with her back against the pillar, and listened.

People thought a big clock was loud. It wasn't. It was vast and quiet, a tide rather than a surf, and if you sat inside it long enough you felt yourself fall into its rhythm the way you fell into sleep. For five years this had been the only place her body fully relaxed—the workshop was vigilance, the lodgings were vigilance, the street was performance—but here, inside the works, she was just a pair of hands serving something indifferent. Gears didn't wonder about her. Steel didn't ask where she was born.

*Where were you born, Mr. Montrose?*

Astrid Lindqvist's voice, weeks old now, still arriving punctually, like everything in this city.

She closed her eyes. Below, the foghorn spoke, and the pendulum answered, and for a while the two sounds braided together and she let herself believe the clock was still just a clock—a thing of beauty, a solved problem, the finest work she would ever do.

Then she began, helplessly, to calculate.

This was the curse of her trade and the truth of her mind: she could not see a system without solving it. Curfew at ten. Patrols rotating on the quarter hour, four posts along the harbor wall, two at the rail yard, one at the customs basin. Synchronized to the observatory standard, their watches would agree with one another to the second—which meant no gaps, no arguments, no patrolman arriving late because his cheap watch ran slow. The very sloppiness that had let a man slip between posts on a foggy night, that had let a hundred smugglers and deserters and desperate brothers-in-law move through the seams of the city—all of it would be machined away. By her.

She thought about the coal barges that worked the northbound run. She had grown up around harbors; she knew how they kept their own time, loose as sailors' knots, casting off whenever the tide and the loading agreed. But the proclamation said *all departures.* Under a synchronized regime, a barge that lingered forty minutes past its filed departure time wasn't late—it was *anomalous.* Anomalous things got boarded.

She thought about a specific barge, though she had no reason yet to think about it, no plan, no promise newly invoked—only a name in the back of her skull that had lived there for five years like a splinter under a nail. Emil. If Emil ever needed a boat, the boat would leave on official time now. Official time would be hers.

Her masterpiece. Her flawless, celebrated, three-seconds-a-week masterpiece, converted into a net with a dial painted on it.

The pendulum swung. Tick. Tick. Each stroke falling on the world below like a small verdict.

"You're doing it wrong," she said aloud to the machine, and heard how young her voice sounded in the stone room. "You were supposed to tell the truth. Not testify."

But machines don't choose what they measure. That was the whole point of them. She had built a thing incapable of mercy and called it her best work, and the city had agreed, and now the city was going to use it.

* * *

She stayed until the lamplighter passed below the hill, then locked the case, signed the log, and walked home through streets that had begun, already, to obey her.

She noticed it at the corner of Hafenstrasse: a patrolman checking his watch against the church clock, frowning at the discrepancy, making a note. Two blocks on, a station clerk chalked a departure board with times rounded to the minute, neat as typeset. Even the whores along the quay had shifted their hours, someone joked in a doorway, since the paper came out. The whole port was quietly reorganizing itself around a pendulum swinging in a room on the hill, and the pendulum answered to her hands, and her hands, for the first time in five years, felt like weapons instead of tools.

At her lodgings she lit the lamp and sat among her own small clocks—eleven of them on the shelves, none agreeing exactly, each with its own character, its own honest drift—and hated them a little for being free.

She took out paper. Not to write to anyone; she had no one to write to. To think. This was another habit of the bench: lay the problem out in parts.

*Part one:* the clock goes certified on the first of November. Before that date, the city still runs on disagreement—church bells, harbor guns, private watches, slack. After it, unity. Whatever anyone ever needed to do unseen in this port, they had twenty days left in which the doing was possible, and after that the seams would be closed by her own hand.

*Part two:* she owed nothing yet. No one had asked her for anything. The promise had been made in peacetime by a girl who no longer existed, to a laughing boy on a dock, and promises like that were wine corks pressed back into bottles—they held nothing, they meant nothing, they were sentimental shapes. She wrote this sentence down almost verbatim and looked at it and knew it was the largest lie she had ever produced, larger than her name, larger than her bindings, because those lies protected her and this one endangered someone else.

*Part three:* if—*if*—a person needed to leave this city unnoticed, after the first of November, they would need the patrols confused. Not bribed, not evaded—confused. And confusion, in a city running on one clock, could only come from one source.

She stared at what her hand had written next, without quite deciding to write it: *A clock can be made inaccurate.*

It was the closest thing to blasphemy her creed allowed. Every lesson Hessler had ever taught her, every hour she had spent lapping stones and poising wheels at three in the morning, existed to make that sentence impossible. Accuracy wasn't a skill. It was a moral position. You did not lie with steel; steel held you honest when everything human in you wanted to bend.

She blew out the lamp and sat in the dark with the ticking, and understood that she had just heard, faint and far off, the first tick of a different clock entirely—a clock she might have to build inside her masterpiece, a lie inside the truth, running toward a day she couldn't yet name.

Twenty days until certification.

Somewhere across town, a census taker was filing forms in a bureau full of lists, and one of those lists had names on it, and Bram did not know yet whose name, or that the woman who copied lists was the sister of the boy on the dock. She knew only that the net had been finished, and blessed, and hung over the harbor—and that she had woven it herself, stitch by perfect stitch, and been thanked.

The smallest of her eleven clocks struck eleven, slightly late, honestly wrong.

She envied it all night.
