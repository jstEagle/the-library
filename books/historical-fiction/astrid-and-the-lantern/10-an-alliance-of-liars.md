## 10. An Alliance of Liars

The observatory smelled of cold stone and hot metal, and Astrid stood in the doorway of the clock room for a full minute before the apprentice on the ladder noticed her.

Bram Montrose was half inside the master clock, sleeves rolled to the elbow, a brass tool held between her teeth like a rose stem. She worked the way Emil used to work—absorbed, unbothered by the world, as if the mechanism and she had come to some private understanding that excluded everyone else. It was strange, watching her brother's friend from five years ago become this narrow-shouldered young man with soot in his hair, and stranger still to know what Astrid knew: that there was no young man at all.

She let the door click shut behind her. Bram's head emerged from the clockworks like an animal scenting weather.

"You're not due to verify me until Thursday," Bram said around the tool. She took it from her mouth. "And you're supposed to announce yourself."

"I'm a census taker. I announce myself to people who have something to hide."

"Everyone has something to hide. That's why the census exists." Bram climbed down, unhurried, wiping her hands on a rag with the deliberate calm of someone who had rehearsed being calm for years. "If you've come about my brother's file—"

"He's not my brother."

"No," Bram said. "He isn't. Forgive me. Old habit."

It was a lie, and they both knew it was a lie, and something in the way Bram said it—flat, careful, testing—told Astrid that everything said in this room tonight would be weighed twice before it was allowed to mean anything.

* * *

Astrid had spent three days deciding how to do this. She had drafted speeches on the tram, in the queue at the bakery, in the grey hour before sleep. In every version she began differently—in anger, in appeal, in cold bureaucratic fact—and in every version Bram Montrose did exactly what she was doing now: nothing. Waiting. Making Astrid spend her words first, like a merchant letting the customer name a price.

So Astrid abandoned the speeches.

"Emil is on the deportation list," she said. "Three weeks. The fourteenth."

Something moved behind Bram's eyes—not surprise. Confirmation. As if she'd been carrying the news already and had only been waiting for someone else to make it official.

"I know," Bram said.

"You know."

"The harbor dismissed him Tuesday. Word travels through the workshops faster than it travels through your bureau." Bram folded the rag into quarters, then eighths, then set it down and unfolded it again. "You came here to tell me something I already know. That's not why you came."

"No."

"Then say it."

Astrid made herself look at the clock instead of at Bram—the great dial face seen from behind, all its machinery exposed, weights and wheels and the long pendulum swinging with a patience that felt almost mocking. *I could stand here forever,* the pendulum seemed to say. *Can you?*

"I can delay his file," Astrid said.

The pendulum swung. Bram did not move.

"Clerical errors," Astrid went on, before her nerve could fail. "A transposed registry number. A misspelled surname. A file routed to the wrong desk and left there while the correction works its way back through the system. I've done it once already—it bought him nine days he doesn't know about. I can do it again. Twice more, maybe three times, if I'm careful. But every time I touch that file I commit a crime against my own office, and every time it buys him days, not weeks. Days are not escape, Montrose. Days are just a slower deportation."

"And you want me to turn those days into weeks."

"I want you to keep a promise you made five years ago to a man who is currently sleeping in a customs shed and eating food his sister steals from her own kitchen."

The words came out harder than she intended. Bram flinched—actually flinched, a small motion of the shoulders, quickly mastered—and Astrid understood that she had scored somewhere real. Good. Let it hurt. She had carried this alone for three days, the knowledge of Emil's faith, that idiotic unbroken faith, and she was tired of being the only one in the city who knew what it cost.

"You spoke to him," Bram said quietly.

"I found him under the pier sheds Wednesday night. He'd been there two days. He thought—" Astrid stopped. Started again. "He thought you were already arranging something. He said, *Bram will have it in hand, she always has it in hand.* Do you understand what that means? He's not hiding from the patrols because he believes you're coming. Every day he waits, he's waiting for *you*. And every day you don't come, he decides you're just being careful, because that's what he decided about you when you were both nineteen and drunk on someone else's wine, and he has never once in his life updated that decision."

* * *

Bram turned away from her. Went to the workbench, where a disassembled escapement lay in rows on green baize like organs awaiting transplant, and stood over it with her back straight and her hands flat on the wood.

"You should understand what you're asking," she said to the bench.

"Then explain it to me. I'm told clockmakers are good at explaining mechanisms."

"This one is simple." Bram's voice had gone level, instructional, and Astrid recognized the tone—Emil used it too, when he was buying time against feelings. "In three weeks, maybe less, the guild certifies this clock as the city's official timekeeper. Curfews, train departures, patrol rotations—all of it keyed to this machine. When that happens, the man who keeps this clock becomes, whether he likes it or not, part of the apparatus that catches people like Emil. If I am caught helping him—if anyone even suspects—I lose everything. Not my freedom. My *name*. The name I built gear by gear for six years. There are men in this city who would pay well to learn what I am, and men who would pay more to never speak of it, and neither kind of payment leaves me whole. You're asking me to wager the only self I have left on a promise made by a girl who no longer exists."

"Birgitta," Astrid said.

Bram went very still.

"Yes," Astrid said. "I know the name. I've known it since the census. I told you that, and you looked at me like a man looks at a loaded gun on the table between us, and we agreed—without agreeing—to pretend the gun wasn't there. Well. It's there. And here is what you need to understand about me, Montrose: I have held that name in my hands for weeks now, and I have written nothing down, told no one, filed nothing. Your secret is sitting in my head next to my brother's death warrant, and I have protected them both, and I will go on protecting them both, and I am *tired*, and I am out of patience for being the only person in this arrangement who keeps her word."

The pendulum swung. Somewhere below, the fog horn on the breakwater moaned its long note and gave up.

"What do you want?" Bram said at last. Not hostile. Worse—genuinely asking, like a craftsman presented with a broken movement and no manual.

"A bargain," Astrid said. "You find a way to move Emil out of this city before the list reaches the fourteenth. Whatever way—papers, a boat, a freight car, I don't care, I don't want details, details are evidence. In exchange, I keep his file moving slow enough that the patrols are looking at a corrected date while he's already gone. And when it's done, when he's north or gone or whatever he becomes, we never speak of any of this again. Not the promise. Not the name. Nothing."

"And if it fails?"

"Then it fails separately. You were never involved. I made clerical errors; I'll take my reprimand like a clerk. You keep your hands clean and your name intact and your clock perfect, and you watch them put him on the boat, and you live with it." Astrid heard her own voice go cold and hated it and didn't stop it. "That's the other half of the contract, Montrose. The failure clause. I've read enough files to know every contract needs one."

* * *

Bram picked up a wheel from the baize—a small thing, no bigger than a coin, toothed like a sunflower—and turned it over and over without looking at it.

"Why?" she said finally. "Not for Emil. You argued with him under the pier; he told me you argued. Why are *you* doing this? You barely knew me. You owe me nothing. Your office, your parents' name, your whole careful life—you're burning it for a promise you weren't even present for."

Astrid considered lying. She was, she realized, quite good at it now; three days of falsifying registry entries had taught her that much about herself, and she disliked the lesson.

"Because I watched our father sign the loyalty declaration in '09," she said, "and I watched my mother burn his socialist pamphlets in the kitchen stove, and I watched them both survive, comfortably, respectably, by deciding every single morning that the safe thing and the right thing were the same thing. And they died respectable. And at the funeral, half the city came, and I stood there thinking: not one of these people would have hidden a single thing for either of them. Not one." She made herself meet Bram's eyes. "I don't want to be survived. I want to be somebody someone would hide a thing for. And apparently the fastest route to finding out if I'm that person is to become her. So. There's my price. Not noble. Don't put it in your ledger as noble."

For the first time since Astrid had entered the room, Bram smiled. It was small and crooked and gone almost immediately, but it was real, and it changed her whole face—made her briefly, startlingly, into whoever she had been before the binding and the boy's name and the six years of rehearsal.

"You argue like Emil," Bram said.

"We're Scandinavian. We argue like weather systems."

* * *

They did not shake on it. Astrid noticed that neither of them offered a hand, and understood why: a handshake was a record. These two women, each of whom lived by documents—one filing them, one forging them—were constructing the only agreement of their lives that must exist nowhere but in memory.

Instead, Bram walked to the great case and opened the small door beneath the dial, where the winding crank sat in its bracket. She took the crank off, weighed it in her palm, and set it on the workbench between them.

"When this clock is certified," she said, "it will be wound on a schedule, by guild men, under supervision. Until then, I wind it. Which means that for the next few weeks, I am the only person in this city who can make official time run fast or slow, and no instrument can prove otherwise." She tapped the crank once with one finger. "Consider that my signature. I can't promise you Emil yet. I can't promise you a method, or a date, or that I won't lose my nerve at the last minute and hate myself forever after. What I can promise is that the answer to your question is no longer *no*. Ask me again in three days."

"That's not a contract."

"It's the only kind I can sign. A contract implies I control the outcome. I control the clock. The rest—" Bram spread her hands, and Astrid saw them properly for the first time: fine-boned, scarred at the knuckles, a burn mark across the back of the right hand old enough to have gone silver. Hands that had learned a trade under a false name and were very, very good at it. "The rest is weather."

"Then I'll bring the umbrella," Astrid said. "Three days. And Montrose—" she paused at the door, one hand on the iron latch, and made herself say the last of it, the part she'd carried up the hill like a stone in her coat. "He kept the watch. The broken one. From the regatta. He sleeps holding it. Whatever you decide you owe him, understand that he decided what you owed him years ago, and he's never once doubted the arithmetic."

She was down two steps of the spiral stair when Bram's voice followed her, quiet enough that the pendulum nearly swallowed it:

"Tell him the watch is fixable. Tell him I've already ordered the parts."

Astrid stood in the dark of the stairwell, heart hammering, and did not let herself smile until she was outside, where the fog could take credit for the wetness in her eyes.

* * *

She walked home the long way, along the harbor road, past the customs sheds where a watchman's lantern swung its dull arc. Three days, Bram had said. Astrid turned it over as she walked, testing it the way she tested any document: for forgery, for weakness, for the tell-tale tremor of a hand under pressure.

It would hold or it wouldn't. But tomorrow morning she would open Emil's file, change a *4* to a *9* in the registry number, initial the correction in her supervisor's style, and buy him another week—and for the first time since she'd copied that list, the treachery would have a partner. That was the terrifying part, she admitted, climbing the hill toward her lodgings with the fog thickening around the lamps. Not the risk. The relief. She had been alone with the crime for three days, and now there were two sets of hands on it, and somehow that made it heavier and lighter at once, the way a shared coffin is both.

Behind her, high on the hill, the observatory clock struck ten—ten clear bells rolling down through the fog over the sleeping port, marking the hour for no one, governing nothing yet.

Give it three weeks, Astrid thought. Give it three weeks, and every bell in this city would be answering to the woman she'd just shaken hands with—without shaking hands.

She pulled her collar up and walked faster, and behind her the bells finished counting, and the fog closed over the sound like water.
