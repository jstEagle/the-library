## 11. Kessel's Curiosity

The trouble with secrets, Bram had learned, was that they weighed nothing until you tried to carry them past someone else. Then they had mass. They had drag. They pulled at every step like a pocket full of lead shot.

She felt the weight on Thursday morning, when she left the observatory with Astrid's note folded into her glove and Kessel's eyes on her back.

*Meet me Thursday, four o'clock, the archway by the fish market. Come alone. — A.*

Alone was a word that had stopped meaning anything in this city. The fog came off the harbor thick as wool, and through it the streets moved with men who counted things now—heads, crates, names. The census had made everyone a number, and numbers were easy to check.

Bram took the long way. Down the Mühlenstraße, across the canal bridge, doubling back through the tannery lane where the smell would cover any scent of nervousness she might be trailing. She checked reflections in shop glass the way other men checked for rain. At the corner of the herring stalls she allowed herself one glance back down the arcade—

Kessel stood by the pickle barrel pretending to read a broadsheet upside down.

Her heart did something ugly and mechanical, a gear tooth skipping. She turned away before he could catch her looking and walked on, unhurried, a boy with nowhere particular to be. Four o'clock. She had twenty minutes to lose him or decide she couldn't.

* * *

The archway by the fish market was a relic of the old wall, a vaulted throat of brick where the fishwives had sold their catch for two hundred years and where, after dark, the city's honest business gave way to its dishonest kind. At four in the afternoon it held only gulls and the reek of low tide.

Astrid was already there, standing with her census satchel squared on her hip like a soldier's kit, looking exactly like what she was: an official woman waiting on official business. That was the genius of it, Bram thought. Nobody questioned a census taker in an archway. She was probably counting the gulls.

"You were followed," Astrid said, without greeting.

"I know."

"Then why—"

"Because if I run, he follows harder." Bram leaned against the cold brick, arms crossed, watching the mouth of the archway. "Kessel doesn't chase things. He waits for them to come to him. It's how he got the foreman's job over older boys. He's patient the way rust is patient."

Astrid studied her. In the gray light her face looked carved, all planes and decision. "You told me he suspected."

"He noticed I don't bathe with the others. He noticed my hands." Bram flexed her fingers. Calloused, ink-stained, precise—the hands of a craftsman, which is what they were; that had never been the lie. "He noticed I flinched when the guild clerk said *boy* too loudly. Small things. He collects small things. He's a horologist of grievances."

"And today?"

"Today he saw me meet the census taker who falsified his rival's file." Bram let out a breath. "Or he saw an apprentice meet a girl behind the fish market, which is its own scandal but a survivable one."

"It isn't survivable," Astrid said flatly. "If he reports you, they'll examine everything. Your papers. Your lodgings. You. And then Emil's file comes under scrutiny, and mine does, and the whole structure—" She stopped herself. Bram watched her do it, watch the sentence get locked up like contraband.

"The whole structure falls," Bram finished. "Yes. I've been doing the arithmetic since the bridge."

For a moment neither spoke. Somewhere beyond the arch a bell rang the half hour—a church clock, sloppy, thirty seconds fast, and Bram caught herself wincing the way she always did at bad timekeeping, even now, even with everything.

"There's another way," Astrid said quietly.

"No."

"You haven't heard it."

"I don't need to. It ends with one of us in front of a magistrate and the other in front of worse." Bram pushed off the wall. "I'll handle Kessel."

"With what? You have no money."

"I have something better than money." And when Astrid frowned: "I have a thing he wants."

* * *

Kessel found her that evening at the workshop, because of course he did; he had a gift for being where the answer was going to be. The others had gone home. Master Hessler was at the guild hall arguing about brass tariffs. The lamps were lit and the benches were clean and the whole room ticked in forty overlapping voices, and into that room Kessel walked like a man entering a chapel he intended to rob.

"Bram." He said the name carefully, tasting it. He was a year older than her, broad through the shoulders, with the soft hands of a boy whose father owned three warehouses and a seat on the grain exchange. His talent was real—that was the insult of it. He could have been a fine clockmaker if ambition hadn't gotten to the movement first. "Working late."

"Somebody has to finish the strike train."

"Mm. The master clock." He drifted along the bench, fingers hovering—not touching, Hessler would flay a man for fingerprints on polished steel, but hovering, proprietary. "They say it'll keep time better than the observatory's own astronomers. They say whoever certifies it will have a career written in gold."

"They say a lot of things."

"They do." He picked up a loupe, set it down. "Funny thing, though. Today I'm buying herring for my mother—she likes them from the fish market, the ones under the arch—and who should come walking through the fog like a man with a guilty conscience but our Bram Montrose. Meeting someone." He smiled. It was a good smile, well-machined. "Do you know who it was?"

"A fishwife."

"A census taker." He let the words sit. "The Lindqvist woman. Sister of the Lindqvist fellow the registry's so interested in. Interesting company for an apprentice whose entire future depends on staying beyond reproach."

Bram kept her hands still on the bench. Stillness was the first discipline of the trade; let your hands tremble and every gear you cut would tremble too. "I was asking her about the census forms. Master Hessler wanted the household registered properly before the certification."

"At the fish market. At four o'clock. Alone."

"Discreet questions need discreet places."

Kessel laughed softly. He came closer, close enough that she could smell the pomade in his hair, and his voice dropped to the register men used when they thought they were being generous. "Here's what I think, Bram. I think you're a careful person with a great many things to be careful about. I think the Lindqvist woman is careless on your behalf. And I think—" his eyes went, briefly, deliberately, to her chest, to the flatness there, and back up, "—that some people work very hard at being someone they're not."

The ticking of the workshop seemed to swell. Forty clocks, forty hearts, all of them suddenly loud.

"If you're going to accuse me of something," Bram said, "accuse me."

"I don't accuse. Accusing is for people who want something *now*." He straightened his cuffs. "I want something later. I want to know that when I need a friend in this workshop, I have one. That's all. Friends look out for each other."

"What do you want?"

"Nothing today." He was almost at the door when he paused, and Bram knew—knew with the certainty of a man watching a fuse burn toward powder—that the next part had been rehearsed. "Although. Old Wenzel died last month, didn't he. The journeyman. His effects went to auction."

Bram said nothing.

"There was a lever escapement in his lot," Kessel said. "An early one. Robin pattern, pre-reform, the kind they stopped making before either of us was born. Some collector from Lübeck bought the whole case lot, but I hear the escapement itself never surfaced. I hear it's still in this building somewhere. Master Hessler bought the effects unseen—he does that, sentimental old man—and it's sitting in the attic inventory, unlisted." He turned. "I collect small things, Bram. Rare things. Things that prove a man can be trusted to keep his hands off what isn't his—or to know when to hand it over."

The attic. Of course. Bram had catalogued that inventory herself, twice, and yes—lot forty-one, miscellaneous movements, one unmarked mechanism wrapped in oilcloth that she had examined at midnight weeks ago with her heart hammering, recognizing it the way you recognize a face from a portrait: a Robin lever, pre-reform, the pivot jewels intact, the kind of thing that appeared at auction once a decade and made grown horologists behave like children.

It was worth more than her year's wages. It was worth more than Kessel's discretion, which was the problem, because Kessel's discretion had no fixed price. It was a subscription, not a sale. Everyone in the trade knew it. Debts to Kessel were never paid once; they compounded, quarterly, forever, like interest owed to a very polite loan shark.

But the alternative was the magistrate, the examination, the unwinding of everything—her name, her papers, Astrid's career, Emil's one chance at a northbound tide.

"Lot forty-one," Bram said.

Kessel's smile widened by a single tooth. "Is that what's in lot forty-one?"

"You'll have it by Sunday."

"Sunday." He nodded slowly, savoring it. "You see? Friends." At the door he stopped again, and this time the pause was genuine, unplanned, and therefore more frightening than everything before it. "One more thing. Purely professional curiosity. The master clock—when it's certified, the patrols synchronize to it. Curfew enforcement, train times, all of it. A machine like that, running perfectly..." He glanced back, and his eyes were bright and empty as jeweled bearings. "It would take a very skilled hand to make it drift. Even slightly. Even for a night."

The cold went through Bram like water through a cracked case.

"I couldn't imagine why anyone would want that," she said.

"No," Kessel agreed pleasantly. "Neither could I. But if one ever did—one would want to know that the friend watching had been paid in advance. Wouldn't one."

He left. The door clicked shut with the sound of a detent engaging.

* * *

Bram sat alone among the clocks for a long time. The lamps burned down. Outside, the fog pressed at the windows like a listener.

She took out Astrid's note and read it again, though she knew it by heart, and thought about the shape of the trap she'd just stepped into. Kessel hadn't asked for the escapement because he loved mechanisms. He'd asked because now he owned the proof of her willingness to steal for silence—and a man who steals for silence will steal again, and each theft is a link, and links make chains, and chains can be pulled from either end.

And that last remark. *Make it drift.* Did he suspect the plan? No—there was no plan yet, not fully, not spoken aloud anywhere but an archway smelling of fish. But Kessel didn't need facts. He collected small things and assembled them later, like a man building a movement from loose parts, and he had just shown her the drawing he was working from: apprentice, secret, census taker, enemy's sister, master clock. He might not know what picture it made. He only needed to know it made one worth holding over her.

She climbed to the attic by lamplight and found lot forty-one where she'd left it, under a broken barometer and a crate of doll parts. The oilcloth came away soft with age. In the lamp's glow the escapement lay in its wrappings like a sleeping animal—steel gone brown at the edges, jewels still burning red and hard, the pallet fork shaped by some dead craftsman's hand fifty years before either Bram or Kessel drew breath.

Beautiful. That was the worst of it. She was giving away something beautiful to buy something worthless.

*You'll pay him this,* she thought, wrapping it again, *and in spring he'll want the next thing, and in summer the next, and one day the price will be something you can't lift from an attic.*

Unless. Unless the debt was called due before spring. Unless the certification came first, and the barge, and the tide, and whatever happened inside the tower on escape night happened while Kessel's silence was still fresh-purchased and new.

One night. She needed his silence for one night. After that, let him spend his prize, let him tell the whole guild, let Birgitta Montrose stand in the light if she must—but Emil would be in neutral waters, and Astrid would be clear, and the cost would land on Bram alone, which was the only place she'd ever let costs land.

It was a thin plan. It was the thinnest plan she'd ever built, and she had built it out of a stolen mechanism and a borrowed month and the hope that a blackmailer's greed would outrun his caution. Every clock in the shop below her seemed to tick faster, as if they'd heard, as if they were counting down something she hadn't agreed to.

She put the escapement inside her shirt, against the bindings, where it lay cold and heavy as a second secret, and began the long walk home through the fog.

At the corner of her street, a figure detached itself from a doorway. For one lurching instant she thought—Kessel, already, too soon—

It was Astrid, hood up, breath steaming.

"You shouldn't be here," Bram whispered.

"Neither should you." Astrid's eyes went to the lump under Bram's coat, sharp as a customs officer's. "What did it cost?"

"More than it looks." Bram glanced up the empty street, then back. "And less than it will. Astrid—we have to move Emil sooner. Whatever we're doing, we do it before the certification. Before Kessel's price goes up. Before the patrols lock to the clock." She heard her own voice drop, urgent, stripped of the workshop's careful calm. "How soon can the barge sail?"

Astrid stared at her. "You don't know?"

"Know what?"

"The tide tables changed yesterday. New military schedule." Her face, in the fog-lamp's weak glow, had gone the color of the mist. "The next northbound coal barge leaves Saturday night. Three days from now. And Bram—the departure signal is the observatory clock striking midnight. If the clock strikes true, the patrol boats are in the channel before Emil clears the breakwater."

Three days. Bram's hand rose without her willing it and pressed flat against her coat, against the stolen escapement, against the place where all her secrets now lived together, ticking.

Saturday. The sabotage she'd sworn she'd never begin would have to begin tomorrow.

And somewhere behind her, in the dark, she could have sworn she heard a clock she knew start to run fast.
