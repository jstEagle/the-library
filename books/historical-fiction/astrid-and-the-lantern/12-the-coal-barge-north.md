## 12. The Coal Barge North

Bram found the foreman on a Tuesday, in the smoke room of the Anchor and Chain, where the harbor men drank with their boots up and nobody asked a boy clockmaker why he'd come.

Vaske. That was the name Emil had said once, years ago, laughing into a beer at the regatta—*old Vaske, he'd smuggle his own mother out of the customs house if she paid in herring.* Bram had filed it away the way she filed everything: small, oiled, ready to be useful someday. She hadn't known then that someday would smell like coal dust and taste like fear.

She wore her work coat and carried a broken deck chronometer under her arm, because a boy with a broken instrument could go anywhere in this city without explanation. She sat down two stools from the foreman and set the chronometer on the bar.

"Yours?" Vaske asked. He was sixty if he was a day, hands like knotted rope, a face that had been weathered down to something patient.

"Mine to fix," Bram said. "But the man I'm fixing it for says you're the only one who knows what time the night tide really leaves."

Vaske turned his glass a quarter turn. "The tide doesn't keep appointments."

"The *Nordlys* does." She'd gotten the name from a stevedore for the price of a repaired watch. "Coal barge. North run."

Something moved behind the old man's eyes—not surprise exactly. Recalculation. "Who's asking?"

"Emil Lindqvist's asking."

The room went on around them—the clink of glasses, a argument about herring prices—but between the two stools there opened up a pocket of stillness. Vaske set his drink down carefully, the way men do when their hands want to shake and they won't let them.

"He's registered now," Vaske said quietly. "They took him off the crane crew Thursday."

"I know."

"You know what happens to registered men after three weeks."

"I know what happens to anyone who helps them, too."

Vaske looked at her then, fully, and Bram understood that he was seeing more than a boy. He was seeing the promise itself, standing there in a work coat, and deciding whether it weighed enough.

"His father pulled me out of the ice when I was younger than you," the foreman said finally. "I've owed that family thirty years." He drained his glass. "*Nordlys* sails Saturday night. Captain Rask. He owes me, not you—that matters. You don't speak to Rask. You don't write anything down. The man goes aboard between the coal sheds, third arch from the water gate, after the patrol passes at twelve-thirty. Not before. If they find him early, Rask can't see him and can't save him."

"And the tide?"

"Twelve-forty, by the old almanac." Vaske's mouth twisted. "Though I suppose it doesn't matter anymore, does it. Word is the observatory clock gets certified next week. After that, every whistle in this city answers to your master." He nodded at the chronometer. "Including the patrols."

Bram's blood went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the fog coming off the river.

"Yes," she said. "After certification, they synchronize."

"So whatever you're planning, boy—" Vaske stood, dropped coins on the bar, and looked down at her with thirty years of harbor patience in his ruined face, "—you'd better plan around the most accurate clock in the Baltic."

* * *

Astrid walked the route herself before she told them about it, because she did not trust routes she hadn't walked.

From the census bureau on Kanzlei Square, west along the Mühlenstraße, past the shuttered fish market, down to the water gate: eleven minutes at a brisk walk. She knew, because she counted. She counted everything now. It was an occupational disease of her profession—she had spent four years reducing human lives to columns and durations, and now the habit had turned on her, and she reduced her own treachery to figures as well.

Eleven minutes to reach the gate. Four minutes through the coal sheds, if one didn't dawdle, and Emil would have to be calm enough not to dawdle. Twelve minutes for the patrol circuit along the quay—she had copied the rotation schedule six weeks ago for verification purposes, legitimate then, damning now—and the gap between the twelve-thirty patrol and its return pass was never more than nine minutes.

Nine minutes to move a frightened man forty meters in the dark.

She stood at the third arch and looked out at the black water and did the arithmetic again, though she already knew how it came out. It came out fine. It came out fine only if everything else held: if the fog held, if Emil held, if Bram held, if Astrid's forged delay on the file held until Saturday and no clerk got curious before then. A chain of ifs, each link thinner than the last, and somewhere at the end of it a coal barge pulling north on a tide that kept no appointments.

And hanging over all of it, the thing she couldn't stop thinking about, the thing she'd come here actually to look at: the observatory.

She turned and looked up at it from the quay—the dome black against the low clouds, the tower below it where the master clock lived, the face lit faintly so the whole city could read it. In eight days the guild would certify that clock. After that, its time would be law. The patrol whistles would blow when it said. The train departures, the curfew sirens, the harbor signals—all of it slaved to a single escapement built and tuned by Bram Montrose's hands.

Which meant, Astrid thought, staring up at the glowing face, that the escape window was measured by the very machine they were trying to beat. If the master clock ran true, the twelve-thirty patrol would arrive at 12:30 exactly, and leave at 12:39 exactly, and the *Nordlys* would cast off at 12:40 exactly, and every second would be accounted for except the ones Emil needed.

If the master clock ran *slow*, though—

She stopped herself. She made herself stop, right there on the public quay, because the thought that followed was the thought of a criminal, and she was still, technically, on the right side of the line. She had delayed a file with clerical errors. That was forgivable, explainable, survivable. What came after that word—*slow*—was not clerical. It was sabotage of municipal property in wartime preparations, and it would not be survivable for either of them.

But the arithmetic didn't care about her respectability. The arithmetic just sat there in her head, patient as a ledger:

*Twelve-forty by a clock that everyone trusts.*

*Or twelve-twenty-eight, if the clock lies.*

She hugged her coat tighter and walked home the long way, past the bureau, past her own dark windows, counting steps the whole way and hating herself for counting.

* * *

They met at midnight in the observatory, because it was the one room in the city where a meeting between a census taker and an apprentice clockmaker could hide inside an honest errand. Astrid had brought a folio of verification forms. Bram had brought the excuse of routine maintenance. Neither had brought much hope.

"It's too tight," Bram said. She had chalked the numbers on the slate wall beside the clock—she worked things out in chalk, always had, and tonight the chalk felt like writing her own confession. "Nine-minute gap. Eleven minutes for Emil to walk it. Even if he leaves early, even if we hold the patrol somehow—"

"We don't hold the patrol." Astrid stood with her arms crossed beneath the great clock's face, its pendulum swinging behind her like a slow silver heartbeat. "We can't touch the patrol. But we can touch the clock."

Bram went very still. "No."

"Bram—"

"No." The word came out harder than she meant it to. She looked at the machine above them—the movement she had rebuilt tooth by tooth, the escapement she'd adjusted to within a half-second a week, the strike train whose voice she knew better than her own. "You know what that clock is? It's the first thing I've ever made that will outlive me. It'll be running when my hands are gone. When everyone who ever called me 'boy' is gone. And you're asking me to put a lie inside it."

"I'm asking you to put Emil inside the *Nordlys*," Astrid said. "The clock is how we buy him the minutes. There is no other currency in this city. They've made sure of that—they've made *time itself* the law. So the only way to break their law is to break their time." Her voice stayed level, but Bram heard the effort in it. "You think I sleep well proposing this? I'm the one who taught this city to count itself, Bram. Every column I've filled in has been a brick in the wall we're trying to dig under. Don't talk to me about complicity."

The pendulum swung. Sixty-one beats, Bram counted automatically, before she trusted her voice again.

"If the drift is caught," she said, "it's caught in the clock, and it points to the clockmaker. One person. One crime. But if we tamper with patrols, or papers, or people—it spreads. It touches you. It touches Vaske, Rask, half the harbor." She wiped her palm on her apron. "Slow the clock by twelve minutes over five days and no inspector alive catches it. They'll check it against the telegraph time signal once, at certification, and after certification no one checks a certified clock—they *are* the check. I can make the drift curve gentle. Natural-looking. Temperature compensation slightly miscalculated, a hair of wear in the pallet stones. It'll look like age."

"And after Emil's clear?"

"After Emil's clear, I fix it." She said it simply, because it was simple. Whatever else happened, whatever the guild did or discovered, the clock would be made true again. That part she could promise without flinching. "Saturday night it runs slow. Sunday morning I begin the correction. By Wednesday it's honest again and nobody's the wiser, and Emil is in neutral waters eating bad bread and being alive."

Astrid studied her for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, she laughed—a short, exhausted sound with no humor in it.

"Do you hear yourself? You've planned the lie down to its repair. You're going to commit a crime and then *maintain* it." She shook her head. "Five days of drift. Starting when?"

"Tomorrow night," Bram said. "Small. Two seconds, maybe three. Then a little more each day. Gentle. Like the machine getting tired." She looked up at the clock face, at the hands that were, for a few more days, the most honest thing in her life. "It'll hurt worse than anything I've ever done to a mechanism. I need you to know that. I need someone to know it isn't easy for me."

"I know," Astrid said quietly. And then, because she was who she was, because duty was a bone in her body that would never be removed, she added: "And I need *you* to know that if this fails—if Kessel talks, or a clerk finds my errors, or the patrol finds Emil in those sheds—I will testify to every choice I made. I won't hide behind you. Whatever happens Saturday, we walk into it as what we are."

"What are we?" Bram asked. It came out before she could stop it, and it wasn't about the crime, and they both knew it wasn't about the crime.

Astrid picked up her folio. At the door of the clock room she paused, her back to Bram, silhouetted against the corridor lamp.

"Accomplices," she said. "God help us both."

* * *

Saturday came gray and windless, the kind of day when the fog doesn't lift so much as settle in for the duration.

By noon the master clock had lost four minutes and eleven seconds against Bram's own pocket watch—the good lie, the gentle curve, invisible to anyone who hadn't been watching since Tuesday. By six o'clock, five minutes flat. The city below went about its evening reading the wrong hour off the observatory face and believing it with all its heart, and Bram stood at her bench in the workshop pretending to regulate a carriage clock while her whole soul leaned against the drifting minute hand like a woman holding shut a door.

At eleven she met Astrid at the corner of the Mühlenstraße, as arranged, and they walked the last stretch together through fog so thick the gas lamps were only smears of yellow. Astrid carried the folio. Bram carried a coil of fine wire and a small oilcan, her tools of both trades now.

At the mouth of the coal sheds they stopped. Somewhere out in the murk, a patrol whistle sounded—12:29 by the lying clock, which meant 12:24 by the truth, which meant everything was exactly as wrong as they'd planned.

"Third arch," Bram whispered. "After this pass. Nine minutes."

"Eight and a half," Astrid whispered back. "I counted."

They pressed into the shadow of the first arch and waited, shoulder to shoulder, breath smoking, listening to the patrol's boots come on through the fog—unhurried, synchronized, trusting completely in the great accurate clock above them that was lying through its beautiful brass teeth.

The boots passed. Faded. Died away toward the customs house.

"Now," Astrid breathed.

And from the darkness deeper in the sheds, where he'd been waiting alone among the coal sacks for two hours with nothing but his fear for company, Emil Lindqvist stepped out into the fog—and froze.

Because standing at the far end of the archway, lantern raised, was Kessel.
