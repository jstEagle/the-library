## 13. Certification Day

Bram woke before the alarm, which was itself a kind of failure. A clockmaker who needed an alarm was a clockmaker who hadn't done her work properly, and she had done her work properly for eleven months. She lay in the dark listening to the six clocks in her rented room, each one a small honest heartbeat, and thought: *today the city will hear the big one.*

She dressed the way she always dressed for guild occasions—collar high, waistcoat buttoned to the last eyelet, the binding underneath pulled tight enough to make her ribs ache by noon. She'd learned years ago that pain was just another kind of discipline. You could carry it the way you carried a toolbox. You simply didn't set it down where anyone could see.

In the workshop, Master Hessler was already there, which meant he had not slept. He stood before the traveling case that held the regulator pendulum like a man standing at a graveside, and when he heard Bram's boots on the stair he turned with an expression she had never seen on him before. It took her a moment to name it. It was pride, and it frightened her more than suspicion ever had.

"Eleven months," he said. "Eleven months, and not one day of excuses. Do you know what Kessel gave me in his first year? Excuses. Do you know what the others gave me? Excuses with better handwriting." He lifted the case. "Today you give them a clock."

"Today the clock gives itself," Bram said. "I only stopped interfering with it."

Hessler grunted—the closest he came to laughter—and carried the case down the stairs ahead of her, and Bram followed her master through the grey morning with her heart going like a train.

* * *

The guild hall had been built by men who wanted it to outlast the city, and it was well on its way. Granite columns, a vaulted ceiling painted with the signs of the zodiac, and at the far end, on a dais of black oak, the demonstration bench where for two hundred years journeymen had set down their masterworks and let the council decide whether the work was worthy of the man.

The council sat in a crescent of high-backed chairs: seven masters, seven faces like weather. At the center sat old Master Vogel, whose hands shook so badly he hadn't held a tool in a decade but whose eye could find a fault in an escapement from across a room. To his right, Master Brandt, who had voted against Bram's apprenticeship five years ago on the grounds that the boy's references were thin. He had never warmed. He was not going to warm today.

And behind the council, in the public gallery, sat the city: the harbor master, two aldermen, a colonel of the garrison whose uniform was new enough to creak, and a scattering of guild wives with programs folded in their gloves. And at the end of the second row, in a plain grey coat that did not belong among the silk, Astrid Lindqvist.

Bram had not expected her. Or rather, she had expected her and had told herself she hadn't. Astrid's face did nothing when their eyes met. That was the agreement, and Astrid kept agreements with the precision of a well-cut gear. She was here in her official capacity, Bram reminded herself. The census bureau would want a record. The bureau wanted a record of everything now. That was the whole trouble with the world in 1911: it had learned to count.

The master clock could not be moved, so the council would be moved to it. They processed out of the guild hall in a slow black river, down the cobbled hill, through streets still smelling of coal smoke and last night's fog, up the winding stair of the observatory tower. Bram walked near the middle of the procession, close enough to hear the colonel say to the harbor master, "—and it will govern the patrol rotations as of Monday," and close enough to feel the words go through her like a needle finding a nerve.

*Monday.* Three days. Three days, and then every whistle, every patrol, every departure of every train and every boat in the harbor would answer to the machine she had rebuilt with her own hands. She had known this since the proclamation in September. She had known it the way you know a debt is coming due. But knowing it in the abstract and hearing it said in a colonel's easy voice, on the stair, in her own tower—those were different weights entirely.

At the top, the clock room was warm from the works and smelled of oil and old brass, the smell Bram loved better than any smell in the world, and she hated it a little this morning, because she had come to understand that even smells could be conscripted.

The clock stood in its oak case against the east wall, nine feet of it, the dial white as a held breath. Behind the case, through the tall window, the harbor lay grey and glittering, and somewhere out on that water, on a coal barge that came and went with the tide, a captain owed a favor to a foreman who owed a favor to Emil Lindqvist. Bram did not look at the harbor. She looked at her clock.

"Master Montrose." Vogel's voice was dry as a dead leaf. "The council has read your reports. Reports are paper. Show us the work."

* * *

She had rehearsed this demonstration for weeks, and then, in the last days, she had deliberately un-rehearsed it, because a demonstration that runs too smooth smells of theater. She began with the case open, the works exposed, and let the council come to her.

"The going train," she said, and walked them through it: the wheels she had re-cut, the arbor she had turned down by two-tenths of a millimeter because the original had worn an egg-shaped hole in its pivot hole and no amount of oil would forgive that. "You can't fix wear with care. You fix it with metal."

Brandt sniffed. "Any journeyman can replace a wheel."

"Any journeyman can replace a wheel," Bram agreed, "and the clock will run, and in four years it will run badly, because the new wheel was cut to the old train's errors. I re-cut the train to the center wheel, and the center wheel to the escapement, because the escapement is the only part of a clock that tells the truth." She let that sit. She had learned from Hessler that the best way to handle Brandt was to agree with him and then walk three steps past him.

She showed them the escapement, the deadbeat pallets she had re-faced and polished to a mirror finish, and she watched Vogel lean in despite himself, watched his shaking hand hover near the anchor without touching it, the way a man hovers near a face he once loved.

"Where did you learn to stone a pallet like this?" Vogel asked.

"From failure, Master. The first three attempts were too aggressive. The fourth was timid. The fifth—" She shrugged. "The fifth I threw away and started thinking instead of grinding."

A sound from the gallery that might have been a laugh, quickly suppressed. The colonel checked his pocket watch. Bram noted the gesture and put it away with all the other things she was carrying.

Then the demonstration proper. The heart of it was simple, as the best demonstrations are: the observatory's transit instrument had taken its meridian observations at noon for the past nine days, and the master clock's rate had been logged against the stars. Nine days of data. Bram laid the logbook on the bench and let the council pass it hand to hand.

"Total divergence over nine days," she said, "is four-tenths of a second. Rate stability is within a tenth of a second per day, and the residual is temperature-driven, not mechanical. I've fitted the pendulum with a compensated gridiron assembly—"

"We read the report," Brandt said, but his voice had lost its edge, and Bram knew why. The numbers were the kind of numbers a man gets maybe once in his working life, and every man in the crescent had spent his life chasing them.

"Then you know the clock is ready," Bram said. "But a clock isn't ready because it's accurate. It's ready because it's *legible*." She turned to the case and opened the rear door. "The old clock told time to whoever climbed the stairs. This one tells the city." She indicated the new electrical impulse relay, the contacts she had designed herself, the circuit that would send a synchronized pulse down copper lines to the harbor office, the rail station, the garrison, the town hall—every public dial in the city stepping forward together, every hour, every day, forever. "One heart. One time. No arguments."

The colonel sat forward. "And the patrols synchronize to it how?"

"Pocket watches are verified against the relay dial at the garrison gate each Sunday," Bram said. "The relay is governed by this clock. There is no drift to accumulate, because there is only one clock. Everything else is a hand pointing at it."

"Everything else is a hand pointing at it," the colonel repeated, with the satisfaction of a man hearing his own strategy read back to him in brass. He looked at the council. "Gentlemen. The garrison's position is that this work exceeds the standard for journeyman certification. Considerably."

Bram felt the praise land and felt nothing. That was the strange thing, the thing she would remember later: standing in her tower, before the seven masters and the city, hearing the best work of her life described in the colonel's clipped official voice—*exceeds the standard considerably*—and feeling the words go past her like wind past a window. Because she knew what the clock was for now. She had built it as an argument with chaos, as her one clean thing, and the city had picked it up and made it a leash. Every tenth of a second she had won from entropy would now be spent marching men to wharves and trains, counting down the three weeks until a registry clerk's pen found a name and a boat left without a Lindqvist on it.

*You built a beautiful thing,* she thought, *and they will use it to count a man out of his own life.*

Vogel was speaking. She made her face attend.

"—and the strike train? The reports note you re-geared the hour strike."

"The old strike train had a lifting piece that fouled at the top of the hour if humidity got into the case," Bram said. "I re-cut the fan and re-pinned the gathering pallet. It will strike twelve at twelve, every twelve, until the wheels wear out, which at current rates is somewhere past both our funerals, Master Vogel."

"Presumption," said Vogel, and then, to the council, with the ghost of something that in a younger man would have been a smile: "Presumption is permitted when the presumption is load-bearing. I'll hear objections."

Brandt opened his mouth. Vogel looked at him. Brandt closed it.

"Then it is the judgment of this council," Vogel said, "that the work of the apprentice Bram Montrose is examined and found—" he paused, an old man savoring the one ceremony left to him, "—flawless."

The word went around the room. Bram heard it arrive in the gallery too, a rustle of coats, and she did not look at Astrid. She looked at the dial of her clock, where the minute hand stood two minutes shy of one, steady as a held note.

*Flawless.* She had spent eleven months removing every flaw from that machine, and she knew with a craftsman's certainty that she could have done it faster, and she knew with a woman's certainty why she hadn't. Every day she had spent perfecting the clock had been a day Emil's file sat in a drawer in the census bureau, gathering the small clerical errors Astrid was feeding it like a nurse feeding a patient she means to smuggle out a window. The perfection had been the delay. The masterpiece had been the alibi. And now the masterpiece was done, and the delay was done, and the colonel had said *Monday*.

* * *

They did the papers in the guild hall, because ceremony wants marble. Hessler stood beside her while the guild secretary wrote out the journeyman's certificate in a hand so fine it looked printed, and when he came to the name he read it aloud—*"Bram Montrose, having demonstrated mastery of his craft"*—and the word *his* went through the room without catching on anything, the way it had for five years, the way it would for the rest of her life if she was careful, and Bram signed beneath it with the quick confident hand of a boy who had never been anyone else.

Hessler shook her hand. His grip was dry and strong and it lasted a half-second longer than a master's grip needed to.

"Eleven months," he said again, quietly. "I've had journeymen take six years and produce less. Whatever you were before you came to my door, boy, you were running from something, and it made you good." His eyes were pale and very steady. "Don't stop being good. This city is about to get very particular about who it trusts, and I'd hate to lose you to a paperwork mistake."

It was a kindness. It was also, Bram understood, a warning, delivered by a man who had perhaps always known more than he said and had decided, in his granite way, that competence was its own nationality. "Thank you, Master."

"Don't thank me. Wind the strike train. It fouled once in March and I haven't forgotten it."

*It fouled once in March because I fouled it,* Bram thought, *because I was learning the machine's weaknesses so I'd know where to put the new ones.* "I'll see to it," she said.

* * *

The aldermen wanted speeches; the colonel wanted a photograph; the guild wives wanted to see the works, and so the afternoon went in small ceremonies, each one a grain of sand in the shoe, and it was nearly four before Bram got free and walked home the long way, along the harbor road, because the long way was where she could think.

The fog was coming in off the water in slow white ropes. The harbor lamps were just being lit, and down at the coal wharf a barge lay low against its fenders, black and patient, taking on its night load. She did not let herself look at it longer than a passing glance. She had gotten good at not looking.

*Flawless.* The word kept pace with her like a second set of footsteps.

She tried to make herself feel it. She had wanted this since she was nine years old, taking apart her mother's mantel clock with a hairpin, since the first time she understood that a machine would never ask her where she was born, would never care what was under her shirt, would only ever ask one question—*is the work true?*—and would accept the answer without a syllable of argument. She had crossed a sea and buried a name and bound her body and learned to answer to *boy* in four languages, all to stand in a guild hall and hear an old man say *flawless*, and now she had heard it, and it was like hearing it about someone else. About Birgitta, maybe. About the girl who would have wept. Bram Montrose just counted the cost.

Because here was the ledger, and she could read it now with the council's own cold clarity. The clock was certified. On Monday the city synchronized to it. On Monday the patrols' pocket watches were set from her dial, and the whistles blew on her hour, and the trains left on her minute—and the coal barge, the patient black barge, would sail on the night tide at 12:40, forty minutes past the hour when every watch in the city would agree with every other watch to the tenth of a second, and the harbor gates would be watched by men whose timing could no longer drift, because she had seen to it personally that it couldn't. She had spent eleven months building the wall between Emil and the water. She had built it so well they'd given her a certificate.

Unless the clock was wrong.

Unless, on one particular night, the flawless machine developed a flaw—small, slow, invisible, the kind of flaw no inspector would catch for days, a drifting minute hand, a strike that lagged, a divergence that would scatter the patrols like beads off a broken string and buy one fog-bound hour, one 12:40, one barge going north.

She had known this was coming since September. She had told herself she had three weeks to decide, and then two, and now the certificate was in her breast pocket, warm from her body, and the three weeks were down to days, and the decision had stopped being a decision and become a date.

Ahead of her, at the corner by the customs shed, a figure detached itself from the fog and fell into step beside her without a word: grey coat, census bureau posture, a woman walking home from work who happened to be walking the same direction. They went a hundred yards before Astrid spoke, quietly, eyes forward.

"Congratulations, Master Montrose. *Flawless.* The whole city will be talking about it."

"The whole city will be *obeying* it," Bram said. "That's rather different."

"Yes." Astrid's breath made a small white flag in the cold air. "I came to tell you something, and I'd rather tell you walking, where the walls can't hear. My supervisor found the errors in Emil's file."

Bram's stride didn't break, but only because she had spent five years training it not to. "Errors."

"Three of them. A transposed date, a misspelled surname, a missing registry stamp. Small things. The kind of things a careful clerk never makes and a tired one always does." Astrid's voice was level as a spirit gauge. "He's ordered me to correct the file personally. By Friday. Which means that by Friday, the registry is clean, the name is flagged, and the deportation order goes to the harbor police for execution." She paused. "The barge sails Thursday night, Bram. 12:40. And after Monday, the whole city keeps time by your clock."

The fog closed around them, and the harbor lamps burned like coins at the bottom of milk, and somewhere behind them the observatory clock struck the quarter hour—clear, true, flawless, audible across the whole sleeping city, every ear in it agreeing on the time.

"Friday," Bram said.

"Friday," said Astrid. "So you'll have to decide what your masterpiece is for. Before it decides for you."
