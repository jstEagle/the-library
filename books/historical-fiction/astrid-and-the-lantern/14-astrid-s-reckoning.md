## 14. Astrid's Reckoning

The summons came at nine o'clock on Monday morning, delivered not by message but by silence. Herr Doktor Voss stood in the doorway of the records room and simply looked at her until the other clerks understood they were not wanted, and then he came in and closed the door behind him with a click that sounded, to Astrid, exactly like a lock.

"Lindqvist." He did not sit. He never sat when the news was bad; sitting was for conversations he intended to have twice. "The Lindqvist file. Enemy registry, number 4471. Emil Lindqvist. Do you know it?"

"It's mine," she said. "By district."

"By district," Voss agreed, and let the word hang there like a fly in amber. "There are irregularities in it. The residence verification is dated after the deportation notice was drafted—which would mean we notified him to appear for processing at an address we had not yet confirmed he occupied. The occupation entry has been struck through and rewritten in a hand I do not recognize. And the forwarding address—" he drew a folder from under his arm and laid it flat on the table between them, but did not open it, as though even the paper were evidence— "the forwarding address reads *care of the harbor mission, north quay*, which ceased operations in March. There is no harbor mission on the north quay, Lindqvist. There is a coal yard and a chapel that flooded."

Astrid kept her hands still on the desk. She had practiced this, though she had told herself she was only tidying files late at night, only being thorough. She had practiced the face: mild, tired, faintly offended. A good clerk's offense—the offense of accuracy.

"I'll review it today," she said.

"You'll correct it today." Voss opened the folder at last and turned it toward her, and there it all was, her own small treacheries laid out in her own careful ink. "You will go to the subject's last verified address personally. You will confirm his presence or record his absence. You will enter the corrections yourself, in your own hand, dated, signed. Friday by close of business." He paused, and something almost gentle moved through his voice, which was worse than anger. "I want you to understand that this is not a punishment. You were assigned the file because your work is trusted. That trust is the entire machinery of this office. Without it we might as well employ pigeons."

"Yes, sir."

"And Lindqvist." He was at the door now, one hand on the frame. He was an old man, a widower, a man who had spent thirty years turning human lives into columns of figures and believed, sincerely, that the figures protected people—that a city which knew exactly who lived where was a city where no one could disappear without a ripple. She had believed it once too. It had been a beautiful belief, like the master clock: precise, gleaming, and indifferent to what it measured. "Your brother is on that register. If anyone else had found these irregularities, the question would not be clerical. Do you understand me?"

She understood him perfectly. He was telling her that he had chosen to see sloppiness where he might have seen treason, and that this mercy had a shape, and the shape was Friday.

"I understand, sir."

The door clicked shut. Astrid sat alone among the shelves of drawers, ten thousand lives in alphabetical order, and listened to the building breathe—the scratch of pens, the cough of the stove, somewhere upstairs the wall clock that every clerk set his watch by. All of it ticking toward Friday.

* * *

She went at noon to the north quay, because the file required it and because she wanted to see the place with her own eyes before she decided what her eyes would report.

The customs shed stood where Bram had said it would, low and black-timbered against the gray water, its doors chained but its side window broken long ago and stuffed with sacking that a determined man could move. Emil was inside. She knew it the way you know a lit window in a dark house—not by seeing him but by seeing the signs of him: a tin cup by the sill, a folded blanket gone gray with coal dust, the ends of bread wrapped in newspaper. He had made a home of four square meters of smuggled space, and he had done it neatly, because Emil had always been neat, even as a boy, folding his shirts into squares while Astrid's room looked like a storm had opinions about it.

"Emil." She rapped on the boards. "It's me."

The sacking shifted. His face appeared in the broken window, thinner than five years should have made it, eyes quick and then, when they settled on her, relieved in a way that hurt to look at.

"Astrid." He smiled. "They've sent my sister to arrest me. The bureau's efficiency improves daily."

"No one sent me. Give me your hand."

She pulled him up over the sill more than he climbed, and they sat together on the floor in the coal-smelling dark, their backs to the wall, like children hiding from a governess. Up close she could see how badly he was eating. She had brought rolls and hard sausage in her bag and she put them in his hands and watched him try to eat slowly and fail.

"Friday," she said.

He stopped chewing.

"What happens Friday?"

"The file comes due. Corrections in my own hand. Either I write down where you are—and then they come here with the watch and the paperwork and it's over—or I write that the premises were checked and found empty, tenant departed, destination unknown." She looked at the wall, at the old tar lines marking high tides going back decades. "Destination unknown buys time. Days. Maybe a week if the weather's bad."

"And then?"

"And then someone else checks the shed, Emil. Someone who isn't me. You cannot live in a hole in the quay while the whole city learns to tell time by one clock." She heard her own voice sharpening and could not stop it. "Every patrol, every train, every whistle synchronized to the minute. Bram finished it. Did you know that? They gave her a medal for it. Your escape route runs on the machine your friend built better than anyone asked her to."

"She didn't finish it," Emil said quietly. "Not yet. There's a barge Friday night. Northbound, the tide at 12:40. Her foreman owes me—" He caught himself. "Owed my old foreman. The captain will take coal, and coal can take a man."

"Friday night." Astrid laughed, and it came out ugly. "Do you hear it? The same day. My deadline and your tide, arriving together like they planned it. The city will run on that clock by Friday, Emil. The patrols will set their watches from its bells. At 12:40 they'll know exactly where they are and exactly where you aren't supposed to be."

"Then Bram will think of something." He said it simply, without drama, the way you'd say *the sun will rise*. "She promised. In peacetime, on the pier, she promised, and Bram doesn't—"

"Bram lied to your face." Astrid turned to look at him. "Did she tell you that? When you came to her door. She told you the promise was dead. I watched her say it."

He was quiet a moment. Then he smiled again, smaller, and shook his head.

"She lied," he agreed. "And then she started planning anyway. Because that's what Bram does. She says no with her mouth and yes with her hands. You'll learn it. You're learning it already—you're sitting in a coal shed feeding a fugitive instead of filing him."

Astrid looked away. Through the broken window the harbor lay flat and pewter-colored, and far off, above the rooftops, the observatory dome caught a thread of pale light.

* * *

That evening she walked home the long way, past the census bureau, past the church whose clock had always been five minutes slow and everyone had always known it, past her parents' street.

She had not meant to stop. But the house was lit, and her mother was at the front room window doing nothing, which meant waiting, and so Astrid went up the steps and let herself in before she could argue herself out of it.

Her father was reading. Her mother rose and poured tea without asking, and for a while the three of them performed the family they had been: respectable, orderly, a household where everything was entered in its proper column. Her father asked about work. She said it went well. Her mother asked if she was eating, and touched her wrist briefly in passing, which was as much tenderness as the room allowed.

It was her mother who finally said it, setting down the pot.

"They're saying the observatory clock is certified Friday. Official city time. Your father's society is invited to the demonstration." A pause, delicate as a held breath. "And they're saying a girl in your office has been careless with an enemy file. They don't name her. But they describe her."

Astrid felt the tea go cold in her hands.

"If it were you," her mother said, not looking at her, addressing the teapot, the wallpaper, the whole apparatus of their respectability, "your father would resign rather than attend the inquiry. He would stand in that hall and let them write his name down as the father of a criminal, because that is what honor requires. And I would stand beside him. That is the life we chose. That is the life we assumed you chose too."

"Mama—"

"We are not asking you anything," her father said mildly, turning a page. "We are only describing ourselves. So that you know what you are carrying, and whom."

She walked home in the fog with those words turning over and over, trying to decide whether she had just been threatened or armed. Both, probably. That was her mother's gift: to make a cage sound like a weapon and a weapon sound like a cage, and leave the daughter to choose which she was holding.

Her parents had given everything to correctness. Their names in the right registries, their taxes paid before the notices arrived, their griefs conducted indoors. When the first war rumors hardened into law they had said *of course*, when neighbors were listed they had said *how unfortunate*, when Emil's name appeared in the newspaper's registry supplement her mother had cut it out with scissors before Astrid came to Sunday dinner, so the table need never mention it. Respectability was not cowardice, they would have said. Respectability was the wall that kept the wolves from the door. And now their daughter was the wolf, standing outside it in the fog, deciding whether to knock.

* * *

At midnight she sat at her kitchen table with the file open before her and the correction form blank beside it, and she made herself look, properly look, at what she was about to do.

If she wrote the truth—*subject confirmed present, customs shed, north quay*—then Emil was taken by Saturday, processed within the month, deported to a country at war's edge that had stopped answering letters. And she would be a faithful servant of the bureau, praised for diligence, promoted perhaps, and every morning for the rest of her life she would wake knowing precisely what her precision had purchased. Voss had said the figures protected people. She would finally learn whether he believed it, watching him sign the order.

Or she wrote the lie—*premises checked, found vacant, subject departed, destination unknown*—and bought him six days, maybe eight, enough for a tide and a barge and a woman with solder burns on her fingers to keep a promise made in peacetime. And if it failed—if Kessel talked, if the watch came early, if Bram's hands slipped—then the lie would unravel backward through the ink to her, and it would not be clerical then either. Her mother had described herself accurately. Honor would require standing in the hall.

Two columns. She had been trained all her life to keep two columns and balance them.

Astrid dipped the pen.

What surprised her was not the decision—it was how quiet the decision was. She had expected it to feel like the moment the master clock struck, audible across the whole city, every ear agreeing. Instead it felt like closing a drawer. Like the hundredth time she had written a date in a box, except this time the box contained a man's life and she was choosing which number to put in it.

*Premises checked 14th inst. Found vacant. Subject appears to have departed prior to notice. Destination unknown.*

Her own hand. Dated. Signed.

She blotted it, squared it, and slid it into the folder where it lay looking exactly like every honest page around it. Then she sat for a long moment in the ticking dark of her kitchen, listening to her little mantel clock—a cheap thing, five minutes fast, never corrected—count out a time that belonged to no law and no bureau.

Loyalty, she thought, had always been presented to her as a place: the bureau, the family, the city, walls you stood inside. She had never been told it could also be a direction. That you could keep faith with a person instead of a system, and that once you chose, the choice did not feel like falling. It felt like arriving somewhere you had already been walking toward for years without admitting the road existed.

She thought of Bram's face across the observatory workbench—*you'll have to decide what your masterpiece is for*—and understood, finally, that the sentence had been meant for both of them.

Tomorrow she would carry the file back to the bureau and watch Voss initial it and say nothing. Tomorrow night she would find Bram at the observatory and tell her the window was real and shrinking: Friday, the 12:40 tide, patrols synchronized to the bells. Somewhere in the days between, two women who barely trusted each other would have to build an escape out of fog, coal, and a clock that must lie beautifully for exactly one hour.

Astrid banked the stove and went to bed, and slept better than she had any right to.

The mantel clock ticked on, five minutes fast, keeping its own private time—and Friday was coming for all of them at once.
