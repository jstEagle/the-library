## 15. What Birgitta Owes

My mother lived on the fourth floor of a building that smelled of boiled cabbage and floor wax, in a room where every clock had been stopped since the day my father died. She kept them that way on purpose. Eleven clocks on the wall, eleven dead faces, and her sitting beneath them like a woman who had outlasted a jury.

She opened the door before I knocked the second time. She always knew my tread. "You look thin," she said, which was how she said *hello* and *I've missed you* and *you are a fool* all at once, in the language we had built over six years of not saying any of those things.

"Mother."

"Come in before the stairwell hears you." She said it without irony, which was the closest she came to admitting she understood the shape of my life. She closed the door, turned the key, and hung it on its nail inside her apron pocket, where it clinked against her scissors like a small, nervous heartbeat.

The room was exactly as it always was: the stove, the two chairs, the lace curtains washed thin as communion wafers. And the clocks. My father had been a repairer of second-rate mantel clocks for first-rate people, and when he died she had let every one of them run down rather than wind them, because winding them would have meant admitting time went on. I had offered, once a year, to set them right. Once a year she refused. It was our liturgy.

"Sit," she said. "There's coffee. It's terrible. I made it for you anyway."

I sat. The chair creaked its familiar complaint. On the table between us lay her mending, a shirt collar half-turned, needle parked in the fabric like a ship at anchor. She poured coffee into two cups that didn't match and pushed the better one toward me, which was her other way of saying things.

"You came on a Thursday," she said. "You always come on Sundays."

"I need something."

"Of course you do." She sat down opposite me, folded her hands over the apron, and looked at me with my own eyes—because they were hers first, that grey that people called unusual on a boy and called *Lindqvist eyes, are you certain* on a girl, and she had watched me learn to lower my lashes over them before I learned long division. "Ask it, then. You've been rehearsing since the stairs."

The coffee was terrible. I drank it anyway. "I need the papers."

She didn't pretend not to know which papers. That was the thing about my mother that I had spent years resenting and had lately, without noticing when it happened, begun to rely on: she never wasted a lie on me.

"The christening record," I said. "Father's guild registration. The family register with the surname written in his hand. Everything that proves who I was before I was no one."

"Birgitta," she said. Just the name. She said it the way you set down something heavy after carrying it a long way.

"Birgitta," I agreed.

"And what does Bram Montrose want with a dead girl's christening record?"

I had prepared an answer. I had prepared it on the walk over, in the fog, past the harbor where the cranes stood like gallows against the grey water, and it had been a good answer, careful and partial, the kind of answer I gave everyone. Sitting in that room under the stopped clocks, with her needle parked and her key in her pocket and her terrible coffee going cold, I found I couldn't lift it.

So I told her the truth.

* * *

I told her about the census taker. Not all of it—I kept Astrid's name out of it, kept the promise out of it, kept Emil's face out of it—but I told her that a man needed to leave the city, that the law had decided his blood was a crime, that the documents that would let him travel had to be real enough to survive a clerk's suspicion, and that the only surname in this city I had any right to use was the one I'd been born under and had buried.

"You want to forge papers in your father's name," she said.

"In our name."

"Your father's name." She said it flat, but her thumb moved over the back of her other hand, the way it did when she was holding something down. "The man who apprenticed you at nine years old because you had quick hands and he was proud of them. The man who, when you were fourteen, sat me down at this table and said, *Marta, our daughter is going to be a clockmaker, and the world will not allow it, so our daughter is going to have to stop being our daughter.*" Her voice didn't rise. It never rose. "He said it like he was reading a repair estimate. *This will cost us the girl. The work will survive.* And I told him he was mad, and he said, *Look at her hands, Marta. Tell me what else they're for.*"

"I remember."

"You remember being fourteen. You don't remember what it cost us." She stood, abruptly, and went to the wall of stopped clocks, and stood in front of them with her back to me. "I never approved. You know that. Six years, and I never once said, *yes, this is right.* I said, *be careful,* and *come on Sundays,* and *eat something.* I let you do it because your father had already decided, and because when I looked at you in that first coat with the shoulders padded out, you stood straighter than you ever had as a girl. That's the truth of it. I hated it and it made you taller. Both at once. That's what being a mother is."

"Mother—"

"Sit still." She was opening the cabinet beneath the clocks, the one I had assumed held linens. Her hands moved among folded things, and then she drew out a tin—biscuit tin, the paint worn to a ghost of roses—and my chest went tight, because I knew that tin. I had believed it burned with the rest of my father's effects, or buried, or lost.

She brought it back to the table and set it down between us without opening it.

"Your father kept everything," she said. "He was a sentimental man pretending to be a practical one. He wrote to a cousin in the north every Christmas for thirty years and never once told me what he wrote. He kept your first milk tooth in his toolbox for eleven years. I found it when he died." Her mouth did something that was almost a smile. "And he kept the papers. Because he was a practical man pretending to be a sentimental one, and he said to me, the last winter, *if it ever goes wrong for Bram, the papers will be the way back. Or the way forward. One of those.* He didn't say which. He never said which."

She opened the tin.

* * *

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth against damp, lay my father's whole case for who I had been. The christening record, the church's seal brown and intact. The family register, my father's hand small and exact as a gear train: *Birgitta Marta Montrose, born the fourth of March, 1891, in the parish of Sankt Anna.* The guild registration that had made him a master in a city that had since stopped asking masters anything except whether they were loyal. A letter, folded and yellowed, that I did not read then and have not read since, because the outside said *For Bram, if needed* in my father's hand, and some doors you only get to open once.

My mother pushed the tin across the table the way she'd pushed the better coffee cup. Slowly. Deliberately. A sentence in our private language.

"You understand what you're asking," she said. "If they catch the man you're helping, they will trace the papers. They will trace them to this name. To this room. To me."

"I know."

"And you'll be traced. Bram Montrose, journeyman, the pride of Hessler's shop. They'll open the record and find a christening and a girl, and everything you built will come down in an afternoon." She said it without drama, the way she announced that the stove needed coal. "I'm not telling you to stop. I stopped telling you what to do when you were fourteen and your father outvoted me. I'm telling you so you can't say later that you didn't know."

"I know," I said again. "I've known since the day the master clock became the city's time. Every patrol, every train, every curfew whistle—synchronized to my work. I built the thing they'll use to catch him. The papers are the only way to unbuild it."

"And the girl?" she asked. "The census girl. The one you don't name."

I looked at her. She looked back, mild as Sunday.

"You think I don't hear it in your voice," she said. "You talk about the clock like a craftsman and you talk about her like a man talking about weather he can't predict. Is she in this? Is she the one who'll be standing next to you when it comes down?"

"I don't know what she is."

"You know exactly what she is. You just don't know if she'll still be it on the other side." She closed the tin, pressed the lid down with both palms, and slid it the last inch into my hands. "Take them. Take all of it. Your father was right about one thing—I'll grant him that much, wherever he is, arguing with me. The papers are the way back or the way forward. You're choosing forward. For someone else. That's the only part that surprises me."

I held the tin. It weighed almost nothing. It weighed everything in the room.

"Why now?" I asked. "Six years you've kept these under the linens. Why give them today?"

She was quiet for a while. The stove ticked as it cooled, the only clock in the room still trying. Then she reached out and took my hand—the right one, the one with the burn scar across the knuckles from the first mainspring I'd ever fought—and turned it over, and looked at it, and set it back down on the table between us, palm up, like something returned.

"Because I finally understand why you became someone else," she said. "I thought it was ambition. Your father said it was necessity. I thought, all these years, it was that you couldn't bear to be small, and I grieved it, Birgitta, I grieved it like a death, every Sunday, watching you eat and not say anything true." Her voice finally cracked, just once, and she pressed it flat again with visible effort. "But it wasn't ambition. It was that you were always going to owe something too large to pay as a girl. You became someone with hands big enough to hold the debt." She stood, and took my empty cup, and refilled it with the terrible coffee. "Drink. Then go. The fog's coming in off the water, and you don't want to be on the stairs when the patrol makes its round."

* * *

At the door, with the key already back in her apron pocket and her hand on the latch, she stopped me.

"Bram." My working name, in her mouth, for the first time in years. She'd said it like she was trying it on and finding it fit better than she'd wanted. "When it's done—whatever happens—bring the tin back. Not the papers. The tin. Your father's tooth is still in the bottom of it, under the oilcloth. I checked this morning."

I didn't trust my voice. I nodded.

"And wind them," she said, nodding at the wall of stopped clocks without looking at them. "When it's over. All eleven. I'll allow it."

I went down the four flights with the tin inside my coat, against my chest, where the binding was, where no one would ever think to look for a girl's whole history. On the second floor a door opened and an old man glanced at me, at my coat, at my boots, and I gave him the flat, busy stare of a young man late for work, and he closed his door.

Outside, the fog had come in exactly as she promised, thick off the harbor, the gas lamps haloed and weak. Somewhere across the city the observatory stood on its hill, and inside it my master clock—my flawless, certified, city-official masterpiece—was keeping perfect time for the patrols that would kill Emil Lindqvist if the papers in my coat weren't good enough.

I had the name now. I had the seal, the register, the parish, the dates. I had a father's handwriting exact enough to copy in my sleep, because I'd learned my own hand from his.

What I didn't have was three days of quiet at the workbench—and tomorrow morning, Kessel would be at the shop, watching, the way he always watched now, and the guild's man was due to inspect the master clock's escapement by Friday.

I turned up my collar and walked into the fog toward the observatory, and in my chest the tin rode light as a promise and heavy as a debt, and I began, step by step, to plan how a clockmaker sabotages her own masterpiece without anyone—not the guild, not the inspectors, not the city, not even the clock—ever knowing it was done on purpose.
