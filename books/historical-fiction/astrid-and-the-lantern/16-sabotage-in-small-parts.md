## 16. Sabotage in Small Parts

The workshop at night was the only church Bram had ever believed in.

She sat alone at the long bench after Hessler had gone home, after Kessel had stamped out with his collar up and his eyes down, after the last apprentice had swept the brass filings into the pan and hung his apron on its nail. The gas was turned low. The master clock's movement sat on the bench in pieces—not all of it, never all of it, only the parts she had brought down from the observatory tower in a canvas bag that weighed less than a hymnal and more than a coffin lid.

The escapement lay before her under the lamp. Pallet fork, escape wheel, the delicate anchor of her own design. She had spent eleven months making this mechanism honest. She had filed the pallets to tolerances her master called excessive and her own hands called necessary. She had adjusted the impulse until the wheel let go of each tooth like a man releasing a held breath—clean, even, without favoritism. When the guild certified this clock, they would certify the finest piece of horological work the free city had seen in a generation, and every patrol whistle, every curfew siren, every deportation order would move to the rhythm of her craft.

She picked up the finest file in the shop. Number four cut. She had owned it since she was fourteen, when she'd bought it with her first wages under a name that wasn't hers yet, and she had never once used it to make a thing worse.

That was the creed. Every clockmaker had one, whether he spoke it or not: *leave the work better than you found it.* Hessler said it the way other men said grace. Bram had built her whole life on the back of it—because a person who mended time could not be accused of breaking anything else. The lie she lived was stitched into a trade whose whole morality was truthfulness. Gears did not deceive. A clock that ran wrong was not lying; it was confessing. Every error was legible to anyone patient enough to read it, and Bram had spent her life becoming the most patient reader in the city.

Now she had to write lies in a language she'd spent twenty years learning to read.

* * *

She started with the pallets.

It was the obvious place and therefore the dangerous place, and she sat for an hour before touching anything, turning the fork in her fingers, looking for the way in. An escapement that lost time did so honestly—friction, wear, dirt. Inspectors knew the honest ways. They would look for them first, and Bram's sabotage had to wear the clothing of honest failure.

Wear. That was the word. She needed the clock to *wear*—to age, in days, the way a good clock aged in decades. She needed to manufacture time's passage inside a machine built to defy it.

The answer, when it came, was almost insultingly simple. She took the file and did not cut the pallets at all. Instead she took a burnisher—soft steel, no cutting edge at all—and worked the faces of the pallet stones in slow, circular strokes, polishing away the microscopically thin oil film that let them glide. Dry pallets. A clock with dry pallets ran slow, gained the error gradually, and showed the inspector a mechanism that had simply been neglected. Neglect was believable. Neglect happened to every clock in the world.

Her hands shook on the first pass, and she set the burnisher down and pressed her palms flat against the bench until the shaking stopped.

It wasn't fear. She had been afraid before, in the fog on the harbor wall, in the census office, in the tower with Kessel's shadow on the stair. Fear was a weather she knew how to work in. This was something else. This was the feeling of a hand turning against its own training, the way a lamed leg turns against the body that feeds it. Every stroke of the burnisher said: *you are making this worse.* And every stroke had to be perfect anyway, because a botched sabotage was worse than none—a clock that stopped would be caught in an hour, but a clock that drifted would be forgiven, explained, tolerated, until the tolerance itself became the weapon.

She worked the left pallet for six minutes. Then the right, for six minutes and eleven seconds. The asymmetry was the point. Symmetric wear was a signature; asymmetric wear was an accident.

When she finished, she assembled the escapement in the bench vise, rigged a test weight, and watched it run. The beat was still true. Of course it was—the error wouldn't show for days, as the oil film degraded unevenly, as the pallets began to grip. By Thursday the clock would lose perhaps four seconds an hour. By Saturday, seven. By the time any inspector thought to check it against the telegraph time signal from the coast station, the drift would look like nothing more than a mechanism that needed oiling—which it would, by then, honestly need.

She sat back and looked at what her hands had done and felt nothing she had a word for.

* * *

The minute hand came next, and the minute hand was harder, because the minute hand was visible.

A clock could lose time invisibly, in the privacy of its own works, but a hand that sat wrong on its arbor was a public confession. So she didn't touch the hand. She touched the cannon pinion beneath it—the small friction-fit sleeve that carried the hand around the dial. She opened the friction joint a hair, a quarter-turn of a screwdriver no wider than a needle, so that the hand would begin to slip under the load of its own counterweight. Not now. Not tomorrow. In four days, five, when the cold snap the almanac promised thickened the oil in every bearing in the tower, the minute hand would begin to lag behind the movement by fractions of a minute, then whole ones, and anyone who looked would see a hand that had slipped on its arbor—the most ordinary failure in the world, the kind that happened to a thousand clocks every winter, the kind that got a shrug and a tightening screw.

She held the pinion up to the lamp. It looked exactly as it had before. That was the horror of it. Good sabotage, like good craftsmanship, left no visible trace. The only difference between the two was intent, and intent lived nowhere a lens could find it.

"You're talking to it," she said aloud, and realized she had been—murmuring to the pinion, apologizing to it, the way her mother used to murmur to bread dough. She stopped. She did not apologize. Apology was a luxury for people whose work would be forgiven.

She thought of Emil instead. Emil at her door in the dark, hat in his hands, asking with his whole ruined face whether the promise still stood, and her saying no—saying it in a steady voice while something behind her ribs tore like wet paper. She had told herself the lie was mercy. She had lasted four nights on that story. Then Astrid had come to the observatory with her census ledger and her brother's name copied out in her own careful hand, and had said, in the flat voice of a woman reading her own sentence: *They deport him on the twenty-sixth. I can delay the file nine days with clerical errors. After that it's out of my hands. Is it in yours?*

And Bram had looked at the master clock above them, ticking in the dark of the tower like a heart she'd built, and had understood that it was in hers, and had always been, and that the promise made on a broken pocket watch five summers ago had simply been waiting for her to grow skilled enough to keep it.

The city would run on this clock. Curfews would open and close on its strike. Patrols would synchronize their watches at its dial. And on the night tide, at 12:40, a coal barge would take on its last ton and cast off north, and every man whose business it was to stop it would be standing where her clock told him to stand—eleven minutes late, or twelve, or however many she could steal and hide inside the mechanism like coins sewn into a coat lining.

She was not breaking the clock. She was teaching it to lie for her. And the only way to teach an honest machine to lie was to lie to it first—file by file, stroke by stroke, in the language it trusted most.

* * *

The strike train was the third part, and the strike train was where she nearly failed.

The bells of the master clock marked the hours for the whole harbor district, and the patrols set their rounds by them. A lagging strike—bells that fell a minute or two behind the hands—would buy her the last, crucial confusion on the escape night: whistles answering a midnight that had already passed, men checking watches against a tower that disagreed with itself. But the strike train was the loudest part of the mechanism, the part every ear in the city listened to without knowing it, and any error there had to be perfect.

She planned it as a governor fault. The fly—the little vaned brake that regulated the strike train's speed—could be made to drag, its arbor binding slightly in a bushing that had, she would ensure, a trace of grit in it. Grit. The oldest story in clockmaking. She crushed a grain of fine sand between her fingers until it was powder, and with a camel-hair brush no thicker than an eyelash she laid a whisper of it into the strike governor's bushing, then oiled over it, then oiled again, burying the crime under three coats of maintenance.

The clock would strike true for a week. Then the grit would find its way into the oil film, and the fly would begin to drag, and midnight would come later than midnight—first by thirty seconds, then by a minute, then by as much as the cold and the grit between them could arrange. And when the inspectors finally opened the case, they would find grit in a bushing, and grit in a bushing was an act of God or of a careless oiler, and Bram Montrose had never once been careless.

She closed the movement's back plate and sat with her hands in her lap.

The lamp hissed. Around the workshop, the wall of regulators she had spent years building ticked in their cases—her own work, every one of them honest, every one of them running within a second of the truth because she had made them that way and checked them every Sunday night. She could hear the lie she'd planted already, or imagined she could: the master clock's movement on the bench, ticking with the same clean beat it had always had, carrying its secret the way she carried hers, in perfect working order.

*You are the same thing,* she thought, looking at it. *You and I. Built to be true. Taught to be otherwise. Still running. God help us, still running well.*

* * *

She made her mistakes the way she made everything: on paper first.

In the back of her ledger, under a column of oil costs, she drew the master clock's rate curve as it would appear over the coming week—seconds lost per day, plotted against the cold. A real mechanism drifted along a curve; a faked one drifted along a line, and inspectors could smell a line the way a dog smelled fear. So she gave the drift a personality. A cold snap on Wednesday to steepen it. A warm front Friday to flatten it. A tiny recovery Saturday morning—mechanisms always seemed to recover just before they failed, and inspectors trusted a mechanism that fought its own decay. She was writing a biography for a fault, and the biography had to be more convincing than the fault.

Kessel would check the clock. She counted on it now. He had been watching her for weeks—she'd bought his silence once with the detent escapement and learned what the purchase cost, which was everything, forever—and a man like Kessel verified what he intended to sell. Let him check it. Let him find a clock running two minutes slow on Saturday and write it in his little book and sit on it, saving it for the moment it could hurt her most. By the time he understood what the drift was for, the drift would have done its work, and the barge would be a black shape in the fog past the harbor light, and his book would be a record of nothing.

That was the plan. She wrote it in oil costs and rate figures and the shorthand of her trade, and then she closed the ledger, and then she put her head down on her arms on the bench, among the brass filings, and stayed there a long time.

Her hands were ruined. That was how it felt—not tired, ruined. Twenty years of training had one commandment, and her hands had broken it eleven times tonight, each adjustment a small deliberate sin, and the worst part, the part she could not say to anyone, not to Astrid, not to the dark, was that they had done it *beautifully*. The burnisher strokes had been even. The grit had been invisible. The rate curve had a shape a master would nod at. Her hands had committed treason with the same excellence they'd given to truth, and excellence, once you had it, did not care what it served.

*Love expressed as imperfection.* She almost laughed into her sleeves. Her whole creed, inverted. Somewhere north of the city Emil was sleeping in a customs shed or a coal hole or whatever shelter Astrid had found him, trusting a promise she'd denied to his face, and the only way left to keep that promise was to become, for one week, the worst clockmaker in the free city—so precisely, so skillfully, that no one would ever know.

She lifted her head. Wiped her eyes with the back of her wrist, briskly, the way you wiped off oil. Got the canvas bag.

* * *

She went up to the observatory at two in the morning, because the tower was the only place the work could be finished and the only place she could not be seen doing it.

The fog had come off the water again, thick as wool, and the city below was a few yellow smudges and the smell of coal smoke. Inside the tower the great movement filled the dark like a cathedral organ, and she climbed the ladder into its works with the bag of altered parts against her chest, and she made the exchange.

Pallet fork out, pallet fork in. Cannon pinion out, pinion in. Governor bushing—she worked by touch and by the lamp on its hook, and her hands, the traitors, the sinners, were steady as they had ever been in her life. The old parts went into the bag. The new lie went into the clock. The beat, when she set it swinging, was true. The strike, when she let it run off the quarter-hour on the test lever, fell clean and bright into the fog, and some dog in the harbor district answered it, and somewhere below a patrolman checked his watch against a midnight that was still, for a few days more, honest.

She sat in the gearwork afterward with her back against the great wheel and let the clock run around her, its weights descending by fractions of a millimeter, its escape wheel releasing tooth by tooth into the dark. Eleven months she had given this machine. It was the best thing her hands had ever made, and she had just spent a night teaching it to betray the city that would praise it, and there was no version of the coming week in which she did not lose something irreplaceable. If she was caught, she lost the name she'd built, the papers she'd bled for, the workshop, the wall of honest clocks. If she wasn't caught, she lost something she couldn't name yet—some clean thing in herself that had believed the work and the person could stay separate.

Astrid would say the clean thing was a luxury. Astrid, who copied names against her oath and fed her brother in a customs shed and called it duty with a straight face. Astrid, who had stood in this tower three nights ago and said, *You'll do it. I watched you decide before you did.* And Bram had hated her for it, and had known she was right, and had known that too was a kind of being seen—the terrifying mercy, the crack in the wall that let the light in and the cold.

The clock struck the half hour above her, one clean note.

She climbed down, shouldered the bag of true parts—the honest pallets, the tight pinion, the clean bushing, evidence of her own virtue that she could not burn and could not keep—and let herself out into the fog.

The streets were empty and curfewed. She walked fast, head down, and she was three streets from the harbor when she saw the light.

It came from the workshop—Hessler & Sons, dark at this hour, dark every hour at this time of night—and yet a lamp burned behind the ground-floor glass, and a shadow crossed it once, unhurried, the shadow of a man standing at her bench.

She stopped in the black of a doorway, the bag of honest parts heavy against her back, and watched her own window glow, and understood that whatever Kessel had found—or thought he'd found—he had decided to stop waiting for it to ripen.
