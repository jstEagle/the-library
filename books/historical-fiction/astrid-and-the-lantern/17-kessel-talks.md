## 17. Kessel Talks

Astrid learned about it the way she learned everything that mattered: from a file.

She had come early to the census bureau, before the clerks arrived and the gas lamps had to be lit, because the quiet hours were when she could move Emil's paper one more step down its long, invented road—a transfer request misfiled to a district office that processed transfers at the speed of continental drift, a duplicate form with his surname spelled *Lindkvist*, which would buy another week while some pedant in Records hunted for a man who did not exist under a spelling that almost did.

The folder was waiting on her desk, square and gray, tied with bureau string.

No clerk left folders on desks overnight. Folders lived in cabinets, in locked rooms, in the custody of men whose whole careers were built on never being able to say they hadn't seen something. A folder on her desk was a message, and she read it standing up, with her coat still on, the way one reads a summons or a diagnosis.

*Re: irregularities in enemy-national registry, district seven.* Her own initials appeared in it three times. The discrepancies were listed with a loving precision that made her stomach go cold—not because they were wrong, but because they were right. The misspelling. The delayed transfer. The inspection date she'd nudged by six days so that Emil's lodgings would be verified after, not before, the deadline for his registration of residence.

Six days. She had thought herself generous and invisible. Whoever wrote this had laid her small sins out on a table like a watchmaker's parts and found each one wanting.

At the bottom, unsigned but unmistakable in its officious hand: *Sources within the trade community suggest irregular contact between bureau personnel and persons connected to observatory clockworks.*

Persons connected to observatory clockworks.

Bram.

Astrid sat down slowly. Outside the window, the fog was doing what it always did this time of year—coming off the water in gray ropes, swallowing the cranes, turning the city into a rumor of itself. Somewhere out there was a man named Kessel who had watched her meet Bram on the harbor steps, who had counted her visits to the workshop, who had done arithmetic with other people's lives and arrived at an answer he could sell.

She should have felt afraid. What she felt first, absurdly, was anger at the phrase *sources within the trade community*. Kessel couldn't even betray them honestly; he had outsourced it, hinted it, kept his hands clean for the moment when clean hands might still be worth something.

Then the fear came, and it came properly.

*

She spent the morning being scrupulously ordinary. She corrected two files she didn't care about. She accepted coffee from old Marta and complained about the damp. When her supervisor, Herr Brandt, passed her desk, she looked up with the mild, tired face of a woman thinking about lunch, and he paused—one beat too long—and said, "Lindqvist. You'll have seen the memorandum."

"About the registry discrepancies? Yes." She let a frown of professional concern gather. "It's troubling. If someone's tampering with deportation schedules—" she shook her head, "—that's not sloppiness, that's sabotage."

Something moved behind Brandt's eyes. Not suspicion exactly. Assessment. "Quite," he said. "There will be a review. You'll cooperate fully, naturally."

"Naturally," said Astrid, and went back to her pen, and wrote four lines of a form in handwriting she was proud of for its steadiness, and understood that she had just testified against herself with a straight face, and that Brandt knew it, and that knowing it gave him no pleasure, which somehow made it worse. Brandt was not a cruel man. He was a careful man in a city that had stopped rewarding anything else.

At noon she walked to the post office, mailed nothing, bought nothing, and took three different routes home, watching windows in the dark glass of shopfronts, watching for the man who watched. Twice she caught it—the same heavyset figure in a fishmonger's oilskin, once behind her on the Zeughaus bridge, once across the market square, reading a newspaper held slightly too high for the wind that day.

They weren't even trying to be subtle anymore. That was the point of it. Surveillance as message: *we see you; we want you to know we see you; we are patient.*

Her flat felt smaller than usual. She lit the stove, put water on, and stood at the window with her back to her own reflection, cataloguing what they could actually prove. Meetings with Bram: observable. The content of those meetings: unknown to them, unless Kessel had heard something specific—and if he had something specific, why hint instead of accuse? Because an accusation required evidence, and evidence required risk, and Kessel, squeezed by the guild over missing stock and doctored time-sheets, wanted leverage more than justice. He wanted Bram to pay him again. The letter to the authorities wasn't a betrayal yet; it was an invoice.

That was survivable. That was even, in its grim way, predictable. What was not survivable was the second thing she had noticed in the memorandum, the thing she kept circling back to like a tongue finding a broken tooth: *persons connected to observatory clockworks.* Plural. Broad. An investigation into *persons* would pull every thread—Bram's papers, Bram's birthplace, Bram's five years of immaculate boyhood—and threads, once pulled, did not stop at the first knot.

They were going to find Birgitta whether or not they found Emil. And when they found Birgitta, they would ask how a girl became a journeyman apprentice without anyone noticing, and the answer would be that people had been paid not to notice, and the paying would lead to the promise, and the promise would lead to a northbound coal barge, and the barge would lead to a gallows or a prison hulk or whatever this city did now to traitors when it wanted to remind itself it was serious.

She poured the boiling water over tea leaves gone bitter from sitting too long in the tin, and drank it anyway, standing, and made the decision the way she made all her decisions now: quickly, and with the sensation of stepping off a quay.

Tomorrow she would warn Bram.

*

The warning proved unnecessary, because Bram came to her.

Astrid saw her from the census office window at half past four the next afternoon—a slim figure in a workman's cap crossing the square with the quick, contained stride Astrid had begun to know better than she liked, and behind her, at a distance no honest pedestrian would keep, two men in the gray coats of the maritime police.

Not following her. Flanking her. The way you walk around something you already own and are only waiting to inventory.

Astrid was downstairs in ninety seconds, taking the service door, cutting through the alley behind the chandler's, and came out onto the quay just as Bram reached the mouth of Fish Lane. "Boy," she called, using the word deliberately, loudly, a word with no meaning between them except as cover. "A word about your master's invoice."

Bram turned. Up close her face was terrible—not frightened, which would have been bearable, but arranged, the way a clock face is arranged, everything in its correct place and nothing alive behind it. "Invoice's settled," she said.

"It isn't." Astrid took her arm, proprietorially, the way a creditor takes an arm, and steered her toward the covered fish market, empty now, reeking of brine and ice, its stalls shuttered for the evening. Under the dripping awning she dropped the act and her voice together. "There are men on you. Maritime police, plainclothes, since at least yesterday. There's a memorandum on my supervisor's desk naming 'irregular contact' between me and the observatory. Someone talked."

"I know who talked." Bram pulled her cap lower. Inside the hood her eyes moved constantly, cataloguing exits, counting the shadows at the lane mouths. "Kessel. He came to the workshop Sunday night. Sat at my bench like he owned it. Told me the guild had questions about parts that walked out of stores, and that questions get expensive, and that he'd hate to see a promising lad ruined over a misunderstanding." Her mouth twisted. "I paid him. Again. Escapement, deadbeat pattern, the good brass. And today I find police walking my streets, so either he took the mechanism and sold the secret anyway, or the taking was never the point and he means to bleed me until there's nothing left to bleed."

"You can't keep paying him."

"No." Bram said it flatly, without drama, the way you'd say *no, the tide won't wait.* "I've known that since Sunday. Debts to Kessel don't close. They compound. I told you that when I bought his silence the first time, and you told me I was a fool, and you were right, and here we are." She laughed, short and airless. "You're always right. It's very annoying in a conspirator."

"This isn't a conspiracy," Astrid said. "Conspiracies have plans. We have a barge, a date, and a growing collection of enemies."

"And eleven days." Bram's voice dropped. "Eleven days until the tide. Eleven days until the file corrections Brandt wants on his desk. Do you know what they'll do if they take me inside the tower? They won't need proof of anything. A woman living as a man, alone, with keys to the building that keeps the city's time—they'll write the story themselves and it will be worse than the truth, because they won't need the truth to hang it on me."

The words landed in the cold air between them and neither pretended otherwise. Astrid looked at this person she had unmade and remade in her own knowledge over a single autumn—this careful, stubborn, infuriating craftsman who bound herself every morning into a shape the world would tolerate—and felt the fear rearrange itself into something harder and more useful.

"Then we stop being clever," Astrid said. "Clever is what got us here. Clever is misspellings and bribed apprentices and hoping Kessel stays bought. From tonight we assume we're watched, we assume everything we do is read afterward by someone hostile, and we plan accordingly."

"How?"

"Separately. You go back through the front of the city, slow, buy something, be seen being boring. I leave by the harbor road. We don't meet for three days—let them log the silence, let them start wondering if their source exaggerated." She held Bram's eyes. "Three days. Then the observatory, after the evening verification round, when the duty warden does his first circuit. Ninety minutes, no more."

"And in the meantime?"

"In the meantime," Astrid said, "you finish the clock."

Bram stared at her. "Finish it? It's nearly certified. Every day it runs true is a day the patrols sync to it, a day closer to—"

"Exactly." Astrid heard her own voice come out low and certain and wondered briefly whose it was. "If you stall it, if the accuracy slips before certification, Kessel's story writes itself: the apprentice sabotaged the works, the census taker's meetings suddenly mean something, everything confirms everything. But a flawless clock, certified on schedule, with your name on the guild roll—that makes you untouchable for exactly as long as they need you. Guilds protect their investments. So give them nothing to hold. Be perfect. Be beyond reproach. Be the last person in this city anyone would believe guilty." She paused. "And do the rest of it in ways that leave no parts on the table."

Bram was quiet a long moment. Somewhere out on the water a foghorn spoke, twice, mournful, and the ice in the gutters ticked as it thawed and froze. "You understand," Bram said finally, "that what you're describing is us finishing the machine that will hunt him."

"I understand that a live conspirator helps him and a jailed one doesn't." Astrid pulled her collar up against the damp. "We keep him alive first and free second and innocent third. In that order. That's the whole strategy now."

"That's a horrible strategy."

"It's the only one that survives contact with Kessel."

Something moved at the corner of Bram's mouth—not a smile, nothing so warm, but the ghost of the place where smiles lived. "Alliance of liars," she said.

"Worse," Astrid said. "An alliance of professionals."

*

For three days Astrid was a monument to tedium.

She filed forms. She ate lunch at her desk. She accepted Brandt's second cup of coffee with the gratitude of a woman with nothing to hide, and answered his questions about her workload with the numbing completeness of a witness who has rehearsed, and watched him decide, incrementally, hour by hour, that Lindqvist was exactly what she appeared: a competent, colorless civil servant with a dull conscience and no imagination. It was the finest performance of her life and it consisted almost entirely of being less interesting than she was.

On the second day the fishmonger in the oilskin coat appeared outside the bureau at noon and did not appear on the third. On the third day, instead, a new man—taller, younger, bad at pretending to read—marked her route from the office to her flat and then, crucially, stopped at the corner rather than following her all the way home. They were rationing their attention. Spreading thin. Which meant either that they had found nothing worth deepening, or that they were watching more than one thing and could not watch everything well.

She thought about that all evening. More than one thing. The observatory.

*

She broke her own three-day rule on the fourth night, and she did it knowingly, and she hated herself for the relief she felt when she saw the tower lights burning above the fog line.

The duty warden's circuit had come and gone. She climbed the worn stair inside the observatory wall, past the library of star charts, past the case where the old transit instruments slept under dust cloaks like furniture in a closed house, and pushed open the door to the clock chamber.

Bram was not at the master clock.

Bram was standing at the window, motionless, lamp unlit, and the room was lit instead by the great pale disc of the moon coming apart above the fog, and by the light of the city below—which was wrong. Astrid had walked these streets for thirty years and she knew the distribution of its darkness the way she knew her own hands, and tonight the darkness had holes in it. Lanterns where lanterns didn't belong. Two of them, stationary, at the foot of the observatory hill. One more, moving slowly along the harbor road, in the rhythm of a patrol that was patrolling nothing.

"They've started on the tower," Bram said, without turning. "Since Tuesday. Two men at the gate at night, one on the road. Tonight there's a boat holding station off the breakwater with a shaded lantern—look for the flicker when the hull rolls. They're not watching for smugglers." Now she turned, and in the moonlight her face had gone calm in the way that Astrid had learned to dread, the calm of someone who has finished calculating and is living downstream of the sum. "They're watching the building that keeps official time, the week it gets certified, and nobody at the guild ordered it. This comes from the military governor's office. Kessel didn't sell them a suspicion. He sold them a story, and the story was good enough to spend soldiers on."

"Then certification—"

"Happens Friday, as scheduled. The council won't postpone; postponing admits doubt, and doubt costs the guild its contract." Bram crossed to the master clock and rested one hand, flat, against its case, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal you loved and intended to deceive. "Which gives us two nights. After Friday, every whistle, every patrol rotation, every troop column in this city moves to the beat of that escapement. Whatever we're going to do for Emil, we do it before this machine becomes the law, or we do it by breaking the law the machine enforces." The hand slid down the wood and closed, briefly, into a fist. "I've been introducing errors. Small ones. A minute a day, drifting. No inspector catches a drift that size—it reads as temperature, humidity, the moon's fault. By Thursday midnight the clock will be four minutes slow and falling further, and every patrol synced to it will be four minutes late to everywhere, and four minutes is a wide door if you run through it sideways."

Astrid felt the plan assemble itself in her chest, terrible and complete. "And if they catch you inside the works?"

"Then I'm an apprentice tampering with his own masterpiece," Bram said, "and the guild ruins me, and the magistrate asks why, and the answers start with a name I buried five years ago." She met Astrid's eyes across the ticking dark. "So it cannot be me they catch. Whatever happens Friday night, whoever stands in this room when the clock stops—it can't be me. I'm too visible now. They know my face, my name, my debts."

"Then who—"

The question died, because they both heard it at the same time: boots on the stair below. Unhurried. Heavy. Two sets, ascending, with the confidence of men who had already decided what they would find.

Bram's hand found hers in the dark and gripped it once, hard, and let go.

"The warden's closet," Bram breathed. "Behind the chart cabinet. Ninety seconds, no more—he checks it every circuit, he'll check it again in twenty minutes, but he hasn't yet."

The boots reached the landing. The door handle began to turn.

Astrid stepped backward into the dark behind the cabinet as the lamp flared, and through the gap she watched two maritime policemen fill the doorway, and watched Bram Montrose turn to face them with the easy, bored expression of a craftsman interrupted at work—and heard the first of them say, pleasantly, terribly:

"Don't mind us, lad. New orders. From tonight, somebody sits with the clock. All night. Every night."

He smiled, and set his lantern down on Bram's bench, among the scattered tools, like a man moving into a house.

"We hope that's not an inconvenience."
