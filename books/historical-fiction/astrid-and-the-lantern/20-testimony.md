## 20. Testimony

The inquiry was held in the guild hall on Ritterstrasse, in a room designed to make small men feel judged and large men feel watched. Astrid had been in it once before, as a child, when her father received a commendation for his surveying work. She remembered oak paneling and the smell of wax, and her mother's hand tight on her shoulder, and the sense that history happened here to other people.

Now she walked in carrying a folder that could end her career, and it felt less like history than like weather. Cold. Coming whether she wanted it or not.

The council sat seven strong behind a long table draped in green baize. Master Hessler occupied the center chair, his hands folded, his face arranged into something older than grief. To his left sat the guild's legal assessor, a dry man named Vogel with spectacles that caught the lamplight and returned nothing. Two seats down, Kessel's uncle—a guild elder with a jaw like a drawer front—sat with the particular stillness of a man guarding a family name.

Kessel stood at the rail below the bench, one arm in a sling from the night at the observatory. He had given his statement the day before: that he had discovered Bram Montrose tampering with the master clock, that he had tried to stop her, that she had struck him with a wrench and fled into the fog. A clean story. Almost too clean. He'd had three days in custody to sand its edges.

Bram stood at the opposite rail. Her left eye was still swollen, her lip split, her knuckles scabbed where they'd been dragged across cobblestones. She stood the way she stood over a movement in pieces—still, attentive, hands quiet at her sides as if they might otherwise start adjusting things.

She did not look at Astrid.

That was the first mercy. Or the first discipline. With Bram Montrose it was impossible to tell the difference, and Astrid was beginning to suspect there wasn't one.

"Fräulein Lindqvist." The assessor, Vogel, consulted a card. "You are employed by the census bureau of this city."

"I am."

"You were present at the observatory on the night of the fourteenth."

"I was."

"In what capacity?"

"The official time of the city is now governed by the master clock," Astrid said. "Curfew enforcement, patrol rotation, train departures—all keyed to its striking. My bureau has responsibility for verifying the population records against which enforcement actions are conducted. I came to observe the synchronization protocols. It was within my duties."

It was true. It was also, word by word, a wall built brick by brick around everything else that was true. She had learned in four years at the bureau that a lie told plainly gets caught, but a truth told narrowly can hold up a cathedral.

Vogel turned a page. "You arrived at what hour?"

"By the clock in the tower lobby, eleven minutes before midnight. By my own watch—" she took it out, set it on the rail so all could see, an apprentice clockmaker's gesture she'd stolen shamelessly from watching Bram do it, "—eleven minutes and forty seconds past eleven."

"And what did you find?"

"I found Apprentice Kessel inside the clock tower without authorization. I found him attempting to force the strike-train governor with a cold chisel—the same chisel, I later confirmed, checked out of the Hessler & Sons tool room under his name nine days prior. I found Apprentice Montrose bleeding above him, having evidently interrupted the sabotage."

"You say 'evidently.'"

"I say only what I saw and what the objects said afterward. I am a census taker, Herr Assessor. Objects are my trade. Names, dates, places, numbers that agree or don't. On the night of the fourteenth, the objects agreed with Apprentice Montrose's account and disagreed with Apprentice Kessel's."

"Which objects?"

Astrid opened her folder. She had rehearsed this the way Bram must have rehearsed a difficult repair—every step forward planned, every tool in its place.

"First: the chisel itself. It bears a fresh notch matching the mark found on the governor's retaining screw. Apprentice Montrose has no access to the Hessler tool room—her privileges were suspended pending certification, and the log confirms no withdrawal under her name or number in six months. Second: the log itself. Kessel signed out the chisel on the third, stating purpose 'observatory maintenance.' No maintenance order for the observatory existed on that date. I checked. The bureau keeps copies of all municipal work orders. Third—" and here she let the pause sit, because she had learned that too from Bram, who could make silence do the work of a hammer, "—Apprentice Kessel's statement claims he confronted Montrose at approximately half past eleven. Patrol records show the watchman Brandt passed the tower at half past eleven and heard two voices raised in argument. If Kessel surprised a saboteur, why were they arguing rather than struggling? And why did the argument precede, not follow, the damage?"

She looked up. "Fourth. This."

She laid a sheet of paper on the rail. It was a copy—she had made it herself, in the bureau's back room, in the hours after the inquiry was announced, using the copying press and the same steady hand that had once copied Emil's file against every rule she owned.

"It is a letter, in Apprentice Kessel's hand, dated the ninth of this month. It reads: *You will leave the east stair door unbarred on the nights I name, and you will say nothing of what I have seen. If you do not, the guild will learn what you are.*"

The room changed temperature. Astrid felt it the way you feel a draft from a door opening somewhere far off.

"That letter," Vogel said carefully, "was addressed to whom?"

"To Apprentice Montrose. It was found—I found it—tucked into the casing of the regulator clock in her lodgings. I cannot say how it came there. I can say that the hand matches entries in the Hessler & Sons order book, which I invite the council to compare."

She had not found it tucked anywhere. Bram had handed it to her two days ago across the scarred table in the workshop, saying only, "He left it when he thought I wasn't looking. I kept it because keeping things is what I do best." Whether Kessel had actually written it as extortion, or had written it knowing it would surface someday as leverage either way, Astrid couldn't be sure. It didn't matter. Truth used selectively was still true. That was the lesson of this whole terrible season, and she intended to pass the examination.

Master Hessler leaned forward. He had not spoken yet. When he did, his voice was gravel over deep water.

"Apprentice Montrose. You heard the letter read. Is it yours? Were you its recipient?"

"Yes, Master."

"And what did Kessel claim to have seen? What were you, that the letter threatened to reveal?"

Astrid's heart went once, hard, like a single bell stroke.

Bram looked at Hessler. Then—at last, deliberately, with the composure of someone setting a balance wheel—she looked at Astrid. And something passed between them that had no words in any language Astrid spoke fluently, though she was learning it: *I'll carry this part. You carried the rest.*

"There are things in my past," Bram said, "that I have kept private because they are mine. Kessel learned one of them and priced it. What it was doesn't bear on the sabotage. He didn't want the guild to know what I am—he wanted me to open doors. When I stopped opening them, he decided to destroy the thing I'd built instead of exposing the thing I'd hidden. Sabotage was faster than blackmail."

"A fair reading," Vogel murmured, "if the letter supports it. But the council should know that other questions exist regarding Apprentice Montrose. Questions raised in certain quarters." His spectacles flashed toward the elder with the drawer-front jaw. "Questions concerning a census record. Concerning a name."

There it was. Not the letter—something older, colder. Astrid had known it would come; Kessel had talked to more people than the authorities. She had spent two sleepless nights deciding what she would do if it surfaced, and she had arrived at the only answer that seemed honest to her: she would not lie. She would simply refuse to volunteer.

"The name Birgitta," Vogel continued, "appears in the birth registers of Vellinge parish, eighteen-ninety-one. The surname is Montrose. There is also, in the same register, a baptismal entry noting the sex of the child. Fräulein Lindqvist—you are a census taker. You verify identities. Did you, in the course of your duties, form knowledge of Apprentice Montrose's origins?"

Every face turned to her. Kessel's mouth curved very slightly, the way a man smiles when he thinks the trap has already sprung and all that remains is to watch.

Astrid folded her hands.

"I verified the identity *Bram Montrose*," she said. "Apprentice of Hessler & Sons, resident of Wexler Lane since nineteen-oh-eight, certified competent in escapement theory, author of the finest piece of horological work this city has seen in thirty years. That identity exists. I have examined its documents myself. As for the parish of Vellinge—" she shrugged, a small, bureaucratic shrug, the shrug of ten thousand filed forms, "—the census records what people declare. People declare many names. Widows remarry. Emigrants adopt new spellings. Men take their mothers' names. The bureau does not interrogate the soul; it counts the household. If the council wishes to investigate the birth records of every journeyman in this hall, I will happily begin drawing up the forms. There are four hundred and twelve members. It takes about six weeks each."

Someone near the back made a sound that might have suppressed laughter. The elder's jaw tightened.

Hessler raised one hand and the room went still.

"Enough," he said. "I will tell the council what I know, since we are trading in truths tonight." He rose, and Astrid saw that he was steadier than she had ever seen him, the way old men sometimes are exactly at the moment everything falls. "I took Bram Montrose as an apprentice five years ago on the strength of her hands and her head. Since then she has repaired two hundred movements, trained two juniors, and built—single-handed, over fourteen months—the master clock that now governs this city's law and commerce. Yesterday I certified her as a journeyman. Today the council proposes to unmake her over a question I have never asked and do not need answered, because I have watched her answer it every morning for five years with her work." He let his gaze travel the bench. "We are a guild of craftsmen, gentlemen, not a confessional. The clock is true. The hands are hers. Find another sin if you need one—but understand that whoever unmakes this clockmaker must next explain to this city, which depends upon that clock, who will keep it true in her place."

Silence. Somewhere in the building a corridor clock struck the quarter-hour, thin and officious, and nobody moved until it finished.

Vogel spoke quietly with the elder. Kessel stared at the floor with the expression of a man watching his own footing crumble. Then the assessor turned back to the room.

"The council finds as follows." His voice had gone dry and procedural, which Astrid recognized as mercy wearing a clerk's coat. "One: the evidence establishes that Apprentice Kessel entered the observatory without authorization and damaged the strike mechanism, intending to implicate Apprentice Montrose. Two: the extortion letter corroborates motive. Three: the testimony of Fräulein Lindqvist and Watchman Brandt is accepted. Kessel is expelled from the guild, his papers revoked, and the matter referred to the magistrate for the assault upon Watchman Brandt's person during his apprehension." A pause. "Four: the question of Apprentice Montrose's origins is closed. The guild recognizes Journeyman B. Montrose, keeper of the master clock, and considers the matter—not asked, not answered—settled."

Not asked, not answered. Survival without absolution. Astrid wrote the phrase in her mind the way she once recorded vital statistics, and knew she would keep it longer than most records.

The gavel fell. The room emptied in the slow, rustling way rooms empty after verdicts, everyone wanting to look at no one. Kessel was led out between two watchmen; at the door he turned and looked at Astrid, and there was nothing in his face now but arithmetic—he had counted his losses and found her among them, filed her away for a day when debts might be called in again. She met his eyes steadily and did not blink. Let him file it. She had stopped being afraid of men who kept ledgers. She lived with one, and that one kept hers in brass and steel, and it ran true.

Outside the guild hall, the fog had come in off the harbor, thick and smelling of coal. Bram stood on the steps with her journeyman's papers in her coat pocket and her swollen eye turned toward the water, and for a moment neither woman spoke.

"He gave them your name anyway," Astrid said finally. "Vogel. The Vellinge register. Someone fed it to him before Kessel ever went to the magistrate."

"I know."

"You're not angry."

Bram considered the question with the seriousness she brought to everything, even now, even bleeding and half-ruined and publicly spared.

"I'm tired," she said. "And I'm—" she searched for it, hands moving slightly, shaping the word like a part that wouldn't fit, "—grateful, maybe. That it's done being a secret even if it isn't done being a lie. There's a difference. I've been living in the space between them for five years, and the space was getting narrow." She looked at Astrid then, fully, and the bruised eye and the split lip and all of it were somehow beside the point. "You stood in front of seven men and defended a fiction you didn't believe in."

"I defended a person I believe in. The fiction just happened to be attached."

Something moved through Bram's face—too quick to name, gone before it landed. "Emil's barge cleared the lightship this morning. The captain signaled it. He's north."

Astrid closed her eyes. For one breath she let herself feel it—not triumph, nothing so clean, but the specific relief of a debt paid in full, the kind you feel in the knees. Her brother was free. Her career was a smoking ruin waiting for the bureau to notice. Her parents' respectability had been spent like cheap coin. And standing in front of her, fog-damp and stubborn and alive, was the reason none of it, somehow, felt like loss.

"The bureau will call me Monday," she said. "Supervisor Brenner already flagged my file discrepancies. They'll ask me to explain Emil's registry delays, my presence at the observatory, my testimony today. I'll lose my post."

"Yes."

"You could sound sorrier."

"I'm the sorriest person in this city," Bram said. "I'm just bad at showing it. Clockmakers learn to keep their faces level—it's the hands that shake." And indeed, as Astrid watched, Bram's right hand trembled once, finely, and was stilled by sheer will, pressed flat against her thigh.

"What happens Monday?" Astrid asked. "When I have nothing? No office, no name worth anything, no—"

"You'll have the workshop on Wexler Lane," Bram said. "There's a second stool behind the bench. It's been there since September. I bought it in September, Astrid. Before any of this. I want you to know that."

Astrid stared at her. The fog drifted between them and past them, and somewhere down by the harbor a ship's bell rang twice, muffled and patient, marking a tide neither of them controlled.

"You bought a stool," she said.

"I plan ahead. It's a professional habit."

And despite everything—the inquiry, the ruined files, the brother on a coal barge, the whole precarious architecture of lies they'd built their lives inside—Astrid laughed. It came out cracked and startled, the first real laugh since the night the clock stopped, and Bram smiled with her ruined lip like it was the finest mechanism she'd ever produced.

"Monday, then," Astrid said.

"Monday," Bram agreed. And then, quieter, turning back toward the fog and the water and the invisible tower where the great clock waited, silent now for five days, its strike-train still jammed, its heart still stopped: "But tomorrow I go back up into the works. It's kept the city waiting long enough—and I've kept a lie going long enough too. Time both of them started telling the truth."

She pulled her collar up against the fog and started down the steps toward Wexler Lane, and Astrid stood a moment longer on the guild hall steps, holding her empty folder, listening to the muffled bells of a city that no longer knew what hour it was—and understanding, with a clarity that felt almost like joy, that she was about to find out.
