## 21. The Repair

The clock came apart in the order it had gone together, which was the only mercy of the work.

I climbed into the tower on a Tuesday morning three weeks after the inquiry, carrying my tools in the same canvas roll I'd carried for six years, and I stood inside the great iron ribcage of the master clock and did not touch anything for a long time. Dust had settled over everything like grey snow. The pendulum hung dead still. The weights sat at the bottom of their shafts like animals that had given up.

Kessel's blood was still on the floor plates near the escapement—someone had scrubbed it, but iron remembers. Or I imagined it remembered. Hard to say, after that night, what was real and what I'd invented to keep myself from thinking about other things: the fog, the whistles, Astrid standing between me and four watchmen with her census ledger held up like a shield she knew was made of paper.

I started with the frame. You always start with the frame. Hessler taught me that when I was fourteen and answering to *boy* and terrified every hour of every day that someone would notice my hands were wrong, or my throat, or the way I flinched when a customer clapped me on the shoulder. *Start with the frame,* he said, *because if the frame is true, everything you build on it has a chance.*

The frame was true. The frame had always been true. It was the hands that had lied—mine first, then the clock's.

* * *

It took eleven days to strip her down.

I worked alone—the guild had seen to that, quietly, without ever saying why. Kessel's arrest had left an apprenticeship vacant and an atmosphere poisoned; the younger men looked at me now with something between awe and suspicion, and Master Hessler had solved it by giving me the tower and telling everyone else to stay out of it. *You broke her,* he said, which was not true but was close enough to true, and then, softer, in the doorway with his back half turned: *You're the only one who can make her honest again. Don't come down until she is.*

So I lived up there, more or less. I slept in the keeper's room under the dome, on a cot that smelled of lamp oil and cold stone, and I woke before dawn to the sound of gulls arguing over the harbor, and I took the movement apart piece by piece and laid each piece out on felt in rows so precise that a stranger could have read the clock's whole anatomy like a book.

Every gear I cleaned by hand. Every pivot I polished until I could see my own face in it, bent and blurred and unmistakably a woman's face, and I let myself look at it this time instead of turning away. There was no one up there to see. There hadn't needed to be anyone for years—but there was no one now, and the difference between those two things was the whole shape of my life since the inquiry.

The guild knew. That was the part I kept expecting to hurt worse than it did. They knew, because Astrid's testimony had dragged it into the light whether anyone wanted it there or not, and the council had met behind closed doors for two days and emerged with a document so dry it practically crackled: the sabotage was attributed wholly to Kessel, the journeyman's papers were confirmed, and one line—*the guild recognizes its obligation to its certified craftsmen regardless of circumstance*—that everyone understood to mean *we know what you are and we have decided not to care, because you are the only person alive who can keep this clock.*

Survival without absolution. I'd expected shame to sit on me like a stone. Instead it sat like a coat—one I could take off up here, in the dust and the quiet, whenever I wanted.

The drift I had built into her came out last. That was deliberate. I made myself find every error I'd planted, the lagging strike train, the minute hand that wandered two minutes slow over a week, the pallet stones I'd microscopically re-angled to steal seconds from every swing of the pendulum. Each one had been an act of love, and undoing them felt like unmaking the only good thing I'd done that autumn, and I did it anyway, because a clock that lies is just a lie with better posture.

On the eleventh day I set the escapement true. I beat the pallets to the arc Hessler had drawn forty years ago and no apprentice had dared correct since. I hung the pendulum, oiled the bearings with exactly as much oil as the bearings wanted and not one drop more, and wound the great barrels until my shoulders burned.

Then I let her go.

The tick filled the tower like a heartbeat fills a chest. I stood inside it with my palm flat against the frame and felt the truth run up through the iron and into my arm, steady, indifferent, absolute, and I will not pretend I didn't weep, because I did—once, briefly, the way you bleed from a splinter, more surprise than pain.

She ran true through the afternoon. She ran true through the evening. At midnight she struck twelve, and the twelve notes fell across the harbor clean and evenly spaced, each one arriving exactly when it should, and somewhere below I heard the patrol sergeant outside the customs house check his watch against the bells and find them agreeing, and I thought: *Emil, wherever you are, time is honest here again. Forgive me how much it cost to make it so.*

* * *

The barge captain's answer came nine days later.

He came to the workshop himself—a squat man named Voss with salt-cracked hands and a face like weathered oak, who had taken Emil north among his coal and never once asked me why. He set a scrap of paper on my bench without ceremony, next to the broken pocket watch I'd been rebuilding in stolen evenings.

"Neutral waters," he said. "Got him to a Danish tramp steamer off Gedser. He gave me this for the girl who keeps the clocks."

*For the girl.* Voss's eyes flicked once to my face, taking in what the inquiry had made public, and there was nothing in his expression but the flat courtesy of a man who hauls cargo for a living and has hauled stranger things than secrets.

The paper held five words in Emil's blunt handwriting, the pencil pressed hard enough to tear:

*Birgitta. The watch was right.*

I read it maybe forty times. I want to be honest about that, since honesty is supposedly the theme of this chapter of my life. Forty times, and each time the last three words landed differently—*the watch was right,* meaning the promise we'd sworn on it, meaning the broken mechanism we'd sworn it on, meaning that a thing can stop dead and still be true.

"You'll carry another message?" I said.

"I carry coal," Voss said. "Messages fall into the coal sometimes." He almost smiled. "What do you want it to say?"

I thought about it while he waited, thumbs hooked in his belt, patient as tide.

"Tell him," I said finally, "that the clock is repaired. All of it."

Voss nodded like that meant exactly what it meant, and left.

* * *

Astrid came to the observatory on the last Friday of the month, which was not arranged and was entirely arranged, the way our meetings had become—unwritten appointments kept by two women who had learned that putting things in writing was how you got people arrested.

I heard her boots on the tower stairs long before I saw her. She climbed the way she did everything, briskly, as though the stairs might reconsider and try to make the climb harder if she showed hesitation. She came up into the lamplight with her hair coming loose from its pins and frost still on her collar, and she stopped when she saw me standing beside the running clock, and something in her face eased that I don't think she knew was there.

"Well?" she said.

"She runs true. Nine days. Not a second lost. I've checked her against the telegraph time twice a day."

"And?"

"And nothing. She's just—" I put my hand flat on the case. "She's just a clock again. That's all I ever wanted her to be."

Astrid walked a slow circle around the movement, hands clasped behind her back like an inspector, though she'd resigned from the bureau ten days earlier and had no authority to inspect anything except, perhaps, me. She'd handed in her resignation the same week her supervisor's letter arrived demanding corrections to Emil's file—arrived, I should say, addressed to a woman who had already decided the file could rot. She told me about it afterward, flatly, the way she told most things: *They wanted me to choose. So I chose. It turns out choosing is much easier than deciding to choose.*

Now she completed her circuit and stopped in front of me.

"Twelve bells tonight," she said. "First night they've been right since October."

"I know. I built them that way."

"I know you did." A pause. She looked at the pendulum swinging its long slow arc, silver in the lamplight, and when she spoke again her voice had dropped into the register she saved for things that mattered, low and stripped of all her bureaucratic armor. "Bram. Birgitta. Whichever of you is listening—I'm glad it was you in that tower. When it all went wrong. Of all the people in this city, I'm glad it was you."

I didn't have an answer for that. I never had answers for her; I had hands, and hands were what I offered, and she took them—my palms, still rough with emery dust—and held them the way you hold something you've decided to keep.

"Midnight," she said. "Stay up here with me. I want to hear them fall correctly. I've been hearing them wrong for months and pretending I couldn't tell."

"You could always tell. You noticed a two-minute drift off a pocket watch."

"Noticed it," she said, "and hated it, and couldn't fix it, and had to live in a city that ran on it. Do you understand what that's been like? Every bell since October has been a small lie told to everyone I know. Tonight I'd like the truth, loudly, please."

So we stayed.

We spread my coat on the floor plates beneath the clock face, where the numerals glowed faintly through the glass from the harbor lights below, and we sat shoulder to shoulder with our backs against the warm iron of the frame—warm, because she was running now, working now, alive—and Astrid talked, which she did when she was nervous, about the school where she'd taken a post teaching arithmetic to children who mostly wanted to know if she'd really helped catch a saboteur, and about the landlady who had stopped pretending not to notice whose rooms she visited, and about spring, which was coming, the ice going rotten out in the roads, the first ships already queuing for the eastern channel.

And I listened, and watched the minute hand climb toward the top of the dial through the little inspection window, and felt the strangest thing settle over me—not peace exactly, peace was too soft a word—it was more like alignment. Like a train of gears, after months of interference, finding the ratio it was always meant to run at.

Eleven fifty-eight. Astrid fell silent too. We both heard it, the way you hear a held breath.

Eleven fifty-nine. My heart was doing something unschooled and unmechanical.

And then midnight came, and the master clock struck twelve, and every note fell where it belonged—on the second, in its turn, at its proper weight—and the sound rolled down over the fog-thinned harbor, over the customs sheds and the coal yards and the sleeping streets, and out along the breakwater where the patrol boats ran their new synchronized routes off a time that was, for the first time in months, simply, plainly, true.

Astrid's hand found mine in the dark and gripped it.

"There," she said, barely above the fading hum of the bells. "That's what honesty sounds like."

"That's what it sounds like when it costs enough," I said.

She laughed—short, surprised, real—and leaned her head against my shoulder, and above us the great clock ticked on into the first minute of the first honest day, keeping time for a city that would never know what the keeping had cost, and below us the lights of the harbor burned steady toward the north, where a ship had carried my oldest promise safely out of my hands at last.

The bells faded. The silence afterward wasn't empty. It was full—full of tick and breath and the small warmth of her shoulder against mine—and I understood, sitting there in the iron dark, that the repair wasn't finished yet.

The clock was done.

We were just beginning.
