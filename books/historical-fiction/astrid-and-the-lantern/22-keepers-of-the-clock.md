## 22. Keepers of the Clock

**Astrid**

The schoolhouse on Västra Lane had eleven desks and a stove that smoked when the wind came off the harbor from the east. Astrid learned this on her first morning in April, when she opened the windows to let out the smell of chalk and coal smoke and watched the fog roll in like something that had followed her home.

She taught arithmetic to children who would never need more than counting coin, and geography to children who might one day see the places she pointed at on the map, and penmanship to everyone, because neat hands opened doors that faces could not. She did not teach them about registries. She did not teach them how a name entered in a ledger could become a rope around a man's neck, or how three weeks could be stretched into a lifetime by nothing more than a clerk's careful error and another clerk's willful blindness.

Some lessons you carried instead of giving away.

The bureau had let her go without scandal. Herr Vogel, her supervisor, had shaken her hand at the door and said, "The city thanks you for your service," in the flat tone of a man who knew exactly what she'd done and had decided it was cheaper to forget than to investigate. He'd retired himself two months later. She heard he kept bees now, out past the orchards. She hoped they stung him.

What surprised her was how little she missed it. She had expected the work to live in her like a splinter—the ledgers, the lists, the small power of the stamp. But the work had never been the point. The point had been a brother's name on a page, and a promise she'd inherited from a drunken summer five years gone, and a clockmaker who looked at her across an observatory full of ticking brass and said, *You don't have to help me. But I'm going to do it anyway.*

Emil's first letter had come in February, forwarded through three hands from a shipping office in a port she wasn't supposed to know. Four sentences. He wrote the way he talked, all momentum and no punctuation. *I am well work is steady the water here is warmer than ours tell Bram the watch still runs I wound it every day like she said.* Astrid had read it four times at the kitchen table while Bram pretended not to watch from the workshop doorway, and then she'd read it aloud, and Bram had turned back to her bench very quickly, and neither of them had said anything for a long time, and the clocks had filled the silence the way clocks do.

Now it was May. The lilacs along the schoolyard wall were opening, and the children smelled of soap and grass, and when the bell rang at noon—a small hand bell, nothing grand, no gears at all—Astrid stood in the doorway and watched them scatter and thought: *this too is a kind of keeping.*

She walked home the long way, past the harbor, past the customs shed where Emil had once slept rough among the rope coils with his collar up and his faith intact. The shed had been repainted. A boy was fishing off the quay with the patience of someone who had never had to run.

At the corner of Hessler & Sons, she stopped, as she always stopped, and looked up at the sign.

It said what it had always said. But underneath, on the door, in letters Bram had painted herself last autumn, in a hand so steady it looked typeset:

*MONTROSE — CLOCKMAKER. JOURNEYMAN OF THE GUILD.*

Her name. Not Hessler's. Not a master's charity. Her own name, on her own door, and the guild seal beside it, and not one soul in the free city who remembered—or admitted remembering—that the journeyman Montrose had once been anyone else.

Astrid pushed open the door. Inside, forty clocks were striking the quarter hour, staggered like laughter.

**Bram**

There was a particular sound a workshop makes when it is honest.

Bram had spent years listening to workshops lie. The tick of a clock that had been hurried, a movement assembled fast and dirty to meet a deadline—that tick has a rasp under it, a hairline fracture in the sound, and she had trained herself to hear it the way other people trained themselves to hear footsteps on stairs at night. Every clock in Hessler & Sons had lied a little, because speed was money and money was survival and nobody upstairs cared whether the third wheel ran true so long as it ran.

But this shop was hers. And she had rebuilt every movement in it with her own hands, slowly, through the winter, while the frost made patterns on the glass and Astrid corrected exercise books at the table by the window and complained—gently, endlessly, wonderfully—about the noise.

"You could oil them," Astrid said, without looking up.

"I did oil them."

"Then you could oil them quieter."

"They're clocks, Astrid. Quiet clocks are dead clocks."

Astrid's pen paused. Bram caught the look that crossed her face—not grief exactly, more like the shape grief leaves behind—and understood, the way she now understood most things about Astrid Lindqvist, that they were both thinking of the same midnight: the whole city holding its breath around a silence where twelve bells should have been.

That silence had cost Bram blood, Kessel's arrest, a guild inquiry, and the last shred of the person she'd been before. It had bought Emil a northbound barge and a life. Some trades you only make once.

The guild had been its own strange mercy. Master Hessler—old, shrewd, dying by inches but refusing to announce it—had stood before the council after Astrid's testimony and said, in his gravel voice, "Gentlemen, the man who built our master clock is the finest repairer this city has produced in thirty years. If he was born a woman, then I say we have been educating women badly and should amend the practice." A terrible speech, by any honest measure. It had worked completely. The fiction was accepted, then absorbed, then forgotten, the way institutions forget everything that embarrasses them. By spring, apprentices who had known her for years spoke of "Montrose" as if the name had always been there, plain as brass.

And it *was* hers now. That was the thing no one had warned her about. Birgitta had been a girl in a house full of disapproval; Bram was a clockmaker with ink-stained fingers and a door with her name on it. She hadn't shed one and put on the other. She'd built a third thing, out of both, and it fit better than either.

Today the bench was clear except for one object, wrapped in flannel.

She'd worked on Emil's pocket watch all winter, an hour at a time, the way you visit a grave or write a letter you may never send. The watch was a ruin—a cracked crystal, a mainspring snapped clean through, the balance staff bent from some fall it had taken years ago, before any of this, back when it was only a broken thing two young fools swore on at a regatta. She had machined a new staff by hand, smaller than a grass seed. She had fitted a new spring and let it down gently, gently, the way you wake a sleeper you don't want to frighten.

Last night, at the bench, alone, she had set it going.

It ran. Of course it ran. She had made sure of it with the same stubbornness she'd once used to stop a master clock, and there was a symmetry in that which she suspected was the closest thing to prayer she'd ever manage.

The door opened. Astrid came in smelling of chalk and lilac, hung her coat on the peg that had become hers without either of them discussing it, and saw the flannel bundle.

"Is it finished?"

"It's finished."

Astrid unwrapped it with the care of someone handling a bird. The case was dull silver, dented at the edge where Emil's thumb had worried it through a hundred conversations. The dial was yellowed. The second hand moved in small, certain steps.

"He asked me to keep it running," Bram said. "In the letter. He said—" She stopped, started again. "He wound it every day, over there. Like it was a duty. Like it was me."

Astrid held it to her ear. Then she held it out, and Bram took it, and for a moment they stood there in the middle of the shop with forty clocks talking around them and one small watch saying the truest thing in the room.

"There's a nail," Astrid said, "above the bench. I noticed it months ago and thought, that's odd, a bare nail in a shop where everything hangs."

Bram looked up. The nail was exactly where it had always been. She had put it there herself, in the first week, and left it bare, and told herself it was for a tool, and known perfectly well it wasn't.

"Hang it," she said.

* * *

**Astrid**

She reached up on her toes—Bram could have done it flat-footed, and they both knew it, and neither of them mentioned it—and set the pocket watch on the nail above the bench, where the light from the window fell across it all day.

From below, the watch looked small and stubborn. A broken thing repaired, hanging in the place where a tool should go, keeping time for no purpose except that someone had promised it would.

"There," Astrid said. Her voice came out less steady than she intended. "Now the shop has a heart."

"The shop has forty hearts."

"One that matters."

Bram didn't answer, but she stood beside Astrid and looked up at it for a long moment, and Astrid watched her profile—the jaw she had once mistaken for a young man's, the scar at the hairline from the night in the tower, the eyes that had learned, slowly and against their nature, to be seen.

Astrid thought about the census form she had filled out five years ago, in a fog-bound spring much like this one. *Place of birth,* the question had demanded, and a young man named Bram Montrose had given an answer with no safe truth in it, and she had written down what he said and slept badly for a week. That was the whole beginning. A question, a lie, a clerk's sleepless conscience. People imagined that lives turned on great events—declarations, departures, midnights when clocks stopped. But hers had turned on a line in a ledger and the decision, made half-asleep at her own kitchen table, not to correct it.

She had lost a career over that decision. She had gained a classroom, a stove that smoked, a coat peg, a place at a table in a shop full of ticking.

No honest accountant could call it a fair trade. Astrid had never been much good at dishonest accounting.

"Vogel sent a letter," she said. "To the school. Asking if I'd consider returning. There's a senior post opening. Registry supervision."

Bram went very still, the way she went still over a movement that wouldn't behave. "What did you tell him?"

"That I teach children now. That children don't get deported for their handwriting." Astrid untied her apron strings, hung the apron, took down the kettle. "I told him the city's records are in excellent order and always have been."

"Liar."

"Clerk." She set the kettle on the ring. "We're the worst kind."

Bram laughed—one short bark, quickly smothered, as if the shop might report her for unseemly noise—and went back to the bench, and the afternoon settled around them the way afternoons had learned to settle here: Astrid marking exercises at the window table, Bram's file whispering against brass, the watch above them ticking its small faithful count toward nothing and everything.

* * *

**Bram**

Toward evening, Bram climbed the observatory hill.

She did this sometimes now, though her official duties required it less each month. The master clock ran true—she had rebuilt it honestly in the winter, part by part, checking every tooth, every pallet stone, letting it find its own voice again after the violence she'd done it. The city synchronized to it without thinking. Curfews had softened as the war fears softened; the patrols thinned; the harbor filled with ships from ports that were merely foreign now instead of enemy. Time had become time again, ordinary and shared, and only Bram knew how close it had come to being a weapon, and what it had cost to make it innocent twice.

The keeper on duty nodded her through. She climbed the tower stairs, opened the case, and stood in the dark listening to the escapement breathe.

Tick. Tick. Tick.

Five years ago she had promised a drunk, laughing Emil Lindqvist that she would move the hands of the city for him if he ever needed leaving. She had kept it. Not the way promises are supposed to be kept—not cleanly, not without blood—but kept, and she had learned that this was the only kind of keeping there was. You kept a promise the way you kept a shop: daily, imperfectly, with your hands, whether or not anyone watched.

You kept a person the same way. Astrid had stayed. Not because she was swept anywhere, not because of any grand declaration—neither of them had ever managed one—but because staying, chosen again each morning over tea and chalk dust and the small friction of two solitary people sharing a roof, was its own vow, renewed without ceremony, and neither of them needed it said aloud. Though lately Bram had begun to think that maybe, someday, she might say it anyway. Just to see Astrid's face.

On the walk home, the lamps were being lit, and the fog was coming in soft off the water, and somewhere north a man she loved like a brother was winding a watch in a warm room in a country that would take him.

At the shop door she paused, as she always paused, and looked up.

Through the window: lamplight, a woman at a table with a stack of exercise books, forty clocks, and above the bench a small dull-silver watch turning its second hand through the dark, patient as a promise, keeping time until morning.

Bram unlocked the door and went in.

* * *

**Astrid**

Later—much later, past midnight, when the shop had gone quiet enough that Astrid could hear the single note of the watch above the bench cutting clear through all the rest—she found the letter.

It had come with the evening post and Bram had left it propped against the kettle where Astrid would see it, which was Bram's way of saying everything she couldn't say out loud. The envelope bore a foreign postmark and a shipping office's forwarding stamp, and inside was a single sheet in Emil's slashing hand.

*I have saved passage money. In autumn I can afford to bring Mother out. Tell Bram: start the clocks. Tell my sister: I will want a good school nearby.*

Astrid read it twice. Then she set it down on the table, next to the cold tea and the red-penned exercise books, and looked across the dim shop at the door of the room where Bram slept—or pretended to sleep, as Astrid had long suspected, listening to the same forty hearts she did.

Autumn. Their mother. A school.

Above the bench, the little watch ticked on, and outside, far off across the rooftops, the observatory began to strike the hour—twelve bells, true and unhurried, falling one by one into the fog like stones dropped into deep water.

Astrid counted them all the way down, and smiled in the dark, and got up to put the kettle on.
