## 1. The Weight of Water

The trick was not to think about the air.

Gideon Eriksen had learned that at fourteen, the first time the Meridian overseer strapped the weighted belt to her hips and pointed at the water and said *down*. Thinking about air made you greedy for it. Greed made you rise too fast, and rising too fast got you a dockside beating and half wages. So she did not think about air. She counted instead.

*One.* The cold closed over her like a hand.

*Two.* Sunlight broke apart above her, scattered coins on the ceiling of the world.

*Three.* She let herself fall.

Roebuck Bay in the wet season was warm as bathwater near the surface and cool as a grave below it, and Gideon dropped through both layers with her feet together and her arms crossed, sinking toward the shell beds forty feet down. The pressure found her ears and squeezed. She worked her jaw, swallowed, felt them pop. Below her the sea floor waited, pale and rippled, littered with shells the color of old ivory and the ghosts of shells — the empty ones, the picked-over ones, the ones the company had already taken everything from.

She turned onto her back at the bottom and put her hands into the sand.

*Four. Five. Six.*

A pearl shell was a living thing, and it clamped shut when you touched it, which meant every one you took had to be pried open down here, in the dark, while your lungs burned and your heartbeat climbed into your throat. She slid the knife under the lip of the first one, felt the animal resist, then yield. She tucked the shell into the net bag at her waist. Another. Another. The bag grew heavy against her hip and the world narrowed to the work: pry, tuck, move. Pry, tuck, move.

*Fifteen. Sixteen.*

Her father had taught her to count without counting — to let the numbers run underneath the work like current under a boat. *You don't fight the water, little fish,* he'd say, his Pidgin softening the English, his hands rough as coral. *You just stay longer than it expects you to.* He could hold his breath four minutes. He used to float face-down in the shallows off Dampier Creek while she sat on the mudbank doing sums in the dirt, and she would look up in a panic because he had been still too long, and he'd surface laughing with a shell already in his fist.

That was before the company's boats came south with their new contracts and their printed terms nobody in the camps could read. Before the deep-water stations. Before the season they called the bad season, though every season after it was bad too.

*Twenty-two. Twenty-three.*

Her chest tightened. She ignored it the way she ignored the ache in her shoulders, the salt stinging the inside of her nose, the faint metallic taste at the back of her tongue that meant she was close to the bottom of what she had. She pried one more shell loose — a big one, seven inches if it was an inch, its mother-of-pearl throat flashing green when it gaped — and kicked for the surface.

The light above swelled. She rose through the layers, warm water, warmer, and broke through into the enormous glare of the morning with a gasp that felt like being born.

"Time," called the tender in the boat above, and hauled up her bag by the rope.

Gideon hung on the gunwale a moment, breathing in great deliberate gulps, letting the counting drain out of her. Around the station lugger, six other divers bobbed at their stations, faces slick and dark against the water. Old Man Tjilpa. The brothers from the north camp. Sefu, who was twenty-three and built like a bollard and who grinned at her now across the swell, showing white teeth, holding up three fingers to say how many shells were in his bag. Three. Good haul.

She held up two. His grin dimmed a fraction — sympathy, not scorn — and he turned back to his own line.

The tender dumped her bag on the deck boards and the opener went to work with his knife, quick and practiced, and everyone on the boat watched the same way they always watched: not hoping for pearls exactly, because hope cost something and none of them had much to spend, but watching anyway, because a pearl meant a bonus and a bonus meant meat that week instead of rice.

No pearl. The opener shrugged and tossed the meat into the basket and threw the shell onto the heap that was growing at the station's edge, mountainous, pale, smelling of low tide forever.

"Back down," said the tender. "Overseer wants full bags by noon."

Gideon put the belt back on.

*One,* said the water, closing over her head.

* * *

The Meridian Trading Company station at Broome was a company town inside a town: barracks roofed with corrugated iron, a store where wages were paid in chits good only at the store, a drying ground where the shells were laid out in rows so vast they looked from the ridge like the ribs of some beached leviathan. At the gate stood a customs officer's post, and beyond the gate stood the road to Broome proper, and between Gideon and that road stood three checkpoints, though she had never once tried to pass them. There was nowhere to go. Her contract ran five years and she was nineteen and the contract was written in a language of clauses, and the only thing she owned outright was a tin trunk containing two dresses, her mother's comb, and a photograph of a man squinting into sunlight on a beach that no longer existed.

She kept her head down. That was the whole of her philosophy, learned early and confirmed often. Keep your head down, do your numbers, don't be first or last in any line, don't ask why the rations shrank in the months when the catches didn't. When the overseer, Mr. Callaghan, walked the barracks rows with his stick tapping against his boot, Gideon became furniture. She mended netting. She was very good at mending netting.

"You there." The stick stopped beside her one evening in March, on the shell heaps' windward side, where the smell was thickest. "Eriksen's girl."

She looked up the correct amount. Not insolent. Not cringing. "Sir."

"You're the deep diver now, are they saying? Since your old man went under?" He said *went under* the way a man says *gone to Perth*. "Deep water pays double rates. You'll do the outer beds Thursday."

"The outer beds have no safety line, sir," she said. It was not a complaint. Complaints were punished. It was information, offered flatly, the way you'd note the weather.

Callaghan smiled with the side of his mouth. "Safety lines slow the boats. You want to eat this quarter or not?"

"Yes, sir."

"That's the spirit." He moved on, stick tapping, and Gideon bent again over her netting, and her hands were steady, and somewhere behind her sternum a small hot coal lay in the ash where she kept it, banked, unattended, fed nothing.

Thursday she dove the outer beds. No line. Just her, the belt, the knife, and forty feet of green water with a current running through it that pulled sideways like a hand on her ankle. She made eleven dives that day. On the ninth she stayed down a few seconds past prudent because a shell of extraordinary size sat in a crevice and bonuses were bonuses, and when she surfaced the boat was ten yards off and drifting, and for one long moment — three heartbeats, maybe four — no one knew where she was, including the sea itself, and she trod water in the open bay feeling the size of it, the sheer indifferent acreage of water between her and everywhere else, and understood with perfect clarity that if she drowned here it would be entered in a book somewhere as a figure. A loss. A rounding error against the season's take.

She swam to the boat. Nobody had noticed. She took her bag and went back down.

This was peace, people told her. This was as good as it got, and wasn't she lucky to have it — contract, rations, a roof. The sisters at the mission said gratitude was a virtue. The store clerk said it when he shorted her chits. Even Sefu, who was the closest thing she had to a friend, said a version of it once, sitting on the shell heaps at dusk with his long legs dangling over the pale slope: *"Better here than the stations up north, Gidi. I've seen the northern ledgers."* He'd gone quiet after that, in the way of men who have said one word more than they meant to.

Ledgers. Everyone kept ledgers. The company kept ledgers, the customs men kept ledgers, the mission kept ledgers of baptisms and deaths. Whole lives reduced to columns. Gideon sometimes thought that when she died, the sum total of her would be one line in one book, and the line would read *diver, female, 19, output adequate*, and someone would rule a pen beneath it and turn the page.

She was wrong about that, though she wouldn't know it for another month, until the night the storm came and the child was left at her door. Wrong about the ledger, wrong about the line, wrong about how loudly a ruled pen can echo.

But that evening she knew nothing yet. That evening she sat on the shell heap with Sefu and ate her ration of salt beef and damper and watched the sun go down over the water her father had died in, and felt — or believed she felt — nothing at all.

* * *

Her father had died on a Tuesday.

It was the bad season, four years ago, and the Meridian luggers were working the deeper grounds past the point because the shallow beds were stripped, and Tomas Eriksen — Gideon's father, though everyone at the station called him Tom and the company books called him *Diver No. 41* — went down on a morning so clear the water looked solid enough to walk on.

He was thirty-nine years old and the best diver on the coast, and everyone said so, including the white skippers who otherwise said very little about black men except what they paid them. Four minutes. Five, on a good day. He'd bring up bags so heavy the tenders groaned lifting them, and he'd surface smiling, always, as if the whole business were a game he'd agreed to play and was winning.

The story the company gave was simple. Cramp. Deep water, cold current, a man alone on the outer beds with no line — the lines slowed the boats — and cramp takes the strongest swimmer. Nothing to be done. Very sad. Wages to the end of the month, less store debts, and here is a receipt for the balance, sign here.

Gideon signed. She was fifteen.

What she remembered, and had never said aloud to anyone, was the boat that day. The *Meridian Rose*, working two hundred yards off, and the way her tender — a boy, sixteen, terrified of the overseer — had hesitated at the rail when Tom's station showed bubbles but no Tom, minute after minute, the bubbles coming slower, then stopping altogether. The boy had looked toward the skipper. The skipper had looked at his watch. And the watch had ticked through ninety seconds — a hundred and twenty — before anyone put a boat over the side, because the rule, unwritten but absolute, was that you did not stop work for a man until stopping work was cheaper than explaining why you hadn't.

They brought him up gray. They laid him in the bow among the shells, which was somehow the worst part, that he traveled home in the hold with the catch, weighed and counted together. Gideon had stood on the jetty when they carried him past, and she had not cried, because Callaghan was standing nearby with his stick, and because crying marked you, marked you as trouble, and trouble in the camps had a way of finding extra shifts and short rations and, once, in the case of a woman named Ada who'd struck a skipper with an oar, a transfer north that no one ever returned from.

So she hadn't cried. She'd signed the receipt. And that night, alone in the barracks room she'd shared with her father, she had taken his spare diving knife from under his bunk and held it and thought about the water and about what it would take to walk into it and keep walking, and then she had put the knife away and eaten the bread he would not need and slept, because grief was a luxury priced beyond her wages.

In the morning she reported for work. The overseer noted her attendance and remarked to the store clerk, within her hearing, that Eriksen's girl had sense — none of the wailing and carrying-on you got from some. A credit to the camp. And Gideon had taken the compliment the way you take a coin you know is counterfeit, and said thank you, and kept her head down, and kept it down for four years.

The coal in her chest never went out. She simply never fed it. That was the arrangement she had made with herself, the treaty, the long truce: *you may burn, but quietly, and you may not light anything else.*

* * *

Now, on this ordinary evening at the end of the pearling season's first quarter, Gideon finished her last dive of the day, climbed aboard, and stood dripping while the opener worked through her bag. Two shells. One small pearl, baroque, worth perhaps ten shillings to a buyer in town and a two-shilling chit to her. The tender recorded it in the station book. Somewhere in a office in Perth, a man she would never meet added her day to a column and was satisfied.

Sefu fell into step beside her on the walk back to the barracks, the road pale with shell dust, the heat finally breaking. He smelled of brine and sweat and the kerosene the company issued for cleaning suits.

"Outer beds Thursday again?" he asked.

"Again."

"I'll trade you my shallow line. My shoulder's bad." He rolled it as proof, wincing theatrically.

"And lose double rates? You're a fool, Sefu."

"I'm a fool with a working shoulder." He glanced at her sidelong. "Gidi. You went down long today. Past counting. I saw the tender watching the water."

"I'm still here."

"I know you're still here. That's not what I asked."

But she didn't answer, and he didn't press, because pressing was a kind of trouble too, and they had all agreed without ever discussing it to leave each other's silences alone. That was the etiquette of the camps. You survived side by side and asked nothing.

At the barracks gate the lamplighter was making his rounds, and the wind had come around from the northwest, and it smelled different than it had that morning — heavier, greener, carrying something from far out over the Timor Sea. Sefu paused and lifted his face to it like a man reading.

"Wet season's coming early," he said. "Big one, maybe. You feel that?"

Gideon felt it. A pressure behind the wind, a held-breath quality to the air, the whole horizon seeming to lean. She thought of the counting, of breath held and released, of the sea waiting with its endless patience to take whatever wasn't careful.

"Storms come," she said. "We're still here after."

"That we are." He tapped her doorframe twice with his knuckles — his habit, a blessing or a superstition, he never said which — and went on to his own room.

Gideon went inside, lit her candle stub, and checked the bolt on her door, which she did every night, not because she expected anything but because bolting a door was one of the few acts available to her that presumed she had something worth keeping.

Outside, the wind rose. Far out past the point, the first clouds were assembling themselves in the dark, patient and enormous, and the sea beneath them had begun, very softly, to count.
