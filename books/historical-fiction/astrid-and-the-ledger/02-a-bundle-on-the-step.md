## 2. A Bundle on the Step

The storm came in from the northwest the way the old divers said the bad ones always did — a green bruise along the horizon first, then a wind that smelled of iron, then nothing for an hour, as if the sea were holding its breath before it screamed.

Gideon counted her breaths through all three stages.

She had learned that from her father, back when she was small enough to ride the luggers' decks while he worked the shallows off Cable Beach. *A diver who panics spends her air on fear,* he told her once, his hands rough on the rope as he coiled it. *A diver who counts spends it on living.* She was nineteen now and had been under contract with the Meridian Trading Company for six years, long enough that counting breaths had stopped being a trick and become a kind of prayer. Four in. Six out. The number never changed no matter what the water did.

That night, in the barracks she shared with eleven other women, the water did plenty.

Rain hit the corrugated roof like gravel thrown by an angry hand. The shutters banged loose twice and Gideon got up both times to wedge them closed with a slat of driftwood, moving quietly so as not to wake Marta in the next bunk, who swore at storms in Portuguese even in her sleep. The other women slept the way divers slept — heavily, completely, bodies spent from the day's work, ears still ringing faintly with pressure. Gideon envied them that. Her own sleep came thin and late and always left too early, and she lay now on her cot listening to the wind try to peel the roof off the world.

She should have been tired enough to sleep. They'd pulled shells since dawn, the whole line of them bent double over the lugger's rail working the air hoses, hauling up shell after shell until her shoulders burned and her fingers went white and wrinkled as drowned fruit. The take had been good. Meridian would be pleased, and a pleased company meant full rations and no docked pay, and that was as close to happiness as the ledger of her life allowed.

*Keep your head down. Do the work. Take the pay. Don't be trouble.*

Her father's rules. He'd lived by them for eleven years in company waters, and they hadn't saved him, but Gideon kept them anyway because the alternative — the alternative was what happened to men like Tam Okafor, who had asked questions about the safety valves on the compressors and been reassigned to a station up the coast from which letters took four months to arrive and none ever came back. She was not brave the way some people were brave, loud and burning. She was brave the way the reef was: still, patient, taking the sea's punishment without complaint, giving back nothing.

So when the sound came — a knock, or half a knock, more a thump against the barracks door than anything deliberate — she lay still for a long moment and told herself it was the storm. A branch. A shutter from the drying shed torn loose. Things knocked in cyclones. That was what storms did.

The thump came again. Softer this time. Almost apologetic.

Four in. Six out.

She rose, wrapped her blanket around her shoulders, and crossed the cold boards to the door. The women's barracks sat at the edge of the compound, last building before the wire fence and the track down to the jetty, which meant whatever came to their door came by mistake or by malice, and she could think of no one who wished them either.

She unbarred the door and pulled it open a hand's width, rain driving in sideways past her face, and looked down.

There was a bundle on the step.

For one stupid heartbeat she thought it was a sack of shell grit someone had abandoned, or a coil of rope blown against the wall. Then the bundle moved. It drew breath — a small, hitching breath, the kind a crying child makes when it has cried itself nearly empty — and a fist escaped the wrappings, tiny fingers flexing against the wet air.

Gideon stood very still.

The child couldn't have been more than two years old. It was wrapped in a wool blanket gone grey with wear, soaked through at the edges, and it lay on the covered part of the step where the eave threw a narrow shadow, which meant whoever had left it had thought about where the rain fell and chosen accordingly. Someone careful. Someone who knew Broome weather and compound layouts and exactly how long a walk it was from the gate to this particular door.

"Hello?" Gideon said into the rain, uselessly, her voice swallowed whole.

Nothing answered but the wind.

She looked up the track toward the overseer's quarters, dark. Toward the gatehouse, where a single lantern swung in its bracket, its light jumping and failing. Whoever had come and gone had done it inside the last few minutes, in the teeth of a rising cyclone, and had not wanted to be seen, and had known the watch rotation well enough to pick its blind hour.

Gideon crouched and gathered the bundle against her chest. It was lighter than she expected. The child's head lolled against her collarbone, hot with fever or simply exhausted, cheeks tracked with dried tears, hair matted flat. A girl, she saw as she stepped back inside and barred the door behind her. Small. Maybe eighteen months. Eyes closed, lashes clumped wet.

In the blanket's folds, pressed against the child's stomach, was a folded square of oilcloth tied with string.

Gideon carried them both to her bunk and set the girl down on the thin mattress, working fast now, pulling the wet blanket away and replacing it with her own dry one, chafing the small hands, checking breath — four in, six out, steady enough — and only when the child was breathing warm and even against her pillow did she sit back on her heels and turn to the oilcloth package.

It was heavier than it looked. She untied the string with fingers that had hauled two hundred shells that day and yet shook now, and didn't know why.

Inside the oilcloth was paper. A slim book, maybe forty pages, bound in board covers gone soft at the corners, wrapped around itself with a second fold of cloth. And tucked into the top fold, a slip of paper with five words written in pencil:

*Forget the face. Keep the book.*

Gideon read it three times. Outside, the storm found its full voice, a long descending roar, and the roof held, and the lantern flame leaned hard and righted itself.

Then she opened the book.

* * *

She knew the handwriting before she understood a single word.

That was the thing about reading — you could know a hand the way you knew a voice, before sense arrived. The letters were cramped and forward-leaning, crowded into the lines like men into a lifeboat. The *g*s looped low. The numerals were written in the old island style her father had learned from a Malay trader in Thursday Sound, the seven with its little cross-stroke through the middle, the four open-topped like a sail.

Gideon Eriksen knew that hand because she had watched it write her own name across a hundred birthday notes, because she had seen it fill the flyleaf of her mother's Bible, because it had been the last human thing she touched on the morning the company boat came back without him — a note he'd left pinned to his kit bag saying *gone to the far bank, back by tide-turn*, in these exact leaning letters, and the tide had turned three days ago now, going on eight years, and he had never come back.

Her father had been dead for eight years. Drowned, officially. Lost with gear, off the Meridian grounds at Lagrange Bay, one of those accidents the sea collected every season and everyone accepted the way they accepted weather.

And here was his handwriting, fresh enough that the pencil had not faded, on paper that had not yellowed, in a book that could not be more than months old.

Gideon sat down on the floor because standing had stopped being possible. The lantern guttered and steadied. The child slept on, breathing in her blanket, one fist curled near her mouth.

*Forget the face.*

Whose face? The child's? Hers? And keep the book — keep it how, keep it where, keep it from whom? The note assumed things. It assumed she could read. It assumed she would not run screaming to the overseer, which any sensible contracted woman would do, because finding an abandoned child on your step in a company compound was trouble, and trouble at Meridian was paid for in ways you didn't get to choose.

It assumed, most of all, that she was someone specific. That the book had been left with *her*, on *this* step, deliberately — because there was no accident in this, none. You did not stumble through a cyclone to the wrong barracks door.

She turned pages with hands she ordered to be steady. Columns of figures. Names — some in full, some reduced to initials or trade-marks, the little symbols companies used for contracted men whose names were considered too much trouble to spell. Dates running backward through the year and then further, into last year. Payments. Sums received and sums disbursed, entered in a neat clerk's rhythm, except the clerk was her father, dead eight years, writing entries dated four months ago.

Page nine stopped her cold.

An entry headed with a date in March, a place-name — Lagrange Bay — and beneath it, in her father's tight script: *Diver lost, J. Amosa. Recorded as: loss, equipment fault. Paid to family: nil. Paid to inspector (R.M.): £12.*

Below that, another. *Diver lost, T. Okr. Recorded: transferred. No transfer exists. Paid to inspector: £8.*

*T. Okr.*

Tam Okafor. Reassigned up the coast, from which letters took four months and none came back. Gideon felt the floor tilt gently, as if she were back on deck in a swell. She gripped the bunk frame. The child stirred, whimpered, settled again with a sigh.

Someone had died and been erased, and someone had been paid to erase him, and her father — her dead, drowned, impossible father — had written it down.

She turned more pages. Deaths recorded as losses. Losses recorded as transfers. Bribes logged with the blandness of grocery lists. And children — she stopped again, breath catching — children, entered between shipments of shell: *girl, approx 2 yrs, from station north, delivered to —* and the name that followed was smudged, water-damaged, unreadable, though something in the shape of it made her stomach drop as if she'd missed a rung on the diving ladder.

Delivered. Not fostered. Not adopted. Delivered, like cargo, like shell.

Gideon looked at the sleeping child on her bunk. Grey blanket. Feverish heat. Cheeks still showing the geography of old tears.

*Forget the face.*

The wind slammed the shutters and the driftwood wedge held, barely. Down the room, Marta muttered and rolled over. Somewhere beyond the fence the sea was tearing itself apart against the rocks, and Gideon sat on the cold floorboards with a dead man's book in her lap and a stranger's daughter breathing ten inches from her knee, and understood that she was holding something worth killing for. Not might be. Was. The care of the delivery, the storm as cover, the five penciled words — whoever had brought this had risked everything to put it in a diver's hands, and people only risked everything against people who took everything.

Four in. Six out. Her breaths were coming too fast; she slowed them by force.

Options arranged themselves in her mind like shells on a sorting table. Take the child to the overseer in the morning. Report the book. Be thanked, perhaps, be questioned certainly, be watched forever afterward — and hand her father's last living words into the keeping of the men the book accused. Or hide it. Both of it. The girl and the book. Hide a child in a compound where eleven women shared a roof and the walls were thinner than honesty.

Both choices ended somewhere terrible. She could feel that the way divers felt a change in pressure before the storm showed itself.

The child woke at dawn's grey edge, just as the storm blew itself hoarse, and looked at Gideon with eyes the deep brown of wet shell, and did not cry. She studied Gideon's face with a grave, assessing solemnity, as if filing it away somewhere safe, and then she reached up one small hand and touched Gideon's cheek, and patted it, twice, matter-of-factly, the way you'd pat a horse to tell it you meant no harm.

"What am I going to do with you?" Gideon whispered.

The child said something in answer. One word, soft and slurred with sleep, in a language Gideon half-knew from the northern stations where the best divers were hired and the worst rules applied. She caught only the end of it, a name-shape, a sound like *—osa.*

Amosa.

J. Amosa. Diver lost, Lagrange Bay. Recorded as: loss, equipment fault. Paid to family: nil.

Gideon went very still, the child's fingers still resting on her cheek, and outside the storm-washed morning came up pale and clean over the compound, over the shell heaps, over the Meridian flag snapping dry and bright above the office where the overseer would soon unlock the door and begin the day's accounting — and somewhere in that accounting, she now understood, there might be a column with this child's name in it, waiting to be balanced.
