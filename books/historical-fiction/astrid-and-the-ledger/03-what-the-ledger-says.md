## 3. What the Ledger Says

The barracks went quiet in stages. First the lamps, one by one, as the women saved their oil. Then the talk, dropping from gossip to murmurs to the small wet sounds of sleep. Last came the sea, which never quieted at all — it only changed its argument with the shore.

Gideon lay on her bunk and waited through all of it.

She had done this every night for three nights now, lying still until the breathing around her evened out, counting the rhythms the way she counted breaths underwater. Old Marta snored on her left. The girl called Pim slept like she was still diving, quick shallow pulls of air. When Gideon could no longer distinguish one sleeper from another, when the room had become a single slow animal, she slid the ledger out from beneath her mattress where it lived wrapped in a spare shirt, and she carried it to the corner behind the water casks where the candle stub couldn't be seen from the door.

She'd stolen the candle from the overseer's stores. A half-inch of tallow, maybe two nights' worth if she was careful. It felt strange to steal something so small after a lifetime of stealing nothing, but then, everything about the ledger made small thefts seem like nothing at all.

Her father's handwriting looked different by candlelight. By daylight it had been merely familiar — the lean, slanted letters she'd watched move across shipping manifests while he sat at the company desk he was allowed to use on Sundays. But at night, alone, flame throwing his words up the wall in shivering shadows, it was like hearing his voice again. *Gid,* he used to say, *numbers don't lie, but men make them lie.* She hadn't understood what he meant. She was beginning to.

* * *

The first night she'd only managed the opening pages before terror sent her back to bed.

It was a station ledger, that much was plain. Dated entries, columns, initials in the margins. But it wasn't the official record — the official records lived in the company office under lock, and those were clean books, fair copies, the ones shown to inspectors. This was the other thing. This was the book behind the book.

She'd known such things existed the way you know the sea has a floor. You never see it. You build your whole life on top of it.

The first entry that stopped her breath was dated four years ago, the season her father died.

*Meridian Trading Co., Broome Station — private memorandum.*

*Received: 40 divers, contract term 3 years. 6 replacements required (see losses).*

*Losses: J.E. — drowned, channel grounds. No recovery. Wages forfeit per clause 9.*

Gideon read it six times. Her father's own hand, recording his own death. *J.E. Jonas Eriksen. Drowned, channel grounds.* Except her father hadn't drowned in the channel. She'd been fourteen, standing on the jetty when they brought the boat in, and she'd heard the diver who carried him say the words she'd spent five years trying to forget: *the line fouled on something down there. Wire. New wire, strung where no wire should be.* And the overseer had said, loudly, for everyone on the jetty: *the girl's father was careless. Careless men drown.*

She had believed it. That was the worst part, the part that made her want to put her fist through the wall of the barracks now, quietly, without waking anyone. She had believed it because believing it was safer. A man who drowned carelessly was an accident. A man who drowned because someone strung wire across a diving ground — that was something else, and something else got you killed too, or worse, got you *noticed*.

She sat in the dark with the candle guttering and understood, finally, why her father had kept this book hidden, and why whoever left it on her step had chosen her door. Not sentiment. Not kindness. They'd left it with her because she was the daughter of the man whose death was written in its pages, and because a woman who had learned to keep her head down was the last person anyone would search.

They had guessed wrong about the last part. Or she was going to prove them wrong about it.

* * *

The second night, she found the money.

Bribes were recorded with a bookkeeper's tenderness, each payment itemized like pearls at market. *Inspector Halloran — gratuity, quarterly audit passed — £12.* *Inspector Halloran — Christmas consideration — £5.* *Harbourmaster's clerk — discretion re: manifest discrepancies — £3 10s.* Page after page. Men whose names appeared in the newspapers as guardians of the public trust, purchased for less than the price of a good saddle.

There was a column for deaths. It wasn't labeled deaths. It was labeled *losses*, and losses were subtracted from wages owed, and beside several entries, in a different ink — the overseer's ink, Gideon knew it from the pay notices nailed to the mess hall door — were notations like *recovered shell retained* and *no search ordered, weather unsuitable.* The weather had been flat calm the week Tomas Reyes went missing. Everyone remembered the weather. Nobody had said anything, because Tomas had complained about rations two days before, loudly, in front of the overseer, and everyone had done the arithmetic silently: complain, and then the sea takes you, and the sea is not questioned.

The sea was questioned in this book. That was what made her hands shake. Every lie the company told was written down here in her father's careful figures, and the lies added up. He'd been adding them up for years. There was a running total at the bottom of each page, small and neat, like a man keeping score in a game only he knew was being played.

On the third page of bribes she found a name that made the candle flame swim in front of her eyes.

*Ward, E. — junior officer, customs — declined gratuity — note: watch this one.*

Elias Ward. She didn't know him beyond the face — young, sharp-eyed, the customs man who'd come through the station twice and looked at everything including her, which nobody else bothered to do. He'd declined a bribe. In this book, that made him either a fool or a threat, and the company did not suffer fools or threats.

She should have felt nothing for a name on a page. Instead she felt something dangerously close to hope, and she blew it out along with the candle and lay awake until dawn doing sums of her own.

* * *

The third night — tonight — she reached the children.

She'd been dreading it without knowing why. Perhaps because the child slept ten feet away, in the crate Gideon had lined with blankets, breathing her small fierce breaths, and perhaps because some part of Gideon had already understood what the book would tell her and had been walking her toward it page by page, the way the tide walks you toward something you can see coming and cannot stop.

The entries began three years ago. *Transfers.* That was all the heading said. Transfers.

*One female child, approx. age 2, delivered Broome Station per arrangement. Mother deceased (diver, loss). Father — see termination, p. 41. Disposition: south, to Mrs. Hargrave, Fremantle. Payment received: £8.*

£8. Gideon read the figure until it stopped looking like numbers and started looking like teeth.

She turned to page 41. Her father's handwriting here was cramped, hurried, the letters of a man writing fast in a place where being caught meant more than losing the book.

*R. Kaili came to me today. Says he will go to the magistrate in Perth unless they return his daughter. I told him what happens to men who go to magistrates. He says he does not care. He showed me the paper the company gave him — indenture signed with a thumbprint he cannot read. I have copied the names into this book. If anything comes of it, let it be said the company sold his child for eight pounds and wrote it down as freight.*

Below, in the overseer's ink, one line:

*Kaili — terminated, channel grounds, 11 March.*

The same grounds where the wire was strung. The same entry format as her father. *Terminated*, as though a man were a contract.

Gideon closed the book and pressed it against her chest and sat very still in the dark behind the water casks, and the stillness was not calm. It was the stillness of a woman holding something live and venomous and deciding, breath by breath, whether to throw it away or let it strike her.

The child. Two years old, three years ago. Delivered to Broome, then sent south — except she was here, wasn't she, asleep in a crate in a barracks full of women who dove for shells until their lungs bled. Someone had brought her back. Someone had taken her off that boat to Fremantle, or intercepted her, or — Gideon's mind moved carefully, testing each thought like a diver testing a rotten shell — someone had decided the magistrate's daughter was worth more than eight pounds as evidence.

And then they'd panicked. Whoever held her had held her for three years, and then the audits came closer, or a conscience came due, and they'd set her on a stranger's step in a storm with a note that said *forget the face.*

Forget the face. Because the face was evidence. Because somewhere there might be a photograph, a record, an aunt, a grandmother, a man's sister in a coastal village who could look at a three-year-old and say *that is Rafe Kaili's girl, that is the child they took.*

Gideon rose on silent feet and crossed to the crate and looked down at Nell — she'd started calling her that in her head, though she hadn't said it aloud; naming a thing made it yours, and nothing in this barracks was allowed to be yours — and the child slept with one fist curled under her chin and her brows drawn, dreaming hard, the way children dream when they've seen too much too young.

"You're not freight," Gideon whispered. "You're a witness."

The words hung in the dark and changed shape as they settled. Because a witness was worse than a burden. A witness was a reason. The company had killed a man over eight pounds and a thumbprint. What would they do over a living, breathing, growing proof that could one day stand in a courtroom and say *my name is—*

She didn't know the child's name. That was the hole in everything. The book said *one female child, approx. age 2* and nothing more, because to the company the child had never been a person, only a line item, and the father who could have given her a name was terminated eleven March, channel grounds, no search ordered, weather unsuitable.

But someone knew. Somewhere between here and Fremantle, between the mother's grave and the aunt nobody had found, someone knew a name. Names could be traced. Names could be proven. Which meant the child wasn't just a moral weight on Gideon's conscience anymore. She was a thread, and threads led places, and places led to people, and people — if Gideon was very careful, very patient, very brave in the way her father had been brave, writing truth in secret for years with no hope of delivery — people could testify.

She looked at the ledger in her lap. Forty-odd names of the dead. Twenty pages of bribes. A child priced and shipped like pearl shell. Her father had died carrying the knowledge of this book, or died because of it — the wire across the channel suddenly explained itself, didn't it, sudden and cold and obvious: he'd been getting close to something, or someone had gotten close to him, and the company solved its problem with forty fathoms of water and a length of cable.

They had thought drowning him would drown the book.

Instead the book had floated to her door.

Gideon folded the ledger into its shirt, pushed it deep beneath her mattress, and pinched out the candle. In the darkness the arithmetic arranged itself with terrible clarity. She could burn the book. She could carry the child to the church in town and leave her on the steps and walk away and keep her head down for the remaining years of her indenture and maybe, if the sea was kind, buy out her contract someday and be free in the small exhausted way freedom came to people like her.

Or she could finish what her father started.

Outside, the tide turned. She heard it change against the pilings, the long exhale of the ebb, and she thought: my father's body is somewhere under that water, and the company wrote *no recovery, wages forfeit*, and they thought that was the end of the entry.

It wasn't. She was the end of the entry. She was the sum at the bottom of the page.

In the morning she would need to think about how a woman with no rights, no money, and no voice moves a dangerous book past men with rifles at a gate. Tonight she only permitted herself one more look at the sleeping child, one more silent promise spoken into the dark where no one could hear her spend it:

"They wrote you down for eight pounds," she whispered. "I'm going to write you back."

Then she lay down and counted her breaths until sleep came, and for the first time since the storm, the counting felt less like endurance and more like rehearsal.

Because tomorrow — or the day after, when the quarterly supply run brought strangers through the gate — she would have to find out which side the customs man stood on. *Watch this one*, her father had written. Gideon intended to do exactly that.

She just had to make sure he wasn't watching her back.
