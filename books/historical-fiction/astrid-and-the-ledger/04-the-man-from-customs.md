## 4. The Man from Customs

Elias Ward had been in Broome eleven days when he understood that the town ran on two currencies: pearl shell and silence. The first was weighed openly on the company's scales. The second was traded in doorways and half-lit verandas, in the pause before an answer, in the way a man's eyes went flat when you asked about the diving season.

He was twenty-eight years old, a customs officer with the Commonwealth's new-fangled authority stitched into his shoulders, and he had learned early that his uniform opened doors and closed faces in equal measure. Men who would happily discuss the price of mother-of-pearl by the ton would go quiet as churchyards if you asked how many divers had come up from the last run.

His father had gone quiet the same way, once. Elias carried that memory like a stone sewn into his coat lining — always there, always weighing, never spoken of.

The Meridian Trading Company station lay four miles north of town, past the mangrove flats where the tide went out so far it seemed to abandon the land entirely. He'd been assigned to inspect it as part of the quarterly review: manifests, cargo weights, headcounts of indentured labour, the usual paper ritual that everyone involved understood to be theatre. The companies kept two sets of books. The inspectors knew they kept two sets of books. The inspectors wrote reports based on the clean set, collected their signatures, and went home to their dinners.

Elias had signed such reports before. He told himself each time that the system was bigger than he was, that a man who made noise got crushed under it, that his father's ruin proved the arithmetic of survival. His father had been a harbourmaster in Fremantle, once upon a time, before he'd questioned a shipping concern's tonnage figures and found himself audited into oblivion, dismissed, and dead of drink within six years while Elias was still learning to shave. The lesson had been absorbed whole: *look, record what you're given to record, go home.*

But something about Meridian itched at him. And Elias Ward had learned to trust an itch the way other men trusted a compass.

* * *

He rode out to the station on a Tuesday morning, the light already hard as hammered tin. The compound resolved out of the heat shimmer gradually: the long low barracks with their corrugated roofs, the shell heaps rising like pale dunes, the jetty walking itself out into the bay on crooked timber legs, the company house set back on higher ground with its wide veranda and its air of deliberate, expensive indifference.

And everywhere, people. Divers coming off the boats, their skin bleached white with salt, moving with the unhurried economy of the exhausted. Luggers at anchor. Kanakas and Malays and Japanese and a scatter of Aboriginal women whose contracts the company filed under headings Elias suspected no magistrate had ever read closely.

The overseer, a broad red-faced man named Craddock, met him at the gate with a handshake like a manacle and a smile like a ledger entry.

"Officer Ward. We've been expecting you. Bit early in the season for the paperwork men, aren't you?"

"The schedule moved," Elias said. "I'll want the manifests, the wage books, and a walk-through of the sorting sheds."

"Of course, of course." Craddock gestured him through, and Elias felt the man's hand hover near his shoulder — not quite steering, but suggesting — as they crossed the yard. "Fair warning, we're mid-sorting. Shell dust everywhere. Ruins a good coat."

"My coat has seen worse."

They walked the sheds first. The smell hit him in layers: brine, rot, the sweetish reek of dying oyster, tobacco, sweat. Women sat along benches cracking shells open with knives worn thin as moonlight, their hands moving in rhythms too fast to follow, pulling pearls when there were pearls and dropping them into graded trays without expression. None of them looked up. That was the first thing wrong, Elias thought. In every other station he'd inspected, someone always looked up. Curiosity was human. This silence was trained.

Craddock talked the whole way, a river of figures and reassurances. Production up eight percent on last quarter. No losses to speak of. A fine healthy lot of divers this season, best in years.

"No losses," Elias repeated. "None at all?"

"Well." Craddock's smile didn't move but something behind it did, some small gear shifting. "We lost one or two to the bends, naturally. It happens. The sea takes its tithe."

"How many is one or two?"

"I'd have to check the book."

"You keep the book."

Something flickered across Craddock's face then, quick as a fish turning — annoyance, maybe, or recalculation. "Three," he said. "Three losses. I'll have the exact figures for your report by evening."

*Losses.* Not deaths. Losses, as though the men had been mislaid, left on a train, forgotten in a shed. Elias wrote the word down in his notebook exactly as spoken, and watched Craddock watch him write it.

It was in the sorting shed that he first noticed her.

She stood at the far bench, apart from the others — not by arrangement, he thought, but by gravity. The other women bent over their work; she bent over hers, but her attention was elsewhere. She was watching him. Not the way the others watched when forced to — the quick dart of eyes, down again, punished with a look from the floor manager. She watched him the way he imagined a chess player watches an opponent's hand hovering over a piece. Measuring. Anticipating.

She was young — nineteen, perhaps twenty — with dark hair cut short in the practical fashion of divers, salt-bleached streaks in it, and eyes the colour of deep water over sand. There was a scar along her forearm, old and clean. Her hands were wrecked, the way all divers' hands were wrecked, knuckles swollen, fingertips cracked and healed and cracked again. But she held them still, and stillness in that place was its own kind of statement.

When she saw that he'd seen her, she didn't look away. She finished whatever motion her knife was making, laid the shell down, and only then dropped her gaze to her work — a courtesy performed rather than compelled.

"What's her name?" Elias asked Craddock, keeping his voice casual.

Craddock followed his glance and shrugged. "Gideon. Eriksen. Diver's brat, born on the boats. Father drowned a few years back — one of the sea's tithes, as I said." He said it lightly, but Elias caught the faint emphasis, the little flag planted on the sentence. *Don't dig here.*

"Gideon," Elias repeated. "Unusual name."

"Bible name. Her father was a God-botherer, they say." Craddock laughed. "Sea fixed that."

Elias made a noncommittal sound and moved on. But twice more that morning, crossing the yard between buildings, he caught her watching him — once from the shadow of the barracks eaves where she stood wringing water from her hair, once from the jetty where she coiled rope with the absent competence of someone who could do it blind. Each time, the same measured look. Each time, the same deliberate withdrawal of it.

Everyone else in the compound had perfected invisibility. She alone seemed to have decided that being seen, carefully, on her own terms, was worth the risk.

He didn't know yet what that meant. He only knew it meant something.

* * *

The inspection took three days. On the second day, Elias asked to see the sick bay, and Craddock's hospitality developed a limp.

"Sick bay's nothing to see. A cot and a bottle of liniment."

"Then it'll take two minutes."

It took longer than two minutes, because the sick bay turned out to be a lean-to behind the stores with one cot, no liniment, and a boy of maybe sixteen shivering under a blanket, his chest rattling like a jar of shells. Pneumonia, by the sound of it, well advanced.

"When did he come in?" Elias asked the woman who acted as unofficial nurse, a big calm islander named Mama Rua whom everyone in the compound seemed to orbit without acknowledging.

"Four days," she said.

"Has a doctor seen him?"

"Doctor comes Thursdays. From town. If the boat runs."

Today was Wednesday. Elias did the arithmetic and didn't like the answer. "Get him to town tonight. My launch is going back; there's room."

Craddock appeared in the doorway before Elias had finished speaking, materialising with the unerring instinct of management for trouble. "Now, Officer, no need to trouble yourself. The boy's being cared for. Thursday's not far."

"He'll be dead by Thursday."

"These things happen—"

"They happen more often when nobody counts them." Elias heard his own voice go flat and cold, his father's voice, the voice from before the ruin, and stopped himself. Careful. Careful. You are here to count shells and sign papers. "My launch goes at six. Put him aboard."

There was a silence in which several calculations occurred behind Craddock's eyes, none of them charitable. Then he smiled his ledger-entry smile and said, "Whatever the officer requires," and Elias understood that he had just spent a coin he couldn't afford to spend. Every favour extracted was a debt recorded. Craddock would be writing to someone in Perth tonight.

That night, sitting on the veranda of the guest quarters with the manifests spread around him, Elias found the anomaly.

It was small. That was what made it beautiful. The quarterly manifest listed shell shipments by weight and by boat, and the wage book listed divers by contract and by run. Cross-referenced, the numbers almost matched — almost. Three boats' worth of dives logged in the wage book that never appeared in any shipment manifest. Six weeks of labour, accounted for in wages paid, producing shell that had apparently evaporated between the lugger and the scales.

Or produced shell that had been sold somewhere the company's books didn't reach.

He sat with it a long time, the lamp hissing, moths ticking against the glass. It could be clerical error. Clerical error was the kind thing to assume, and the safe thing, and nine times in ten it was even the true thing.

But three boats' worth wasn't error. Error came in ones. Three was a pattern, and patterns were decisions, and decisions were made by men with names.

He thought of his father then, unexpectedly and completely — not the ruined man at the end but the one before, standing on the Fremantle quay in his good coat, saying to seven-year-old Elias: *Numbers don't lie, son. People do. That's why you follow the numbers.* Two years later the same man had taught him a different lesson without meaning to, taught it with his dismissal and his bottle and his funeral, and both lessons lived in Elias side by side like squabbling siblings.

He copied the figures into his private notebook, closed the manifests, and blew out the lamp.

In the dark, the sea sounded enormous.

* * *

On the third morning, before the inspection formally resumed, Elias walked the shell heaps.

It was a habit of his — walking ground alone before anyone could arrange it for him. Inspectors who let themselves be escorted saw what they were shown. He wanted the raw compound: the midden stink of the heaps, the boats pulled up for careening, the laundry lines, the dog kennels, the small economies of a place that housed two hundred people and pretended to house inventory.

She was at the water point, filling a bucket.

Up close, Gideon Eriksen was smaller than she'd seemed at distance and considerably more composed. She registered his approach without appearing to — a slight turn of the head, nothing more — and continued filling her bucket as though the assistant customs officer of the entire district were a feature of the landscape, like the jetty or the heat.

"Miss Eriksen."

"Officer." Her voice was low and even. She didn't curtsy. Around here, he'd noticed, the indentured women curtsied to anyone with a lanyard; it was reflex, drilled in. She hadn't. Either she'd forgotten, or she'd decided.

"You've dived long?" he asked. Conversational. Harmless.

"Since I was twelve."

"That's young."

"It's the age the contract starts." She said it without heat, which somehow made it worse — a fact stated the way one states the tide table.

"And your father? Craddock mentioned he dived too."

For the first time, something moved behind her eyes. Not grief exactly — grief worn smooth, an old stone — but a sharpening. "He drowned. Four years ago. Company waters."

"Company waters?"

"Waters under company lease." She lifted the full bucket easily, settling it against her hip. "Most of the water here is company waters, Officer. You'd know that better than me. It's in your papers."

The barb was small and perfectly placed. Elias felt it lodge. "I'm here to inspect the manifests," he said.

"Yes," she said. "I expect the manifests will inspect very well."

And there it was — the whole conversation in miniature, two blades passing close enough to spark. She knew something. Or she suspected everything, which amounted to the same thing in a place like this. And she had just told him, in front of God and the water tank, that she knew the inspection was theatre — which meant either that she was very foolish, or very controlled, and nothing else he'd observed about her suggested foolish.

"Why are you telling me this?" he asked quietly.

She looked at him then, fully, and he had the uncomfortable sensation of being read — weighed, priced, entered into some ledger of her own. Whatever she found there made her shake her head slightly.

"Telling you what, Officer? I said the weather's fine for diving." She hoisted the bucket. "You should be careful, though. Weather changes fast on this coast. One minute it's clear." She paused, and the pause was a door held open a crack. "Next minute there's a storm, and everything loose gets blown somewhere it shouldn't be."

Then she walked away, bucket swinging, spine straight, and left him standing at the water point with his heart going harder than a three-minute exchange should account for.

* * *

He thought about it all afternoon, through the final tedious hours of signatures and countersignatures, through Craddock's farewell dinner of tinned beef and warm beer, through the long ride back toward town in the failing light.

Everything loose gets blown somewhere it shouldn't be.

It could mean nothing. Storm talk. Coastal small talk, the universal currency of people who live by weather. He'd heard a hundred variations of it from a hundred harbour workers.

But she had chosen to say it to *him*, on the one morning he'd separated himself from his escort, and she had chosen the word *loose*, and she had held his eyes while she said it, and Gideon Eriksen did nothing — he was now quite certain — without choosing.

Someone at that station was carrying something. He didn't know what. Papers, most likely; it was always papers, in the end. Money moved quietly, but paper left trails, and trails were his trade. And someone had looked at the newly arrived customs officer — the outsider, the variable, the one man in the compound whose loyalties weren't bought and paid for — and had begun, cautiously, dangerously, to wonder whether he might be a door instead of a wall.

His father's voice, from the quay, from the grave: *Follow the numbers.*

And another voice, newer, his own, cautious as a man testing ice: *And remember what following them cost him.*

At the customs house in Broome, he lit the lamp, opened his private notebook, and wrote beneath the anomalous figures a single line:

*Meridian station — diver, Eriksen, G. Watch.*

He looked at the words a long moment. Then, because he was honest with himself in the privacy of ink if nowhere else, he added:

*Watch closely.*

Outside, the tide was turning, and far up the coast, over the Meridian compound, the first clouds of the season were gathering out of the dark sea like a rumour no one had confirmed.
