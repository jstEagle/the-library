## 5. Checkpoint One: The Gate

The audit was coming.

Gideon learned it the way she learned everything at Meridian — sideways, through half-heard sentences and the behavior of men who thought the women around them were furniture. She was mending a net outside the drying sheds on Tuesday when Mr. Calloway's clerk crossed the yard with an armful of ledgers, and she heard him say to the cooper, *quarterly's moved up — auditor comes the first week of the moon*, and the cooper said something about burning barrels, and both of them laughed the way men laugh when the joke is someone else's life going up in smoke.

Burning barrels. Loose papers. The word *audit* spoken like a weather report, mild and inevitable.

She kept her eyes on the net, her fingers working the twine in their blind, practiced rhythm, and inside her chest her heart went from walking to running without moving a single muscle of her body.

The ledger had lived for nine days now beneath the loose floorboard under her cot, wrapped first in oilcloth and then in Nell's spare blanket, because Gideon had discovered that Nell slept better if the bundle stayed near her, as if the child could smell her mother's ghost in the paper. Nine days of carrying it through every hour — at the wash troughs, at the shell heaps, in the mess line where the overseer's deputy walked the rows with his stick tapping his boot. Nine days of being a woman with a secret so heavy it changed how she stood, how she breathed, whether she met anyone's eyes.

She had told herself there was no hurry. That was a lie she had needed, the way a diver needs the lie that the air will last one more minute. There had always been a hurry. Now it had a date: the first week of the moon.

* * *

She worked out the plan the way she worked out a dive — in pieces, in the dark, testing each part against what could kill her.

The ledger could not leave all at once. A whole book was a shape; a shape could be found. But pages — pages could be made into nothing, flattened into the lining of a diving suit no overseer would ever look twice at, because the suits belonged to the water and the water belonged to Meridian and no one searched the tools of their own profit.

There was also this: she could not carry it herself past the town checkpoint. Her face was known. Eriksen's daughter, the quiet one, the one who never makes trouble — trouble would be the first thing they looked for precisely because she never made any. But the suit could travel. Suits went to Broome for repair at the sailmaker's on Carnarvon Street, three or four each quarter, carried by whoever was making the supply run. This quarter the run fell to Marta Kessing, a Malay-Scottish diver older than Gideon, broad as a jetty piling, who had been at Meridian eleven years and whose silence was the kind you could build a house on.

Marta owed Gideon nothing. That was the difficulty. Everything at Meridian was debts — company scrip against company stores, breath against bottom-time, silence against survival — and Gideon had spent nineteen years keeping her account at zero. Asking Marta to carry the suit meant opening an account. It meant trusting someone with the fact that Gideon Eriksen wanted something, and women who wanted things at Meridian ended up like her father, face-down in company water while the company wrote *loss* beside his name.

But the suit could not walk to town alone.

She chose a night two days after she'd heard about the audit, when the wind had dropped and the barracks slept its exhausted sleep, and she sat on the floor by Nell's cot with the ledger open across her knees, and she began to choose which names would travel first.

Not the whole book. Not yet. The whole book would go later, by some road she hadn't found, or wouldn't go at all and would die with her under the floorboard, and even that would be better than it dying in a burning barrel. But these pages — these she would send ahead like scouts.

She chose carefully. The page with the payments to Inspector Renwick, dated over four years, sums neat as prayer beads. The page where twelve deaths were entered not as deaths but as *losses — stock*, with replacement costs listed beside each name, as though a drowned man were a cracked helmet, a thing to be written down and written off. And the page with the transfers — children sold between stations, prices paid, initials of the clerks who handled them like cargo.

She read each page twice before she cut it free. Her father's handwriting. All of it her father's hand — she'd known it before she knew anything else about the book, that slanted, patient script she'd watched him make across grocery lists and tide tables and the margins of her first reading primer. He had written down what he saw, entry by entry, year by year, right up until the entries stopped and the sea closed over him. *Forget the face,* the note said. Someone had wanted the child forgotten. Someone had wanted the book destroyed. Instead it had come to her door in the storm, and she was beginning to believe — carefully, the way you test ice — that her father had arranged it that way before he ever went into the water.

She cut the pages free with her sewing scissors, close along the spine, and stacked them flat, and then she took out the diving suit.

It hung from the nail behind the door, black rubber gone grey at the shoulders, smelling of brine and sulphur and her own sweat soaked into it over four years. She turned it inside out and laid it across her knees and studied the lining the way she'd study a reef head before committing to a descent — looking for the way in, the cover, the current.

Then she began to sew.

Her needle was a diver's needle, curved, made for patching rubber, and she used waxed thread pulled fine so the stitches would sit flat against the canvas lining. Three pages per panel. She sewed them face-down against the fabric between the layers at the small of the back, where the suit was doubled anyway for wear, where a seam was expected to be thick. Stitch, knot, stitch. Small stitches. Patient ones. Her hands did not shake, and she noticed that they didn't, and wondered briefly what it said about her that they didn't — that fear had gotten so far inside her it had stopped reaching her hands.

Nell woke once and stood up in her cot, hair wild, watching her with those grave dark eyes that seemed too old for a child not yet two.

"Home," Nell said. It was one of her six words. She said it pointing sometimes at the cot, sometimes at Gideon, sometimes at nothing at all, and every time it landed in Gideon's chest like a stone dropped down a well.

"Not yet," Gideon whispered. "Soon. Sleep."

Nell slept. Gideon sewed until the candle guttered, until nine pages lay hidden in the lining like a second spine, and then she turned the suit right side out and hung it back on its nail, where it looked exactly like what it had always been — a dead woman's weight of rubber waiting to take another woman down into the dark.

* * *

Marta came to her at dawn.

That was how it happened — not with a conversation Gideon had planned and rehearsed, but with Marta simply appearing at the wash troughs, taking the scrubbing brush out of Gideon's hand, and saying, low, without looking at her:

"You've got a smell about you lately, girl."

Gideon's stomach dropped through the floor of the world. "I don't—"

"Candle wax. You burn candles in your bunk after lights-out. I sleep two bunks down." Marta wrung a shirt hard enough to crack its seams. "And rubber cement. Somebody's been patching a suit indoors, which is stupid, because the fumes carry."

The trough water was cold on Gideon's wrists and she was grateful for it, grateful for anything that gave her hands a reason to exist. "Marta—"

"I'm not asking." Marta set the wet shirt down and finally looked at her, and her face was the face Gideon had trusted for years without knowing she trusted it — lined, unreadable, tired in the permanent way of people who had survived by refusing to be surprised. "I'm telling you the supply run goes Thursday. My suit and two others go to Halvorsen the sailmaker, and whatever else I put in the bundle comes home patched or doesn't come home, and nobody at the gate opens a bundle that's mine, because I've been making this run since before that fat overseer could grow his first whisker." She picked up the brush again. "Whatever you've been patching, have it ready by first light Thursday. Don't tell me what it is. I'm old enough to know that what I don't know can't be beaten out of me."

She walked away, and Gideon stood at the trough with the cold water running over her knuckles and understood that she had just opened an account she could never close.

* * *

Thursday came in grey and windless, the sea lying flat as poured tin, which the old divers said was worse than any storm — flat water meant something was holding its breath.

Gideon rose before the bell. She dressed Nell in the dark, fed her the heel of bread she'd saved, and carried the suit bundle — suit folded around a change of clothes, a mending kit, ordinary things stacked over the extraordinary — out to the store shed where Marta waited with the rest of the run.

Marta weighed the bundle in one hand. Looked at Gideon. Said nothing. Put it in the cart under the sacking, between the spare helmets and a crate of pitch, and climbed onto the bench, and that was all. It took less than a minute. Eleven years of trust compressed into the space of a nod, and Gideon watched the cart roll toward the gate feeling as though she had handed her own heart up onto that bench to ride to town in sacking.

Then the gate.

The station gate at Meridian wasn't much to look at — timber posts, a bar, the company sign gone pale in the salt air — but everyone at the compound understood it was the most important thirty feet of ground in their lives, because it was the place where the compound became the world and the world was not allowed in without permission and they were not allowed out. Beyond it ran the track to Broome, eight miles of red dirt and spinifex, and at the end of it: the magistrate, the law, the possibility — thin as wire, real as wire — that somewhere outside the company's arithmetic there existed another kind of counting.

Overseer Brack stood at the gate that morning, as he stood there most mornings, a wide man in a white shirt gone translucent with sweat, his stick in his fist and his deputy at his shoulder. Random searches, he called them. Everyone called them something else.

Gideon had planned her morning around the gate the way she planned a dive around slack tide. She joined the queue of women heading to the north paddocks with their hoes — work detail, unremarkable, twenty women and a guard — and she put herself in the middle of it, neither first nor last, and she fixed her eyes on the back of the neck of the woman ahead of her and began to count her breaths.

Four counts in. Six counts out. Her father's rhythm, taught to her at ten years old in the shallows off Cable Beach, the rhythm that had kept him alive through a thousand descents and failed him only once, at the end, in water she had not been allowed to see him die in. Four in. Six out. The count was a rope and she climbed down it, away from the surface noise, away from the fear, into the cold clear place where her body worked and her mind could watch.

Brack was working down the line. He stopped the fourth woman and made her turn out her apron pockets. Stopped the ninth and ran his hands along her waistband while she stared straight ahead with the terrible blankness Gideon knew from her own mirror. Stopped the thirteenth, a girl of sixteen named Adelie, new to the station, who flinched when he touched her, and Brack laughed and pushed her along harder than he needed to, and the whole line felt it pass through them like a current.

He was working inward from both ends of the queue. Gideon counted the intervals. Fourth. Ninth. Thirteenth. Eighteenth. She did the arithmetic without wanting to — the pattern was roughly every fifth, closing from the edges — and she placed herself, when the line compressed at the gate bar, eleventh from the front, fifteenth from the back, in the dead center of the net.

But Brack did not follow patterns forever. That was the thing about Brack. That was the thing that had kept the compound obedient for twenty years — not his strength, but his unpredictability, the sense every diver had that the rules of chance bent around him. He liked to prove it.

At the gate he stopped the tenth woman. Then he skipped two and stopped the thirteenth again — Adelie's friend, who wept silently while he searched her. Then he stepped back, looked down the remaining line with the slow satisfaction of a man choosing meat, and his eyes traveled — past the fourteenth, past the fifteenth —

— and stopped on Gideon.

Four counts in. Six counts out. She held the rope.

"Eriksen." He beckoned with two fingers. "Come here."

The line shifted around her, relieved, guilty, glad it wasn't them, and Gideon walked forward out of the crowd into the open space in front of the gate, and the sun hit her shoulders and she felt every stitch she had sewn sitting against her spine like a row of hot coins.

She had decided, in the nights of planning, what she would be if this moment came. Not innocent — innocence invited suspicion, innocence made men dig deeper to find the guilt they assumed was buried there. She would be bored. Boredom was armor at Meridian. Boredom was the face of a woman with nothing, wanting nothing, whose whole horizon was the next shift and the next meal. She let it come up over her features now, the weary dull patience of nineteen years, and she stood in front of Brack with her arms slightly away from her body and waited.

"You're Eriksen's girl," Brack said. He said it the way he said everything, as though the sentence were slightly beneath him and he were performing its recitation as a favor. "The quiet one."

"Yes, sir."

"The quiet ones." He smiled. His teeth were yellow and even. "The quiet ones are always hiding something. The loud ones are empty. You learn that, doing my job."

He circled her once, slowly, the stick tapping against his boot — tap, tap, tap — and Gideon counted her breaths and looked at the middle distance where the track disappeared into spinifex and heat shimmer. Behind her, faintly, she heard the cart creak. Marta's cart, already through the bar, already rolling, the driver waving his chit at the gatekeeper, the wheels turning north toward Broome with nine pages of her father's hand folded into a diving suit's spine. Rolling. Rolling away.

Which meant that whatever happened in the next sixty seconds, the worst had already been survived. If Brack found nothing on her, the pages were gone, beyond recall, beyond burning. If Brack found something on her — her mind touched the thought and withdrew from it, the way a hand withdraws from a stove.

He stopped in front of her. "Turn around."

She turned. She heard him breathe. She felt the air move as the stick came up — not touching her, never touching where witnesses gathered, but hovering along the line of her back, an inch off the fabric, the way a man reads a wall with his palm. Down the spine. Across the shoulders. At the small of her back the stick paused.

Four counts in. Six counts out. Her heartbeat was enormous and perfectly silent, a bell ringing underwater.

"Patchwork," Brack said behind her, disgusted. "This suit's been repaired so many times it's more patch than suit. Company issues gear to you people and you treat it like rubbish."

"Yes, sir," Gideon said, and put boredom into it, all nineteen years of boredom, and Brack grunted and stepped around to face her again.

For a moment he looked at her — really looked, the searchlight sweep, hunting for the flicker that would betray her — and she met his eyes with the flat gray patience of the sea itself and offered him nothing, absolutely nothing, a surface with no wreck beneath it visible from above.

"Go on," he said finally, and flicked the stick toward the paddocks. "You're worth less than the time."

She walked through the gate.

She did not hurry. Hurrying was a confession. She walked at the pace of the work detail, hoe over her shoulder, dust rising around her boots, and she passed the last post, the last thirty feet of the most important ground in her life, and she was outside. Outside. The wind came off the flats and touched her face and it smelled of nothing at all, of emptiness, of freedom, and she nearly staggered with the size of it.

Behind her, the gate closed for the next woman. Ahead of her, eight miles north, a cart rolled toward a sailmaker on Carnarvon Street carrying the first proof of murder the world would ever see.

Gideon reached the paddock, took up her row, and bent to the earth, and dug, and dug, and did not stop shaking until well past noon — small shakes, deep inside, where no one could see — and all the while her mind kept returning to the same point on the compass, turning back to it the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth:

Marta would reach Broome by dusk. The suit would sit in Halvorsen's shop among a dozen others. And the magistrate — she had a name now, Elias Ward had given it to her without meaning to, that customs officer with the careful eyes who watched everything and said nothing — Magistrate Corrigan, on Eagleton Lane, who received petitions on Saturday mornings.

Three days. She had three days to get the rest of the story out of the compound and into a courtroom, and the quarterly audit coming behind it like fire behind a fuse.

She raised her head to wipe her brow, and across the yard she saw Nell — who was supposed to be in the company crèche, who was supposed to be anywhere but here — standing alone in the shadow of the shell heaps in her small worn dress, looking directly at Gideon, one small fist raised and opening, closing, opening.

And as Gideon started toward her, the child spoke a single word into the windless morning, clear as a bell across water, and the word was a name Gideon had read only three nights ago, in ink, in her father's hand, on a page now sewn into a suit riding north to Broome.
