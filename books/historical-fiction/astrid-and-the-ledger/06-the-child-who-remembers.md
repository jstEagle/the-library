## 6. The Child Who Remembers

The child had a name. Of course she had a name — everyone had a name — but for eleven days she had been *the child*, *the bundle*, *her*, and every time the overseer's boots rang along the veranda Gideon pressed the girl's face into her shoulder and prayed to a God she'd stopped believing in at thirteen.

She couldn't keep calling her *the girl*. Names were dangerous. Names were how the company owned you — Gideon Eriksen, diver, contract 114, entered in a book in an office in Broome and never once asked whether she agreed. To name something was to claim it, and to be claimed was to be lost.

But you couldn't feed a child without calling her something.

"Nell," she tried one evening, kneeling on the barracks floor with a tin of rice porridge. It had come from nowhere. Her grandmother's sister maybe, a woman who'd died in Thursday Island before Gideon was born, a name her father used to say like a joke — *Aunty Nell could outswim a shark.* The girl looked up from the spoon with rice on her chin and considered it.

"Nell," she repeated. Then, with the terrible authority of the very young: "Nell *mine*."

"Yours," Gideon agreed, and felt something shift in her chest that she would spend the rest of that night refusing to examine.

* * *

The trouble with Nell was that she remembered things.

Gideon had assumed a child not yet three would be a blank — a small warm animal that slept and ate and cried and knew nothing. But Nell watched everything. She watched the way Gideon checked the window before lighting the candle. She learned to go silent when boots passed the door, quicker than some of the divers did. She had arrived already trained in silence, which told Gideon more about the child's short life than any note could have.

And she talked. Not sentences yet — words, dropped like shells into a bucket. *Water. Boat. Hot. No.* And names. That was the strange part. Children Nell's age said *mama* and *papa* and the dog's name, if they had a dog. Nell said other things.

One night, ten days after the bundle came through the storm, Gideon sat cross-legged on her bunk with the ledger open across her knees, working by the light of a candle stub shielded behind an upturned pannikin. She had developed a system. Two hours a night, no more — long enough to make progress, short enough that the candle burn wouldn't show in the morning ash. She read the way her father must have written it: fast, furtive, memorizing.

*J. Okonkwo, station transfer, 14 March 1889, £9 paid to W. Harrow.*
*M. Castellanos, deceased, recorded as loss, 2 June 1889. No inquiry.*
*Payment received, R. Sandoval, one female child, age two, per arrangement.*

Her finger stopped on that last line. She'd read it four times now and each time it got worse. One female child. Age two. Per arrangement. A payment received, not made — meaning someone had *paid the company*, or someone inside it, to take a little girl off their hands the way you'd sell a calf.

Behind her, Nell stirred in the nest of blankets Gideon had built against the wall. She didn't cry anymore when she woke in the dark; she just lay still and listened, the same as Gideon did. Presently a small warm weight climbed onto the bunk beside her, uninvited, and peered at the ledger upside down.

"No," Gideon said automatically, tilting it away. Then she stopped. There was no reason a toddler couldn't look at a book of numbers. It was only numbers.

Nell put one finger on the page anyway — children always did, they touched what they were forbidden to touch — and dragged it down the column of entries until it landed on a line of handwriting, and she said, clearly, in the round certain voice of a child announcing the obvious:

"Sando."

Gideon's blood went cold so fast it hurt.

"What did you say?"

"Sando." The finger tapped. Tap, tap. "Sando."

There was only one Sandoval in the ledger. R. Sandoval, payer of nine pounds for one female child, age two, per arrangement. Gideon turned the candle up a fraction and found the fuller entry she'd flagged days ago, deeper in the book, where her father's hand grew cramped and hurried:

*R. Sandoval, diver, attempted deposition before resident magistrate, re: conditions at Cossack station. Deposition quashed. Sandoval transferred. Wife deceased, childbirth, Feb. Child placed per company arrangement with R.S., payment of £9 recorded 11 Aug. Note: R.S. signed receipt under protest. Watch this one.*

Watch this one. Her father had watched him. And somewhere between February and August, a man who tried to testify about conditions at a station had been given his own dead wife's daughter back for nine pounds and a signature he'd made under protest — and then what? Then nothing. Then the trail went cold. Then a storm night, and a bundle on a step, and a note that said *forget the face.*

*Sando,* said Nell, tapping the page, pleased with herself. *Sando. Sando mine.*

"Not yours," Gideon whispered. Her mouth was dry. "Sandoval. Ramón Sandoval." She said the name aloud for the first time and the room seemed to lean in around it. "Is that your papa? Is Ramón your papa?"

Nell looked at her with those dark serious eyes that were too old for her face, and said, "Papa boat."

Papa boat. Papa went on a boat. Papa went on a boat and didn't come back, and somebody wrapped you in a blanket in a storm and carried you through the wet dark to the one door in the compound belonging to a dead man's daughter, and left you there with a book.

Gideon closed the ledger. Her hands were shaking, which annoyed her — fear was a luxury, fear cost breath — but they kept shaking anyway. She blew out the candle and held the child in the dark and thought: *I am holding evidence.* Not the ledger. Not the paper. The paper could be burned, torn, sunk, denied. But this warm breathing girl was a fact no fire could touch. Ramón Sandoval had signed a receipt for his own child under protest, and the child was alive, and the child remembered his name, and if anyone ever stood in a courtroom and asked her — no. They couldn't ask her. She was two. She could say *Sando* and *boat* and nothing else that would hold water under oath.

But she didn't have to hold water under oath. She only had to exist. A missing man and a delivered child and a nine-pound entry in a dead bookkeeper's hand — together they were a story, and stories were the one thing the Meridian Trading Company could not buy back once they started walking around on their own legs.

*Forget the face,* the note had said.

Too late, whoever you are. The face has a name now.

* * *

Hiding Nell had been simple for eleven days because hiding is mostly a matter of routine. Gideon rose before the bell, dressed Nell in the dark, fed her, and stowed her in the space beneath the floorboards behind the loose plank — a crawlspace meant for storing dive gear, dry enough, big enough for a toddler and a blanket and a clay doll Gideon had carved with her diving knife during a sleepless second night. Nell went in without crying now. She'd learned the game: *quiet game, quiet like fish.* Gideon hated herself for teaching it. A child who knew how to disappear was a child who'd been taught the world required it.

But after *Sando*, the arithmetic changed.

If Nell was Sandoval's daughter, then someone had brought her here deliberately. Not abandoned — *delivered*. Which meant someone knew Gideon. Someone knew her father, or her father's ledger, or both. The note's handwriting had been disguised, block letters, but the choice of her door wasn't random. Somebody had trusted her, or used her, or both, and either way that person might come back — and so might whoever was looking for them.

And the overseer, Mr. Quillan, had begun to notice.

It started small. He paused outside her door one morning as she swept the step, Nell's doll — stupidly, carelessly — visible on the windowsill behind her. Quillan was a narrow man with a narrow face and the patient eyes of a fisherman watching a line. He looked at the doll. He looked at Gideon.

"Getting domestic, Eriksen?"

"Borrowed it to mend for one of the Filipino women," Gideon said. The lie came out level. Nine years in the compound had taught her that too: lies, like breaths, counted best when you didn't rush them.

Quillan nodded slowly, the way he nodded at everything, and moved on, and Gideon swept the same patch of step for another full minute while her heart hammered and her mind ran the ledger's columns like a man counting money in the dark.

That afternoon the matron who managed the women's mess called her aside. "You're eating double rations," she said. Not accusing. Worse — curious.

"Growing," Gideon said, and gave her a flat look until the woman laughed and let it go. Growing. Nineteen years old and five foot four and done growing since fifteen. But the lie held, the way most lies held, because nobody expected a diver to be clever. That was the one gift the company had ever given her: its contempt. People didn't search pockets they didn't think existed.

Still. Double rations. A doll. A child who cried at night — Nell rarely cried, but rarely wasn't never, and barracks walls were paper. Eleven days was already eight days longer than luck usually lasted. Gideon began to plan the move she'd been avoiding: out of the barracks entirely, out to the shell heaps north of the station where the discarded shell piled up in white dunes twenty feet high, riddled with hollows and overhung with spinifex. Nobody went there. Nobody wanted to. It smelled of low tide and old death and the whole coast knew it.

She cached water there first, in tins, under a flat stone. Then a blanket. She told herself it was contingency — everyone should have a contingency — and did not tell herself that she was building a home for a child in a rubbish dump, because if she said it plainly she might hear what it meant, which was that she had already decided the child was hers to protect, and protection, on Meridian waters, was the most expensive thing a person could buy.

* * *

On the fifteenth day, Sefu almost ruined everything, and saved it instead.

He caught her at the tanks at dusk, rinsing Nell's spare cloth — a small thing, a scrap of flannel, but you don't rinse a single cloth at dusk unless it's all you dare wash. Sefu was the oldest diver on the station, a Karajarri man who'd worked the beds before the company came and worked them after, and he missed nothing that happened within sight of salt water. He leaned against the tank stand and looked at the flannel and looked at her.

"That's a small cloth," he said.

"It's a small stain."

"Mm." He was quiet a moment. Out past the jetty the water went gold and then grey, and somewhere down the beach the evening crew was hauling gear, and Gideon stood very still and calculated distances to lies.

"You've been buying sweet biscuits," Sefu said. "From the cook. Every week. A woman who eats sweet biscuits buys them for herself, unless—" He stopped. He didn't finish it. Instead he said, in Nyangumarta first and then in slow English, so there could be no mistake, "My sister hid a child once. In the mangroves, in the flood year. It lived."

Gideon's throat closed. She hadn't realized until that moment how badly she needed one person to know — not the ledger, not yet, not the whole shape of it, but one person to see the weight and not ask her to set it down.

"If you're wrong," she said, "you've said nothing."

"I've said nothing." Sefu pushed off the stand. "If I'm right — the cook owes me, and the matron owes me, and you owe me a biscuit." He walked off toward the drying sheds, unhurried, an old man going about an old evening, and Gideon stood at the tanks with wet hands and understood that her secret now had a circumference: herself, a dead man's handwriting, a toddler named for a great-aunt, and Sefu.

Small. Still small. But growing, the way everything dangerous grows.

* * *

That night she took the risk she'd been circling for a week. She lit the candle early, while Nell was awake, and sat with the girl between her knees and the ledger open on the floor, and she went through it page by page saying names aloud. Not for evidence. For something else — she wasn't sure what. Perhaps to teach the child her own history before the company taught her none. Perhaps because a room with two people in it felt less like drowning than a room with one.

"Okonkwo," she said, touching the ink. "Castellanos. Sandoval."

"Sando," said Nell, satisfied.

"Sandoval." Gideon turned pages. "Wu. Ferreira. Ah Chee." Some of the names were only initials, men reduced to letters by clerks who couldn't be bothered, or wouldn't. Some entries were sums only, deaths folded neatly into arithmetic: *loss, £4 replacement cost.* Four pounds. Her father had been worth four pounds to somebody, once, on paper, in June of 1883.

"Papa?" Nell asked, patting the page.

"No, love." Gideon hesitated. "Yes. Sort of. This book is — " How do you explain a ledger to someone who can't count past three? "This book remembers people."

"People," Nell agreed gravely, and put her whole palm flat on the page, covering four names at once, the way you'd cover sleeping birds to keep them safe.

Gideon looked down at the small brown hand on the small black writing and felt the thing she'd been refusing to feel all week finally arrive, whole and unavoidable: this was not a burden she was carrying. This was a person. The ledger was proof, yes, and proof was leverage and danger and duty, all the cold hard words. But Nell was the reason the proof mattered. Every name under that palm had been somebody's *mine* — somebody's daughter, somebody's papa-boat — and the company had weighed them and found them worth four pounds, nine pounds, whatever the sea allowed.

They won't weigh her, Gideon thought. Whatever else happens. Whatever it costs. They will not open a book and write a number next to this child.

She banked the candle, folded the ledger into its oilcloth, and slid it back beneath the floorboard beside the doll and the spare blanket — the beginnings, though she didn't name them so, of a household. Above the boards, Nell burrowed into her side, already asleep, one fist closed around Gideon's shirt collar like a small anchor.

Gideon lay awake listening to the tide turn, doing the arithmetic she hated: rations, glances, Quillan's patient eyes, the matron's curiosity, Sefu's knowing. Fifteen days of luck spent. Luck was like air in the helmet — it lasted exactly as long as it lasted, and the only question was whether you surfaced on purpose or were hauled up blue.

Three days later, at morning muster, Mr. Quillan posted a notice on the mess-room board, and Gideon read it twice while the other women shuffled past.

*By order of the Resident Engineer: all persons entering or leaving the compound after Sunday next will be subject to search. All irregular persons and effects to be declared. Rewards offered for information respecting undeclared goods or persons.*

Undeclared persons.

Quillan was standing by the board. When Gideon looked up, he was watching her, and he smiled the way a hook smiles coming out of the water.
