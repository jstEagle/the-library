## 7. Elias's Dilemma

The manifests arrived on the first Tuesday of the month, as they always did, bundled in brown paper and tied with the particular slovenly knot that meant the Meridian Trading Company's clerk had packed them in a hurry. Elias Ward cut the twine with his pocketknife, squared the pages against the desk, and began — as he had begun every month for two years — to look for the lie.

He found it at half past nine.

It was small. It was almost nothing. A shipment of shell logged out of the Meridian station on the fourteenth of March, weight recorded as one hundred and forty tons, and the freight charge calculated on one hundred and twenty-six. Fourteen tons of pearl shell, paid for by someone, shipped by no one, or shipped and never entered against the company's own account. A clerk's error, most likely. Clerks made errors the way the tide made foam — constantly, meaninglessly.

But clerks also made errors on purpose, when someone paid them to.

Elias set down his pen and looked out the window of the customs office at Broome's main street, where the heat stood up off the road in visible columns and a dray loaded with shell bags ground past toward the jetty. He thought about fourteen tons. He thought about what fourteen tons of shell was worth, and what it would buy, and who at Meridian might want something bought quietly.

Then he did what he always did with anomalies: he wrote it in his private notebook, in his own shorthand, and he said nothing to anyone.

His father had talked once. That was the whole lesson of Thomas Ward's life, distilled: he had talked, in 1879, to a commission of inquiry about short-weighted wool at the Fremantle yards, and he had been right, and it had not mattered. The commission had found nothing provable. The merchants had found everything provable about Thomas Ward — his debts, his drinking, a discrepancy in an expense account from six years earlier that no one could now disprove. Within eighteen months he was finished, unemployable, a man who sat in the parlor of the house in Fremantle while his son brought him the newspaper and watched him read the same column three times without turning the page. He died when Elias was nineteen, and the last coherent thing he said was: *Count twice before you speak, boy. They only need you wrong once.*

Elias had counted ever since. He counted so well that at thirty-one he was the youngest senior examining officer in the district, with a reputation for thoroughness that the trading houses complained about and respected, and a salary that let him rent a decent room near the office and send five pounds a quarter to his mother in Perth. He had ambition the way other men had a heartbeat — constant, unnoticed, sustaining. Senior examiner. Then inspector. Then, if the winds were fair, a posting south, a chair in some committee room in Perth where the ledgers of entire colonies came across his desk and men like his father's destroyers came to *him* hat in hand.

That was the arithmetic of his life, and it balanced, and it had balanced for years, and he intended it to go on balancing.

He picked up his pen again. And then, because the anomaly would not leave him — because fourteen tons kept sitting in his mind like a stone in a boot — he pulled the previous three months' manifests back across the desk and started looking for its brothers.

* * *

He found them after midnight, by lamplight, in the customs office with the shutters closed because the moths came in off the water otherwise and threw mad shadows over the figures. Three more discrepancies in as many months, each small, each plausible alone, each following the same pattern: shell shipped at weights that didn't reconcile, freight charged low, and always — always — in the weeks surrounding a visit from the government diving inspector.

Elias leaned back in his chair until the front legs lifted and the floor creaked. The pattern was elegant. It was almost beautiful. Shell went out unrecorded; the shortfall was absorbed into the wet-weight allowance, which varied with every cargo anyway; and the inspector's visits coincided because the inspector was being shown a clean house. Paid to see clean. Which meant someone above the station clerk was arranging it, which meant the clerk wasn't a fool but a functionary, which meant —

Which meant this went up the chain, and chains went up to Perth, and Perth went up to boards, and boards went up to names that appeared in newspapers next to photographs of racehorses and daughters' weddings.

He closed the notebook. His father's voice, count twice, they only need you wrong once. And underneath it, another voice, younger, his mother's, the winter after the funeral: *Your father wasn't wrong, Eli. Remember that. Being ruined isn't the same as being wrong.*

He blew out the lamp and sat in the dark listening to the sea work at the pilings under the town, and told himself he was merely gathering evidence. That there was no decision yet to make. That a good examiner documented; he did not act; acting was for magistrates and ministers and other men with better armor.

It was a comfortable sentence. He repeated it several times, the way you repeat anything you don't quite believe, and then he walked home through the warm dark past the Meridian offices, whose windows were dark but whose presence felt less like a building than like weather.

* * *

The inspection of the Meridian station was scheduled for the end of the month — quarterly, routine, a day of walking through drying sheds and counting shell bags while the overseer smiled and the divers stood in rows like implements. Elias had done it four times before. This time he went early, without notice, on the pretext of checking a consignment note, and caught the station at its ordinary hour: shell dust in the light, the smell of rot and salt, women hosing down the washing yard with their sleeves rolled.

He noticed her within ten minutes.

Not because she did anything. Because she *didn't*. Every other diver glanced at him — quick, assessing, the look of people calculating what a uniform meant for them today. She looked at him exactly once, and then away, and then — this was the thing — she arranged herself so that she could see him without appearing to. He'd seen it before in smugglers' wives and hotel keepers: the stillness of someone who has decided that attention is dangerous and is rationing hers to the drop.

She was young. Nineteen, maybe twenty. Broad-shouldered from the work, sun-browned, with a face that gave away nothing except tiredness, and even the tiredness seemed guarded, as though weariness were something she could be taxed for. Her contract tag said ERIKSEN, G., and the number beneath it, and Elias knew the name vaguely — a diver lost some years ago, father or husband, one of the drownings the company recorded in a single line each.

He watched her work for a while longer than the inspection required. She was methodical. She cleaned her gear with the economy of someone who owned nothing spare. When the overseer, Mr. Callaghan, came puffing along the walkway to attach himself to Elias's elbow, she turned her back with a smoothness so natural it took Elias a moment to realize it had been deliberate.

"Anything particular you're wanting to see, Mr. Ward?" Callaghan asked, wiping his neck. "We've got the new pump-house going in, if you'd care —"

"The manifest copies," Elias said. "I'll take them now, actually."

Something crossed Callaghan's face — not guilt, nothing so legible, just a flicker like a shadow of a bird over water. "Now? They'll be bound properly by Friday, we could —"

"Now is fine."

They were brought out in a canvas satchel, loose, untied. Elias counted them in front of Callaghan, pleasantly, slowly, letting the man watch him count. Twelve sheets. He weighed them in his hand. "You've a new clerk?"

"Borrowed one from the town office," Callaghan said. "Temporary. Young fellow."

"Mm." Elias signed for the documents and tucked the satchel under his arm. As he turned to go, he looked back once across the washing yard, an idle glance, an officer taking the measure of a station.

Gideon Eriksen was watching him. Not pretending otherwise this time. Just watching, level and unblinking, with the expression of someone doing arithmetic of her own.

He tipped his hat. She did not respond, and turned back to her work.

* * *

That night, cross-checking the station's manifest copies against the shipping returns in his office, Elias found the fourteenth anomaly — and this one was different. The others had been drift, erosion, shell leaking out at the edges. This one was a hole. A diver listed on the station roll, wages drawn for six months, no corresponding entry in any cargo tally for the period in which he'd supposedly worked. A man who existed on paper as labor and not at all as product.

The name was Okafor, J.

Elias wrote it down. He sat looking at it for a long time. Wages drawn by whom? He checked the pay authorization: countersigned by Callaghan. He checked the death register, because dead divers were the commonest explanation for such gaps — men drowned, their last months' accounts settled late. No entry. He checked the hospital returns from the mission. Nothing. A man who worked, was paid, produced nothing, died nowhere, and was buried in no record at all.

There were explanations. There were always explanations; that was the first thing fraud taught you, that it dressed itself in bookkeeping accidents. Clerks misfiled. Registers burned. Men left on other boats under other names.

But Elias had spent eleven years learning the texture of honest paperwork, and honest paperwork did not feel like this. This felt like a page torn cleanly out of a book and replaced with a page that had been aged by hand.

He thought of his father again — not the ruin this time but the beginning of it, the night before Thomas Ward gave his statement, when he'd paced the parlor and said, *If I hold my tongue I'm safe and they go on stealing, and if I speak I'm probably finished either way, because men like me are only ever believed when it costs the powerful nothing.* Elias had been fifteen and hadn't understood it. He understood it now with the whole weight of his career pressing down on him, because that was precisely his situation, inverted: he could document, and document, and deliver his neat notebook to his superiors, and his superiors would weigh it against Meridian's influence, and Meridian's influence was measured in tons and guineas while his was measured in a reputation for thoroughness. If he moved too soon, he'd be the junior officer who cried fraud without proof. If he moved too late, the paper trail would be tidied behind him, and he'd have nothing, and worse than nothing: he'd have announced himself.

Or — the third option, the one that sat in the room with him like a third man — he could close the notebook, initial the monthly summary *no irregularities noted*, and continue the climb. Plenty did. Most did. The system did not run on fools; it ran on reasonable men making reasonable calculations, and every reasonable calculation pointed the same direction.

He got up and poured himself the last inch of the bottle he kept for celebrations and emergencies, and couldn't tell which category tonight fell into.

Outside, a wind had come up from the northwest, carrying the smell of rain that wouldn't arrive, and somewhere down toward the native barracks a dog was barking at nothing. Elias stood at his window with the glass warming in his hand and thought about the woman at the station who watched without watching, who had looked at him with that flat measuring gaze as if he were not a man but a current in the water, something to be read and swum around.

Divers read currents because the current decides whether they live. He wondered what current she had read in him. He wondered why a contract diver — a girl indentured, property in all but law, with every reason to keep her head below the surface — should look at a customs officer as though he were either a door or a wall, and as though her life depended on knowing which.

It was none of his business. He told himself that plainly, the way you state terms to yourself before signing. His business was fourteen tons of shell and a man named Okafor who had been erased, and whatever else moved through that station was beneath his line and outside his warrant.

He finished the drink. In his notebook, beneath *Okafor, J.*, he wrote a date — the date of the next scheduled inspection — and beside it, after a long pause, a single word:

*Early.*

Then he put out the lamp, and lay in the dark listening to the wind come up off the water, and did not sleep for a long time. Somewhere out past the township, in the barracks he could not see, a woman was keeping a secret the size of a book, and the whole coast between them was silent as held breath.
