## 8. The Informer

Sefu found me at the washing line on the third morning after the storm, when I was pinning Nell's blanket to the wire and trying not to think about what slept folded in oilskin beneath my bunk.

He didn't say anything at first. Just stood there with his hands loose at his sides, watching me work the pegs the way he watched everything — patient, unhurried, a man who had learned that stillness kept you alive. The wind came off the water carrying the smell of rotting shell and brine, and somewhere down the beach the grinding wheels of the shell sheds turned and turned.

"You've changed," he said finally.

I didn't look at him. "The storm. Everything smells different after."

"That's not it." He picked up one of my wet shirts from the basket, held it like it might tell him something. "You used to walk through this camp like a woman counting her own footsteps. Now you count for two."

My hands went still on the pegs. That was the trouble with Sefu — he'd been diving since before I could swim, and men who survive twenty years underwater learn to read what moves on the surface.

"I don't know what you mean."

"No," he said mildly. "You don't."

* * *

He let it go that morning, which was its own kind of message. Sefu never let anything go; he simply set it down where he could find it again.

We worked the shallows that afternoon, the whole line of us strung along the reef flat while the lugger's tender idled nearby. The water was green glass, warm as bathwater near the surface, and I went down and came up and went down again, filling my basket with shell until my shoulders screamed. It was good, in a way, to be exhausted by something honest. My body understood this work. My body had always understood this work better than it understood anything else.

It was on the last dive, hanging in the blue while my lungs burned and the light above me shrank to a coin, that I understood what Sefu had been circling toward all day. Not suspicion exactly. Invitation.

He surfaced beside me as we swam for the tender, and he said it quietly, between breaths, so the other divers couldn't hear:

"Whatever you're carrying, Gideon — you shouldn't carry it alone."

I nearly swallowed seawater.

"Don't look at me like that," he said. "I've been nineteen once. I remember what fear looks like when it's living in someone's chest instead of passing through. Yours has moved in. Furnished the rooms."

"I don't—"

"Your door faces the yard," he said. "The yard faces the sea. And three nights ago, in the worst weather this coast has seen since your father died, somebody walked through both to leave something at your step. Somebody who knew which door. Somebody desperate." He pulled himself up the tender's ladder, then looked back down at me, water streaming off his grey-shot beard. "Desperate people leave things they can't keep. Things too heavy to hold and too dangerous to burn."

I climbed up beside him, saying nothing, and the tender carried us back to the jetty over water so clear I could see our shadows fleeing across the sand below.

* * *

That night I brought him into the barracks when the others were at the mess, and I showed him.

Not everything. One page — the same page I'd shown myself first, the one I'd gone back to again and again until I'd memorized it without meaning to. *J. Okafor, Station 4, lost at depth, no recovery attempted. Payment rendered to station overseer: £3.* Three pounds. My father's neat hand recording my father's worth.

Sefu read it twice. His lips moved slightly on the second pass, the way old men's do when reading is effort, and I realized I'd never actually seen him read before. Most of the divers couldn't. The company preferred them that way.

"Where did you learn writing?" I asked, because I needed to ask something, anything, that wasn't about the page.

"My mother was mission-taught. Zanzibar." He set the page down on my bunk with exaggerated care, as if it were an egg that might crack. "She said letters were the only cargo the ships ever carried that made people free or ruined them. Usually both." He looked up at me. "This is your father's hand."

"Yes."

"He wrote this?"

"The company gave him the ledger-keeping because of his English. He recorded their payments. Their losses." The word *losses* sat wrong in my mouth, the way it always did. "Their dead."

"And then he died in their waters," Sefu said, "and nobody recorded that."

The lamp flame shivered. Outside, the tide was turning, and the shell heaps at the edge of the compound creaked and settled against each other, mountains of empty shells the company would never bother grinding — millions of them, white as bone in the moonlight.

"There are names in there," I said. "Dozens. Divers sold between stations like stock. Inspectors paid off. Children—" I stopped. I thought of Nell asleep in the drawer-bed, her fist curled under her chin. "There are things in there the company hanged men for knowing less about."

Sefu was quiet for a long time. Then: "Who did you plan to give it to?"

"A magistrate. In town."

"Alone? Past the gate? Past the jetty?" He shook his head slowly. "Gideon. You are one girl with one book. They are a company with a country behind it."

"So I should burn it?"

"I didn't say that." He leaned forward, and his voice dropped to almost nothing. "I said you shouldn't carry it alone. There are eleven of us in this barracks who can't read but aren't fools. There are men on the luggers who owe the company more than the company knows. There is a cook at the station house who sees everything and says nothing because she has four children and no choices — but if she had a choice..." He spread his hands. "A thing like this isn't a weapon one person lifts. It's a net. Many hands, many knots."

"Why?" I asked. It came out harder than I meant. "Why risk it? You've survived longer than anyone here by keeping your head down. Everyone knows it. You taught half of us how to do it."

Something crossed his face then — old, tired, and very deep down, angry.

"Because I have been keeping my head down for thirty years," he said, "and the only thing I have to show for it is that I am still here to watch everyone else not be. A man can drown in that, too, Gideon. Slowly. Standing up." He touched the ledger page once, lightly, with one finger. "Let me help. Not to carry it — just to carry some of the watching. Two pairs of eyes see more than one. Four see more than two."

And God help me, I wanted to. That was the terrible truth of that night — I stood in my own barracks with proof of murder in my hands, and what I felt most, looking at Sefu's lined face, was relief. The relief of setting down a load I hadn't realized was crushing me.

"All right," I said. "Watching only. You don't touch it, you don't speak of it, you—"

"Gideon." He almost smiled. "I've hidden from better men than Meridian for three decades. Trust me to know how."

* * *

For six days, it worked.

Six days of ordinary life laid over the secret like clean linen over a wound. Sefu took the dawn watch outside the barracks without being asked, smoking his pipe on the step where any man approaching would pass him first. He found reasons to walk me past the overseer's office at shift change, his chatter easy and loud, covering the moments when my eyes wanted to dart. He even got Nell laughing one evening — a game with a shell and her toes — and I watched them together and thought: this is what it would be like, having family that chose you.

Nell was talking more now. Words coming loose one at a time, mostly nonsense, mostly *ba* and *da* and a fierce little *no* that she deployed with great authority. But sometimes, playing on the floor with her shells, she'd go quiet and say a soft word I couldn't place, and pat the floorboards, and look at the door.

On the sixth night, Sefu caught my arm as we came off the jetty.

"Someone was in the office today asking about mail," he said, low. "Not company business — a man from the shipping agent, going through the outward post. And the overseer had two of the house boys sweep the barracks store room. Twice." His pipe was unlit in his hand, which I'd learned meant trouble. "Somebody's nervous. Or somebody's sniffing."

"Do you think—"

"I don't think anything yet. Thinking's for when we know." But his eyes went to the barracks, to my window, and stayed there a moment too long.

* * *

They came for the search on the seventh night, and Sefu's watching saved nothing, because there was nothing to watch — the report had already been made.

It was Quillan, the assistant overseer, a thin man with a voice like a hinge, and he brought four company police with lanterns. They went barrack to barrack. We heard them before they reached ours: boots on coral gravel, the scrape of trundle-beds being dragged, a woman crying out as her tin box was overturned and her dead husband's letters scattered across the floor.

"They're looking for a bundle," Quillan announced to our room, holding up a paper. "An anonymous report. Suspicious bundle, concealed property, contraband correspondence. Anyone found holding undeclared papers will be charged under contract clause fourteen."

Clause fourteen. Theft of company intelligence. I'd seen a man flogged for less.

I stood at attention with the others while they tore through our things, and Nell woke and started to cry, and I held her against my hip and bounced her and stared straight ahead at the wall and did not let my eyes move one inch toward the space beneath my bunk.

Where there was nothing.

Nothing, because forty minutes earlier, Sefu had appeared at my door with a torn sleeve and a story about a splinter, and while he'd held my arm and talked loudly about the infirmary and the state of his hands, his other hand had slipped the oilskin parcel out from under my bunk and into the hollow of his coat, and he had walked it out of the barracks and away into the dark without breaking stride.

So they found nothing. Quillan searched my mattress slats, my sea chest, the lining of my spare suit, my hair — a gloved finger raking through it while I held Nell and hated him with a purity I'd never managed before. Then he stood in the middle of the wrecked room, unsatisfied, chewing on his disappointment.

"Someone sent that report," he said, to the room, to me. "Which means someone saw something. And whoever it is will see something again." He pointed his rolled paper at me, almost casually. "Eriksen. Your father was a record-keeper. Clever families run clever habits. I'll be looking at you."

They left. The women put their rooms back together in silence. Nell cried herself hoarse against my neck, and I sang to her, the low diving song my father used to hum, and over her head I caught Sefu's eye across the barracks and he gave me a nod so small it might have been a trick of lamplight.

But I didn't sleep. Because the parcel was still out there, in Sefu's keeping, in a camp full of frightened people, one of whom had written a report tonight — had seen something, or claimed to, or wanted the overseer distracted from something else entirely. And every face I turned over in my mind — the cook, the lugger men, the women in my own room, Sefu himself — wore suddenly two possible expressions at once, and I could no longer tell which was real.

Trust and betrayal, I thought, staring at the rafters. Same breath.

At first light I went to Sefu, and he took me without a word across the compound's back fence, out past the grinding sheds to the shell heaps — those vast white hills of discarded shell that rose between the compound and the mangroves, higher than the roofs, older than most of the workers. He climbed the nearest heap with the ease of long practice and, halfway up, pressed aside a slab of broken mother-of-pearl the size of a hatch cover.

Behind it, a cavity. Dry, dark, invisible from any angle unless you already knew.

Inside lay the oilskin parcel, wedged among the shells, safe as a heart in a ribcage.

"Nobody searches the heaps," Sefu said. "Too big. Too many. And the company thinks of them as rubbish." He settled the slab back into place and it vanished — just another gleam of nacre among ten million. "Only you and I know. Not even the cook. Not anyone. You understand me, Gideon? From tonight, whatever happens, whoever asks, whoever bleeds — you and I never spoke of it at all."

I looked out over the heaps, white and silent in the early sun, stretching toward the mangroves like a field of graves with no names. Somewhere under all that shell, the only honest record on this coast lay buried among the refuse of the trade that killed its writers.

"Then why show me where it is?" I asked.

Sefu lit his pipe at last, and the match flared and died in the wind.

"Because tomorrow," he said, "they arrest me."
