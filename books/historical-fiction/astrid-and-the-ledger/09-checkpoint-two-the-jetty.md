## 9. Checkpoint Two: The Jetty

Gideon

The note came folded inside a fish wrapped in banana leaf, left on the barracks step by a boy who ran before she could thank him. Sister Bernadette. St. Brigid's. The second Thursday of each month she takes the mail boat to town for supplies and returns by noon. If there is something that must travel, bring it to the jetty at half past seven. She will be wearing grey.

Second Thursday of the month. That was tomorrow.

Gideon sat on her bunk with the fish untouched in her lap and let the words settle over her like cold water. A nun. An actual sister of the Church, with a habit and a boat ticket and no reason on earth to be searched as thoroughly as a Meridian diver. It was the first door that had opened since Sefu's arrest, and doors like that did not stay open long.

She thought about Sefu in the lockup, thinking about his hands around a tin mug he'd never get to drink from, and made herself stop. Grief was a current. You didn't fight it; you used it to move.

The ledger had lived in the shell heaps for eleven days now, wrapped in oilskin inside a hollowed shell midden that stank so powerfully of rot that even the overseer's dogs gave it distance. Every night she'd told herself: tomorrow I move it further. Every night fear had answered: it's safer where it is. But safer was a lie divers told themselves. Nothing was safe. There was only moving and being moved, breathing and being breathed for.

Tomorrow, then. Half past seven. Grey habit.

She took Nell onto her knee. The girl was two now, or nearly — Gideon had decided her birthday would fall in the wet season, because every child deserved a birthday and Nell had never been given one — and she was chewing the corner of a rag doll Gideon had sewn from an old diving sleeve.

"Going to see a lady," Gideon told her. "A lady in grey."

"Lady," Nell agreed, with the solemn authority of someone who understood exactly nothing and everything.

* * *

Elias

The order came down from Inspector Halloway himself, which was the first thing wrong with it. Halloway did not issue orders; he issued suggestions shaped like favors, and you paid for them later in ways you couldn't itemize. But this one was plain enough: search the jetty Thursday morning, all departing passengers, all cargo. Company business. Discreetly done.

Discreetly. Elias turned the word over twice before he recognized what it actually meant. Not thorough. Quiet enough not to offend anyone who mattered.

He went anyway, because refusing Halloway was a career ended in a single afternoon, and because — though he hated admitting it — some part of him had begun keeping its own ledger. Three weeks of anomalies in the manifests. Shell weights logged at Broome that didn't match the tonnage received in Singapore. Deaths recorded as "losses" with no cause listed, no wage settlement, no next of kin notified, which was itself illegal under the indenture acts if anyone cared to enforce them. Nobody did. That was rather the point.

He stood at the rail of the customs walkway above the jetty and watched the morning build itself out of mist and rope-creak. The mail boat sat low at her moorings, taking on crates. A handful of passengers waited on the planks: two Chinese merchants, a pearling master's clerk with a leather case chained to his wrist, and —

He saw her before he fully registered seeing her, which was how it always was now. Gideon Eriksen stood near the far bollard with a child balanced on her hip, and she was doing the thing she always did, the thing that had snagged his attention the first day he'd inspected the station: she watched everything while appearing to watch nothing. Her eyes touched the clerk's chained case, the crate tally, the two constables loitering by the gangway, and came back to rest on her own hands as if hands were all she had ever thought about.

The child was new. He'd seen her once before through the station fence, carried on that same hip. Children weren't permitted at the jetty without a pass. Technically.

He descended the stairs.

* * *

Gideon

She heard his boots on the planks and knew them before she looked up. Of course. Of course it would be Ward. The one man at the checkpoints who looked at people instead of past them, assigned to the exact morning, the exact stretch of timber where she needed to be invisible.

Sister Bernadette stood at the gangway in her grey habit, checking her ticket with the unhurried patience of a woman who had outlived three bishops. Gideon had passed her the bundle thirty seconds ago — ledger, oilskin, wrapped in altar linen the sister had brought for the purpose, looking for all the world like vestments bound for repair in Perth. The handoff had taken less time than handing someone a hymn book. Clean. Done.

Except now the customs officer was coming down the stairs with his notebook already open, and between him and the gangway there was nowhere to go but the water.

Keep your head down. Count your breaths. Her father's rules. She shifted Nell's weight and became, with the whole of her will, a tired diver with a sick child hoping for the mission doctor in town.

"Morning." Ward stopped an arm's length away. Up close he had the kind of face that gave nothing away on principle, and eyes that gave everything to anyone patient enough to read them. "Traveling?"

"The child's unwell. Sister's arranged passage for us both."

"Passage." He wrote something. Or mimed writing something; from this angle she couldn't tell. "I don't have you on my list."

"I don't imagine you have anyone's name. I've watched you check four people and write nothing legible once."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Suppressed. "Company property requires a release chit to leave station grounds," he said. "Even small company property." His gaze flicked to Nell, and Gideon felt the moment tilt — because Nell, being two years old and constitutionally incapable of silence, chose that instant to say, loudly and clearly:

"Lady!"

Ward looked at the child. At the grey habit already halfway up the gangway. Back at Gideon.

"You know the sister?"

"She's burying my father's memory properly, if that's a crime. The mission took up a collection."

It wasn't quite a lie. It also wasn't anything close to true, and she watched him decide — visibly, the way a man decides whether to step on or off a plank — whether it mattered. The tide sucked against the pilings below. Somewhere behind them the overseer's clerk was shouting a tally.

Then Ward closed his notebook.

"No chit, no problem today," he said. "Mission travel's exempt. Tell the sister the customs service wishes her safe passage." And then, quieter, in a voice pitched for her alone: "Mind the swell off the point. Water's high for the season."

High water. She knew what high water meant on the old channel. Everyone knew. It was either a warning or a trap, and she could not for the life of her tell which, and he was already walking away, boots measured on the planks, pausing only to inspect a crate of dried fish with the thorough boredom of a man doing exactly his job.

She got Nell up the gangway on legs that felt borrowed.

At the top she risked one look back. Ward stood at the rail of the customs walkway, watching the boat, not the water, not the manifest, not his notebook. Watching her. And when their eyes met across forty feet of morning air, he did not look away, and neither did she, and neither of them smiled, and it was the most honest conversation Gideon had ever had with anyone she could not trust.

* * *

Elias

He let the mail boat cast off and stood at the rail until the grey habit and the woman and the child were small enough to blur into the glare, and then he stood longer than that, which told him things about himself he wasn't ready to inventory.

He opened the notebook. On the page where he should have recorded the inspection, he found what his own hand had written during those thirty seconds at the gangway, in his smallest, most deniable script:

E. — mission passage — exempt.

No surname. No child noted. No bundle noted, though the woman had held her arm oddly against her side when the child shifted, the way a person holds something when both arms are occupied and only one thing can be dropped.

He knew what that posture meant. He'd spent six years learning what bodies said at checkpoints, because bodies were the only witnesses that never lied to protect themselves. She'd been carrying something. She might still be carrying something. The bundle could have gone up the gangway ahead of her — into the sister's luggage, into the linen, anywhere.

Or it hadn't, and he was inventing crimes out of a woman's elbow.

Halloway appeared beside him with the particular lightness of a man arriving precisely when he wanted to be seen arriving. "Quiet morning."

"Quiet enough."

"The Eriksen woman. Diver. You spoke to her some time."

"Checked her papers."

"Hm." Halloway watched the boat diminish. "Company's asked that we keep particular attention on the mission traffic going forward. Nothing specific. A feeling." He smiled with all the warmth of a ledger line. "You know how feelings are."

Elias knew exactly how feelings were. He had one right now, and it was telling him that Halloway had known about this morning before Elias had, that the order to search the jetty had been less an order than a test with the answer key already written, and that somewhere in the machinery of the Meridian Trading Company a gear had turned and his name had come up on the other side of it.

"A feeling," he repeated. "I'll keep it in mind."

"Do." Halloway clapped his shoulder once and left, and Elias stayed at the rail with the wind coming off the water and the taste of the whole thing turning slowly in his mouth like brine.

That evening he pulled the manifests again. Found the anomaly he'd been circling for three weeks, the one he'd told himself was a clerical error: eleven names, over fourteen months, entered as losses, each within a fortnight of a payment entry to the same account. He copied the page into his notebook in his smallest script and sat looking at it until the lamp burned low.

Eleven names. Eleven payments. One account.

And a woman at a jetty holding her arm like that, lying to him fluently and badly, while a nun in grey sailed for Perth with whatever it was the Meridian Trading Company would burn a jetty full of men to recover.

He didn't know yet what he'd do with any of it. He only knew, with the certainty of a man stepping off a marked channel into deep water, that he had already chosen. He just hadn't told himself yet.

* * *

Gideon

Perth was three days' sail. Gideon counted every one of them the way she counted breaths underwater — not because counting helped, but because stopping was unthinkable.

Nell slept against her chest in the mission bunk while rain worked the roof, and Gideon lay awake doing arithmetic of a different kind: the quarterly audit was nineteen days out. The copy was moving, but the original still lay in a stinking shell heap guarded only by rot and luck, and the man from customs knew she'd been carrying something — she'd seen it land in him like a hook, seen him choose not to set it.

Why? Men didn't let fish off hooks out of kindness. Either he wanted her to run so he could follow, or—

Or.

She turned the thought over carefully, the way you turn a shell to see if there's pearl in it. He'd warned her about the water. High for the season. You didn't say that to a suspect. You said that to someone whose drowning you'd rather prevent.

In the morning the sister found her on deck and pressed a folded paper into her hand without breaking stride. Inside, in a firm convent hand: *Received and hidden. God sees the columns too. Come to me when you need a door.*

Gideon folded it small and put it where the ledger used to live, against her ribs, and watched the coast of home slide past — the smoke of the station, the shell heaps, the jetty shrinking to a splinter.

Nineteen days. One original. One man whose allegiance hung somewhere between his oath and whatever he'd seen in her face on the gangway.

When the mail boat came back, she decided, she would be standing on it. And she would find out which way Elias Ward fell — before the company did it for her.
