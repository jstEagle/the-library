## 10. What Elias Knows

The summons came through channels so ordinary they were almost insulting. A clerk's note, folded twice, left on his desk at the customs house between one tide report and the next: *Meridian station, Thursday, manifest discrepancy, follow up with station manager.* No name attached to the discrepancy. No hint of what had been noticed, or by whom.

Elias read it three times and understood it for what it was — not an instruction but a test. Someone upstairs wanted him back at the Meridian station, and wanted it done quietly, and wanted a paper trail that would show diligence rather than suspicion. He thought about the shipping manifests he'd been pulling apart for six weeks, the tonnages that didn't reconcile, the shell weights that shrank between the jetty scale and the Broome office ledger as if the sea itself were skimming a percentage.

He thought, too, about Gideon Eriksen's eyes on him the last time he'd walked those barracks rows. Not the flat deference every diver gave an inspector — the lowered gaze, the stillness, the performance of being nobody. Something sharper underneath. A woman counting his steps. A woman who knew what he was there for before he did.

*Thursday,* the note said. Elias decided he would go Wednesday instead.

* * *

He arrived an hour after first light, when the mist was still lifting off Roebuck Bay and the divers were moving down to the water in their slow, shuffling line, lugging the heavy boots and helmets that would carry them to the bottom of the world. The station in morning was almost beautiful — the long sheds, the mounds of shell gleaming bone-white, the luggers swinging at their moorings like patient animals. Almost. Elias had learned young that the prettiest operations were usually the ones spending the most on paint.

The station manager, a soft-bellied man named Craddock whose handshake felt like gripping a wet rope, met him at the office door with too much warmth and two cups of tea already poured.

"Inspector Ward. Good of you to come early." Craddock's smile showed all his teeth and none of his pleasure. "Though I wasn't expecting you till tomorrow."

"Discrepancies don't wait," Elias said, and took the tea because refusing it would be its own message.

They went through the manifests for two hours. It was theater, both of them knowing it. Craddock produced documents in tidy bundles, each figure accounted for, each explanation smooth as oiled timber. Weather losses. Scale calibration. Human error, regrettable but inevitable. Elias marked three entries with his pencil and said nothing, watching Craddock's hands instead of his face. The man's fingers went still whenever the *Kestrel*'s cargo figures came up. Just still. Half a second of stillness, like a clock catching on a tooth.

By noon Elias had what he'd come for, which was confirmation that Craddock feared the *Kestrel*, and something else besides — a glimpse through the office window, while Craddock fetched a second set of books, of the overseer Voss walking a woman toward the store shed. Not marching her. Walking beside her, close, the way a man walks with someone he is questioning without witnesses.

The woman was small and dark-haired and she moved like the water moved — no wasted motion, nothing offered free. Gideon Eriksen.

Elias watched until they passed out of sight behind the shell heaps, and found that his chest had gone tight in a way he refused to examine.

* * *

She was at the washing lines behind the barracks when he found her, late afternoon, pegging dive suits with the economy of someone who had done ten thousand small chores and expected to do ten thousand more. The other women had drifted toward the cookhouse. The wind carried the smell of brine and boiling rice and, faintly, the sweet rot of shell discarded too long in the heaps.

"Miss Eriksen."

She didn't startle. That was the first thing. She finished the peg in her hand, smoothed the suit's shoulder, and only then turned, her face arranged into the polite blankness he remembered from the gate inspection.

"Inspector."

"You know my name."

"You're the man from Customs. Everyone knows the man from Customs." Her voice was even, uninflected. "It comes with the job."

"It does." He stepped closer, stopped at a respectful distance, and let the silence do some work. Around them the wind worried the washing lines; a gull argued with something in the heaps. "I'm going to ask you a question, and I'd take it as a kindness if you considered whether answering it costs you anything."

She waited. She was good at waiting. He'd seen men crack under less patience than hers.

"Three weeks ago I inspected your station and you watched me the way a person watches weather coming in. Last week the *Kestrel*'s manifests came through my office four tons light, and this morning your station manager couldn't keep his hands quiet when I mentioned it." He kept his voice low, conversational, the tone of a man discussing freight rates. "I don't believe in coincidence, Miss Eriksen. So here's the question. Is there something at this station you want found?"

Something moved behind her eyes — quick, submerged, gone. Like a shape under water.

"No, Inspector," she said.

"No," he repeated. "That's what I thought you'd say. Here's the trouble with that answer." He glanced toward the cookhouse, then back. "If there's nothing, you'll say it too quickly and sleep tonight. If there's something, you'll say it exactly the way you just did — carefully, correctly, like a woman reciting a lesson she's determined not to flunk." He paused. "You said it the second way."

For a long moment she simply looked at him, and Elias felt the strange sensation of being measured by instruments better than his own. Then she unpinned another suit from the line, folded it over her arm, and said, quietly:

"There's a place past the third shell heap where the wind doesn't reach. If a person wanted to talk to me without being seen standing next to me, that's where a person would stand. Alone. In about ten minutes."

And she walked off toward the barracks with the folded suit under her arm, not hurrying, not looking back, leaving him holding the conversation like a man left holding a rope that might be anchored to anything.

* * *

The spot past the third heap was a trough between mounds of shell, white and glittering even in the failing light, sharp-edged enough to slice a careless boot. The shells whispered against each other in the wind with a dry sound like distant applause. Elias stood there alone for nine minutes feeling like a fool, and in the tenth minute heard the small slide of footsteps and she was there, having approached along the far side where the heaps blocked any sightline from the barracks.

"You came," she said.

"I had ten minutes."

"You had a choice." She stood an arm's length away, arms crossed, and the last light caught the salt-cracked skin of her hands, the diver's calluses, the fine white scars on her forearms where coral had taken its tax. "Before you waste them, Inspector, let's understand each other. You suspect me of something. I suspect you of wanting something. Only one of us can afford to be wrong."

"And which one is that?"

"Me." No hesitation. "You lose a promotion. I lose—" she paused, chose, "—more than that. So before I say one more word, you tell me why you're really here. Not the manifest nonsense. Why you pulled the *Kestrel*'s records six weeks running when any other officer in Broome would've stamped them and gone to lunch."

Elias considered lying. It was his profession, more or less — the polite fictions of inspection, the dance of plausible figures. But something about the flatness of her attention made lying feel like bringing a knife to a depth-sounding.

"My father," he said, and heard his own voice go quieter than he intended. "He ran a chandlery in Fremantle. Supplied two of the pearling fleets. When he found irregularities in one company's accounts — this would have been twenty years ago, I was nine — he wrote to the owners. Politely. Thoroughly. With columns and evidence, the way he did everything." The shells whispered. Somewhere out on the water a lugger's lamp was coming up, small and yellow. "Within a year he had no fleet, no credit, no chandlery. Within two he had no heart, if you follow me. Died believing he'd made an arithmetic error somewhere." He looked at her. "There was no error. There never is. That's why I pull records six weeks running. Because somebody has to count, and most somebodies would rather not."

Gideon said nothing for a moment. The wind pushed a lock of hair across her face and she didn't bother with it.

"My father died in company waters," she said at last. "Officially, a shallow-water blackout. Unofficially, they sent him down on a fouled air line because the shell was rich and the season was short." Her voice didn't change register at all, which somehow made it worse. "I was twelve. They docked his final wages against the cost of recovering the body. I signed my indenture papers at fourteen because the alternative was the mission home, and the mission home sends girls to stations further inland, and I have lived near water my whole life and I will not live away from it."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be. Be accurate." She uncrossed her arms. "Now. You asked if there's something here you should find. There is. And I'm going to show you a piece of it, and then we're both going to stand very still and see what we are to each other. Understood?"

"Not entirely."

"That's all right. Neither am I."

She reached into the front of her blouse and brought out a single sheet of paper, folded to the size of a playing card, worn soft at the creases from carrying. Even before she opened it Elias's pulse changed. Paper like that didn't survive in a barracks. Paper like that was protected, hoarded, slept beside.

She held it where the last light could reach it and unfolded it once, showing him one page only, her thumb covering the upper margin.

Columns. Names — or initials and numbers, coded. Dates. Figures in pounds, shillings, pence. And running down the right-hand side, in a cramped, disciplined hand, annotations in a different ink: *paid — J.V., per arrangement.* *Loss recorded, no inquiry requested.* *Third payment, hold remainder.*

And at the foot of the page, a signature.

Elias read the signature and felt the blood leave his face in a slow, cold tide, the way he imagined it must feel to surface too fast and watch the water go dark at the edges of vision.

*H. Ashcombe.*

Not a name from the ledgers of some rival company. Not a stranger. Henry Ashcombe, Collector of Customs, Port of Broome. His superior. The man who had signed Elias's own commission. The man who had, six weeks ago, personally reassigned him to "routine inspections" and then, three days ago, sent the clerk's note arranging this very visit — the visit designed to establish, on paper, that Ward had looked and found nothing.

The page trembled slightly. He realized the trembling was in his hand.

"Where," he said, and his voice came out sanded down to bare timber, "did you get this."

"A dead man wrote it. Another dead man countersigned it." She refolded the paper with great care, as though it were something living that could be bruised. "There are more pages. Forty-one more. Bribes logged. Divers recorded as 'losses' — equipment losses, Inspector, that's the term, we're entered on the same sheets as helmets and air pumps. Children moved between stations with payments on either end." She met his eyes. "One of those children is asleep in my bunk right now."

The wind rose a little, and the shell heaps sang their dry applause, and Elias Ward stood in the white trough between mountains of profit and understood that his life had just divided into two parts, the way a channel divides around a reef, and that he was going to have to choose one side of it in the next thirty seconds and swim in it for the rest of his life.

Report her. It was the simple course. Take the page, arrest the diver, write the report Ashcombe was clearly hoping for, climb the ladder on a rung made of her silence. Every ambition he had — senior inspector, a transfer south, a name that opened doors — lay along that path like lamps lit in windows.

Or.

Or take the page, give it back, walk out of this shell trough a conspirator, and spend whatever remained of his career looking over his shoulder at the man who owned his signature.

"You understand what you've done," he said. "Showing me this. If I'm the wrong kind of man—"

"You're not."

"You can't know that."

"Yes I can." She said it without drama, the way she seemed to say everything, facts laid down like shell on a scale. "A wrong kind of man would've taken the page from my hand already. Or pretended he saw nothing and left fast. You've been standing here doing sums. Wrong men don't do sums. Wrong men are already certain."

He almost laughed. It came out as breath. "You've had me weighed and measured since the gate, haven't you."

"Since the gate," she agreed.

He looked at the folded paper in her hand, at the signature he now carried behind his eyes where it would stay forever — *H. Ashcombe*, in ink that had dried years ago and was still wet with consequence.

"The quarterly audit," he said. "When is it."

"Eleven days. After the audit, loose papers burn. That's how it works. That's how it's always worked."

"Then the original has to reach a magistrate before the audit. Not a copy — Corrigan will want the book itself, the whole thing, chain of custody, dates, binding. A loose page is an allegation. A bound ledger is a case." He was thinking aloud now, fast, the habit of manifests and tonnage tables repurposed into something that felt dangerously like hope. "There are three checkpoints between this station and Corrigan's chambers. The gate. The jetty. The telegraph office. Ashcombe watches all three."

"I know," Gideon said. "I've been counting."

Of course she had. Of course she'd been counting since before he arrived, probably since the night the ledger came into her hands. He looked at this woman who had survived the bottom of the sea by keeping her head down and was now, for reasons she hadn't fully explained and he hadn't fully asked, holding her head up into the worst possible weather, and he felt the choice close around him like water closing over a helmet.

"Give me the page," he said.

Her chin lifted a fraction. Guarded. Ready.

"If I'm going to betray my superior," Elias said, "I need to know his handwriting intimately. All of it. Every entry, every date, every initial. I can't help you smuggle a book I haven't read."

For the first time since he'd known her, Gideon Eriksen smiled. It was a small thing, quickly governed, but it reached her eyes, and Elias understood that he had just been given something rarer than trust — a first installment of it, advanced against everything that was coming.

"Ten minutes past full dark," she said. "The old net shed, north side. Bring nothing. Follow nothing. And Inspector—" she paused at the edge of the trough, the shell light glinting off her, half sea-creature, half ghost, "—the child's name is Nell. If you're going to be in this, you should know what it's for."

She slipped away between the heaps, and Elias stood alone in the whispering white, holding one folded page and the knowledge that there was no longer any version of his life that did not contain her.

Out on the bay, the *Kestrel* rode at anchor, riding low in the water.

Four tons light. Eleven days.

He turned toward the north side of the station and began to walk.
