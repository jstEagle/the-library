## 11. Love Against Duty

**Gideon**

The third time Elias Ward climbed the ladder behind the drying sheds, Gideon stopped pretending she hadn't been waiting for him.

She sat on an upturned shell crate with Nell asleep against her shoulder, and when his head came above the roofline — hat off, hair flattened by wind, eyes doing their quick arithmetic of the dark — she said, "You're late."

"The tide was late. I only borrowed its excuse." He dropped onto the crate beside her, close enough that his knee touched hers. In three weeks of meetings he had learned exactly how much closeness the darkness allowed. "The nun's boat sails Thursday."

"I know. Sefu heard it from the jetty boys." She felt him go still at the name. They'd had this argument twice already. "He doesn't know what we carry. He knows something's carried. There's a difference, and in Broome there has to be, or nobody breathes."

"Gideon—"

"You want a plan with no people in it. Plans don't work like that here." Nell stirred; Gideon rocked her without thinking, a motion so practiced it cost nothing now. "Thursday. The *Star of Broome*. Sister Bernadette takes mail and medicine down the coast, and no one searches a woman of God because searching her would be admitting what we all pretend not to know about how things move through this port."

Elias turned his hat in his hands. In the lamplight leaking from the shed seams she could see the line between his brows that appeared whenever duty and sense pulled opposite directions. She was beginning to know his face too well. That was the danger she never said aloud.

"Manifests," he said instead. "I've been reading manifests for six weeks, and every fourth entry on Meridian shell shipments is signed by Inspector Calloway without inspection dates. A man can't inspect forty tons of shell on a day he spent in Derby. Someone upstairs is rubber-stamping the company's paper, and Calloway answers directly to—" He stopped.

"Say it."

"If this goes wrong, it won't be the overseer who buries us. It'll be the man who signs my pay."

"Then we make sure it doesn't go wrong." She shifted Nell higher. "Tell me about the telegraph office again."

He told her again. The clerk on nights was a man named Prewitt who drank. The operator's room locked at ten. The wire ran south along the pole road where a constable walked his beat at eleven and again at one. The back door opened onto an alley that smelled of fish guts and was watched by nobody because nobody wanted to stand in it.

"And the original?" Elias asked, though they both knew.

"The original goes to Corrigan in person. Copies go down the wire. If they catch one, they don't catch both." She recited it flatly, the way she counted breaths before a dive. Thirty words, then hold. "You take the book. I draw the eyes."

"No."

"Yes."

"Gideon." His voice dropped low, and something in it made her turn her head. "If they take you, there's no magistrate in this colony who'll weigh your word against Meridian's. You're indentured. Your testimony is worth less than the paper you'd swear it on. Mine at least buys a hearing first."

"Your word buys a cell with a window. Mine buys the channel floor." She said it without heat, which somehow made it worse; she saw him flinch. "That's why it has to be me drawing the eyes, Elias. They watch me already. Let them watch me walking somewhere loud and obvious while you walk quiet."

"That's not strategy, that's arithmetic with your life as the remainder."

"What else have I got to spend?"

* * *

**Elias**

He lay awake in his quarters above the customs house and did sums he couldn't finish.

Six weeks ago Gideon Eriksen had been a problem: a diver who looked at him too carefully during an inspection, whose stillness had the quality of a held breath rather than peace. He'd written her name in his notebook and underlined it. Now her name lived in a different part of his mind entirely, the part that kept track of things worth losing.

It was unprofessional. Worse, it was imprudent. An officer of customs conducting a private inquiry into his own inspector's signatures, meeting after dark with an indentured diver, planning — there was no softer word for it — smuggling. If Calloway ever traced the anomaly in the manifests back through the jetty morning, through the nun's boat, through the girl with the child, Elias would hang beside her. Or worse: the sea took people quietly here, and companies paid for quiet.

And yet.

He thought of his father, thirty years gone now, ruined for asking one honest question about one dishonest cargo. His father had died poor and certain, which Elias had always considered the worse trade. Ambition had carried Elias into the service and conscience had kept him mediocre at it, and here at last the two were pointed the same direction for once, and he could not even enjoy it because pointing them together meant aiming at himself.

She had shown him one page. One. Her father's hand recording payments to men Elias knew by their office furniture. He had gone pale, she'd said afterward, like a man seeing his own signature on a confession. She hadn't been wrong.

Tonight at the sheds she'd asked him why he stayed. He'd given her the answer about duty, about finishing what honest men start. It was true as far as it went. What he hadn't said was simpler: that watching her refuse to be afraid had made his own fear look like a choice, and he was no longer willing to choose it.

There was also the smaller truth, the one he handled carefully even alone in the dark. When she laughed — rare, quick, always slightly surprised at itself — he forgot the manifests. When she put Nell's small hand against his palm and said *hold still, she likes you*, he forgot his career. When she talked about the ledger's names, her voice went low and level like a diver conserving air, and he understood that he loved a woman who measured love the same way she measured everything: by what it could carry.

Thursday, then. The nun's boat for the copies' route confirmation. And after that, the wire.

He got up and lit the lamp and took out the manifest pages he'd copied in his own hand — evidence against himself as much as anyone — and began arranging them in order. If he was going to drown, he intended to surface holding something.

**Gideon**

Sundays the station emptied. Half the divers walked to the mission beach, half slept off the week, and the overseer played cards in town until the lamp oil gave out. Sunday was theirs.

They met in different places each week — the wreck of the old lugger at low tide, the mangrove cut, once, memorably, the loft above the cooperage where they'd had to whisper over the smell of pitch. This Sunday it was the far end of Cable Beach where the sand held the day's heat long after dark, and Nell ran shrieking at the ghost crabs while two adults sat close and committed treason.

"Prewitt drinks arrack, not beer," Elias said, sketching in the sand with one finger. "By nine he's careless. By ten he's asleep at his desk with the outer door unlocked because locking it means standing up. We send the copies between ten and eleven, before the constable's walk."

"Who sends them? Not you. Your hand is known at the office."

"A telegram needs no signature until the account settles. I'll open an account under a shipping agent's name — there's a real agent, dead six months, whose name still appears in the directory. Messages will read as routine charter inquiries. Perth address buried inside commercial noise." He drew a rectangle, tapped it. "Newspaper's telegraphic address looks like any other firm."

Gideon watched his finger in the sand and thought: *he has planned this like a man planning to survive it*, and then thought, immediately after: *he has planned it like a man who does not expect to.* Both thoughts were true. She'd learned that people could hold both.

"Nell," she called, and the child came pelting back with a crab leg held aloft like a scepter. Elias lifted her onto his knee without being asked, and Nell, who trusted almost nothing and everyone slowly, leaned back against his coat and began to narrate her crab campaign in a language only she spoke.

"She'll need schooling," Elias said quietly.

"She'll need reading. Everything else grows out of that." Gideon looked out at the water, black and patient. "My father taught me letters from a prayer book he stole. Said a person who can read owns the only property that can't be searched." She stopped. She had never told him about her father beyond the fact of the ledger.

Elias didn't push. That was another thing she was learning about him — he waited out silences the way she waited out tides. After a while he said, "After Thursday, how long to prepare the wire run?"

"Two weeks. Maybe three. The audit's coming; papers are burning already; we can't wait for perfect." She paused. "There's something else. Sefu's been asking questions. Good ones. He guessed about the jetty morning — not the book, but that I moved something past someone. He wants in."

"Gideon—"

"He carried my father's body out of the water. He's carried me since I was Nell's age. I'm not going to treat him like a risk to be managed."

Elias was quiet. Then: "All right. But he learns the shape of the plan, not the location of the book. And if he's followed even once—"

"Then I'll tell him to walk away, and he'll tell me the same thing you do, and neither of us will listen." She almost smiled. "We're all alike, us stubborn ones. That's how they keep us down and how we get out."

Nell fell asleep against Elias's shoulder, mid-narration, one fist still closed around the crab leg. Neither of them moved to break the moment. The tide breathed in and out. Somewhere behind them the station lamps burned over people who would call this night a crime if they knew of it.

"Gideon," Elias said. "When it's done — when the wire's sent and Corrigan has the book — what happens to you?"

"I don't know." It was the truest sentence she'd said in years. "I've never had a *what happens next*. Only a *what happens tonight*." She looked at him, and let herself say the rest. "Maybe I'd like to find out what the other kind feels like."

He reached over and took her hand, sand and salt and all, and held it the way he held everything — carefully, as if evidence, but this time as if it mattered more than any case.

"Thursday," he said.

"Thursday," she agreed.

Neither of them mentioned the sea, or prison, or the odds. They didn't need to. Love built inside danger comes with the danger included; they'd both read those terms and signed anyway.

* * *

**Elias**

Wednesday night he walked the jetty one last time before the plan went live, wearing the boredom of an officer making rounds.

The *Star of Broome* rode low with freight. Sister Bernadette's crates were stacked aft, marked MEDICAL, and Elias checked the manifest with a lantern and found nothing worth finding — because Gideon's copies wouldn't travel in the crates at all. They traveled sewn into the hem of the nun's spare habit, folded to the size of a missal. Gideon's idea. She thought like water, finding the crack in anything.

He was halfway back along the planks when he saw the figure at the rail of the Meridine barge, and his stomach dropped.

Sefu.

The big diver stood in the lamplight looking out at the water, and he turned as Elias passed, and for one long moment the two men regarded each other with complete understanding and total silence. Sefu knew. Not the details — the fact. And he was here, the night before the run, standing in plain sight near the boat, which meant either colossal carelessness or a message.

Sefu inclined his head, barely. Then he said, in a voice pitched for Elias alone: "Wind's coming round tomorrow. From the south. Bad for small boats crossing the bar."

Then he walked away, unhurried, a man taking his evening air.

Elias stood very still. Wind from the south meant the harbor master would log weather advisories. Weather advisories meant extra constables at the jetty, extra eyes, extra reasons to open a nun's luggage. Either Sefu was warning him that conditions would change — or warning him that someone else knew about tomorrow and would make sure of those eyes.

He thought of Gideon's voice: *he carried my father's body out of the water.*

He thought of the overseer's reputation, and of how a man like that collected leverage the way driftwood collects barnacles — silently, everywhere, over time.

At midnight Elias stood at his window with the copied manifests in his satchel and made himself look at the possibility squarely: someone had talked. Not Sefu — Sefu had just handed him a warning at personal risk, which was the opposite of talking. Someone else. Someone close enough to Gideon to guess, close enough to the station to be seen guessing.

Tomorrow the nun would sail into a harbor full of new eyes, carrying a habit full of names, and Gideon would be at the jetty among the crowd, and Elias would be expected to search whatever he was ordered to search.

He took out his notebook, found the page with her name underlined from six weeks ago, and tore it out. Burned it in the lamp. Then he packed his satchel properly, loaded his revolver — not to use, but because the weight of it steadied his hands — and sat down to wait for dawn, listening to the wind come round from the south, bringing with it either the storm they needed or the one that would end them.

Either way, by Thursday night, everything would be different.
