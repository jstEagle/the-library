## 12. Sefu Pays

The trouble began with a kindness.

I should have known. Kindness at Meridian was like a pearl in an empty shell — pretty, and proof that something had once lived there before it was pried out.

Sefu had been watching me for weeks. I'd felt it the way you feel a current under calm water: nothing on the surface, everything underneath. He was a Thursday Island man, broad through the shoulders, quiet in the way of men who had learned that their voices carried further than they wanted. We dove the same stretch of shell beds off the station's eastern ground, and in the water he was the only one who never crowded me, never drifted close to steal my patch or my air. There was an etiquette among divers, unspoken and absolute: what happened below stayed below.

It was Sefu who found me at the wash troughs two days after I moved the ledger into the shell heaps, my hands still shaking from the digging, sand worked so deep into my knuckles it looked like age.

"You've been carrying something," he said.

I kept scrubbing. The water ran grey off my fingers. "Shell grit."

"Shell grit doesn't make a woman look over her shoulder at the gate every morning." He said it mildly, without accusation, the way he might have noted a change in the tide. "And it doesn't make her dig holes in the heaps at low water when she thinks nobody's watching."

My heart went cold and slow, the way it did underwater. "You've been following me."

"I've been *noticing* you. There's a difference." He glanced down the row of troughs — empty at this hour, the morning shift already out on the luggers. "Whatever it is, it's got the overseer sniffing too. He's been asking questions about you. Not to you. Around you. That's worse."

I shut off the tap. In the silence I could hear the yard beyond the wall, boots on coral gravel, the winches groaning as the boats came in. The ordinary sounds of a place where ordinary people were owned.

"Sefu—"

"Don't tell me." He held up one huge hand. "I don't want to know. Knowing is a weight, and I've got enough weight." His eyes, though — his eyes wanted to know. They were kind and curious and afraid all at once, and I recognized the combination because I saw it in my own reflection every day. "I'm saying this: if you ever need hands that don't ask questions, mine are steady. My mother was taken by a recruiter from a beach not much bigger than this one. She signed a paper she couldn't read. I can read now. That's the whole of my politics."

He walked away before I could answer, which was its own mercy. I stood at the trough with wet hands and a dry throat and understood that I had just been given something more dangerous than help.

I had been given witness.

* * *

The arrest came eleven days later, on a morning so bright it seemed staged for it.

I was mending netting on the barracks verandah when the sound reached me — boots, several pairs, moving fast and without the shuffle of men going to work. I looked up and saw three of the overseer's island police crossing the yard toward the divers' quarters, and behind them Mr. Quill himself, the overseer, walking with the unhurried gait of a man attending a ceremony.

They went past four doors and stopped at the fifth.

Sefu's door.

"No," I said, out loud, to no one. My hands kept working the netting needle because they had learned long ago that stillness drew the eye, but everything else in me stopped.

They brought him out between them. He didn't struggle; struggling would have been a confession of something. But he turned his head as they crossed the yard, and his gaze swept the verandahs the way a man checks the weather, and when it passed over me it did not pause. Not a flicker. Not a half-second's hesitation. He looked at me the way he looked at everyone, and I understood that even now, even being marched to whatever came next, he was protecting me.

The charge, when it traveled through the yard mouth to mouth, was theft. A brass diving helmet, missing from the stores shed. Found, they said, hidden under Sefu's bunk.

Found. Hidden. Planted in the night by hands I could not see but could name in the dark if I let myself.

Quill stood in the middle of the yard while the police secured Sefu to the post outside the storehouse to await the magistrate's circuit — the circuit that came to Broome twice a year, or came whenever the company needed it to come. Quill made a little speech. I heard most of it across the yard, pitched deliberately loud.

"Theft will not be tolerated. A diver's gear is a diver's livelihood, and a man who steals another man's livelihood steals from every man here. This station runs on trust." He said *trust* the way other men said *God*. "There are those among us who think themselves clever. Who think they can carry secrets out through our gate like contraband." His small eyes moved along the verandahs, unhurried, cataloguing. "Cleverness is noticed. Cleverness is paid for. Let this be the payment."

Nobody breathed. Nobody moved. Two hundred people studied their own hands.

They left Sefu tied to the post through the heat of the day. It was punishment and theater both — the company never wasted suffering when suffering could be displayed. I sat on the verandah with my needle going through the mesh, and I watched him, and he watched the sea.

Once, late in the afternoon when the shadows went long and the guards had grown bored and wandered to the far side of the storehouse, Sefu turned his head and found me. He didn't signal. He didn't mouth words. He did something smaller and worse: he smiled. Just slightly, just enough to move the muscles at the corners of his mouth, the smile of a man reassuring a child that thunder is only noise.

Then he faced forward again, and I sat in the failing light with guilt sitting on my chest like a grown man kneeling.

* * *

That night I nearly went to Quill.

I want that recorded somewhere, even if only in the ledger of my own skull: there was a version of me, standing in the dark of the barracks with Nell asleep against my hip, who almost walked to the overseer's house and offered a trade. The book for the man. The pages for Sefu's freedom. It would have been simple. It would have been quick. Quill wanted the ledger; I wanted Sefu whole; the arithmetic of desperation is always clean until you do it in blood.

What stopped me wasn't courage. Courage is a word for afterward. What stopped me was arithmetic of a colder kind. If I traded the ledger, Sefu would be freed and every name in those columns would stay buried — Tomas Eriksen, my father, recorded as a *loss*, a rounding error, six pounds eight shillings written off against a season's profit. And the divers after him would keep diving until the sea took them or the bends did, and their deaths would go into the same column, and there would be no one left holding the count.

And — I knew this with the certainty of the tide — Quill would take the book and hang me with it. Evidence against the company in the hands of the company's enemy was worth something. Evidence in the company's hands was a confession with my name waiting to be written under it. Sefu would walk free for a month, perhaps. Then some new charge, some quieter arrangement. The company did not forgive witnesses; it recycled them.

So I stood in the dark with my daughter-by-circumstance breathing against my collarbone, and I chose the many over the one, and I have never stopped paying for the choosing.

Nell stirred. She'd begun saying a word in her sleep lately — a name, though she didn't know it was a name. *Mara.* Over and over, soft as surf. In the ledger there was an entry dated March 1889: *M. Anaya, transferred, Roebuck station, fee £4.* A woman sold between stations for less than the cost of a helmet. And a toddler who remembered her voice in dreams.

I rocked her and did my counting. Four counts in, seven counts hold, eight counts out. My father taught me the rhythm before he taught me letters. Breathe the way the water wants, he used to say, and the water forgets you're there.

But the water never forgot. That was the lie we all agreed to live inside.

* * *

The magistrate's circuit arrived on a Thursday, riding in from town in a sulky with a clerk who looked seasick on dry land. The hearing was held in the storehouse among the crates and coils, before an audience of whoever the company allowed to stand at the back — which meant the overseers, the foremen, and a scattering of divers deemed harmless.

I was deemed harmless. I stood at the back with my arms folded and my face set to neutral, the expression I'd worn since I was twelve years old, and I watched them try Sefu.

*Try* flatters it. The helmet was produced. The constable testified to finding it beneath the bunk. Sefu, asked how it came there, said: "I don't know." Asked if he denied putting it there, he said: "I deny nothing except doing it." Asked if he had anything to say about the missing property, he looked at the helmet — a beautiful thing, all copper and glass, worth more than a year of any diver's labor — and said, "It's a good helmet. Whoever uses it should check the neck seal. The third rivet works loose."

Even then. Even bound and charged, he thought of the next man who'd wear it.

The magistrate was a tired man with ink-stained cuffs, and I watched him listen, and I watched him understand — you could see it cross his face like cloud-shadow — that the case was rotten and that knowing changed nothing. Company stations ran on company testimony. The constable swore; the foreman swore; the storekeeper swore. Three oaths against one silence.

Guilty. Six months' hard labor and transfer — *transfer*, the word landed in the room like a dropped anchor — to the station at Cossack. Anyone who knew anything about Cossack knew what that meant: further from town, harder water, rougher masters, and a death rate the company recorded in pencil because pencil could be rubbed out.

As they led him out, Sefu passed within arm's reach of me. The yard was loud with dispersal, everyone moving at once, everyone careful. And in that churn he spoke, low, without turning his head, lips barely moving — a trick of divers, talking around a mouthpiece, wasting no breath:

"Dig deeper next time. The heaps shift with the tide."

Then he was through the door and gone, and I stood in the emptying storehouse amid the smell of hemp and brine, holding his last gift: confirmation that he had known exactly what I buried and where, and had told no one, and had spent his silence buying mine.

* * *

They shipped him out on the morning tide two days later, aboard the *Lilyfontaine*, a coastal trader with rust in her seams and a hold that had carried indentured labor before and would again.

I was not supposed to be at the jetty. Divers weren't permitted on the wharf during loading — too much chance of talk, of trade, of seeing the world that existed past the gate. But the rule bent for laundry detail, and the jetty needed its weekly wash-down, and I volunteered with a meekness that raised no eyebrows because meekness was my uniform.

I scrubbed planks twenty feet from where they loaded him.

He came down the gangway in irons, walking steadily, head up, taking in the harbor the way he took in everything — without complaint, without waste. At the rail they paused him while the mate argued about manifest papers, and in that pause Sefu looked out across Roebuck Bay, at the water he'd worked and the water that had taken so many of us, and he said something to the guard beside him. I was too far to hear it. The guard laughed.

Then up the gangway, into the dark of the hold, and the hatch came down over him like a lid over a jar, and I scrubbed the planks until my knuckles bled because stopping would have meant screaming.

The *Lilyfontaine* warped off the piles just after ten. I watched her until she was a smudge, then a suggestion, then nothing, and the bay lay flat and glittering and indifferent as a coin.

* * *

Grief is fuel. Nobody tells you that. They tell you grief is a weight, an illness, a wave to be endured. But grief, properly banked, burns. I learned it that afternoon, sitting on the end of the jetty with my feet over the water and Nell in my lap, watching the light go bronze on the bay.

Every name in the ledger had been an abstraction to me — terrible, yes, but abstract, ink on a dead man's page. Sefu made it flesh. Sefu, who had known my secret and guarded it with his body; Sefu, whose mother had been bought off a beach for a price the ledger would have shown to the penny if the entry existed; Sefu, gone to Cossack in irons because he had offered a steady pair of hands to a woman he owed nothing.

He had paid for my cargo. The debt was mine now, and I intended to collect it — not from him, never from him, but from them. From Quill and the men behind Quill, from the signatures in the margins, from every inspector who'd taken his envelope and initialled his *inspected and found satisfactory*.

That night I went down to the shell heaps at slack water, when the station slept and even the dogs had given up. I dug with a wooden scoop this time — Sefu's lesson — and lifted the oilskin bundle clear, and carried it back to the barracks wrapped inside a basket of washing.

By candlelight, with Nell asleep, I read the ledger again. But differently now. Before, I had read it as evidence. Tonight I read it as a roll call.

*T. Eriksen, lost, 6.8.*
*M. Anaya, transferred, 4.0.*
*J. Okada, lost, 7.2.*

One by one, I whispered the names aloud, giving each one the dignity the columns had denied them — not *losses*, not *fees*, but men and women with mouths and mothers. When I came to the end of the page I started again, and Nell stirred at the sound of my voice and murmured her word — *Mara, Mara* — as if she were reading over my shoulder, as if somewhere in her small broken memory she knew that names were the beginning of everything.

The quarterly audit was coming. Loose papers burned; the auditor was due within weeks. Whatever was going to be done with this book had to be done soon, done fast, and done past the second checkpoint — the jetty, where Elias Ward would be standing with his customs warrant and his suspicious eyes and whatever it was that lived behind them.

I closed the ledger and blew out the candle and sat in the dark, counting breaths that belonged to no one but me.

Four in. Seven hold. Eight out.

Hold on, Sefu. I'm coming.
